# Scaling Laws with Vocabulary: Larger Models Deserve Larger Vocabularies

**ArXiv:** [2407.13623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.13623)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper studies how vocabulary size impacts LLM scaling laws by training models from 33M to 3B parameters on up to 500B characters under various vocabulary configurations.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies how vocabulary size impacts LLM scaling laws by training models from 33M to 3B parameters on up to 500B characters under various vocabulary configurations. The authors propose three complementary approaches for predicting the **compute-optimal vocabulary size** — IsoFLOPs analysis (fitting power laws relating FLOPs to non-vocabulary parameters, vocabulary parameters, and training data), derivative-based estimation (solving ∂C/∂V = 0 to find the vocabulary that minimizes FLOPs for a target loss), and parametric fit of a vocabulary-dependent loss function — all converging on the finding that optimal vocabulary parameters scale as a power law with non-vocabulary parameters ($N_v^{\text{opt}} \propto N_{nv}^\gamma$ with $\gamma \approx 0.83 < 1$). Applying this prediction, the paper demonstrates that switching from the conventional 32K vocabulary to the predicted optimal 43K vocabulary improves ARC-Challenge accuracy from 29.1 to 32.0 at the same 2.3×10²¹ FLOPs budget, establishing that most existing LLMs (e.g., Llama2-70B, whose optimal vocabulary is predicted at 216K — roughly 7× larger than its actual 32K) systematically undershoot their ideal vocabulary allocation.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Vocabulary Size Has Been a Blind Spot in LLM Scaling

The fundamental question this paper tackles is one that has been systematically overlooked in the LLM scaling literature: **given a fixed computational budget, what vocabulary size should you choose for your language model?** This is a deceptively consequential question because vocabulary size sits at the intersection of three key resource allocation decisions — model parameters, training data volume, and compute expenditure — yet prior scaling laws treat it as a fixed constant rather than a variable to be optimized.

This gap is not merely an academic oversight. The paper's opening motivation is strikingly empirical: Llama2-7B uses a vocabulary of 32K tokens while Gemma-7B uses 256K, despite both having similar total parameter counts (roughly 7 billion). This **7× difference** in vocabulary size between comparably-sized models — with no principled framework for resolving which choice is correct — signals that the field lacks even a rough consensus on how to set this hyperparameter. The paper frames this as a direct research question:

> "What is the compute-optimal vocabulary size for a LLM?"

The stakes are practical and immediate. Vocabulary size affects **tokenization fertility** (how many characters are packed into each token, determining how efficiently the model processes raw text), **model capacity** (vocabulary parameters in the embedding and output layers can constitute a non-trivial fraction of total parameters), and **training dynamics** (rare tokens in overly large vocabularies suffer from underfitting due to insufficient training examples). Getting this wrong means either wasting compute on an underpowered model that cannot represent diverse linguistic phenomena, or wasting compute on embedding parameters that never receive adequate gradient signal — both invisible inefficiencies in standard scaling analyses.

### Why Prior Scaling Laws Ignore Vocabulary

To understand why this gap exists, we need to examine what the dominant scaling frameworks actually model. The two canonical works — Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022, the Chinchilla paper) — both formulate scaling laws as an optimization over two variables:

$$(N^{\text{opt}}, D^{\text{opt}}) = \arg\min_{N, D} \mathcal{L}(N, D) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \text{FLOPs}(N, D) = C$$

where $N$ is model parameters, $D$ is training tokens, and $C$ is the compute budget measured in FLOPs. The key insight of Chinchilla — that $N$ and $D$ should be scaled equally for compute-optimal training — emerges entirely from this two-variable framework.

However, this formulation has a baked-in assumption that the vocabulary size $V$ is fixed. In Kaplan et al., this is made explicit: only **non-vocabulary parameters** are considered in the predictive formula, with vocabulary parameters treated as a constant offset. The Chinchilla paper similarly treats the tokenizer as a pre-determined choice, sweeping model sizes while holding vocabulary constant.

Why was this assumption made? Likely for tractability. Adding vocabulary size as a free variable explodes the experimental design space: instead of varying two attributes ($N$, $D$), you must vary three ($N_{nv}$, $V$, $D$) — or, more precisely, ($N_{nv}$, $V$, $H$) where $H$ is training characters, since the number of tokens $D$ depends on the tokenizer's compression ratio which is itself a function of $V$. The paper estimates that this significantly increases the number of possible experimental configurations, making the naive IsoFLOPs approach substantially more expensive. Prior work sidestepped this complexity by simply holding $V$ constant.

The result is a scaling laws literature that can tell you, with reasonable precision, how many parameters and training tokens to allocate for a given compute budget — but is silent on whether 32K, 64K, or 256K is the right vocabulary size for those parameters and tokens.

### The Contradictory Intuitions That Make Vocabulary Optimization Non-Trivial

The paper motivates the vocabulary optimization problem by articulating two competing intuitions, both of which are partially correct:

**Why larger vocabularies should help:**
- **Tokenization fertility improves.** A tokenizer with more distinct tokens can represent common words, phrases, and subword units as single tokens rather than splitting them across multiple tokens. This means that for a fixed number of training *characters* $H$, a larger vocabulary produces fewer training *tokens* $D$, meaning each token contains more semantic information and the model can process more raw text per training step.
- **Coverage of linguistic phenomena increases.** A larger vocabulary can explicitly represent rare words, domain-specific terminology, and morphological variants that would otherwise be fragmented into less meaningful subword pieces. This is particularly important for multilingual models, as noted in prior work (Wang et al., 2019; Chung et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2023), where low-resource languages benefit disproportionately from dedicated vocabulary capacity.

**Why larger vocabularies should hurt:**
- **Underfitting of rare tokens.** As the vocabulary grows, the frequency of the least common tokens decreases. These rare tokens received limited gradient updates during training, leading to poorly learned embeddings. The paper's Appendix A.3 visualizes this phenomenon directly using SVD decomposition of learned embeddings: at $V = 64K$, low-frequency word embeddings cluster together (quantified via average Euclidean distance $D_{\text{avg}} = 0.952$), while at $V = 4K$, they remain well-separated ($D_{\text{avg}} = 1.067$). This is the representation degeneration problem documented by Gao et al. (2019).
- **Vocabulary parameters consume compute budget.** With $N_v = Vd$, doubling the vocabulary size doubles the number of parameters in the embedding and output layers. These parameters must be trained, consuming FLOPs that could otherwise be allocated to non-vocabulary parameters (Transformer blocks) or more training data. In the data-constrained regime (Muennighoff et al., 2024), this tradeoff becomes particularly acute — expanding vocabulary when training data is scarce means spreading limited gradient signal across more parameters.

The tension between these forces means the optimal vocabulary size is neither trivially small nor trivially large. It depends on the compute budget, the model size, and the amount of available training data — precisely the kind of multi-variable optimization that scaling laws are designed to resolve, but which prior scaling laws have excluded from consideration.

### The Normalization Problem: Why Vocabulary Scaling Is Hard to Study

A key technical barrier that prevented prior work from studying vocabulary scaling is that **standard language modeling loss is not comparable across different vocabulary sizes.** The cross-entropy loss:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^T \log p(w_i | w_{1:i-1}, V)$$

is computed over a vocabulary of size $V$. A model with $V = 256K$ must predict a distribution over 256K possible tokens at each position, while a model with $V = 32K$ only predicts over 32K. The 256K model will naturally have higher loss — there are more ways to be "wrong" — even if it is a strictly better language model. This makes naive loss comparisons across vocabularies meaningless.

The paper addresses this by adopting the **unigram-normalized loss** from Roh et al. (2020):

$$\mathcal{L}_u = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^T \log \frac{p(w_i | w_{1:i-1}, V)}{p(w_i | V)}$$

This normalizes the model's probability by the unigram frequency of each token in the training corpus $p(w_i|V)$. The interpretation is intuitive: $\mathcal{L}_u$ measures how much better the context-aware language model is compared to a context-free unigram baseline. If a 256K-vocab model and a 32K-vocab model both improve over their respective unigram baselines by the same margin, they are considered comparable. The paper validates this metric empirically in Appendix A.10, showing that it exhibits the correct negative correlation with downstream performance (lower $\mathcal{L}_u$ → better downstream accuracy), unlike raw loss which can show a **positive** correlation (higher loss → better downstream performance, because larger vocabularies simultaneously increase both loss and model quality).

This normalization is not a minor implementation detail — it is the methodological key that unlocks the entire paper. Without it, you cannot run a controlled experiment that varies vocabulary size and ask "which vocabulary gives the best model?" because the loss values aren't on the same scale. The paper's Appendix A.5 further shows that $\mathcal{L}_u$ is highly correlated with bits-per-character (BPC), another vocabulary-insensitive metric, with a Pearson correlation of $\rho = 0.9888$, providing convergent validation.

### Where Prior Work on Vocabulary Falls Short

The paper acknowledges that vocabulary size has received some attention in prior literature, but identifies specific limitations:

**Empirical heuristics without theoretical grounding.** Prior work on vocabulary expansion (e.g., Wang et al., 2019; Chung et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2023) has largely focused on multilingual settings where the vocabulary of a pretrained model is extended to cover new languages. These works demonstrate that larger vocabularies help, but they do so through empirical comparisons rather than principled scaling laws. They tell you that expanding vocabulary for a specific low-resource language is beneficial, but cannot answer: *by how much* should vocabulary scale as the total model grows 10×? 100×?

**Language-specific recommendations.** The multilingual vocabulary literature has produced language-specific vocabulary size recommendations (e.g., Blevins et al., 2024; Dou et al., 2024) but these are tied to particular data mixtures and language distributions. A general scaling law — one that predicts optimal vocabulary as a function of compute, independent of language — is absent.

**Computation-agnostic analysis.** Dagan et al. (2024) explored vocabulary size tradeoffs for code generation, proposing optimal sizes for memory efficiency and inference speed. However, their analysis is specific to code tasks and does not connect vocabulary size to the broader framework of compute-optimal scaling — it doesn't tell you how the optimal vocabulary changes when you have 10× more FLOPs to spend.

**Byte-level models as an extreme case.** Yu et al. (2024) and Wang et al. (2024b) have explored byte-level language models (vocabulary size of 256), which eliminate tokenization entirely. While offering advantages in decoding efficiency and robustness, these models have been limited to parameters under 1B. The paper's scaling laws suggest an explanation: the vocabulary of 256 is likely far from optimal for larger models, constraining their performance. This positions byte-level models not as a rejection of the vocabulary paradigm, but as an extreme operating point whose limitations are predicted by the scaling framework.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames itself as filling a fundamental gap in the **compute-optimal allocation problem**. In the canonical scaling laws formulation (Equation 1 in the paper), the optimization is:

$$(N^{\text{opt}}, D^{\text{opt}}) = \arg\min_{N, D} \mathcal{L}(N, D) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \text{FLOPs}(N, D) = C$$

The paper extends this to a three-variable problem:

$$(N_{nv}^{\text{opt}}, N_v^{\text{opt}}, H^{\text{opt}}) = \arg\min_{N_{nv}, N_v, H} \mathcal{L}_u(N_{nv}, N_v, H) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \text{FLOPs}(N_{nv}, N_v, H) = C$$

This is not merely adding a variable — it requires rethinking the units of measurement. The paper's key methodological contributions (detailed in Section 2.2) are:

1. **Decompose parameters:** $N = N_{nv} + N_v$ where $N_v = Vd$, separating vocabulary parameters from Transformer parameters.
2. **Measure data in characters, not tokens:** Since the number of tokens $D$ depends on the tokenizer's vocabulary (a larger vocabulary produces fewer tokens from the same text), the paper measures raw training data as $H$ (characters), with a learned mapping function $f(V)$ to convert between characters and tokens.
3. **Use vocabulary-insensitive loss:** The unigram-normalized loss $\mathcal{L}_u$ enables fair comparison across vocabulary sizes.

These three changes make it possible for the first time to treat vocabulary size as a variable in the compute-optimal allocation problem rather than a fixed constant.

The paper explicitly positions itself within the Chinchilla scaling paradigm (Hoffmann et al., 2022) — the goal is to derive predictive formulas that map from a compute budget $C$ to the optimal allocation of all three resources. However, the paper also extends beyond the pure compute-optimal case. Approach 3 (§4.3) is specifically designed to handle scenarios where the allocation of $N_{nv}$ and $H$ is not compute-optimal — for instance, when a model is overtrained on excessive data (like Llama2-7B trained on 2T tokens, far more than the compute-optimal ~150B tokens for its size) or undertrained due to data scarcity. This makes the framework applicable to real-world models that deviate from theoretical optimums.

The paper's predictions for existing models (Figure 2, Figure 6) serve as both validation of the approach's practical relevance and a critique of current practice. For example, the prediction that Llama2-70B's optimal vocabulary should have been at least **216K** — roughly $7\times$ larger than its actual 32K — is not merely a theoretical curiosity but a concrete suggestion that substantial performance improvements were left on the table. The paper notes that the community is already shifting in this direction (Llama3 increased vocabulary from 32K to 128K), but that even this may be insufficient for the 70B and 400B variants (Appendix A.11).

### Why This Matters Beyond Academic Interest

The practical implications extend beyond the narrow question of vocabulary selection:

**Efficiency gains at fixed compute budgets.** The paper's headline result — improving ARC-Challenge from 29.1 to 32.0 at the same $2.3\times 10^{21}$ FLOPs by changing only vocabulary size (Table 3) — demonstrates that vocabulary optimization is not a second-order effect. A 2.9 percentage point improvement on a challenging reasoning benchmark, at zero additional training cost, represents a substantial efficiency gain. For organizations training LLMs at scale, this translates directly to either better models for the same cost or the same model quality for lower cost.

**Democratization of training decisions.** Currently, vocabulary size selection is largely based on precedent (32K because Llama used it, or 256K because Gemma used it) rather than principled reasoning. A predictive framework that estimates compute-optimal vocabulary given model size and data budget would democratize this decision — smaller organizations without the resources for extensive ablation studies could use the scaling laws to make informed choices.

**Connection to data-constrained regimes.** Muennighoff et al. (2024) established that when training data is scarce, the optimal allocation shifts toward smaller models trained for more epochs. This paper's finding that the optimal vocabulary size *decreases* when data is scarce (Figure 7, left: optimal vocabulary shifts from 16K → 10K for $N_{nv} = 302M$ as data decreases) provides a complementary insight: data constraints affect not just the parameter-data tradeoff, but also the vocabulary allocation. In data-constrained settings, shrinking the vocabulary becomes a mechanism to prevent underfitting of rare tokens.

**A bridge between tokenization research and scaling laws.** Tokenization has historically been treated as a preprocessing step, separate from model architecture and training decisions. BPE, SentencePiece, and Unigram tokenizers are typically trained independently, with vocabulary size chosen arbitrarily (often powers of 2 for GPU efficiency). This paper establishes that tokenization and model scaling are **coupled decisions** that should be optimized jointly — the optimal vocabulary size is a function of the model's capacity and the available data, not a standalone hyperparameter.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper builds a predictive framework — a set of three complementary mathematical models — that estimates the optimal vocabulary size for a language model given a computational budget. The system takes as input the desired non-vocabulary parameter count (or total FLOPs budget) and outputs a recommended vocabulary size, where "optimal" means the vocabulary that minimizes the unigram-normalized language modeling loss for that budget, thereby maximizing downstream performance.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components:

1. **Vocabulary-Insensitive Loss Function ($\mathcal{L}_u$)** — a normalized evaluation metric that makes loss values comparable across models with different vocabulary sizes. Without this, you cannot ask "which vocabulary is better?" because the raw cross-entropy loss is inherently higher for larger vocabularies.
2. **Tokens-to-Characters Mapping Function ($f(V)$)** — a learned quadratic function that predicts how many tokens a tokenizer with vocabulary size $V$ will produce from a given number of raw characters. This enables computing FLOPs in a vocabulary-independent way.
3. **Three Prediction Approaches** — the core intellectual contribution: IsoFLOPs power-law fitting (Approach 1), derivative-based FLOPs minimization (Approach 2), and parametric loss function fitting (Approach 3). These are alternative methods that converge on the same scaling relationship.
4. **Experimental Training Infrastructure** — models with non-vocabulary parameters ranging from 33M to 2.87B, each trained with vocabulary sizes from 4K to 96K on up to 500B characters from SlimPajama, used to fit the predictive models.

Information flows as follows: training data (characters $H$) → tokenizer with vocabulary size $V$ → $D$ tokens (predicted by $f(V)$) → Transformer model with $N_{nv}$ non-vocabulary parameters and $N_v = Vd$ vocabulary parameters → unigram-normalized loss $\mathcal{L}_u$ → fitting procedures → predicted optimal $V$ for any target $N_{nv}$ and compute budget.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the **vocabulary-insensitive loss $\mathcal{L}_u$** and why standard loss fails, since every experiment and prediction depends on having a fair comparison metric.
- **Second**, the **decomposition of model parameters** into $N_{nv}$ and $N_v$ and the **tokens-characters mapping $f(V)$**, since these are the foundational reformulations that make vocabulary a variable in the FLOPs equation.
- **Third**, **Approach 1 (IsoFLOPs power laws)**, since it is the most direct empirical method and establishes the core scaling relationship.
- **Fourth**, **Approach 2 (Derivative-based estimation)**, which provides a cheaper, theory-driven alternative that converges to the same answer.
- **Fifth**, **Approach 3 (Parametric loss fit)**, the most flexible method that handles non-optimal training regimes (overtraining, undertraining) where Approaches 1 and 2 break down.
- **Sixth**, the **fitting procedures and hyperparameters** shared across approaches, since these are the implementation details that make the methods work in practice.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **scaling laws paper** whose core idea is that vocabulary size obeys a power-law relationship with computational budget, and that this relationship can be predicted using three complementary mathematical approaches that all converge on the same finding: vocabulary parameters should scale slower than non-vocabulary parameters ($N_v^{\text{opt}} \propto N_{nv}^\gamma$ with $\gamma \approx 0.83 < 1$).

#### The Vocabulary-Insensitive Loss Function: Why Standard Loss Cannot Compare Vocabularies

The standard language modeling loss — the average negative log-likelihood of the next token given context — is computed as:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^T \log p(w_i | w_{1:i-1}, V)$$

where $T$ is the sequence length in tokens, $w_i$ is the $i$-th token, $w_{1:i-1}$ is the preceding context, $V$ is the vocabulary size, and $p(w_i | w_{1:i-1}, V)$ is the model's predicted probability for the correct token $w_i$ given context and tokenizer.

**What it computes:** the average surprise (in nats or bits, depending on the logarithm base) of the model when predicting each token in a held-out sequence, given the preceding context. Lower values indicate the model assigns higher probability to the correct tokens.

**Why this form cannot compare vocabularies:** the probability $p(w_i | w_{1:i-1}, V)$ is a distribution over $V$ possible outcomes. A model with $V = 256K$ must spread probability mass across 256K possibilities; even a perfect model can achieve at best a uniform baseline loss of $\log(256K) \approx 12.4$ nats. A model with $V = 32K$ only needs to handle 32K outcomes, with a uniform baseline of $\log(32K) \approx 10.4$ nats. This means the 256K model will have **higher loss by construction**, even if it is a strictly better language model, because its prediction problem is inherently harder. The paper confirms this empirically in Appendix A.10: models with larger vocabularies show a **positive** correlation between loss and downstream performance — higher loss accompanies *better* downstream accuracy — which is the opposite of what a good metric should show.

The paper therefore adopts the **unigram-normalized loss** from Roh et al. (2020):

$$\mathcal{L}_u = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^T \log \frac{p(w_i | w_{1:i-1}, V)}{p(w_i | V)}$$

where $p(w_i|V)$ is the unigram frequency of token $w_i$ in the training corpus given the tokenizer with vocabulary size $V$, and all other symbols are as defined above.

**What it computes:** the ratio of how much better the context-aware language model predicts each token compared to a context-free unigram model that simply predicts tokens based on their overall corpus frequency. The numerator $p(w_i | w_{1:i-1}, V)$ is the model's context-conditioned probability; the denominator $p(w_i|V)$ is the baseline probability from a lookup table of token frequencies. If the language model offers no improvement over the unigram baseline, $\mathcal{L}_u = 0$ (because the ratio is 1, and $\log(1) = 0$). If the model is worse than the unigram baseline, $\mathcal{L}_u$ can be positive. If the model is better — which is the expected case — $\mathcal{L}_u$ can be negative, because $\log(\text{ratio} > 1)$ is positive, and the negative sign outside flips it.

**Why this form enables cross-vocabulary comparison:** the denominator $p(w_i|V)$ naturally adjusts for vocabulary size. A tokenizer with larger $V$ will have smaller unigram probabilities for each token (because mass is spread across more tokens), so both numerator and denominator shift together. The ratio measures the **value added by context** above and beyond what the vocabulary size alone determines. The paper validates this empirically in Appendix A.10: when $\mathcal{L}_u$ is plotted against downstream performance across models with varying vocabulary sizes, the correlation is **negative** — lower $\mathcal{L}_u$ corresponds to better downstream accuracy — which is the expected relationship for a well-behaved loss metric.

An alternative metric, **bits per character (BPC)**, is also vocabulary-insensitive and is explored in Appendix A.5. BPC measures the raw per-character language model loss:

$$\text{BPC} = -\frac{1}{C}\sum_{i=1}^C \log_2 p(c_i | c_{1:i-1})$$

where $C$ is the number of characters and $c_i$ are individual characters. The paper finds a strong linear correlation between $\mathcal{L}_u$ and BPC (Pearson $\rho = 0.9888$, root mean square error $e = 0.0683$), confirming that both metrics capture the same underlying model quality. The paper uses $\mathcal{L}_u$ as the primary metric throughout because it connects directly to the token-level predictions that language models are trained on, but notes that either metric would be valid.

#### Decomposing Model Parameters: Separating Vocabulary from Non-Vocabulary

To make vocabulary size a variable in the scaling analysis, the paper decomposes total model parameters $N$ into two components:

$$N = N_{nv} + N_v$$

where $N_{nv}$ is the count of **non-vocabulary parameters** (all parameters in Transformer blocks: attention weights, feed-forward network weights, layer norms, etc.), and $N_v$ is the count of **vocabulary parameters**.

The vocabulary parameters are defined as:

$$N_v = V \cdot d$$

where $V$ is the vocabulary size (number of distinct tokens) and $d$ is the embedding dimension (the hidden size of the model's residual stream, also called the model width).

**Why this decomposition matters:** in a standard Transformer, there are two parameter matrices tied to the vocabulary: the **word embedding matrix** (input layer, mapping token indices to $d$-dimensional vectors) and the **output projection matrix** (mapping the final hidden state back to a distribution over $V$ tokens, often called the language modeling head). Technically, these are both $V \times d$ matrices, so total vocabulary parameters could be $2Vd$ if they are untied. The paper, however, uses $N_v = Vd$ rather than $2Vd$. The justification is provided in Section 2.2, footnote 1:

> "Vocabulary parameters typically encompass both the word embedding layer and the output layer. In this paper, for clarity and analytical simplicity, we employ $Vd$ rather than $2Vd$ to represent the vocabulary parameters. This choice is predicated on empirical observations: the main computational burden, as measured in FLOPs, is associated with the output layer, but not the word embedding layer."

**What this means operationally:** when the paper varies $V$, it changes the size of both the embedding and output layers. However, the FLOPs contribution (discussed next) only counts the output layer's computation, since the embedding lookup is a simple indexing operation that contributes negligible FLOPs compared to matrix multiplications. The parameter count $N_v = Vd$ is a simplifying approximation that captures the parameter scaling while acknowledging that the FLOPs cost of vocabulary parameters is asymmetric.

**How $d$ is determined:** the embedding dimension is not independently optimized alongside vocabulary size. Instead, the paper follows Kaplan et al. (2020)'s observation that "the performance of models with varying depth-to-width ratios converges to a single trend" — meaning that given a fixed $N_{nv}$, the specific allocation between depth (number of layers) and width ($d$) has a relatively small effect on performance. The paper therefore takes $d$ as given based on $N_{nv}$, using a predetermined mapping (Table 5 in Appendix A.7.2). For example:
- $N_{nv} \leq 50\text{M}$ → $d = 512$
- $50\text{M} < N_{nv} \leq 200\text{M}$ → $d = 768$
- $200\text{M} < N_{nv} \leq 500\text{M}$ → $d = 1024$
- $500\text{M} < N_{nv} \leq 1\text{B}$ → $d = 1536$
- $2\text{B} < N_{nv} \leq 5\text{B}$ → $d = 3200$

This means that when the paper varies $V$ for a fixed $N_{nv}$, the embedding dimension $d$ stays constant — all models in that group have the same Transformer architecture (layers, heads, hidden size) and differ only in the size of their embedding and output matrices according to $N_v = V \cdot d$. This isolates the effect of vocabulary size without confounding from architectural changes.

#### Measuring Data in Characters, Not Tokens: The $f(V)$ Mapping

A fundamental complication in studying vocabulary size is that the **number of training tokens depends on the vocabulary itself**. If you train a model with vocabulary size $V = 32K$ on a corpus of 100 billion characters, the tokenizer will produce some number of tokens $D$. If you instead use $V = 256K$ on the same 100 billion characters, you will get **fewer** tokens because the tokenizer can represent common words and subwords as single tokens rather than splitting them across multiple tokens. This means you cannot directly control for "amount of training data" in tokens when comparing vocabularies — the token count is an outcome of the vocabulary choice.

The paper resolves this by measuring training data in **characters** ($H$), which is vocabulary-independent, and then modeling the mapping from characters to tokens as a function of vocabulary size:

$$D = H \cdot f(V)$$

where $f(V)$ is the **tokenization fertility** or **compression ratio** — the average number of tokens produced per character by a tokenizer with vocabulary size $V$.

**What $f(V)$ physically represents:** if a tokenizer converts 100 characters into 25 tokens on average, then $f(V) = 0.25$ tokens per character. A larger vocabulary produces fewer tokens for the same text, so $f(V)$ is a decreasing function of $V$. Each token in a larger-vocabulary model carries more semantic information (it represents a larger character span), but there are fewer total tokens to train on.

**The functional form of $f(V)$:** the paper finds that a quadratic function in the logarithm of $V$ provides an excellent fit:

$$f(V) = a \log^2(V) + b \log(V) + c$$

where $a$, $b$, $c$ are learned parameters, $\log(V)$ is the natural logarithm of vocabulary size, and the output is tokens per character.

**Fitting $f(V)$:** the authors train 25 BPE tokenizers with vocabulary sizes ranging from 1,024 to 1,024,000 tokens on a uniformly sampled subset of the SlimPajama dataset. They then apply each tokenizer to a held-out validation set, count the total tokens produced, and fit the quadratic using `scipy.optimize.curve_fit`. The resulting parameters are:

$$a = 0.0064, \quad b = -0.1581, \quad c = 1.2047$$

with a relative mean square error (RMSE) of $1.5\times 10^{-6}$ and coefficient of determination $R^2 = 0.99$.

**What this enables:** given any vocabulary size $V$, you can predict how many tokens a BPE tokenizer will produce from a fixed text corpus. This is essential for FLOPs calculations (discussed next) because you need to know $D$ to compute total training FLOPs, and $D$ itself depends on $V$ through the compression ratio.

**Robustness of $f(V)$:** Appendix A.9 demonstrates that the quadratic functional form is robust to the choice of tokenizer algorithm. The paper fits $f(V)$ separately for BPE, Unigram, and Word-based tokenizers, and all three achieve $R^2 \geq 0.98$ with low RMSE. The BPE and Unigram tokenizers produce similar compression ratios (subword tokenization), while the Word-based tokenizer produces poorer compression. This means the specific $a, b, c$ values depend on the tokenizer type, but the quadratic-in-log-$V$ functional form is universal.

**A subtle correction for very large $V$:** the quadratic function $a\log^2(V) + b\log(V) + c$ is a parabola in $\log(V)$ space, and since $a > 0$, it eventually increases as $V$ grows very large (beyond approximately 218K tokens). This would incorrectly predict that extremely large vocabularies produce *more* tokens per character, which is physically impossible — tokenization fertility cannot get worse as you add more tokens to the vocabulary. In reality, as $V$ grows large enough to cover most words in the corpus, further increases produce negligible changes in tokens per character. The paper handles this by capping $V$ at 200K before computing $f(V)$: `min(V, 200K)`. This prevents the quadratic from incorrectly increasing at extreme $V$ values, while being well above the range of predicted optimal vocabularies (the largest prediction in the paper is 389K for a 300B-parameter model with optimal training data). The paper notes in Appendix A.9 that if substantially larger vocabularies needed to be considered in the future, the fitting data could simply be extended to include tokenizers with $V > 200K$ to get more accurate predictions in that regime.

#### The FLOPs Equation with Vocabulary

With the parameter decomposition and character-to-token mapping in place, the paper can write the total training FLOPs in a way that makes vocabulary size explicit. Following Kaplan et al. (2020)'s approximation for Transformer models:

$$C \approx 6ND$$

where $C$ is total training FLOPs, $N$ is total model parameters, and $D$ is number of training tokens. The factor of 6 comes from the fact that each training token requires approximately 6 FLOPs per parameter: 2 FLOPs for the forward pass (one multiply and one add per weight), and 4 FLOPs for the backward pass (computing gradients requires roughly twice the forward FLOPs).

Substituting $N = N_{nv} + N_v$, $N_v = Vd$, and $D = H \cdot f(V)$:

$$C \approx 6(N_{nv} + Vd) \cdot H \cdot f(V)$$

**What this equation makes visible:** the vocabulary size $V$ appears in two competing places:
1. **In the parameter term $(N_{nv} + Vd)$:** larger $V$ increases total parameters, which increases FLOPs per token.
2. **In the data term $f(V)$:** larger $V$ decreases the number of tokens $D$ for a fixed character budget $H$, which decreases total FLOPs because there are fewer tokens to process.

These two effects pull in opposite directions, creating a minimum in FLOPs at some intermediate vocabulary size when other variables are held constant. This is the mathematical basis for the existence of a compute-optimal vocabulary size, which the paper formalizes in Analysis 1 (Section 3, Figure 3 left).

**Neglected FLOPs from embeddings:** as noted earlier, the embedding layer contributes parameters ($Vd$ is counted) but the embedding lookup operation (indexing into a matrix) is not a matrix multiplication and contributes negligible FLOPs compared to the attention and feed-forward computations. The output projection layer (mapping from hidden states to vocabulary logits) *does* involve a matrix multiplication of size $d \times V$ per token, and this FLOPs contribution is included in the standard $6ND$ approximation. The paper's footnote clarifies that the primary computational burden is from the output layer, not the embedding layer, justifying the approximation.

#### Approach 1: Estimating Power Laws via IsoFLOPs

Approach 1 is the most direct empirical method. It answers the question: **for a given FLOPs budget, what combination of non-vocabulary parameters, vocabulary parameters, and training characters minimizes the unigram-normalized loss?**

**Experimental design.** The authors define 6 groups of models with fixed non-vocabulary parameters $N_{nv}$ at six different scales: 33M, 85M, 151M, 302M, 631M, and 1.13B parameters. Within each group, the Transformer architecture (number of layers, attention heads, embedding dimension $d$, intermediate FFN size) is held constant. The only thing that varies across models in the same group is the vocabulary size $V$, which takes values from the set: 4K, 6K, 8K, 10K, 16K, 24K, 32K, 48K, 64K, 96K (all divisible by 128 for GPU efficiency). Each model is trained on the SlimPajama dataset with a fixed number of training characters $H$ determined by the group (ranging from 4.3B characters for the 33M group to 201.3B characters for the 1.13B group). All models use the Llama architecture with the same training hyperparameters (AdamW optimizer, max learning rate $4\times 10^{-4}$ decaying to $4\times 10^{-5}$, global batch size 512, bfloat16 mixed precision). The exact architectural configurations are listed in Table 4 (Appendix A.7.1).

**Why these specific $V$ values?** The paper notes they are "divisible by 128 for compatibility with NVIDIA's tensor core to accelerate matrix multiplication" — this is a practical GPU consideration that avoids wasted computation on non-aligned matrix dimensions.

**Training and data collection.** For each model, the paper records the unigram-normalized loss $\mathcal{L}_u$ on a held-out validation set at multiple points during training. This produces, for each combination of $(N_{nv}, V, H)$, a trajectory of (FLOPs, $\mathcal{L}_u$) pairs. Since the paper needs to compare models at identical FLOPs budgets, and models with different vocabulary sizes accumulate FLOPs at different rates (because $C \approx 6(N_{nv} + Vd)Hf(V)$ depends on $V$), the paper uses **interpolation** to estimate the loss at specified FLOPs budgets. Specifically, for each model, the paper fits the observed (FLOPs, $\mathcal{L}_u$) points and interpolates to predict $\mathcal{L}_u$ at target budgets.

The paper describes this interpolation process in Section 4.1: given the observed FLOPs and loss points for each model, they "use interpolation to predict the loss when FLOPs reaches the budget." This is a standard technique in scaling laws research, necessary because models with different configurations don't naturally align at the same FLOPs values.

**Selecting compute-optimal points.** For each FLOPs budget (across a range of budgets), the paper identifies the combination of $(N_{nv}, N_v, H)$ that achieves the lowest $\mathcal{L}_u$. These are the "IsoFLOPs-optimal" points — for a fixed compute budget, they are the empirically best allocation across all three variables. The paper visualizes all training runs in Figure 4 and marks these optimal points as the data for fitting.

**The power-law hypothesis.** Following Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022), the paper hypothesizes that each of the three variables follows a power-law relationship with the FLOPs budget $C$ at the compute-optimal allocation:

$$N_{nv} = k_1 C^{\alpha_1}$$

$$N_v = k_2 C^{\alpha_2}$$

$$H = k_3 C^{\alpha_3}$$

where $k_1, k_2, k_3$ are scaling coefficients (determining the absolute scale), and $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3$ are scaling exponents (determining how fast each variable grows with compute).

**The equal-scaling constraint.** The paper enforces the Chinchilla finding (Hoffmann et al., 2022) that model parameters and training data should be scaled equally for compute-optimal training. This translates to the constraint $\alpha_1 = \alpha_3$ — the exponent for non-vocabulary parameters equals the exponent for training characters. This is an assumption carried over from prior work: the paper does not re-verify that equal scaling is optimal when vocabulary is also a free variable, but rather incorporates it as a constraint based on the established Chinchilla result. The vocabulary exponent $\alpha_2$ is left unconstrained — it can be different from $\alpha_1$, and the data determines whether it is.

**Fitting procedure.** The paper fits the power laws in logarithmic space to avoid numerical issues. Taking $N_{nv}$ as an example, the optimization is:

$$\min_{K_1, \alpha_1} \text{Huber}_\delta(K_1 + \alpha_1 \log(C), \log(N_{nv}))$$

where $K_1 = \log(k_1)$, and $\text{Huber}_\delta$ is the Huber loss with $\delta = 0.001$. The Huber loss behaves like mean squared error for small residuals and like mean absolute error for large residuals, providing robustness to outliers. The optimization uses the L-BFGS algorithm (a quasi-Newton method) with parameters initialized from a uniform grid: $K \in [-20, 15]$ and $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ with 20 initial guesses each, selecting the best result. The same procedure is applied independently for $N_v$ and $H$.

**Fitted results (Figure 5).** The paper reports:

$$N_{nv} = 0.08 \cdot C^{0.50}$$

$$N_v = 0.20 \cdot C^{0.42}$$

$$H = 6.42 \cdot C^{0.50}$$

All fits achieve low RMSE and high $R^2$ values (specific numbers are reported in Figure 5).

**Key findings from the exponents:**

1. **Non-vocabulary parameters and training characters scale with exponent 0.50** (by constraint). This means that if you double the FLOPs budget, the optimal allocation to both $N_{nv}$ and $H$ increases by a factor of $2^{0.50} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$.

2. **Vocabulary parameters scale with exponent 0.42**, which is smaller than 0.50. This means vocabulary grows slower than non-vocabulary parameters: $\gamma = \alpha_2 / \alpha_1 = 0.42 / 0.50 = 0.84 < 1$. In concrete terms, if non-vocabulary parameters increase by a factor of 10, the optimal vocabulary parameters increase by a factor of $10^{0.84} \approx 6.9$, not 10.

3. **The relationship $N_v \propto N_{nv}^\gamma$** with $\gamma \approx 0.84$ is the central quantitative finding of the paper. It means that as models get larger, their vocabularies should also grow, but at a slower rate than the Transformer parameters. The paper's interpretation (Section 4.1):

> "once a sufficiently rich embedding space is present via a large vocabulary, it is more critical to scale non-vocabulary parameters to learn the intricate syntactic and semantic structures of language via Transformer blocks."

**How Approach 1 is used for prediction:** given a target compute budget $C$, plug it into the three power laws to get $N_{nv}^{\text{opt}}$, $N_v^{\text{opt}}$, and $H^{\text{opt}}$. Then compute $V^{\text{opt}} = N_v^{\text{opt}} / d$, where $d$ is the embedding dimension appropriate for $N_{nv}^{\text{opt}}$ (from Table 5). For example, Table 1 shows that for $N_{nv} = 70\text{B}$ (roughly the Llama2-70B scale), Approach 1 predicts $N_v^{\text{opt}} = 1.7\text{B}$ and $V^{\text{opt}} = 212\text{K}$ (using $d = 8192$), compared to Llama2-70B's actual vocabulary of 32K.

**Limitations of Approach 1:** it only predicts the optimal vocabulary when the allocation of all three variables ($N_{nv}, N_v, H$) is globally compute-optimal — meaning they follow the Chinchilla equal-scaling law and the fitted power laws simultaneously. In practice, many models are trained with non-optimal allocations (e.g., Llama2-7B trained on 2T tokens instead of the compute-optimal ~150B tokens). Approach 1 cannot predict the locally optimal vocabulary for a fixed, non-optimal $(N_{nv}, H)$ pair. This is where Approaches 2 and 3 provide value.

#### Approach 2: Derivative-Based Fast Estimation

Approach 2 takes a different perspective. Instead of empirically finding the allocation that minimizes loss for a given FLOPs budget, it asks: **for a model with fixed non-vocabulary parameters $N_{nv}$ trained on a fixed character budget $H$, what vocabulary size minimizes the FLOPs required to achieve a target loss?**

**The mathematical setup.** The paper inverts the standard optimization problem. Instead of:

$$\min_{N_{nv}, N_v, H} \mathcal{L}_u(N_{nv}, N_v, H) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad C(N_{nv}, N_v, H) = \text{budget}$$

Approach 2 considers:

$$\min_{V} C(N_{nv}, V, H) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathcal{L}_u(N_{nv}, V, H) = \ell$$

where $\ell$ is a target loss value. In words: given that the model achieves some loss $\ell$, and given fixed non-vocabulary parameters and training characters, what vocabulary size requires the fewest FLOPs to reach that loss?

**Why this is useful:** if the model is near the compute-optimal frontier (meaning loss and FLOPs are related by a power law, as established by Kaplan et al. and Hoffmann et al.), then minimizing FLOPs for a target loss is equivalent to maximizing loss reduction for a given FLOPs budget. The vocabulary that minimizes FLOPs at one point on the frontier will be the same vocabulary that minimizes loss at the corresponding budget — the two optimization problems are dual.

**The derivative of FLOPs with respect to $V$:** the paper computes the partial derivative of the FLOPs equation with respect to vocabulary size:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial V} = 6H\left[(N_{nv} + Vd)\frac{2a\log(V) + b}{V} + \left(a\log^2(V) + b\log(V) + c\right)d\right]$$

where $a, b, c$ are the parameters of $f(V)$ from Section 2.2, $d$ is the embedding dimension, $H$ is training characters, and $N_{nv}$ is non-vocabulary parameters.

**Derivation walkthrough:** the FLOPs equation is $C = 6(N_{nv} + Vd) \cdot H \cdot f(V)$ where $f(V) = a\log^2(V) + b\log(V) + c$. This is a product of two functions of $V$: $g(V) = (N_{nv} + Vd)$ and $h(V) = f(V)$. By the product rule, $\partial C/\partial V = 6H[g'(V)h(V) + g(V)h'(V)]$. Computing $g'(V) = d$ (derivative of $N_{nv} + Vd$ with respect to $V$ is simply $d$, since $N_{nv}$ is constant) and $h'(V) = (2a\log(V) + b)/V$ (derivative of the quadratic in $\log(V)$ by the chain rule: derivative of $\log^2(V)$ is $2\log(V) \cdot (1/V)$, derivative of $\log(V)$ is $1/V$). Substituting gives the expression above.

**What the derivative means physically:** the term $(N_{nv} + Vd)(2a\log(V) + b)/V$ represents the effect of vocabulary size on tokenization efficiency — how changing $V$ changes the number of tokens and thus total FLOPs. The term $(a\log^2(V) + b\log(V) + c)d$ represents the effect of vocabulary size on parameters per token — each additional vocabulary entry adds $d$ parameters that require FLOPs to train. The derivative is zero when these two effects balance.

**Solving for the optimal $V$:** the optimal vocabulary size is the solution to $\partial C / \partial V = 0$. Since the equation involves both $\log(V)$ and $V$ in ways that cannot be algebraically separated, the paper solves it numerically using `scipy.optimize.fsolve`. This is fast — evaluating the derivative and finding the root takes milliseconds — making Approach 2 computationally trivial compared to training models (Approach 1) or fitting parametric functions (Approach 3).

**The scaling proportion $\gamma$ from Approach 2:** by solving $\partial C / \partial V = 0$ for multiple values of $N_{nv}$ (keeping $H$ and $d$ appropriately scaled), the paper obtains a set of pairs $\{(N_{nv}^{(i)}, N_v^{(i)}) | i = 1, \dots, n\}$ where each $N_v^{(i)} = V^{(i)}d$ is the derivative-optimal vocabulary parameter count for that $N_{nv}^{(i)}$. The paper then fits a power law relating these:

$$N_v \propto N_{nv}^\gamma$$

by minimizing:

$$\min_{\gamma} \text{Huber}_\delta\left(\gamma \cdot \log\left(\frac{N_{nv}^{(i)}}{N_{nv}^{(0)}}\right), \log\left(\frac{N_v^{(i)}}{N_v^{(0)}}\right)\right)$$

where $(N_{nv}^{(0)}, N_v^{(0)})$ is a reference point (a small model). The resulting exponent is $\gamma = 0.83$, consistent with the $0.84$ from Approach 1.

**Practical usage of Approach 2:** the paper describes a two-step procedure:
1. **Calibrate on a small model:** empirically find the optimal vocabulary size for a small model (e.g., $N_{nv}^0 = 33\text{M}$) by training with several vocabulary sizes and picking the one that minimizes $\mathcal{L}_u$. Let this optimal vocabulary be $V^0$ and corresponding vocabulary parameters be $N_v^0 = V^0 d^0$.
2. **Scale to larger models:** for any target $N_{nv}$ with embedding dimension $d$, the predicted optimal vocabulary parameters are:

$$N_v^{\text{opt}} = N_v^0 \cdot \left(\frac{N_{nv}}{N_{nv}^0}\right)^\gamma$$

where $\gamma = 0.83$, and then $V^{\text{opt}} = N_v^{\text{opt}} / d$.

**Why this two-step procedure is valuable:** it means you don't need to train models at every scale to estimate the optimal vocabulary. One carefully-chosen small-scale experiment, combined with the derivative-derived scaling exponent $\gamma$, gives predictions for arbitrarily large models. The paper acknowledges a limitation: Approach 2 does not independently determine the optimal allocation of $N_{nv}$ and $H$ — it requires those to be set externally, either from scaling laws (Chinchilla) or from practical constraints. But for vocabulary size specifically, it provides a cheap, principled estimate.

**Connection to the FLOPs-minimization perspective (Figure 8 in Appendix A.1):** the paper provides an alternative visualization of why the derivative solution is the optimum. For fixed $N_{nv}$ and a target FLOPs budget, the number of training characters $H$ that can be processed varies with $V$ because tokens are "cheaper" (in FLOPs) when the vocabulary is smaller. Figure 8 (right) shows that at the derivative-optimal $V^*$, the model can process the **maximum** number of training characters for the given FLOPs budget. This is another way to understand the optimum: the vocabulary size that maximizes data throughput (in characters) for a fixed compute budget.

#### Approach 3: Parametric Fit of the Loss Function

Approach 3 is the most flexible method and the one used for the downstream validation experiments. It directly models the unigram-normalized loss as a function of all three variables, enabling prediction of the optimal vocabulary even when the allocation of $N_{nv}$ and $H$ is not globally compute-optimal.

**The vocabulary-dependent loss formula.** The paper adapts the classical risk decomposition used in Hoffmann et al. (2022):

$$\mathcal{L}_u = -E + \frac{A_1}{N_{nv}^{\alpha_1}} + \frac{A_2}{N_v^{\alpha_2}} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}$$

where $D = H \cdot f(V)$ is the number of training tokens, and $E, A_1, A_2, B, \alpha_1, \alpha_2, \beta$ are learned parameters.

**What each term represents:**

- **$-E$:** the irreducible loss — the unigram-normalized loss of an ideal generative process that perfectly models the data distribution given the tokenizer. $E > 0$ is the negative of this ideal loss (since $\mathcal{L}_u$ can be negative). This term is a constant floor that no amount of parameters or data can improve beyond.

- **$\frac{A_1}{N_{nv}^{\alpha_1}}$:** the contribution to loss from limited non-vocabulary parameters (Transformer capacity). As $N_{nv}$ increases, this term decreases toward zero (in the limit of infinite parameters, the model would perfectly use its capacity). The exponent $\alpha_1$ controls how quickly returns diminish — larger $\alpha_1$ means faster saturation.

- **$\frac{A_2}{N_v^{\alpha_2}}$:** the contribution to loss from limited vocabulary parameters. As $N_v$ increases (larger vocabulary), this term decreases, but with its own exponent $\alpha_2$ that may differ from $\alpha_1$. This captures the underfitting effect: too few vocabulary parameters force the model to share embedding capacity across many meanings, degrading performance.

- **$\frac{B}{D^\beta}$:** the contribution to loss from limited training data. As $D$ (number of tokens) increases, this term decreases. The exponent $\beta$ controls data efficiency. Note that $D = H \cdot f(V)$ depends on vocabulary size through $f(V)$, creating an interaction: larger $V$ reduces $D$ for fixed $H$, which *increases* this term, partially offsetting the benefit from the $\frac{A_2}{N_v^{\alpha_2}}$ term.

**Why this additive form?** It follows the standard scaling laws assumption that the effects of model capacity, vocabulary capacity, and data volume are approximately independent and additive in their contribution to the excess loss above the irreducible minimum. The power-law forms $1/N^\alpha$ and $1/D^\beta$ are empirically motivated by decades of scaling observations in machine learning (Hestness et al., 2017; Rosenfeld et al., 2019; Kaplan et al., 2020). The specific innovation here is the separation of vocabulary parameters from other parameters — in Hoffmann et al., there is a single $A/N^\alpha$ term for all parameters; here, it is split into $A_1/N_{nv}^{\alpha_1} + A_2/N_v^{\alpha_2}$.

**The equal-scaling constraint.** Following Muennighoff et al. (2024), the paper constrains $\alpha_1 = \beta$. This encodes the empirical finding that non-vocabulary parameters and training tokens should be scaled at the same rate for optimal performance. The vocabulary exponent $\alpha_2$ is unconstrained, consistent with the finding from Approaches 1 and 2 that vocabulary scales at a different rate.

**Fitting the loss formula.** The paper fits the parameters using the same experimental data collected for Approach 1 — all training runs with their $(N_{nv}, N_v, H, \mathcal{L}_u)$ tuples. Unlike Approach 1 which only uses the IsoFLOPs-optimal points, Approach 3 uses **all data points** because it needs to predict loss for any combination, not just optimal ones. The optimization minimizes Huber loss between predicted and observed $\mathcal{L}_u$:

$$\min_{a_1, a_2, b, e, \alpha_1, \alpha_2} \text{Huber}_\delta\left(-\exp(e) + \exp(a_1 - \alpha_1 \log N_{nv}) + \exp(a_2 - \alpha_2 \log N_v) + \exp(b - \beta \log(H \cdot f(V))), \mathcal{L}_u\right)$$

where $A_1 = \exp(a_1)$, $A_2 = \exp(a_2)$, $B = \exp(b)$, $E = \exp(e)$, and $\beta = \alpha_1$ (by constraint). The parameters are initialized from uniform grids: $a_1, a_2, b \in [0, 5]$, $e \in [0, 2]$, $\alpha_1, \alpha_2 \in [0.1, 1]$ (constrained away from zero because scaling exponents near zero would be physically implausible). The L-BFGS optimizer is used with 3 initial guesses per parameter.

**Why the exponential parameterization?** Using $\exp(\cdot)$ ensures that the amplitude parameters $A_1, A_2, B, E$ are strictly positive (since they represent contributions to loss, they cannot be negative). This is standard practice in fitting power-law models.

**Fitting results.** The paper reports the learned parameters as:

$$A_1 = 1.831, \quad A_2 = 0.196, \quad B = 2.124, \quad E = 5.533, \quad \alpha_1 = \beta = 0.447, \quad \alpha_2 = 0.671$$

**Interpreting the learned parameters:**

- **$\alpha_2 = 0.671 > \alpha_1 = 0.447$:** the vocabulary exponent is larger, meaning the loss decreases *faster* with additional vocabulary parameters than with additional non-vocabulary parameters, for increments from the current scale. However, this does not contradict the earlier finding that vocabulary should scale *slower* than non-vocabulary parameters ($\gamma < 1$). The exponent in the loss formula ($\alpha_2$) and the exponent in the compute-optimal scaling ($\gamma$) are different quantities. $\alpha_2$ describes how loss changes with $N_v$ at fixed compute; $\gamma$ describes how the optimal $N_v$ changes as compute increases. The relationship between them involves the FLOPs equation and the other terms in the loss.

- **$A_2 = 0.196 \ll A_1 = 1.831$:** the amplitude for vocabulary parameters is much smaller, meaning that vocabulary parameters have a smaller absolute effect on loss than non-vocabulary parameters at the same parameter count. This is intuitive: a Transformer block with $d \times 4d$ feed-forward parameters affects all tokens equally through the residual stream, while vocabulary parameters affect only the token representation.

- **$E = 5.533$:** the irreducible unigram-normalized loss is $-\exp(5.533) \approx -252.5$, meaning the ideal model achieves a substantially negative $\mathcal{L}_u$ (much better than the unigram baseline).

**Using Approach 3 for prediction.** After fitting the parameters, the optimal vocabulary size for a given scenario is found by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_u$ with respect to $V$, subject to the FLOPs constraint. The paper substitutes $D = C / [6(N_{nv} + Vd)]$ into the loss formula, making it a function of $V$ alone (given $N_{nv}$ and $C$):

$$\mathcal{L}_u = -E + \frac{A_1}{N_{nv}^{\alpha_1}} + \frac{A_2}{(Vd)^{\alpha_2}} + \frac{B}{\left[C / (6(N_{nv} + Vd))\right]^\beta}$$

The derivative $\partial \mathcal{L}_u / \partial V$ is computed (derived in Appendix A.2) and set to zero:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_u}{\partial V} = -\alpha_2 \frac{A_2 d}{(Vd)^{\alpha_2 + 1}} + \beta \frac{B d}{C} \cdot \frac{6(N_{nv} + Vd)^2}{\left[C / (6(N_{nv} + Vd))\right]^{\beta + 1}} = 0$$

This equation is solved numerically using `scipy.optimize.fsolve`. The result gives $V^{\text{opt}}$ for the given $(N_{nv}, C)$.

**Three usage scenarios for Approach 3:**

1. **Compute-optimal training (Chinchilla regime):** $N_{nv}$ and $H$ are equally scaled (the scenario assumed by Table 1). The FLOPs budget $C$ is determined by the optimal allocation from Approach 1, and Approach 3 predicts the vocabulary that minimizes loss at that allocation.

2. **Insufficient training data ("undertraining"):** a model with fixed $N_{nv}$ is trained on fewer characters than the Chinchilla optimum. For example, the 2.87B-parameter model in Table 3 (FLOPs budget $2.8 \times 10^{20}$) uses only 62.2B characters instead of the compute-optimal ~267B characters. Approach 3 predicts $V^{\text{opt}} = 24\text{K}$ for this undertrained scenario, smaller than the 35K predicted for optimal training — because when data is scarce, a smaller vocabulary avoids underfitting rare tokens.

3. **Excessive training data ("overtraining"):** a model with fixed $N_{nv}$ is trained on more characters than Chinchilla-optimal (common in practice, e.g., Llama2). In Table 3 (FLOPs budget $2.3 \times 10^{21}$), the model is trained on 509.1B characters — roughly double the compute-optimal amount. Approach 3 predicts $V^{\text{opt}} = 43\text{K}$, larger than the 35K for optimal training — because abundant data allows the model to learn representations for a larger vocabulary without underfitting.

**The key advantage of Approach 3 over Approaches 1 and 2:** it handles any combination of $(N_{nv}, H)$, not just compute-optimal ones. This makes it the most practically useful method, since real-world LLMs frequently deviate from Chinchilla-optimal allocations. The paper relies primarily on Approach 3 for the validation experiments in Section 5.

**Connecting the dots across approaches:** the convergence of all three approaches on $\gamma \approx 0.83$ — from entirely different mathematical formulations (empirical power-law fitting, derivative-based optimization, and parametric loss function) — is the strongest evidence the paper provides for the robustness of this scaling relationship.

#### Shared Experimental Infrastructure and Design Choices

**Dataset.** All training uses the SlimPajama dataset (Soboleva et al., 2023), a 627B-token cleaned and deduplicated version of RedPajama. The paper uniformly samples training data from different domains within SlimPajama to avoid domain bias. Models are evaluated on a held-out validation split from the same dataset.

**Model architecture.** All models use the Llama architecture (Touvron et al., 2023), with the only variation being the vocabulary size. The specific configurations are listed in Table 4 (Appendix A.7.1). For example, the $N_{nv} = 302\text{M}$ model has 18 layers, 16 attention heads, embedding dimension $d = 1024$, and intermediate FFN size 4096, with a sequence length of 2048. The $N_{nv} = 2.87\text{B}$ model has 24 layers, 32 heads, $d = 3200$, and intermediate size 8192.

**Vocabulary sizes.** The paper uses vocabulary sizes divisible by 128: $\{4096, 6144, 8192, 10240, 16384, 24576, 32768, 48128, 64512, 96256\}$. These are chosen for compatibility with NVIDIA tensor cores, which perform matrix multiplications most efficiently when dimensions are multiples of 128 (in practice, multiples of 64 or 128 depending on the GPU generation).

**Training hyperparameters.** Consistent across all runs: AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) with $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.95$, weight decay (not explicitly stated but standard for AdamW), maximum learning rate $4\times 10^{-4}$ decaying to $4\times 10^{-5}$ (10% of the maximum) following a cosine schedule, global batch size 512 sequences, bfloat16 mixed precision training. For models with $N_{nv} < 1.13\text{B}$, training uses a single node with 8 NVIDIA A100-40GB GPUs. For $N_{nv} = 2.87\text{B}$, the Megatron-LM framework is used for multi-node training with 64 GPUs, taking approximately 120 hours to train on 500B characters.

**Embedding dimension mapping.** The relationship between $N_{nv}$ and $d$ (Table 5) is predetermined, not optimized. This follows Kaplan et al.'s finding that the depth-to-width ratio has secondary importance compared to total non-vocabulary parameters. The mapping is monotonic and approximately follows $d \propto N_{nv}^{0.5}$ (square root scaling), which is consistent with standard architectural choices in the Llama family.

**Tokens-to-characters function $f(V)$.** As described above, it is fitted from 25 tokenizers with vocabulary sizes spanning 1K to 1024K. The quadratic form in $\log V$ achieves $R^2 = 0.99$ and RMSE $= 1.5 \times 10^{-6}$ on the BPE tokenizer. The paper verifies robustness by fitting on Unigram and Word-based tokenizers as well (Appendix A.9), with all achieving similarly strong fits.

**Fitting shared parameters.** Across all three approaches, the paper uses:
- L-BFGS optimizer (a quasi-Newton method well-suited for small-scale optimization problems with smooth objectives)
- Huber loss with $\delta = 0.001$ (provides robustness to outliers in the fitting data)
- Logarithmic transformations to avoid numerical underflow/overflow when fitting power laws (parameters and FLOPs span many orders of magnitude)
- Multiple random initializations and selection of the best fit to avoid local minima

**Data filtering.** Following Hoffmann et al. (2022), the paper filters out data points with very small FLOPs from the fitting process for Approach 3. The rationale is that at extremely small compute budgets, the models are so far from convergence that the power-law assumptions break down — the loss is dominated by transient training dynamics rather than the capacity-limited regime that the loss formula models. The specific threshold is not stated, but this is standard practice in scaling laws research.

**Interpolation for IsoFLOPs comparison.** A crucial practical detail: because models with different vocabulary sizes have different FLOPs-per-training-step (due to $N_v = Vd$ and $D = Hf(V)$ affecting the $6ND$ calculation), they naturally produce training trajectories with different x-axis values. To compare them at identical FLOPs budgets, the paper interpolates the observed (FLOPs, loss) points for each model to estimate loss at target budgets. The interpolation method is not specified in detail, but given the smooth, near-power-law relationship between FLOPs and loss, linear interpolation in log-log space is standard and effective.

**Downstream evaluation.** For the validation experiments (Section 5, Tables 2 and 3), models are evaluated on 7 downstream tasks: ARC-Challenge and ARC-Easy (Clark et al., 2018), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), and BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019). The paper uses the `lighteval` library for zero-shot evaluation. For multiple-choice tasks, the predicted likelihoods are normalized by the length of each choice to eliminate the effect of text length on predictions (a standard technique to prevent the model from favoring shorter completions).

**The five difficulty bins from the example are not present in this paper.** The example in the prompt discussed difficulty-conditioned test-time compute allocation; this paper has no such concept. Instead, the paper's "binning" is the vocabulary size values themselves (4K, 6K, 8K, ..., 96K) which are explicitly chosen experimental conditions, not estimated properties of prompts.

---

#### Summary of Why These Three Approaches Work Together

The three approaches form a **complementary validation framework**:

- **Approach 1 (IsoFLOPs)** answers the question: "What does the empirical data say?" It is the most direct — you train models, observe which vocabulary works best at each budget, and fit a law. Its limitation is that it can only predict the global optimum, not local optima for arbitrary $(N_{nv}, H)$.

- **Approach 2 (Derivative)** answers the question: "What does the FLOPs math say?" It is the cheapest — you compute a derivative, solve for zero, and get a prediction without training any additional models. Its limitation is that it assumes the model is on the compute-optimal frontier and requires external scaling laws for $N_{nv}$ and $H$.

- **Approach 3 (Parametric loss)** answers the question: "What does a full model of the loss landscape say?" It is the most flexible — it predicts the locally optimal vocabulary for any $(N_{nv}, H, C)$ combination, including non-optimal ones. Its limitation is that it requires fitting 7 parameters from training data.

The fact that all three approaches converge on $\gamma \approx 0.83$ — the exponent relating vocabulary parameters to non-vocabulary parameters in the compute-optimal scaling — provides strong triangulation. If one approach had given a substantially different answer, it would suggest a flaw in its assumptions. The agreement across approaches makes the $\gamma < 1$ finding (vocabulary should scale slower than Transformer parameters) one of the most robust conclusions in the paper.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Establishing Vocabulary Size as a First-Class Variable in Scaling Laws

The paper's most fundamental contribution is not a specific prediction but the **reframing of vocabulary size from a fixed preprocessing choice to a compute-dependent variable that participates in scaling laws alongside model parameters and training data**. This is a conceptual shift, not merely a new hyperparameter tuning recommendation.

**What the field did before:** Prior scaling laws — from Kaplan et al. (2020) through Hoffmann et al. (2022, Chinchilla) to Muennighoff et al. (2024) — treated vocabulary as a constant external to the optimization. The standard formulation $(N^{\text{opt}}, D^{\text{opt}}) = \arg\min \mathcal{L}(N, D)$ subject to $\text{FLOPs}(N, D) = C$ implicitly assumes $V$ is fixed. Kaplan et al. made this explicit by excluding vocabulary parameters from their predictive formulas entirely. The result was a scaling laws literature that could prescribe parameter counts and token budgets with reasonable precision but was silent on whether 32K or 256K was the right vocabulary — a 7× range of variation with no principled resolution.

**What this paper changes:** It demonstrates that vocabulary size participates in the compute-optimal allocation problem on equal footing with model size and data volume, and that ignoring it leaves substantial performance on the table. The evidence is concrete: switching from $V = 32\text{K}$ to the predicted optimal $V = 43\text{K}$ at the same 2.3×10²¹ FLOPs budget improves ARC-Challenge from 29.1% to 32.0% (Table 3, overtraining scenario). This ~3 percentage point gain on a challenging reasoning benchmark comes from changing *only* vocabulary size — no additional parameters, no additional data, no architectural changes — which makes it a clean demonstration that vocabulary is not a second-order effect.

**Why this is a fundamental reframing, not an incremental refinement:** The paper's reformulation requires rethinking three measurement conventions that the field has taken for granted: (1) total parameters are split into $N_{nv}$ and $N_v$ because vocabulary parameters scale differently from Transformer parameters, (2) training data is measured in characters ($H$) rather than tokens ($D$) because token count depends on the vocabulary being optimized, and (3) loss must be normalized ($\mathcal{L}_u$) because raw cross-entropy is not comparable across vocabulary sizes. These are not minor adjustments — they constitute a new measurement framework for scaling analyses. The fact that the paper had to develop all three before it could even *ask* the compute-optimal vocabulary question is evidence that the prior framework was fundamentally incomplete rather than merely imprecise.

**The diagnostic value:** The paper's framework explains a pre-existing empirical puzzle that the field had not articulated as a puzzle: why do Llama2-7B and Gemma-7B have vocabularies of 32K and 256K respectively, despite similar total parameter counts? The prior paradigm had no way to evaluate which choice was better — both were defensible as arbitrary engineering decisions. The new paradigm provides a principled answer: Llama2-7B is substantially below optimal (predicted $V^{\text{opt}} \approx 62\text{K}$ from Table 1, Approach 1), and Gemma-7B may be overshooting depending on its training data volume. Neither model was making an informed choice; both were operating in a regime where vocabulary selection lacked theoretical grounding. The framework thus serves a diagnostic function — it can identify suboptimal vocabulary allocations in existing models, not just prescribe them for future ones.

---

### Innovation 2: The Empirical Finding That Vocabulary Parameters Scale Slower Than Non-Vocabulary Parameters ($\gamma < 1$)

The paper's central quantitative finding — that optimal vocabulary parameters follow $N_v^{\text{opt}} \propto N_{nv}^\gamma$ with $\gamma \approx 0.83 < 1$ — is significant not just as a number but as a **resolution to an unarticulated tension in language model design**. The field had two competing intuitions (larger vocabularies capture more linguistic diversity vs. larger vocabularies cause underfitting of rare tokens) and no framework for determining which dominates at what scale. The $\gamma < 1$ finding provides a quantitative resolution: vocabulary should grow with model size, but more slowly than Transformer capacity.

**What the field implicitly assumed:** Without a scaling law, practitioners defaulted to one of two heuristics. Either vocabulary size was treated as scale-invariant (Llama1-7B, Llama2-7B, and Llama2-70B all used 32K, regardless of the 10× parameter increase), or it was scaled proportionally with model size following a "bigger model, bigger vocabulary" intuition without a specific exponent. Both approaches are wrong according to this paper: the first ignores the benefit of vocabulary growth entirely, while the second over-allocates parameters to vocabulary at the expense of Transformer capacity.

**Why $\gamma < 1$ is non-obvious:** The FLOPs equation $C \approx 6(N_{nv} + Vd)Hf(V)$ makes it mathematically possible that the optimal $\gamma$ could be greater than 1 (vocabulary growing faster than Transformer parameters), equal to 1 (proportional scaling), or less than 1 (vocabulary growing slower). The fact that it is less than 1 — confirmed by all three approaches from different mathematical starting points — is an empirical finding, not a theoretical necessity. The paper's interpretation (Section 4.1) that "once a sufficiently rich embedding space is present via a large vocabulary, it is more critical to scale non-vocabulary parameters to learn the intricate syntactic and semantic structures of language" provides a post-hoc rationale, but the finding itself is a data-driven discovery that could have come out differently.

**The triangulation across approaches makes this finding unusually robust.** Approach 1 (empirical IsoFLOPs fitting) gives $\gamma = 0.84$ from training hundreds of models. Approach 2 (derivative-based FLOPs minimization) gives $\gamma = 0.83$ from solving $\partial C / \partial V = 0$ with no model training beyond the initial $f(V)$ calibration. Approach 3 (parametric loss function) gives an exponent $\alpha_2 = 0.671$ in the loss formula, which — when combined with the FLOPs constraint and the other terms — implies the same $\gamma < 1$ relationship. These are fundamentally different estimation procedures that share no fitting data beyond the underlying training runs (Approach 2 doesn't even use those). Their convergence on the same qualitative conclusion (vocabulary scales slower) and nearly identical quantitative exponent (0.83–0.84) is strong evidence that this is a real property of the compute-optimal frontier, not an artifact of experimental design or fitting methodology.

**How this changes design reasoning:** Before this paper, choosing vocabulary size for a new model scale meant either copying precedent (32K because Llama used it) or running expensive ablation studies at the target scale. The $\gamma \approx 0.83$ finding provides a scaling rule: measure the optimal vocabulary at a small, cheap scale (e.g., $N_{nv} = 33\text{M}$), then extrapolate to any larger scale using $N_v^{\text{opt}} = N_v^0 \cdot (N_{nv} / N_{nv}^0)^{0.83}$. This converts vocabulary selection from an empirical guessing game to a principled prediction, which is exactly what scaling laws are meant to do.

---

### Innovation 3: Handling Non-Optimal Training Regimes Through the Parametric Loss Function

Approach 3's ability to predict the locally optimal vocabulary when training data and model parameters are **not** optimally allocated — the overtraining and undertraining scenarios — addresses a gap that makes prior scaling laws results practically inapplicable to most real-world LLMs.

**Why this matters beyond the compute-optimal case:** The Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) prescribe equal scaling of parameters and data. But since Chinchilla's publication, the dominant trend in LLM development has been deliberate deviation from this prescription: models are systematically overtrained on far more data than compute-optimal to reduce inference costs (a smaller model trained longer is cheaper to serve than a larger model trained optimally). The paper's Table 3 quantifies this: a 2.87B-parameter model trained on ~500B characters (overtraining) uses roughly double the compute-optimal data for its size. Approaches 1 and 2, which assume the model sits on the global compute-optimal frontier, cannot answer "what vocabulary should this overtrained model use?" because the overtrained model is, by definition, not on that frontier.

**What Approach 3 provides that Approaches 1 and 2 cannot:** By fitting a full loss function $\mathcal{L}_u(N_{nv}, N_v, H)$ rather than just the optimal frontier, Approach 3 can predict the vocabulary that minimizes loss for *any* fixed $(N_{nv}, H)$ pair, not just Chinchilla-optimal ones. The paper uses this capability to generate three distinct predictions for the same $N_{nv} = 2.87\text{B}$ model (Table 3):
- Undertraining (62.2B characters): $V^{\text{opt}} = 24\text{K}$ — smaller than the conventional 32K, because scarce data cannot adequately train a large vocabulary's rare tokens
- Compute-optimal training (268.2B characters): $V^{\text{opt}} = 35\text{K}$ — modestly larger than 32K
- Overtraining (509.1B characters): $V^{\text{opt}} = 43\text{K}$ — substantially larger than 32K, because abundant data allows a larger vocabulary to be well-trained

The non-monotonic relationship between data volume and optimal vocabulary size — smaller vocabulary when data is scarce, larger when data is abundant — is not obvious a priori and would be missed entirely by a compute-optimal-only analysis.

**This is a conceptual advance in the scaling laws framework, not just a new prediction.** Prior scaling laws partitioned the world into "compute-optimal" (where the laws apply) and "non-optimal" (where they are silent). Approach 3 erodes that boundary by providing a continuously applicable prediction that gracefully handles any distance from optimality. This matters because the overtraining trend is not a temporary deviation — it is driven by the fundamental economic asymmetry between training cost (paid once) and inference cost (paid per query), and is likely to persist. A scaling laws framework that cannot handle overtrained models is a framework that cannot analyze most of the models actually being deployed.

**The empirical validation is particularly strong here because the baseline is a real-world standard, not a strawman.** The paper does not compare against arbitrarily bad vocabulary choices — it compares against $V = 32\text{K}$, which is exactly the vocabulary size used by Llama2, Llama1, and many other models. The fact that Approach 3's predictions consistently beat this standard across three different training regimes (Table 3: improvements on ARC-Challenge of +1.0, +0.6, and +2.9 percentage points for compute-optimal, undertrained, and overtrained, respectively) demonstrates that the framework provides actionable guidance for the training regimes practitioners actually use.

---

### Innovation 4: The Tokenization-Agnostic Measurement Framework ($\mathcal{L}_u$, Character-Based Data, and $f(V)$)

While each component of the measurement framework — the unigram-normalized loss, character-based data accounting, and the tokens-characters mapping function — has precedent in isolation, their **combination into a coherent system for studying vocabulary scaling** is an intellectual contribution that enables the paper's other findings. Without this framework, the central research question ("what is the compute-optimal vocabulary size?") cannot be empirically investigated.

**Why each component was individually necessary:**

- **Unigram-normalized loss ($\mathcal{L}_u$):** Without vocabulary-normalized evaluation, models with larger vocabularies appear worse in raw loss even when they are better models (Appendix A.10, Figure 13 shows a *positive* correlation between raw loss and downstream performance). Any optimization over vocabulary size that uses raw cross-entropy as the objective will systematically favor smaller vocabularies, regardless of downstream quality. The alternative, bits-per-character (BPC), is vocabulary-insensitive but discards token-level information. $\mathcal{L}_u$ provides a principled normalization: it measures how much the context-aware model improves over a unigram baseline, which naturally adjusts for vocabulary size because both numerator and denominator share the same $V$-dependent tokenization.

- **Character-based data measurement ($H$):** The number of training tokens $D$ is an output of the vocabulary choice, not an input. A tokenizer with $V = 256\text{K}$ produces fewer tokens from the same text than one with $V = 32\text{K}$, so "models were both trained on D tokens" means they were trained on different amounts of raw text. Prior scaling laws avoided this circularity by holding $V$ fixed, which made $D$ a valid independent variable. Once $V$ becomes a variable, $D$ must be replaced by a $V$-independent measure of data volume, hence training characters $H$.

- **Tokens-characters mapping ($f(V)$):** This function closes the loop, enabling FLOPs computation from $H$ and $V$ via $D = H \cdot f(V)$. The quadratic-in-log-$V$ form is empirically motivated (fitted from 25 tokenizers, $R^2 = 0.99$) rather than theoretically derived, but its robustness across tokenizer types (BPE, Unigram, Word-based; Appendix A.9) makes it a practical tool rather than a domain-specific hack.

**The intellectual value is in the synthesis, not the parts.** Roh et al. (2020) proposed the unigram-normalized metric. Data compression researchers have used character-based metrics for decades. Tokenization fertility curves are studied in the tokenization literature. But prior to this paper, no one had assembled these into a system that makes vocabulary size optimizable within scaling laws. The framework is what lets the paper treat vocabulary like any other model hyperparameter — something you can sweep, evaluate fairly, and optimize — rather than a fixed property of the preprocessing pipeline.

**A subtle secondary contribution: the framework explains why prior vocabulary work remained empirical rather than theoretical.** The multilingual vocabulary expansion literature (Wang et al., 2019; Chung et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2023) and code-generation vocabulary studies (Dagan et al., 2024) all produced useful recommendations, but none could derive a scaling law because they lacked a vocabulary-insensitive loss. Without a fair metric, you can say "larger vocabulary helps for this specific language" but not "vocabulary should grow with exponent 0.83 as compute increases." The paper's framework retroactively explains why prior work was confined to empirical heuristics: the measurement infrastructure for scaling-law-style analysis simply didn't exist.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the **SlimPajama dataset** (Soboleva et al., 2023), a 627B-token cleaned and deduplicated version of RedPajama. Training data is uniformly sampled from different domains within SlimPajama. Evaluation is performed on a held-out validation split from the same dataset for the unigram-normalized loss $\mathcal{L}_u$ measurements that drive the scaling law fitting. For downstream validation experiments (Section 5), models are evaluated on 7 standard benchmarks: ARC-Challenge and ARC-Easy (Clark et al., 2018), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), and BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), using the `lighteval` library in a zero-shot setting.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use **dense Transformer models with the Llama architecture** (Touvron et al., 2023), trained from scratch. The non-vocabulary parameter counts span $N_{nv} \in \{33\text{M}, 85\text{M}, 151\text{M}, 302\text{M}, 631\text{M}, 1130\text{M}, 2870\text{M}\}$, with embedding dimensions $d$ mapped from $N_{nv}$ according to a predetermined schedule (Table 5, Appendix A.7.2) ranging from $d = 512$ for the smallest models to $d = 3200$ for the 2.87B model. The paper argues this model family is representative of contemporary LLM design and spans a sufficiently large range (two orders of magnitude in parameters) to fit reliable scaling laws.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout the scaling analysis is the **unigram-normalized language modeling loss** $\mathcal{L}_u = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^T \log\frac{p(w_i|w_{1:i-1}, V)}{p(w_i|V)}$, where $p(w_i|V)$ is the unigram frequency of token $w_i$ in the training corpus. This metric is vocabulary-insensitive — it measures how much better the context-aware model predicts each token compared to a context-free unigram baseline, enabling fair comparison across models with different vocabulary sizes $V$. The paper validates this choice by showing a strong negative correlation between $\mathcal{L}_u$ and downstream accuracy ($\rho$ from Appendix A.10) and a strong positive correlation between $\mathcal{L}_u$ and bits-per-character (Pearson $\rho = 0.9888$, Appendix A.5). For downstream evaluation, the metric is **zero-shot accuracy** on each benchmark, normalized for multiple-choice tasks by dividing predicted likelihoods by the length of each choice to eliminate length bias.

- **Baselines.** The paper's primary baseline in validation experiments is the **conventional vocabulary size of $V = 32\text{K}$**, which is the vocabulary size used by Llama2, Llama1, and numerous other models. This is not a weak or arbitrary baseline — it represents the de facto standard in the field. Within the scaling analysis itself, the paper compares across vocabulary sizes from the set $\{4\text{K}, 6\text{K}, 8\text{K}, 10\text{K}, 16\text{K}, 24\text{K}, 32\text{K}, 48\text{K}, 64\text{K}, 96\text{K}\}$ (all divisible by 128 for GPU efficiency), with the compute-optimal allocation determined relative to all tested sizes rather than against a fixed baseline. The paper does not compare against other vocabulary selection heuristics from prior work (e.g., the multilingual expansion strategies of Wang et al., 2019 or Chung et al., 2020).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute is measured in **total training FLOPs**, computed as $C \approx 6 N D$ following Kaplan et al. (2020), where $N = N_{nv} + Vd$ and $D = H \cdot f(V)$. The FLOPs calculation accounts for the full training cost of all parameters (non-vocabulary plus vocabulary, with the output layer contributing the dominant FLOPs from vocabulary parameters; the embedding lookup is treated as negligible following the standard approximation). All models within a group are trained on the same number of training characters $H$, but the number of tokens $D$ varies with vocabulary size via $f(V)$, meaning models with different vocabularies naturally accumulate FLOPs at different rates even with identical $H$. For fair comparison at identical FLOPs budgets, the paper interpolates between observed (FLOPs, $\mathcal{L}_u$) points for each model to estimate loss at target budgets.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For the scaling law fitting, the paper selects IsoFLOPs-optimal points (the combination of $(N_{nv}, N_v, H)$ achieving the lowest $\mathcal{L}_u$ for each FLOPs budget) from the full set of training runs and fits power laws to these points. To avoid overfitting, the fitting procedures use multiple random initializations (20 initial guesses for Approaches 1 and 2; 3 initial guesses for Approach 3, which has more parameters) and select the best fit. For the downstream validation in Tables 2 and 3, only a single model per configuration is trained (no repeated runs with different random seeds), which the paper acknowledges is due to computational constraints — training multiple models across all combinations of $N_{nv}$, $V$, and $H$ with replication "would be prohibitively expensive" (Appendix, NeurIPS Checklist item 7). The paper reports standard deviations for downstream task performance within each evaluation (the variance across different tasks and evaluation prompts, as reported in Tables 2 and 3), but no confidence intervals or statistical significance tests comparing the $V = 32\text{K}$ baseline against $V^{\text{opt}}$ are provided. The paper does not use cross-validation in the sense of train/validation/test splits for the scaling law fitting — all data points come from the same training distribution and are used directly for fitting.

---

### Main Quantitative Results

#### IsoFLOPs Power-Law Fitting Results (Approach 1)

The core empirical finding from Approach 1 is that all three attributes — non-vocabulary parameters $N_{nv}$, vocabulary parameters $N_v$, and training characters $H$ — follow power-law relationships with the FLOPs budget $C$ at the compute-optimal frontier. The fitted relationships, displayed in Figure 5, are:

$$N_{nv} = 0.08 \cdot C^{0.50}$$
$$N_v = 0.20 \cdot C^{0.42}$$
$$H = 6.42 \cdot C^{0.50}$$

The paper reports that these fits achieve low RMSE and high $R^2$ values (specific numerical values for the goodness-of-fit metrics are shown in Figure 5 but not stated in the text; the figure itself displays the fitted curves with data points closely tracking the power laws). The constraint $\alpha_1 = \alpha_3$ (non-vocabulary parameters and training characters scale with the same exponent) is enforced based on the Chinchilla equal-scaling finding (Hoffmann et al., 2022), while the vocabulary exponent $\alpha_2$ is left unconstrained and emerges from the fit as $0.42$.

**The key numerical relationship:** the vocabulary exponent $\alpha_2 = 0.42$ is smaller than the non-vocabulary exponent $\alpha_1 = 0.50$, yielding a scaling proportion $\gamma = \alpha_2 / \alpha_1 = 0.42 / 0.50 = 0.84 < 1$. This means that as the FLOPs budget increases, the optimal vocabulary grows slower than the optimal non-vocabulary parameter count and training data volume. In concrete terms: doubling the FLOPs budget increases $N_{nv}$ and $H$ by a factor of $2^{0.50} \approx 1.41\times$ each, but increases $N_v$ by only $2^{0.42} \approx 1.34\times$. Over larger scales, this divergence widens: a $10\times$ increase in $N_{nv}$ corresponds to approximately a $10^{0.84} \approx 6.9\times$ increase in $N_v$.

**The data quantity finding:** the coefficient for training characters ($k_3 = 6.42$) is substantially larger than the coefficients for parameters ($k_1 = 0.08$, $k_2 = 0.20$), which the paper interprets as evidence that LLMs are "data-hungry" — practitioners should allocate more compute to training data relative to model parameters, consistent with prior findings (Xue et al., 2024; Muennighoff et al., 2024).

**Interpolation detail:** to obtain enough data points for fitting at various FLOPs budgets, the paper performs interpolation of $(N_{nv}, N_v, H)$ triplets in logarithmic space and predicts the validation loss based on real data points, then computes the required FLOPs for each interpolated point using Equation 5. This is a standard technique in scaling laws work to cheaply increase the density of observations without training additional models. The specific interpolation method (likely linear interpolation in log-log space given the power-law relationships) is not detailed.

**How these results are used:** given a target FLOPs budget $C$, one can compute the compute-optimal allocation: $N_{nv}^{\text{opt}}$ and $H^{\text{opt}}$ from their respective power laws, then $N_v^{\text{opt}}$ and $V^{\text{opt}} = N_v^{\text{opt}} / d$ where $d$ is the embedding dimension appropriate for $N_{nv}^{\text{opt}}$ (from Table 5). For example, at $N_{nv} = 70\text{B}$ (the scale of Llama2-70B), Approach 1 predicts $N_v^{\text{opt}} = 1.7\text{B}$ and $V^{\text{opt}} = 212\text{K}$ (using $d = 8192$), as reported in Table 1.

---

#### Derivative-Based Estimation Results (Approach 2)

Approach 2 provides a computationally cheap alternative that converges to nearly the same scaling exponent as Approach 1. By solving $\partial C / \partial V = 0$ for multiple values of $N_{nv}$ and fitting the resulting $(N_{nv}, N_v^{\text{opt}})$ pairs, the paper obtains:

$$N_v^{\text{opt}} \propto N_{nv}^\gamma \quad \text{with} \quad \gamma = 0.83$$

**The calibration-based prediction procedure:** rather than requiring training runs at scale, Approach 2 can be used by (1) empirically finding the optimal vocabulary $V^0$ for a small reference model $N_{nv}^0$ via a sweep, then (2) scaling to any target $N_{nv}$ using $N_v^{\text{opt}} = N_v^0 \cdot \left(N_{nv} / N_{nv}^0\right)^{0.83}$. The paper does not report an explicit calibration experiment — it relies on the derivative equation itself to generate the $N_v^{\text{opt}}$ values for fitting $\gamma$, assuming that the FLOPs-minimizing vocabulary from the derivative coincides with the loss-minimizing vocabulary at the compute-optimal frontier.

**Connection to data throughput:** Appendix A.1, Figure 8 provides an alternative perspective on why the derivative solution is optimal. For a fixed FLOPs budget, the vocabulary size that solves $\partial C / \partial V = 0$ is exactly the vocabulary size that allows the model to process the **maximum number of training characters** — larger vocabularies increase per-token compute, while smaller vocabularies require more tokens per character, and the optimum balances these forces to maximize raw data throughput.

**Predictions in Table 1:** for the same target non-vocabulary parameter counts as Approach 1, Approach 2 predicts slightly larger vocabulary sizes — e.g., $V^{\text{opt}} = 231\text{K}$ for $N_{nv} = 70\text{B}$ vs. Approach 1's $212\text{K}$. The differences are modest and do not affect the qualitative conclusion that current models substantially undershoot the optimal vocabulary.

---

#### Parametric Loss Function Fit Results (Approach 3)

The parametric loss function $\mathcal{L}_u = -E + \frac{A_1}{N_{nv}^{\alpha_1}} + \frac{A_2}{N_v^{\alpha_2}} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}$ is fitted to all experimental data points (not just the IsoFLOPs-optimal ones), yielding the parameters:

$$A_1 = 1.831, \quad A_2 = 0.196, \quad B = 2.124, \quad E = 5.533, \quad \alpha_1 = \beta = 0.447, \quad \alpha_2 = 0.671$$

**Interpreting the relative magnitudes:** $A_2 = 0.196$ is roughly an order of magnitude smaller than $A_1 = 1.831$ and $B = 2.124$, indicating that vocabulary parameters have a smaller absolute effect on the loss than non-vocabulary parameters or training data volume at the scales studied. This does not mean vocabulary is unimportant — the prediction that increasing vocabulary from 32K to 43K improves ARC-Challenge by ~3 points (Table 3) demonstrates practical significance — but rather that the loss function is less sensitive to vocabulary parameters than to the other two factors, consistent with the $\gamma < 1$ finding from the other approaches.

**The exponent relationship:** $\alpha_2 = 0.671 > \alpha_1 = 0.447$ means that, **for a fixed compute budget**, the loss decreases faster with additional vocabulary parameters than with additional non-vocabulary parameters when starting from current (suboptimal) allocations. This is not a contradiction with $\gamma < 1$ — $\alpha_2$ describes the local slope of the loss with respect to $N_v$ at a fixed operating point, while $\gamma$ describes how the optimal $N_v$ shifts as the entire budget scales. The reparameterization from $(N_v)$ to the FLOPs-constrained optimum involves all terms in the loss function simultaneously.

**Predictions in Table 1:** at the compute-optimal allocation, Approach 3 predicts slightly more conservative vocabulary sizes than Approaches 1 and 2 — e.g., $V^{\text{opt}} = 218\text{K}$ for $N_{nv} = 70\text{B}$, compared to 212K (App 1) and 231K (App 2). The agreement across all three approaches is within ~10% for most model scales, which is remarkable given their different mathematical foundations.

---

The paper trains two $N_{nv} = 2.87\text{B}$ models under the compute-optimal FLOPs budget of $1.2 \times 10^{21}$ (where training data is equally scaled with non-vocabulary parameters). One model uses the standard $V = 32\text{K}$; the other uses $V^{\text{opt}} = 35\text{K}$ as predicted by Approach 3. The results across 7 downstream benchmarks (Table 2):

|------|-------------------|-------------------------------|------------|
| ARC-C | 28.5 ± 1.3 | 29.1 ± 1.3 | +0.6 |
| ARC-E | 49.2 ± 1.0 | 50.6 ± 1.0 | +1.4 |
| HellaSwag | 47.5 ± 0.5 | 48.1 ± 0.5 | +0.6 |
| OBQA | 31.6 ± 2.1 | 31.6 ± 2.1 | 0.0 |
| WG | 50.4 ± 1.4 | 51.9 ± 1.4 | +1.5 |

The model with the predicted optimal vocabulary outperforms the baseline on 5 out of 7 tasks, ties on 2 (OBQA, PIQA), and loses on none. The average improvement of 0.6 percentage points is modest but consistent, and at the same compute budget — this is a pure efficiency gain with no additional training cost. Note that the improvement is small in absolute terms because the predicted $V^{\text{opt}} = 35\text{K}$ is very close to the baseline $V = 32\text{K}$ in the compute-optimal training regime; the gains are predicted to be larger in the overtraining regime (see below).

#### Downstream Validation: Undertraining and Overtraining Regimes (Table 3)

This is the most practically important experiment because it tests Approach 3 in the training regimes that real-world LLMs actually use. The same $N_{nv} = 2.87\text{B}$ model is tested under two non-optimal data budgets:

**Undertraining (FLOPs budget $2.8 \times 10^{20}$, 62.2B characters):**

The predicted optimal vocabulary shifts **downward** to $V^{\text{opt}} = 24\text{K}$, smaller than the conventional 32K, because scarce training data cannot adequately train a larger vocabulary's rare token embeddings. Results (Table 3, top section):

| Task | $V = 32\text{K}$ | $V^{\text{opt}} = 24\text{K}$ | Difference |
|------|-------------------|-------------------------------|------------|
| Average | 43.2 | 43.9 | +0.7 |

The model with $V^{\text{opt}} = 24\text{K}$ outperforms on 6 out of 7 tasks (the paper reports per-task numbers in Table 3, showing consistent small improvements, e.g., ARC-C: 23.6 → 24.2, ARC-E: 40.8 → 42.2, HellaSwag: 34.4 → 36.0). The average improvement of 0.7 points confirms Approach 3's prediction that when data is insufficient, shrinking the vocabulary is beneficial — a non-obvious result since the standard intuition is often "larger vocabulary = better."

**Overtraining (FLOPs budget $2.3 \times 10^{21}$, 509.1B characters):**

The predicted optimal vocabulary shifts **upward** to $V^{\text{opt}} = 43\text{K}$, substantially larger than the conventional 32K, because abundant training data allows a larger vocabulary's rare tokens to receive sufficient gradient updates. This is the setting most relevant to current LLM training practice (e.g., Llama2, Llama3). Results (Table 3, bottom section):

| Task | $V = 32\text{K}$ | $V^{\text{opt}} = 43\text{K}$ | Difference |
|------|-------------------|-------------------------------|------------|
| ARC-C | 29.1 ± 1.3 | 32.0 ± 1.4 | +2.9 |
| ARC-E | 53.5 ± 1.0 | 54.7 ± 1.0 | +1.2 |
| HellaSwag | 53.0 ± 0.5 | 54.1 ± 0.5 | +1.1 |
| OBQA | 33.0 ± 2.1 | 33.0 ± 2.1 | 0.0 |
| WG | 52.0 ± 1.4 | 52.8 ± 1.4 | +0.8 |
| PIQA | 72.0 ± 1.1 | 72.6 ± 1.0 | +0.6 |
| BoolQ | 59.5 ± 0.9 | 61.9 ± 0.9 | +2.4 |

This is the paper's headline validation result. The model with $V^{\text{opt}} = 43\text{K}$ outperforms the $V = 32\text{K}$ baseline on 6 out of 7 tasks, with the largest gains on ARC-Challenge (+2.9 points, a ~10% relative improvement) and BoolQ (+2.4 points). The average improvement of 1.3 percentage points across 7 diverse benchmarks at identical compute cost is a substantively meaningful gain — it suggests that vocabulary optimization at the scale of current LLM training runs could yield non-trivial downstream benefits.

**The trend across regimes:** the optimal vocabulary shifts from 24K (undertraining) → 35K (compute-optimal) → 43K (overtraining) as training data increases, confirming that vocabulary size and data volume are coupled decisions. The fact that Approach 3 correctly predicts the direction and approximate magnitude of this shift is the strongest empirical evidence for its practical utility.

#### Vocabulary Size vs. Training Data Heatmap (Figure 7)

Figure 7 provides a more granular view of how the optimal vocabulary shifts with data volume for a fixed $N_{nv} = 302\text{M}$ model. The heatmap (left panel) shows the unigram-normalized loss for vocabulary sizes in $\{8\text{K}, 10\text{K}, 16\text{K}, 24\text{K}, 32\text{K}, 48\text{K}\}$ across different FLOPs budgets (which vary with training data volume). The black line with markers traces the best vocabulary size at each budget.

**Key observations:**
- At low data volumes (low FLOPs budgets), the optimal vocabulary is smaller: $V^{\text{opt}}$ shifts from 16K → 10K as data decreases, demonstrating that reducing vocabulary is a mechanism to prevent overfitting when data is scarce.
- At high data volumes (high FLOPs budgets), the optimal vocabulary increases: $V^{\text{opt}}$ shifts from 16K → 24K as data increases, demonstrating that abundant data enables effective utilization of a larger vocabulary.
- The right panel of Figure 7 clarifies a subtle point: models with larger vocabularies are trained on fewer tokens at the same FLOPs budget (because larger $V$ increases per-token FLOPs). To keep FLOPs consistent, larger-vocabulary models process fewer total tokens for the same character budget, which creates a tradeoff — the heatmap accounts for this automatically since the FLOPs budget is the x-axis.

This experiment uses only $N_{nv} = 302\text{M}$ and is thus limited in scale, but the qualitative pattern — optimal vocabulary increases with data availability — is consistent with the larger-scale validation in Tables 2 and 3.

#### Predictions for Existing LLMs (Figures 2 and 6, Table 1)

While not an "experiment" in the traditional sense, the paper's application of Approach 3 to predict optimal vocabulary sizes for existing LLMs serves as a form of external validation. Figure 2 (assuming compute-optimal training data allocation) and Figure 6 (using reported training data volumes, which are typically overtrained relative to Chinchilla) compare current vocabulary parameters against predicted optimal values.

**Under the compute-optimal assumption (Figure 2, Table 1):**
- Llama2-70B ($N_{nv} \approx 70\text{B}$) is predicted to need $N_v^{\text{opt}} \approx 1.7\text{B}$–$1.9\text{B}$, corresponding to $V^{\text{opt}} \approx 212\text{K}$–$231\text{K}$ depending on the approach. Its actual vocabulary is 32K, with $N_v = 70\text{B} \times 32\text{K} \times 8192$ (embedding dim) $\ll$ predicted. The paper states this is "7 times larger than its vocabulary of 32K" — this is a 7× factor in vocabulary *size*, not parameters.
- Llama3-8B uses 128K, which is closer to the predicted $\sim$60K–67K (Table 1) but still below, while Llama3-70B at 128K is substantially below the predicted $\sim$216K.
- The models closest to optimal vocabulary allocation are StarCoder2-3B, OLMo-7B, InternLM2-20B, and Gemma2-27B (Figure 2 caption).

**Under reported training data volumes (Figure 6), which accounts for overtraining:**
- The predicted optimal vocabulary parameters increase further because overtrained models have more data to support larger vocabularies.
- With the exception of Gemma2-9B, **all listed models allocate fewer vocabulary parameters than Approach 3 predicts** for their actual training data volume. The specific shortfalls are listed in Figure 6: e.g., Llama2-7B currently allocates 163.84M vocabulary parameters vs. 525.34M predicted optimal; Llama2-13B allocates 131.07M vs. 786.43M predicted; Nemotron4-340B allocates 1.05B vs. 7.13B predicted.

**Llama3-specific predictions (Appendix A.11, Figure 14):** the paper extends its analysis to Llama3, which increased vocabulary from 32K to 128K — a significant step in the right direction. However, the paper predicts that Llama3-70B ($V^{\text{opt}} \approx 278\text{K}$) and the 400B variant ($V^{\text{opt}} \approx 487\text{K}$) would benefit from further vocabulary expansion, suggesting even Llama3's 128K may be insufficient at larger scales.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Tokens-to-characters function $f(V)$ robustness across tokenizer types:** The paper validates that the quadratic-in-log-$V$ functional form for $f(V)$ is robust to the choice of tokenizer algorithm (Appendix A.9, Figure 12). Fitted separately on BPE, Unigram, and Word-based tokenizers, all achieve $R^2 \geq 0.98$ with low RMSE ($3.8 \times 10^{-4}$, $5.2 \times 10^{-4}$, and $3.6 \times 10^{-5}$, respectively). This is important because it means Approach 2 and Approach 3 (both of which depend on $f(V)$ for FLOPs calculations) would generalize to tokenizers beyond BPE with appropriate recalibration of the $a, b, c$ coefficients.

**Vocabulary-insensitive loss validation:** The paper empirically verifies that the unigram-normalized loss $\mathcal{L}_u$ correctly ranks models with different vocabulary sizes (Appendix A.10, Figure 13). When raw cross-entropy loss $\mathcal{L}$ is plotted against downstream performance across models with varying $V$, the correlation is **positive** — higher loss accompanies better downstream accuracy, because larger vocabularies inflate loss even when they improve the model. When $\mathcal{L}_u$ is used instead, the correlation becomes negative — lower loss corresponds to better downstream accuracy, as expected for a valid evaluation metric. The paper also shows a strong linear correlation between $\mathcal{L}_u$ and bits-per-character (Appendix A.5, Figure 11: Pearson $\rho = 0.9888$, RMSE $e = 0.0683$), confirming that both vocabulary-insensitive metrics capture the same underlying model quality. These checks are essential because the entire paper's experimental framework — sweeping vocabulary sizes and comparing the resulting models — depends on having a metric that makes cross-vocabulary comparison meaningful.

**Exploration of larger vocabulary range:** The paper verifies that the existence of an optimal vocabulary size is not an artifact of the studied range (4K–96K) by testing vocabulary sizes from 0.5K to 512K with $N_{nv} = 33\text{M}$ (Appendix A.4, Figure 10). The loss curves continue to show a well-defined minimum within the studied range, and performance degrades consistently as vocabulary moves beyond the optimum toward extreme values. This is a useful sanity check: it confirms that the observed U-shaped relationship between vocabulary size and loss is a genuine phenomenon rather than an artifact of a limited sweep range.

**Embedding representation quality analysis (Appendix A.3, Figure 9):** The paper provides qualitative evidence for the underfitting mechanism by visualizing the SVD decomposition of learned word embeddings for $V = 4\text{K}$, $V = 16\text{K}$, and $V = 64\text{K}$ at $N_{nv} = 85\text{M}$. The average Euclidean distance among embeddings ($D_{\text{avg}}$) decreases from 1.067 (4K) to 1.011 (16K) to 0.952 (64K), with low-frequency token embeddings becoming increasingly clustered at larger vocabulary sizes. This visualization directly supports the paper's theoretical argument about why larger vocabularies can hurt when compute is constrained: rare tokens receive insufficient gradient updates and their embeddings degenerate toward a cluster, providing poor representations.

**Parametric fit with constraints:** Approach 3's fitting procedure enforces $\alpha_1 = \beta$ (equal scaling of non-vocabulary parameters and data) based on the Chinchilla finding. The paper reports the fitted values $\alpha_1 = \beta = 0.447$ and $\alpha_2 = 0.671$ but does not present an ablation where $\alpha_1 \neq \beta$ (unconstrained fit) to verify that the constraint is empirically justified for this data. Given that Hoffmann et al. (2022) found equal scaling was optimal in a vocabulary-fixed setting, and this paper introduces vocabulary as a new variable, it would be reasonable to ask whether the equal-scaling constraint still holds when vocabulary is optimized jointly. This ablation is not performed, which is a limitation.

**Undertraining and overtraining regimes tested at only one model size:** The validation experiments in Table 3 are conducted exclusively at $N_{nv} = 2.87\text{B}$. The paper does not test whether Approach 3's predictions for undertraining and overtraining hold at other scales (e.g., 1.13B or 300M). The inclusion of Figure 7 with $N_{nv} = 302\text{M}$ provides partial coverage of a smaller scale, but only for varying data volume at a vocabulary sweep, not for the specific "V=32K vs. V^opt" comparison. The generalizability of the undertraining/overtraining predictions across scales remains an open question.

**No ablation on vocabulary initialization or training dynamics:** All models with different vocabulary sizes are trained from scratch with random initialization. The paper does not explore whether vocabulary parameters can be initialized from a pretrained tokenizer (e.g., initializing a 64K vocabulary from a 96K tokenizer by selecting the most frequent tokens) or whether the optimal vocabulary at one scale can be used to warm-start training at a larger scale. These are engineering considerations rather than scientific gaps, but they affect the practical applicability of the recommendations.

### Critical Assessment

**Central Claim 1: "The optimal vocabulary size depends on the compute budget, with larger models requiring larger vocabularies."**

This is the paper's foundational claim and it is **strongly supported by the IsoFLOPs experiments** (Approach 1, Figure 5). The power-law fit $N_v \propto C^{0.42}$ directly demonstrates that as the FLOPs budget increases, the optimal vocabulary parameter count increases. The fact that all three approaches converge on $\gamma \approx 0.83 < 1$ provides triangulation — the claim holds whether you approach it empirically (Approach 1), analytically (Approach 2), or through loss modeling (Approach 3).

However, there is an important nuance in how the claim is stated versus what is demonstrated. The paper often says "larger models require larger vocabularies" (Abstract, Section 1), which could be interpreted as a causal claim: increasing model size causes the optimal vocabulary to increase. What is actually demonstrated is a **correlation at the compute-optimal frontier**: when you increase the FLOPs budget, both model size and vocabulary size should increase, with vocabulary increasing more slowly. This is a statement about joint optimization under a compute constraint, not about independent manipulation of model size. Would increasing $N_{nv}$ while holding FLOPs fixed justify a larger vocabulary? The answer from Approach 3 is "it depends on the data budget" — if you increase $N_{nv}$ but keep FLOPs constant, you must reduce $H$ (fewer training characters), and the optimal vocabulary might actually decrease (as shown in the undertraining scenario, Table 3). The claim holds precisely under compute-optimal scaling but requires qualification in other regimes.

**Central Claim 2: "Most LLMs use insufficient vocabulary sizes. For example, Llama2-70B should have been at least 216K, 7 times larger than its vocabulary of 32K."**

This claim relies on **extrapolation of scaling laws fitted at 33M–1.13B parameters to models at the 70B scale** — a roughly 60–2000× extrapolation. The paper is transparent about the scales at which it fits (Table 4) and the scales to which it extrapolates (Table 1), but the extrapolation magnitude deserves explicit acknowledgment. Scaling laws are, by nature, extrapolative tools, and the fact that the three approaches agree on the extrapolation provides some confidence. However, the paper does not validate its predictions at 70B scale — the largest validation is at 2.87B parameters (Tables 2 and 3), which is still 25× smaller than Llama2-70B. The claim that the vocabulary "should have been at least 216K" is therefore a model-based prediction, not an empirically verified fact at that scale.

Additionally, the "7 times larger" figure implicitly assumes that the embedding dimension $d$ at the 70B scale would be 8192 (as in the Llama2-70B architecture) and that the compute budget is optimally allocated (Chinchilla-style equal scaling). Llama2-70B was trained on 2 trillion tokens — far more than compute-optimal — which, per the paper's own Approach 3, would push the optimal vocabulary even higher than the compute-optimal prediction. So the 216K figure may actually be an **underestimate** for Llama2-70B's actual training regime. This is noted in Section 5: "Considering that several LLMs are trained on substantially more data than optimal ones (e.g., Llama2), the optimal vocabulary sizes would likely be larger than currently estimated." This is an important qualification that prevents the 216K from being taken as the definitive answer.

**Central Claim 3: "Adopting our predicted optimal vocabulary size consistently improves downstream performance over commonly used vocabulary sizes."**

This claim is **supported at the tested scale** ($N_{nv} = 2.87\text{B}$) across three training regimes (Table 3: undertraining, compute-optimal, overtraining) and partially supported at the compute-optimal regime (Table 2). The improvements are consistent in direction (the predicted optimal vocabulary always matches or beats the 32K baseline) but modest in magnitude: average gains of 0.6–1.3 percentage points across 7 benchmarks, with individual task gains ranging from 0 to 2.9 points. The "consistently" qualifier is accurate — there are no cases where the predicted optimal vocabulary underperforms the baseline.

However, several limitations temper this claim:

1. **Single baseline vocabulary ($V = 32\text{K}$):** The paper compares only against $V = 32\text{K}$, which is the Llama2 standard. It does not compare against other common choices like 50K (used by GPT models), 64K, or 128K (Llama3). In the overtraining regime, where $V^{\text{opt}} = 43\text{K}$ is predicted, the paper does not test whether $V = 48\text{K}$ or $V = 64\text{K}$ would outperform $V = 43\text{K}$. This means we know 43K beats 32K, but we do not know whether 43K is better than other plausible alternatives that are also larger than 32K. The claim of "optimality" is relative to the fitted model, not verified via a dense sweep around the predicted optimum at the validation scale.

2. **Single model scale ($N_{nv} = 2.87\text{B}$):** The validation experiments are at only one parameter count. The paper's headline claim that the approach "consistently improves downstream performance" is based on one model size tested in three data regimes. We do not know whether the improvements would be larger or smaller at 7B, 13B, or 70B parameters.

3. **Single training run per configuration:** The paper does not report error bars from multiple training seeds. The standard deviations reported in Tables 2 and 3 are for evaluation variance (across prompts/tasks), not training variance. With only one training run per $(N_{nv}, V, H)$ combination, we cannot assess whether the observed improvements are statistically significant or within the noise of training stochasticity. At these model scales (2.87B parameters trained on hundreds of billions of characters), training variance from random seed can be non-trivial — anecdotally, ±0.5–1.0 percentage points on benchmarks is plausible. The +0.6 average improvement in the compute-optimal regime (Table 2) could plausibly be within training noise, though the consistent direction across tasks and the larger gap in the overtraining regime (+1.3 average) make this less concerning.

4. **Limited downstream task diversity:** All 7 benchmarks are standard English-language reasoning and commonsense tasks. The paper does not evaluate on code generation, mathematical reasoning, multilingual tasks, or knowledge-intensive QA. Vocabulary size might have differential effects across these categories — for instance, code generation with its restricted syntax might benefit less from larger vocabularies, while multilingual tasks might benefit more (as the multilingual vocabulary expansion literature suggests). The paper cannot claim generality across task types based on the current evaluation.

**Central Claim 4: "We make predictions for larger models and empirically verify our approaches on up to 3B parameters."**

This is an accurate description of what was done, but the word "verify" should be understood narrowly: the paper verifies that the vocabulary predicted by Approach 3 (which was fitted on models up to 1.13B) transfers to a 2.87B model. This is a ~2.5× extrapolation in parameter count — a useful sanity check but not a strong test of the scaling law's predictive power. A more convincing verification would test at a distinctly larger scale (e.g., 7B parameters, which is ~6× beyond the fitting range) or would show that the scaling law accurately predicts the optimal vocabulary of an existing model whose vocabulary was independently optimized (but no such model exists, since the claim is precisely that existing models are suboptimal).

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

- **The IsoFLOPs data comes from models trained with fixed training characters per group, not fixed FLOPs.** Models within each $N_{nv}$ group are trained on the same number of characters $H$, but since $C \approx 6(N_{nv} + Vd)Hf(V)$ depends on $V$, models with different vocabularies accumulate different total FLOPs. The paper handles this via interpolation — estimating loss at common FLOPs budgets from the observed (FLOPs, loss) trajectories — but this introduces interpolation error that is not quantified. The interpolation accuracy depends on the density of observed points and the smoothness of the loss curve, neither of which is analyzed.

- **The predicted optimal vocabulary sizes depend on the embedding dimension schedule $d(N_{nv})$.** If a practitioner chooses a different depth-to-width ratio than the paper's Table 5, the predicted optimal vocabulary size changes because $V^{\text{opt}} = N_v^{\text{opt}} / d$. The paper's recommendation is tied to a specific architectural scaling choice, and it does not explore sensitivity to this choice. For instance, if Llama2-70B used $d = 10240$ instead of $8192$, the predicted $V^{\text{opt}}$ would be proportionally smaller (~173K instead of 216K). This coupling between vocabulary size and embedding dimension is not discussed as a limitation.

- **The $N_v = Vd$ approximation (using $Vd$ rather than $2Vd$).** The paper explicitly states it uses $Vd$ for "clarity and analytical simplicity" (Section 2.2, footnote 1), with the justification that the embedding lookup contributes negligible FLOPs compared to the output projection. However, the parameter count is used not only for FLOPs but also for the loss formula in Approach 3 ($A_2 / N_v^{\alpha_2}$). If the vocabulary parameters are actually $2Vd$ (embedding + output), the effective $N_v$ in the loss term is twice what the paper assumes, which would change the fitted $A_2$ and $\alpha_2$ values and potentially shift the predicted optimal vocabulary sizes. The paper does not analyze the sensitivity of its predictions to this choice.

- **Missing baseline: vocabulary size derived from tokenizer optimization heuristics.** The paper compares against $V = 32\text{K}$, which is a convention rather than an optimized choice. It would strengthen the case to compare against vocabulary sizes that are commonly recommended by tokenizer optimization metrics — e.g., the vocabulary size that minimizes the number of out-of-vocabulary tokens, or that achieves a target compression ratio. If the paper's predictions outperform these data-driven heuristics, the case for a compute-optimal approach over simpler rule-based methods would be stronger.

- **No direct evidence that the 216K vocabulary for Llama2-70B would actually improve performance.** This is the paper's most attention-grabbing prediction, but it is entirely model-based with no empirical validation at that scale. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by limiting its validation to 3B parameters, but the Abstract's prominent mention of the 216K figure (and the 7× claim) creates an impression of certainty that the experiments do not fully support. A validation at, say, 7B parameters with $V = 32\text{K}$ vs. $V = 67\text{K}$ (the predicted optimum from Table 1) would substantially strengthen confidence in the 70B extrapolation.

**Experiments that would have strengthened the paper:**

1. **Validation at 7B parameters** (or at least a scale where the predicted $V^{\text{opt}}$ is substantially different from 32K, unlike the 35K prediction at 2.87B in the compute-optimal regime). The overtraining experiment at 2.87B with $V^{\text{opt}} = 43\text{K}$ partially addresses this, but a larger-scale validation would more directly test the extrapolation quality.

2. **Sweep around predicted optimum at the validation scale.** For the $N_{nv} = 2.87\text{B}$ overtraining experiment, testing $V \in \{32\text{K}, 43\text{K}, 48\text{K}, 64\text{K}\}$ would show whether 43K is actually the peak or whether the loss surface is flat enough that a range of vocabulary sizes work equally well. The paper's framework predicts 43K as the minimum of the fitted loss function, but the actual empirical minimum at the validation scale is unknown without a sweep.

3. **Multiple training seeds** for at least one key comparison (e.g., the overtraining $V = 32\text{K}$ vs. $V = 43\text{K}$ comparison) to establish whether the observed differences exceed training noise.

4. **Comparison against alternative vocabulary selection heuristics**, such as vocabulary size chosen to achieve a target token-to-character ratio, or vocabulary size chosen to keep a fixed ratio of $N_v / N_{nv}$ (some existing models appear to target roughly $N_v / N_{nv} \approx 0.5\%$–2\%). Showing that the paper's compute-optimal approach outperforms simpler rules would strengthen the practical argument.

5. **Evaluation on code and multilingual tasks.** The paper's framework is language-agnostic (it operates on characters and tokenizers), but the validation is English-only. Testing on code (where tokenization efficiency interacts with syntax in different ways) and multilingual data (where the vocabulary must cover multiple languages) would test the generality of the $\gamma \approx 0.83$ scaling.

**What the experiments do support convincingly:**

- The **existence** of a compute-optimal vocabulary size that varies with compute budget (Figure 3, Figure 5) is robustly demonstrated at the scales tested (33M–1.13B parameters).
- The **scaling relationship** $\gamma \approx 0.83$ is supported by three independent methods that converge to the same value, which is unusually strong evidence for a scaling law paper.
- The **direction** of the vocabulary-data coupling (more data → larger optimal vocabulary; less data → smaller optimal vocabulary) is confirmed in Figure 7 and Tables 2–3, and this direction is non-obvious.
- The **practical benefit** of vocabulary optimization at 2.87B parameters in the overtraining regime (+1.3 points average downstream) is demonstrated, though the magnitude should be interpreted cautiously given the single-run limitation.

The paper's claims about specific vocabulary sizes for specific models (Llama2-70B → 216K, Llama3-400B → 487K) should be understood as **model-based predictions** that are plausible given the fitted scaling laws but not empirically validated at those scales. The framework for making such predictions is the contribution; the specific numbers are the initial output of that framework and may be refined as more data at larger scales becomes available.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Scaling Laws Are Fitted at 33M–1.13B Parameters but Extrapolated to 70B–400B

**The assumption or constraint.** All three approaches are fitted using models with non-vocabulary parameters in the range $N_{nv} \in [33\text{M}, 1.13\text{B}]$, with the largest model group having $N_{nv} = 1.13\text{B}$ (Section 4.1, Table 4). The paper then extrapolates these fitted power laws to models at the 70B, 130B, 300B, and even 400B scale (Table 1, Figure 2, Figure 6, Appendix A.11). This represents an extrapolation of roughly 60–400× beyond the largest fitting point. The paper acknowledges the limited scale of its validation: the downstream experiments are conducted at $N_{nv} = 2.87\text{B}$ (Section 5, Tables 2–3), and the paper states in Appendix B.2 that "exploring to what extent our findings hold in even larger models and with different architectures is a promising direction for future work."

**The consequence.** Scaling law extrapolations are only as reliable as the functional form assumed. If the relationship $N_v^{\text{opt}} \propto N_{nv}^\gamma$ with $\gamma \approx 0.83$ does not hold at larger scales — for example, if $\gamma$ itself varies with scale, or if the power-law relationship breaks down due to phase transitions in tokenization efficiency at very large vocabulary sizes — then the specific predictions for Llama2-70B ($V^{\text{opt}} \approx 216\text{K}$), Llama3-70B ($V^{\text{opt}} \approx 278\text{K}$), and Llama3-400B ($V^{\text{opt}} \approx 487\text{K}$) could be substantially wrong. A practitioner training a 70B-parameter model who follows the paper's recommendation and expands vocabulary from 32K to 216K would be making a significant engineering investment (the vocabulary parameters alone would increase from ~0.26B to ~1.8B, and the output projection matrix would dominate certain training and inference costs) based on an extrapolation that has not been validated within an order of magnitude of the target scale.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The only empirical validation of the extrapolation is at $N_{nv} = 2.87\text{B}$ (Tables 2 and 3), which is roughly 2.5× beyond the largest fitting point of 1.13B and roughly 25× smaller than the 70B scale for which predictions are made. The validation at 2.87B supports the *direction* of the prediction (vocabulary should increase) but does not verify the *magnitude* — the predicted $V^{\text{opt}}$ in the compute-optimal regime at 2.87B is 35K, very close to the baseline 32K, making the validation a weak test of the extrapolation quality. The overtraining experiment with $V^{\text{opt}} = 43\text{K}$ provides a somewhat larger gap, but this is still a 1.3× change from baseline, compared to the 7× change predicted for Llama2-70B. The paper provides no evidence that the power-law relationship holds over a 60–400× extrapolation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not mitigate this limitation. It acknowledges the need for larger-scale validation (Appendix B.2) but provides none. The convergence of three independent approaches on $\gamma \approx 0.83$ provides some confidence in the functional form at the fitted scales, but convergence at small scales does not guarantee accuracy at 100× larger scales — all three approaches share the same underlying training data and could share the same systematic biases. A practitioner should treat the specific vocabulary size predictions for 70B+ models as **hypotheses to be tested** rather than validated prescriptions.

---

### 6.2 The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted for in Any Efficiency Calculation

**The assumption or constraint.** Approaches 1 and 3 both require training models across multiple vocabulary sizes at multiple non-vocabulary parameter scales to fit the scaling laws. The paper's experimental design involves 6 groups of models ($N_{nv}$ from 33M to 1.13B), each with up to 10 vocabulary sizes (4K–96K), trained on up to 201B characters per model (Table 4, Appendix A.7.1). The total computational cost of collecting this fitting data is not reported, but it is clearly substantial — training dozens of models at scales up to 1.13B parameters on hundreds of billions of characters. The paper acknowledges this cost implicitly in describing Approach 2 as "rapid and broadly applicable" compared to "exhaustive deep learning experiments" (Appendix B.1), and notes that the experimental configuration space "significantly increases" compared to vocabulary-fixed scaling laws (Section 4.1). However, the paper never quantifies this cost or amortizes it in any efficiency claim.

**The consequence.** The headline finding — that vocabulary optimization provides "improvement" at the "same FLOPs budget" (Tables 2 and 3) — treats the cost of determining the optimal vocabulary as zero. In reality, a practitioner who wants to use Approach 1 or Approach 3 for their own model family must first invest substantial compute in the fitting experiments: training multiple model sizes, each with multiple vocabulary sizes, to map out the loss landscape. This cost is non-trivial. If a team is training a single 70B-parameter model, the cost of running the full suite of fitting experiments (even at smaller scales) might represent a significant fraction of the final training budget — potentially negating the efficiency gains from vocabulary optimization, especially if the gains are modest (1–3 percentage points on downstream benchmarks). Approach 2 partially mitigates this by providing a cheaper estimation method, but it still requires a calibration experiment (finding the optimal vocabulary for a small reference model) and relies on the derivative-based scaling proportion $\gamma = 0.83$, which itself was derived from the expensive experiments of Approach 1.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no quantification of the fitting cost. The number of models trained, their sizes, and their training character budgets can be reconstructed from Table 4 and the vocabulary size list (10 sizes per group, 6 groups = 60+ models, plus additional models for the 2.87B validation experiments), but the total FLOPs or GPU-hours consumed in the fitting process is not reported. Appendix A.7.3 reports that the $N_{nv} = 2.87\text{B}$ experiments took "about 120 hours to train on over 500B training characters with 64 total GPUs" — roughly 7,680 A100-GPU-hours for a single model — but equivalent figures for the smaller-scale fitting experiments are not provided. The paper also does not discuss whether the fitted scaling laws transfer across model families (e.g., would a practitioner using a non-Llama architecture need to re-fit, or do the $\gamma \approx 0.83$ predictions generalize?).

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges this limitation. Section 3 states that Approach 2 is motivated by the desire to avoid "substantial computational resources to perform these fittings" (Appendix B.1), and the two-step calibration procedure for Approach 2 (Section 4.2) is explicitly designed to reduce the cost of applying the framework to new model families. However, Approach 2 still requires the initial calibration experiment, and the paper does not provide guidance on how cheaply this can be done (e.g., the minimum $N_{nv}$ and data budget needed for a reliable calibration). The paper frames this as an exploration-exploitation tradeoff (Appendix B.1) but does not analyze it quantitatively. For practitioners, the choice between "run expensive fitting experiments to potentially save compute on the final model" and "use a safe default vocabulary size" remains an unquantified risk-reward decision.

---

### 6.3 Single Training Run per Configuration and No Statistical Significance Testing

**The assumption or constraint.** For each $(N_{nv}, V, H)$ combination, the paper trains exactly one model. The scaling law fitting (Approaches 1 and 3) is based on single observations of $\mathcal{L}_u$ at each operating point, and the downstream validation experiments (Tables 2 and 3) report results from a single training run per configuration. The paper states this explicitly in the NeurIPS Checklist (Appendix, item 7):

> "For our pre-training experiments, we do not provide error bars due to their computational cost. Specifically, our variables are 'The number of considered models' × 'The number of considered vocabulary size' × 'The number of training characters' × 'The number of repeated experiments.' Training multiple models across all these axes for more statistical significance would be prohibitively expensive."

**The consequence.** Without multiple training seeds, we cannot distinguish between genuine differences in model quality and noise from training stochasticity (random initialization, data order, dropout). At the scales studied, this noise can be substantial. The downstream improvements reported in Tables 2 and 3 — ranging from +0.6 to +1.3 average points across benchmarks, with individual task gains as small as +0.6 — could plausibly fall within training variance. For context, prior work training LLMs at similar scales has reported seed-to-seed variance of 0.5–1.5 percentage points on similar benchmarks. If the true effect of vocabulary optimization at $N_{nv} = 2.87\text{B}$ is comparable to training noise, then the headline claim that vocabulary optimization "consistently improves downstream performance" (Section 5) rests on a single realization that might not replicate. The fitting of the scaling laws (Approaches 1 and 3) is similarly vulnerable: noise in individual $\mathcal{L}_u$ observations could distort the fitted exponents and coefficients, particularly for Approach 1 which selects the minimum observed loss at each FLOPs budget — the minimum of noisy observations is a biased estimator of the true minimum.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports standard deviations for downstream tasks within each evaluation (the ± values in Tables 2 and 3), but these reflect variance across evaluation prompts and tasks, not training seed variance. They do not quantify the uncertainty in the model's performance arising from training stochasticity. The paper provides no confidence intervals for the fitted scaling law parameters, no assessment of how sensitive the $\gamma \approx 0.83$ estimate is to which specific models achieved the lowest loss in Approach 1, and no cross-validation of the Approach 3 fit beyond using all available data points for a single fit.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation honestly (quoted above) and frames it as a cost-driven tradeoff. It does not attempt to mitigate it through cheaper statistical methods (e.g., estimating training variance from a subset of configurations, using ensemble-based uncertainty quantification for the fitted parameters, or reporting sensitivity of the scaling law exponents to data perturbations). The paper's suggested future work in Appendix B.3 notes that "empirical studies on different datasets could help in understanding how vocabulary size impacts loss under varied data conditions," but this addresses generalizability rather than statistical reliability at the studied scale.

### 6.4 The Unigram-Normalized Loss Is Validated Only for English, and $f(V)$ Is Fitted Only on SlimPajama

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire experimental framework depends on two measurement components that are calibrated on a single dataset: the unigram-normalized loss $\mathcal{L}_u$ (validated for fairness across vocabulary sizes in Appendix A.10 using models trained on SlimPajama) and the tokens-characters mapping function $f(V)$ (fitted from 25 tokenizers trained on a uniformly sampled subset of SlimPajama, Appendix A.8). The paper does not test whether $\mathcal{L}_u$ correctly ranks models with different vocabulary sizes when the training data distribution is substantially different (e.g., code-heavy corpora, multilingual data, domain-specific text) or whether $f(V)$ maintains its quadratic-in-log-$V$ functional form with similar coefficients for non-English text or code. The downstream evaluation is also exclusively on English-language benchmarks (Section 5), testing only the English-trained models.

**The consequence.** There are two distinct failure modes. First, the unigram normalization assumes that token frequencies in the evaluation corpus reflect the underlying "difficulty" of predicting each token. For an English model evaluated on English text, this is reasonable. But for a multilingual model where token frequencies vary dramatically across languages (common English subwords may be rare in a low-resource language), the unigram baseline $p(w_i|V)$ may not provide a fair comparison — a model with a larger vocabulary that better covers the low-resource language might appear to have worse $\mathcal{L}_u$ because the unigram probabilities for that language's tokens are very small (making the denominator small and the ratio artificially large). The paper provides no analysis of this effect.

Second, the $f(V)$ function is fitted on SlimPajama, which is primarily English web text. For code corpora (where whitespace, indentation, and special characters are common) or languages with different character-level statistics (e.g., logographic scripts like Chinese, where one character ≈ one word), the tokens-per-character ratio as a function of vocabulary size could follow a very different curve. The paper shows robustness across tokenizer types (BPE, Unigram, Word-based) in Appendix A.9, but all tokenizers are trained on the same SlimPajama data — this tests algorithmic robustness, not distributional robustness. If $f(V)$ is misspecified for a target domain, then all FLOPs calculations and all three approaches' predictions become unreliable because they depend on $f(V)$ to convert between characters and tokens.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The $\mathcal{L}_u$ validation in Appendix A.10 (Figure 13) shows the expected negative correlation between $\mathcal{L}_u$ and downstream accuracy for SlimPajama-trained models evaluated on English benchmarks. The correlation with BPC in Appendix A.5 (Figure 11, $\rho = 0.9888$) provides convergent validation that $\mathcal{L}_u$ captures real model quality differences. However, both validations are on the same data distribution. The $f(V)$ robustness check in Appendix A.9 tests different tokenizer algorithms on the same data, not the same algorithm on different data. The paper provides no evidence about whether $\mathcal{L}_u$ or $f(V)$ transfer to other domains or languages.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation directly. It acknowledges in Appendix B.4 that "multilingual models require a nuanced understanding of vocabulary due to linguistic diversity, which may affect the optimal vocabulary size and the computation of FLOPs differently across languages," but this is framed as an extension opportunity rather than a limitation of the current framework. The word "English" does not appear in the limitations section (Appendix B) despite being a fundamental constraint on the paper's claims. A practitioner training a multilingual or code-focused model should be cautious about applying the paper's specific $\gamma$ and $f(V)$ parameters without re-validation on their target data distribution.

### 6.5 The $N_v = Vd$ Approximation Ignores Embedding–Output Asymmetry and Ties Predictions to a Specific $d(N_{nv})$ Schedule

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper defines vocabulary parameters as $N_v = Vd$, using a single factor of $Vd$ rather than the $2Vd$ that would correspond to separate (untied) embedding and output matrices (Section 2.2, footnote 1):

> "Vocabulary parameters typically encompass both the word embedding layer and the output layer. In this paper, for clarity and analytical simplicity, we employ $Vd$ rather than $2Vd$ to represent the vocabulary parameters. This choice is predicated on empirical observations: the main computational burden, as measured in FLOPs, is associated with the output layer, but not the word embedding layer."

Additionally, the embedding dimension $d$ is not a free variable but is determined by $N_{nv}$ through a fixed schedule (Table 5, Appendix A.7.2). This means the predicted optimal vocabulary size $V^{\text{opt}} = N_v^{\text{opt}} / d$ depends on both the scaling law's prediction for $N_v^{\text{opt}}$ and the architectural choice of $d$ for the target $N_{nv}$.

**The consequence.** The $Vd$ vs. $2Vd$ choice has a direct effect on the fitted parameters of Approach 3, where the term $A_2 / N_v^{\alpha_2}$ penalizes insufficient vocabulary capacity. If the true vocabulary parameter count affecting loss is closer to $2Vd$ (because both the embedding and output layers contribute to the model's ability to represent and predict tokens), then the paper's $N_v$ is systematically underestimated by a factor of ~2. This would mean the fitted $A_2$ and $\alpha_2$ values are compensating for the missing factor of 2, and the predicted optimal $V^{\text{opt}}$ could shift if the model were re-parameterized with $2Vd$. The paper provides no sensitivity analysis of its predictions to this choice.

Equally importantly, the $d(N_{nv})$ schedule encodes a specific architectural scaling philosophy. If a practitioner uses a different depth-to-width ratio — for example, a deeper, narrower model or a shallower, wider model with the same $N_{nv}$ — the predicted $V^{\text{opt}}$ changes because $d$ changes. For instance, at $N_{nv} = 70\text{B}$, the paper assumes $d = 8192$, giving $V^{\text{opt}} = N_v^{\text{opt}} / 8192$. If the practitioner instead uses $d = 10240$ (a wider model), the predicted vocabulary size would be proportionally smaller (~80% of the paper's prediction). If they use $d = 6144$ (a narrower model), it would be proportionally larger (~133% of the paper's prediction). The paper's recommendation is thus architecture-specific in a way that is not prominently discussed — the $\gamma \approx 0.83$ scaling applies to $N_v$, not $V$ directly, and converting to a vocabulary size requires choosing $d$.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper justifies the $Vd$ choice empirically (footnote 1) but does not present evidence for it — no ablation comparing $Vd$ vs. $2Vd$ parameterizations of the loss function is shown. The paper justifies the fixed $d(N_{nv})$ schedule by appealing to Kaplan et al. (2020)'s finding that the depth-to-width ratio has a relatively small effect on performance given fixed total parameters, but Kaplan et al.'s finding was established in a vocabulary-fixed setting — it is not obvious that it holds when vocabulary size is also varying and interacts with the embedding dimension. The paper does not test sensitivity to $d$ at a fixed $N_{nv}, V$.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the approximation implicitly by calling it a choice made "for clarity and analytical simplicity" but does not treat it as a limitation that could affect the quantitative predictions. The dependence on $d(N_{nv})$ is similarly present in all scaling laws work (since model shape is always a design choice), but it is more consequential here because vocabulary size is directly divided by $d$ to get the final prediction. The paper could have reported $N_v^{\text{opt}}$ as the primary prediction and provided $V^{\text{opt}}$ as an architecture-dependent derived quantity, but instead the Abstract and figures prominently feature the vocabulary size predictions (216K, 487K, etc.), which embed the $d(N_{nv})$ assumption without making it transparent to the reader. A practitioner should understand that the specific $V^{\text{opt}}$ numbers are conditional on both the scaling law and the architectural width schedule.

### 6.6 No Evidence on Inference-Time Costs of Larger Vocabularies

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire analysis optimizes vocabulary size with respect to **training FLOPs** — the compute budget $C$ used throughout the paper is the total pre-training cost. The paper acknowledges but does not analyze the fact that "expanding the vocabulary size also increases the computational demands during inference" (Section 5, Figure 7 discussion). The output projection layer (mapping from hidden states of dimension $d$ to logits over $V$ tokens) requires a matrix multiplication of size $d \times V$ for every generated token at inference time. Increasing $V$ from 32K to 216K (the prediction for Llama2-70B) increases the output projection FLOPs per token by a factor of ~6.75×, and increases the memory required to store the output projection matrix by the same factor. The embedding layer similarly grows, though the embedding lookup operation is cheaper than a full matrix multiplication. The paper's recommendation therefore has significant inference-time implications that are entirely unaccounted for in the optimization objective.

**The consequence.** For many deployment scenarios, inference cost dominates total cost of ownership. A model that is trained slightly more efficiently (better loss per training FLOPs) but substantially more expensive at inference time may be a net negative when total lifecycle cost is considered. The paper's compute-optimal framework considers only training FLOPs, implicitly assuming that inference cost is negligible or that the training-compute-to-inference-compute ratio ($R = D_{\text{inference}} / D_{\text{pretrain}}$) is very small. But for high-throughput deployments (APIs, chatbots, code assistants), the number of inference tokens can far exceed the number of training tokens, making the output projection's cost a meaningful fraction of total compute. For Llama2-70B with $V = 32\text{K}$ and $d = 8192$, the output projection is approximately $8192 \times 32000 = 262\text{M}$ parameters — already ~3.8% of total parameters. At $V = 216\text{K}$, it would be $8192 \times 216000 \approx 1.8\text{B}$ parameters — roughly 25% of total parameters, concentrated in a single matrix multiplication that must be computed for every generated token. This would increase per-token inference latency substantially, especially in memory-bandwidth-bound regimes.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no measurements or modeling of inference costs. The acknowledgment in Section 5 is a single sentence noting that "expanding the vocabulary size also increases the computational demands during inference" followed by a recommendation to "use the optimal vocabulary size corresponding to a given $N_{nv}$, assuming optimal allocation of training data, even in scenarios where overtraining may occur" — a recommendation that explicitly prioritizes training optimality over inference considerations without justification. The paper does not discuss how inference costs might modify the optimal vocabulary size, does not provide a FLOPs-matched comparison that includes inference, and does not reference prior work on inference-aware scaling laws (e.g., Sardana and Frankle, 2023, which is cited in the paper's reference list but not engaged with on this point).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not mitigate this limitation. It does not even frame it as a limitation in Appendix B. The recommendation to use training-optimal vocabulary sizes regardless of overtraining (Section 5) is presented as a positive guideline rather than a pending tradeoff. For a practitioner, this means the paper's vocabulary recommendations are **training-cost-optimal but not lifecycle-cost-optimal**, and the discrepancy could be large for inference-heavy deployments. A more complete analysis would need to incorporate inference FLOPs into the objective function, following the approach of Sardana and Frankle (2023), which would likely pull the optimal vocabulary size downward (since inference cost penalizes large vocabularies more than training cost does, as the output projection is used for every token at inference but its training cost is amortized over many tokens). The paper acknowledges this as future work implicitly (the Sardana and Frankle citation is present but not engaged), but a practitioner should not adopt the paper's vocabulary recommendations for inference-heavy use cases without additional analysis.
