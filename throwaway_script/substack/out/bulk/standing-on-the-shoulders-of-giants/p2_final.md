Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay (a reflection on learning through apprenticeship, late-night incident response, and the debt owed to exceptional teammates):

**Learning from the engineers around you**

Ryan opens with a scene from a late-night production incident. He was at the computer debugging "with a cold sweat"—prod was broken but the team had no idea why. They chased a bunch of theories for the cause of the breakage, but all of them led to dead ends. After a long silence in the chat, someone linked an unexpected query that explained the breakage through a seemingly unrelated service no one had considered. That same engineer provided a quick fix that "single-handedly saved the day."

The moment stuck with him not just because the crisis was averted, but because it crystallized a pattern of learning that only happens in close collaboration: "There are so many things you pick up by working with strong engineers that you can't learn anywhere else." From that specific incident, he absorbed two things directly—an unintuitive relationship between services, and a diagnostic tool he'd use for future incidents—but the deeper lesson was about the transmission of **tribal knowledge** that no documentation or course can provide.

**Behaviors you absorb by proximity**

Knowledge of systems is one thing; ways of working are another. Ryan notes that when you work closely with other engineers, "you see what actions they take and why." You absorb not just their conclusions but the process that led to them—how they narrow a search space, when they decide to escalate vs. dig deeper, what questions they ask first. These behaviors, he argues, are "also difficult to learn anywhere else." The medium of learning is apprenticeship, not instruction: you're watching someone's decision-making in real time, under pressure.

**Responsiveness as a force multiplier**

Skill alone isn't what makes the collaboration productive—it's skill paired with availability and work ethic. Ryan reflects that he "got lucky" on this front: "When I worked late into the night, my top teammates were right there with me discussing things in real time." That responsiveness collapsed the feedback loop from what "could have been days down to minutes," which dramatically accelerated his learning. The combination of real-time presence and expertise turned incidents into classrooms.

He frames collaborations with such engineers as a privilege: "Work hard and never let these opportunities go to waste. If you do it right, you'll learn skills fast that will serve you for the rest of your career." The essay is, in effect, a dedication to the mentors and peers whose proximity—especially at odd hours—shaped his growth. He closes by naming two engineers directly: **Haixia Shi** and **Runshen Zhu**, calling them "two of the strongest engineers I've worked with who have taught me much of what I know," and expressing lasting gratitude for "the late-night collaborations and fun I had building systems together."

Ryan's entire framework here rests on a single conviction: that **tribal knowledge** and **learned behaviors**—the things you can't get from documentation or courses—are transmitted almost entirely through proximity and availability. The essay is titled "Standing on the Shoulders of Giants," and the metaphor is literal for him: the late-night chat window where a teammate links the unexpected query is the shoulder he stood on, collapsing a days-long debugging cycle into minutes and depositing durable diagnostic tools into his repertoire. The named dedication to Haixia Shi and Runshen Zhu closes the loop on a career stance that treats collaborative intensity not just as a productivity tactic but as the primary engine of professional growth.
