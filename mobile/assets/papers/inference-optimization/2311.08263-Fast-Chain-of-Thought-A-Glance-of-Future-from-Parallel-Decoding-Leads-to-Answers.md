## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **FastCoT**, a model-agnostic framework that accelerates Chain-of-Thought inference by integrating parallel Jacobi decoding with standard autoregressive decoding, using the by-products of Jacobi decoding — approximate tokens that would normally be discarded — as a **glimpse of the future** (partial, forward-looking token predictions that let the model anticipate the answer before completing the full rationale). Across Llama-7B, Llama-13B, Llama2-7B, and Llama2-13B models on the CSQA, StrategyQA, and AQuA reasoning benchmarks, FastCoT achieves up to 21.80% wall-clock speedup (e.g., reducing AQuA inference on Llama-13B from 589.44s to 460.99s) with a performance drop held within 3%, establishing that approximate token information can substitute for complete rationale generation only when the CoT task admits partial-rationale saturation — the corrupted-rationale experiments show accuracy plateaus at as little as 40% of the full rationale on StrategyQA, bounding the approach to datasets and models where fragmented reasoning steps already carry sufficient signal.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Chain-of-Thought's Speed Tax

The fundamental tension this paper tackles is straightforward: **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting substantially improves LLM reasoning accuracy, but it imposes a severe latency penalty** because the model must autoregressively generate hundreds of tokens of intermediate rationale before producing the final answer. This "speed tax" is not incidental — it is structurally baked into how CoT works. The model does not jump from question to answer; it thinks step by step, one token at a time, with each token conditioned on all previous ones. For a typical CoT reasoning problem, the rationale can easily span 50–200 tokens, and each of those tokens requires one full forward pass through the model.

This matters because **CoT has become a standard technique across the LLM landscape**. Since Wei et al. (2022) introduced CoT prompting, demonstrating that simply prepending "Let's think step by step" to a prompt dramatically improves performance on arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning tasks, the approach has been adopted as a default strategy. The reason is clear: reasoning accuracy often jumps by 10–30 percentage points compared to direct-answer prompting. But this accuracy gain is purchased entirely with additional inference compute — and, crucially, the majority of that compute is spent on tokens the user never sees, cares about, or explicitly requests. The rationale is scaffolding, not the final product.

The paper identifies several downstream consequences of this speed tax (Section 1):

- **Industrial throughput constraints**: Systems processing large volumes of reasoning queries — customer support automation, educational assessment, legal document analysis — face a direct tradeoff between accuracy and throughput. Every additional rationale token is latency that compounds across thousands of queries.
- **User experience degradation**: Interactive applications (chatbots, tutoring systems, coding assistants) require sub-second response times. CoT's multi-second rationale generation can push latency past the threshold where users perceive the system as sluggish, regardless of accuracy improvements.
- **GPU underutilization**: The authors note that autoregressive decoding "may not fully utilize the capabilities of GPUs" (Section 1). Modern GPUs are optimized for high-throughput, parallel matrix operations. Autoregressive decoding, by generating one token at a time with a batch size of 1 per sequence, leaves substantial compute capacity idle. This is a hardware-software mismatch: the GPU *could* compute more in each forward pass, but the sequential dependency structure of autoregressive generation prevents it from doing so.

The problem is not that CoT is slow in absolute terms — it is that **the rationales contain substantial redundant information relative to what is strictly necessary to derive the answer**. The authors hypothesize that "partial generation of the complete rationale with a glimpse of future is enough" (Figure 1 caption), and their corruption experiments (Section 5.2, Figure 5) provide evidence: when 40% of the rationale tokens are replaced with `[PAD]`, performance on StrategyQA is already saturated — meaning 60% of the generated rationale was, in some sense, unnecessary for arriving at the correct answer. The wasted compute is not hypothetical; it is substantial and measurable.

### A Gap in the Literature: No One Has Tried CoT-Specific Inference Acceleration

The authors position their work at the intersection of two active research areas — **XoT prompt engineering** and **lossless decoding acceleration** — and argue that neither has addressed the unique opportunity presented by CoT tasks.

#### Prior Work in XoT Prompt Engineering: Better Accuracy, Same Speed (Or Worse)

The XoT literature (Section 2, first paragraph) has been relentlessly focused on improving reasoning *accuracy*, with inference speed treated as a fixed cost or ignored entirely. The paper catalogs the major variants:

- **Self-Consistency** (Wang et al., 2022): Sample multiple reasoning paths and majority-vote the answers. This *increases* inference cost by a factor equal to the number of samples (typically 5–40).
- **Self-Ask** (Press et al., 2022), **Self-Verification** (Weng et al., 2022): The model generates follow-up questions or self-checks, producing even *more* rationale tokens per query.
- **Tree-of-Thought** (Long, 2023), **Graph-of-Thought** (Besta et al., 2023): These structured reasoning frameworks explore multiple reasoning branches, generating orders of magnitude more tokens than standard CoT.
- **Rationale distillation** (Magister et al., 2022; Hsieh et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023): Train a smaller student model on CoT-generated rationales from a larger teacher. This can reduce inference cost *per token* (because the student is smaller), but it does not reduce the *number of tokens* that must be generated — the rationale is still produced autoregressively.

The common thread: all these methods "compromise the speed of inference" (Section 2) relative to direct-answer prompting, and none attempt to reduce the number of forward passes required to generate the rationale. The authors explicitly claim to be "the first to propose exploiting the concept of approximate rationale to expedite the completion of XoT-series tasks" (Section 2). This is a specific and testable claim of novelty.

#### Prior Work in Decoding Acceleration: Lossless Guarantees, No Use of Imperfect Tokens

A separate body of work has attacked the decoding speed problem head-on, but with a strict requirement: **the accelerated decoder must produce exactly the same tokens as standard autoregressive decoding**. The authors survey the major families (Section 2, "Accuracy Lossless Decoding Acceleration"):

**Speculative decoding** (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023): Use a smaller, faster "draft" model to propose multiple candidate tokens at once, then have the large model verify them in a single forward pass. If the verification passes, multiple tokens are accepted simultaneously, reducing the number of large-model forward passes. This approach is elegant, widely adopted, and strictly lossless — the final output is identical to what the large model would have produced autoregressively. However, it requires training or obtaining a suitable draft model, and it generates zero value from draft tokens that fail verification (they are simply discarded).

**Blockwise parallel decoding** (Stern et al., 2018): Add K auxiliary prediction heads to the base model that predict K future tokens simultaneously. This requires modifying the model architecture and training pipeline. The auxiliary heads produce predictions that are checked by the main model; incorrect predictions are discarded.

**Non-autoregressive translation** (Gu et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2022): In machine translation specifically, models are trained to generate all output tokens in parallel rather than sequentially. This achieves dramatic speedups but is domain-specific (mostly translation), requires "careful parameter tuning" (Section 2), and typically sacrifices output quality relative to autoregressive models.

**Jacobi decoding for translation** (Santilli et al., 2023): This is the most direct precursor to FastCoT. Santilli et al. (2023) applied Jacobi iteration — a classical numerical method for solving systems of equations — to autoregressive text generation. The insight is elegant: treat the autoregressive generation as a fixed-point iteration problem $y = f(y)$, where $f$ is the model's greedy decode function. Jacobi iteration converges to the fixed point (the greedy decoding trajectory) while allowing parallel token updates. Santilli et al. showed this works well for machine translation, achieving lossless speedups by accepting only tokens that match the autoregressive trajectory.

**But here is the critical gap that FastCoT exploits**: Santilli et al. (2023), like all prior lossless methods, **treated the intermediate Jacobi iterates as waste products**. Tokens that did not perfectly match the autoregressive trajectory were simply discarded — they had no value because the goal was exact output reproduction. The authors of FastCoT ask a fundamentally different question: *what if those imperfect, discarded tokens actually contain useful information for certain tasks?* Specifically, in reasoning tasks where the model needs a "glimpse" of where its thinking is heading, those approximate, not-quite-right tokens might be good enough to trigger the correct answer even if they wouldn't pass a strict correctness check. This reframing — from "imperfect tokens are errors to be discarded" to "imperfect tokens are approximate reasoning that may be sufficient" — is the conceptual pivot that distinguishes FastCoT from all prior decoding work.

### Conflict Between Two Well-Established Facts Creates the Opportunity

The paper implicitly identifies a productive tension between two observations:

1. **Fact 1 (from the XoT literature):** CoT rationales improve reasoning accuracy dramatically. The model genuinely benefits from "thinking step by step."
2. **Fact 2 (from the authors' corruption experiments, Section 5.2):** Complete, perfectly generated rationales are not necessary. When rationales are randomly corrupted (40–60% of tokens replaced with `[PAD]`), the model still produces correct answers at near-full accuracy on several benchmarks.

These two facts seem contradictory: if rationales help, why does corrupting them not hurt? The resolution the authors propose is that **the model does not need the full rationale text; it needs certain key signals or "anchor" concepts that emerge early in the reasoning process**. The first few reasoning steps might establish the critical entities, relationships, or problem framing, and everything after that is elaboration — helpful for verifying correctness, perhaps, but not strictly necessary for answer selection. The approximate tokens produced by Jacobi decoding, even if they contain errors, may carry enough of these anchoring signals to let the model short-circuit the remaining rationale generation.

This is a specific, falsifiable hypothesis: *the information density of CoT rationales is front-loaded, and the tail of the rationale contributes diminishing returns for answer accuracy.* The corruption experiments in Figure 5 provide circumstantial support — accuracy saturates at 40% rationale overlap on StrategyQA — but the paper does not do a fine-grained analysis of *which* tokens matter most (e.g., do reasoning tokens matter more than factual retrieval tokens? Do early tokens matter more than late ones?). This is a limitation of the motivational argument, but the observed saturation is sufficient to motivate trying the Jacobi by-product approach.

### Positioning Relative to Jacobi Decoding Specifically

Because FastCoT builds directly on Santilli et al. (2023), it's important to understand exactly what the prior work did and where FastCoT departs.

In Santilli et al.'s formulation, the Jacobi decoding procedure for autoregressive generation works as follows: instead of predicting one token at a time and feeding it back as input, the model is given a **context window** — a span of $c$ placeholder tokens initialized to some value (e.g., `[PAD]` tokens) appended after a verified prefix. In each forward pass, the model predicts *all* $c$ tokens in the window simultaneously, using its own previous predictions as input for the later positions (through the causal attention mask). Over multiple iterations, these predictions converge to a fixed point — the tokens that standard autoregressive decoding would have produced. Tokens that match between consecutive iterations have "converged" and are accepted as correct. The speedup comes from the fact that multiple tokens can converge in a single iteration, reducing the total number of forward passes.

**What Santilli et al. discard**: At each iteration, some tokens in the context window have converged (they match the previous iteration's prediction) and some have not (they changed). The unconverged tokens are approximate — they are the model's current best guess for what should appear at that position, but the guess hasn't stabilized yet. In lossless Jacobi decoding, these unconverged tokens are discarded and re-predicted in the next iteration.

**What FastCoT does differently**: FastCoT *keeps* these unconverged, approximate tokens and appends them to the prompt before asking the model for the final answer. The reasoning is that even if they "cannot pass the verification and therefore cannot be accepted as text generated by lossless decoding" (Section 3.1), they may still carry enough semantic signal — key entities, partial logical relationships, tentative conclusions — to trigger the correct answer generation. This is the "glimpse of the future" metaphor: the approximate tokens are like blurry, partially-formed thoughts that give the model a preview of where its reasoning is heading, allowing it to jump to the answer before the reasoning is fully articulated.

The authors also make a practical contribution relative to Santilli et al.: they "designed a parallel decoding framework to support a wide range of large language models with almost no modification to the source code... based on the huggingface implementations" (Section 1, contribution 3). Santilli et al.'s implementation did not support batch computation, which limits practical throughput. FastCoT's framework handles variable-length KV caches within a batch through two padding mechanisms (Type1 and Type2 padding, Figure 7), making it more deployable.

### Why the Problem Is Hard: The Verification Bottleneck

A subtle but important challenge that the paper addresses implicitly is the **verification bottleneck** in speculative-decoding-style approaches. In standard speculative decoding, the large model must verify each draft token against what it would have produced. This verification step costs exactly as much as the large model's forward pass — you're paying for the large model's computation either way, and the speedup comes only from being able to run the large model less frequently because it verifies multiple tokens at once.

In CoT reasoning, a related problem arises: **there is no ground-truth verifier for reasoning tokens**. In machine translation, you can verify a candidate token by comparing to the autoregressive output — the model has a well-defined "correct" token at each position. In reasoning, the notion of a "correct" rationale token is far murkier. Multiple different rationales can lead to the same correct answer. The authors sidestep this problem by not attempting to verify the approximate tokens at all — they simply append them as-is and ask the model to produce the answer. The "verification" is implicit: if the approximate tokens contain enough useful signal, the model's answer accuracy won't degrade; if they contain misleading signal, accuracy will suffer. The paper's empirical results (Figure 6) show that the net effect is positive or neutral for most model-dataset pairs, but the lack of explicit verification means there's no theoretical guarantee — it's an empirical bet.

### How the Paper Frames Its Contribution

The authors position FastCoT along three dimensions (Section 1, three enumerated contributions):

1. **Introduction of Jacobi decoding into CoT reasoning tasks**, specifically exploiting the by-product approximate tokens — previously discarded — as a "glimpse of the future" that can trigger early answer generation. This is a *conceptual reframing* more than a new algorithm: the same Jacobi machinery, applied with a different objective (answer-triggering rather than lossless decoding).

2. **Empirical demonstration of up to ~20% wall-clock speedup across four models and three datasets** with accuracy degradation held within 3%. The paper explicitly frames this as "the first study to accelerate inference of CoT tasks with inaccurate reasoning steps" — a claim that appears to hold based on the related work survey.

3. **A batch-compatible parallel decoding framework** built on HuggingFace transformers that supports the variable-length KV cache handling required when different batch elements converge at different rates. This is an engineering contribution that makes the approach practically deployable.

The paper is careful to note that FastCoT is "model-agnostic" and requires "no training or modification" (Abstract) — it is a pure inference-time strategy that can be applied to any pretrained causal LM. This distinguishes it from speculative decoding (which needs a draft model), blockwise parallel decoding (which needs architectural changes), and non-autoregressive methods (which need specialized training). The tradeoff, which the paper is explicit about, is that FastCoT is *not lossless* — the outputs may differ from what the model would produce under standard autoregressive decoding. The claim is that for CoT reasoning specifically, this is an acceptable trade because the intermediate rationale tokens are means to an end (the final answer), and approximate means can be sufficient.

### Summary of the Gap This Paper Fills

- **Prior work on CoT accuracy:** Extensively studied, but ignored the inference speed cost.
- **Prior work on decoding speed:** Extensively studied, but required lossless output reproduction and treated imperfect intermediate tokens as worthless.
- **Prior work on Jacobi decoding:** Applied to translation with a lossless constraint, never to reasoning, and never leveraging by-product tokens.
- **The gap:** No prior work asked whether approximate reasoning tokens — tokens that are "on the right track" but not identical to what full autoregressive generation would produce — could accelerate CoT inference by triggering early answer generation, accepting a small accuracy tradeoff in exchange for significant latency reduction.
- **FastCoT's position:** It fills this gap by repurposing Jacobi decoding's normally-discarded intermediate tokens as a "glimpse of the future," demonstrating empirically that this glimpse is sufficient on reasoning benchmarks where corrupted-rationale experiments show that complete rationales are informationally redundant.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

FastCoT is a pure inference-time framework that overlays Jacobi decoding on top of standard autoregressive decoding, then repurposes the normally-discarded approximate tokens from Jacobi's iterative process as partial reasoning rationale. The problem it solves is the latency penalty of Chain-of-Thought — hundreds of rationale tokens generated serially before the model even begins producing the answer — and the shape of the solution is to let the model "peek ahead" at blurry, work-in-progress reasoning tokens that emerge during parallel Jacobi iteration, then trigger the answer early before the full rationale is complete.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The FastCoT system has five interconnected components that operate in a closed loop:

1. **Approximate Tokens Buffer (ATB):** A persistent array holding the complete token sequence — both verified exact tokens and unverified approximate tokens — for each batch element. It acts as the working memory that evolves across iterations, initialized with the tokenized question itself.

2. **Jacobi Decoder Core:** The language model forward pass, configured to predict all tokens within a fixed-length context window in parallel (using causal masking). It consumes the prompt, exact tokens from the ATB, and the previous iteration's approximate tokens as input, and produces new predictions for every position in the window.

3. **Verification Module:** A comparison step that checks which of the newly-predicted tokens match the previous iteration's predictions at the same positions. The first contiguous segment of matching tokens from the start of the context window onward is declared "exact" and accepted; everything after the first mismatch remains "approximate."

4. **Answer Trigger:** A fixed text suffix (in the experiments, `"So the answer is"`) appended after the ATB contents when the iteration stop condition fires. It instructs the model to bypass further rationale generation and produce only the final answer.

5. **Stop Condition Controller:** Logic that terminates the Jacobi iteration loop based on either a pre-calibrated iteration budget derived from a validation subset, or detection of an end-of-sentence token in the exact segment.

The information flow is: *prompt construction* → *ATB initialization with the question* → *iterative loop of [LLM forward pass → verification → cache update → stop check]* → *answer triggering* → *final answer generation*. Within each iteration, the LLM simultaneously generates one more exact token (via the verified prefix extension) and `$c$` approximate lookahead tokens (via the Jacobi context window). The approximate tokens in the ATB get overwritten with better estimates each iteration, gradually converging toward the autoregressive trajectory while also serving as the glimpse-of-future context for early answer triggering.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the Jacobi decoding mechanism itself** (Section 3.1) — the mathematical formulation, what context windows are, and the critical cost-up-to-a-point-insensitivity property shown in Figure 2. This is the engine that generates approximate tokens.
- **Second, the Approximate Tokens Buffer** — its initialization strategy, its two-part structure (exact prefix + approximate suffix), and how it evolves across iterations. This is the state that the system maintains.
- **Third, the main iteration loop** — the FastCoT inference step, the verification rule, and the KV cache update logic. This is where the two decoding modes (lossless and approximate) are integrated.
- **Fourth, the answer triggering mechanism and stop conditions** — how FastCoT decides when to stop iterating and how it converts the accumulated approximate tokens into a final answer. This is where the accuracy-speedup tradeoff is concretely controlled.
- **Fifth, the batch compatibility mechanisms** (Type1 and Type2 padding) — how variable-length sequences within a batch are handled when different instances converge at different rates.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems paper** whose core idea is that approximate Jacobi tokens — which are normally discarded as verification failures — contain enough semantic signal in CoT reasoning tasks to trigger the final answer before the full rationale is autoregressively generated, trading a small accuracy loss for a significant latency reduction.

#### Jacobi Decoding as a Token Generation Engine

Jacobi decoding is the underlying generation mechanism that FastCoT repurposes. To understand what FastCoT does, you must first understand what Jacobi decoding computes and what it produces as side-effects.

The standard autoregressive greedy decoding rule for a causal language model with parameters `$\theta$`, given a prompt `$x$` and previously generated tokens `$y_{1:i-1}$`, selects the next token by taking the argmax over the model's output distribution:

$$y_i \leftarrow \arg\max \ p_\theta(y_i \mid y_{1:i-1}, x)$$

where `$y_i$` is the token at position `$i$`, `$p_\theta(y_i \mid y_{1:i-1}, x)$` is the model's predicted probability for each token in the vocabulary conditioned on the prompt and all preceding tokens, and `$\arg\max$` selects the token with highest probability. This generates exactly one token per forward pass, and each forward pass depends on the output of the previous one.

Jacobi decoding breaks this sequential dependency by predicting multiple future tokens in a single forward pass, using a **context window** of size `$c$`. The key insight is that causal language models can predict token at position `$i+k$` even if positions `$i$` through `$i+k-1$` are filled with *guesses* rather than verified tokens — the causal attention mask simply treats those guesses as if they were real tokens, and the model produces a distribution for the next position conditioned on them. Over multiple iterations, these guesses converge toward the autoregressive ground truth.

Formally, one iteration of Jacobi decoding at position `$i$` with context window size `$c$` and previous iteration's guesses `$\widehat{Y}^{t-1}$` produces:

$$
\begin{cases}
y_i &\leftarrow \arg\max \ p_\theta(y_i \mid x, y_{1:i-1}) \\[4pt]
\widehat{Y}^{t}_{i+1} &\leftarrow \arg\max \ p_\theta(y_{i+1} \mid x, y_{1:i-1}, \widehat{Y}^{t-1}_{i}) \\[4pt]
\widehat{Y}^{t}_{i+2} &\leftarrow \arg\max \ p_\theta(y_{i+2} \mid x, y_{1:i-1}, \widehat{Y}^{t-1}_{i:i+1}) \\[4pt]
&\ \ \vdots \\[4pt]
\widehat{Y}^{t}_{i+c} &\leftarrow \arg\max \ p_\theta(y_{i+c} \mid x, y_{1:i-1}, \widehat{Y}^{t-1}_{i:i+c-1})
\end{cases}
$$

where `$y_i$` is the exact token at the current decoding position (produced from verified context only), `$\widehat{Y}^{t}_{i+k}$` for `$k = 1, \ldots, c$` are the Jacobi approximations for future positions, each conditioned on the model's previous guess `$\widehat{Y}^{t-1}$` at the preceding positions, `$t$` is the iteration index, and `$c$` is the context window size — the number of lookahead positions being predicted in parallel.

The authors provide a compact notation for this operation, which is useful for following the algorithm:

$$y_i, \widehat{Y}^{t} \leftarrow \text{JD}(x, y_{<i}, \widehat{Y}^{t-1}, i, c)$$

where `$x$` is the prompt, `$y_{<i}$` are all exact tokens before position `$i$`, `$\widehat{Y}^{t-1}$` is the previous iteration's guess array (of length `$c$`), `$i$` is the starting position, and `$c$` is the window size. The output `$y_i$` is one new exact token, and `$\widehat{Y}^{t}$` is an array of `$c$` approximate tokens — the current iteration's best guesses for the next `$c$` positions.

**Why this form works:** The causal attention mask in transformer language models means that position `$j$` can only attend to positions `$< j$`. When the model processes position `$i+1$`, it attends to the prompt `$x$`, the verified tokens `$y_{1:i-1}$`, and the guess token `$\widehat{Y}^{t-1}_i$` at position `$i$`. The guess might be wrong, but the model still produces a *conditional* distribution — what token would be most likely at `$i+1$` *if* the guess at `$i$` were correct. Over iterations, as the guesses improve, these conditional distributions approach the unconditional distributions that autoregressive decoding would produce. This is mathematically a fixed-point iteration: the token sequence is a fixed point of the greedy decoding function `$f$`, and Jacobi iteration converges to that fixed point under certain conditions.

**What is computed:** In a single forward pass, the model produces `$c+1$` token predictions: one for the current exact position (based only on verified history) and `$c$` for future positions (based on verified history plus previous guesses). All of these are computed simultaneously because the causal mask is applied to the concatenated sequence `$[x, y_{<i}, \widehat{Y}^{t-1}]$`, and the model's final-layer logits at each position give the prediction for the next token. The forward pass cost is essentially the cost of processing a sequence of length `$|x| + (i-1) + c$`, which — critically — is only marginally more expensive than processing just the verified prefix when `$c$` is modest relative to the total sequence length.

This last point is crucial for the efficiency of the entire approach. The authors demonstrate in Figure 2 that increasing the context window size `$c$` from 0 (pure autoregressive) to 38 produces essentially no increase in forward-pass latency across prompt lengths of 400, 600, and 800 tokens on a Llama2-13B model with an A100 GPU. Forward time stays around 35–37ms for prompt length 400, and around 43–46ms for prompt length 800, regardless of `$c$`. The explanation is that "when the decoding length is comparatively limited, the consequent diminutive matrix blocks can not fully leverage the computational capabilities of CUDA and Tensor cores to their fullest extent" (Section 3.1). In plain language: GPUs are so over-provisioned for small decoding steps that adding a few dozen more tokens to the input costs almost nothing — the GPU was already underutilized, and the extra computation fills otherwise-idle capacity.

This is not a universal property — it holds only when the total sequence length (prompt + generated tokens + context window) is still within a regime where the GPU's matrix multiplication units are not saturated. The paper does not explore where this breaks down, but for the CoT settings studied (prompts of a few hundred tokens, generated sequences of a few hundred tokens, context windows up to ~40), the cost insensitivity holds. This is what makes Jacobi decoding viable for acceleration: you get `$c$` approximate tokens essentially for free alongside the one exact token.

#### The Approximate Tokens Buffer: State Representation

The Approximate Tokens Buffer (ATB) is the persistent state that FastCoT maintains across iterations. It is denoted `$Y$` and has a bipartite structure:

$$Y = [Y^{t}_{0:I}, \ \widehat{Y}^{t}_{I:}]$$

where `$Y^{t}_{0:I}$` represents the **exact segment**: a contiguous prefix of tokens from position 0 up to (but not including) position `$I$` that have passed verification and are guaranteed to match what standard autoregressive decoding would produce. `$\widehat{Y}^{t}_{I:}$` represents the **approximate segment**: a suffix of tokens starting at position `$I$` that are the model's current best guesses from Jacobi iteration but have not passed verification. The index `$I$` is a batch-sized vector of integers — one per batch element — indicating the boundary between exact and approximate tokens for each instance.

At initialization (`$t = 0$`), the authors set `$I = 0$` for all batch elements, meaning the entire ATB is initially approximate. They populate the ATB with the tokenized question text itself (rather than `[PAD]` tokens as in Santilli et al., 2023), based on "empirical results" showing this "would provide a more diverse initial solution for early stage's Jacobi decoding" (Section 4.2). This initialization choice matters because Jacobi decoding starts from a guess — the closer the initial guess is to the final tokens, the faster convergence occurs. Using the question as the initial guess provides semantically relevant tokens in the approximate positions rather than meaningless padding, giving the model a more informative starting point for its parallel predictions.

As iterations proceed, `$I$` monotonically increases (or stays the same) as tokens at the start of the approximate segment pass verification and join the exact segment. The ATB is **overwritten** each iteration — the new Jacobi predictions `$\widehat{Y}^{t+1}_{I+1:I+c+1}$` replace the old approximate tokens at positions `$I+1$` through `$I+c+1$` — while the exact segment `$Y^{t}_{0:I}$` is preserved and extended by however many tokens were verified in the current iteration.

#### The Iteration Loop: Inference, Verification, and Cache Update

Each iteration of FastCoT consists of three sequential steps: a combined inference pass, a verification step, and a cache update. These are depicted in Figure 4 and described in Section 4.3.

**Step 1: FastCoT Inference (Parallel Decoding).** The language model processes a concatenated input consisting of the prompt `$x$`, all exact tokens `$y_{<I}$` up to the verification boundary, and the previous iteration's approximate tokens `$\widehat{Y}^{t}_{I:I+c}$` (the content of the ATB's approximate suffix plus a window of size `$c$` beyond `$I$`). The model's forward pass produces logits at every position in the input, but only the predictions corresponding to the approximate suffix positions are used. The operation is:

$$y_I, \ \widehat{Y}^{t+1}_{I+1:I+c+1} \leftarrow \text{PD}(x, \ y_{<I}, \ \widehat{Y}^{t}_{I:I+c}, \ I, \ c)$$

where the notation `$\text{PD}$` (Parallel Decoding) is functionally identical to the Jacobi Decoding operation `$\text{JD}$` described earlier — it produces one exact token `$y_I$` at the boundary position and `$c$` new approximate tokens for the subsequent positions. The "parallel" in the name emphasizes that the exact and approximate predictions are computed simultaneously within the same causal forward pass, exploiting the fact that the causal attention mask naturally handles mixed-length inputs where later positions have longer visible prefixes.

**What physically happens:** The input token sequence is `$[x_1, \ldots, x_{|x|}, y_1, \ldots, y_{I-1}, \widehat{Y}^t_I, \widehat{Y}^t_{I+1}, \ldots, \widehat{Y}^t_{I+c}]$`. This sequence is embedded, passed through all transformer layers with causal masking (so position `$j$` attends only to positions `$\leq j$`), and the final-layer hidden states at positions `$|x|+I$` through `$|x|+I+c$` are projected through the language model head to produce logits. Taking argmax at each of these positions yields `$y_I$` and `$\widehat{Y}^{t+1}_{I+1}$` through `$\widehat{Y}^{t+1}_{I+c+1}$`. The computation at the exact position `$I$` uses only the verified history `$y_{<I}$` as context; the computations at subsequent positions condition on the previous positions' *guesses* from iteration `$t$`.

**Step 2: Verification.** The verification step determines how many of the newly predicted approximate tokens can be promoted to exact status. The rule is simple: compare the new tokens at positions `$I$` through `$I+K$` with the old tokens at the same positions, and accept the longest prefix that matches:

$$K \leftarrow \arg\max_{K} \ \left(Y^{t+1}_{I:I+K} = Y^{t}_{I:I+K}\right)$$

where `$Y^{t+1}_{I:I+K}$` is the sequence of `$K$` tokens from the new iteration starting at position `$I$`, `$Y^{t}_{I:I+K}$` is the sequence of `$K$` tokens from the previous iteration at the same positions, and the equality check is element-wise — every token in the new sequence must match the corresponding token in the old sequence. `$K$` is the largest integer such that this holds (i.e., the match extends unbroken from position `$I$` up to position `$I+K-1$`). The token at position `$I+K$` is the first mismatch — it changed between iterations — and is therefore still approximate.

**What this means:** If `$K = 1$`, only the single exact token `$y_I$` was verified (it matched the previous iteration's guess `$\widehat{Y}^t_I$`), and all subsequent tokens remain approximate. If `$K > 1$`, multiple tokens converged in a single iteration — the model's predictions stabilized across two consecutive iterations for `$K$` consecutive positions. This is where Jacobi decoding's speedup comes from: instead of advancing one token per forward pass, you advance `$K$` tokens.

**Why this verification rule works (and what it guarantees):** The rule checks for a fixed point of the Jacobi iteration at each position. If `$Y^{t+1}_{j} = Y^{t}_{j}$`, then position `$j$`'s prediction did not change from one iteration to the next, which means (under the greedy decoding assumption) that the token has converged to the autoregressive decoding output. The chain of equalities `$Y^{t+1}_{I:I+K} = Y^{t}_{I:I+K}$` ensures that the entire prefix up to `$I+K-1$` is at a fixed point. The strict contiguous requirement (break at the first mismatch) is conservative — a token could be correct even if it changed between iterations, but FastCoT opts for the guarantee that accepted tokens are stable.

**Step 3: Cache Update.** After verification, the ATB is updated:

- Tokens at positions `$I$` through `$I+K-1$` are promoted to exact status: they are moved from the approximate suffix to the exact prefix. The boundary index advances: `$I \leftarrow I + K$`.
- The new approximate tokens `$\widehat{Y}^{t+1}_{I+1:I+c+1}$` (the remaining predictions from the current forward pass that were not verified) are written into the ATB's approximate suffix positions, replacing the old approximate tokens.
- The position `$I$` token `$y_I$` — which was produced as an exact token but has now been verified — is included in the exact prefix.

The effect is that the exact prefix grows by `$K$` tokens per iteration, and the approximate suffix is refreshed with new predictions for the next `$c$` positions. The ATB always contains `$I$` exact tokens followed by `$c$` approximate tokens (except near the end of generation when the total sequence length approaches the model's maximum context length).

**KV Cache Management.** A practical complication arises because the transformer's key-value (KV) cache — which stores the attention keys and values for all previously processed tokens to avoid recomputation — must be updated to reflect which tokens are now exact and which are still approximate. Different batch elements will have different values of `$I$` (they converge at different rates), so their KV caches have different lengths of verified prefix. The authors call the procedure for extracting and preserving only the verified prefix's KV entries **Strip KV**, which "consumes 18.58 seconds (about 5.6% of total time)" in their Llama2-13B CSQA experiment (Table 2). This is a non-trivial cost — roughly 5.6% of total inference time — that is specific to the batch setting and not present in single-sequence Jacobi decoding.

#### Answer Triggering and Stop Conditions

The central innovation of FastCoT — and what distinguishes it from Santilli et al. (2023) — is that the approximate tokens in the ATB are not discarded when the iteration loop terminates. Instead, they are **appended to the prompt as partial rationale**, and the model is then asked to produce the final answer directly.

**Answer Trigger.** The answer trigger is a fixed text suffix that, when appended after the ATB contents, instructs the model to bypass further rationale tokens and produce only the answer. In the experiments (Section 5.1), the trigger is `"So the answer is"` — a natural-language prompt that appears in many few-shot CoT examples and signals to the model that the reasoning is complete and only the conclusion remains.

When the stop condition fires, FastCoT constructs the final input as:

```
[Original Prompt] + [ATB Exact Tokens] + [ATB Approximate Tokens] + [Answer Trigger]
```

and runs one additional autoregressive forward pass (or possibly a few) to generate the answer. The exact tokens in this input are guaranteed to match what autoregressive decoding would produce up to position `$I$`. The approximate tokens are the Jacobi guesses — they may contain errors, non-standard phrasing, or partially-formed thoughts, but they carry semantic signal about the reasoning direction. The answer trigger tells the model to interpret everything before it as rationale and to produce only the final answer.

**Why this might work (the glimpse-of-future hypothesis):** The approximate tokens are not random — they are the model's own best guesses, conditioned on the exact prefix, about what tokens should come next. In a reasoning task, the first few tokens of the rationale often establish key entities, frame the problem type, or set up the logical structure. Later tokens elaborate, verify, or hedge. If the approximate tokens capture enough of this early structure — even imperfectly — the model may have sufficient context to select the correct answer without needing the full, precisely-phrased rationale. The corruption experiments in Section 5.2 (Figure 5) provide empirical backing: on StrategyQA, accuracy saturates when only 40% of the original autogressive rationale tokens are visible, with the remaining 60% replaced by `[PAD]`. The approximate tokens are likely *better* than `[PAD]` — they are, after all, the model's best predictions at those positions — but they are worse than the exact autoregressive tokens. The empirical question is whether they lie above the saturation threshold.

**Stop Conditions.** FastCoT needs to decide when to stop iterating and trigger the answer. The authors design two complementary conditions (Section 4.4), either of which terminates the loop:

1. **Pre-calibrated iteration budget:** For each model-dataset pair, the authors randomly select a small subset `$S_{\text{cal}}$` of the dataset as a calibration set. They run the CoT task on this subset and measure, for each iteration count, what the performance loss is relative to full autoregressive decoding. They then determine the minimum number of iterations required to keep the performance loss below a chosen threshold. This minimum becomes the iteration budget for the full test set. The calibration process amortizes the cost of determining the budget across the entire dataset (it is done once per model-dataset pair, not per question). The paper does not specify the exact calibration subset size or what performance loss threshold was used in their main experiments — these are important parameters that directly control the speedup-accuracy tradeoff.

2. **End-of-Sentence (EOS) detection:** If the exact token `$y_I$` produced during Jacobi inference is the EOS token, and this EOS has been verified (i.e., it appears in the verified prefix), generation is complete and the answer trigger fires. This condition handles cases where the model naturally terminates its rationale before the iteration budget is exhausted, preventing unnecessary additional forward passes.

The two conditions are combined with an OR: stop if *either* the iteration budget is reached *or* EOS is detected in the verified segment. This provides both a safety bound (the budget prevents unbounded iteration) and an early-exit optimization (EOS detection saves compute on shorter rationales).

**Design choice — why a fixed budget rather than a convergence criterion?:** The authors could have used a convergence-based stop condition — e.g., stop when the approximate tokens stop changing (all `$c$` tokens are at a fixed point). This would ensure lossless decoding relative to the full Jacobi fixed point. They chose instead to use a pre-calibrated budget because their goal is explicitly to stop *before* full convergence — to exploit the partial, approximate tokens as a rationale shortcut. A convergence-based condition would defeat the purpose: by the time all tokens converge, the full rationale has been generated. The budget-based approach lets them stop at an intermediate point where the approximate tokens are "good enough" for answer triggering even though they haven't fully stabilized. The calibration step quantifies how early they can stop while keeping the accuracy degradation within acceptable bounds.

**The accuracy-speedup tradeoff is encoded in the calibration threshold.** A tighter threshold (e.g., 1% performance loss) would yield a larger iteration budget and less speedup. A looser threshold (e.g., 5% performance loss) would yield a smaller budget and more speedup. The paper reports experiments with a threshold that keeps performance loss "within 3%" (Abstract), achieved with speedups of 3–22% depending on the model-dataset pair (Table 1). The calibration procedure makes this tradeoff explicit and controllable.

#### Batch Compatibility: Two Padding Mechanisms

A significant practical contribution of FastCoT is its support for batched inference with variable-length generated sequences. In the batch setting, different instances converge at different rates — some may have `$I = 47$` exact tokens while others have `$I = 12$`. This creates two problems:

1. **The KV caches have different lengths of verified prefix**, so they cannot be naively stacked into a single tensor for the next forward pass.
2. **The Jacobi context windows may contain different numbers of approximate tokens** because some instances have more unconverged tokens remaining than others.

The authors design two padding mechanisms to handle these mismatches, illustrated in Figure 7:

**Type 1 Padding (KV Cache Length Padding):** When stacking the past KV caches from different batch elements, the verified prefix lengths differ. Type 1 Padding pads the shorter KV caches with zeros (or a designated padding value) so that all caches have the same sequence length dimension. The padded positions are masked out during attention computation so they don't affect the output. The padding is applied on the **left** side of the sequence (or equivalently, the shorter sequences are padded to match the longest verified prefix). This ensures the batched KV cache tensor has a uniform shape while preserving the alignment of token positions — position `$j$` in one instance's cache corresponds to position `$j$` in another's, even if the tokens at those positions differ in verification status.

**Type 2 Padding (Jacobi Window Length Padding):** Within a batch, different instances may have different numbers of approximate tokens remaining in the Jacobi context window because their verification boundaries `$I$` differ. Type 2 Padding pads the approximate token sequences so that all instances have exactly `$c$` approximate tokens in the window. The padding tokens are inserted at the end of each instance's approximate token sequence. Again, these are masked during attention.

Both padding operations add overhead — 5.33 seconds for Type 1 and 0.84 seconds for Type 2 in the Llama2-13B CSQA experiment (Table 2) — but these costs are small compared to the inference time saved (38.27 seconds saved out of 365.20 seconds total, a 10.47% net reduction). The combined padding overhead (~6.2 seconds) is about 1.9% of the total autoregressive inference time, making it a worthwhile overhead for enabling batched Jacobi decoding.

**The Strip KV operation.** After each iteration, the verified prefix grows by `$K$` tokens for each batch element. The KV cache entries corresponding to the now-verified tokens need to be separated from the still-approximate tokens so they can be preserved across iterations. The Strip KV operation extracts the exact prefix's KV cache entries (from position 0 to `$I+K$`) and retains them, discarding the KV entries for the approximate suffix which will be recomputed in the next iteration from the updated approximate tokens. This extraction and re-shaping operation costs 18.58 seconds in the Llama2-13B CSQA experiment, which is the single largest non-inference overhead in FastCoT (about 5.6% of total time). The authors do not provide implementation details, but the operation likely involves slicing the KV cache tensors along the sequence dimension and possibly re-arranging memory for efficient access in the next forward pass.

#### Context Window Size Selection and Its Effect on Performance

The context window size `$c$` is a critical hyperparameter that must be set before inference. The paper investigates its effect on downstream CoT accuracy through the iteration process in an experiment shown in Figure 8 (Section 5.5), using Llama2-13B on CSQA.

The findings reveal a **non-monotonic relationship between window size and accuracy** that evolves as iterations progress:

- **Early iterations (0–20):** Larger context windows correlate with *worse* performance. The authors attribute this to the fact that "at the beginning of the iterations, the tokens decoded by Jacobi decoding may not be highly relevant to the question and can even have a negative impact" (Section 5.5). In the first few iterations, the approximate tokens are essentially random guesses (initialized from the question text, not from reasoning), and having more of these low-quality guesses in the context window dilutes the signal from the exact prefix.

- **Mid iterations (10–65):** The context window size begins to have a positive effect, with larger windows producing better accuracy. At iteration 20, the curves for different `$c$` values have separated, with `$c=20$` and `$c=25$` outperforming `$c=0$` (no context window, equivalent to FastCoT without by-products) by a visible margin. The approximate tokens improve in quality as the Jacobi iteration converges, and having more of them provides more reasoning context for the answer trigger.

- **Late iterations (65+):** The performance gap between different window sizes "gradually diminishes" (Section 5.5), with all curves converging toward the full autoregressive CoT accuracy (around 64–66% for this model-dataset pair). By this point, the Jacobi iteration has nearly converged, the approximate tokens are close to their final values, and the marginal benefit of seeing more of them shrinks.

This pattern has an important implication for FastCoT's design: **the optimal window size depends on when you plan to stop iterating.** For aggressive early stopping (small iteration budget), a smaller window is better because the approximate tokens are noisy and a large window amplifies the noise. For more conservative late stopping (larger budget), a larger window helps because the approximate tokens are higher quality and provide more useful context. The paper does not propose an adaptive mechanism for adjusting `$c$` during iteration — it is kept fixed — but the authors suggest in Section 7 (Limitations) that "the process of controlling the context window throughout the iteration can be seen as a Markov Decision Process" and that reinforcement learning could be used to dynamically adjust `$c$`. This is flagged as future work.

**Practical choice of `$c$`:** The paper uses a fixed context window throughout each experiment but does not specify the exact value used for each model-dataset pair in the main results (Table 1). Figure 8 sweeps `$c \in \{0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25\}$` on CSQA with Llama2-13B. The context window sizes tested are modest — 5 to 25 tokens — which aligns with the cost-insensitivity result from Figure 2 (which tested up to 38 tokens). The window size does not need to cover the entire rationale; it only needs to provide enough lookahead that the approximate tokens capture key reasoning elements. Even 20–25 tokens likely covers 1–2 sentences of reasoning, which the corruption experiments suggest may be sufficient.

#### Design Choices: Why This Approach Over Alternatives

**Why Jacobi decoding rather than speculative decoding?** Speculative decoding requires a separate draft model that must be trained or selected to produce proposals that the large model will frequently accept. This introduces a model dependency and a quality-speed tradeoff in draft model selection that FastCoT avoids. Jacobi decoding uses the *same* model for both the exact and approximate tokens — there is no draft model to train, no additional memory footprint, and no distribution mismatch between drafter and verifier. The cost is that Jacobi's approximate tokens are less likely to be correct than a good draft model's proposals, but for the FastCoT use case (where tokens don't need to be correct, just informative), this is acceptable.

**Why Jacobi decoding rather than blockwise parallel decoding?** Blockwise parallel decoding requires adding auxiliary prediction heads to the model architecture, which means modifying the model and retraining. FastCoT is explicitly designed to work with any pretrained causal LM "with almost no modification to the source code" (Section 1). Jacobi decoding only requires the standard language model forward pass with a specific input construction — no architectural changes, no fine-tuning.

**Why greedy Jacobi decoding rather than sampling?** The paper uses greedy argmax for both the exact and approximate tokens (Equations 1 and 2). Sampling (e.g., nucleus or top-k) would introduce stochasticity into the approximate tokens, which could either help (by providing more diverse glimpses) or hurt (by introducing noise). The paper does not explore sampling, sticking to the deterministic greedy formulation that makes the Jacobi fixed-point analysis well-defined.

**Why initialize the ATB with the question text rather than `[PAD]`?** The standard Jacobi decoding initialization from Santilli et al. (2023) uses a special padding token like `[PAD]`. The authors empirically found that using the question text provides "more diverse initial solutions for early stage's Jacobi decoding" (Section 4.2). This makes intuitive sense: the question text is semantically related to the expected rationale (it contains key entities, the question type, etc.), so using it as an initial guess for the approximate tokens gives the model a more relevant starting point than an empty/null token. The "diversity" likely refers to the fact that different questions produce different initial guesses, giving each question a tailored starting point rather than a uniform one.

**Why a fixed answer trigger rather than learning when to trigger?** The answer trigger (`"So the answer is"`) is a simple, deterministic rule that fires when the stop condition is met. The authors could have trained a classifier to decide when the ATB contains sufficient information, but this would introduce a learned component and additional complexity. The fixed trigger is model-agnostic and requires no training, consistent with the paper's design philosophy. The calibration procedure for setting the iteration budget serves as a lightweight, offline way to tune the triggering point without per-question decision-making.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Decoding Errors as Informational Resources

The fundamental conceptual move in this paper is not a new algorithm but a **reframing of what constitutes useful computation in language model decoding**. The dominant paradigm across all lossless decoding acceleration work — speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023), blockwise parallel decoding (Stern et al., 2018), and lossless Jacobi decoding (Santilli et al., 2023) — treats any token that does not match the autoregressive ground truth as a **failure to be discarded**. These methods are built around a strict binary: a token is either correct (matching what greedy AR decoding would produce) or it is waste. The entire engineering effort goes into maximizing the ratio of correct tokens to wasted computation, with zero attention paid to whether the "wasted" tokens contain any useful information.

The authors of FastCoT ask a question that seems obvious in retrospect but has no precedent in the decoding acceleration literature: **what if the "wrong" tokens are still useful for certain tasks?** Specifically, in Chain-of-Thought reasoning, the intermediate rationale tokens are scaffolding — they exist to guide the model toward the correct answer, not as an end product the user consumes. If a token is "wrong" relative to what greedy decoding would produce at that position, it might still contain the key semantic content — the right entity, the right relationship, the right problem framing — that the model needs to select the answer. The standard verification step in Jacobi decoding declares such tokens as verification failures and replaces them. FastCoT keeps them, appends them to the prompt, and asks the model to produce the answer directly.

This is a genuine **category shift** in how to think about decoding accuracy for reasoning tasks. It moves from a *process-oriented correctness* standard (does each token match what the model would produce under ideal conditions?) to an *outcome-oriented sufficiency* standard (do the tokens, taken together as a noisy signal, provide enough information to select the correct answer?). The paper does not merely claim this shift is possible — it validates it empirically through the corrupted-rationale experiments (Section 5.2, Figure 5), which demonstrate that replacing 60% of rationale tokens with `[PAD]` leaves accuracy essentially unchanged on StrategyQA. This diagnostic experiment is itself a conceptual contribution: it isolates the *information redundancy* property that makes the reframing viable, showing that the rationale contains substantial information that is unnecessary for answer selection.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond the specific Jacobi decoding mechanism. It opens the door to a class of **lossy inference acceleration methods** for reasoning tasks — approaches that deliberately trade token-level fidelity for latency reduction, accepting that imperfect reasoning tokens can be "good enough" for correct answers. This is a fundamentally different design space than the lossless paradigm, and FastCoT is the first paper to explicitly operate in it for CoT reasoning. The distinction between process-oriented and outcome-oriented correctness is also a useful diagnostic concept for future work: it predicts that the approach should work well on tasks where the rationale-to-answer mapping is robust to perturbation (as in commonsense QA where key entities anchor the reasoning) and poorly on tasks where precise logical derivations matter (as in multi-step arithmetic where a single wrong intermediate value cascades). The paper provides partial evidence for this prediction — the speedup-accuracy tradeoff varies across datasets (Table 1, with StrategyQA showing 5.13–21.29% speedup and AQuA showing 10.20–21.80% speedup) — but does not systematically characterize which task properties modulate the tradeoff, leaving this as an open question.

### Innovation 2: The Glimpse-of-Future Construct as a Test-Time Compute Allocation Strategy

Beyond the general reframing, the paper introduces a specific construct — the **glimpse of the future** — that operationalizes the idea of partial, approximate reasoning tokens as a mechanism for early answer triggering. This construct is more than a metaphor; it represents a particular strategy for allocating inference compute along a *depth-versus-width* axis in token generation.

In standard autoregressive decoding, compute is allocated purely along the depth dimension: one token is generated per forward pass, and the model must traverse the full token sequence depth before producing the answer. Speculative decoding and Jacobi decoding add width — generating multiple tokens per forward pass — but with the constraint that only correct tokens count, so the width expansion is limited by verification accuracy. FastCoT decouples width from correctness: it generates wide (multiple approximate tokens per forward pass via Jacobi's context window) and then **uses that width to reduce depth** — the approximate tokens from early iterations serve as a preview of future reasoning steps, allowing the model to jump to the answer before traversing the full rationale depth.

This depth-width tradeoff is a distinct computational strategy from both pure depth (AR decoding) and verified-width (speculative decoding). It can be understood as a form of **test-time compute allocation where approximate computation substitutes for precise computation in a task-specific way**. The model invests a fixed compute budget (forward passes) into generating approximate future tokens that provide a coarse-grained reasoning trajectory, rather than spending that same budget on generating fewer but higher-quality tokens.

The innovation here is not the depth-width idea per se — non-autoregressive models have explored width-for-depth trading for years — but the recognition that **reasoning tasks specifically are well-suited to this tradeoff because of the information redundancy property**. Machine translation, the domain where lossless Jacobi decoding was applied, requires high token-level fidelity because the output *is* the product — a mistranslated word directly degrades quality. In CoT reasoning, the output is the answer, and the rationale is an intermediate representation that can tolerate substantial noise. This task-specificity is the key insight: the depth-width tradeoff is not universally attractive, but for tasks where the intermediate computation is informative but not user-visible, it becomes viable. The paper identifies CoT reasoning as a natural member of this class and provides the first demonstration that the tradeoff works in practice.

The figure-of-merit for this strategy is not tokens-per-second or FLOPs-per-token — the standard metrics for decoding speed — but **answer accuracy per unit wall-clock time**. This is a more holistic metric that captures the end-to-end value of the computation, and it is what makes the 20% speedup meaningful: the model produces correct answers 20% faster, even though some of the intermediate tokens are "wrong" by AR decoding standards.

### Innovation 3: Diagnosing CoT Rationale Redundancy as the Enabling Condition for Acceleration

A third contribution that is conceptually distinct from the FastCoT method itself is the **empirical diagnosis that CoT rationales contain substantial information redundancy relative to what is necessary for answer selection**. The corrupted-rationale experiment (Section 5.2, Figure 5) is a diagnostic tool that quantifies this redundancy, and the results are striking: on StrategyQA, accuracy saturates when only 40% of the original rationale tokens are visible; on CSQA, saturation occurs at roughly 60–70% visibility; on AQuA, the accuracy curve rises more gradually but still reaches near-saturation before full rationale visibility.

This finding is significant because it challenges an implicit assumption underlying the entire CoT literature: that the model *needs* to generate the full rationale to reason correctly. The CoT paradigm, from Wei et al. (2022) onward, treats the rationale as the mechanism of reasoning — the model "thinks step by step" by producing intermediate reasoning tokens, and the answer emerges from this process. If the rationale could be substantially compressed without losing answer accuracy, then the CoT mechanism is not operating exactly as the "step-by-step reasoning" framing suggests. The model may be using the rationale more as a **structured context-construction process** — building up a semantic representation that anchors the answer — rather than as a precise logical derivation where each step is load-bearing.

The paper does not fully develop this implication — it uses the redundancy finding pragmatically to motivate FastCoT — but the finding itself is a contribution that should influence how researchers think about CoT. It suggests that CoT's benefits may come more from establishing the right conceptual framing and key entity relationships early in the rationale, with later tokens providing diminishing returns (elaboration, hedging, verification of already-established points). If this interpretation is correct, it has implications for prompt engineering (maybe shorter, denser CoT examples work as well as long ones?), for rationale distillation (what parts of the rationale should be prioritized in training student models?), and for understanding failure modes (when does CoT reasoning break down — when early framing is wrong, or when later verification steps fail?).

The paper also makes a methodological contribution through the corruption experiment design itself. By corrupting rationales in two distinct patterns — sequential masking from the end (to simulate partial generation) and random uniform masking (to simulate Jacobi-style scattered errors) — the experiment disentangles the effects of *rationale length* from *rationale completeness*. The finding that both masking patterns produce similar saturation curves (the paper states the two patterns occur "with equal likelihood" and the results are aggregated, which unfortunately obscures any differences between them) strengthens the claim that it is the amount of information, not the specific pattern of missing tokens, that drives the redundancy.

The limitation of this diagnostic contribution is that it is purely correlational — it shows that the model *can* answer correctly with partial rationales, but does not establish *why* or identify *which* tokens in the rationale are load-bearing. A finer-grained analysis (e.g., ablating specific semantic categories of tokens — entities vs. logical operators vs. hedges) would strengthen the diagnostic, but even in its current form, the redundancy finding serves as a necessary condition for FastCoT's approach and as a useful empirical fact for the broader CoT research community.

### Innovation 4: Demonstrating That Verifier-Free Approximate Decoding Is Viable for Reasoning

A fourth contribution, more systems-oriented than conceptual, is the demonstration that **Jacobi decoding can be productively applied to reasoning tasks without the verification guarantee that prior work considered essential**. Santilli et al. (2023) showed that Jacobi decoding works for machine translation, but their method was strictly lossless — the output matched greedy AR decoding exactly. The open question was whether Jacobi decoding could provide benefit in domains where *lossless* operation is not required, and specifically whether the approximate intermediate tokens — which Santilli et al. explicitly discarded — could be useful.

FastCoT answers this question affirmatively with a concrete, measured demonstration. The numbers in Table 1 are the first published evidence that Jacobi decoding's by-products carry task-relevant information in reasoning domains. The speedups range from 3.20% (Llama-7B on StrategyQA) to 23.71% (Llama2-7B on CSQA), with performance loss held to 1.23–2.66%. These are modest but real improvements — not the 2–3× speedups that speculative decoding can achieve in some settings, but meaningful in a deployment context where every percentage point of latency reduction translates to user experience or throughput gains.

The significance of this demonstration is that it **establishes a lower bound on what is possible with approximate decoding in reasoning**. FastCoT is a relatively simple method — fixed context window, fixed answer trigger, pre-calibrated iteration budget — and yet it achieves consistent speedups across four models and three datasets. This suggests that more sophisticated approaches (adaptive window sizing, learned triggering, integration with speculative decoding, dynamic budget allocation) could yield substantially larger gains. The paper thus serves an enabling function for a research program on approximate decoding for reasoning, providing the first proof-of-concept that the approach is viable.

The time-composition analysis in Table 2 is an underappreciated contribution in this regard. By breaking down FastCoT's total time into inference, padding, decoding, context decoding, KV cache manipulation, and other overhead, the paper provides a diagnostic that identifies where the remaining bottlenecks are. The inference time drops by ~23% (274.08s vs. 358.15s for AR), but this gain is partially offset by the Strip KV overhead (18.58s, or 5.6% of total time) and the context decoding overhead (5.42s). This breakdown tells future researchers exactly where to focus optimization efforts: reducing KV cache manipulation cost (perhaps through more efficient memory management or by limiting the number of Strip operations) and reducing approximate token processing overhead. It also reveals that the core Jacobi mechanism — generating more tokens per forward pass — is working as intended (inference time drops substantially), and that the implementation overheads, not the algorithmic concept, are the primary limit on net speedup. This is a useful calibration for the research community: the approach has headroom, and engineering improvements can directly translate to larger speedups without algorithmic changes.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on three widely-used reasoning benchmarks: **CSQA** (CommonsenseQA; Talmor et al., 2018) — a commonsense question-answering dataset, **StrategyQA** (Geva et al., 2021) — an implicit reasoning benchmark, and **AQuA** (Algebra Question Answering; Ling et al., 2017) — a dataset of algebraic word problems requiring multi-step mathematical reasoning. All experiments use the official test split for each dataset. The CSQA dataset is used because commonsense reasoning represents one of the primary application domains for CoT prompting; StrategyQA requires implicit, multi-hop reasoning strategies that stress the model's ability to construct non-obvious rationales; AQuA tests mathematical reasoning where intermediate computation steps are essential. The paper does not report exact sizes for any of the test splits — this is a notable omission since dataset size directly affects the reliability of accuracy comparisons.

- **Base model(s).** Experiments are conducted on four LLaMA-family models: **Llama-7B**, **Llama-13B** (Touvron et al., 2023a), **Llama2-7B**, and **Llama2-13B** (Touvron et al., 2023b). These models span two generations of the LLaMA architecture and two parameter scales (7B and 13B), providing coverage of model quality improvements (Llama2 generally outperforms Llama1 on reasoning tasks) and scale effects. The authors do not provide an explicit justification for why these specific models were chosen beyond their availability as open-weight models, but the choice enables testing whether FastCoT's speedup-accuracy tradeoff generalizes across both model generations and scales. Notably absent are models at the 70B scale or models from other families (e.g., Mistral, Falcon, or instruction-tuned variants) — the results are confined to the LLaMA ecosystem at modest scales.

- **Metrics.** Two primary metrics are evaluated: **accuracy** and **efficiency**. Accuracy is "gauged by the proportion of correct responses" — the fraction of test questions for which the model's final answer (after answer triggering) matches the ground truth. Efficiency is "discerned through the metrics of wall-clock time and iteration count" (Section 5.1). Wall-clock time is measured as total inference time across the full test set and is the headline metric for speedup claims. Iteration count is used internally to understand where time savings originate — specifically, the "IS/TI" column in Table 1 reports (Iterations Saved / Total Iterations), which decomposes the speedup into the portion attributable to Jacobi decoding accepting multiple tokens per iteration versus the portion from early stopping via answer triggering. The paper does not report tokens-per-second, FLOP counts, or GPU utilization metrics, relying solely on wall-clock time as the practical endpoint metric. This is appropriate for a deployment-focused evaluation but limits the ability to compare FastCoT's efficiency to methods evaluated under different hardware configurations.

- **Baselines.** The paper defines three comparison methods (Section 5.1):

  1. **Vanilla CoT (AR):** Standard autoregressive decoding with Chain-of-Thought prompting. The model generates the full rationale token-by-token using greedy decoding, then produces the answer. This is the accuracy upper bound and the speed baseline — FastCoT's speedup is measured against this, and accuracy degradation is measured as the drop relative to this.

  2. **FastCoT(w/o by-products):** Autoregressive decoding with the generated rationale truncated to the same number of exact tokens that FastCoT would have produced at a given iteration count. The truncated rationale is then used (with the answer trigger) to generate the answer. This baseline is specifically designed to isolate the effect of the approximate tokens — it has the same exact token count and the same early-stopping iteration as FastCoT, but zero approximate tokens. Any accuracy difference between FastCoT and FastCoT(w/o by-products) is therefore attributable purely to the approximate tokens from the Jacobi context window. The authors explicitly call this "a very strong baseline" because it controls for the early-stopping effect, which confounds a naive comparison against full AR decoding.

  3. **Non-CoT:** Direct-answer prompting without any chain-of-thought rationale. This is shown as a horizontal reference line in Figure 6, representing the accuracy floor that CoT methods improve upon. It establishes the performance that would be achieved if no rationale were generated at all — the extreme case of zero test-time reasoning compute.

  The three baselines form a hierarchy: Non-CoT (no rationale, fast but inaccurate) < FastCoT(w/o by-products) (partial rationale, no approximate tokens) < FastCoT (partial rationale with approximate tokens) < Vanilla CoT (full rationale, slow but accurate). This hierarchy enables attribution of FastCoT's accuracy gains to either the partial exact rationale (shared with the w/o by-products baseline) or the approximate tokens (unique to FastCoT).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The unit of compute is **wall-clock time in seconds** across the full test set, measured on a server with a 32-core CPU, 64 GiB host memory, and a single Nvidia A100-SXM (80G) GPU (Section 5.1). The generation budget is not explicitly parameterized as a knob to sweep (unlike best-of-N or beam search analyses in other papers); instead, it is implicitly controlled through the **iteration budget** — the pre-calibrated number of Jacobi iterations after which the answer trigger fires. This budget is determined per model-dataset pair using a held-out calibration subset and is the primary mechanism for trading accuracy against speed. The paper does not report the calibration subset size, the exact performance loss threshold used for calibrating the budget, or sensitivity of results to different thresholds — these are significant omissions that make the speedup-accuracy tradeoff difficult to reproduce or tune. For the time-composition analysis (Table 2), compute is further decomposed into six categories: Inference, Type1 Padding, Type2 Padding, Decode, Context Decode, Strip KV, and Other, measured in seconds. This breakdown enables diagnosis of where FastCoT's overhead comes from and where the savings materialize.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation in the conventional sense. Instead, it uses a **calibration-based protocol** for setting the iteration budget (Section 4.4, condition 1). For each model-dataset pair, the authors "randomly select a small portion of it as S_cal and perform the CoT task within this subset for each iteration. We calculate the minimum number of iterations required to achieve a certain performance loss threshold and use this value as the upper bound for iterations." This calibration is performed once per model-dataset pair, and the resulting budget is applied uniformly to all questions in the test set. There is no per-question adaptation of the iteration budget, no reporting of variance across calibration runs, and no statistical significance testing on the accuracy differences between FastCoT and Vanilla CoT. The corruption experiment (Section 5.2) uses 100 distinct random seeds to generate different corruption patterns for the same question, but these results are aggregated into a single saturation curve with no error bars or confidence intervals reported.

### Main Quantitative Results

The experimental narrative is organized around answering four questions posed in Section 5.1: (Q1) whether approximate rationales help reasoning tasks get the correct answer, (Q2) whether FastCoT achieves fast inference speed, (Q3) what the time composition difference is between FastCoT and autoregressive decoding, and (Q4) how context window size influences downstream CoT task performance. The results are distributed across four subsections: the corruption experiments (Section 5.2), the performance analysis (Section 5.3), the time analysis (Sections 5.4.1 and 5.4.2), and the context window analysis (Section 5.5).

#### Corruption Experiments: Establishing That Complete Rationales Are Not Necessary

The corrupted-rationale experiment (Section 5.2) is a diagnostic that precedes and motivates the main FastCoT method. It asks: if we give the model only a partially intact rationale, does answer accuracy degrade, and at what point does it saturate?

The authors first ran Vanilla CoT on each dataset to generate full rationales and answers. For each question, they then created corrupted versions of the rationale by replacing a predefined fraction of tokens with `[PAD]` tokens, using two corruption patterns with equal probability: (1) sequential masking from the end of the rationale toward the beginning, simulating partial generation where only the first portion of the rationale is produced, and (2) random uniform masking across the full rationale, simulating scattered errors of the type Jacobi decoding might produce. For each question and each corruption ratio, 100 independent random seeds were used to generate different corrupted rationales. The corrupted rationale was then concatenated with the original prompt and fed to the same LLM to produce an answer.

**Figure 5 reports the key finding:** As more of the original rationale is revealed (moving left to right on the x-axis, which shows `1 - corruption_ratio`, i.e., the fraction of original tokens kept), accuracy increases and saturates before reaching 100% rationale visibility. The saturation points differ by dataset:

- **StrategyQA:** Accuracy saturates earliest, at approximately 40% rationale visibility (60% corruption). The normalized performance curve reaches near-maximum accuracy when only 40% of the original rationale tokens are present, and additional tokens beyond this point provide negligible benefit.
- **CSQA:** Saturation occurs at approximately 60–70% rationale visibility (30–40% corruption). The curve rises more gradually than StrategyQA's but plateaus before full visibility.
- **AQuA:** The accuracy curve rises more gradually across the full range, with less pronounced saturation. The paper does not report the exact saturation threshold for AQuA, but Figure 5 shows that accuracy continues to improve at higher overlap ratios more than on the other two datasets.

The interpretation the paper offers (Section 5.2) is that "a reasonable overlap ratio would lead to a saturated accuracy" — in other words, the rationale contains redundant information relative to what is strictly needed for correct answer selection. The earlier saturation on StrategyQA suggests that implicit reasoning tasks rely on fewer key tokens than commonsense or mathematical reasoning, which may require more complete intermediate computation.

**Critical detail on experimental design:** The two corruption patterns (sequential and random) are meant to simulate different types of information loss. Sequential masking from the end approximates what happens when FastCoT stops early — the model has generated the first portion of the rationale exactly and has approximate tokens (which may contain scattered errors) for the remainder. Random masking simulates Jacobi-style errors that are distributed throughout the rationale rather than concentrated at the end. However, the paper does not report separate saturation curves for the two patterns — they are aggregated — which prevents diagnosing whether the model is more sensitive to early-token loss (sequential masking) or scattered-token loss (random masking). This is a missed opportunity, since the two patterns have different implications for how FastCoT should allocate its approximate token budget.

#### Performance Analysis: Do Approximate Tokens Help Beyond Exact Tokens Alone?

The main performance experiment (Section 5.3, Figure 6) addresses Q1 and Q2 simultaneously by comparing FastCoT, FastCoT(w/o by-products), Vanilla CoT, and Non-CoT across iterations for each model-dataset pair.

**Figure 6 presents eight subplots** — two datasets (CSQA and StrategyQA) × four models (Llama2-13B, Llama2-7B, Llama-13B, Llama-7B). In each subplot, the y-axis shows accuracy (%), the x-axis shows the number of Jacobi iterations, and four curves are plotted: FastCoT (with approximate tokens), FastCoT(w/o by-products) (no approximate tokens, same exact token count), Vanilla CoT (horizontal reference line showing full AR decoding accuracy), and Non-CoT (horizontal reference line showing direct-answer accuracy). Vertical dashed lines mark the "converge point" — the iteration at which each method first reaches its asymptotic accuracy.

The key comparison is between **FastCoT and FastCoT(w/o by-products)** — any accuracy gap between these two curves at a given iteration count is attributable purely to the approximate tokens in FastCoT, since both methods have the same number of exact tokens.

**Results by model family (the pattern is not uniform):**

- **Llama-13B and Llama-7B (first-generation):** FastCoT "almost always performs better than FastCoT(w/o by-products)" on both CSQA and StrategyQA (Section 5.3). On Llama-13B with CSQA, FastCoT rises from approximately 30% at iteration 0 to roughly 58% by iteration 100–120, while FastCoT(w/o by-products) trails by 2–5 percentage points throughout. On Llama-7B with StrategyQA, FastCoT reaches roughly 28% at iteration 140, compared to roughly 22% for FastCoT(w/o by-products) — a 6-percentage-point gap. The approximate tokens provide a consistent, unambiguous benefit for these models.

- **Llama2-13B and Llama2-7B (second-generation):** The pattern is mixed. On Llama2-13B with CSQA, FastCoT and FastCoT(w/o by-products) trade off — FastCoT is higher at some iterations and lower at others — with both curves oscillating in the 45–60% range. On Llama2-13B with StrategyQA, both methods show a **decreasing accuracy trend** in the early-to-mid iteration range (roughly iterations 20–80), with FastCoT dropping from approximately 65% to around 57% before recovering. The authors acknowledge this pattern explicitly: "when the Llama2-13B model executes the StrategyQA question, whether it is FastCoT or FastCoT(w/o by-products), there is a phenomenon that the accuracy decreases as the number of precise rationale tokens increases within some iteration range." They attribute this to "the faithfulness of CoT" citing Lanham et al. (2023) and Radhakrishnan et al. (2023) — works that study whether CoT rationales accurately reflect the model's actual reasoning process — but provide no further analysis.

**The decreasing accuracy phenomenon on Llama2-13B StrategyQA** is the most surprising result in Figure 6 and deserves careful attention. It means that having more exact tokens (i.e., more of the correct, autoregressive-quality rationale) *reduces* accuracy compared to having fewer exact tokens plus some approximate tokens. This is counterintuitive: if the exact tokens are "correct" (matching AR decoding), how can having more of them hurt? The faithfulness interpretation suggests that the model's generated rationales may contain tokens that, while correct from a next-token-prediction standpoint, are misleading or contradictory for the downstream answer — a form of self-interference where the model's own reasoning text leads it astray. The approximate tokens, being "wrong" relative to AR decoding, might actually be *better* for answer triggering in these cases because they don't contain the specific misleading patterns. This is speculative based on the paper's discussion, but it highlights a genuine complexity: the "correctness" of rationale tokens by AR standards is not always aligned with their utility for answer selection.

**Convergence behavior:** The vertical dashed lines in Figure 6 mark the "converge point" for each method. On Llama-13B CSQA, FastCoT converges around iteration 100–120, while FastCoT(w/o by-products) converges later (around iteration 120–140). This pattern — FastCoT converging earlier than the no-by-products baseline — is consistent across most model-dataset pairs and is the direct evidence that approximate tokens provide information that accelerates the approach to asymptotic accuracy. The earlier convergence translates to the speedup: you can stop iterating sooner with FastCoT and still achieve accuracy comparable to what FastCoT(w/o by-products) would require more iterations to reach.

**Final accuracy at the converge point:** FastCoT's converged accuracy is close to but slightly below Vanilla CoT accuracy in most cases. On Llama2-13B StrategyQA, Vanilla CoT achieves roughly 65–66% while FastCoT converges to approximately 63–64%. On Llama-13B CSQA, Vanilla CoT achieves approximately 58–60% and FastCoT converges to roughly 56–58%. The accuracy gap is typically 1–3 percentage points, consistent with the "performance drop within 3%" claim in the Abstract. The paper does not report these gaps numerically in the text — they must be read from Figure 6 — which makes precise comparison difficult.

**What Figure 6 does NOT show:** The AQuA dataset is absent from Figure 6, despite being used in the time analysis (Table 1). The authors do not explain this omission. AQuA is the most mathematical of the three datasets and might show different behavior (e.g., more sensitivity to rationale completeness) given its multi-step arithmetic requirements. Its absence from the main performance plot is a gap in the experimental coverage.

#### Time Analysis: How Much Speedup, and Where Does It Come From?

The time analysis is presented in two parts: aggregate wall-clock measurements (Section 5.4.1, Table 1) and a time-composition breakdown comparing FastCoT to AR decoding (Section 5.4.2, Table 2).

**Table 1 reports wall-clock time and speedup across all four models and three datasets.** Each row corresponds to one model-dataset pair, with columns for FastCoT total time (seconds), Vanilla CoT total time (seconds), time saved (seconds = CoT Time – FastCoT Time), time ratio (percentage = Time Saved / CoT Time), performance loss (PL, percentage), and IS/TI (iterations saved / total iterations).

Key results from Table 1:

- **Speedup range:** The worst speedup is 3.20% on Llama-7B with StrategyQA (293.72s vs. 303.05s). The best speedup is 23.71% on Llama2-7B with CSQA (184.98s vs. 242.47s). Most configurations fall in the 8–22% range.

- **Performance loss:** Ranges from 1.23% (Llama-7B on CSQA) to 2.66% (Llama2-13B on AQuA). On Llama-13B with AQuA, the speedup is 21.80% with a 2.50% performance loss — a notably favorable tradeoff. On Llama-7B with CSQA, the speedup is only 3.64% with 1.23% performance loss — a less favorable tradeoff where the speedup is modest.

- **Model scale effects:** There is no clear scaling law within the 7B–13B range. Llama2-7B achieves the best speedup (23.71% on CSQA), but Llama2-13B achieves only 10.47% on the same dataset. Llama-7B achieves the worst speedups (3.64% on CSQA, 3.20% on StrategyQA), but Llama-7B also shows modest speedups. The relationship between model scale, model quality, and FastCoT speedup appears non-monotonic and dataset-dependent.

- **Dataset effects:** AQuA consistently shows large absolute time savings (29.42s to 128.45s) and high speedup percentages (10.20–21.80%). StrategyQA shows the smallest savings (9.63s to 79.33s) and the widest range (3.20–21.29%). CSQA is intermediate. The pattern may reflect AQuA's longer rationales (more tokens to save via early stopping) versus StrategyQA's shorter, denser rationales (where approximate tokens need to be more precise).

- **IS/TI decomposition:** The "Iterations Saved / Total Iterations" column provides insight into the mechanism of speedup. For Llama2-13B on CSQA, 896 iterations were saved out of 14,684 total (6.1%). For Llama-13B on AQuA, 1,773 out of 21,674 (8.2%). These fractions represent the portion of speedup attributable to Jacobi decoding accepting multiple tokens per forward pass (the lossless component). The remainder of the speedup comes from early stopping — FastCoT simply runs fewer iterations than would be needed for full rationale generation. The paper does not report what fraction of total speedup comes from each mechanism, but the IS/TI numbers suggest that early stopping (the answer triggering) is the dominant contributor, not the per-iteration token savings from Jacobi convergence. This is consistent with FastCoT's design — the primary speedup comes from not generating the full rationale, not from generating it faster.

**Table 2 provides the time-composition breakdown** for a single configuration: Llama2-13B on CSQA (the configuration with 10.47% speedup). This table decomposes the total inference time into six operational categories for both FastCoT and AR decoding.

The key findings from Table 2:

- **Inference time is the dominant cost and the primary source of savings.** FastCoT's inference time (the GPU forward passes) is 274.08s, compared to 358.15s for AR decoding — a reduction of 84.07s, or approximately 23.5%. This is the core algorithmic gain: fewer forward passes are needed because Jacobi decoding advances multiple tokens per pass and early stopping eliminates the tail of rationale generation.

- **New overheads in FastCoT partially offset the inference savings.** FastCoT introduces five categories of overhead not present in AR decoding: Type1 Padding (5.33s), Type2 Padding (0.84s), Context Decode (5.42s), Strip KV (18.58s), and increased Other costs (21.48s vs. 3.99s). The total overhead is 51.65s (sum of FastCoT-specific costs above the AR equivalents). Subtracting this from the inference savings (84.07s) gives 32.42s — which accounts for most of the net speedup (38.27s). The slight discrepancy suggests additional small savings or overheads in categories not individually reported.

- **Strip KV is the single largest overhead (18.58s, 5.6% of total time).** This operation extracts and preserves the KV cache for the verified prefix, discarding the approximate suffix's cache entries so they can be recomputed in the next iteration. At 5.6% of total time, it is a substantial tax on the Jacobi approach. The paper does not detail the implementation, but the cost likely stems from slicing large KV cache tensors and possibly re-arranging memory for batched access.

- **AR Decoding's Overheads Are Minimal (3.99s total "Other").** The comparison makes clear that standard autoregressive decoding has very low implementation overhead — 98.9% of its time is spent in GPU inference. FastCoT, by contrast, spends 19.3% of its time on non-inference operations (52.85s out of 326.93s). This gap represents the engineering cost of the Jacobi decoding approach and is the primary reason the net speedup (10.47%) is less than half the inference time reduction (23.5%). Any future work that reduces these overheads could directly increase the net speedup without changing the underlying algorithm.

**What Table 2 does not report:** The paper does not provide time-composition breakdowns for any other model-dataset pair, so it's unknown whether the overhead proportions are consistent or vary substantially across configurations. The Llama2-13B CSQA configuration is representative of a mid-range setting (10.47% speedup), but configurations with higher speedups (e.g., 23.71% on Llama2-7B CSQA) may have different overhead profiles.

#### Context Window Size Analysis

The context window size experiment (Section 5.5, Figure 8) investigates Q4 by sweeping the context window size `c` from 0 to 25 in increments of 5, measuring accuracy as a function of iteration count for Llama2-13B on CSQA.

**Figure 8 shows six curves** — one for each window size — plotting accuracy (%) against iteration number (0 to approximately 65). The key pattern:

- **Early iterations (0–20):** Accuracy is inversely related to window size. At iteration 5, `c=0` achieves approximately 54% accuracy, while `c=25` achieves approximately 52% — a 2-percentage-point gap. The authors attribute this to low-quality approximate tokens in early iterations: "the tokens decoded by Jacobi decoding may not be highly relevant to the question and can even have a negative impact" (Section 5.5). A larger window amplifies this noise by exposing the answer-triggering step to more low-quality tokens.

- **Mid iterations (20–40):** The curves cross. Larger windows begin to outperform smaller windows. At iteration 30, `c=20` and `c=25` achieve approximately 57.5% accuracy, while `c=0` achieves approximately 55.5%. The approximate tokens have improved in quality through Jacobi convergence, and seeing more of them now provides useful reasoning context.

- **Late iterations (40–65):** The gap narrows. By iteration 65, all window sizes (including `c=0`) converge to approximately 62–64% accuracy, with no clear ordering. The Jacobi iteration has largely converged, the approximate tokens are close to their final values, and the marginal benefit of additional approximate tokens diminishes.

- **Window sizes above 20:** The authors note "there is no significant difference observed for context windows larger than 20" (Section 5.5). The curves for `c=20` and `c=25` are nearly indistinguishable across all iterations, suggesting a saturation effect — beyond ~20 tokens, additional approximate tokens in the context window provide negligible additional information for the CoT task.

The practical implication is that the context window size needs to be chosen in conjunction with the iteration budget. For aggressive early stopping (small budget), a small window (e.g., `c=5–10`) is preferable to minimize noise. For later stopping (larger budget), a larger window (e.g., `c=15–20`) is better to maximize useful context. The paper does not implement adaptive window sizing — it uses a fixed window across iterations — but the results in Figure 8 motivate exactly this kind of dynamic control, which the authors flag as future work in Section 7.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper does not contain a dedicated ablation section with systematically varied components. However, several experiments serve ablation-like functions by isolating specific aspects of the method:

- **Approximate tokens vs. exact tokens only (FastCoT vs. FastCoT(w/o by-products)):** This is the central ablation in the paper, presented in Figure 6 across all model-dataset configurations. It isolates the effect of the approximate Jacobi tokens by controlling for the number of exact tokens and the early-stopping iteration. The finding is that approximate tokens provide a consistent accuracy benefit on Llama-7B and Llama-13B but a mixed/negligible benefit on Llama2-7B and Llama2-13B. This suggests the benefit is model-dependent — first-generation LLaMA models rely more on the approximate token context than second-generation models — but the paper does not investigate why.

- **Context window size (Figure 8):** Sweeps `c ∈ {0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25}` on Llama2-13B CSQA. Key finding: window size matters most in mid-iteration range, with early iterations favoring small windows and later iterations favoring larger windows. The convergence of curves after ~65 iterations indicates that window size effects are transient.

- **Corruption pattern (sequential vs. random masking):** The corruption experiment (Section 5.2) uses two masking patterns with equal probability to simulate different types of information loss. The paper aggregates the results into a single curve (Figure 5), so the effect of corruption pattern is not separately reported. This is technically an ablation of the corruption type, but the aggregation prevents any conclusion about whether one pattern is more harmful than the other.

- **Model scale and generation:** Four models at two scales (7B and 13B) across two generations (Llama1 and Llama2) are tested. This provides ablation-like evidence about the effect of model scale and model quality. The finding is that FastCoT's benefits are not monotonic in scale — Llama2-7B shows the best speedup while Llama2-13B shows among the worst for the same dataset — suggesting that model-specific factors (perhaps reasoning style, rationale verbosity, or self-consistency of generated tokens) are more important than raw parameter count.

- **Dataset domain:** Three datasets (CSQA, StrategyQA, AQuA) covering commonsense, implicit reasoning, and mathematical reasoning serve as an ablation of task type. The corruption experiment (Figure 5) shows that StrategyQA saturates earliest (least rationale needed), AQuA saturates latest (most rationale needed), and CSQA falls in between. This pattern aligns with intuition: mathematical reasoning (AQuA) likely requires more complete intermediate computation steps, while implicit reasoning (StrategyQA) can succeed with fewer anchor tokens.

**What is notably missing as an ablation:**

- **Answer trigger format:** The paper uses exactly one answer trigger (`"So the answer is"`). There is no ablation of different trigger formats, trigger lengths, or trigger positions relative to the approximate tokens. The sensitivity of the method to the trigger design is unknown.

- **ATB initialization strategy:** The paper mentions that initializing the ATB with the question text works better than `[PAD]` based on "empirical results," but these results are not reported. There is no comparison of different initialization strategies (question text vs. padding vs. random tokens vs. learned initialization).

- **Greedy vs. sampling for Jacobi tokens:** The paper uses greedy argmax for both exact and approximate tokens. There is no comparison to sampling-based Jacobi decoding (e.g., nucleus or top-k sampling for approximate tokens), which might produce more diverse glimpses.

- **Iteration budget calibration threshold:** The speedup-accuracy tradeoff is controlled by the performance loss threshold used in calibration, but no sweep of this threshold is reported. The paper does not show how speedup and accuracy vary as the threshold is tightened or loosened, which would characterize the Pareto frontier of the method.

- **Batch size effects:** All experiments use a single A100 GPU, but the batch size is not reported. The time-composition analysis (Table 2) includes padding overheads that depend on batch size, but there is no sweep of batch size to understand how the overhead scales.

### Critical Assessment

The experimental evidence supports the paper's central claims, but with significant qualifications and several gaps that prevent a complete assessment of FastCoT's robustness and generality.

**Claim 1: FastCoT accelerates CoT inference by nearly 20% with negligible performance drop.** The evidence in Table 1 supports this claim for most configurations: speedups range from 3.20% to 23.71%, with 9 of 12 model-dataset pairs achieving speedups of 8.92% or higher. Performance loss is within 3% for all configurations (range: 1.23–2.66%). However, the claim is empirically supported only at the level of aggregate wall-clock time — the paper does not demonstrate that these speedups hold at the per-query level (some queries may speed up dramatically while others slow down), does not report variance in speedup across the test set, and does not characterize the distribution of speedups. The 20% figure is an approximate ceiling, not a typical-case result — the average speedup across configurations is approximately 12.6%. Additionally, the speedup is measured against Vanilla CoT, which is the appropriate baseline, but it does not account for the calibration cost (computing the iteration budget on S_cal) or the difficulty of determining the budget for a new model-dataset pair. In a deployment setting where the calibration cost must be amortized, the effective speedup would be lower.

**Claim 2: Approximate tokens from Jacobi decoding act as a "glimpse of the future" that improves accuracy compared to using only exact tokens at the same iteration count.** The evidence in Figure 6 partially supports this claim but reveals important heterogeneity. On Llama-7B and Llama-13B (first-generation LLaMA), FastCoT consistently outperforms FastCoT(w/o by-products), with accuracy gaps of 2–6 percentage points in mid-iteration ranges. On Llama2-7B and Llama2-13B, the gap is smaller, sometimes negative, and often oscillates across iterations. The paper does not explain this discrepancy, which undermines the universality of the claim. Possible explanations include: Llama2 models were trained with different data or objectives that changed the self-consistency of their Jacobi iterations (affecting approximate token quality), or Llama2 models produce rationales with different information density (affecting how much the approximate tokens add beyond the exact prefix). Without investigating this, the claim that approximate tokens are generally beneficial remains qualified: they help on first-generation LLaMA models, and their benefit on Llama2 is inconsistent and model-dataset-dependent.

**Claim 3: Complete rationales are not necessary for CoT reasoning — partial information suffices.** The corruption experiments in Figure 5 provide compelling evidence for this claim, but with important caveats. First, the corruption uses `[PAD]` tokens rather than approximate model-generated tokens — `[PAD]` is a clean "no information" signal, while approximate Jacobi tokens carry semantic content that may be partially correct, partially misleading. The experiment demonstrates that *missing* information is tolerable up to a point; it does not demonstrate that *incorrect* information (which approximate tokens can contain) is tolerable. The FastCoT setting involves approximate tokens that may contain wrong entities, incorrect logical relationships, or contradictory statements — a more challenging condition than simply missing tokens. Second, the saturation curves show that the required rationale fraction varies by dataset (40% for StrategyQA, 60–70% for CSQA, higher for AQuA), which means the "unnecessary" portion differs substantially. FastCoT's speedup depends on how much rationale can be skipped, which in turn depends on how much rationale is redundant — the paper does not connect these two quantities quantitatively.

**Claim 4: The method is model-agnostic and requires no training or modification.** This claim is supported by the implementation: FastCoT is a pure inference-time wrapper that works with standard HuggingFace models. The four tested models are all LLaMA-family causal LMs, which is a limited test of model-agnosticism — the method has not been demonstrated on non-LLaMA architectures (e.g., MPT, Falcon, Mistral, or encoder-decoder models like T5), on instruction-tuned models (which may have different sensitivity to approximate context), or on models at larger scales (70B+) where GPU utilization characteristics may change the cost-insensitivity of the context window (Figure 2). The claim of "no modification" is accurate at the model weight level, but the implementation does require substantial code-level integration — the KV cache manipulation, padding mechanisms, and verification logic are non-trivial additions to a standard inference loop.

**Specific weaknesses in the experimental design:**

- **No confidence intervals or error bars anywhere.** Every figure and table reports point estimates without any measure of variance. The 500-question MATH test set in a typical CoT evaluation paper at least reports accuracy with binomial confidence intervals. FastCoT's test set sizes are not even reported, and none of the accuracy numbers, speedup numbers, or time measurements include error estimates. This makes it impossible to assess whether the observed differences (e.g., the 2–3% accuracy gaps in Figure 6, or the speedup differences across models in Table 1) are statistically reliable or within noise.

- **No per-query time or accuracy distribution.** The wall-clock time in Table 1 is a single aggregate number. There is no analysis of how speedup varies across questions — do some questions speed up by 50% while others slow down? The method could be "fragile" in the sense that it works well on most queries but catastrophically fails (producing wrong answers or taking longer) on a small subset. The aggregate metrics would mask this.

- **AQuA is omitted from Figure 6.** Given that AQuA is the most mathematically demanding dataset and shows the longest rationales (largest absolute time savings in Table 1), its absence from the main performance analysis is a significant gap. The saturation curve for AQuA in Figure 5 is also the least saturated, suggesting that mathematical reasoning may be more sensitive to rationale completeness — which has direct implications for FastCoT's viability on this dataset.

- **The calibration procedure is not reproducible.** The size of the calibration subset, the exact performance loss threshold, and the procedure for computing the minimum iterations are not specified. This makes the paper's specific speedup-accuracy tradeoff points impossible to reproduce without guesswork. A sensitivity analysis of the calibration threshold — showing how speedup and accuracy vary as the threshold changes — would characterize the achievable tradeoff frontier and is a notable absence.

- **No comparison to speculative decoding or other acceleration methods.** The paper positions itself as complementary to prompt engineering efforts but does not compare against any other decoding acceleration method. A comparison against speculative decoding (with a small draft model) on the same hardware would contextualize whether 20% speedup with 2% accuracy loss is better or worse than alternative approaches. The lack of such a comparison is understandable — speculative decoding requires a draft model, and the paper emphasizes FastCoT's training-free nature — but it leaves the practical value proposition ambiguous.

- **Single GPU, single precision, single batch size.** All experiments are on one A100-80G. The cost-insensitivity property shown in Figure 2 (context window size not affecting forward time) depends on the GPU's compute utilization characteristics — on a less powerful GPU where the matrix units are more saturated at typical CoT sequence lengths, the context window might incur a measurable latency cost. Similarly, the time-composition breakdown (Table 2) may not generalize to different hardware configurations.

- **The Strip KV overhead (5.6% of total time) is a single-point measurement.** If this overhead scales poorly with batch size or sequence length, it could become a dominant cost in higher-throughput settings. The paper does not investigate how Strip KV cost scales.

**Experiments that would have strengthened the paper:**

1. **A sweep of the calibration performance-loss threshold** showing the Pareto frontier of speedup vs. accuracy. This would let practitioners choose their operating point on the tradeoff curve rather than being locked into the paper's single chosen threshold.

2. **A comparison against a speculative decoding baseline** using a standard draft model (e.g., a 1.5B parameter LLaMA variant) to provide context for the 20% speedup claim.

3. **Per-dataset rationale length statistics** correlated with speedup to test the hypothesis that speedup scales with rationale redundancy.

4. **A content analysis of approximate tokens** — how often do they match the AR tokens? How semantically similar are they? This would ground the "glimpse of the future" metaphor in measurable token-level fidelity metrics.

5. **Breakdown of speedup into Jacobi multi-token acceptance vs. early stopping** contributions per configuration, to determine which mechanism dominates and under what conditions.

6. **Testing on instruction-tuned chat models** (Llama2-chat, Vicuna, etc.), which are far more commonly deployed in practice than base models for CoT tasks, and may exhibit different sensitivity to approximate context.

The paper's experiments are sufficient to establish FastCoT as a viable approach with promising initial results, but the lack of variance estimates, the omission of AQuA from the main performance analysis, the unreported calibration details, and the absence of comparisons to alternative acceleration methods leave substantial uncertainty about the method's practical advantages and the conditions under which it works best.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted for in Speedup Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** FastCoT's speedup depends on an iteration budget that is pre-calibrated per model-dataset pair using a held-out subset $S_{\text{cal}}$. The calibration procedure described in Section 4.4 requires running the full CoT task on this subset for multiple iteration counts to determine the minimum iterations needed to stay below a performance loss threshold. The authors state:

> "For a large dataset, we randomly select a small portion of it as $S_{\text{cal}}$ and perform the CoT task within this subset for each iteration. We calculate the minimum number of iterations required to achieve a certain performance loss threshold and use this value as the upper bound for iterations."

This calibration cost — running the CoT task on some number of questions for multiple iteration budgets — is not included in any of the wall-clock time measurements in Table 1 or Table 2. The speedups reported (3.20% to 23.71%) are measured on the test set *after* the budget has already been determined, meaning they represent the marginal inference savings without amortizing the one-time calibration overhead.

**The consequence.** The practical speedup in a deployment setting is lower than reported, and the degradation depends on how often recalibration is needed. If a practitioner deploys FastCoT on a new dataset, they must first run the full autoregressive CoT on $S_{\text{cal}}$ at multiple iteration budgets to find the optimal stopping point. This calibration cost could be substantial: if $S_{\text{cal}}$ contains even 10% of the dataset, and calibration requires evaluating 5–10 candidate iteration budgets, the total calibration compute could exceed the inference compute for a single pass over the test set. The paper provides no guidance on the size of $S_{\text{cal}}$, the number of budgets tested during calibration, or the sensitivity of the optimal budget to the calibration subset — all of which affect the amortized cost. For a one-time evaluation (e.g., processing a fixed test set once), the calibration overhead could completely negate the inference speedup. For repeated inference on the same task distribution, the amortized cost becomes negligible, but the paper does not characterize the crossover point.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper explicitly acknowledges this limitation in passing but does not measure it: the calibration procedure is described in Section 4.4, but the size of $S_{\text{cal}}$ is never specified, the performance loss threshold used in the main experiments is never reported as an exact number, and no sensitivity analysis of the budget to calibration subset size or threshold appears in the experimental sections. Table 1 reports the resulting performance loss for each configuration (1.23% to 2.66%), confirming that a threshold was applied, but the threshold itself, the calibration cost, and the number of budgets evaluated are absent.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not amortize calibration cost into the speedup numbers, does not report calibration overhead, and does not discuss the tradeoff between calibration thoroughness and net speedup. The authors do not propose methods to reduce calibration cost (e.g., extrapolating the budget from a smaller calibration set, using the convergence behavior of early iterations to predict the optimal budget online, or sharing budgets across related datasets). This is a practical gap that a deployer would need to resolve through trial and error.

### 6.2 The Method Cannot Help When the Base Model's Reasoning Is Fundamentally Insufficient

**The assumption or constraint.** FastCoT operates by taking the base model's own Jacobi-generated approximate tokens — which are iterative predictions of what the model *would* generate autoregressively — and using them as partial reasoning context. The quality of these approximate tokens is bounded above by the quality of the model's fully autoregressive rationale. The authors do not explicitly state this as a limitation, but it follows directly from the mechanism: Jacobi decoding converges toward the greedy AR decoding trajectory, and early iterations produce approximations that are no better than the converged output. If the model's autoregressive CoT reasoning is wrong on a given question (producing an incorrect answer even with the full rationale), FastCoT cannot correct it — the approximate tokens will be approximations of an incorrect reasoning path, and using them as answer context can at best preserve the (incorrect) AR accuracy, not improve it.

**The consequence.** FastCoT provides no benefit — and may actively degrade performance through misleading approximate tokens — on questions where the base model's CoT accuracy is near zero or where the reasoning path is fragile. The corruption experiments (Section 5.2, Figure 5) show that accuracy saturates as rationale completeness increases, but they do not test whether approximate Jacobi tokens are *better* than `[PAD]` tokens at low overlap ratios. If the model's early Jacobi iterations produce tokens that are semantically plausible but factually wrong for the specific question, they could induce incorrect answers that even a truncated exact-only rationale (FastCoT(w/o by-products)) would avoid. This failure mode is most likely on hard problems where the model lacks the necessary knowledge or reasoning capability, and it cannot be detected from aggregate metrics — it would manifest as a subset of questions where FastCoT underperforms both Vanilla CoT and FastCoT(w/o by-products).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 6 provides indirect evidence. On Llama2-13B with StrategyQA, both FastCoT and FastCoT(w/o by-products) show a decreasing accuracy trend through the mid-iteration range (roughly iterations 20–80), with accuracy dropping from ~65% to ~57% before recovering. This indicates that on this model-dataset pair, *more* exact tokens (and, for FastCoT, more approximate tokens) can reduce accuracy relative to having fewer tokens. The approximate tokens in FastCoT partially mitigate this (FastCoT's dip is less severe than FastCoT(w/o by-products) in some regions), but they do not eliminate it. The paper attributes this to "the faithfulness of CoT" (Section 5.3), but does not isolate which questions drive the dip or whether FastCoT fails on a different subset than Vanilla CoT. The AQuA dataset — the most mathematically demanding of the three — is absent from Figure 6 entirely, so no performance-by-iteration data is available for the domain where reasoning insufficiency is most likely.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. There is no analysis of FastCoT's performance conditional on Vanilla CoT accuracy (e.g., does FastCoT maintain accuracy on questions that Vanilla CoT gets right, and what happens on questions Vanilla CoT gets wrong?), no breakdown by question difficulty, and no discussion of when approximate tokens might be harmful rather than helpful. The calibration procedure (Section 4.4) selects an iteration budget that minimizes aggregate performance loss, but it does not account for per-question heterogeneity — some questions may suffer much larger accuracy drops than the average, while others benefit. A practitioner would want to know whether FastCoT's accuracy degradation is concentrated on already-hard questions (which might be acceptable) or scattered across the difficulty spectrum (which would be more concerning). The paper provides no such analysis.

### 6.3 Strip KV and Padding Overheads Consume a Substantial Fraction of the Gross Inference Savings

**The assumption or constraint.** FastCoT's net speedup is the difference between gross inference time savings (fewer forward passes due to Jacobi multi-token acceptance and early stopping) and the additional overheads introduced by the Jacobi decoding framework: KV cache manipulation (Strip KV), padding for batched variable-length sequences (Type1 and Type2 Padding), approximate token decoding (Context Decode), and other bookkeeping. The paper assumes that these overheads are manageable and that the net speedup remains positive, but Table 2 reveals that in the one configuration analyzed in detail, the overheads are large relative to the savings.

**The consequence.** The net speedup is substantially smaller than the gross improvement in inference time, and the overhead profile may scale unfavorably with batch size, sequence length, or model size. In the Llama2-13B CSQA configuration (Table 2), inference time drops by 84.07 seconds (from 358.15s to 274.08s, a 23.5% reduction), but the net speedup is only 38.27 seconds (10.47%) because FastCoT adds 45.80 seconds of overhead not present in AR decoding (Type1 Padding: 5.33s, Type2 Padding: 0.84s, Context Decode: 5.42s, Strip KV: 18.58s, additional Other: 17.49s relative to AR). The overhead consumes 54.5% of the gross inference savings. This means that even if the inference time were reduced to zero (impossible, but as a thought experiment), the maximum possible net speedup in this configuration would be approximately 1 – (51.65s overhead / 365.20s AR total) ≈ 14.2% — the overhead alone caps the achievable speedup.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2 provides the only overhead breakdown in the paper, for a single configuration (Llama2-13B on CSQA). The Strip KV operation alone accounts for 18.58 seconds, or 5.6% of FastCoT's total time and approximately 22% of the overhead. The paper acknowledges this: "Equally imperative within FastCoT is the handling of the key-value (KV) cache generated by the model's computation, and we call this procedure Strip KV, which consumes 18.58 seconds (about 5.6% for total time)" (Section 5.4.2). However, no overhead breakdowns are provided for any other model-dataset pair (Table 1), so it is unknown whether the overhead-to-savings ratio is consistent across configurations. Configurations with higher reported speedups (e.g., 23.71% on Llama2-7B CSQA) presumably have either lower overhead or higher gross inference savings, but the decomposition is not provided. The padding mechanisms are described in Figure 7 and costed in Table 2, but their scaling with batch size is not investigated.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies Strip KV as a cost but does not propose optimizations or analyze its scaling behavior. The authors do not discuss whether the overhead could be reduced through engineering improvements (more efficient KV cache slicing, avoiding full recomputation of approximate suffix attention, or merging padding operations with inference), and they do not characterize how the overhead profile changes with batch size, sequence length, or context window size — all of which would affect the net speedup in different deployment scenarios. The future work section (Section 7) mentions the possibility of "quickly conduct iterations by using a relatively smaller model for some iterations," which could reduce inference cost, but does not address the overhead side of the equation.

### 6.4 The Method Is Validated on a Single Model Family, a Single GPU Architecture, and No Instruction-Tuned Models

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments are conducted on LLaMA-family base models (Llama-7B, Llama-13B, Llama2-7B, Llama2-13B) running on a single Nvidia A100-SXM 80GB GPU. The paper claims FastCoT is "model-agnostic" (Abstract) and "can be applied to any pretrained causal LM" (implicit in Section 1), but this claim is supported only within a narrow slice of the model and hardware space. Critically, the experiments use **base models** — not the instruction-tuned or chat-fine-tuned variants (Llama2-chat, Vicuna, etc.) that dominate practical CoT deployment. The cost-insensitivity result in Figure 2, which underpins the viability of the Jacobi context window, is measured on one GPU architecture (A100) with one model (Llama2-13B), and the paper's argument for why the insensitivity holds — "when the decoding length is comparatively limited, the consequent diminutive matrix blocks can not fully leverage the computational capabilities of CUDA and Tensor cores" (Section 3.1) — is hardware-specific and would not necessarily transfer to GPUs with different compute-to-memory-bandwidth ratios, or to inference frameworks with different kernel implementations.

**The consequence.** The speedup numbers, the overhead breakdown, and the context-window cost insensitivity may not generalize to other deployment scenarios. Instruction-tuned models are trained to follow specific prompt formats and may respond differently to approximate tokens in context — they could be more robust (better at extracting signal from noisy partial rationales due to instruction-following training) or more fragile (more sensitive to out-of-distribution approximate text that violates the expected assistant-style response format). On different GPU architectures — consumer GPUs with less compute headroom, inference-optimized hardware like TPUs or Inferentia, or multi-GPU setups — the context window cost insensitivity may break, and the Strip KV overhead may scale differently relative to inference time. The paper provides no evidence that the 20% speedup figure is representative outside the specific LLaMA + A100 configuration tested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper tests four models, all LLaMA-family, all base (non-instruction-tuned), at two scales (7B and 13B). The results in Table 1 show significant variation across these models even within this narrow range: speedup on CSQA ranges from 3.64% (Llama-7B) to 23.71% (Llama2-7B), a ~6.5× range. This variation — *within the same model family* — strongly suggests that FastCoT's effectiveness is model-dependent and that extrapolation to other architectures (Mistral, Falcon, Qwen, Gemma, encoder-decoder models like T5) is unreliable. The decreasing-accuracy phenomenon on Llama2-13B StrategyQA (Figure 6) further demonstrates model-specific behavior: an effect present in one generation of LLaMA models that is absent in the previous generation. Figure 2 tests a single GPU (A100-80G) and a single model (Llama2-13B). The padding mechanisms (Figure 7) and Strip KV operation are described generically but costed only for one configuration.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the generalizability of results across model families, model types (base vs. chat), or hardware platforms. The "model-agnostic" claim in the Abstract is not qualified with the actual testing scope. The authors do not flag this as a limitation in Section 7 (Limitations), which focuses instead on the black-box API inapplicability and the context window control problem. A practitioner considering FastCoT for a production deployment using an instruction-tuned model (e.g., Llama2-70B-chat for a customer-facing CoT application) on different hardware (e.g., T4 GPUs for cost efficiency) would have no empirical basis from this paper to estimate the expected speedup or accuracy impact.

### 6.5 The Method Provides No Benefit on Hard Problems Where the Model Lacks Fundamental Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** FastCoT's mechanism — using approximate Jacobi tokens as partial rationale — depends on the base model being capable of generating a correct autoregressive reasoning path. The approximate tokens are approximations of what the model's greedy decoding *would* produce; if that trajectory leads to an incorrect answer, the approximate tokens point in the wrong direction. The paper's formulation in Section 4.4 acknowledges that Early Stopping relies on the approximate tokens being "good enough" to trigger the correct answer, but never addresses the regime where no amount of iterative refinement can produce correct reasoning because the model lacks the necessary knowledge or reasoning capability.

**The consequence.** FastCoT provides zero — or negative — benefit on the subset of questions that are fundamentally outside the model's CoT capability. This is not incidental; it is a hard boundary on the method's applicability. On CSQA and StrategyQA (commonsense and implicit reasoning), base LLaMA models already achieve moderate-to-high accuracy (Figure 6 shows convergence in the 50–65% range), which partially masks this limitation — there are relatively few questions where the model has zero capability. But on tasks where the base model's CoT accuracy is low — mathematical reasoning, multi-step logical deduction, or domain-specific expert reasoning — the population of "unsolvable for this model" questions is larger, and FastCoT cannot help on that fraction regardless of how the iteration budget or context window is tuned. The speedup metric, which measures wall-clock time on the full test set, would still show improvement (since easy and medium questions are processed faster), but the accuracy on hard questions would remain at the base model's low level — meaning the method does not expand the set of answerable questions.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** This limitation is inferred from the method's design rather than directly measured, because the paper does not partition results by question difficulty. However, the corruption experiments (Figure 5) provide indirect evidence: on AQuA (mathematical reasoning), the accuracy curve rises more gradually and does not reach clear saturation even at full rationale visibility — suggesting that mathematical reasoning depends more heavily on complete, correct intermediate steps. If the model's Jacobi approximations are themselves mathematically imprecise (as they likely are in early iterations on arithmetic-heavy problems), the approximate tokens may contain calculation errors that actively mislead the answer. The omission of AQuA from Figure 6 (the main performance analysis) is particularly significant here — without seeing the iteration-by-iteration accuracy curves for AQuA across FastCoT and FastCoT(w/o by-products), it is impossible to assess whether FastCoT helps or hurts on the hardest mathematical questions. Table 1 reports speedups on AQuA (10.20–21.80%) with performance loss of 2.24–2.66%, but these are aggregate numbers that could mask poor performance on the subset of questions requiring precise multi-step arithmetic.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. There is no analysis of FastCoT's performance conditional on question difficulty, no stratification by Vanilla CoT accuracy, and no discussion of when approximate tokens are likely to be counterproductive. The calibration procedure (Section 4.4) selects an iteration budget based on aggregate performance loss, but this implicitly weights all questions equally — a budget that minimizes average loss may be suboptimal for hard questions specifically. The authors do not propose any mechanism for detecting when approximate tokens are low-quality and reverting to full autoregressive decoding, which would be a natural safety mechanism for hard questions. The future work section (Section 7) focuses on dynamic context window control and Jacobi decoding acceleration, not on robustness to low-quality approximate tokens.

### 6.6 The Method Introduces a Serial Dependency That Prevents Full Parallelism Across Queries

**The assumption or constraint.** FastCoT's iteration loop is inherently serial within each query: each forward pass depends on the previous iteration's approximate tokens and produces a new set that updates the ATB. While the paper demonstrates speedup in wall-clock time (Table 1), this speedup is measured as total time to process the test set serially. In a high-throughput batch inference setting — where many queries are processed simultaneously and the GPU is kept saturated by feeding new queries as soon as previous ones complete — the serial dependency within each query creates a different kind of bottleneck: **query-level latency is reduced, but the number of forward passes per query is variable**, meaning queries complete at different times, creating stragglers that hold up batch completion.

**The consequence.** FastCoT's wall-clock speedup on a fixed test set may not translate to throughput improvements in a continuous serving setting. If queries require anywhere from, say, 20 to 100 Jacobi iterations depending on convergence speed and the iteration budget, the slowest queries (requiring the most iterations) determine the batch turnaround time. The Strip KV and padding mechanisms (Table 2) are specifically designed to handle variable-length sequences within a batch (Section 5.4.2), but they do not eliminate the fundamental issue: fast queries finish early and their GPU resources sit idle (or are backfilled with new queries, if the serving system supports it) while slow queries continue. The paper's aggregate wall-clock time metric masks this heterogeneity — it reports total time summed across all queries, which is the relevant metric for offline batch evaluation, but not for online serving throughput.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not report the distribution of iterations per query, the variance in convergence speed, or the impact of stragglers on batch throughput. The IS/TI column in Table 1 reports only the *total* iterations saved across all queries, not the per-query distribution. The time-composition analysis (Table 2) is a single aggregate number for the entire test set. The Type1 and Type2 padding mechanisms (Figure 7) are described as solutions to the variable-length KV cache problem, but their overhead (5.33s and 0.84s in Table 2) is measured in aggregate, not as a function of the variance in query lengths within the batch. The paper acknowledges the batching challenge implicitly through the padding mechanisms, but does not characterize the throughput impact.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss serving throughput, batch-level straggler effects, or the latency-vs-throughput distinction that is standard in inference systems papers. The speedup claims are framed in terms of total wall-clock time ("FastCoT accelerates inference by nearly 20%", Abstract), which is the appropriate metric for the offline evaluation setting tested, but a practitioner deploying FastCoT in a production serving system would need to know whether the per-query latency reduction translates to throughput gains under continuous load. The authors do not flag this as a limitation or discuss strategies for mitigating it (e.g., dynamic batching that groups queries with similar expected iteration counts, or early-exit mechanisms that terminate slow queries at a maximum latency budget). The future work discussion of using a "relatively smaller model for some iterations" (Section 7) could partially address this by reducing the cost of slow iterations, but the serial dependency within each query remains.
