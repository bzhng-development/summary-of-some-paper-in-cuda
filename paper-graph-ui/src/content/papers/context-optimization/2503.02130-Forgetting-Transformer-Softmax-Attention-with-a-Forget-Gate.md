# Forgetting Transformer: Softmax Attention with a Forget Gate

**ArXiv:** [2503.02130](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.02130)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces the **Forgetting Transformer (FoX)**, a Transformer variant that incorporates a data-dependent forget gate into softmax attention by down-weighting unnormalized attention scores based on learned per-token scalar values—operationalized as an additive bias `D_ij = Σ log f_l` applied to the query-key logits before the softmax.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces the **Forgetting Transformer (FoX)**, a Transformer variant that incorporates a data-dependent forget gate into softmax attention by down-weighting unnormalized attention scores based on learned per-token scalar values—operationalized as an additive bias `D_ij = Σ log f_l` applied to the query-key logits before the softmax. Evaluated on long-context language modeling (LongCrawl64, 760M-parameter models, 16K training context length), FoX outperforms the standard Transformer on per-token loss, length extrapolation, and short-context downstream tasks, while retaining the Transformer's near-perfect needle-in-the-haystack retrieval accuracy within the training context length—in contrast to recurrent baselines (Mamba-2, HGRN2, DeltaNet) whose per-token loss plateaus after ~5K–10K tokens. The paper also introduces a **"Pro" block design** integrating QK-norm, output gates, output normalization, and data-dependent key-value token shift, which yields consistent improvements for both FoX and the Transformer, establishing that a forget gate can be naturally embedded into softmax attention without sacrificing long-context retrieval capabilities only when the forget gate is data-dependent rather than fixed or merely data-independent.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Transformers Lack Explicit Forgetting

The fundamental tension this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **recurrent sequence models have forget gates, and Transformers don't — yet both need to manage long-range information.** This matters because forgetting is not merely about discarding useless information; it is about *selective retention* — knowing what to keep and what to let go of based on the current context. In a long document, the relevance of earlier paragraphs shifts dramatically as you read further. A model that treats all past tokens equally risks either drowning in noise (giving equal weight to irrelevant and relevant context) or relying on brittle, data-independent heuristics (like fixed positional decay) that cannot adapt to the content.

The forget gate, originally introduced in LSTMs (Gers et al., 2000) and recognized as critically important across decades of recurrent architecture research (Greff et al., 2016; Van Der Westhuizen & Lasenby, 2018), solves this by learning to modulate the retention of past information at each timestep based on the current input. It has become ubiquitous in the recent wave of revived recurrent sequence models (Peng et al., 2021; Gu & Dao, 2023; Yang et al., 2023; Dao & Gu, 2024). Yet the Transformer — still the dominant architecture for long-context tasks — has no analogous mechanism. Its self-attention gives each query access to all keys, with the only "forgetting" coming from the softmax normalization (which must allocate probability mass across all positions) and from positional embeddings (which apply data-independent biases).

This gap is not merely an architectural curiosity. It represents a missing inductive bias: the ability to say "this information is no longer relevant for what I'm predicting right now" in a way that depends on *what* that information is, not just *how far away* it is.

### Why This Gap Matters

The paper's motivation rests on three converging lines of evidence that make the absence of a forget gate in Transformers a practical problem, not just a theoretical one:

**1. Recurrent models still underperform Transformers on long-context tasks.** Despite the excitement around linear-complexity recurrent architectures — Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023), Mamba-2 (Dao & Gu, 2024), HGRN2 (Qin et al., 2024a), DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2024), and others — these models consistently fall short of Transformers in tasks requiring genuine long-range retrieval and reasoning (Hsieh et al., 2024; Waleffe et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2024a). The likely cause, as hypothesized by Jelassi et al. (2024) and empirically visible in the per-token loss curves in this paper's Figure 2, is that recurrent models have **relatively small fixed-size hidden states** that act as an information bottleneck. They can compress and carry forward some information, but when the model needs to "copy" or reference a specific detail from 10,000 tokens ago, the compressed state representation may have already overwritten or blurred that detail.

In other words, recurrent models are forced to forget because of architectural constraints (finite state capacity), while Transformers have the opposite problem: they *never* forget unless explicitly designed to, giving them superior long-range retrieval but potentially weaker local coherence and higher susceptibility to distraction from irrelevant past context.

**2. Forgetting is essential for short-context performance.** The forget gate is not just about managing long sequences; it has proven critical even in short-context settings. The extensive literature on LSTM variants (Greff et al., 2016) and modern recurrent architectures (Peng et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023; Gu & Dao, 2023) consistently shows that gating mechanisms — particularly forget gates — are among the most important architectural components for modeling local linguistic structure, maintaining grammatical coherence, and handling recency effects. A Transformer without any forgetting mechanism may attend arbitrarily far back, but this comes at the cost of not having an explicit, learnable bias toward recency when the task demands it.

**3. The evidence for forgetting in Transformers is scattered and partial.** Several existing techniques implicitly implement forms of forgetting in Transformers, but each tackles only part of the problem:

- **ALiBi** (Press et al., 2021) adds a data-independent, linearly decaying bias to attention logits based on the key-query distance. This provides a fixed recency bias — tokens further away are always penalized by a fixed amount — but cannot adapt to content. A token that says "the answer is X" remains important regardless of distance, and a nearby token that is irrelevant should still be ignored. ALiBi treats both cases identically.

- **RoPE** (Su et al., 2024) encodes relative positions through rotation, which indirectly creates a decay effect that strengthens with increasing relative distance. However, this decay is also data-independent and baked into the position encoding rather than being a separate, controllable mechanism. The paper's ablations (Section 4.5, Table 3) show that RoPE is not sufficient on its own — removing it from FoX causes only minor performance changes, indicating that the data-dependent forget gate provides something RoPE cannot.

- **Sliding window attention** forces forgetting by restricting the attention window to a fixed size, but this is a crude solution: it permanently discards information beyond the window, making long-range retrieval impossible, rather than selectively retaining what matters.

- **Other bias-based methods** like LAS-attention (Zimerman & Wolf), CoPE (Olsson et al., 2022), and Selective Attention (Leviathan et al., 2024) modify attention logits in various data-dependent ways, but these either rely on computing sums of transformed logits from previous timesteps (potentially incompatible with FlashAttention), use non-standard attention formulations, or introduce different biases that don't directly correspond to a forget gate.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies a specific and previously unaddressed configuration: **a Transformer with an explicit, data-dependent forget gate embedded directly into softmax attention, compatible with FlashAttention, and requiring no positional embeddings by default.** Each component of this configuration addresses a gap in prior work:

**Gap 1: No clean forget gate mechanism for softmax attention.** Gated linear attention models like GLA (Yang et al., 2023) and Mamba-2 (Dao & Gu, 2024) have shown that forget gates can be expressed in a parallel attention form by multiplying unnormalized attention scores by cumulative forget gate products (the `F_ij` terms). The paper's key insight — what makes it more than just "add a bias to attention logits" — is that **this exact same mechanism works for softmax attention without any modification to the standard attention formulation.** The derivation (Section 2.2) shows that gated linear attention's parallel form (Equation 10) is structurally identical to softmax attention (Equation 2) except for the choice of kernel. Simply swapping the linear attention kernel back to the exponential dot-product kernel yields softmax attention with a forget gate. This is a simple but non-obvious observation: previous work had treated gated attention as specific to linear attention variants, not recognizing that the gating mechanism is *kernel-independent*.

**Gap 2: Data-independent forgetting is insufficient.** The paper explicitly tests this (Section 4.5, Figure 7). A fixed forget gate (equivalent to ALiBi) and a learnable but data-independent forget gate (where `f_t = σ(b_head)` — same value regardless of input) both underperform the data-dependent version (`f_t = σ(w_f^T x_t + b_f)`). The data-dependent gate allows the model to decide, per token and per head, how much to forget based on what that token represents. A token containing a section header might trigger strong forgetting (past context is now less relevant), while a token continuing the same sentence might trigger weak forgetting (the preceding context remains important). Neither ALiBi nor data-independent scalar gates can capture this.

**Gap 3: Existing Transformer improvements don't capture the full forget gate architecture.** Several works have modified attention with learnable biases, gating, or decay:
- LAS-attention (Zimerman & Wolf) applies multiplicative exponential decay to attention logits, which is a fixed, position-based form of forgetting.
- CoPE and Selective Attention modify the current query's attention logits based on aggregates of past logits, which introduces data-dependence but through a different mechanism (sums of transformed logits) that may complicate FlashAttention compatibility.
- Geometric attention (Csordás et al., 2021) and stick-breaking attention (Tan et al., 2024) use stick-breaking processes to compute attention scores, yielding a data-dependent decay effect. However, these fundamentally modify the attention formulation — they are alternatives to softmax attention, not improvements upon it. The paper positions FoX differently: as a **minimal modification to standard softmax attention** (one additional scalar per head per timestep, computed via a simple linear+sigmoid transformation) that integrates seamlessly with existing infrastructure.

**Gap 4: Hybrid architectures don't solve the problem in the attention mechanism itself.** Several models combine recurrent layers with quadratic attention (Ma et al., 2022; 2024; Ren et al., 2024), effectively using recurrent layers to compress the past and attention layers for local processing or occasional global retrieval. But in these architectures, the forgetting happens *outside* the attention mechanism, in separate recurrent components. The attention layers themselves remain forget-gate-free. The paper's approach embeds forgetting directly into attention, meaning that even a pure Transformer with no recurrent sublayers can learn when to retain and when to discard past information.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames FoX not as a replacement for Transformers or recurrent models, but as a **synthesis**: it takes the forget gate — the most consistently valuable architectural component from the recurrent lineage — and integrates it into the softmax attention mechanism that gives Transformers their long-context retrieval superiority. The goal is to get the best of both worlds: the recency bias and adaptive retention of gated recurrent models, with the unbounded context access and retrieval accuracy of quadratic attention.

This positioning is reflected in the paper's choice of baselines and evaluation. Rather than comparing only against other Transformer variants, the paper evaluates against both pure Transformer baselines (LLaMA-style and Pro) and pure recurrent baselines (Mamba-2, HGRN2, DeltaNet). The needle-in-the-haystack test (Section 4.3, Figure 4) is specifically chosen because it is a stress test for long-range retrieval — exactly where recurrent models struggle and Transformers excel. The key question is: does adding a forget gate (which introduces a recency bias) damage the Transformer's ability to retrieve distant information? The paper's answer, shown in Figure 4, is a clear **no**: FoX achieves near-perfect needle retrieval within its training context length, matching or exceeding the standard Transformer and dramatically outperforming all tested recurrent models. The forget gate does not force forgetting — it enables *selective* retention, and the model learns to keep distant information when it matters.

The introduction of the **Pro block** (output gates, output normalization, QK-norm, data-dependent KV token shift) further clarifies the paper's positioning. These components are common in recurrent architectures (GLA uses output gates and output normalization; Mamba-2 uses output gates; various models use QK-norm) but are not standard in Transformer implementations, which typically follow the LLaMA recipe. By showing that the Pro block improves both FoX *and* the standard Transformer, the paper argues that some "recurrent" architectural innovations are actually general-purpose enhancements that the Transformer community should adopt regardless of whether they also use a forget gate.

The paper's self-positioning is ultimately as a **unifying contribution**: it identifies a mechanism (the forget gate) that has been independently validated in multiple architectural paradigms, shows it can be cleanly transferred to softmax attention with minimal modification, and demonstrates that the resulting model inherits the strengths of both lineages — the adaptive gating of recurrent models and the long-context retrieval of Transformers — without the weaknesses of either.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

**What the system is:** The Forgetting Transformer (FoX) is a standard Transformer language model where the softmax attention mechanism has been modified to accept an additional per-token, per-head scalar input — called a forget gate — that multiplicatively down-weights the contribution of past key-value pairs before they compete for the query's attention probability mass. **What problem it solves and the shape of the solution:** The problem is that standard softmax attention has no explicit, learnable mechanism for discarding or de-emphasising past context based on its content; the solution is to introduce a data-dependent decay factor that accumulates multiplicatively over time, expressed as an additive log-bias on the attention logits, which enables the model to learn *when* and *how much* to forget based on what each token represents, while preserving exact long-range retrieval when the learned forget gate values dictate retention.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The FoX architecture modifies a standard decoder-only Transformer at the attention layer. The system has six major components, five of which are identical to a standard Transformer and one of which is new:

1. **Input embedding layer** — converts token IDs to dense vectors `$x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$`, exactly as in a standard Transformer.
2. **Forgetting Attention layer** — the core modification. In addition to computing queries, keys, and values from the input, this layer computes a **scalar forget gate** `$f_t \in (0, 1)$` for each position `$t$` and each attention head via a learned linear projection followed by a sigmoid. These forget gate values are accumulated multiplicatively over time into a matrix `$F$` where `$F_{ij} = \prod_{l=j+1}^i f_l$` represents how much position `$i$` has "forgotten" position `$j$`. The log of this matrix, `$D_{ij} = \log F_{ij}$`, is added to the query-key dot products before the softmax, meaning that a token's influence on the current prediction decays multiplicatively with each intervening forget gate. The rest of the attention computation — softmax normalization, value aggregation — is unchanged.
3. **Output gate (Pro architecture only)** — an additional sigmoid-gated linear projection `$g_t = \sigma(W_g x_t)$` that element-wise multiplies the attention output after output normalization, controlling how much of the attention output flows into the residual stream. This component is common in recurrent architectures (GLA, Mamba-2) but not standard Transformers.
4. **QK-normalisation (Pro architecture only)** — RMSNorm applied to queries and keys before they enter the attention computation, stabilising training and improving length extrapolation.
5. **Data-dependent key-value token shift (Pro architecture only)** — a learnable interpolation between the current key/value and the previous timestep's key/value, controlled by an input-dependent gating scalar `$\alpha_t^{\text{key}}, \alpha_t^{\text{value}} \in (0, 1)$`, enabling the model to smooth its representations across time.
6. **Feed-forward network (SwiGLU MLP) and residual connections** — unchanged from the standard Transformer architecture.

Information flows as follows: an input token enters → embedding layer produces `$x_t$` → RMSNorm → the Forgetting Attention layer computes `$q_t, k_t, v_t, f_t$` (and optionally `$g_t$`, with key-value shift modifying `$k_t, v_t$`) → the attention output is computed using the forget-gate-augmented softmax → (optionally) output normalisation and output gating are applied → residual addition → RMSNorm → SwiGLU MLP → residual addition → output to next layer.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the core mathematical mechanism: how the forget gate is defined, how it accumulates into the `$F$` matrix, and how it enters the attention computation as the log-bias `$D$`. This is the single novel technical contribution — everything else is architectural integration.
- **Second**, the derivation pathway from gated linear attention to Forgetting Attention, because this shows *why* the formulation takes this specific form and connects it to the existing literature on gated recurrences.
- **Third**, the connection to ALiBi, because it reveals that FoX is a strict generalisation of the most popular data-independent positional bias method — and that the data-dependence is what provides the empirical gains.
- **Fourth**, the hardware-aware implementation, because demonstrating FlashAttention compatibility is critical for practical adoption and is a claimed advantage over alternative approaches like CoPE and Selective Attention.
- **Fifth**, the two architecture variants (LLaMA-style and Pro), with a detailed breakdown of each Pro component, because the Pro block is presented as a secondary contribution that independently improves the Transformer.
- **Sixth**, the design choices around positional embeddings (specifically, the decision to not use RoPE by default on FoX), because this is a counterintuitive result that distinguishes FoX from nearly all modern Transformer variants.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method paper** whose core idea is that the multiplicative forget gate from gated recurrent architectures can be embedded into softmax attention as an additive log-bias on the attention logits, and that this minimal modification yields consistent empirical gains without sacrificing long-context retrieval.

#### The Forgetting Attention Mechanism: Mathematical Definition

The Forgetting Attention mechanism modifies the standard causal softmax attention in exactly one place: the pre-softmax logits. In standard attention (Equation 2 in the paper), the output at position `$i$` is:

$$o_i = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^i \exp(q_i^\top k_j) \, v_j}{\sum_{j=1}^i \exp(q_i^\top k_j)}$$

where `$q_i, k_j, v_j \in \mathbb{R}^d$` are the query, key, and value vectors at positions `$i$` and `$j$`, and `$d$` is the head dimension. The exponential kernel `$\exp(q_i^\top k_j)$` measures the unnormalised relevance of key `$j$` to query `$i$`.

In Forgetting Attention (Equation 11), this becomes:

$$o_i = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^i F_{ij} \,\exp(q_i^\top k_j) \, v_j}{\sum_{j=1}^i F_{ij} \,\exp(q_i^\top k_j)} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^i \exp(q_i^\top k_j + D_{ij}) \, v_j}{\sum_{j=1}^i \exp(q_i^\top k_j + D_{ij})}$$

where:
- `$f_t = \sigma(w_f^\top x_t + b_f) \in (0, 1)$` is the **scalar forget gate** at position `$t$`, computed from the input `$x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$` at that position using a learned weight vector `$w_f \in \mathbb{R}^d$` and bias `$b_f \in \mathbb{R}$`, with `$\sigma$` being the sigmoid function;
- `$F_{ij} = \prod_{l=j+1}^i f_l$` is the **cumulative forget gate product** from position `$j+1$` to position `$i$`, representing the fraction of information from position `$j$` that survives to position `$i$` after passing through all intervening forget gates, with the convention `$F_{ii} = 1$` (the current position is never forgotten);
- `$D_{ij} = \log F_{ij} = \sum_{l=j+1}^i \log f_l$` is the **log-cumulative-forget-weight**, with the convention `$\log 0 = -\infty$`.

**What it computes:** For each query position `$i$` and each key position `$j \leq i$`, the mechanism multiplies the standard attention score `$\exp(q_i^\top k_j)$` by the cumulative forget factor `$F_{ij}$`. Since `$f_l \in (0, 1)$` and thus `$\log f_l \leq 0$`, the quantity `$F_{ij}$` is always in `$(0, 1]$` and decreases (or stays constant) as `$i$` increases for a fixed `$j$`. The log-bias `$D_{ij}$` is therefore always non-positive. This means the forget gate can only *reduce* the effective attention score for past positions — it cannot amplify them. The softmax then normalises these reduced scores against the scores of all other positions, so a heavily forgotten position `$j$` (small `$F_{ij}$`, large negative `$D_{ij}$`) will receive a proportionally smaller share of the attention probability mass at position `$i$`.

**Why this form:** The additive log-bias form `$\exp(q_i^\top k_j + D_{ij})$` is chosen for three reasons. First, it is mathematically equivalent to the multiplicative form `$F_{ij} \cdot \exp(q_i^\top k_j)$`, because `$\exp(a + b) = \exp(a)\exp(b)$`, but the additive form is more amenable to hardware-efficient implementation — it fits the standard FlashAttention pattern of "compute logit, add bias, apply softmax." Second, the multiplicative accumulation `$F_{ij} = \prod_{l=j+1}^i f_l$` is the natural way to model cumulative forgetting: each intervening token can independently decide to retain or discard the past, and the overall retention is the product of all those decisions. An additive forgetting mechanism (e.g., `$D_{ij} = -\sum_{l=j+1}^i \delta_l$` for some `$\delta_l > 0$`) would be equivalent in log-space but would require the model to learn `$\delta_l$` values, which is less natural because the plausible range of `$\delta_l$` depends on the context length — the sigmoid-gated multiplicative form naturally bounds `$f_l$` to `$(0, 1)$` regardless of sequence length. Third, this form directly mirrors the forget gate in LSTMs (which multiplies the cell state by a value in `$(0, 1)$`) and generalises ALiBi (as discussed below), providing a principled connection to both the recurrent and attention literatures.

**Multi-head extension:** For multi-head attention with `$H$` heads, the model maintains `$H$` independent sets of forget gate parameters `$\{w_f^{(h)}\}_{h=1}^H$` and `$\{b_f^{(h)}\}_{h=1}^H$`, and computes separate forget gate values `$\{f_t^{(h)}\}_{h=1}^H$` for each head. This allows different heads to learn different forgetting behaviours — some heads may retain information across very long spans (forget gates consistently near 1), while others may focus only on very recent context (forget gates consistently near 0). The paper's visualisations in Figures 3, 27, and 28 confirm that this heterogeneity emerges in practice: some heads exhibit strong decay (most entries of `$F$` near 0, attention focused locally), while others exhibit weak decay (attention distributed across the full context).

#### Derivation from Gated Linear Attention

The paper motivates Forgetting Attention by deriving it as the natural kernel-generalisation of gated linear attention. Understanding this derivation is important because it explains why the forget gate takes this specific multiplicative-accumulation form rather than some other mechanism.

**Step 1: Linear attention.** Standard linear attention (Katharopoulos et al., 2020) replaces the exponential dot-product kernel with a feature-based kernel `$\phi: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^{d'}$`, where `$\mathbb{R}_+$` denotes non-negative reals:

$$o_i = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^i (\phi(q_i)^\top \phi(k_j)) v_j}{\sum_{j=1}^i \phi(q_i)^\top \phi(k_j)}$$

The key property of linear attention is that it can be computed either in parallel (as above, with `$O(L^2)$` complexity) or recurrently (with `$O(L)$` complexity) by maintaining a running state:

$$S_t = S_{t-1} + v_t \phi(k_t)^\top, \quad z_t = z_{t-1} + \phi(k_t), \quad o_t = \frac{S_t \phi(q_t)}{z_t^\top \phi(q_t)}$$

where `$S_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d'}$` is a matrix-valued state accumulating key-value outer products weighted by key features, and `$z_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d'}$` is a vector normaliser. Both are initialised to zero.

**Step 2: Adding a forget gate to the recurrent form.** The natural place to introduce forgetting into the recurrent computation is at the state update. At each timestep `$t$`, before adding the new key-value information, the old state is multiplied by a scalar forget gate `$f_t \in (0, 1)$`:

$$S_t = f_t S_{t-1} + v_t \phi(k_t)^\top$$
$$z_t = f_t z_{t-1} + \phi(k_t)$$
$$o_t = \frac{S_t \phi(q_t)}{z_t^\top \phi(q_t)}$$

Note the paper explicitly points out that this differs from most gated linear attention models in the literature — GLA (Yang et al., 2023) and Mamba-2 (Dao & Gu, 2024) do not have the normalisation term `$z_t$`. The paper keeps this normalisation to maintain structural similarity with softmax attention.

**Step 3: Unrolling the recurrence into parallel form.** By unrolling the recurrent computation, the state at position `$i$` can be written as a weighted sum over all past positions:

$$S_i = \sum_{j=1}^i \left(\prod_{l=j+1}^i f_l\right) v_j \phi(k_j)^\top = \sum_{j=1}^i F_{ij} \, v_j \phi(k_j)^\top$$
$$z_i = \sum_{j=1}^i \left(\prod_{l=j+1}^i f_l\right) \phi(k_j) = \sum_{j=1}^i F_{ij} \, \phi(k_j)$$

where `$F_{ij} = \prod_{l=j+1}^i f_l$` is the cumulative forget factor (with `$F_{ii} = 1$`). Substituting these into the output equation:

$$o_i = \frac{\left(\sum_{j=1}^i F_{ij} v_j \phi(k_j)^\top\right) \phi(q_i)}{\left(\sum_{j=1}^i F_{ij} \phi(k_j)\right)^\top \phi(q_i)} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^i F_{ij} \left(\phi(q_i)^\top \phi(k_j)\right) v_j}{\sum_{j=1}^i F_{ij} \left(\phi(q_i)^\top \phi(k_j)\right)}$$

This is Equation 10 in the paper: gated linear attention in parallel form.

**Step 4: Kernel substitution.** The paper's key observation is that Equation 10 and standard softmax attention (Equation 2) are structurally identical except for the choice of kernel function inside the sum — `$k_\phi(q, k) = \phi(q)^\top \phi(k)$` for linear attention versus `$k_{\exp}(q, k) = \exp(q^\top k)$` for softmax attention. Both are specific kernel choices, and the forget gate mechanism `$F_{ij}$` is kernel-independent — it enters as a multiplicative weight on the kernel value. Therefore, **replacing the linear kernel with the exponential kernel while keeping the forget gate mechanism yields Forgetting Attention**:

$$o_i = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^i F_{ij} \exp(q_i^\top k_j) v_j}{\sum_{j=1}^i F_{ij} \exp(q_i^\top k_j)}$$

**Why this derivation matters:** It establishes that Forgetting Attention is not an ad-hoc modification but rather the natural generalisation of a mechanism that has been independently validated in the recurrent sequence modelling literature. The forget gate in gated linear attention is not a heuristic — it emerges from the desire to make the recurrent state adaptively retain or discard past information. Forgetting Attention inherits this same mechanism and its associated inductive bias. The derivation also explains why the forget gate is scalar (rather than vector-valued): in the gated linear attention derivation, a scalar gate multiplies the entire state `$S_{t-1}$`, which is a matrix. A vector gate would require element-wise multiplication and would break the clean parallel form. The scalar choice is both computationally minimal and proven effective in the gated linear attention context.

#### Connection to ALiBi

ALiBi (Press et al., 2021) adds a data-independent, linearly decaying bias to the attention logits:

$$o_i = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^i \exp(q_i^\top k_j - (i - j) m_h) v_j}{\sum_{j=1}^i \exp(q_i^\top k_j - (i - j) m_h)}$$

where `$m_h > 0$` is a head-specific slope and `$i - j$` is the distance between the query and the key.

In the FoX framework, this is equivalent to using a **fixed, head-specific, and data-independent forget gate**:

$$f_t^{(h)} = \exp(-m_h)$$

To verify this, note that with this fixed forget gate, the cumulative product is:

$$F_{ij} = \prod_{l=j+1}^i \exp(-m_h) = \exp(-(i - j) m_h)$$

And therefore the log-bias is:

$$D_{ij} = \log F_{ij} = -(i - j) m_h$$

This is exactly the ALiBi bias. So ALiBi is a special case of Forgetting Attention where the forget gate is constant for all positions within a head.

**What this reveals:** The paper's empirical finding (Section 4.5, Figure 7) that data-dependent forget gates outperform both fixed gates (ALiBi) and data-independent but learnable gates (`$f_t = \sigma(b_h)$`, where only the bias is learned) demonstrates that the value of Forgetting Attention over ALiBi comes from the **content-dependence**, not from the learnability of the decay rate. A model with a learnable per-head constant forget gate could in principle discover the optimal ALiBi slopes through training rather than having them set by a heuristic, but Figure 7 shows that this still underperforms the data-dependent gate. The interpretation is that different positions within a sequence need different rates of forgetting — a section break might warrant heavy forgetting, while the middle of a sentence warrants none — and a single per-head constant cannot capture this variation.

There is a subtle connection to the initialisation of data-independent forget gates. The paper introduces a function `$T(b) = 1 / (-\log \sigma(b))$`, which maps a bias `$b$` to the number of timesteps needed for the forget gate `$\sigma(b)$` to achieve a decay of `$1/e$`. For data-independent forget gates with `$H$` heads, the biases `$\{b^{(h)}\}_{h=1}^H$` are initialised such that `$T(b^{(h)}_{\text{init}})$` ranges exponentially from `$T_{\min}$` to `$T_{\max}$` — for example, with `$(T_{\min}, T_{\max}) = (2, 128)$` and `$H=4$`, the initial effective decay horizons are `$\{2, 8, 32, 128\}$`. This initialisation is critical: without it (i.e., zero-initialised biases), data-independent forget gates perform "extremely poorly" (Appendix D). For data-dependent forget gates, zero-initialisation of `$b_f$` works fine, because the input-dependent term `$w_f^\top x_t$` can learn to produce the appropriate range of forget gate values without careful initialisation.

#### Hardware-Aware Implementation with FlashAttention

A key practical claim of the paper is that Forgetting Attention is compatible with FlashAttention (Dao, 2023), the standard algorithm for memory-efficient exact attention on GPUs. This claim is important because several alternative attention modifications (CoPE, Selective Attention) are noted by the authors as potentially incompatible with FlashAttention due to their need to compute "sums of transformed logits from previous timesteps."

**The FlashAttention algorithm (briefly):** FlashAttention avoids materialising the full `$L \times L$` attention matrix in GPU high-bandwidth memory (HBM) by computing attention in tiles — small block-sized chunks of queries and keys that fit in the fast shared memory (SRAM) of the GPU. For each tile of queries (size `$B_r \times d$`) and each tile of keys (size `$B_c \times d$`), the algorithm computes the partial attention logits `$S = Q_{\text{tile}} K_{\text{tile}}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{B_r \times B_c}$` in SRAM, applies the softmax with online rescaling (to avoid storing all intermediate values), and accumulates the weighted values. The output is written to HBM only after all key tiles have been processed for a given query tile.

**Forgetting Attention modification (Algorithm 1):** The key insight is that the log-bias `$D_{ij}$` depends only on the cumulative sum of `$\log f_l$`, not on the queries or keys. This means:

1. **Pre-computation:** Before the attention computation, compute the cumulative sum `$c_i = \sum_{l=1}^i \log f_l$` for all positions `$i \in \{1, \ldots, L\}$` and store this vector in HBM. This costs `$O(L)$` time and memory and is negligible compared to the `$O(L^2)$` attention computation.

2. **On-the-fly bias computation:** When a query tile (positions `$\{i_1, \ldots, i_{B_r}\}$`) and a key tile (positions `$\{j_1, \ldots, j_{B_c}\}$`) are loaded into SRAM, load the corresponding segments of the cumulative sum vector `$c$` as well — one scalar per position. For each query-key pair `$(i, j)$`, compute `$D_{ij} = c_i - c_j$` directly in SRAM and add it to the attention logit `$q_i^\top k_j$`. This requires only one additional subtraction per logit, which is negligible compared to the dot-product computation.

3. **Otherwise unchanged:** The rest of the FlashAttention algorithm — the online softmax, the value accumulation, the output rescaling — is unmodified.

The backward pass (Algorithm 2) follows the same principle: the gradient with respect to `$D_{ij}$` is simply the gradient with respect to the attention logit (since `$D_{ij}$` is added before the softmax), and this gradient is back-propagated through the cumulative sum to obtain gradients with respect to the forget gate values `$f_t$`, and ultimately with respect to `$w_f$` and `$b_f$`.

**Why this works and what it avoids:** The cumulative sum decomposition `$D_{ij} = c_i - c_j$` means that the bias for any `$(i, j)$` pair can be computed from two scalars, without needing to sum over the intermediate range every time. This is critical: if `$D_{ij}$` required computing `$\sum_{l=j+1}^i \log f_l$` from scratch for every pair, the tile-based approach would either need to load all intermediate forget gate values into SRAM (breaking the tiling) or repeatedly compute the sum (adding `$O(L)$` work per logit). The cumulative sum pre-computation amortises this cost to `$O(1)$` per logit and `$O(L)$` total memory.

The paper explicitly contrasts this with CoPE and Selective Attention, which modify attention logits based on "sums of transformed logits from some previous timesteps" — these would require more complex state management within the tiled FlashAttention loop and may not be feasible without materialising intermediate results.

**Practical implementation details:** The paper implements Forgetting Attention using the Triton (OpenAI, 2021) FlashAttention implementation in the Flag Attention library (FlagOpen, 2023). They note that this implementation is "without significant optimization" and that the officially optimised CUDA FlashAttention for the standard Transformer achieves higher throughput (38k tokens/sec vs. 27-30k tokens/sec for the Triton-based FoX implementations, per Table 6). The authors expect comparable throughput if FoX were implemented with the same level of CUDA optimisation — the additional computation (one subtraction and one addition per attention logit) is marginal relative to the matrix multiplications that dominate attention.

#### Architecture Variants: LLaMA and Pro

The paper tests FoX with two architecture configurations, differing in the non-attention components surrounding the Forgetting Attention mechanism.

**FoX (LLaMA):** This is the minimal modification to the standard LLaMA architecture (Touvron et al., 2023). The only change is replacing Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) with the forget gate mechanism in the attention layers. All other LLaMA components are retained: pre-norm RMSNorm, SwiGLU MLP, no bias terms in linear layers (except for the forget gate), and standard residual connections. RoPE is removed entirely — the forget gate handles both the position-dependent recency bias and the lack of explicit positional encoding (since the cumulation of forget gates provides an implicit positional signal through its monotonic decay pattern).

**FoX (Pro):** This is an enhanced architecture that integrates several components commonly found in recurrent sequence models. The Pro block design (Figure 1, right) modifies the LLaMA architecture as follows:

1. **QK-norm (Dehghani et al., 2023):** RMSNorm is applied to queries and keys before they enter the attention computation:
   $$q_t = \text{RMSNorm}(W_q x_t), \quad k_t = \text{RMSNorm}(\text{token-shifted key})$$
   The paper notes an implementation detail: due to an accident, the RMSNorm scaling parameters are shared across all heads within a layer (rather than having one set per head), but the authors verified that this has "no observable impact on performance" (Appendix A, footnote 4).

2. **Data-dependent key-value token shift (KV-shift):** A simplified variant of the token shift mechanism from Peng et al. (2024). For keys, the computation is:
   $$\tilde{k}_t = W_k x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$$
   $$\alpha_t^{\text{key}} = \sigma(w_k^\top x_t) \in (0, 1)$$
   $$k_t = \text{RMSNorm}(\alpha_t^{\text{key}} \tilde{k}_{t-1} + (1 - \alpha_t^{\text{key}}) \tilde{k}_t)$$
   where `$w_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is a learned vector. The value computation is identical except without the final RMSNorm:
   $$\tilde{v}_t = W_v x_t, \quad \alpha_t^{\text{value}} = \sigma(w_v^\top x_t), \quad v_t = \alpha_t^{\text{value}} \tilde{v}_{t-1} + (1 - \alpha_t^{\text{value}}) \tilde{v}_t$$

   **What this computes:** At each timestep, the model interpolates between the previous timestep's projected key/value and the current timestep's projected key/value, with the interpolation weight `$\alpha_t \in (0, 1)$` determined by the current input. When `$\alpha_t$` is close to 1, the key/value is mostly copied from the previous timestep (producing temporal smoothing); when `$\alpha_t$` is close to 0, the key/value is mostly the current input's projection (producing sharp representation changes). This gives the model a mechanism for controlling the temporal granularity of its representations — tokens within the same phrase can share similar keys/values via high `$\alpha_t$`, while phrase boundaries can trigger low `$\alpha_t$` to reset the representation.

   **Why this form:** The authors describe this as a "simplified variant" of the token shift in Peng et al. (2024). It is data-dependent (unlike a fixed exponential moving average) and uses only one previous timestep (unlike more complex convolution-based shifts). The RMSNorm on the keys after shifting is important because the linear interpolation can change the scale of the resulting vector, which would affect the dot product with queries.

3. **Output gate:** Before the final output projection, the attention output is modulated by a sigmoid gate:
   $$o_i^{\text{gated}} = o_i \odot \sigma(W_g x_i)$$
   where `$\odot$` is element-wise multiplication and `$W_g \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` (or `$\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{head}} \times d_{\text{head}}}$` per head in the multi-head case). This allows the model to scale down or suppress the attention output at positions where the attention context is uninformative.

   **Parameter accounting:** When output gates are used, the number of parameters in the MLP is reduced so the total parameter count remains the same. This is important for fair comparison — the paper wants to show that the Pro architecture is genuinely more parameter-efficient, not just that adding parameters improves performance.

4. **Output normalisation:** RMSNorm is applied to the attention output before the output gate:
   $$o_i^{\text{final}} = W_o (\text{RMSNorm}(o_i) \odot g_i)$$
   This is analogous to the normalisation in GLA (Yang et al., 2023) and Mamba-2 (Dao & Gu, 2024), and serves to stabilise the scale of the attention output before it is gated and projected.

**Multi-head treatment:** Each head independently maintains its own copies of QK-norm parameters, KV-shift parameters, forget gate parameters, and output gate parameters. All RMSNorms (QK-norm and output norm) are applied per-head — each head's `$d_{\text{head}}$`-dimensional vectors are independently normalised before cross-head mixing.

**What the Pro architecture accomplishes:** Empirically, the Pro architecture significantly outperforms the LLaMA architecture for both FoX and the standard Transformer (Table 3: FoX (Pro) achieves 6.62 perplexity vs. 7.08 for FoX (LLaMA), and Transformer (Pro) achieves 6.82 vs. 7.49 for Transformer (LLaMA), all at 360M parameters). The Pro components are drawn from the recurrent sequence modelling literature (output gates and output norm from GLA/Mamba-2, QK-norm from Dehghani et al., 2023, token shift from Peng et al., 2024), and their effectiveness on the Transformer suggests that these "recurrent" innovations are actually **general-purpose sequence modelling enhancements** that the Transformer community has under-utilised. The paper explicitly recommends (Section 6) that future work adopt FoX (Pro) and Transformer (Pro) as baselines alongside the standard LLaMA architecture.

#### Positional Embeddings

A striking claim in the paper is that FoX **does not require positional embeddings**, while the standard Transformer does. This is not just an empirical observation — there is a mechanistic explanation.

**Why the Transformer needs positional embeddings:** In standard softmax attention without any positional signal, the attention computation is permutation-equivariant — shuffling the input sequence produces an identically shuffled output. The model cannot distinguish "token A appears before token B" from "token B appears before token A" without some source of position information. This is why virtually all modern Transformers use either learned absolute position embeddings (original Transformer, GPT-2), sinusoidal position embeddings (original Transformer), RoPE (LLaMA, Mistral, most recent models), or ALiBi (certain efficient Transformer variants).

**Why FoX does not need them:** The forget gate mechanism inherently breaks permutation equivariance. The forget gate values `$f_t$` are computed for each position in sequence, and the cumulative product `$F_{ij}$` depends on the order of positions — permuting the sequence would permute the forget gate values, which would change `$F_{ij}$` for every `$(i, j)$` pair and thus change the attention output. Moreover, the forget gate values form a monotonically decaying signal: for any two positions `$j_1 < j_2 < i$`, the cumulative forget factor `$F_{i,j_1}$` is always less than or equal to `$F_{i,j_2}$` (because `$F_{i,j_1} = F_{i,j_2} \cdot \prod_{l=j_2+1}^{j_1} f_l$` and each `$f_l \leq 1$`). This means the model can infer relative position from the magnitude of the forget gate bias — more distant positions will, on average, have smaller `$F_{ij}$` values, providing a learned, content-dependent form of distance encoding.

**Empirical evidence:** Table 3 shows that adding RoPE to FoX (LLaMA) provides a minor improvement (7.25 → 7.19 perplexity, a 0.8% reduction), while adding RoPE to FoX (Pro) provides essentially no improvement (6.62 → 6.63 perplexity). In contrast, removing RoPE from the standard Transformer (LLaMA) causes catastrophic degradation (7.49 → 29.30 perplexity). And perhaps most tellingly, removing both the forget gate and RoPE from FoX (Pro) — the last row of Table 3 — causes a severe performance collapse (6.62 → 7.40 perplexity). The forget gate and RoPE are partially substitutable as positional information sources, but the forget gate provides additional value (the data-dependent decay) that RoPE cannot.

**Why the paper defaults to no positional embeddings for FoX:** Given that RoPE provides at best marginal improvements for FoX (Pro) and minor improvements for FoX (LLaMA), the paper opts for architectural simplicity: removing RoPE eliminates a hyperparameter (the RoPE base angle `$\theta$`), simplifies the model, and demonstrates that the forget gate is a sufficient positional mechanism on its own. For the standard Transformer, RoPE is retained with `$\theta = 500000$` (following Xiong et al., 2023), because without it the model fails to learn.

#### Design Choices: Why This Specific Mechanism?

The paper makes several non-obvious design choices that deserve explicit justification, drawn from the text and from the broader context:

**Why scalar forget gates rather than vector?** Vector-valued forget gates (e.g., `$f_t \in (0, 1)^d$` that element-wise multiply the key or value vectors, or `$f_t \in (0, 1)^{d \times d}$` that matrix-multiply the state `$S_t$`) would provide more expressive forgetting. However, the derivation from gated linear attention (Section 2.2) naturally yields a scalar gate — it multiplies the entire state matrix `$S_{t-1}$`. A vector gate would require a different recurrence (e.g., `$S_t = \text{diag}(f_t) S_{t-1} + v_t k_t^\top$`), which would not factor into the clean `$F_{ij}$` product form in the parallel representation. The scalar choice is deliberate: it preserves the simple multiplicative accumulation structure that makes the FlashAttention modification trivial and the ALiBi connection explicit.

**Why the normalisation term `$z_t$` in the derivation, when GLA and Mamba-2 omit it?** The paper explicitly notes (Section 2.2, footnote-like text) that "most gated variants of linear attention models, such as GLA and Mamba-2, do not have the normalization term." The reason the paper retains it is **to maintain similarity with softmax attention**. Softmax attention has an implicit normalisation (the denominator in Equation 2), and the paper's stated goal is to derive a forget gate mechanism that integrates naturally with softmax attention. Removing the normalisation would correspond to linear attention with a forget gate but without the denominator, which is structurally further from softmax attention. The authors are transparent that this is a deliberate choice for conceptual clarity, not a claim of superiority of the normalised form.

**Why edit-based token shift rather than convolution?** The KV-shift mechanism interpolates between the previous and current token's projections using a learned scalar gate `$\alpha_t$`. This is a first-order mechanism — it only looks back one step. More sophisticated token shift mechanisms (e.g., in Mamba, which uses a short convolution) can look back multiple steps. The paper describes this as a "simplified variant" and doesn't ablate against multi-step convolution — the choice appears to be motivated by simplicity and minimal additional parameters (two learned vectors `$w_k, w_v$` per layer). Whether a convolution-based shift would improve performance is left as an open question.

**Why no systematic ablation on the number of heads / head dimension for the forget gate?** The forget gate is computed per head, so the number of heads determines how many independent forgetting patterns the model can learn. The paper tunes the head dimension (and thus implicitly the number of heads, given a fixed `$d_{\text{model}}$`) for the main experiments but does not report a dedicated ablation. The finding that "FoX often prefers higher learning rates and more heads/smaller head dimensions than the Transformer" (Section 4.1) suggests an interaction between attention granularity and forgetting that is not fully explored.

**Why not explore bidirectional / non-causal Forgetting Attention?** The paper only considers causal (autoregressive) sequence modelling. In a bidirectional setting, the forget gate mechanism would need to be redefined — there is no natural "forward in time" direction for the cumulative product to accumulate along. The paper acknowledges this as future work (Section 6). This limits the applicability of the current formulation to encoder-only or encoder-decoder architectures (e.g., BERT, T5 encoder) without modification.

**Why initialise forget gate biases `$b_f$` to zero?** For the data-dependent forget gate, all biases `$b_f$` are initialised to zero, meaning `$f_t = \sigma(w_f^\top x_t + 0) \approx 0.5$` at initialisation (since `$w_f^\top x_t$` is initialised with small values from `$\mathcal{N}(0, 0.02^2)$`). This means the model starts with no strong forgetting bias — each position initially retains about half of the past information. The model must learn to push forget gate values toward 0 (forget quickly) or 1 (retain everything) during training. In contrast, the data-independent forget gates require careful exponential-range initialisation (described in Section 3.4, Connection to ALiBi) to work at all — zero initialisation for data-independent gates performs "extremely poorly." This asymmetry highlights that the data-dependent mechanism is more robust: the input `$x_t$` provides a rich signal from which the model can learn appropriate forgetting rates, while a pure bias term has no such signal and must be carefully placed in the right range from the start.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Forgetting as Kernel-Independent Attention Decay — A Unifying Abstraction

The paper's most intellectually distinctive contribution is not the forget gate itself — forget gates have existed since LSTMs in 2000 — but the **recognition that the multiplicative forget gate mechanism is independent of the attention kernel**. This is a conceptual reframing that connects two previously separate research threads (gated recurrences and softmax attention) through a shared mathematical structure.

**What the field did before:** The dominant assumption, visible in the architecture design of every gated recurrent model from LSTMs to Mamba-2, was that forget gates belong to the *recurrent* lineage — they are mechanisms for updating a fixed-size state over time. The parallel attention forms derived in GLA (Yang et al., 2023) and Mamba-2 (Dao & Gu, 2024) were treated as convenient mathematical equivalences for training efficiency, not as revelations about the generality of the gating mechanism. Meanwhile, the Transformer community pursued positional biases (ALiBi, RoPE, learned absolute positions) and architectural modifications (sliding windows, sparse attention) to manage long-range context, without recognising that the forget gate concept from the recurrent world could be cleanly ported over.

**The reframing:** The paper demonstrates that the forget gate in gated linear attention enters the parallel form as a **multiplicative weight `F_ij` on the kernel evaluation `k(q_i, k_j)`**, and this weighting is structurally independent of which kernel is used. The linear kernel `k_φ(q, k) = φ(q)^⊤φ(k)` and the exponential kernel `k_exp(q, k) = exp(q^⊤k)` are interchangeable — swap one for the other and you get either gated linear attention or Forgetting Attention. The forget gate mechanism itself (`F_ij = ∏ f_l` accumulating multiplicatively over time) is the invariant core. This is not an analogy or an inspiration — it is an exact mathematical correspondence revealed by the parallel form.

**Why this is a fundamental shift, not incremental:** Prior work treated "gated attention" as a property of *linear* attention specifically — a technique for making linear attention competitive with softmax attention by introducing state-dependent decay. The paper shows that gating is a **general attention mechanism** that can be applied to any kernel. This transforms the forget gate from a component of specific recurrent architectures into a first-class attention primitive. It also suggests that future research should not ask "should we use recurrent models or Transformers for long context?" but rather "what gating mechanisms work best, and what kernel should we pair them with?" — a unification of the two architectural families rather than a competition between them.

**Evidence anchoring:** The derivation pathway (Section 2.2 → Equation 10 → Equation 11) provides the mathematical foundation. The empirical confirmation comes indirectly but powerfully from the ALiBi connection: ALiBi is shown to be a special case of Forgetting Attention with a fixed, data-independent forget gate `f_t = exp(-m_h)`. This means the paper's framework **subsumes the most popular data-independent positional bias method** as a constrained special case. When the paper then demonstrates (Figure 7) that relaxing the data-independence constraint (making `f_t` input-dependent) yields consistent improvements, it validates that the framework isn't just a notation change — it identifies a previously constrained degree of freedom and shows it matters empirically.

### Innovation 2: The Data-Dependence Diagnostic — Why Position-Based Forgetting Fails

The paper provides a clean empirical diagnosis of **why data-independent decay (ALiBi, RoPE, fixed forget gates) is fundamentally limited**, establishing that the critical property of a forget gate is not that it *exists*, but that it **depends on what the input tokens actually are**. This is a reframing of the positional encoding problem: it's not about encoding distance, it's about learning when distance *matters*.

**What the field did before:** The standard view, encoded in architectures from the original Transformer (sinusoidal positions) through ALiBi (linear distance bias) to RoPE (rotary distance encoding), is that attention needs position information, and position information is fundamentally about **distance** — how far apart are the query and key? This view treats all positions as equivalent: the penalty for attending 100 tokens back is the same regardless of whether those 100 tokens span a section boundary or are in the middle of a single coherent paragraph. The ALiBi slopes, the RoPE frequencies, and the sinusoidal wavelengths are all designed to encode relative distance, not relative relevance.

**The diagnostic:** The paper constructs a controlled comparison that isolates the effect of data-dependence while holding the forget gate mechanism constant. It tests three variants with the same multiplicative accumulation structure: (1) a fixed forget gate where `f_t = σ(b)` with `b` not updated during training — equivalent to ALiBi with learned biases; (2) a data-independent but learnable forget gate where `f_t = σ(b)` with `b` trained — the model can learn the optimal per-head constant decay rate but that rate is the same for all tokens; and (3) the full data-dependent forget gate `f_t = σ(w_f^⊤x_t + b_f)` — the decay rate varies per token per head based on input content. Figure 7 shows: data-independent learnable > fixed, but data-dependent > data-independent learnable, and the gap is substantial.

**Why this is more than just "learned beats fixed":** If the advantage of data-dependence were merely that it adds parameters, then a model with per-head learnable biases should perform similarly — it has additional parameters over the fixed version too. The fact that data-independent learnable gates underperform data-dependent ones despite both having trainable parameters demonstrates that **the input signal `x_t` provides information that is necessary for good forgetting decisions**. The model needs to know *what* the current token is to decide how much to forget — a section header token should trigger different forgetting than a mid-sentence token, and no single per-head constant can encode this distinction.

**The reframing of positional encoding:** This finding recasts the positional encoding problem. Rather than asking "how do we tell the model where tokens are?", the paper's results suggest asking "how do we let the model decide, based on what it's reading, which tokens to keep and which to discard?" Distance becomes an emergent property — tokens that are further away will tend to have smaller `F_ij` values on average because more forget gates have multiplied in between, but the model can override this tendency when content demands it (as shown in Figure 3, where some heads exhibit weak decay and attend across the full context despite large distances). Position is learned indirectly through the content-dependent forgetting dynamics rather than being explicitly encoded.

**Evidence anchoring:** Figure 7 shows the per-token loss for all three forget gate variants with both LLaMA and Pro architectures. The fixed and data-independent variants require careful exponential-range initialisation (`T_max` sweeps) to work at all, while the data-dependent variant is robust to zero-initialisation of biases. This asymmetry — data-dependence making initialisation less fragile — is an additional signal that input content provides a meaningful learning signal that pure biases lack. Table 3 further corroborates: adding RoPE to FoX (Pro) provides no improvement (6.62 → 6.63 perplexity), suggesting that once content-dependent forgetting is available, explicit position encoding becomes redundant.

### Innovation 3: The "Forgetting Without Losing Retrieval" Result — Separating Recency Bias from Information Erasure

The paper provides a specific empirical finding with significant architectural implications: **a forget gate can introduce a strong recency bias into attention without damaging the model's ability to retrieve distant information when needed**. This contradicts the intuitive concern that adding a mechanism designed to "forget" would necessarily impair long-range access.

**What the field assumed:** The intuition, which the paper acknowledges by making it a central research question, is that forget gates and long-context retrieval are in tension. Recurrent models have forget gates, and recurrent models lose long-context retrieval ability because their fixed-size states act as information bottlenecks. The causal arrow is ambiguous: do recurrent models fail at retrieval *because* they have forget gates (which gradually erase distant information), or *because* their state capacity is limited (forcing compression into insufficient dimension)? Most prior work conflated these two factors. The universal presence of forget gates in recurrent models that struggled with needle-in-the-haystack (Mamba-2, HGRN2, DeltaNet — all failing in Figure 4, Appendix F.5) reinforced the assumption that recency bias and retrieval are opposing forces.

**The finding:** FoX achieves near-perfect needle-in-the-haystack retrieval within its training context length (Figure 4, both easy and standard modes), matching or exceeding the standard Transformer while dramatically outperforming all recurrent baselines. Per-token loss curves (Figure 2) show FoX maintaining a monotonically decreasing slope through the full 16K training context, indicating effective utilisation of the entire context. Visualisation of the forget gate matrices (Figure 3, Figures 27–28) reveals the mechanism: **different heads learn different forgetting profiles**. Some heads exhibit strong decay (most `F_ij` entries near zero, attention focused locally), providing the recency bias. Other heads exhibit very weak decay (attention distributed across the full context), providing the retrieval capability. The model doesn't choose between recency and retrieval — it **allocates different heads to different temporal regimes**.

**Why this matters for architecture design:** This finding dismantles the (implicit or explicit) assumption that attention must make a uniform tradeoff between local and global processing. A standard Transformer with no positional decay gives all heads equal access to the full context, requiring each head to learn through its query-key projections whether to attend locally or globally — a harder learning problem. Recurrent models force all information through a single state bottleneck, making global retrieval impossible regardless of head specialisation. FoX's forget gate provides a middle ground: each head can independently specialise its temporal range through learned gating, and global retrieval is preserved because some heads can simply learn to never forget (f_t consistently near 1). This is a **capacity allocation** argument — the forget gate decouples the decision of *what* to attend to (content-based, via query-key matching) from *how far back* to attend (decay-based, via forget gates), allowing the model to allocate these two decisions to different parameters.

**Evidence anchoring:** Figure 4 is the critical piece of evidence — the needle test directly measures the model's ability to retrieve a specific fact from an arbitrary position within a long document. FoX's near-perfect scores within the training context length (both standard and easy modes) demonstrate that the forget gate does not permanently erase information; it only biases the model's default attention distribution. When a query strongly matches a distant key (as in the needle test where the model is directly prompted about the needle content), the content-based attention score `exp(q^⊤k)` can overcome even a large negative forget gate bias `D_ij`, allowing retrieval. The supplementary visualisations in Figures 27–28 provide mechanistic evidence for the head-specialisation interpretation, showing clear heterogeneity in `F` matrix sparsity patterns across heads and layers.

### Innovation 4: The Pro Block as a Cross-Paradigm Design Pattern — Recurrent Innovations Are General-Purpose

The paper's secondary contribution is an empirical demonstration that several architectural components conventional wisdom assigns to the "recurrent sequence model" category are actually **general-purpose sequence modelling enhancements** that improve Transformers independently of any recurrence or gating mechanism.

**What the field did before:** The standard LLaMA architecture (Touvron et al., 2023), which has become the de facto Transformer template, uses a minimalist design: pre-norm RMSNorm, RoPE for position encoding, SwiGLU MLP, and no output gates, QK-norm, or token shift. The recurrent modelling community developed a distinct set of architectural norms: output gates (GLA, Mamba-2), output normalisation (GLA), data-dependent token shift (Mamba, Eagle), and QK-norm (used in some vision transformers and occasionally in language models). These components were developed and evaluated in the context of recurrent or linear-attention architectures, and there was an implicit assumption — never stated but visible in practice — that they were necessary *because* of the limitations of recurrence (e.g., output gates to control state readout when the state is capacity-limited, QK-norm to stabilise linear attention training).

**The finding:** The paper's Pro block adds four components to the standard Transformer — output gates, output normalisation, QK-norm, and data-dependent KV token shift — and evaluates both FoX (Pro) and Transformer (Pro) against their LLaMA-style counterparts. Table 3 shows: FoX (Pro) at 6.62 perplexity vs. FoX (LLaMA) at 7.08, and Transformer (Pro) at 6.82 vs. Transformer (LLaMA) at 7.49 (360M parameters). The perturbation-style ablation confirms that each component contributes positively: removing QK-norm degrades performance (6.62 → 6.79), removing output gates degrades it (6.62 → 6.86), removing output norm degrades it (6.62 → 6.69), and removing KV-shift degrades it (6.62 → 6.80). For the Transformer, the incremental-style ablation shows the same pattern: adding QK-norm, output gates, output norm, and KV-shift each provide monotonic improvements.

**Why this reframes architectural innovation:** The standard narrative positions the recurrent and Transformer communities as developing separate architectural toolkits for separate model families. The Pro block results suggest a different interpretation: both communities are exploring the same underlying design space of sequence modelling primitives, and the components that work well are not specific to any particular attention mechanism. Output gates help regardless of whether the attention is linear or quadratic. QK-norm stabilises training regardless of the kernel. Token shift provides temporal smoothing that benefits any sequence model. The "recurrent vs. Transformer" framing has, to some extent, obscured which innovations are paradigm-specific and which are universal.

**Connection to the forget gate innovation:** This insight reinforces Innovation 1. Just as the forget gate is kernel-independent (it works for both linear and exponential kernels), the Pro block components are architecture-independent (they work for both FoX and the standard Transformer). The paper is implicitly arguing for a decomposition of sequence modelling into **mechanism-independent primitives** (gating, normalisation, temporal smoothing) and **mechanism-specific choices** (kernel function, state size, attention pattern). This is a more modular and productive way to think about architecture design than the current "Transformer variant" vs. "recurrent model" taxonomy.

**Evidence anchoring:** Table 3 provides the comprehensive ablation. The baseline Transformer (LLaMA) achieves 7.49 perplexity. Removing RoPE from it causes catastrophic collapse (29.30), confirming that positional encoding is non-negotiable for the standard architecture. Transformer (Pro) achieves 6.82 — a 9% improvement from the Pro components alone, without any forget gate. Adding the forget gate (i.e., FoX Pro) achieves 6.62, and removing it (i.e., the `- forget gate` ablation, which effectively makes it Transformer Pro minus RoPE) causes severe degradation to 7.40. This clean separation shows that the Pro components and the forget gate provide independent, compoundable benefits.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All models are trained on **LongCrawl64** (Buckman, 2024), a long-sequence subset of RedPajama-v2 (Together Computer, 2023) pre-tokenized with the TikToken tokenizer (OpenAI, 2022) for GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2019). The main experiments use a **45 × 2^30-token** subset (roughly 48B tokens) for training. The validation set consists of a **2 × 2^30-token** subset of the LongCrawl64 held-out set with sequences of **65536 tokens** — deliberately longer than the training context length of 16384 tokens to test length extrapolation. For short-context supplementary experiments, the paper also trains on **SlimPajama** (Soboleva et al., 2023) with a 2048-token context length, following the 340M-parameter/15B-token configuration from Yang et al. (2024).

- **Base model(s).** The primary experiments use models with roughly **760M non-embedding parameters** and 24 layers. Additional ablation and scaling analyses use configurations at **360M parameters** (24 layers, `d_model` = 1024) trained on ~7.5B tokens, and **125M parameters** (12 layers, `d_model` = 768) trained on ~2.7B tokens. All models follow a decoder-only (causal) Transformer architecture. The head dimension is tuned within {64, 128} independently for FoX and the Transformer, with FoX generally preferring smaller head dimensions (64) and the Transformer preferring larger ones (128). Weight initialisation follows the HuggingFace LLaMA convention: `N(0, 0.02^2)` for all linear layer weights and embedding parameters.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **per-token loss** `L(i)` on the validation set, defined as:

  $$L(i) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{j=1}^M -\log\left[(p_i^{(j)})^\top y_i^{(j)}\right]$$

  where `M` is the number of validation sequences (2 in the main setting), `y_i^{(j)}` is the one-hot target for the `i`-th token in the `j`-th sequence, and `p_i^{(j)}` is the model's predicted probability vector. Per-token loss is chosen because its slope as a function of token index `i` directly reflects context utilisation: a monotonically decreasing curve indicates the model is using tokens from across the full context, while plateaus indicate an effective context horizon beyond which tokens are ignored. The paper also reports **perplexity** `P(l) = exp((1/l) * Σ L(i))`, but warns (Appendix C) that perplexity curves can misleadingly continue decreasing even after per-token loss plateaus, since they average over all positions. Downstream evaluation uses **accuracy**, **length-normalised accuracy** (for HellaSwag, ARC-challenge, OpenbookQA), **F1**, **Rouge-L**, **edit similarity**, and **perplexity** depending on the benchmark, with GPT-4o-2024-08-06 scoring on a 1–10 scale for the needle-in-the-haystack test.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against:
  - **Transformer (LLaMA):** Standard LLaMA architecture with RoPE (θ = 500,000, following Xiong et al., 2023), pre-norm RMSNorm, and SwiGLU MLP.
  - **Transformer (Pro):** The standard Transformer augmented with the Pro block design (QK-norm, output gates, output normalisation, KV token shift), but still using RoPE and no forget gate.
  - **Mamba-2** (Dao & Gu, 2024): A state-space model with structured state space duality, representing the recurrent sequence model class.
  - **HGRN2** (Qin et al., 2024a): A gated linear RNN with state expansion.
  - **DeltaNet** (Yang et al., 2024): A linear attention model with a delta rule update.
  - **Samba** (Ren et al., 2024): A hybrid architecture combining sliding window attention (window size 2048) with Mamba layers.
  - **Transformer-SWA (LLaMA):** A standard LLaMA Transformer where full quadratic attention is replaced by sliding window attention with a window size of 2048.
  
  All implementations are based on the Flash Linear Attention repository (Yang & Zhang, 2024), with FoX's Forgetting Attention implemented in Triton via Flag Attention (FlagOpen, 2023).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** This paper does not use a "generations" budget in the inference-scaling sense — it evaluates language model quality directly as perplexity and downstream accuracy. Training compute is controlled by comparing models at the same parameter count (roughly 760M non-embedding parameters for main results) and same training data (48B tokens of LongCrawl64, except where training token counts are varied for analysis). Table 6 reports estimated forward-pass FLOPs per token: ~2.72 × 10^9 for all quadratic-attention models (FoX and Transformer variants, scaling as `2N + 2 * n_layers * d_model * L`), versus ~1.54–1.65 × 10^9 for the recurrent models (Mamba-2, HGRN2, DeltaNet), which scale linearly in context length `L` rather than quadratically. Throughput measurements (Table 6) show the recurrent models enjoy 44–48k tokens/sec on 4 NVIDIA L40S GPUs vs. 27–38k tokens/sec for the FoX/Transformer variants, though the latter's Triton implementations are noted as unoptimised.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** Learning rates are tuned individually for each model in the main 760M/48B experiment via grid search over `{1×10^i, 2×10^i, 5×10^i}` (Table 5), with the optimum selected based on average training loss over the last 512 × 2^20 tokens. For smaller-scale configurations (360M, 125M), learning rates are tuned for Transformer (LLaMA) at the 16K context length and transferred to other models and training context lengths, which the authors acknowledge may disadvantage some models (e.g., FoX prefers higher learning rates). Three random seeds are run for 360M-parameter FoX (LLaMA) to assess variance, showing "small variance across seeds" (Appendix F.9, Figure 26). Training uses AdamW with (β₁, β₂) = (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.1, gradient clipping 1.0, linear warmup for the first 256 × 2^20 tokens, and cosine decay to zero. Each training batch contains 0.5 × 2^20 tokens. bfloat16 mixed-precision training is used throughout. The paper explicitly notes that LongCrawl64 applies random sequence rolling (via `np.roll` with random shift) to remove position bias, ensuring that tokens at different absolute positions are equally difficult in expectation given the same context length.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Long-Context Language Modeling (Section 4.2)

The headline finding from Figure 2: with either the LLaMA or Pro architecture, **FoX outperforms the standard Transformer on per-token loss both within and beyond the training context length**. At 760M parameters and 48B training tokens, and examining the per-token loss curves:

- **FoX (Pro) achieves the lowest per-token loss across all token positions** from 0 to 64K, maintaining a monotonically decreasing slope through the full 16K training context length (vertical dashed line in Figure 2 left). Beyond 16K, the loss continues decreasing, indicating length extrapolation capability.

- **FoX (LLaMA) tracks slightly above FoX (Pro)** but consistently below both Transformer variants.

- **Transformer (Pro) and Transformer (LLaMA)** both maintain decreasing per-token loss within the training context length, demonstrating effective context utilisation. However, their absolute loss values are higher than their FoX counterparts at every token position. Beyond 16K, the Transformer variants show continued decreasing loss similar to FoX.

- **All recurrent models show early plateaus.** Mamba-2, HGRN2, and DeltaNet all exhibit per-token loss that flattens starting at approximately 5K tokens and substantially plateaus by 10K tokens (Figure 2 left). This directly visualises their effective context horizon — beyond 10K tokens, additional context provides negligible benefit for prediction. In terms of absolute loss values, FoX (Pro) "clearly outperforms HGRN2, DeltaNet and Mamba-2" (Section 4.2).

The perplexity curves (Figure 2 right) corroborate: FoX (Pro) achieves the lowest perplexity (~5.2 at 16K validation context length), followed by FoX (LLaMA), Transformer (Pro), Transformer (LLaMA), and then a cluster of recurrent models at ~5.4–5.5.

**Context utilisation interpretation (Appendix C):** Because LongCrawl64 randomly rolls sequences to remove position bias, the difficulty of predicting the `i`-th token given the same-length preceding context is roughly uniform. Therefore, if `L(100) < L(90)`, the first 10 tokens in the context genuinely contribute to the prediction at position 100. The steeply decreasing slope of FoX and Transformer curves within 16K indicates effective use of the entire training context, while the recurrent models' plateaus indicate an inability to leverage tokens beyond ~5–10K distance.

#### Needle-in-the-Haystack (Section 4.3)

Figure 4 presents needle-in-the-haystack results scored by GPT-4o on a 1–10 scale, testing both an "easy mode" (needle contains both question and answer) and a "standard mode" (needle contains only the answer; the question is asked separately after the document). The key results within the 16K training context length (vertical dashed line):

- **FoX (Pro) and FoX (LLaMA) both achieve near-perfect (score ~9–10) retrieval across all document lengths and depth percentages within 16K**, in both easy and standard modes. This demonstrates that the forget gate does not impair the Transformer's long-range retrieval ability.

- **Transformer (Pro) and Transformer (LLaMA) achieve perfect retrieval in easy mode but show occasional failures in standard mode** — the heatmaps in Figure 4 show some red/orange cells within the 16K boundary on the standard mode panels, indicating incomplete retrieval. The authors note these are "small models without instruction-tuning" and expect larger/instruct models would close this gap.

- **All recurrent models fail significantly even within the training context length.** Mamba-2 (Figure 4, bottom rows) shows extensive failures in both easy and standard modes within 16K, with many cells scoring in the 1–4 range. DeltaNet performs similarly poorly. HGRN2 (shown in Appendix F.5, Figure 15) "performs even worse than Mamba-2."

- **Beyond 16K:** FoX (Pro), FoX (LLaMA), and Transformer (Pro) show partial extrapolation, with successful retrieval extending to document lengths of 20–25K in some configurations, but degrading at longer lengths. The paper explicitly examines the sensitivity of this extrapolation to hyperparameters (Section 4.3, Figure 5, and Appendix F.7) and finds it varies with both the number of training tokens and the learning rate. Notably, **more training tokens often lead to worse extrapolation** — FoX (Pro) trained on 16B tokens extrapolates better than the same model trained on 48B tokens (Figure 5). The authors interpret this as models "gradually 'overfitting' to their training context length during training" (Section 4.3).

- **Transformer-SWA and Samba** (Appendix F.4, Figure 14) both show "early plateau in the per-token loss" and perform "poorly in the needle-retrieval task," confirming that sliding window attention permanently discards information beyond the window regardless of relevance.

#### Short-Context Downstream Tasks (Section 4.4, Table 1)

Evaluated on 12 tasks from LM-evaluation-harness (Gao et al., 2024) at roughly 760M parameters / 48B training tokens, all zero-shot:

- **FoX (Pro) achieves the highest average score (50.88)** across the 12-task suite, outperforming Transformer (Pro) at 50.39, FoX (LLaMA) at 49.82, and Transformer (LLaMA) at 49.09. The recurrent models cluster in the 47.99–50.21 range, with Mamba-2 at 50.21.

- Task-level results show FoX (Pro) leads on Wikitext perplexity (23.04 vs. Transformer Pro's 24.12), LAMBADA perplexity (14.91 vs. 16.16), PIQA (64.09 vs. 64.04, marginal), HellaSwag length-normalised accuracy (38.39 vs. 36.60), ARC-easy (52.23 vs. 51.98), ARC-challenge length-normalised accuracy (26.54 vs. 25.26), COPA (71.00 vs. 62.00), and SciQA (85.10 vs. 82.80).

- **FoX (LLaMA) outperforms Transformer (LLaMA)** on 10 of 12 tasks, with the gap being particularly large on COPA (69.00 vs. 66.00), SciQA (81.90 vs. 78.90), and LAMBADA accuracy (40.17 vs. 38.27).

- The Pro architecture provides independent gains: Transformer (Pro) outperforms Transformer (LLaMA) on 7 of 12 tasks (average 50.39 vs. 49.09), and FoX (Pro) outperforms FoX (LLaMA) on 9 of 12 tasks (average 50.88 vs. 49.82).

#### Long-Context Downstream Tasks (Section 4.4, Table 2)

Evaluated on 14 tasks from LongBench (Bai et al., 2023), using the default metrics for each task (F1, Rouge-L, accuracy, or edit similarity):

- **FoX (Pro) and Transformer (Pro) perform on par**, with FoX (Pro) leading on some tasks (NarrativeQA: 13.38 vs. 11.42; MultiFieldQA: 28.73 vs. 22.89; Musique: 6.49 vs. 6.09; MultiNews: 12.27 vs. 8.11; TREC: 63.5 vs. 55.0; SamSum: 22.74 vs. 30.66) and Transformer (Pro) leading on others (HotpotQA: 19.58 vs. 15.27; QMSum: 13.51 vs. 10.7; RepoBench-P: 14.25 vs. 9.1).

- **Both FoX and Transformer substantially outperform recurrent models** on most tasks. For example, on NarrativeQA: FoX (Pro) 13.38, Transformer (Pro) 11.42, vs. Mamba-2 10.65, DeltaNet 9.36. On TREC: FoX (Pro) 63.5, Transformer (Pro) 55.0, vs. Mamba-2 28.5, HGRN2 16.5.

- The FoX vs. Transformer gap is smaller on long-context tasks than on short-context tasks — FoX's advantages appear concentrated in tasks where local coherence and recency management matter more than pure long-range retrieval.

#### Short-Context Training on SlimPajama (Appendix F.3)

At 340M parameters trained on ~15B tokens of SlimPajama with a 2048-token context length:

- **Per-token loss (Figure 12):** FoX (Pro) and Transformer (Pro) are nearly identical within the 2048 training context, with FoX (Pro) slightly lower. Mamba-2 is competitive. Beyond 2048, FoX (Pro) extrapolates notably better than Transformer (Pro).

- **Downstream tasks (Table 7):** FoX (Pro) achieves the highest average (43.29) among the paper's models, outperforming Transformer (Pro) at 42.74. However, FoX (LLaMA) at 41.76 does not clearly outperform Transformer (LLaMA) at 41.34, and Mamba-2 at 41.85 is competitive. The authors note these are "small-scale experiments without extensive hyperparameter tuning" and results "might not transfer to larger scales."

#### Scaling Behavior Across Model Sizes and Context Lengths (Section 4.5, Figure 6)

The paper investigates how the advantage of FoX over the Transformer varies with model scale and training context length, using both 350M-parameter models trained on 7.5B tokens and 760M-parameter models trained on 16B tokens, across training context lengths of 4096, 8192, 16384, and 32768 tokens:

- **Advantage of FoX increases as training context length increases.** At 350M/7.5B and a 4096-token context length, FoX (Pro) and Transformer (Pro) per-token loss curves nearly overlap. At 16384, FoX (Pro) is clearly lower across all token positions. At 32768, the gap widens further, with FoX (Pro) maintaining a steeper downward slope deeper into the context.

- **Advantage of FoX decreases as model size (and training tokens) increases.** At 760M/16B, the gap between FoX (Pro) and Transformer (Pro) at 16384 context length is smaller than at 350M/7.5B. The authors interpret this as: "larger models can better model long contexts, and thus forgetting may be less important" (Section 4.5).

- **Long-context training damages short-context performance** for both FoX and the Transformer — a known effect the authors attribute to "reduced document diversity within training batches" as longer sequences mean fewer independent documents per batch.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

All ablation results below use 360M-parameter models trained on ~7.5B tokens of LongCrawl64 unless specified otherwise. The reference corruptions are relative to FoX (Pro), which achieves 6.62 perplexity at a 16384-token validation context length (Table 3).

- **Forget gate vs. RoPE (Table 3, also Figure 8 and Figure 9):** Removing RoPE from Transformer (LLaMA) causes catastrophic degradation (7.49 → 29.30 perplexity), confirming that the standard Transformer is structurally dependent on positional embeddings. Adding RoPE to FoX (LLaMA) provides a minor improvement (7.25 → 7.19), while adding RoPE to FoX (Pro) provides essentially no change (6.62 → 6.63). Removing the forget gate from FoX (Pro) while keeping RoPE (i.e., the Transformer (Pro) configuration) yields 6.82 perplexity — a 3.0% degradation. Removing both the forget gate and RoPE from FoX (Pro) (the last row of Table 3) causes severe degradation to 7.40 — an 11.8% increase in perplexity — showing that at least one source of positional/decay signal is essential, but the forget gate alone is sufficient.

- **QK-norm (Table 3, "FoX (Pro) - QK-norm"):** Removing QK-norm from FoX (Pro) increases perplexity from 6.62 to 6.79 (+2.6%). The paper also notes, from a smaller-scale 125M-parameter ablation (Appendix F.2, Figure 11), that "QK-norm seems to be helpful for length extrapolation" — its benefit extends beyond in-distribution performance.

- **Output gate (Table 3, "FoX (Pro) - output gate"):** Removing the output gate increases perplexity from 6.62 to 6.86 (+3.6%). This is the largest single-component degradation among the Pro ablations, highlighting the importance of gating the attention output before it enters the residual stream.

- **Output normalisation (Table 3, "FoX (Pro) - output norm"):** Removing the output normalisation (RMSNorm before the output gate) increases perplexity from 6.62 to 6.69 (+1.1%).

- **KV-shift (Table 3, "FoX (Pro) - KV-shift"):** Removing the data-dependent key-value token shift increases perplexity from 6.62 to 6.80 (+2.7%). This mechanism provides temporal smoothing of key and value representations, and its positive contribution holds for both FoX and the Transformer.

- **Incremental addition of components to Transformer (LLaMA) (Table 3, top half, and Figure 8):** Starting from Transformer (LLaMA) at 7.49 perplexity: adding the forget gate (→ FoX LLaMA) yields 7.08 (-5.5%); adding QK-norm (→ 6.88, -2.8% further); adding the output gate (→ 6.80, -1.2%); adding output normalisation + KV-shift (→ 6.62, the full FoX Pro, -2.6% further). The cumulative improvement from the full Pro stack over the LLaMA baseline is 11.6% for FoX.

- **Data-dependent vs. data-independent vs. fixed forget gates (Section 4.5, Figure 7):** This is the key ablation isolating the value of data-dependence. For both the LLaMA architecture (left/middle-left panels) and the Pro architecture (middle-right/right panels), and for various choices of the `T_max` hyperparameter controlling the decay horizon (256, 2048, 16384): the **data-dependent forget gate always achieves the lowest per-token loss**, consistently outperforming both the data-independent learnable variant (`f_t = σ(b_head)`) and the fixed variant (`f_t = σ(b_head)` with `b_head` not updated during training). The gap is substantial — roughly 0.2–0.3 nats in per-token loss across most token positions. The data-independent and fixed variants are themselves sensitive to `T_max`, with `T_max = 16384` performing best at long context lengths (unsurprisingly, since it allows slower decay), but even the best `T_max` for these variants underperforms the data-dependent gate. The data-independent/fixed variants also require careful exponential-range initialisation of the biases `b^{(h)}` using `T_min` and `T_max` (Appendix D); without this initialisation, they perform "extremely poorly."

- **Forget gate initialisation (Appendix D):** For data-dependent forget gates, zero-initialisation of `b_f` is sufficient — the input-dependent term `w_f^⊤ x_t` can learn the appropriate range. For data-independent gates, zero-initialisation causes "extremely poor" performance; the exponential-range initialisation with `T_min = 2` and various `T_max` is essential. This asymmetry reinforces that input content provides a learning signal that pure bias terms cannot bootstrap from.

- **Sensitivity of length extrapolation to hyperparameters (Section 4.3, Figure 5, Appendix F.7, Figures 22–24):** The needle retrieval and per-token loss patterns beyond the 16K training context length are **sensitive to both the number of training tokens and the learning rate**. For FoX (Pro), models trained on 16B tokens with LR = 0.001 or 0.002 both extrapolate to 32K with high retrieval accuracy, while the 48B-token models (same LRs) show substantially degraded extrapolation — more red/orange cells in the needle heatmaps and flatter per-token loss curves beyond 16K. For Transformer (Pro), the pattern is similar but noisier. This suggests that length extrapolation is not a stable property of the architecture but an emergent behaviour that depends on optimisation dynamics and that models may "overfit" to their training context length with extended training.

- **Stability across random seeds (Appendix F.9, Figure 26):** Three seeds of 360M-parameter FoX (LLaMA) show "small variance across seeds" in per-token loss curves. The curves are nearly indistinguishable across the full 64K validation context, confirming that the reported improvements are not noise artifacts.

- **Additional comparison with sliding window and Samba (Appendix F.4, Figures 13–14, Tables 8–9):** Both Transformer-SWA (2048-token window) and Samba (hybrid sliding window + Mamba) show early plateaus in per-token loss (Figure 13), poor needle retrieval beyond their window size (Figure 14), and competitive but not superior short-context downstream performance (Table 8). This confirms that sliding window attention permanently loses information beyond the window and cannot match full quadratic attention or FoX on long-context retrieval.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: FoX outperforms the Transformer on long-context language modelling, length extrapolation, and short-context downstream tasks.**

The evidence supports this claim for the specific model sizes (125M–760M parameters), training data volumes (2.7B–48B tokens), and context lengths (4K–16K) tested, but with important boundary conditions. The per-token loss curves (Figures 2, 16–18) consistently show FoX variants below their Transformer counterparts at the same architecture tier, and the gap widens with longer training context lengths. The ablation in Figure 6 directly quantifies the dependence: the advantage is largest at small model sizes and long context lengths, and narrows at larger model sizes. This means the claim holds most strongly in the **relatively small model, long context** regime — exactly the setting where a pure Transformer might be expected to struggle with learning how to manage distant context. Whether the advantage persists at billion-parameter scales and beyond (where Transformers have more capacity to learn implicit recency biases through query-key interactions) is untested.

The short-context downstream gains (Table 1, FoX Pro: 50.88 vs. Transformer Pro: 50.39) are real but modest — a 1% relative improvement on the average across 12 tasks. Individual task swings (e.g., COPA: 71.00 vs. 62.00) hint that the benefit is task-dependent, but with a 500-question test set and no reported confidence intervals, the statistical reliability of per-task differences is uncertain. The long-context downstream results (Table 2) show FoX and Transformer performing "on par" (the paper's own characterisation), with no consistent winner — some tasks favour FoX, others favour the Transformer. This is the weakest part of the claim: on the downstream tasks specifically designed to test long-context understanding, the forget gate does not provide a clear advantage. This raises the question of whether the per-token loss improvements translate to practical long-context reasoning benefits, or reflect improved modelling of local statistical patterns that affect perplexity more than task accuracy.

A missing experiment that would strengthen this claim: scaling to larger models and longer contexts (e.g., 1B+ parameters, 32K+ context) to verify the trend suggested by Figure 6 — that the gap narrows with scale. If the advantage shrinks to zero at 7B parameters, the practical value of the forget gate for large-scale deployments would be limited to length extrapolation settings.

**Claim 2: FoX retains the Transformer's superior long-context retrieval capabilities over recurrent sequence models, achieving near-perfect needle-in-the-haystack accuracy.**

This claim is strongly supported by Figure 4. The contrast between FoX's near-perfect retrieval and Mamba-2/DeltaNet/HGRN2's extensive failures within the training context length is stark and unambiguous. The mechanistic insight — that different heads learn different forgetting profiles, with some retaining information across the full context — is visually corroborated by the forget gate matrix visualisations in Figure 3 and Figures 27–28.

However, there is an asymmetry in the comparison: the recurrent baselines (Mamba-2, HGRN2, DeltaNet) are evaluated at the same parameter count and training data, but they have lower theoretical FLOPs per token (Table 6) due to their linear complexity. The paper acknowledges this explicitly: "an exact FLOPs-matched comparison would be interesting, it will require recalibrating the scaling law for the long-context setting and is beyond the scope of this work." This means the recurrent models may be operating at a disadvantage — if they were scaled up to match the FLOPs of the quadratic-attention models, they might close some of the retrieval gap. The paper's contribution is better framed as: **at equal parameter count and training tokens, FoX matches Transformer-level long-context retrieval while recurrent models do not** — not that no recurrent model could ever match it with sufficient scale.

The needle test also has a specific format (retrieve a single sentence from a long document) that may favour the Transformer's exact token-level access over recurrent models' compressed state representations. Different long-context tasks (multi-hop reasoning, summarisation) might show different relative strengths, and Table 2's LongBench results — where recurrent models are sometimes competitive (e.g., Mamba-2 achieves 15.17 on RepoBench-P vs. FoX Pro's 9.1) — suggest the needle test alone does not tell the full story.

**Claim 3: The forget gate must be data-dependent; fixed (ALiBi) and data-independent forget gates underperform.**

Strongly supported by Figure 7 and Table 3. The controlled sweep over `T_max` values for data-independent and fixed gates, combined with the clear separation from the data-dependent curve, leaves little room for doubt. The fact that data-independent learnable gates (which have trainable parameters) still underperform data-dependent gates demonstrates that the input signal `x_t` provides information that a per-head constant cannot capture.

A limitation: the paper only tests the data-independent gate in the form `f_t = σ(b_head)`, which restricts each head to a single decay constant for all tokens. There may be intermediate designs — e.g., a data-independent gate that varies with absolute position or with a learned embedding of the token index — that could capture some of the data-dependence benefit without being fully input-dependent. These are not explored. Additionally, the comparison to ALiBi is via the equivalence `f_t = exp(-m)`, but the paper does not directly compare against an ALiBi Transformer trained under identical conditions (same dataset, same hyperparameter tuning). The fixed forget gate with exponential-range initialisation is *equivalent* to ALiBi, but whether the initialisation and training procedure reproduce exactly the behaviour of a standard ALiBi implementation is not verified.

**Claim 4: The Pro block design significantly improves both FoX and the Transformer.**

Supported by Table 3. The LLaMA-to-Pro delta for both FoX (7.08 → 6.62 perplexity) and the Transformer (7.49 → 6.82 perplexity) is substantial. The perturbation ablation confirms each component contributes positively. However, the Pro block combines four components simultaneously, and there is no full factorial ablation exploring interactions between them. It is possible that some components are redundant when others are present — for example, output normalisation might be less important when QK-norm is already stabilising the query-key dot products. The incremental addition order in Table 3 (forget gate → QK-norm → output gate → output norm → KV-shift) is one specific ordering, and the contribution of each component might depend on what was added before it. A proper interaction analysis would require a more systematic design.

More fundamentally, the Pro block components (output gates, output normalisation, QK-norm, KV-shift) add parameters and computation that are not fully accounted for in the main perplexity comparison. The paper states that MLP parameters are reduced to keep total parameter count constant, but the per-token FLOPs formula in Table 6 (`2N + 2 * n_layers * d_model * L`) is listed as identical for all FoX and Transformer variants — if the Pro block adds operations (the KV-shift interpolation, the output gate and normalisation, the QK-norm), these should increase the constant factor, yet the table does not reflect this. Either the formula is approximate, or the additional computation is negligible, but this is not clarified.

**Cross-cutting concerns:**

1. **Single training dataset.** All long-context results are on LongCrawl64, a specific long-sequence subset of RedPajama-v2. Whether FoX's advantages transfer to other long-context corpora (books, code, scientific papers, multilingual data) is untested. The SlimPajama short-context experiments provide some evidence of transfer but at a much smaller scale (340M parameters, 2048 context).

2. **Single tokenizer.** All experiments use the GPT-2 TikToken tokenizer. Modern LLMs (LLaMA, Mistral) use different tokenizers with different vocabulary sizes and compression rates. The forget gate's behaviour might interact with tokenization — a tokenizer that produces longer sequences for the same text would give the forget gate more opportunities to decay, which could change the optimal forgetting rates.

3. **Unoptimised Triton implementation.** The throughput comparison (Table 6) shows FoX (Pro) at 27k tokens/sec vs. Transformer (LLaMA) at 38k tokens/sec on identical hardware. The paper attributes this to the CUDA-optimised FlashAttention vs. Triton-based implementation and expects parity with proper CUDA optimisation, but this is an unverified claim. Until a CUDA-level Forgetting Attention kernel is demonstrated, the practical throughput cost of the forget gate remains uncertain.

4. **No bidirectional evaluation.** The forget gate mechanism is inherently causal — it accumulates multiplicatively forward in time. The paper does not test a bidirectional variant, which would be necessary for encoder-only models (BERT-style) or encoder-decoder architectures. This limits the claimed generality of the approach.

5. **Positional embedding claims require qualification.** The paper claims FoX "does not require any positional embeddings." This is true within the tested context (16K training, some extrapolation), but the extrapolation behaviour is shown to be hyperparameter-sensitive (Figure 5, Appendix F.7), with more training tokens sometimes degrading extrapolation. If models overfit their training context length, the claim of not needing positional embeddings may hold only when the inference context length matches or only modestly exceeds the training length — exactly the regime where learned positional embeddings also work. The absence of systematic extrapolation evaluation across scales and training durations weakens the strength of this claim.

6. **Test sets are small and evaluation is zero-shot.** The needle test uses GPT-4o scoring on a 1–10 scale, which introduces evaluator model variance that is neither quantified nor discussed. The downstream evaluations (LM-eval-harness, LongBench) use standard metrics on fixed test sets, but for a 760M-parameter model, many of the scores are low (e.g., ARC-challenge accuracy ~24–26%), making it difficult to distinguish meaningful architectural improvements from noise. Confidence intervals or statistical tests are not reported for any downstream metric.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Scale Ceiling: All Experiments Cap at 760M Parameters and 48B Training Tokens

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire empirical case for FoX rests on experiments conducted at a single relatively small scale: 760M non-embedding parameters trained on 48B tokens with a 16K training context length, with supplementary ablations at 125M–360M parameters. The paper explicitly acknowledges this:

> "due to our limited computing resources, our main experiments only use models up to 760M parameters, 48B tokens, and a training context length of 16384 tokens. Thus, an important direction for future work is to test FoX at larger scales." (Section 6)

**The consequence.** The paper's own scaling analysis (Section 4.5, Figure 6) shows that the advantage of FoX over the Transformer **shrinks as model size and training tokens increase**. At 350M parameters trained on 7.5B tokens, the gap between FoX (Pro) and Transformer (Pro) at 16K context length is clearly visible. At 760M parameters trained on 16B tokens, the gap at the same context length has narrowed visibly — the per-token loss curves are closer together. This trend, combined with the authors' interpretation that "larger models can better model long contexts, and thus forgetting may be less important," raises a sharp question: does the advantage asymptotically approach zero at the billion-parameter scale and beyond? If larger Transformers can learn implicit recency biases through the query-key interaction alone (which becomes easier with more capacity and more training), the forget gate could become redundant — an architectural complication that adds parameters and implementation burden without providing returns at deployment scale. The paper provides no evidence to rule this out. Conversely, if the advantage stabilises at some non-zero value, the paper provides no evidence for that either. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt FoX for a 7B-parameter model trained on trillions of tokens is operating entirely outside the paper's evidence envelope.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 6 (left panel: 350M/7.5B tokens; right panel: 760M/16B tokens) is the only scaling analysis. Both panels show the gap narrowing with scale. There is no experiment at 1B+ parameters, no experiment with more than 48B training tokens, and no training context length beyond 32K (and only in the supplementary configuration). The SlimPajama experiments (Appendix F.3) use 340M parameters and a 2K context — a substantially different regime from the paper's main claims about long-context benefits.

**Mitigation status.** The authors explicitly flag this as future work and do not attempt to extrapolate or predict scaling behaviour. The paper's claims are appropriately scoped to the tested scale, but the title and framing ("Forgetting Transformer") imply a general architectural contribution whose generality is unverified at practical scales.

### Difficulty Estimation: No Cheap, Practical Method for Setting Forget Gate Hyperparameters

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper demonstrates that data-independent forget gates (including those equivalent to ALiBi) require careful exponential-range initialisation of the per-head biases using two hyperparameters, `T_min` and `T_max`, that control the effective decay horizons. The paper states:

> "For these data-independent forget gate designs, we find it crucial to initialize b^{(h)} properly." (Section 4.5)

For data-dependent forget gates, this sensitivity is absent — zero-initialisation suffices — but a different question emerges: **how should a practitioner tune the forget gate's learning rate, and how does it interact with other hyperparameters?** The paper finds that "FoX often prefers higher learning rates and more heads/smaller head dimensions than the Transformer" (Section 4.1) and tunes these separately for each model in the main experiment, but provides no systematic guidance for how to set these hyperparameters in a new setting (different model size, different dataset, different context length). The interaction between forget gate training dynamics and overall model convergence is not analysed.

**The consequence.** A practitioner adopting FoX faces an expanded hyperparameter search space: they must tune the head dimension (which affects the number of independent forgetting patterns), the learning rate (which FoX prefers higher than the standard Transformer for reasons that are not explained), and potentially the forget gate initialisation if they experiment with data-independent variants. The paper provides evidence that these choices matter — Figure 5 and Appendix F.7 show that length extrapolation behaviour varies substantially with both learning rate and number of training tokens — but provides no methodology for making these choices without extensive trial-and-error. In the standard Transformer, the head dimension and learning rate are tuned once and often transfer across scales (following scaling law prescriptions). The paper does not establish whether FoX hyperparameters transfer similarly, or whether each new model scale requires a fresh search. This is a practical barrier to adoption that the paper does not address.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 5 reports the individually tuned learning rates for the main experiment (FoX Pro: 2e-3, Transformer Pro: 1e-3, FoX LLaMA: 1e-3, Transformer LLaMA: 5e-4). The head dimension tuning (64 for FoX, 128 for Transformer) is reported in Table 4. For the smaller-scale experiments, the paper tunes head dimensions and learning rates for Transformer (LLaMA) at 16K context and transfers them to all other models and context lengths, which the authors acknowledge "may disadvantage some models (e.g., FoX typically prefers higher learning rates than the Transformer)." The sensitivity analysis in Figure 5 shows that FoX (Pro) trained with LR=0.001 vs. LR=0.002 at 16B tokens produces visibly different extrapolation behaviour. There is no learning rate sensitivity analysis for the data-dependent forget gate parameters (`w_f`, `b_f`) specifically.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper provides no hyperparameter transfer guidelines, no scaling law for forget gate learning rates, and no analysis of how sensitive the reported gains are to suboptimal hyperparameter choices. The learning rate tuning is described as a grid search until "locally optimal" values are found — a pragmatic approach for a research paper but not a deployment recipe.

### Extrapolation Instability: Length Generalisation Is Sensitive to Training Duration and Not a Reliable Property

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper presents FoX's ability to extrapolate beyond its training context length (16K → 32K → 64K on the validation set and needle tests) as an advantage. However, Section 4.3 and Appendix F.7 reveal that this extrapolation is **hyperparameter-sensitive and degrades with more training**:

> "we find that the extrapolation behaviors of these models could be hyperparameter-dependent... more training tokens often leads to worse extrapolation, indicating that the models may be gradually 'overfitting' to their training context length during training." (Section 4.3)

**The consequence.** This is not merely an inconvenience — it undermines a core claimed advantage. If FoX's length extrapolation is a transient property that peaks at intermediate training durations and degrades with continued training, then it is not a reliable architectural property. A model trained to convergence on a 16K context length may extrapolate *worse* than a partially trained checkpoint, meaning the practitioner faces a tradeoff between in-distribution performance and extrapolation ability that is not captured by the standard training loss. This also complicates the paper's claim that FoX "does not require any positional embeddings": while FoX does not *need* positional embeddings for in-distribution performance, its extrapolation to longer contexts without them is brittle in ways that models with explicit positional encodings (such as RoPE with a sufficiently large base angle) may not be. The paper acknowledges that the standard Transformer with RoPE also partially extrapolates, and the FoX advantage in this regime is not cleanly established.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 5 (main text) and Figures 22–24 (Appendix F.7) show needle retrieval and per-token loss for FoX (Pro) and Transformer (Pro) at 16B vs. 48B training tokens with two learning rates each. At 16B tokens, LR=0.001, FoX (Pro) extrapolates well (high retrieval scores to ~32K, smoothly decreasing per-token loss beyond 16K). At 48B tokens, the same LR shows substantially degraded extrapolation — more retrieval failures beyond 16K, flatter per-token loss curves. Transformer (Pro) shows similar degradation patterns. The paper does not report extrapolation for the other recurrent baselines (Mamba-2, HGRN2, DeltaNet have no meaningful extrapolation to degrade) and does not test the data-independent or fixed forget gate variants in this analysis.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies the phenomenon but does not resolve it. The suggested mechanism — overfitting to training context length — is plausible but unverified (no ablation showing that training with variable-length sequences prevents the degradation). The authors do not propose a mitigation (e.g., context length schedule, regularisation, or architectural modification to preserve extrapolation). This limitation is presented as an empirical observation, not as a solved or bounded problem.

### FLOPs and Throughput Accounting: Headline Comparisons Are Not Compute-Matched

**The assumption or constraint.** All comparisons between FoX/Transformer variants and recurrent models (Mamba-2, HGRN2, DeltaNet) are conducted at equal parameter count and equal training tokens. However, the recurrent models have fundamentally lower computational complexity per token — O(L) vs. O(L²) — due to their linear attention formulation. Table 6 reports estimated forward-pass FLOPs: ~2.72 × 10⁹ per token for all FoX and Transformer variants, versus ~1.54–1.65 × 10⁹ for the recurrent models — a ~1.7–1.8× difference. The paper acknowledges:

> "an exact FLOPs-matched comparison would be interesting, it will require recalibrating the scaling law for the long-context setting and is beyond the scope of this work." (Appendix B.2)

**The consequence.** The paper's central claim that "FoX retains the Transformer's superior long-context capabilities over recurrent sequence models" is evaluated under conditions where the recurrent models have effectively received less compute. If Mamba-2 were scaled up to match the FLOPs of FoX (Pro) — either through more parameters, more training tokens, or both — the performance gap might narrow or close. The same applies to HGRN2 and DeltaNet. This does not invalidate the paper's findings (parameter-matched comparisons are standard and informative), but it complicates the claim of superiority. A practitioner choosing between architectures should care about performance per FLOP, not performance per parameter, since training and inference budgets are measured in FLOPs. The paper provides evidence that FoX is better at a given parameter count, but does not establish that it is better at a given compute budget — which is what matters for resource-allocation decisions.

Additionally, within the FoX/Transformer comparison, the paper uses an unoptimised Triton implementation of Forgetting Attention versus the highly optimised CUDA FlashAttention for the standard Transformer. Table 6 shows Transformer (LLaMA) at 38K tokens/sec versus FoX (Pro) at 27K tokens/sec and FoX (LLaMA) at 30K tokens/sec — a 21–30% throughput gap. The paper attributes this entirely to implementation quality, expecting parity with CUDA optimisation, but this is unverified. If a production-grade CUDA implementation of Forgetting Attention incurs even a 5–10% overhead from the additional cumulative sum loads and bias additions, the perplexity gains would need to be weighed against this throughput cost.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 6 provides the FLOPs formulas and measured throughput. The throughput gap between the CUDA FlashAttention (Transformer LLaMA, 38K tok/s) and the Triton implementations (FoX Pro, 27K tok/s; Transformer Pro, 30K tok/s; FoX LLaMA, 30K tok/s) is attributed to "without significant optimization." However, the paper does not profile how much of this gap is due to the forget gate computation versus general Triton overhead — the fact that Transformer (Pro), which uses RoPE and no forget gate, also runs at 30K tok/s in Triton suggests at least part of the gap is Triton-vs-CUDA rather than forget-gate-specific.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed beyond the acknowledgment. The paper provides no FLOPs-controlled experiment, no scaling law analysis that would allow extrapolation to compute-matched comparisons, and no estimate of the irreducible overhead of Forgetting Attention in a fully optimised implementation. The authors suggest a FLOPs-matched comparison as future work but do not attempt even a small-scale version (e.g., training Mamba-2 with proportionally more parameters on proportionally fewer tokens to equalise approximate total FLOPs).

### Bidirectional Gap: The Forget Gate Is Inherently Causal and Not Extended to Non-Autoregressive Settings

**The assumption or constraint.** The Forgetting Attention mechanism is defined entirely in terms of a forward-in-time multiplicative accumulation: `F_ij = ∏_{l=j+1}^i f_l`. This fundamentally assumes a causal structure — there is a "past" (positions j < i) that gets progressively forgotten, and a "present" (position i) that does the forgetting. The paper explicitly acknowledges this scope limitation:

> "we only consider causal sequence modeling in this work. It would be interesting to extend Forgetting Attention to the non-causal case." (Section 6)

**The consequence.** This limitation excludes FoX from a significant fraction of the Transformer deployment landscape. Encoder-only models (BERT, RoBERTa, and their many derivatives for classification, retrieval, and representation learning) use bidirectional attention where every position attends to every other position. Encoder-decoder models (T5, BART, and most machine translation and summarisation architectures) use bidirectional attention in the encoder. In these settings, there is no natural "forward in time" direction for the forget gate to accumulate along — the concept of "intervening tokens" between key and query is ill-defined when attention is not masked. Extending Forgetting Attention to bidirectional contexts would require a fundamentally different formulation: perhaps two independent forget gates (one for left-to-right, one for right-to-left), or a distance-based rather than direction-based accumulation, or a graph-structured generalisation where each attention edge `(i, j)` receives a forget weight based on some path through the sequence. The paper does not explore any of these directions, leaving the forget gate mechanism causally constrained.

This is particularly consequential because several of the Pro block components (QK-norm, output gates, output normalisation) are not causally constrained and could transfer directly to bidirectional architectures. A practitioner who adopts the Pro block for a bidirectional Transformer would get those benefits but would not be able to use the forget gate — which is the paper's primary contribution.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper contains no bidirectional experiments, no discussion of how the forget gate might be adapted, and no speculation on what the performance impact would be. The SlimPajama experiments (Appendix F.3) remain causal (autoregressive language modelling). All downstream evaluations (LM-eval-harness, LongBench, needle-in-the-haystack) use the pretrained causal language model.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies this as future work but provides no preliminary results, no theoretical analysis of possible extensions, and no discussion of the challenges involved. It is presented as an open question, not as a limitation with a clear path to resolution.

### Pro Block Interaction Effects: Components Are Evaluated Additively, Not Factorially

**The assumption or constraint.** The Pro block combines four architectural modifications — QK-norm, output gates, output normalisation, and KV token shift — and evaluates their contributions through an "incremental" ablation (adding components one at a time to Transformer LLaMA) and a "perturbation" ablation (removing one component at a time from the full FoX Pro). Both of these are **one-dimensional probes** of a four-dimensional design space. The incremental ablation fixes an ordering (forget gate → QK-norm → output gate → output norm → KV-shift) that may overstate or understate the contribution of components based on what has already been added. The perturbation ablation examines each component's marginal contribution given the other three, but does not test whether components are **redundant in combination** — two components that each independently help might provide no additional benefit when both are present.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot reliably determine which Pro components to adopt. If computational budget is tight and the full Pro block cannot be implemented, which subset provides the best return? The paper provides no guidance. For example, the incremental ablation shows that adding the output gate to FoX LLaMA with QK-norm improves perplexity from 6.88 to 6.80, and adding output normalisation + KV-shift further improves to 6.62. But would output normalisation without the output gate provide similar benefits? Would KV-shift without QK-norm be effective, or does it depend on the query-key scale stabilisation that QK-norm provides? These interaction questions are not explored, making the Pro block more of a recipe ("use all these components together") than a principled architecture contribution with understood component interactions.

This also complicates the paper's claim that the Pro block represents a "cross-paradigm design pattern" — if the components interact synergistically, they may not be independently useful primitives but rather an interdependent system that happens to work well as a bundle. Understanding whether the Pro block is a genuinely modular toolkit or a specific working configuration would require factorial experiments that the paper does not provide.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 contains the two styles of ablation. The incremental addition order is one specific sequence. The perturbation ablations start from the full FoX Pro and remove one component at a time. No two-way, three-way, or full factorial interactions are reported. The incremental Transformer (Pro) ablation in Appendix F.2 (Figure 11, 125M-parameter scale) shows that QK-norm alone improves length extrapolation, but again the ordering is fixed. No ablation tests, for example, whether KV-shift provides benefits when output gates are absent.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the Pro block as a validated architectural configuration and recommends its adoption as a baseline, but the internal structure of the design space — which components are essential, which are complementary, and what interactions exist — is not analysed. This limits the Pro block's intellectual contribution relative to its empirical contribution: we know it works, but we don't know precisely why or in what combinations.
