# Stuffed Mamba: Oversized States Lead to the Inability to Forget

**ArXiv:** [2410.07145](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07145)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper studies why Mamba-2 models fail to generalize to context lengths beyond their training length, diagnosing the root cause as a **state overparameterization** problem where the recurrent state is excessively large relative to the training context, allowing the model to achieve strong language modeling without learning to effectively forget past tokens.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies why Mamba-2 models fail to generalize to context lengths beyond their training length, diagnosing the root cause as a **state overparameterization** problem where the recurrent state is excessively large relative to the training context, allowing the model to achieve strong language modeling without learning to effectively forget past tokens. Through experiments on Mamba-2 models ranging from 36M to 780M parameters using the RedPajama corpus and passkey retrieval tasks, the authors demonstrate that the inability to forget manifests as high retention strength of early tokens throughout the training window (some heads retaining over 0.997 times the first token at the 8K training boundary) and that artificially inducing forgetting via two interventions—reduced memory retention and insertion (RRI, scaling decay and insertion multipliers) and an exact sliding window mechanism—can mitigate performance degradation. The paper empirically establishes a linear relationship between state size and the minimum training length required for the model to learn robust forgetting ($T_{\text{forget}} = 5.172 \cdot N_S - 4.469$, with $R^2 > 0.999$), and further shows that the maximum context length for accurate 5-digit passkey retrieval scales exponentially with state size ($T_{\text{recall}} = 4.756 \cdot (1.365^{N_S} - 1) - 0.742$), establishing that a 370M parameter Mamba-2 model can achieve near-perfect retrieval at 256K tokens—outperforming similarly sized transformers—but only when trained with context lengths that exceed the state capacity threshold.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RNNs Fail to Generalize Beyond Their Training Length

The fundamental question this paper tackles is: **why do state-of-the-art recurrent language models like Mamba-2 catastrophically break down when processing contexts longer than the sequences they were trained on?** This is a puzzle because, in theory, recurrent architectures should handle arbitrarily long sequences gracefully. Unlike transformers, whose self-attention complexity scales quadratically with sequence length, RNNs maintain a fixed-size recurrent state $h_t$ that encodes all prior context into a constant memory footprint — their per-token time and space costs are $O(1)$ with respect to context length, making them extremely efficient for long sequences.

Yet, as the paper documents in Figure 1 and Figure 2, this theoretical advantage evaporates in practice. Mamba-2 models trained on 8K-token sequences exhibit a sharp spike in language modeling loss when the context exceeds roughly 8K tokens, and their passkey retrieval accuracy drops to near zero for 16K contexts, regardless of whether the passkey is positioned near the beginning or end of the sequence. The failure is not subtle — it is a complete collapse. And counterintuitively, the paper finds that **larger models suffer worse length generalization** (Section 2.2). The 1.3B parameter model degrades more severely than the 130M model, which is precisely the opposite of what one would expect if the issue were simply insufficient capacity to store information.

This failure mode is particularly surprising given Mamba-2's architecture. The update rule (Equation 2 in the paper),

$$h_t = \alpha_t h_{t-1} + B_t x_t,$$

includes an explicit exponential decay term $\alpha_t \in (0, 1)$ that, in principle, controls how strongly past information is retained. When $\alpha_t \to 1$, the state retains nearly all prior context; when $\alpha_t \to 0$, the state effectively resets, forgetting earlier tokens. On paper, this is a stable, well-behaved mechanism. The model *should* learn to produce small $\alpha_t$ values to clear out stale context and make room for new tokens — exactly what forgetting mechanisms in classical RNN variants like LSTMs and GRUs were designed to do.

The paper's central diagnostic insight, developed across Sections 3 and 4, is that **Mamba-2 fails to learn this forgetting behavior in practice**. The decay terms remain stubbornly close to 1 throughout the training context window (Figure 3 shows some heads with cumulative first-token retention $\alpha_{1:t}$ above 0.997 at $t = 8\text{K}$), meaning the model is trying to cram 8K tokens' worth of information into the state without adequately discarding older, less relevant content. This works fine within the training distribution — the "newlines" prompt in Figure 5 shows that the state's mean and variance remain stable for $t \leq T_{\text{train}}$ — but once the context exceeds the training length, the state distribution destabilizes dramatically. Certain channels in certain heads exhibit explosive variance (Figure 5, Figure 6), producing the incoherent outputs and retrieval failures visible in the benchmarking results.

### Why This Problem Matters: Practical and Theoretical Significance

The practical stakes are straightforward. The recent explosion of interest in long-context language models — Gemini 1.5 with its million-token context window, MiniMax-01 with lightning attention for long sequences — has been driven almost entirely by transformer-based architectures. But transformers pay a steep efficiency price for their long-context abilities: KV cache memory grows linearly with context length, and attention compute grows quadratically (or linearly with clever optimizations). For deployment scenarios where inference cost or latency is critical — edge devices, real-time systems, high-throughput APIs — the constant-cost property of RNNs is enormously attractive. If Mamba-2 and similar recurrent models could reliably handle contexts of 100K+ tokens, they could dramatically reduce the infrastructure required for long-context applications.

The paper's finding that a **370M parameter Mamba-2 model achieves near-perfect passkey retrieval at 256K context length** when properly trained (Section 5.3, Figure 9) shows that this potential is real — the architecture *can* handle extremely long contexts. The barrier is not inherent to recurrence; it is a training problem. Understanding and fixing that training problem opens a path to recurrent models that combine the efficiency of $O(1)$ per-token inference with the long-context capabilities currently monopolized by transformers.

The theoretical significance runs deeper. The paper's core contribution is demonstrating that **forgetting is not a passive consequence of exponential decay** — it is a behavior that must be actively learned, and that learning is gated by the relationship between state capacity and training context length. This challenges a tacit assumption in much of the RNN literature: that gating mechanisms and decay terms will automatically produce good forgetting behavior if the architecture is expressive enough. The paper shows that, at least for Mamba-2, the optimization dynamics during language model training actively *discourage* forgetting when the state is large enough to simply memorize everything within the training window. This is a form of overfitting — not to specific tokens, but to the distribution of state contents induced by short training contexts — and it has implications for how we think about training recurrent models more broadly.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

#### The Transformer Dominance and Its Discontents

Most prior work on long-context language modeling has focused on the transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017). The fundamental challenge for transformers is positional encoding: because self-attention is permutation-invariant, transformers need explicit mechanisms to encode token order. The specific choice of positional encoding determines how the model behaves on sequences longer than those seen during training. Research in this direction has been extensive: RoPE extensions (Peng et al., 2024b; Ding et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024), self-extending context windows without fine-tuning (Jin et al., 2024), and Bayesian optimization over RoPE parameters (Zhang et al., 2025) have achieved impressive results, with some methods enabling training-free length generalization to certain extents. The paper acknowledges this body of work but positions it as solving a *different problem* — the transformer's length generalization challenge arises primarily from positional encoding, while the RNN's challenge arises from state management.

This distinction matters because it means the transformer literature's solutions do not transfer. You cannot apply YaRN or SelfExtend to Mamba-2; the architectures have fundamentally different bottlenecks. The paper is filling a gap: while transformers have a well-developed research program for length generalization, RNNs do not, despite their theoretical advantages for long sequences.

#### The Mamba-Specific Literature: Symptoms Without Causes

Prior work on Mamba and length generalization falls into two categories. First, there are empirical observations of the problem. Jelassi et al. (2024) showed that Mamba struggles to copy from context unless its state size grows linearly with the context length — a finding that aligns with the paper's observation that Mamba-2's state size is equivalent to only 128 transformer tokens per layer (Appendix A.1). Waleffe et al. (2024) and Ben-Kish et al. (2024) documented sharp performance drops beyond training length. These works established that the problem exists but did not explain *why* it occurs.

Second, there are heuristic interventions. The GitHub user jzhang38 proposed simply multiplying $\Delta_t$ by a constant (0.5) to reduce decay rates, and Ben-Kish et al. (2024) developed a task-dependent heuristic for identifying which heads to modify. The paper tests these approaches — the $\Delta_t$ scaling method produces near-zero accuracy across all passkey positions and context lengths (Appendix C), and while the authors' own interventions (RRI and sliding window, Section 3.2.2) can mitigate the degradation, they do so at the cost of short-context performance (Figure 4 shows RRI and sliding window degrading the loss within the training length). These heuristics treat the symptom without understanding the cause, and the paper's analysis explains why they're fragile: they impose forgetting externally rather than fixing the underlying failure to learn it.

#### The "Recency vs. Over-Smoothing" Framing

Wang et al. (2025), a concurrent work, frames the problem in terms of over-smoothing: the memory decay term, when too close to 1, causes the state to smooth over token representations, losing the distinction between recent and past information. The paper's framing is subtly but importantly different. The authors emphasize *interference* rather than smoothing: the problem is not that the state becomes homogeneous, but that it becomes *contaminated* by too many partially retained token representations that conflict with one another when queried. Equation 7 makes this precise: querying the state with $C_t = B_s$ to retrieve information inserted at time $s$ produces a retrieval error term $\sum_{i \neq t} \alpha_{i:t} C_t B_i x_i$, which grows with the number of preceding tokens unless the model learns to set $\alpha_t$ small enough to suppress them. This is an interference problem, not a smoothing problem, and it explains a critical observation that Wang et al.'s framing might miss: **the model fails to recall even very recent tokens when the context exceeds the training length**. If the issue were merely smoothing (losing old information), the model should still perform well on tokens near the end of the context. The fact that it doesn't (Figure 2, rightmost columns showing poor retrieval at all answer depths for 16K contexts) indicates that excessive retention of *early* tokens is actively damaging the recall of *recent* tokens through interference.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper presents itself as a **diagnostic investigation** rather than a method proposal. The authors are not introducing a new architecture or a new training objective. They are dissecting why an existing, widely-used architecture underperforms on a critical task, and in doing so, they surface fundamental properties of the relationship between state size, training length, and memory management that the field has not previously characterized.

The paper's positioning has three key features. First, it draws a direct parallel to the concept of **overparameterization** familiar from the deep learning theory literature — just as an overparameterized neural network can memorize training data without learning generalizable features, an "overparameterized" recurrent state ($N_S$ too large for $T_{\text{train}}$) can store raw token information without learning the compression and selective forgetting that generalization to longer contexts requires. The evidence for this is the "more training $\Rightarrow$ less forgetting" result in Section 4.1 and Figure 8: as training progresses, the model's passkey retrieval accuracy *decreases* for contexts longer than 512 tokens, while increasing for contexts within the training length. The model is converging toward a solution that works perfectly on the training distribution but relies on brute-force memorization rather than principled forgetting.

Second, the paper positions its two quantitative laws — the linear relationship $T_{\text{forget}}(N_S)$ and the exponential relationship $T_{\text{recall}}(N_S)$ — as foundational empirical findings that should inform future RNN design. These are not arbitrary curve fits; they have clear mechanistic interpretations. The linear scaling of $T_{\text{forget}}$ with state size emerges because the training length needs to exceed the state's raw capacity to store information — beyond that point, the model must learn to selectively discard less useful tokens to make room, which is precisely the forgetting behavior that prevents interference in longer contexts. The exponential scaling of $T_{\text{recall}}$ with state size emerges because, in the passkey task, the amount of novel information in the distracting "grass is green" context is constant regardless of length (it's repetitive), so the state's capacity to store the *distinction* between the passkey and the noise grows exponentially — each additional state dimension provides an additional bit of representational capacity.

Third, the paper situates itself in a broader conversation about **compute-optimal context sizes** (Buckman and Gelada, 2024). If the training context length must scale with state size, and state size scales with model size (Figure 12 shows this relationship for Mamba-1, Mamba-2, and RWKV), then there is a tradeoff: larger models, which have larger states, *require* longer training contexts to learn effective memory management. Current practice — training Mamba-2 models on 8K sequences regardless of size — is deeply suboptimal for larger models, which explains the counterintuitive result that larger models have worse length generalization (Figure 1). The paper provides the quantitative relationship needed to determine the right training length for a given model size, making a direct contribution to the emerging literature on scaling laws for context length.

The paper also explicitly acknowledges its scope limitations. It focuses on Mamba-2 primarily (with additional checks on Mamba-1, RWKV-5, and RWKV-6 in the appendices) because Mamba-2 has publicly available checkpoints across multiple scales and uses a particularly large state size (8× larger than Mamba-1 at the same parameter count, per Appendix A.1). The authors note that their findings may apply to other gated linear attention variants — GLA, RetNet, RWKV — but leave exhaustive cross-architecture studies to future work. And they explicitly exclude architectures with fundamentally different memory mechanisms, such as Gated DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2025), RWKV-7 (Peng et al., 2025), xLSTM (Beck et al., 2024), and Titans (Behrouz et al., 2024), which have moved beyond simple gating-based decay and are outside the scope of this analysis.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper presents a **diagnostic methodology** for identifying why Mamba-2 recurrent language models fail when processing sequences longer than their training context, centered on a systematic measurement of memory retention, two complementary intervention techniques that artificially induce forgetting to test whether over-retention is causal, and a controlled training-length sweep that maps out the quantitative relationship between state size and the context length required to learn effective memory management. The paper's solution "shape" is: hypothesize that the failure stems from insufficient forgetting caused by state overparameterization → measure retention strength and state distribution changes to detect the failure mode → intervene on the update rule to verify that inducing forgetting fixes the problem → train models across different sizes and context lengths to establish the scaling laws that predict when the model will spontaneously learn to forget.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The paper's diagnostic framework has five major components, arranged as successive investigative layers:

1. **Level-1: Behavioral Characterization** — measuring language modeling loss and passkey retrieval accuracy as functions of context length for existing Mamba-2 checkpoints, establishing that the failure exists and documenting its pattern (degradation beyond training length, worse for larger models, failure at *all* retrieval positions).

2. **Level-2: Retention Auditing** — instrumenting the model to extract and visualize the memory retention strengths $\alpha_{1:t}$ (the cumulative product of per-step decay factors) to determine whether the model is actually forgetting early tokens or retaining them with near-1 coefficients across the entire training window.

3. **Level-3: Interventional Testing** — developing two methods to artificially impose forgetting (Reduced Retention & Insertion, which scales down $\alpha_t$ and $B_t$ by fixed multipliers, and Sliding Window, which exactly subtracts out tokens older than a window $w$ using the linearity of the weighted-sum representation) and measuring whether these interventions rescue length generalization, establishing causality between over-retention and the failure.

4. **Level-4: Training Dynamics Analysis** — tracking how the model's forgetting behavior evolves *during* training by evaluating intermediate checkpoints on passkey retrieval, revealing that the model *starts* with good forgetting but progressively loses it as training progresses, analogous to overfitting.

5. **Level-5: Scaling Law Extraction** — training models of different state sizes across a sweep of context lengths, measuring two thresholds ($T_{\text{forget}}$, the minimum training length for the model to learn robust forgetting, and $T_{\text{recall}}$, the maximum context from which a 5-digit passkey can be retrieved with >95% accuracy), and fitting parametric relationships ($T_{\text{forget}}$ scales linearly with state size; $T_{\text{recall}}$ scales exponentially with state size).

Information flows sequentially through these layers: each layer's findings motivate and inform the next, building from symptom documentation through causal verification to quantitative prediction.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal definition of Mamba-2's recurrence and the mathematical machinery of memory retention, because every subsequent measurement and intervention depends on understanding how the state $h_t$ accumulates a weighted sum of past token information and how the cumulative decay $\alpha_{1:t}$ quantifies retention.
- **Second**, the retention auditing methodology — how the paper extracts per-head $\alpha_t$ values, what Figure 3 means, and what "high retention" concretely is — because this is the primary evidence for the paper's central claim that the model fails to forget.
- **Third**, the two interventional techniques (RRI and Sliding Window), their mathematical justifications, their implementation details, and the specific multiplier and window choices, because these provide the causal evidence that over-retention *causes* the length generalization failure.
- **Fourth**, the state distribution analysis (inspecting hidden state statistics on the "newlines" prompt as context extends beyond training length), because this reveals *how* over-retention manifests — as distributional shifts and variance explosions in specific channels of specific heads — providing a mechanistic link between the retention measurements and the downstream performance degradation.
- **Fifth**, the training dynamics methodology (evaluating intermediate checkpoints during a 512-length training run), because this establishes the "overfitting" analogy that motivates the state overparameterization hypothesis.
- **Sixth**, the controlled training-length sweep — the model configurations, data processing, training hyperparameters, and the criteria used to determine $T_{\text{forget}}$ and $T_{\text{recall}}$ — because these experiments produce the paper's two quantitative scaling laws that are the primary prescriptive contribution.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **diagnostic analysis paper** whose core idea is that Mamba-2 models trained on contexts shorter than what their state size can memorize never learn the forgetting behavior needed to manage interference in longer contexts, and that this failure can be quantitatively characterized, causally verified, and predicted from the relationship between state size and training length.

#### Mamba-2 Recurrence and the Memory Retention Framework

The paper's entire diagnostic approach rests on the fact that Mamba-2's recurrent state $h_t$ can be expressed as a weighted sum over all past token contributions. The per-step update rule (Equation 2 in Section 2.1),

$$h_t = \alpha_t h_{t-1} + B_t x_t,$$

where $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times P}$ is the recurrent state at time $t$, $\alpha_t \in (0,1)$ is a scalar memory decay multiplier (equivalently, the retention strength) computed as $\alpha_t = \exp(-\Delta_t \exp(A))$ with $A \in \mathbb{R}$ being a trainable parameter and $\Delta_t \in \mathbb{R}$ being a time-dependent gating variable parameterized as $\Delta_t = \text{Softplus}(u_t W_\Delta + b_\Delta)$, $B_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1}$ is the insertion strength vector computed as $B_t = \Delta_t B_t$ where $\tilde{B}_t$ is the output of a SiLU-gated convolutional layer applied to the input $u_t$, and $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times P}$ is the value vector to be inserted, also produced by a SiLU-gated convolution.

**What it computes:** at each time step, the new state is a convex combination of the previous state (weighted by $\alpha_t$) and the new token's information (weighted by $B_t$). When $\alpha_t \approx 1$, the previous state is almost entirely preserved and the new token adds a small amount of information on top; when $\alpha_t \approx 0$, the previous state is almost entirely discarded and the new token essentially resets the state. The state dimension $N$ is set to 128 in Mamba-2, and the head dimension $P$ is 64, meaning each head's state is a $128 \times 64$ matrix storing structured representations of past context.

**Why this form:** this is a gated linear recurrence, which enables the state to be unrolled into the explicit weighted-sum form used throughout the paper's analysis:

$$h_t = \sum_{i=1}^{t} \alpha_{i:t} B_i x_i, \quad \text{where} \quad \alpha_{i:t} = \prod_{j=i}^{t} \alpha_j \in (0, 1).$$

The cumulative product $\alpha_{i:t}$ represents the fraction of token $i$'s contribution that survives to time $t$. This decomposition is crucial because it makes the memory of every past token *independently quantifiable* — $\alpha_{i:t}$ is a scalar that decays monotonically as $t$ increases beyond $i$, and the product structure means that even if each individual $\alpha_j$ is extremely close to 1 (e.g., 0.9998), the cumulative effect over thousands of steps can produce significant decay (0.9998$^{8000} \approx$ 0.20) — or, conversely, if $\alpha_j$ values stay sufficiently close to 1, early tokens can remain strongly represented throughout the entire context. The paper's core finding is that the latter happens: the model learns $\alpha_j$ values so close to 1 that the cumulative decay $\alpha_{1:t}$ remains above 0.997 at $t = 8000$ for some heads, meaning the first token's contribution is retained almost entirely.

The retrieval error analysis in Section 3.1 further explains *why* this over-retention is harmful. When the model queries its state to retrieve information inserted at time $s$, it computes $y_t = C_t h_t$ with $C_t = B_s$ (the model produces a query vector matching the insertion vector of the target token). This yields:

$$y_t = \alpha_{s:t}(C_t B_s) x_s + \sum_{i \neq s} \alpha_{i:t} C_t B_i x_i.$$

The first term is the desired retrieval (the target token's value scaled by its cumulative retention and the query-key match $C_t B_s$), and the second term is the **retrieval error** — interference from all other tokens whose insertion vectors $B_i$ are not perfectly orthogonal to the query $C_t = B_s$. The paper argues that this error grows with the number of preceding tokens because the $B_i$ vectors cannot remain mutually orthogonal as the sequence gets long; the model must therefore learn to suppress this error by setting $\alpha_{i:t}$ small for tokens $i$ that are far from $t$, effectively forgetting them.

#### Retention Auditing: Measuring $\alpha_{1:t}$ Across Heads

The paper's first diagnostic tool is a direct extraction of the per-step $\alpha_t$ values from the model during inference and the computation of the cumulative retention $\alpha_{1:t}$ for the first token across all heads. The implementation is: (1) run the model on a prompt (the paper uses the "newlines" prompt — a string consisting only of "\n" tokens — because it produces smooth, consistent layer statistics, though the authors verify similar behavior on real corpus data), (2) for each head at each time step, record the scalar $\alpha_t$ computed by the parameterization in Equation 4, (3) for the first token specifically, compute the running product $\alpha_{1:t} = \prod_{j=1}^{t} \alpha_j$ to obtain the fraction of token 1's contribution that survives to time $t$.

**Why monitor $\alpha_{1:t}$ rather than individual $\alpha_t$:** the per-step values $\alpha_t$ are individually uninformative — an $\alpha_t$ of 0.998 corresponds to a 0.2% per-step decay, which seems negligible, but the cumulative effect matters. The product $\alpha_{1:t}$ directly answers the question "how much of the very first token is still present in the state after $t$ steps?" This is the paper's operational definition of forgetting: if $\alpha_{1:t}$ drops below some small threshold (e.g., 0.01), the first token is effectively forgotten; if it remains above 0.9, the model is retaining it almost entirely.

**What Figure 3 shows:** for the Mamba-2 370M model, the paper plots $\alpha_{1:t}$ over $t \in [0, 8000]$ for the first eight heads of layer 38 (this is the 38th of 48 transformer-like blocks, selected as representative; similar behavior is observed in many other heads and layers). Three of the eight heads (H0, H2, H6) show $\alpha_{1:t}$ values above 0.997 at $t = 8000$, meaning the cumulative retention of the first token after processing the entire 8K training context is over 99.7%. Given that this is a cumulative product, the per-step $\alpha_t$ values must be extraordinarily close to 1 — specifically, $\alpha_t > 0.999996$ on average if the cumulative product is to stay above 0.997 over 8000 steps. These heads have essentially learned to *never* forget what they see, relying instead on the additive $B_t x_t$ term to stack new information on top without removing anything old.

This finding is the paper's primary evidence for its central claim: the model has not learned to forget. The "newlines" prompt is deliberately content-poor (every token is identical), so there is no pressure from the input to force forgetting — the model's default behavior in this prompt reflects what it has learned during training. That the default is near-perfect retention across the full training window means the model has optimized its parameters to preserve all information within the training context, which will fail catastrophically when the context extends beyond what the state can hold.

#### Interventional Testing: Artificially Inducing Forgetting

To establish that over-retention is not merely correlated with length generalization failure but is *causal*, the paper introduces two methods for inducing forgetting *without additional training* — that is, by modifying the inference-time computation of the recurrent state to force older tokens to be discarded. If over-retention causes the failure, then artificially forcing forgetting should rescue performance on long contexts.

##### Reduced Retention and Insertion (RRI)

The RRI method operates on the intuition that $\alpha_t$ controls retention strength and $B_t$ controls insertion strength. By scaling both downward, the method simultaneously makes the model forget old information faster (smaller $\alpha_t$) and insert new information more weakly (smaller $B_t$). The implementation is a direct multiplicative intervention on the values computed during inference:

$$\alpha_t^{\text{(RRI)}} = c_\alpha \cdot \alpha_t, \quad B_t^{\text{(RRI)}} = c_B \cdot B_t,$$

where $c_\alpha \in (0, 1]$ and $c_B \in (0, 1]$ are fixed hyperparameters. The paper reports using $c_\alpha = 0.9999$ and $c_B = 0.75$, chosen by validation on the average pre-training loss at 32K context length.

**Why these specific numbers:** $c_\alpha = 0.9999$ means that each $\alpha_t$ is reduced by a factor of $10^{-4}$ — a tiny per-step effect that compounds over time. For 8000 steps, the cumulative retention $\alpha_{1:t}^{\text{(RRI)}} = (c_\alpha)^t \times \alpha_{1:t} = (0.9999)^{8000} \times \alpha_{1:t} \approx 0.449 \times \alpha_{1:t}$, so the first token's retention drops to roughly 45% of its original value. For the heads with $\alpha_{1:t} > 0.997$, this pushes the retention down to about 0.45 — a significant reduction that forces forgetting while still preserving some earlier context. $c_B = 0.75$ reduces the magnitude of new token insertions by a quarter, which prevents the state from being dominated by the most recent tokens now that older ones are being forgotten more aggressively. The values were chosen via grid search on a held-out validation set to balance short-context performance (which degrades if forgetting is too aggressive) against long-context generalization.

**Why this form rather than a purely additive bias:** a multiplicative intervention preserves the relative dynamics across heads — heads that learned to retain more (higher $\alpha_t$) still retain more than heads that learned to forget faster, but the absolute level of retention is shifted downward. An additive bias $-$constant would disrupt the learned head-wise specialization more severely.

##### Sliding Window via Exact State Subtraction

The sliding window method is more principled: it exactly limits the model's effective context to the last $w$ tokens by exploiting the linearity of the weighted-sum representation. From the unrolled form $h_t = \sum_{i=1}^{t} \alpha_{i:t} B_i x_i$, the contribution of only the last $w$ tokens is:

$$h_t^{(w)} = \sum_{i=t-w+1}^{t} \alpha_{i:t} B_i x_i.$$

This can be rewritten as the difference between the full state at $t$ and the full state at $t-w$, scaled by the cumulative decay between $t-w+1$ and $t$:

$$h_t^{(w)} = h_t - \alpha_{t-w+1:t} \cdot h_{t-w}.$$

**What it computes:** at each time step $t$, the sliding-window state is obtained by (1) taking the full state $h_t$ (which includes all tokens since time 0), (2) subtracting $h_{t-w}$ (the state as of $t-w$ tokens ago, which contains all information up to and including token $t-w$) scaled by $\alpha_{t-w+1:t} = \prod_{j=t-w+1}^{t} \alpha_j$ (the cumulative decay accounting for how much of that older information would have naturally decayed between $t-w$ and $t$). The subtraction perfectly cancels out all contributions from tokens at position $t-w$ and earlier, leaving exactly the weighted sum over the last $w$ tokens.

**Why this form:** the naive approach to implementing a sliding window — restarting the recurrence from scratch at position $t-w+1$ with $h_{t-w+1} = 0$ — would require $O(w)$ computation per step to catch up to $t$, defeating the constant-time property of the recurrence. The subtraction trick achieves the same result in $O(1)$ time per step, requiring only that the model maintains two additional cached values: $h_{t-w}$ (the state from $w$ steps ago) and $\alpha_{t-w+1:t}$ (the cumulative decay product over the window). The storage cost is $O(N \times P)$ for the state cache, which is the same order as the state itself.

**Floating-point stability concern:** the cumulative decay $\alpha_{t-w+1:t}$ is a product of $w$ values in $(0, 1)$, and for large $w$ (e.g., $w = 8000$ for a window matching the training length), this product can underflow in finite-precision arithmetic. The paper addresses this by noting that $\alpha_t = \exp(-\Delta_t \exp(A))$, so $\alpha_{t-w+1:t} = \exp(-\sum_{j=t-w+1}^{t} \Delta_j \exp(A))$, and the sum $\sum_{j} \Delta_j$ can be maintained incrementally in FP32 without underflow. At each step, the cumulative decay is recomputed as $\exp(-\Delta_{t-w+1:t} \exp(A))$ where $\Delta_{t-w+1:t}$ is the maintained sum. This is both numerically stable and computationally cheap (one exponential evaluation per head per step).

**What Figure 4 shows:** both RRI and the sliding window substantially reduce the language modeling loss at positions beyond the 8K training length (e.g., from ~5 to ~3 at 32K tokens for the sliding window), while the original model spikes sharply. However, the sliding window method is more effective — it achieves lower loss at all positions beyond the training length — and both methods slightly degrade performance *within* the training length, because they are discarding information that the model was relying on. This tradeoff is exactly what one would expect if over-retention causes the failure: forcing the model to forget rescues long-context performance at the cost of short-context performance, because the model was not trained to operate with aggressive forgetting and its downstream layers expect certain information to be present in the state.

#### State Distribution Analysis: Detecting Variance Explosions

The paper's third diagnostic layer examines what happens to the actual numerical values in the recurrent state when the context length exceeds the training length. The insight is that if the state is accumulating interference from too many partially-retained tokens, this should manifest as a change in the distribution of state elements — either growing means (accumulation of contributions) or growing variances (increased interference causing larger-magnitude fluctuations).

**Methodology:** the paper runs the Mamba-2 370M model on the "newlines" prompt (a string of "\n" tokens repeated for 32K positions) and records, for each head in each layer, the mean and variance of the state's elements at each time step. Figure 5 shows these statistics for the first 8 heads of layer 38 (the same heads shown in Figure 3), and Appendix G provides plots for all 48 layers. The choice of the "newlines" prompt is deliberate: it provides a constant, repetitive input that isolates the effect of the recurrence itself from any content-dependent variations. With constant input, any change in the state distribution over time is purely a function of the accumulated recurrence dynamics, not of changing input statistics.

**What Figure 5 reveals:** for $t$ within the training length (0 to ~8000), the mean and variance of each head remain stable — the state is in a steady-state distribution. At approximately $t = 8000$, several heads (most prominently H2 and H6) begin to exhibit sharp changes: their means grow linearly (from ~0 to ~0.1 over the next 20K steps for H2) and their variances explode (from ~10 to over 150 for H6 by $t = 30\text{K}$). This is a clear distributional shift from the regime the model was trained on — during training, the state values for these heads never reached the magnitudes that appear at $t > 8000$. The downstream layers, whose weight matrices were optimized for inputs in the training-time range, now receive inputs orders of magnitude larger, causing the sharp increase in language modeling loss visible in Figure 1.

**Figure 6 zooms in** on the state at $t = 20\text{K}$ for one head (head 2, layer 38). The full state has $N \times P = 128 \times 64 = 8192$ elements distributed across channels (the $N = 128$ channels per head, each with a $P = 64$ dimensional vector). The histogram shows that most channels have values tightly clustered near zero, but a small number of outlier channels have values reaching up to ~100–150 in both positive and negative directions, with a heavy-tailed distribution. At $t = 8\text{K}$ (within training), the same head shows values clustered within a much smaller range. The variance explosion is thus driven by a minority of channels that become unstable — a finding the paper interprets as evidence that the interference from accumulated token representations disproportionately affects certain dimensions, and that the model's parameters for those dimensions are not regularized to handle the larger magnitudes.

**Why this matters for the overparameterization hypothesis:** if the state were forced to forget aggressively, the accumulation would saturate — old tokens would be suppressed before their contributions could grow the state values beyond the training-time range. The fact that the values do explode implies the model is retaining far more information than it was designed to handle, and the explosion mechanism (a few outlier channels rather than uniform growth) suggests that the failure is not a graceful degradation but a catastrophic nonlinearity — small over-retention in a few dimensions cascades into large-magnitude outputs after passing through the nonlinearities in subsequent layers.

#### Training Dynamics Analysis: "More Training $\Rightarrow$ Less Forgetting"

The paper's fourth diagnostic layer tracks how forgetting behavior evolves during the training process itself, by evaluating intermediate checkpoints on the passkey retrieval task. This experiment is designed to test whether the inability to forget is a property the model is born with or one it acquires during training, which has direct implications for the state overparameterization hypothesis.

**Setup:** the authors pre-train a Mamba-2 370M model from scratch on the RedPajama corpus with a training context length of $T_{\text{train}} = 512$ tokens (deliberately short to accelerate the experiment and isolate the effect of training progress from training length). They save checkpoints at 10B, 20B, and 40B tokens of training data and evaluate each checkpoint on the passkey retrieval task at context lengths of 1K, 4K, and 8K — all longer than the 512-token training length. The passkey task is the same 5-digit retrieval task used throughout the paper.

**What Figure 8a shows (10B tokens):** the model shows a clear "recency bias" — it retrieves the passkey accurately (near 100%) when the passkey is positioned late in the context (answer depth > 80%), but accuracy drops when the passkey is earlier. This is the behavior one would expect from a model that has started to learn a forgetting mechanism: it retains recent information well and forgets older information, so passkeys positioned late in the context (closer to the query) are remembered while passkeys positioned early are forgotten.

**What Figure 8c shows (40B tokens):** the model's retrieval pattern has inverted. Accuracy is now near 0% across *all* answer depths and *all* context lengths — even when the passkey is positioned at 95% depth in a 1K context, the model fails. This is precisely the "inability to forget" failure mode: the model is retaining *too much* information from early in the context (the repetitive "grass is green" filler text), which creates interference that prevents even recent information from being recalled accurately. The paper refers to this as "less forgetting" because the model's decay terms have converged toward values that preserve more information.

**Within-training-length performance:** while the paper does not plot it, the text states that the model's accuracy for contexts shorter than the training length *improves* from 10B to 40B tokens. This is the signature of overfitting: the model learns to exploit the full state capacity to minimize loss on the training distribution, which improves in-distribution performance but degrades out-of-distribution (longer context) performance because the strategy that works in-distribution (retain everything) fails out-of-distribution (the state overflows).

**Why this supports state overparameterization:** if the state were too *small* for the training context, the model would be forced to learn selective forgetting from the very beginning of training because it would need to decide what to keep and what to discard to achieve low loss. The fact that the model can *improve* in-distribution performance by reducing forgetting means that the state is large enough to simply store the required information for 512 tokens without any selection. The model is therefore never pressured to learn which information is worth forgetting, and the forgetting mechanism (the parameters that produce $\alpha_t$) atrophies during training as the model discovers that $\alpha_t \approx 1$ everywhere works fine for short contexts.

#### Controlled Training-Length Sweep: Finding $T_{\text{forget}}$ and $T_{\text{recall}}$

This is the paper's most resource-intensive experiment and the source of its two quantitative scaling laws. The core idea is to train Mamba-2 models of different sizes with different training lengths and measure when they spontaneously learn to forget and how far they can extrapolate, establishing the empirical relationship between state size, training length, and memory management.

##### Model Configurations

The paper uses six model sizes spanning two orders of magnitude in parameter count and state size, as summarized in Table 2:

- **36.4M parameters**, 6 layers, hidden dimension 512, 16 heads, $N_S = 0.8\text{M}$ (state size in parameters; $N_S = 2 \cdot d \cdot N \cdot L = 2 \cdot 512 \cdot 128 \cdot 6 = 786,432$).
- **47.0M parameters**, 12 layers, hidden dimension 512, 16 heads, $N_S = 1.6\text{M}$.
- **84.6M parameters**, 12 layers, hidden dimension 768, 24 heads, $N_S = 2.4\text{M}$.
- **130M parameters**, 24 layers, hidden dimension 768, 24 heads, $N_S = 4.8\text{M}$ (official checkpoint, continued pre-training).
- **370M parameters**, 48 layers, hidden dimension 1024, 32 heads, $N_S = 12.9\text{M}$ (official checkpoint, continued pre-training).
- **780M parameters**, 48 layers, hidden dimension 1536, 48 heads, $N_S = 19.3\text{M}$ (official checkpoint, continued pre-training).

The three smallest models are trained from scratch on RedPajama-V2 using the configurations in Table 2. The three largest models are official Mamba-2 checkpoints (pre-trained on 8K contexts) that receive additional continued pre-training on longer contexts. The paper notes that the 780M model, despite having the largest state size (19.3M), still shows poor length generalization at training lengths up to 128K, and the authors did not have the compute budget to train it beyond that length.

##### Data Processing and Training Setup

The training corpus is RedPajama-V2, a 30T-token open web dataset. To ensure the data contains long-range dependencies (essential for the model to encounter situations where forgetting is necessary), the paper filters out documents shorter than 4K tokens, which removes approximately 97.6% of the original corpus. Longer sequences are constructed by concatenating documents separated by a special End-of-Sequence (EOS) token.

**Truncated Backpropagation Through Time (TBPTT):** rather than initializing the recurrent state $h_0$ to zero for each sample (the default in the original Mamba-2 training), the paper carries forward the final state from the previous sequence as the initial state for the next, only truncating gradient flow at boundaries between concatenated sequences. In practice, they concatenate 12 sequences and stop backpropagation at each sequence boundary. This technique, adopted from Yang et al. (2024a), increases the diversity of state initializations the model encounters during training — instead of always starting from zero, the model sees states with realistic accumulated content from prior context. The paper hypothesizes that this variability in initial states helps the model learn to manage accumulated information rather than relying on the zero-initialization shortcut.

**Training hyperparameters:**
- **Learning rate scheduler:** WSD (Warmup-Stable-Decay) with 10% decay steps, 1000 warmup steps linear, 50K decay steps linear. The paper chose WSD over cosine scheduling because it allows simple resumption from intermediate checkpoints (the stable phase has constant learning rate).
- **Learning rate:** swept over $\{1 \times 10^{-5}, 2 \times 10^{-5}, 5 \times 10^{-5}, 1 \times 10^{-4}, 2 \times 10^{-4}, 5 \times 10^{-4}, 1 \times 10^{-3}\}$, selecting the best by validation on passkey retrieval. The text notes that "the loss of many checkpoints was highly similar, their performance in passkey retrieval can differ a lot," suggesting that loss-based early stopping is insufficient for detecting length generalization ability.
- **Batch size:** 0.5M tokens per batch. The authors found this "gives more stable results for continual pre-training" than the 1M token batch used in the original Mamba-2 paper.
- **Precision:** BF16 for most computations, with the recurrent state activations in FP32 (following the official Mamba-2 implementation).
- **Optimizer:** AdamW with weight decay 0.1, gradient clipping at 1.0.

**Evaluation to determine the best checkpoint:** the model with the best passkey retrieval accuracy on a validation set is selected, not the model with the lowest validation loss. This is a critical choice because, as the training dynamics experiment showed, loss and retrieval accuracy can be anti-correlated during over-retention.

##### Defining and Measuring $T_{\text{forget}}$

The paper defines $T_{\text{forget}}$ operationally as the minimum training context length after which the model exhibits "robust forgetting." The specific criterion is: feed the model prompts with 1M tokens (far longer than any training length) and check whether the language modeling loss at any position exceeds $2 \times$ the maximum loss observed within the first $T_{\text{train}}$ tokens (where $T_{\text{train}}$ is the training length). The loss is averaged over 128 prompts.

**What this criterion means:** if the model has learned to forget, its loss on tokens at position $t \gg T_{\text{train}}$ should remain within a factor of 2 of the steady-state training loss, because the state management dynamics (forgetting old tokens, inserting new ones) generalize to arbitrarily long contexts. If the model has not learned to forget, the loss will spike when the state fills up and interference begins — which for a training length $T_{\text{train}}$ typically happens at $t > T_{\text{train}}$. The factor-of-2 threshold is chosen as a conservative criterion that catches the catastrophic loss spikes visible in Figure 1 and Figure 10 before they exceed 5–10× the training loss.

**Why this operationalization rather than directly measuring $\alpha_t$:** the retention auditing in Section 3.2.1 showed that over-retentive heads have $\alpha_{1:t} > 0.997$ at training length, but this is a continuous measure — there is no single threshold for "has learned to forget." The 1M-token extrapolation test provides a binary criterion: either the loss stays bounded (forgetting works) or it doesn't (forgetting fails). The 1M-token length is chosen to be far beyond the training length (at least $20\times$ longer than the largest training length considered, 256K) to ensure that any failure to forget will be clearly visible.

##### What Figure 10 Shows

Figure 10 plots language modeling loss as a function of token position for Mamba-2 130M and 370M models trained with different context lengths. For the 130M model, training at 4K context fails catastrophically (loss spikes from ~3.2 to ~4.5 at ~10K tokens), while training at 16K context keeps the loss bounded even at 32K tokens. For the 370M model, training at 8K context fails, 16K context partially helps (loss rises to ~3.3 at ~30K then declines), and 64K context maintains the loss below 3.0 throughout 128K tokens. The threshold $T_{\text{forget}}$ is the training length at which the loss curve transitions from "spikes" to "flat" — for 130M, this is between 8K and 16K; for 370M, this is between 32K and 64K.

##### The Scaling Law: $T_{\text{forget}}(N_S)$

Figure 11 plots the estimated $T_{\text{forget}}$ threshold against state size $N_S$ for the five sizes where the threshold could be determined (36.4M through 370M; the 780M model's threshold is >128K and could not be measured). The data points are fit to a linear model:

$$T_{\text{forget}} = 5.172 \cdot N_S - 4.469,$$

where $N_S$ is the state size in millions of parameters (the product of hidden dimension, state dimension $N = 128$, head count, and layer count, as derived in Appendix A.1) and $T_{\text{forget}}$ is in thousands of tokens. The $R^2$ value exceeds 0.999.

**What this means operationally:** for a Mamba-2 model with a state size of $N_S$ million parameters, if you train with context length $T_{\text{train}}$ tokens, the model will learn robust forgetting if and only if $T_{\text{train}} \gtrapprox 5.172 \cdot N_S - 4.469$ (with $T_{\text{train}}$ in thousands of tokens). For example, applying this to the 370M model ($N_S = 12.9\text{M}$) gives $T_{\text{forget}} \approx 5.172 \times 12.9 - 4.469 \approx 62.3\text{K}$, and indeed the experimental data shows the model needs 64K training length to generalize (Figure 10b).

**Why this form (linear):** the authors interpret the linear relationship as evidence for the state capacity hypothesis. If each unit of state capacity can store roughly some fixed amount of token information (with the constant of proportionality being $5.172 \text{K tokens} / 10^6 \text{ state parameters}$, or about 5.2 tokens per 1000 state parameters), then a training context of $T$ tokens contains about $T / 5.172\text{K}$ "state-capacity-equivalents" of information. When $T_{\text{train}} > 5.172 N_S - 4.469$, the context contains more information than the state can simply memorize, so the model is forced to learn selective forgetting. When $T_{\text{train}}$ is below this threshold, the state has spare capacity, and the optimization process discovers that retaining everything minimizes loss without requiring any forgetting decisions — which is the state overparameterization regime.

The intercept of $-4.469$ (i.e., negative) means the fitted line crosses zero at $N_S \approx 0.86\text{M}$ — below this state size, the model would theoretically learn forgetting at *any* positive training length. The paper does not have data points in this regime and treats this as an artifact of the linear fit rather than a physically meaningful prediction.

##### Defining and Measuring $T_{\text{recall}}$

The paper defines $T_{\text{recall}}$ operationally as the maximum context length at which the model achieves over 95% accuracy on the 5-digit passkey retrieval task, averaged across answer depths. The passkey retrieval prompt is the same template used throughout the paper: a 5-digit number buried in repetitive "The grass is green. The sky is blue." filler text, with the model asked "What is the passkey? The passkey is" at the end.

The key difference between this task and the language modeling loss test used for $T_{\text{forget}}$ is that the filler text is highly repetitive and contains negligible new information. The total informational content of a 256K-token passkey prompt (net of the one novel 5-digit number) is roughly constant regardless of context length — it's the same few sentences repeated thousands of times. Therefore, the state capacity needed to *distinguish* the passkey from the noise does not grow with $T$; it depends only on the complexity of the signal-to-noise separation problem, which is determined by the statistical properties of the filler text (how strongly its token embeddings correlate with the passkey's token embeddings).

**Evaluation protocol:** for each model checkpoint and each context length $T \in \{1\text{K}, 2\text{K}, 4\text{K}, 8\text{K}, 16\text{K}, 32\text{K}, 64\text{K}, 128\text{K}, 256\text{K}\}$, the paper generates prompts with the passkey positioned at evenly spaced depths from 0% to 100% of the context (the exact number of depth positions is $\lfloor T / 1000 \rfloor$ to ensure approximately one sample per 1000 tokens). The model is prompted with greedy decoding and 32-bit floating-point precision for both parameters and activations to eliminate floating-point imprecision as a confounding factor (Appendix B notes that BF16 introduces errors around $10^{-3}$ in $\Delta_t$ and $\alpha_t$, though the state explosion is observed regardless). If >95% of trials produce the correct 5-digit number, the context length is considered within the recall threshold.

##### The Scaling Law: $T_{\text{recall}}(N_S)$

Figure 9 plots the measured $T_{\text{recall}}$ values against state size $N_S$ and fits an exponential model:

$$T_{\text{recall}} = 4.756 \cdot (1.365^{N_S} - 1) - 0.742,$$

where $N_S$ is the state size in millions of parameters and $T_{\text{recall}}$ is in thousands of tokens. The $R^2$ value again exceeds 0.999.

**What this means operationally:** doubling the state size multiplies the maximum recallable context length by a factor of roughly $1.365^{N_S^{\text{(new)}} - N_S^{\text{(old)}}}$ — the gain is super-linear. For the 370M model ($N_S = 12.9\text{M}$), the formula predicts $T_{\text{recall}} \approx 4.756 \times (1.365^{12.9} - 1) - 0.742 \approx 4.756 \times (55.3 - 1) - 0.742 \approx 257\text{K}$, which matches the experimental finding that this model achieves near-perfect retrieval at 256K when trained with sufficiently long contexts (specifically, 64K training length).

**Why exponential form:** each additional unit of state capacity adds a multiplicative factor to the distinguishability of the signal (the passkey) from the noise (the repetitive filler text). Because the noise is constant in information content regardless of context length, the problem reduces to storing the passkey's representation in a way that remains accessible despite the accumulated filler-token representations in the state. With $N_S$ million state parameters, the state can represent approximately $C \cdot \exp(\beta \cdot N_S)$ distinct configurations (the exponential comes from the combinatorics of the continuous-valued state — each parameter adds a dimension to the representational space, and the volume of distinguishable configurations grows exponentially with dimensionality). If each configuration can "store" one token's worth of distinguishing information, then the model can separate the passkey from the filler for contexts up to length proportional to the number of distinguishable configurations.

A critical caveat: this exponential scaling only holds for tasks where the contextual information content is constant — i.e., where extending the context adds repetition rather than novelty. For language modeling on original text (where each new token carries novel information), the maximum context length would scale more modestly, likely linearly with state size as in the $T_{\text{forget}}$ relationship.

##### The Gap Between $T_{\text{forget}}$ and $T_{\text{recall}}$

A key insight from comparing Figures 9 and 11 is that $T_{\text{recall}} \gg T_{\text{forget}}$ for all state sizes. For the 370M model, $T_{\text{forget}} \approx 64\text{K}$ while $T_{\text{recall}} \approx 256\text{K}$. The paper interprets this as evidence that the model retains some useful contextual information beyond the point where it cannot perfectly preserve *all* information — the forgetting threshold is the point where the state is full (can't store everything), but the retrieval threshold is the point where the signal-to-noise ratio for a specific, highly salient token (the passkey) drops below a usable level. Because the passkey is a single novel number amid repetitive filler, it can remain retrievable far beyond the point where the model would fail on a dense language modeling task that requires tracking many distinct pieces of information.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Length Generalization Failure as a Failure to Forget, Not a Failure to Retain

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is to invert the default diagnosis for why recurrent models fail on long contexts. The intuitive assumption — and one implicitly adopted by much prior work on length generalization for Mamba (Ben-Kish et al., 2024; Zhang, 2023) — is that RNNs struggle with long sequences because they *cannot retain information long enough*: the exponential decay is too aggressive, the state "forgets" too quickly, and older tokens become inaccessible. The natural fix, under this assumption, is to make the decay weaker — to push α_t closer to 1 so the state retains more context. This is precisely what the Concurrent work LongMamba (Zhang, 2023) does by dividing ∆_t by a constant, which raises α_t and increases retention.

This paper demonstrates that this diagnosis is exactly backward for Mamba-2. The problem is not that the model forgets too much — it is that the model *never learns to forget at all*. The evidence is both direct and causal. Directly, Figure 3 shows that some heads maintain a cumulative first-token retention α_{1:t} above 0.997 at the full 8K training boundary, meaning the very first token's contribution is preserved almost entirely. Causally, the two interventional experiments in Section 3.2.2 show that *artificially inducing more forgetting* — either by scaling down α_t and B_t (RRI) or by enforcing an exact sliding window — rescues language modeling performance on contexts beyond the training length (Figure 4). If the problem were insufficient retention, making the model forget *more* should hurt, not help. The fact that stronger forgetting helps is the paper's cleanest demonstration that the default assumption is wrong.

This inversion has significant implications beyond Mamba-2. Many recently proposed RNN architectures — RWKV, GLA, RetNet, and the broader family of gated linear attention models — share the same weighted-sum structure (Equation 6) and the same exponential decay mechanism. The paper's diagnostic framework suggests that any of these models trained on contexts shorter than their effective state capacity may exhibit the same failure mode: learn to retain everything, never learn to forget, collapse on longer contexts. The field's prior focus on improving retention — making the state "more memory-like" — may have been solving the wrong problem. The bottleneck is not storage capacity but *selective discarding*, a fundamentally different optimization challenge.

What makes this insight distinctive is that it is not merely a new empirical finding but a *reframing of the problem space*. Before this work, the dominant axis of investigation for RNN length generalization was retention strength. After this work, the axis shifts to forgetting as a learned behavior — something that must be actively acquired through appropriate training, not automatically guaranteed by an exponential decay formula. This is a conceptual shift analogous to the recognition in the 2010s that deep networks don't automatically generalize well just because they have many parameters — they need explicit regularization, data augmentation, and careful optimization to avoid overfitting. The paper positions forgetting as the recurrent analog of generalization: architecture provides the *mechanism* (exponential decay), but training dynamics determine whether that mechanism actually produces the desired *behavior* (selective discarding of irrelevant context).

The distinction between *can forget* (architectural capability) and *does forget* (learned behavior) is the paper's single most portable idea, and it changes how one should think about training recurrent language models.

---

### Innovation 2: The Concept of "State Overparameterization" as a Training Pathology

The paper introduces **state overparameterization** as a diagnostic concept that explains *why* Mamba-2 fails to learn forgetting. This is not merely a restatement of the "the state is too large" observation — it is a claim about the *training dynamics* that emerge when state capacity exceeds the information content of the training context, and about the analogy between this failure mode and classical overfitting.

Prior work has observed that Mamba struggles on contexts longer than training length (Jelassi et al., 2024; Waleffe et al., 2024; Ben-Kish et al., 2024), but these observations were phenomenological — they reported that degradation happens without explaining the mechanism behind it. The field lacked a causal model for *why* training length matters. The paper's state overparameterization hypothesis fills this gap: when the state size N_S is large enough relative to the training context length T_train, the model can minimize language modeling loss by simply retaining *all* tokens with near-maximal α_t values, cramming T_train tokens' worth of information into the fixed-size state without needing to make any decisions about what to discard. This strategy works perfectly on the training distribution — the model sees exactly T_train tokens and needs to predict the next one; retaining everything provides maximal context for every prediction. But it fails catastrophically on longer contexts, because the state fills up and interference between retained token representations begins to dominate.

The key evidence that elevates this from a plausible story to a compelling hypothesis is the **training dynamics** experiment in Section 4.1 and Figure 8. When a Mamba-2 model is trained from scratch with a deliberately short context (T_train = 512), the model *starts* with a reasonable recency bias — early checkpoints (10B tokens) accurately retrieve passkeys positioned near the end of the context and forget earlier ones. As training progresses (20B, 40B tokens), the model's retrieval accuracy for contexts longer than 512 *decreases* while its within-context performance *increases*. This is exactly the signature of overfitting: the model is converging toward a solution that exploits the training distribution (by retaining everything) at the expense of generalization (by never learning to forget). The paper explicitly frames this as "a kind of overfitting because the state's distribution with short contexts is not varied enough for the model to generalize to the distribution in longer contexts" (Section 4.1).

This framing is intellectually significant because it connects a seemingly architecture-specific problem to a well-understood concept in machine learning. Overfitting is a training pathology with known remedies: more data, regularization, and better data distributions that force the model to learn the desired generalization. By analogizing state overparameterization to overfitting, the paper suggests a research program: rather than changing the architecture (adding more sophisticated gating, different decay parameterizations), change the *training* — specifically, train with longer contexts that exceed the state's raw storage capacity, forcing the model to learn which information is worth keeping and which should be discarded. This is exactly the intervention the paper demonstrates in Section 5.

The distinction from classical overfitting is worth noting. In standard supervised learning, overfitting means the model memorizes training examples rather than learning generalizable features, and the remedy is to increase the training set diversity or reduce model capacity. Here, overfitting means the model learns to *retain everything* rather than learning a forgetting policy, and the remedy is to increase the *context length* (not the dataset size per se) to force information content beyond state capacity. The conceptual analogy holds — the model exploits a feature of the training distribution (finite context length) that does not generalize — but the specific mechanism and remedy are novel to the recurrent setting.

This insight is fundamental rather than incremental: it identifies a previously unrecognized failure mode of recurrent language model training, provides a mechanistic explanation with testable predictions (the linear T_forget(N_S) scaling law), and suggests a concrete path forward (train longer). It transforms the problem from an architectural limitation into a training optimization problem.

### Innovation 3: Empirically Establishing the T_forget(N_S) Scaling Law as a Prescriptive Design Principle

The paper does not merely claim that longer training contexts help — it provides a **quantitative law** that tells practitioners exactly how long they need to train for a given model size. The linear relationship T_forget = 5.172 · N_S − 4.469 (with R² > 0.999) is, to the best of my knowledge, the first empirically derived scaling law linking a recurrent model's state size to its minimum required training context length. This is significant because it transforms a qualitative observation ("train longer") into a prescriptive engineering guideline ("for a model with N_S million state parameters, you must train on at least 5.172 · N_S − 4.469 thousand tokens to get robust length generalization").

The existence of such a law is not obvious a priori. One could imagine that the relationship between state size and required training length is non-monotonic (larger models converge faster and therefore need relatively shorter training, or larger models are harder to optimize and need relatively longer training), or that it depends on dataset characteristics and training hyperparameters in ways that preclude a simple parametric fit. The remarkably tight fit (R² > 0.999 across a 16× range of state sizes, from 0.8M to 12.9M state parameters) suggests that the relationship is robust and architecture-determined — it reflects a fundamental information-theoretic constraint rather than a contingent property of the specific training setup.

What distinguishes this from the many scaling laws in the literature (e.g., Chinchilla, Kaplan et al.) is its **mechanism-specific nature**. Most scaling laws predict macroscopic quantities (loss, accuracy) from macroscopic inputs (parameters, data, compute) without specifying *why* the relationship takes a particular functional form — they are empirical regressions validated by predictive accuracy. The T_forget law, by contrast, emerges from a specific hypothesized mechanism: the state can store roughly C tokens of information per unit of state capacity, and when the training context exceeds this capacity, the model is forced to learn selective forgetting. The linear form is thus a *prediction* of the capacity hypothesis, not an arbitrary curve fit, and the tightness of the fit (R² > 0.999) serves as evidence for the hypothesis.

This has two downstream implications. First, it provides a direct answer to the question "how long should I train my Mamba-2 model?" — a question that previously had no principled answer. The paper notes that the official Mamba-2 checkpoints were all trained on 8K contexts regardless of model size, which is adequate for the 130M model (N_S = 4.8M, T_forget ≈ 20.4K, so 8K is insufficient but not catastrophically so) but severely inadequate for the 780M model (N_S = 19.3M, T_forget ≈ 95.3K — far above the 8K training length). This explains the otherwise puzzling finding in Figure 1 that larger models have *worse* length generalization: they are more severely undertrained relative to their state capacity, so the over-retention pathology is more pronounced.

Second, it provides a framework for thinking about the relationship between model scale and training efficiency that goes beyond simple parameter-count scaling laws. If larger models *require* longer training contexts to learn effective memory management, then scaling up model size without scaling up context length proportionally is not just suboptimal — it is actively harmful to length generalization. This insight changes how one should think about the design space for recurrent models, suggesting that model size and training context length are coupled in a way that has not been previously recognized.

The innovation is fundamentally a **new diagnostic concept** (state-relative undertraining) with **quantitative predictive power** (the 5.172 coefficient). It is incremental in the sense that it builds on the state overparameterization hypothesis and the training-length sweep methodology, but fundamental in that it converts a conceptual diagnosis into an engineering tool that can guide future model development.

---

### Innovation 4: Demonstrating That Properly Trained Small RNNs Can Match or Exceed Transformers on Extreme-Length Retrieval

While the paper's primary contributions are diagnostic, its **positive result** on passkey retrieval represents a significant empirical demonstration: a 370M parameter Mamba-2 model, when trained with a context length (64K) that exceeds its state capacity threshold, achieves near-perfect retrieval accuracy at 256K tokens (Figure 9). The paper states this is "to the best of our knowledge, no previous models with less than 1B model parameters have near-perfect accuracy at this length in this task" (Section 5.3).

This result matters for two reasons. First, it serves as an **existence proof** that the diagnosed problem — inability to forget due to state overparameterization — is the primary bottleneck. When the model is trained in a regime where it *must* learn to forget (because T_train > T_forget), it spontaneously develops the memory management skills needed to handle contexts far beyond its training length, without any architectural modification. The failure was a training problem, not a fundamental architectural limitation, and fixing the training procedure unlocks the latent capability. This shifts the conversation from "RNNs can't handle long contexts" to "RNNs can handle long contexts if trained appropriately" — a much more optimistic framing that opens pathways for future work rather than closing them.

Second, it challenges the narrative that transformers are inherently superior for long-context tasks. The paper does not claim that Mamba-2 beats transformers on all long-context metrics, but the specific benchmark — 256K passkey retrieval with <1B parameters — is a striking data point. Transformer models at similar scale typically require positional encoding tricks (YaRN, SelfExtend, LongRoPE) to reach comparable context lengths, and even then, performance often degrades gradually rather than maintaining near-perfect accuracy. The fact that a recurrent model with constant-cost inference can achieve this through straightforward training (longer sequences, no architectural changes) suggests that the efficiency benefits of recurrence may not need to be traded off against long-context capability, at least for tasks where the context is repetitive or information-sparse.

The caveat, which the paper itself emphasizes, is that this exponential scaling (T_recall ∝ exp(β · N_S)) applies specifically to tasks where the contextual information content does not grow with length — the passkey retrieval task uses repetitive filler text, so extending the context from 64K to 256K adds no new information that the model needs to retain. For dense language modeling on novel text, the scaling would be linear, governed by the T_forget relationship. This distinction is crucial: the exponential scaling is not a claim that Mamba-2 can perform arbitrary language tasks at 256K context length with a 370M model, but rather a claim about the *representational capacity* of the state when the signal-to-noise ratio is favorable.

The significance of this innovation is primarily **empirical and rhetorical** — it demonstrates that the diagnostic insights translate into real performance improvements, which strengthens the paper's central argument that the inability to forget is the core problem. Without this positive result, the paper would be purely a critique: "Mamba-2 fails at long contexts, and here is why." With it, the paper offers a constructive path forward: "Mamba-2 can succeed at long contexts, and here is how to make it work."

The innovation is incremental in the sense that it follows directly from applying the T_forget scaling law (train at 64K for the 370M model) and evaluating on a standard benchmark, but fundamental in its implications: it reopens the possibility space for recurrent architectures as viable long-context models, suggesting that the field's pivot toward transformers for long-context tasks may have been premature given the underexplored potential of properly-trained RNNs.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary training corpus is RedPajama-V2 (Computer, 2023), an open web dataset containing approximately 30T tokens. To ensure the data contains long-range dependencies critical for studying forgetting behavior, the paper filters out documents shorter than 4K tokens, which removes roughly 97.6% of the original corpus. Longer training sequences are constructed by concatenating filtered documents with a special End-of-Sequence (EOS) token delimiter. For the passkey retrieval evaluation, the authors construct synthetic prompts using a fixed template adapted from Zhang et al. (2024a), consisting of repetitive filler text ("The grass is green. The sky is blue.") with a 5-digit passkey embedded at varying depths. Validation for selecting optimal training checkpoints is performed on passkey retrieval accuracy.

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses Mamba-2 (Dao & Gu, 2024) as the primary architecture, with six model sizes spanning 36.4M to 780M parameters. Three models (36.4M, 47.0M, 84.6M) are trained from scratch on RedPajama-V2, while three larger models (130M, 370M, 780M) are official Mamba-2 checkpoints that receive continued pre-training on longer contexts. The key architectural parameter is the state size $N_S$, defined as the total number of recurrent state parameters across all layers and heads (see Appendix A.1): $N_S = 2 \cdot d \cdot N \cdot L$, where $d$ is hidden dimension, $N = 128$ is the per-head state dimension, and $L$ is the number of layers. This ranges from 0.8M for the 36.4M model to 19.3M for the 780M model. Additional architectures evaluated include Mamba-1 (130M, 370M, 790M, 1.4B, 2.8B), RWKV-5 (1.5B, 3B), RWKV-6 (1.6B), and HGRN-2 (1.3B) for breadth, though the primary quantitative analysis focuses on Mamba-2.

- **Metrics.** Two primary metrics are used. For language modeling, the paper reports **cross-entropy loss** as a function of token position — this is the standard next-token prediction loss computed individually at each position $t$ in a sequence, averaged across 128 prompts. For the passkey retrieval task, the metric is **retrieval accuracy** — the fraction of trials where the model's greedy-decoded output exactly matches the 5-digit passkey, with accuracy computed as a function of both context length and the relative answer depth (passkey position divided by total context length). For the $T_{\text{recall}}$ threshold determination, a model is considered to have "accurate" retrieval at a given context length if it achieves over 95% accuracy averaged across answer depths. For the $T_{\text{forget}}$ threshold determination, the criterion is whether the language modeling loss at any position in a 1M-token prompt exceeds 2× the maximum loss observed within the first $T_{\text{train}}$ tokens, averaged over 128 prompts.

- **Baselines.** The paper's investigation is primarily diagnostic rather than comparative, so formal baselines are limited. However, several reference points serve as implicit baselines: the **original Mamba-2 checkpoints** evaluated at their training length (8K) represent the status quo; **LongMamba** (Zhang, 2023), a prior length-extrapolation method that divides $\Delta_t$ by a constant (0.5), is tested as a heuristic intervention baseline; **majority voting** or other decoding strategies beyond greedy are not used, as preliminary results indicated they gave "noticeably worse performance on passkey retrieval" (Appendix B). For the FLOPs-matched training comparisons, the paper contrasts models trained with different context lengths at fixed model sizes, with the training-length-as-baseline being the official 8K configuration.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a generation budget or FLOPs-matching framework for its primary experiments — the analysis focuses on evaluating fixed models at varying context lengths rather than comparing inference-time strategies at equal compute. Training cost is reported in terms of tokens processed (e.g., intermediate checkpoints at 10B, 20B, 40B tokens), training context length (512 to 256K tokens), and batch size (0.5M tokens per batch). Inference is performed with greedy decoding at FP32 precision to eliminate numerical precision artifacts. The 1M-token prompts used for $T_{\text{forget}}$ determination represent a fixed evaluation cost independent of model training.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For hyperparameter selection (learning rate sweep over $\{1 \times 10^{-5}, 2 \times 10^{-5}, 5 \times 10^{-5}, 1 \times 10^{-4}, 2 \times 10^{-4}, 5 \times 10^{-4}, 1 \times 10^{-3}\}$), the paper selects the configuration with best passkey retrieval accuracy on a held-out validation set, noting that "the loss of many checkpoints was highly similar, their performance in passkey retrieval can differ a lot" (Appendix F.1) — suggesting that standard validation loss is insufficient for detecting length generalization quality. The RRI multipliers ($c_\alpha = 0.9999$, $c_B = 0.75$) are chosen by validation using average loss on pre-training data with 32K context length. The passkey retrieval evaluation at each context length $T$ uses $\lfloor T / 1000 \rfloor$ evenly spaced answer depths to ensure comprehensive coverage. No formal confidence intervals or standard errors are reported for the scaling law fits.

### Main Quantitative Results

The paper's experimental results are organized into four axes: (1) behavioral characterization of existing Mamba-2 checkpoints, (2) causal verification through interventional experiments, (3) training dynamics analysis showing the emergence of over-retention, and (4) scaling-law determination via controlled training-length sweeps.

#### Behavioral Characterization: Mamba-2's Length Generalization Failure

The paper first establishes the existence and pattern of the length generalization failure in official Mamba-2 checkpoints. Figure 1 plots language modeling loss as a function of token position for four model sizes (130M, 370M, 780M, 1.3B parameters), all trained on 8K contexts. All models exhibit a sharp increase in loss when the context length exceeds roughly 8K tokens, but the degradation is worse for larger models: the 1.3B model's loss rises more steeply and to higher absolute values than the 130M model's loss. This is a counterintuitive finding — one would expect larger models to be more robust — and it provides the first clue that the failure is related to state management rather than capacity shortages.

Figure 2 provides complementary evidence from the passkey retrieval task, evaluated on the same four model sizes. Within the 8K training length, all models except the smallest (130M) achieve near-perfect retrieval accuracy across all answer depths. When the context is extended to 16K, performance collapses: all model sizes show near-zero accuracy at all answer depths, regardless of whether the passkey is positioned at the beginning (low depth) or end (high depth) of the context. The paper emphasizes a critical detail: the model fails to recall even recent tokens positioned at 90–100% depth in the 16K context. If the problem were simply that old information decays away (insufficient retention), the model should still retrieve tokens placed near the end of the context successfully — the fact that it doesn't indicates that interference from retained earlier tokens is damaging the recall of recent tokens.

This pattern — degradation beyond training length, worse for larger models, failure at all retrieval positions — provides the phenomenological foundation that motivates the diagnostic investigation.

#### Causal Verification: Artificially Inducing Forgetting Rescues Performance

Figure 4 presents the paper's key causal evidence, showing language modeling loss as a function of token position for Mamba-2 370M under four conditions: the original model, the LongMamba $\Delta_t$-scaling intervention, the RRI method ($c_\alpha = 0.9999$, $c_B = 0.75$), and the sliding window method. At positions within the 8K training length, the original model achieves the lowest loss (~2.7), while the interventions degrade short-context performance (RRI loss ~3.0, sliding window loss ~3.2, LongMamba loss ~3.5). Beyond the training length, the pattern reverses: the original model's loss spikes from ~3.2 at 8K to ~5.0 at 32K, while the sliding window maintains loss below ~3.2 throughout, RRI maintains loss around ~3.5, and LongMamba partially escapes the degradation but underperforms the other interventions, with loss rising to ~4.0 at 32K.

The sliding window method achieves the best long-context performance because it exactly eliminates all tokens beyond the window — there is zero interference from early context. The RRI method helps by globally reducing retention and insertion strength, but because it applies the same multiplier to all heads regardless of their learned specialization, it cannot recover performance as fully as the exact window. LongMamba, which makes $\Delta_t$ smaller (pushing $\alpha_t$ closer to 1), works in the opposite direction of RRI — it increases retention — and provides only modest improvement, consistent with the paper's claim that the problem is over-retention, not under-retention.

It is important to note that LongMamba was tested with a scaling factor of 0.5 for $\Delta_t$, but the paper states that sweeping different values for passkey retrieval yielded "near-zero accuracy across all passkey positions and context lengths" (Appendix C). This negative result for a retention-increasing approach further supports the over-retention hypothesis.

#### Training Dynamics: Forgetting Degrades as Training Progresses

Figure 8 tracks how passkey retrieval accuracy evolves during the training of a Mamba-2 370M model from scratch with a deliberately short training length of 512 tokens. At 10B training tokens (Figure 8a), the model shows a pronounced recency bias: retrieval accuracy is high (>75%) when the passkey is positioned at 80–100% depth in the context, and drops to near zero for passkeys at 0–40% depth. This is the expected behavior for a model that has learned to forget older tokens and retain recent ones.

At 20B training tokens (Figure 8b), the recency bias begins to erode: accuracy at high depths (80–100%) drops to 25–50%, while accuracy at low depths remains near zero. The model is retaining more information from earlier in the context, which creates interference that harms even recent-token retrieval. The overall accuracy decreases, particularly for the longest (8K) context length.

At 40B training tokens (Figure 8c), the retrieval pattern has inverted: accuracy is near zero at all answer depths and all context lengths (1K, 4K, 8K). The model no longer exhibits any recency bias — it has converged to a state of near-complete retention of all tokens, which causes such severe interference that no token position can be reliably retrieved. Meanwhile, the text reports that "the model's accuracy for contexts shorter than the training length increases" from 10B to 40B tokens (Section 4.1), establishing the overfitting signature: in-distribution performance improves while out-of-distribution performance degrades.

This result is central to the state overparameterization hypothesis. The model starts with the ability to forget (recency bias at 10B tokens) but loses this ability during training because the loss function, computed only on tokens within the 512-length context, rewards the strategy of retaining all information. The 512-token context is well within the 370M model's state capacity, so the model never encounters a training signal that would penalize excessive retention or require selective forgetting.

#### Scaling Law Determination: $T_{\text{forget}}(N_S)$ via Controlled Training-Length Sweep

The paper's most resource-intensive experiment trains Mamba-2 models at five different sizes (36.4M, 47.0M, 84.6M, 130M, 370M) with varying training context lengths, then evaluates each trained model on 1M-token language modeling prompts to determine whether robust forgetting has been learned.

Figure 10 shows representative loss-vs-position curves. For the 130M model (Figure 10a), training at 4K length produces a severe loss spike beyond ~10K tokens (rising from ~3.2 to ~4.5). Training at 8K partially alleviates the spike (loss rises to ~3.5 at ~15K), and training at 16K completely eliminates it — the loss remains stable near 2.8–3.2 throughout the 32K evaluation length. Thus, for the 130M model with $N_S = 4.8\text{M}$, the forgetting threshold lies between 8K and 16K training length.

For the 370M model (Figure 10b), training at 8K produces catastrophic failure beyond ~12K tokens (loss spikes from ~2.7 to ~4.0). Training at 16K improves somewhat but loss still rises to ~3.3 at ~30K before declining. Training at 32K further improves (loss stays below ~3.0 up to ~80K, with a mild bump to ~3.1 at ~100K), and training at 64K achieves stable loss below ~3.0 throughout the entire 128K evaluation length. Thus, for the 370M model with $N_S = 12.9\text{M}$, the threshold is approximately 64K.

The paper formalizes these observations into a binary criterion: a training length $T_{\text{train}}$ is above $T_{\text{forget}}$ if the model's loss on 1M-token prompts never exceeds 2× the maximum loss within the first $T_{\text{train}}$ tokens. This criterion is applied across all five model sizes, producing the data points in Figure 11.

Figure 11 plots $T_{\text{forget}}$ (in thousands of tokens) against state size $N_S$ (in millions of parameters). The five data points are fit to a linear model:

$$T_{\text{forget}} = 5.172 \cdot N_S - 4.469$$

with $R^2 > 0.999$. For the smallest model (36.4M, $N_S = 0.8\text{M}$), $T_{\text{forget}} \approx 5.172 \times 0.8 - 4.469 \approx -0.33\text{K}$, which is below zero — the paper notes this means the model learns forgetting at any positive training length, consistent with the state being small enough that even short training contexts exceed its capacity. For the largest successfully tested model (370M, $N_S = 12.9\text{M}$), $T_{\text{forget}} \approx 62.3\text{K}$, and the experimental data confirms that 64K training length is sufficient.

The paper reports that the 780M model ($N_S = 19.3\text{M}$) still exhibits poor length generalization at training lengths up to 128K, consistent with the predicted $T_{\text{forget}} \approx 95.3\text{K}$ — but due to computational constraints, the authors could not train beyond 128K to verify whether 96K+ training would achieve forgetting. This rightmost point is absent from Figure 11, making the scaling law an interpolation across the 0.8M–12.9M range rather than an extrapolation to larger models.

#### Scaling Law Determination: $T_{\text{recall}}(N_S)$ via Passkey Retrieval Evaluation

For each model checkpoint trained in the length sweep, the paper also evaluates passkey retrieval accuracy at context lengths up to 256K, with the passkey embedded at evenly spaced depths. The metric is whether retrieval accuracy exceeds 95% — if so, the model is considered to have achieved accurate recall at that context length.

Figure 9 plots the maximum context length with >95% passkey retrieval accuracy ($T_{\text{recall}}$) against state size $N_S$. The data points are fit to an exponential model:

$$T_{\text{recall}} = 4.756 \cdot (1.365^{N_S} - 1) - 0.742$$

with $R^2 > 0.999$. For the 370M model ($N_S = 12.9\text{M}$), the formula predicts $T_{\text{recall}} \approx 4.756 \times (1.365^{12.9} - 1) - 0.742 \approx 257\text{K}$, matching the experimental finding that this model achieves near-perfect retrieval at 256K when trained with 64K contexts (which exceeds its $T_{\text{forget}} \approx 62\text{K}$).

The exponential form is notable because it implies that each additional unit of state capacity multiplies the maximum recallable context length by a factor of approximately 1.365 — a super-linear gain. The smallest model (36.4M, $N_S = 0.8\text{M}$) achieves perfect retrieval only up to about 4K tokens; the 130M model ($N_S = 4.8\text{M}$) reaches ~64K; and the 370M model reaches ~256K. The growth is dramatic: a 10× increase in state size (from 1.3M to 12.9M) produces roughly a 64× increase in recallable context length (from ~4K to ~256K).

A critical comparison: at the same state sizes, $T_{\text{recall}} \gg T_{\text{forget}}$. For the 370M model, $T_{\text{forget}} \approx 62\text{K}$ while $T_{\text{recall}} \approx 256\text{K}$. The paper interprets this gap as reflecting the difference between dense language modeling (where every token carries novel information and the state must manage interference across all retained representations) and sparse retrieval (where the contextual information is constant regardless of length — the filler text is repetitive — so the state can maintain a distinguishable representation of the passkey far beyond the point where it would fail at coherent language modeling).

#### Additional Architecture Evaluations

The paper extends its evaluation to other recurrent architectures in the appendices. Figure 13 shows RWKV-5 (1.5B and 3B) on passkey retrieval; these models maintain reasonable accuracy at 16K contexts (unlike Mamba-2 at comparable scale), particularly for passkeys positioned at high depths. Figure 14 shows similar results for RWKV-6 (1.6B): the recency bias is preserved even at 16K, with accuracy at 90–100% depth remaining around 75%. Figure 15 shows Mamba-1 (790M, 1.4B, 2.8B) on passkey retrieval, and it exhibits the same inability-to-forget pattern as Mamba-2 — accuracy collapses at all depths when context exceeds training length.

The paper hypothesizes that RWKV's better length generalization arises from its smaller state size (Figure 12 shows the state-size-to-model-size relationship for these architectures): RWKV-5/6 have proportionally smaller recurrent states than Mamba-2, meaning their training on standard context lengths may already exceed the state capacity threshold that forces forgetting to be learned. This is consistent with the state overparameterization hypothesis but is presented as an interpretation rather than a verified claim, since the paper did not perform controlled training-length sweeps on RWKV architectures.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Floating-point precision effects on passkey retrieval**: The paper reports (Appendix B) that BF16 introduces errors around $10^{-3}$ in $\Delta_t$ and $\alpha_t$ computations, but the state channel explosion and retrieval failure patterns are consistently observed across FP32, FP16, and BF16. All reported passkey results use FP32 for both parameters and activations to eliminate numerical precision as a confounding variable in the main experiments.

- **Passkey prompt template variations**: Preliminary experiments with slight variations to the passkey retrieval template (Appendix B.1) do not produce considerable differences in results. The specific template — "The passkey is 34847. Remember it. 34847 is the passkey." — is adapted from Zhang et al. (2024a) and used consistently.

- **Greedy vs. alternative decoding strategies**: The paper states that "other decoding parameters give noticeably worse performance on passkey retrieval" (Appendix B), so all reported passkey results use greedy decoding. This suggests that the length generalization failure is not merely a decoding artifact.

- **Training length thresholds for intermediate model sizes**: The scaling laws in Figures 9 and 11 are fit to five data points spanning 0.8M to 12.9M state sizes. The 780M model ($N_S = 19.3\text{M}$) is not included in the fit because its $T_{\text{forget}}$ could not be determined — it still shows poor generalization at 128K training, consistent with the predicted $T_{\text{forget}} \approx 95.3\text{K}$, but the necessary training budget for 128K+ contexts was infeasible. This means the exponential fit for $T_{\text{recall}}$ also cannot be verified at the 19.3M scale. The extrapolation quality to larger state sizes is unknown.

- **Data filtering threshold (4K tokens)**: The paper filters out documents shorter than 4K tokens from RedPajama-V2, removing roughly 97.6% of the corpus. The paper acknowledges that Buckman & Gelada (2024) have shown this type of filtering to be "critical for training effective long-context models," but does not ablate the threshold to test sensitivity — it is possible that a higher threshold (e.g., 8K) would produce different results, or that the 97.6% data removal introduces selection bias into the training distribution.

- **TBPTT concatenation length**: The paper uses truncated backpropagation through time with 12 concatenated sequences. This parameter is adopted from Yang et al. (2024a) and preliminary tests, but no ablation over different concatenation lengths is reported. The effectiveness of TBPTT in diversifying state initializations may depend on this parameter, and different values could shift the $T_{\text{forget}}$ thresholds.

- **WSD scheduler vs. alternatives**: The WSD learning rate scheduler is chosen because it allows simple resumption from intermediate checkpoints and is "competitive with the commonly used cosine scheduler." No direct comparison of WSD vs. cosine scheduling on the forgetting behavior is reported.

- **Validation metric choice**: The paper emphasizes that validation loss and passkey retrieval accuracy can be poorly correlated — "the loss of many checkpoints was highly similar, their performance in passkey retrieval can differ a lot" (Appendix F.1). This is a significant methodological finding: standard validation loss is insufficient for early stopping when the goal is length generalization, because loss within the training context length can remain low even as out-of-distribution retrieval collapses. All hyperparameter and checkpoint selections use passkey retrieval accuracy as the validation metric.

- **State statistics on alternative prompts**: The paper primarily reports state distribution statistics on the "newlines" prompt for reproducibility and smoothness. It states that "similar state distribution and model behavior are observed on prompts extracted from the pre-training corpus, the passkey retrieval task, or other randomly generated sequences" (Appendix I), but does not show these comparisons quantitatively. The "newlines" prompt is a controlled environment that isolates recurrence dynamics from input content effects, which is appropriate for the diagnostic purpose but means the reported variance explosion patterns (Figure 5, Figure 16, Figure 17) may not fully represent behavior on natural text.

- **Convolutional state stability**: Appendix G and Figure 17 show that the short convolutional states (which operate on the last 4 tokens) remain stable throughout 32K-token sequences, in contrast to the recurrent states which exhibit variance explosions. This ablation confirms that the instability is specific to the recurrent state accumulation and not a general architectural property.

- **Get architectures beyond Mamba-2**: HGRN-2 and RWKV-6 are evaluated on the "newlines" prompt (Appendix H) and also exhibit perplexity degradation, though the degradation for RWKV-6 is less severe and occurs before the training length is reached — a finding the paper interprets as consistent with the overparameterization hypothesis given RWKV-6's smaller state size but concedes may also reflect training distribution effects. No controlled training-length sweeps are performed for these architectures.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments reported in this paper provide substantial empirical support for its central claims, but several important qualifications must be applied. Each major claim is examined in light of what was actually tested.

**Claim: Mamba-2's length generalization failure is caused by the inability to forget earlier tokens.**

The evidence for this claim is strong and multi-layered. The retention auditing (Figure 3) directly shows that cumulative first-token retention $\alpha_{1:t}$ exceeds 0.997 at a training length of 8K for some heads, which is a striking quantitative demonstration of over-retention. The interventional experiments (Figure 4) provide causal evidence: artificially inducing forgetting improves long-context language modeling, while retention-increasing approaches (LongMamba, Appendix C) provide minimal or zero benefit. The training dynamics (Figure 8) show that the model starts with good forgetting and progressively loses it, consistent with the hypothesis that over-retention is acquired during training rather than inherent to the architecture.

However, the claim is demonstrated most directly for the **Mamba-2 370M model at 8K training length** — the retention auditing in Figure 3 is shown for one layer (38) out of 48, and the paper states that "similar observations can be found in other heads and in other layers as well," but full-layer statistics are only provided for state means and variances (Figures 16–17 in Appendix G), not for $\alpha_{1:t}$ retention specifically. The passkey and language modeling failures (Figures 1, 2) are shown across multiple model sizes and architectures (Mamba-1, RWKV, HGRN-2 in appendices), but the full diagnostic chain (retention auditing → interventional testing → training dynamics) is only complete for Mamba-2 370M. This is a reasonable scope for a diagnostic paper but means the generality of the "inability to forget" mechanism across model sizes and architectures remains partially extrapolated.

**Claim: State overparameterization — where state size exceeds the information content of the training context — explains why forgetting is not learned.**

The evidence for this claim comes primarily from two sources: the training dynamics experiment (Figure 8) and the scaling law determination (Figures 10, 11). The training dynamics provide compelling evidence for the overfitting analogy — the model's retrieval accuracy for out-of-distribution contexts decreases as training progresses while in-distribution performance improves — which is precisely the signature of a model exploiting its excess capacity. The linear $T_{\text{forget}}(N_S)$ relationship ($R^2 > 0.999$) is consistent with the capacity-threshold hypothesis because it implies a constant information-per-state-parameter ratio ($5.172 \text{K tokens} / 10^6 \text{ state parameters}$, or approximately 5.2 tokens per 1000 state parameters).

However, the evidentiary chain has missing links. The paper does not directly measure **how much information content is actually stored in the state** at different training lengths — the claim that "when the context contains more information than the state can simply memorize, the model is forced to learn selective forgetting" is a mechanistic interpretation of the linear fit, not a directly verified causal pathway. A direct test would involve measuring the mutual information between the state and past tokens as a function of training length, or demonstrating that models trained below $T_{\text{forget}}$ can be shown to store exact token identities while models trained above it compress information. The paper's evidence is consistent with the capacity-threshold hypothesis but does not rule out alternative explanations — for instance, longer training contexts might simply provide more diverse state initializations (via TBPTT), teaching the model to handle a wider range of state distributions without any necessary connection to capacity limits.

Additionally, the paper does not ablate the state size directly while holding other architectural parameters constant — the state size varies naturally with model size (hidden dimension × layers), so the linear relationship might be confounded by total model capacity or depth rather than being specific to recurrent state size. Training a Mamba-2 model with the same total parameters but different state sizes (by varying $N$ or $P$ while adjusting other dimensions) would isolate the state-size effect, but such experiments are not reported.

**Claim: The minimum training length required for learning forgetting scales linearly with state size ($T_{\text{forget}} = 5.172 \cdot N_S - 4.469$).**

This claim is supported by a remarkably clean fit across a 16× range of state sizes with $R^2 > 0.999$. The fit is based on five data points (36.4M through 370M models), each requiring a binary threshold determination ("does the loss exceed 2× the training-time maximum on 1M-token prompts?"). The binary-to-continuous conversion (fitting a line to binary threshold points) is somewhat coarse — the paper does not report uncertainty intervals on the threshold estimates, and the exact position of each threshold depends on the specific criterion (2× multiplier, 1M evaluation length, 128-prompt average). A sensitivity analysis varying these criterion parameters would strengthen confidence in the precise coefficient (5.172).

The linear relationship is extrapolated to the intercept ($N_S \approx 0.86\text{M}$ where $T_{\text{forget}} = 0$), but no models were tested in this regime. The intercept being negative is physically implausible (training length cannot be negative), but the paper treats this appropriately as an artifact of the linear fit rather than a prediction. More importantly, the rightward extrapolation to larger state sizes (the 780M model at $N_S = 19.3\text{M}$) is untested — the predicted $T_{\text{forget}} \approx 95.3\text{K}$ cannot be verified because training beyond 128K was computationally infeasible. If the relationship deviates from linearity at larger scales (e.g., due to optimization difficulties with very long sequences, or diminishing returns from TBPTT), the practical applicability of the scaling law to models in the 1B+ parameter range would be limited.

**Claim: The maximum context length for accurate passkey retrieval scales exponentially with state size ($T_{\text{recall}} = 4.756 \cdot (1.365^{N_S} - 1) - 0.742$).**

This is the paper's most striking quantitative result — an exponential fit with $R^2 > 0.999$ across the same five data points. However, the exponential form is fit to only five points, and exponential fits to small datasets are notoriously sensitive to outliers and susceptible to overfitting. A difference of one data point (e.g., if the 370M model actually achieved perfect retrieval only at 128K rather than 256K) could substantially change the base of the exponent (1.365). The paper does not report alternative functional forms or goodness-of-fit comparisons (e.g., would a polynomial or power-law fit equally well?).

The mechanistic interpretation — that exponential scaling arises because the amount of information in repetitive filler text is constant, and the state's distinguishable configurations grow exponentially with dimensionality — is plausible but not directly tested. Confirming this interpretation would require measuring retrieval accuracy as a function of filler-text diversity (e.g., varying the vocabulary size or repetition rate of the distracting context) to verify that the exponential scaling depends on constant information content as claimed.

**General Limitations**

The evaluation is conducted primarily on a single model architecture (Mamba-2) with supplementary checks on Mamba-1 and RWKV variants, but the core quantitative laws ($T_{\text{forget}}$ and $T_{\text{recall}}$) are only established for Mamba-2. The paper hypothesizes that these laws may apply to other gated linear attention models (GLA, RetNet, RWKV) but provides no evidence. The state-size analysis in Figure 12 shows that Mamba-2 has an unusually large state relative to its parameter count compared to other RNNs, so the specific coefficient (5.172) is likely Mamba-2-specific even if the linear form generalizes.

The paper does not evaluate on any standard long-context benchmark beyond passkey retrieval (e.g., LongBench, $\infty$Bench, needle-in-a-haystack with natural text). The passkey retrieval task, while clean and diagnostic, is a minimal test of retrieval ability — it does not assess whether the model can use retrieved information for reasoning, integrate information across multiple positions, or maintain coherent generation over long contexts. Demonstrating that properly trained Mamba-2 models can perform on these more demanding benchmarks would substantially strengthen the claim that the forgetting issue is the primary bottleneck for practical long-context applications.

The difficulty estimation and computational cost of the controlled training-length sweep is not analyzed in terms of total FLOPs. Training a 370M model at 64K context length is substantially more expensive than at 8K — the paper does not discuss whether the compute cost of long-context training is justified by the retrieval improvements, or whether training at $T_{\text{train}} \approx T_{\text{forget}}$ represents a compute-optimal allocation. This is particularly relevant because the $T_{\text{recall}}$ results suggest that training at $T_{\text{forget}}$ can unlock retrieval capabilities far beyond $T_{\text{forget}}$ — the "return on training investment" in terms of context-length generalization appears to be super-linear, but no formal analysis of this tradeoff is provided.

Finally, the paper's data filtering (removing 97.6% of RedPajama-V2 by discarding documents <4K tokens) raises a concern about training distribution bias. The remaining 2.4% of documents are long-form text with inherently different statistical properties than the broader web corpus, and models trained on this filtered data may develop forgetting strategies specific to long-form text structures rather than general-purpose forgetting. The paper does not discuss whether the $T_{\text{forget}}$ threshold would differ if trained on unfiltered data with a different long-range-dependency profile.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The $T_{\text{forget}}$ and $T_{\text{recall}}$ Scaling Laws Are Extrapolated From Only Five Data Points, With the Largest Model’s Threshold Unverified

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's two quantitative scaling laws — the linear relationship $T_{\text{forget}} = 5.172 \cdot N_S - 4.469$ and the exponential relationship $T_{\text{recall}} = 4.756 \cdot (1.365^{N_S} - 1) - 0.742$ — are fit to five data points spanning state sizes from 0.8M to 12.9M parameters (the 36.4M through 370M models in Table 2). These are the only models for which both thresholds could be determined. The paper explicitly acknowledges a missing data point at the high end:

> "We have confirmed that the 780M model (with a state size of 19.3M) also has poor length generalization at training lengths below 128K, but do not have enough resources to train the model beyond this length." (Section 5.2)

This means the rightmost region of both scaling laws — where state size exceeds 12.9M, which covers models at the scale of practical LLM deployments — is untested extrapolation.

**The consequence.** The practical applicability of these scaling laws depends critically on whether the linear and exponential forms hold at larger state sizes. If the relationship deviates — for example, if $T_{\text{forget}}$ grows super-linearly beyond 12.9M due to optimization difficulties with very long sequences, or if the exponential base (1.365) for $T_{\text{recall}}$ decreases as state sizes grow (because representational capacity hits diminishing returns at higher dimensions) — then the laws cannot be used to prescribe training lengths for models at the 780M, 1.3B, or 2.7B scales. A practitioner wanting to train a Mamba-2 2.7B model ($N_S \approx 67\text{M}$, using the $N_S = 2dNL$ formula from Appendix A.1 with $d = 2048$, $L = 64$, $H = 64$, $N = 128$) would obtain a predicted $T_{\text{forget}} \approx 5.172 \times 67 - 4.469 \approx 342\text{K}$ tokens, but the paper provides no evidence that this prediction is reliable.

The exponential fit for $T_{\text{recall}}$ is particularly sensitive because exponential functions fit to small datasets can be heavily influenced by a single point. The 370M model's measured $T_{\text{recall}} \approx 256\text{K}$ anchors the steep upward bend in Figure 9; if the true threshold were actually 128K (one step lower in the evaluation sweep), the fitted exponential base would drop substantially, and the predicted $T_{\text{recall}}$ for larger models would shrink accordingly. The paper reports the $R^2 > 0.999$ for both fits, which quantifies goodness-of-fit on the five training points but does not provide out-of-sample prediction error or confidence bands for extrapolation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 11 provides the five data points for $T_{\text{forget}}$ with the linear fit and $R^2$ value. Figure 9 provides the five data points for $T_{\text{recall}}$ with the exponential fit and $R^2$ value. The paper reports that the 780M model ($N_S = 19.3\text{M}$) still shows poor length generalization at 128K training, which is *consistent* with the predicted $T_{\text{forget}} \approx 95.3\text{K}$ (i.e., 128K is above 95.3K, so one would expect the model to have learned forgetting if the law holds), but the paper cannot confirm this because it did not verify that 128K training actually produces robust forgetting — it only states that training *below* 128K fails. There is no data point at or beyond 19.3M state size to validate either scaling law.

The paper does not report alternative functional forms (e.g., polynomial, power-law) or perform leave-one-out cross-validation to assess the stability of the fitted parameters. The exponential fit to five points has only three free parameters (base, coefficient, constant), meaning only two degrees of freedom remain; the high $R^2$ is therefore partly a consequence of the small sample size.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation beyond acknowledging the missing 780M data point and the computational infeasibility of training beyond 128K. No uncertainty quantification, confidence intervals, or sensitivity analysis is provided for the fitted coefficients. The authors frame the scaling laws as contributions to be used and further validated by future work, but practitioners relying on these laws for training decisions at larger scales would be operating on untested extrapolation.

### Difficulty Estimation (Whether the Model Has Learned Forgetting) Requires a Computationally Expensive 1M-Token Evaluation Not Included in Training Cost Accounting

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper defines the $T_{\text{forget}}$ threshold using an evaluation that is itself extremely expensive: for each trained model, the paper feeds 128 prompts of 1M tokens each through the model and checks whether the language modeling loss ever exceeds $2 \times$ the maximum loss within the training-length prefix. This means that determining whether a particular training length is adequate requires running inference on 128M tokens — comparable to or exceeding the cost of some of the training runs themselves, especially for the smaller models. The paper states this criterion in Section 5.1:

> "To determine whether the model has learned robust forgetting, we feed prompts with 1M tokens to the model and check if the model's loss exceeds 2× the maximum loss within $T_{\text{train}}$ tokens at any point."

This evaluation is used both to establish the scaling law and, in principle, would be needed by any practitioner who wants to verify that their specific training setup has crossed the forgetting threshold. There is no cheaper proxy metric proposed.

**The consequence.** The practical workflow implied by the paper is: train a Mamba-2 model at a candidate context length, then run a 128M-token evaluation to check if forgetting has been learned. If not, increase the training length and repeat. The evaluation cost is not amortized or accounted for in any of the paper's analyses — the scaling laws are fit using this evaluation as a "free" measurement, but in a production setting where training budget is the constrained resource, the verification cost is non-trivial. For the smallest model (36.4M parameters), the 128M-token evaluation costs roughly the same number of FLOPs as training on hundreds of millions of tokens, potentially rivaling the training budget itself. For larger models, the evaluation cost scales with model size, though it remains a constant factor relative to the training runs at long context lengths (which also process many tokens).

Additionally, the 2× loss multiplier and the 1M-token length are arbitrary thresholds whose sensitivity is not analyzed. A model could fail the 2× criterion at 1M tokens (e.g., loss rises to 2.1× the training maximum) but still exhibit acceptable length generalization in practice; conversely, a model could pass the 2× criterion at 1M tokens but fail at 2M tokens. The binary threshold collapses a continuous degradation pattern into a yes/no decision, and the specific threshold values determine the position of each data point in Figures 9 and 11. Shifting the criterion (e.g., 1.5× multiplier, 500K evaluation length) could change the fitted coefficients.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports the $T_{\text{forget}}$ threshold determination in Sections 4.2 and 5.1, with the 128-prompt, 1M-token, 2× criterion specified. Figure 10 shows the underlying loss-vs-position curves from which the binary decision is derived — for some models (e.g., 130M trained at 8K, Figure 10a), the loss rises above the training maximum but stays below 2×, so the criterion classifies it as "not yet learned" even though the degradation is mild. The sensitivity of the final scaling law coefficients to these criterion parameters is not analyzed.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation, propose cheaper alternatives for verifying the forgetting threshold, or provide a sensitivity analysis of the 2×/1M-token criterion. The scaling law itself (linear fit) is presented with the criterion treated as an objective measurement, but the criterion's arbitrariness adds unquantified uncertainty to the fitted parameters. Future work on cheaper difficulty estimation — perhaps using the $\alpha_t$ retention auditing from Section 3.2.1 as a proxy — is not discussed in the context of the $T_{\text{forget}}$ determination.

### All Quantitative Scaling Laws Are Established Exclusively for Mamba-2 on a Single Training Corpus

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's core quantitative results — the $T_{\text{forget}}$ and $T_{\text{recall}}$ scaling laws — are derived from experiments using only the Mamba-2 architecture trained on the RedPajama-V2 corpus with a specific data processing pipeline (4K minimum document length, document concatenation with EOS tokens, 12-sequence TBPTT, WSD learning rate schedule, 0.5M token batch size). The paper explicitly acknowledges this scope:

> "Most experiments in this study focus on Mamba-2... The other model configurations (36M, 47M, and 85M) are trained from scratch." (Sections 2 and 5)

While the paper evaluates other architectures (Mamba-1, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, HGRN-2) on passkey retrieval and the "newlines" prompt in the appendices, these evaluations are purely behavioral — they show that length generalization failure occurs in these architectures as well — but do not include the controlled training-length sweeps needed to establish $T_{\text{forget}}$ and $T_{\text{recall}}$ for non-Mamba-2 architectures. The paper hypothesizes that RWKV's better length generalization (Figures 13, 14) is due to its smaller state size relative to model parameters (Figure 12), but this remains an interpretation without direct experimental support.

**The consequence.** A practitioner using a different recurrent architecture — GLA, RetNet, RWKV-7, or even Mamba-1 — cannot directly apply the $T_{\text{forget}} = 5.172 \cdot N_S - 4.469$ formula. The coefficient 5.172 emerges from the specific interaction of Mamba-2's architecture (state dimension $N = 128$, head dimension $P = 64$, the specific form of the $\Delta_t$ and $B_t$ parameterizations, the short convolution layers) with the RedPajama-V2 data distribution. Different architectures may store information at different rates per state parameter, or have different optimization dynamics that change the relationship between training length and forgetting behavior. The paper's diagnostic framework (retention auditing, interventional testing, training dynamics tracking) is architecture-agnostic and transferable, but the specific numerical scaling laws are not.

Similarly, training on a different corpus — particularly one with different document length distributions, different topic structures, or different amounts of long-range dependency — could shift the forgetting threshold. The paper's filtering step (removing 97.6% of RedPajama-V2 by discarding documents <4K tokens) creates a specific long-form text distribution; a model trained on a corpus with mostly short documents concatenated together would encounter different patterns of topic transitions and co-reference, potentially affecting the information-per-token ratio and thus the $T_{\text{forget}}$ coefficient.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 2.1 justifies the focus on Mamba-2. Appendix D provides behavioral passkey retrieval results for RWKV-5, RWKV-6, and Mamba-1, showing that length generalization failure occurs in these architectures. Appendix H shows that HGRN-2 and RWKV-6 exhibit perplexity degradation on the "newlines" prompt. Figure 12 compares state sizes across architectures. However, no training-length sweep experiments are reported for any architecture other than Mamba-2. The paper notes this explicitly:

> "Notably, Mamba-2's update rule is similar to many existing RNNs... Thus, some conclusions/insights may apply to other architectures. We leave such exhaustive ablation studies for future work." (Section 2.1)

**Mitigation status.** Partially acknowledged. The paper explicitly scopes its quantitative claims to Mamba-2 and frames the diagnostic approach as the transferable contribution. But the headline scaling laws, which are the paper's most actionable finding, are presented without cross-architecture validation. A practitioner using a non-Mamba-2 recurrent model receives qualitative guidance ("train longer than you think") but no quantitative prescription.

### Data Filtering Removes 97.6% of the Training Corpus, Creating a Potentially Unrepresentative Long-Form-Only Distribution

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's data processing pipeline for all training runs includes filtering out documents shorter than 4K tokens from RedPajama-V2. The paper states:

> "To ensure that the data contains as much long-term structure as possible, we filter out sequences with less than 4K tokens... the above threshold already removes about 97.6% of the data in the original corpus." (Appendix F)

This means the models are trained on a highly skewed subset of the original web corpus — the 2.4% of documents long enough to survive filtering. These are disproportionately formal writing (books, academic papers, long articles) rather than the short-form and conversational content (social media, comments, short web pages) that dominate the unfiltered corpus. The paper does not characterize the distributional shift introduced by this filtering or discuss whether it systematically alters the information density, topic structure, or long-range dependency patterns relative to typical language modeling corpora.

**The consequence.** The forgetting behavior learned by models trained on this filtered corpus may not generalize to settings where practitioners train on more diverse or shorter-document-heavy data. If long-form documents have more coherent topic structures and more predictable long-range dependencies (e.g., a book chapter maintains a consistent topic across thousands of tokens, making it easier to predict what information will remain relevant), a model trained on such data might learn different $\alpha_t$ dynamics than one trained on a mixture of short documents concatenated together, where topic shifts are abrupt and unpredictable. The $T_{\text{forget}}$ coefficient (5.172) could be lower for long-form data (less information per token, so the state can store more tokens before overflowing) than for unfiltered web data with higher per-token information content.

The paper cites Buckman & Gelada (2024) as showing that filtering is "critical for training effective long-context models," which suggests the filtering is a necessary practice rather than an arbitrary choice. But this does not change the fact that the scaling law coefficients are specific to the filtered distribution, and the paper does not quantify how much they would shift under different filtering thresholds (e.g., 2K, 8K, or no filtering).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 97.6% removal figure is reported in Appendix F, along with the justification that this filtering "ensures that the data contains as much long-term structure as possible." No ablation over filtering thresholds is performed. No comparison of model behavior when trained on unfiltered data is reported. The paper does not discuss the distributional characteristics of the surviving 2.4% of documents.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the filtering as a methodological necessity for studying long-context behavior but does not analyze its impact on the forgetting threshold or discuss the generalizability of the scaling laws to other data distributions. A practitioner training on unfiltered web data or domain-specific corpora with different length distributions has no guidance on how to adjust the $T_{\text{forget}}$ coefficient.

### The Paper Does Not Evaluate Whether Long-Context Retrieval Translates to Downstream Reasoning or Generation Tasks

**The constraint.** The paper's positive result — that a properly trained 370M Mamba-2 model achieves near-perfect passkey retrieval at 256K tokens (Section 5.3, Figure 9) — is demonstrated exclusively on the passkey retrieval task, a minimal synthetic benchmark where the model must extract a single 5-digit number from repetitive filler text. The paper does not evaluate the long-context-trained models on any standard long-context benchmark that requires reasoning over retrieved information, integrating information from multiple positions, or generating coherent long-form outputs. The passkey task tests only *whether information remains accessible*, not whether it can be *used* for complex downstream computation.

The paper acknowledges this implicitly in its analysis of the gap between $T_{\text{forget}}$ and $T_{\text{recall}}$ (Section 4.3):

> "The key difference between this task and the language modeling loss test used for $T_{\text{forget}}$ is that the filler text is highly repetitive and contains negligible new information."

This means the exponential $T_{\text{recall}}$ scaling is specifically a measurement of *distinguishability* under constant information load, not of *usable context length for practical language tasks*.

**The consequence.** A practitioner who reads the paper's headline finding — "370M Mamba-2 achieves near-perfect retrieval at 256K tokens, outperforming similarly sized transformers" — might conclude that Mamba-2 trained at $T_{\text{forget}}$ is a viable long-context model for tasks like document summarization, multi-hop question answering, or long-range code completion. The paper provides no evidence for or against this. The passkey result establishes that the state can *retain* a signal amid noise, but language tasks require more: tracking entities across long spans, resolving co-references, integrating facts from multiple paragraphs, and maintaining generation coherence. The retrieval error analysis in Equation 7 shows why passkey retrieval fails when interference dominates, but reversing that failure (by training longer) may not be sufficient to achieve high-quality reasoning — the model may need to learn not just *that* forgetting is necessary, but *what* to forget and *what* to retain in a content-dependent way, which the passkey task does not test.

Additionally, the paper's language modeling loss curves (Figures 1, 4, 10) show that training at $T_{\text{forget}}$ (e.g., 64K for the 370M model) keeps the loss bounded at very long contexts, but "bounded" does not mean "unchanged" — the loss in Figure 10b at 128K tokens for the 64K-trained 370M model is approximately 2.85, compared to ~2.7 within the training length. This ~5.5% increase in perplexity may or may not translate to noticeable downstream performance degradation depending on the task.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The passkey retrieval evaluation methodology is described in Appendix B and B.1, and the $T_{\text{recall}}$ results are presented in Section 5.3 and Figure 9. Language modeling loss curves provide a general-purpose quality signal but do not isolate specific capabilities (factual recall, logical consistency, instruction following) that matter for downstream use. No evaluation is provided on standard long-context benchmarks (e.g., LongBench, $\infty$Bench, SCROLLS, Zero-SCROLLS, needle-in-a-haystack with natural text and multi-hop reasoning requirements). The paper mentions Zhang et al. (2024a)'s $\infty$Bench only as the source of the passkey prompt template, not as an evaluation target.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not claim that the passkey retrieval result implies general long-context capability, but it also does not caution readers against over-interpreting the result. The framing in Section 5.3 — "no previous models with less than 1B model parameters have near-perfect accuracy at this length in this task" — is accurate in its task-specificity, but the broader implications are left for the reader to infer. This is a reasonable scope for a diagnostic paper focused on identifying and fixing the forgetting mechanism, but it leaves a significant gap between the demonstrated capability (retrieval) and the practical capability practitioners need (reasoning over long contexts).

### No Analysis of Latency, Memory Overhead, or Practical Deployment Cost of Long-Context Training and Inference

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures everything in terms of context lengths (tokens) and state sizes (parameters) without analyzing wall-clock time, GPU memory consumption, or the practical engineering challenges of training and deploying models with 64K–256K context lengths. The claim that Mamba-2 has "$O(1)$ per-token time and space complexities" (Section 1) is a theoretical asymptotic statement that does not address constant factors or hardware efficiency at the specific context lengths studied.

**The consequence.** Training a 370M Mamba-2 model at 64K context length (as needed for $T_{\text{forget}}$) requires processing 64K tokens per sample, which means each training step processes 64K × batch_size tokens. With the paper's batch size of 0.5M tokens per batch, this works out to approximately 8 sequences per batch — which is tractable but requires more GPU memory per step than training at 8K would (since activations must be stored for the full 64K-length forward pass, though TBPTT truncates gradient flow to 12 sub-sequences). The paper uses A800 80GB GPUs, some with multiple nodes (Appendix F.1), indicating that the long-context training runs were computationally expensive, but no FLOP counts, GPU-hours, or cost estimates are reported.

Additionally, while Mamba-2's per-token *compute* is $O(1)$, its memory footprint for the recurrent state is $2 \cdot d \cdot N \cdot L$ parameters per layer, or approximately 12.9M FP32 values (~52MB) for the 370M model's recurrent state alone — this must reside in GPU memory throughout inference and grows linearly with model size. For the sliding window intervention (Section 3.2.2), the paper's method requires caching an additional copy of the state at $t-w$ and the cumulative decay $\Delta_{t-w+1:t}$, roughly tripling the state-related memory overhead. For batch inference at scale, these constant factors matter and are not discussed.

The sequential nature of recurrence also means that Mamba-2 cannot parallelize across the context dimension during autoregressive generation — each token must be processed sequentially through all 48 layers, whereas transformers can compute all attention heads in parallel across the context during the prefill phase. For applications where prompt processing latency dominates (long documents, codebases), a Mamba-2 model may have *higher* latency than a similarly sized transformer with optimized attention (FlashAttention, RingAttention), even though its FLOP count is lower. The paper does not compare latency or throughput in any experiment.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Appendix A.1 derives the state size formula $N_S = 2dNL$ and notes that "the state of a Mamba-2 model is equal in size to a KV cache of a vanilla Transformer model" for a certain configuration. Appendix F.1 mentions the hardware used ("A800 80G, some are run with multiple nodes, and others with multiple GPUs on a single node") but provides no quantitative resource accounting. The paper notes that training the 780M model beyond 128K context length was computationally infeasible (Section 5.2), acknowledging a practical cost barrier but not quantifying where the barrier lies (GPU memory? throughput? total FLOPs? wall-clock time?).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper is positioned as a diagnostic investigation rather than an engineering contribution, so the omission of deployment-level cost analysis is within scope. However, a practitioner trying to determine whether the $T_{\text{forget}}$-prescribed training lengths are worth the cost has no information on which to base that decision. The paper does not provide a FLOPs-matched comparison between "train a smaller model longer" and "train a larger model shorter" for length generalization, which would be the natural next step for translating the scaling law into a practical resource allocation guideline.
