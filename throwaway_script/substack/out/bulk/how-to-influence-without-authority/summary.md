Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay on influencing without authority (the core skill for senior engineers who must achieve through collaboration rather than hierarchy):

**The central principle: speak to what others want**

The entire practice of influencing without authority rests on a single Dale Carnegie maxim: "The only way on earth to influence other people is to talk about what they want and show them how to get it." It is disarmingly simple on paper, but Ryan argues it carries a lot of weight in practice—especially when you need another team to prioritize a project that has no reporting-line claim on their roadmap. He notes that many companies hide engineering levels and titles, which makes this skill non-negotiable: to influence others, "you'll need to rely on more than just your place in the hierarchy."

**Understanding what others want (teams and individuals)**

The fastest entry point is reading the other team's planning doc. There you'll find the motivation behind their roadmap and the metrics they are trying to move. A side benefit of starting at the team level is that you can influence whole teams through those motives, not just individual relationships. Ryan treats this as sufficient for getting the majority of cross-team projects prioritized. In some cases, though, it is helpful to also understand individual motives, recognizing that "every person is different so it's hard to generalize." He catalogues three common ones: personal interest and learning (some people pick up work because they want to acquire a new skill), career growth (some are eager for opportunity that advances them), and **making life easier**—people who want to save time or eliminate a high-maintenance legacy pipeline. (Ryan notes that the maintenance cost of an old pipeline was itself a motivation for one of his own projects.)

**Showing them how to get it**

Once you know what they want, the job is to explain how your project delivers that thing. Two tactics carry the load. First, **convincing with data**: queries that show the size of the opportunity, tying the project directly to the metrics they care about. Ryan calls this his personal favorite because "data speaks for itself"—if someone comes to him with proof their project will achieve his team's goals, he'll prioritize it even if he just met them. Second, **establishing credibility**: your past results, domain expertise, or existing relationships give the other team a reason to work with you. Credibility makes influence dramatically easier.

**The "win-win" frame (not manipulation)**

Ryan tackles the charge that this sounds manipulative head-on. Focusing on helping people get what they want is mutually beneficial—you are simply explaining the benefits of your suggestion in the terms they already care about. This is also why influence is not always possible: if there is genuinely nothing in your project that the other side wants, "you won't be able to sell it no matter how good you are." He closes with a warning about tact, citing Samuel Butler: "A man convinced against his will, is of the same opinion still." Upset people won't work with you even if you are right. Ryan's recommended next step is practice—try speaking about your projects in terms of what others want and see how they respond. He also suggests reading Carnegie's *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, but emphasizes that "an even better place to learn is through experience."

---

His **convincing with data** tactic and the principle of reading team planning docs are two expressions of the same core move: locate what the other side already wants and situate your project inside that desire. By rooting influence in existing goals rather than persuasion, Ryan's framework turns the Carnegie maxim into a repeatable engineering practice—one where "data speaks for itself" and credibility compounds, making collaboration more about discovery than about selling.
