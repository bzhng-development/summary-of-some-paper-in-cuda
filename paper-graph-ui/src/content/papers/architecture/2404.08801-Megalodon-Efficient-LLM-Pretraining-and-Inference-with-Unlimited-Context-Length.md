## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **MEGALODON**, an improved neural architecture for efficient sequence modeling that extends the MEGA (exponential moving average with gated attention) framework with multiple novel components—complex exponential moving average (CEMA), timestep normalization, normalized attention, and pre-norm with two-hop residual configuration—to enable unlimited context length while matching or exceeding Transformer performance. Through a controlled head-to-head comparison with LLAMA2, MEGALODON-7B achieves superior data efficiency on 2 trillion training tokens, reaching a training loss of 1.70 that lands "mid-way between LLAMA2-7B (1.75) and 13B (1.67)," while demonstrating 32% faster training at 32K context length. The architecture's chunk-wise attention, combined with the proposed 4-dimensional parallelism strategy, establishes that sub-quadratic sequence models can outperform Transformers on both pretraining efficiency and downstream benchmarks across diverse modalities—ImageNet, speech classification, and long-context QA—without sacrificing short-context accuracy, but only when instability issues in scaling deep architectures are addressed through the proposed normalization and residual innovations.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Transformer Inefficiency on Long Sequences

The fundamental challenge this paper addresses is straightforward to state but extremely difficult to solve: **Transformers do not scale well to long sequences**. The standard self-attention mechanism computes pairwise interactions between every token in a sequence, yielding $O(n^2)$ computational complexity and $O(n^2)$ memory cost for a sequence of length $n$. This quadratic scaling imposes a harsh economic ceiling. As the paper observes in the introduction, even with recently proposed distributed attention solutions that parallelize the computation across multiple GPUs, "computing a single training step of a 7B parameter model over a 1M-token sequence is more than 100 times slower than performing the equivalent computation using 256 separate sequences of 4K tokens each." This is not a minor inefficiency — it means that training on a single long document can be two orders of magnitude more expensive than training on the same number of tokens distributed across many short documents.

This problem is not merely academic. Real-world applications increasingly demand models that can ingest and reason over extremely long contexts: multi-turn conversational agents that remember the entire dialogue history, document comprehension systems that process entire books or legal contracts, video generation models that must maintain coherence across thousands of frames, and code assistants that need to understand entire codebases. In each case, the quadratic complexity of attention means that handling the full context is either computationally prohibitive or requires awkward workarounds — truncation, sliding windows, or retrieval-augmented generation — that sacrifice information fidelity.

Beyond computational cost, there is a second, subtler problem: **Transformers have limited inductive bias for length generalization**. Even when trained on sequences of a fixed length (e.g., 4K tokens), Transformers struggle to extrapolate to longer sequences at inference time. The positional encoding schemes that work well within the training distribution — whether learned absolute positions, sinusoidal encodings, or rotary position embeddings (RoPE) — do not necessarily provide a meaningful signal for positions far beyond those seen during training. This means that simply training a Transformer on longer sequences is not a complete solution; the architecture itself lacks the structural priors that would enable robust generalization to arbitrary lengths.

### Why This Problem Matters Now

The timing of this work is driven by a convergence of practical and theoretical pressures:

**The scaling imperative.** The dominant paradigm in language modeling — training ever-larger Transformers on ever-larger datasets — is reaching a point where sequence length, not just model size, is the critical bottleneck. Models like LLAMA2-7B are trained on 2 trillion tokens at a context length of 4K; extending to 32K, 128K, or 1M tokens with full attention would multiply the computational cost by factors of 8, 32, or 250, respectively. Without architectural innovation, long-context pretraining at scale is economically infeasible.

**The deployment reality.** Even if long-context Transformers could be trained, inference latency would remain a problem. Autoregressive generation with full attention over a million-token context requires recomputing attention over the entire history for every new token generated, making interactive applications impractical. An architecture that achieves linear or near-linear complexity in both training and inference — without sacrificing accuracy — would fundamentally change what is possible in production systems.

**The modality unification argument.** The paper positions long-sequence modeling as the common substrate for multiple modalities: text, speech, images, and video are all, at their core, sequences of tokens. An architecture that efficiently handles sequences of arbitrary length could serve as a unified backbone across modalities, rather than requiring modality-specific architectures (e.g., CNNs for images, specialized speech encoders for audio). This vision — hinted at by the paper's experiments spanning ImageNet, Speech Commands, and language modeling — requires a sequence model that is both efficient and expressive enough to compete with domain-specific architectures.

### Prior Approaches: The Landscape of Sub-Quadratic Sequence Models

The paper enters a crowded field. Researchers have pursued three broad strategies for overcoming Transformer inefficiency on long sequences:

#### Efficient Attention Mechanisms

The first strategy is to approximate or restructure the attention computation itself while retaining the Transformer's fundamental operations. This category includes:

- **Sparse attention patterns** (e.g., BigBird, Longformer): compute attention only for a subset of token pairs — local windows, global tokens, or random patterns — reducing complexity to $O(n \cdot k)$ where $k$ is the number of attended positions per token. The limitation is that sparse patterns are hand-designed heuristics that may miss important long-range dependencies.

- **Low-rank approximations** (e.g., Linformer): project the $n \times n$ attention matrix through a low-rank bottleneck, achieving $O(n)$ complexity. The risk is that the low-rank assumption may not hold for all types of content.

- **Kernel-based methods** (e.g., Performer, Linear Transformers): rewrite attention as a kernel function that can be computed via associative operations, avoiding explicit $n \times n$ matrix construction. These methods trade exactness for speed, and their empirical performance on hard reasoning tasks has been mixed.

- **Chunk-wise and recurrence-based approaches** (e.g., Transformer-XL, Block-Recurrent Transformer): split the sequence into fixed-size chunks, apply full attention within each chunk, and pass a compressed state between chunks. This achieves $O(n \cdot c)$ complexity where $c$ is the chunk size, but the compressed inter-chunk state is lossy — information that does not fit in the state vector is permanently discarded.

The paper acknowledges the broad survey of these techniques (Tay et al., 2020) but positions them as falling short of the original Transformer on real-world tasks (Tay et al., 2022; Gu and Dao, 2023), noting: "the practical application of these methods still falls short of Transformers."

#### Structured State Space Models (SSMs)

The second strategy, which emerged more recently, is to entirely replace attention with a structured state space model — essentially, a continuous-time linear dynamical system discretized for sequence processing. The landmark model in this lineage is **S4** (Gu et al., 2022a), which showed that with careful parameterization (using HiPPO matrices to compress long-range dependencies), SSMs could match or exceed Transformers on the Long Range Arena (LRA) benchmark while maintaining $O(n \log n)$ or $O(n)$ complexity. Subsequent refinements — H3, Hyena, and most recently **Mamba** (Gu and Dao, 2023) — added gating mechanisms, data-dependent state transitions, and more sophisticated parameterizations, progressively closing the gap with Transformers on language modeling.

The key advantages of SSMs are:
- **Linear or near-linear complexity** by design, since the state transition can be computed via parallel scans or convolutions.
- **Inherent sequential inductive bias**: because SSMs process tokens in order with a recurrent state, they naturally capture local dependencies without needing explicit positional encodings.
- **Unlimited context at inference**: the recurrent formulation means that generating token $n+1$ requires only the state from step $n$, not recomputation over the entire history.

Despite these advantages, the paper observes that SSMs still "empirically underperform Transformers in pretraining efficiency and downstream task accuracy." The abstract singles out this gap: "while sub-quadratic solutions like linear attention and state space models exist, they empirically underperform Transformers in pretraining efficiency and downstream task accuracy." Mamba-3B, for instance, achieves 26.2 on MMLU compared to LLAMA2-7B's 45.3, a substantial gap even accounting for model size differences.

#### The MEGA Architecture: Gated Attention with Exponential Moving Average

The third strategy — and the direct predecessor of MEGALODON — is the **MEGA** architecture (Ma et al., 2023). MEGA takes a hybrid approach: it retains attention but augments it with an **exponential moving average (EMA)** component that processes each dimension of the input independently through a damped EMA, then uses the EMA output to compute a *shared representation* from which queries and keys are derived, while values come from the original input. The attention is then applied in chunks (MEGA-chunk), achieving $O(n \cdot c)$ complexity, with the EMA sub-layer theoretically compensating for the information lost at chunk boundaries by capturing local context.

MEGA introduced several important innovations that MEGALODON inherits:
- **Multi-dimensional damped EMA**: each input dimension is expanded into $h$ hidden dimensions via an expansion matrix $\beta$, and each hidden dimension is processed with its own learnable decaying factor $\alpha$ and damping factor $\delta$.
- **Gated attention with reset and update gates**: inspired by GRUs, MEGA uses a reset gate $\gamma$ to control how much attention output is incorporated, and an update gate $\phi$ for a gated residual connection that mitigates instability.
- **Per-dimension query/key scalars and offsets**: rather than using the raw shared representation for queries and keys, MEGA learns dimension-wise scaling ($\kappa_q$, $\kappa_k$) and shifting ($\mu_q$, $\mu_k$) parameters, giving the model fine-grained control over the attention distribution.

The problem — and this is where MEGALODON's motivation crystallizes — is that **MEGA has three critical shortcomings that prevent it from scaling to large-scale pretraining**:

**Shortcoming 1: Performance gap between chunk-wise and full attention.** As the paper states, "the performance of MEGA with chunk-wise attention still fails behind the one with full attention, due to the limited expressiveness of the EMA sub-layer in MEGA." The EMA, with its real-valued decaying factors, can only capture a limited class of temporal dependencies. When attention is restricted to chunks of length $c$, information that should flow across chunk boundaries is either lost or imperfectly propagated through the EMA state. This limited expressiveness means that MEGA-chunk underperforms MEGA-full, and at large scale, using full attention would defeat the purpose of efficiency.

**Shortcoming 2: Architectural divergence across tasks and modalities.** The paper notes that "for different tasks and/or data types, there are architectural divergences in the final MEGA architectures. For example, different normalization layers, normalization patterns (pre-norm vs. post-norm) and attention functions are applied to different data types." This is a significant practical limitation: a sequence model that requires task-specific architectural tuning is not a general-purpose backbone. The vision of a unified architecture for all modalities — which MEGALODON explicitly pursues — requires components that work robustly across diverse data types without manual adaptation.

**Shortcoming 3: No evidence of scalability to large-scale pretraining.** The paper states bluntly: "There are no empirical evidences showing that MEGA is scalable for large-scale pretraining." MEGA was validated on benchmarks like LRA (max 16K tokens), WikiText-103, and ImageNet-1K — all small to medium scale. Whether MEGA's components would remain stable and effective when scaled to 7 billion parameters and 2 trillion training tokens was completely unknown. The normalization and residual connection schemes that worked at small scale might collapse under the increased gradient magnitudes and deeper networks of large-scale pretraining.

### How MEGALODON Positions Itself

MEGALODON is not a fundamentally new architecture but rather a **systematic stabilization and extension of MEGA** specifically designed to address these three shortcomings at scale. Each of the four novel technical components targets a specific failure mode:

1. **Complex Exponential Moving Average (CEMA)** addresses Shortcoming 1 (limited EMA expressiveness) by extending the multi-dimensional damped EMA from the real domain to the complex domain. The key insight is that complex numbers can represent rotating or oscillatory dynamics that real-valued EMAs cannot — a pattern directly inspired by the diagonal state space models in S4 (Gu et al., 2022b), where "almost all matrices diagonalize over the complex plane." By parameterizing the CEMA with a learned base angle $\omega$ that uniformly spaces $h$ arguments over $2\pi$, the model gains the ability to capture a much richer class of temporal dependencies — sinusoidal patterns, phase relationships, and long-range oscillations — using the same number of parameters. The paper explicitly connects this to the success of convolutional models on long sequences: CEMA "preserves the decaying structure in kernel weights, which is a key principle to the success of convolutional models on long sequence modeling."

2. **Timestep Normalization** addresses Shortcoming 2 (architectural divergence) by providing a single normalization approach that works across modalities. The paper observes that Layer Normalization — the standard in Transformers — operates only along the feature dimension, ignoring the sequential dimension entirely. Group Normalization normalizes along both timestep and feature dimensions but cannot be applied to autoregressive models because computing the mean and variance across future timesteps would leak information. Timestep Normalization solves this by computing **cumulative** mean and variance: at each timestep $t$, the statistics are computed over all tokens up to and including $t$, preserving causality. This provides the benefits of spatial normalization (reducing internal covariate shift along the timestep dimension) without breaking autoregressive constraints. For non-autoregressive tasks like image classification, it can be replaced with standard Group Normalization.

3. **Normalized Attention** directly addresses instability issues in the original MEGA attention. Prior work had identified saturation and instability problems in scaled dot-product attention, and various normalized attention mechanisms (cosine attention, QK-normalization) had been proposed as remedies. MEGALODON adopts this insight: it normalizes the shared representation $Z$ to unit length ($Z' = Z / \|Z\|$) before computing queries and keys, and removes the SiLU activation function from the shared representation computation (since "the normalization on Z has incorporated non-linearity into $Z'$"). This has two effects: it prevents the attention logits from growing unboundedly in deep networks, and it eliminates the need for the temperature scaling term $\tau(X)$ that MEGA required. The paper reports that with normalized attention, standard softmax is consistently the best or near-best attention function across tasks — resolving another divergence point in MEGA.

4. **Pre-Norm with Two-Hop Residual** tackles the instability of scaling pre-normalization to 7B parameters. The standard pre-norm configuration (Figure 3b) has the output $Y$ accumulate contributions from each sublayer: $Y = \text{FFN}(\text{Norm}(\hat{Y})) + \text{Attention}(\text{Norm}(X)) + X$. As depth increases, the range and variance of $Y$ drift upward, causing training instability. MEGA attempted to mitigate this with the update gate $\phi$, a learned gating mechanism on the residual connection, but the paper explicitly states that "the update gate $\phi$ introduces more model parameters **and the instability issue still exists when scaling up model size to 7 billion**." The two-hop residual configuration (Figure 3c) solves this by reusing the original input $X$ as the residual connection for the FFN layer: $Y = \text{FFN}(\text{Norm}(\hat{Y})) + X$, while the attention sublayer uses its own residual: $\hat{Y} = \text{Attention}(\text{Norm}(X)) + X$. This means that after two sublayers, the output is $Y = \text{FFN}(\text{Norm}(\hat{Y})) + X$, which has only two terms rather than three, controlling variance accumulation. The update gate $\phi$ can then be removed entirely, simplifying the architecture.

Crucially, MEGALODON positions itself not just as a long-context specialist but as a **general-purpose architecture that matches or exceeds Transformers even on short sequences**. The paper's abstract frames this explicitly: "MEGALODON achieves better efficiency than Transformer in the scale of 7 billion parameters and 2 trillion training tokens," and the results in Table 1 show MEGALODON-7B surpassing LLAMA2-7B on every benchmark — including short-context tasks like MMLU, BoolQ, and PIQA — with an average improvement of roughly 4–7 percentage points. This is significant because many prior sub-quadratic models traded short-context accuracy for long-context efficiency; MEGALODON claims to break this tradeoff.

### The Unstated Motivation: A Practical Path to Unlimited Context

Reading between the lines, there is an additional motivation that the paper does not explicitly state but that is evident from the experimental design: **the Transformer's quadratic complexity is not the only barrier to unlimited context — distributed training infrastructure is equally limiting**. Section 3.5 introduces a fourth parallelism dimension ("chunk parallelism" along the sequence dimension) that is orthogonal to data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism. The paper argues that the three standard parallelism dimensions are "still insufficient to scale up the context length of LLMs," citing recent work on sequence parallelism (Li et al., 2023b; Liu et al., 2024). MEGALODON's chunk-wise attention makes chunk parallelism natural: each device in a chunk-parallel group processes a different chunk of the sequence, with minimal cross-device communication — only the last hidden state of CEMA and the cumulative mean and variance of Timestep Normalization need to be passed between chunks. This communication can be hidden behind computation using asynchronous transfers.

This distributed training motivation is a practical counterpart to the theoretical complexity argument. Even if a Transformer could be trained on 1M-token sequences with linear attention, the memory required to store the full $n \times n$ attention matrix during training is prohibitive. MEGALODON's chunk-wise design decomposes the problem both computationally (linear complexity) and in terms of distributed memory (each device only needs its chunk), making truly long-context pretraining feasible on existing hardware clusters.

### Summary of the Gap

In summary, the paper identifies a specific, well-defined gap in the literature:

- Transformers are accurate but quadratically expensive on long sequences and lack inductive bias for length extrapolation.
- Sub-quadratic alternatives (efficient attention, SSMs) are efficient but empirically underperform Transformers on both pretraining perplexity and downstream benchmarks.
- The MEGA architecture bridged these worlds with chunk-wise attention + EMA, but suffered from limited EMA expressiveness, architectural divergence across tasks, and no demonstrated scalability to large-scale pretraining.
- No existing architecture had demonstrated that a sub-quadratic model could match or exceed a well-tuned Transformer (LLAMA2) in a controlled, compute-matched, large-scale pretraining comparison.

MEGALODON aims to fill this gap by stabilizing and extending MEGA with four targeted innovations — complex-domain EMA, cumulative timestep normalization, normalized attention, and two-hop residuals — and then validating the result at 7B scale with 2T tokens against LLAMA2, establishing the first credible evidence that sub-quadratic architectures can serve as drop-in replacements for Transformers without sacrificing accuracy at any sequence length.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation (Approachable Technical Breakdown)

MEGALODON is a neural network architecture designed to process sequences of tokens (words, image patches, audio samples, etc.) for tasks like language modeling, image classification, and speech recognition. The core problem it solves is the quadratic cost of Transformer attention on long sequences: while Transformers become increasingly expensive as sequences get longer, MEGALODON achieves linear complexity in both training and inference by combining a mathematically richer form of exponential moving average (EMA) with chunked attention, stabilized by novel normalization and residual connection schemes that enable it to scale reliably to 7 billion parameters and match or exceed the performance of well-tuned Transformers like LLAMA2.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The MEGALODON architecture can be understood as a pipeline of four interconnected components within each layer, with a fifth component governing how computation is distributed across GPUs:

1. **Complex Exponential Moving Average (CEMA):** The entry point for the input sequence. Each token's representation is expanded into a hidden space, processed through a damped complex-valued exponential moving average that captures temporal patterns (oscillations, decays, phase relationships), and projected back to the original dimensionality. Think of this as a learned "short-term memory" that summarizes the sequence history up to each position.

2. **Timestep Normalization:** Applied to the CEMA output before attention. Unlike standard Layer Normalization that normalizes across features only, Timestep Normalization computes cumulative means and variances over all tokens seen so far (up to and including the current position), normalizing along both the time and feature dimensions while respecting causality. This reduces internal covariate shift — the phenomenon where the distribution of activations drifts during training — along the sequential axis.

3. **Normalized Attention:** Queries and keys are computed from a shared representation derived from the normalized CEMA output, with the representation first normalized to unit length. The attention weights are computed via standard softmax dot-product attention, but applied only within fixed-size chunks (e.g., 4096 tokens) to achieve linear complexity. Values are computed from the original (pre-CEMA) input.

4. **Two-Hop Residual and Feed-Forward Network:** The attention output is added to the original layer input (first residual hop), then passed through a standard feed-forward network (SwiGLU activation), whose output is added to the *same* original layer input (second residual hop) rather than to the attention output. This dual-residual configuration prevents variance accumulation in deep networks and eliminates the need for learned gating on the residual connections.

5. **Chunk Parallelism (distributed training component):** The input sequence is split across multiple GPUs along the sequence dimension, with each GPU processing a contiguous chunk. Only two pieces of information cross chunk boundaries: the final CEMA hidden state and the cumulative normalization statistics. These are communicated asynchronously and hidden behind computation in other sublayers.

Information flows sequentially through a stack of 32 such layers (in the 7B configuration). The key design principle is that CEMA provides local, sequential context within each chunk, while attention handles long-range interactions within the current chunk. By making CEMA expressive enough (via complex numbers) and stabilizing the deep stack (via timestep normalization and two-hop residuals), the architecture achieves the accuracy of full attention with the efficiency of chunked processing.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

This section explains MEGALODON through six sequential deep dives, ordered to build understanding from the foundational sequence-processing mechanism outward:

- **First, CEMA (Complex EMA):** because it is the innermost temporal processing engine — the component that replaces the real-valued EMA in MEGA and provides the richer temporal inductive bias that makes chunked attention viable. We must understand why complex numbers matter and how they are parameterized.

- **Second, Timestep Normalization:** because it normalizes the CEMA output before attention and addresses the autoregressive constraint that makes standard group normalization inapplicable. The cumulative mean/variance computation and its efficient GPU implementation are central to stability.

- **Third, Normalized Attention:** because it builds on the normalized CEMA output to compute queries, keys, and values, and applies chunked softmax attention. This section explains how MEGALODON achieves linear complexity while retaining the expressiveness of attention within chunks, and why the shared representation is normalized before query/key computation.

- **Fourth, Pre-Norm with Two-Hop Residual:** because it governs how attention and FFN outputs are combined with the input, and is the final piece of the stability puzzle that enables scaling to 7B parameters. The comparison with standard pre-norm and MEGA's gated residual makes the motivation concrete.

- **Fifth, the full MEGALODON layer (integration):** because it shows how CEMA, timestep norm, normalized attention, and two-hop residual connect in a single layer, and clarifies which normalization is applied where and why.

- **Sixth, 4-Dimensional Parallelism (distributed training):** because it is the practical enabler for long-context pretraining at scale, building on the chunk-wise attention design to add sequence-level parallelism orthogonal to data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism.

This is primarily an **architecture design and empirical validation paper** whose core idea is that a carefully stabilized chunk-wise attention architecture, augmented with a complex-valued exponential moving average component, can match or exceed Transformers on both efficiency (linear complexity) and accuracy (pretraining loss, downstream benchmarks) at scale, provided that four specific instability sources are addressed: limited EMA expressiveness, lack of sequential normalization, attention saturation, and residual variance accumulation in deep networks.

#### Complex Exponential Moving Average (CEMA)

The CEMA component is MEGALODON's replacement for the real-valued multi-dimensional damped EMA in the original MEGA architecture. To understand why CEMA is necessary, we must first understand what the original EMA does and where it falls short.

In MEGA's EMA (Equation 1 in the paper), each scalar dimension $x_{t,j}$ of the input at timestep $t$ is first expanded into an $h$-dimensional vector via a learned expansion vector $\beta_j \in \mathbb{R}^h$. This expanded vector $u^{(j)}_t = \beta_j x_{t,j}$ is then processed through a damped exponential moving average:

$$h^{(j)}_t = \alpha_j \odot u^{(j)}_t + (1 - \alpha_j \odot \delta_j) \odot h^{(j)}_{t-1}$$

where $h^{(j)}_t \in \mathbb{R}^h$ is the hidden state for dimension $j$ at time $t$, $\alpha_j \in (0, 1)^h$ is a vector of decaying factors (one per hidden dimension), $\delta_j \in (0, 1)^h$ is a vector of damping factors, and $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. The output for dimension $j$ is then $y_{t,j} = \eta_j^T h^{(j)}_t$, where $\eta_j \in \mathbb{R}^h$ projects the $h$-dimensional hidden state back to a scalar.

**What this computes operationally:** For each of the $d$ input dimensions, the EMA maintains an $h$-dimensional running average of past expanded inputs, where the contribution of each past input decays exponentially with a rate controlled by $\alpha_j$. The damping factor $\delta_j$ further reduces the effective decay rate, allowing the model to learn whether each hidden dimension should be "fast" (quickly forgetting old information) or "slow" (retaining information over long spans). The output is a learned linear combination of these averaged signals.

**The limitation:** Because $\alpha_j$ and $\delta_j$ are real numbers in $(0, 1)$, the EMA can only represent **monotonically decaying** temporal dependencies. Each hidden dimension computes a weighted average where the weights on past inputs strictly decrease as we go further back in time. Real-world sequences — especially those with periodic structure like audio, or those requiring the model to track phase relationships — need the ability to represent oscillatory patterns where the influence of a past input might oscillate between positive and negative, or where certain past positions have higher weight than more recent ones. The real-valued EMA simply cannot express "pay attention to what happened exactly 10 steps ago" or "track a sine wave of frequency $\omega$."

CEMA addresses this by extending the EMA computation into the complex domain. The key equation (Equation 2) is:

$$h^{(j)}_t = \alpha_j(\cos \theta_j + i \sin \theta_j) \odot u^{(j)}_t + (1 - \alpha_j \odot \delta_j)(\cos \theta_j + i \sin \theta_j) \odot h^{(j)}_{t-1}$$

$$y_{t,j} = \text{Re}(\eta_j^T h^{(j)}_t)$$

where $i = \sqrt{-1}$ is the imaginary unit, and the new elements are:
- $\theta_j \in \mathbb{R}^h$: a vector of $h$ phase angles (one per hidden dimension), controlling how each hidden dimension rotates in the complex plane at each timestep.
- $\eta_j \in \mathbb{C}^{h}$: a complex-valued projection vector (replacing the real-valued $\eta_j$ in EMA).
- $\text{Re}(\cdot)$: the real part of the complex number, extracting a real-valued output from the complex hidden state.

The phase angles $\theta_j$ are parameterized to uniformly cover the full $2\pi$ range:

$$\theta_{j,k} = \frac{2\pi k}{h} \omega_j, \quad \forall k \in \{1, 2, \ldots, h\}$$

where $\omega_j \in \mathbb{R}$ is a learnable base angle for dimension $j$. This parameterization means that for a given dimension $j$, the $h$ hidden dimensions are assigned equally spaced angles between $0$ and $2\pi \omega_j$, with spacing $2\pi \omega_j / h$. If $\omega_j = 1$, the $h$ angles uniformly cover $[0, 2\pi]$; if $\omega_j > 1$, they cover a wider range with potential aliasing; if $\omega_j < 1$, they cover a narrower arc.

**What this computes operationally:** At each timestep, the new input $u^{(j)}_t$ is multiplied by a complex rotation $e^{i\theta_j} = \cos \theta_j + i \sin \theta_j$ and added to a decayed, rotated version of the previous hidden state. In the complex plane, multiplication by $e^{i\theta_j}$ rotates the vector by angle $\theta_j$ without changing its magnitude. This means that each hidden dimension's state evolves according to:
- A decay factor $\alpha_j$ (and damping $\delta_j$) that reduces the magnitude over time, preserving the "recent inputs matter more" inductive bias.
- A rotation factor $\theta_{j,k}$ that causes the influence of past inputs to oscillate at a frequency determined by the phase angle, allowing the model to learn periodic attention patterns.

**Why this form — the connection to state space models:** The paper explicitly cites Gu et al. (2022b) for the motivation: "as almost all matrices diagonalize over the complex plane." In structured state space models like S4, the continuous-time dynamics $\dot{h} = Ah + Bu$ are discretized, and the state matrix $A$ is typically diagonalizable with complex eigenvalues. Each complex eigenvalue corresponds to a damped oscillation: the real part controls the decay rate, and the imaginary part controls the oscillation frequency. CEMA can be seen as a discrete-time diagonal state space model where:
- The state transition for hidden dimension $k$ of input dimension $j$ is the complex scalar $\alpha_{j,k}(1 - \delta_{j,k}) \cdot e^{i\theta_{j,k}}$, whose magnitude is $\alpha_{j,k}(1 - \delta_{j,k})$ (always $< 1$ due to $\alpha, \delta \in (0, 1)$) and whose phase advances by $\theta_{j,k}$ at each step.
- The input projection is $\alpha_{j,k} \cdot e^{i\theta_{j,k}}$, and the output projection is the real part of $\eta_{j,k}$.

The uniform spacing of $\theta_{j,k}$ over multiples of $2\pi/h$ is directly inherited from the HiPPO parameterization used in S4, which provides a theoretically optimal basis for compressing long-range dependencies.

**Why the real part for the output:** The hidden state $h^{(j)}_t$ is complex-valued, but the downstream attention mechanism operates on real-valued vectors. Taking the real part $\text{Re}(\eta_j^T h^{(j)}_t)$ extracts a real scalar that encodes both the magnitude and phase information of the hidden state, since $\text{Re}(z) = |z| \cos(\arg(z))$ for any complex number $z$. The imaginary part is discarded, which is a design choice — it means the model must learn to encode useful information in the real component of the projected state.

**The key property preserved:** The paper emphasizes that CEMA "preserves the decaying structure in kernel weights, which is a key principle to the success of convolutional models on long sequence modeling." What does this mean? If we unroll the recurrence, the influence of input at time $t - \tau$ on the output at time $t$ is proportional to $\prod_{s=t-\tau+1}^t [\alpha_j (1 - \delta_j) e^{i\theta_j}] = [\alpha_j (1 - \delta_j)]^\tau e^{i\tau\theta_j}$. The magnitude of this influence is $[\alpha_j (1 - \delta_j)]^\tau$, which decays exponentially with $\tau$ since $\alpha_j(1 - \delta_j) < 1$. So CEMA still has a recency bias (older inputs have exponentially smaller magnitude), but the complex rotation $e^{i\tau\theta_j}$ means that the *phase* of that influence oscillates with lag $\tau$. This combines the stability of exponential decay (preventing unbounded temporal dependencies that would cause gradient issues) with the expressiveness of oscillatory kernels (capturing periodic structure). A purely oscillatory kernel without decay would be unstable; a purely decaying kernel without oscillation would miss periodic patterns. CEMA has both.

**Practical dimension:** In MEGALODON-7B, the hidden dimension $h$ is implicitly determined by the expansion from the feature dimension $d = 4096$. The expansion and projection are done via the matrices $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times h}$ and $\eta \in \mathbb{C}^{d \times h}$, and the number of groups for timestep normalization (discussed next) is related to this expansion. The exact value of $h$ is not explicitly stated in the main paper, but the number of attention heads is $h_{\text{attn}} = 4$ (much smaller than LLAMA2's 32), and the EMA operates per-dimension before the attention heads are formed.

#### Timestep Normalization

Timestep Normalization addresses a specific problem: standard Layer Normalization (LayerNorm) operates exclusively along the feature dimension, computing means and variances across all features for each token independently. This means it cannot reduce a type of internal covariate shift that occurs along the sequence dimension — the phenomenon where activations at early positions in a sequence have different statistical properties than activations at later positions, causing the model's layers to receive inputs with shifting distributions as the sequence progresses. This is particularly problematic for autoregressive models, where the model must process tokens one at a time and the cumulative context grows.

Group Normalization (GroupNorm; Wu and He, 2018) addresses this in computer vision by normalizing across both spatial dimensions (height, width) and a subset of channels. Applied to sequences, this would mean normalizing across the timestep dimension and groups of features — computing a single mean and variance over a block of consecutive timesteps and a group of feature dimensions. However, as the paper states, GroupNorm "cannot be directly applied to Transformer on auto-regressive sequence modeling, due to the leakage of future information via the mean and variance across the timestep dimension." If the mean and variance at position $t$ were computed using positions $t+1, t+2, \ldots$, the model would have access to future tokens through the normalization statistics, violating causality.

Timestep Normalization solves this by computing **cumulative** statistics. For an input sequence $X = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, the features are divided into $k$ groups along the feature dimension, with $d_g = d/k$ features per group. For the first group (and identically for all groups), the mean and variance at timestep $t$ are:

$$\mu_t = \frac{1}{t \cdot d_g} \sum_{i=1}^{t} \sum_{j=1}^{d_g} x_{i,j}$$

$$\sigma^2_t = \frac{1}{t \cdot d_g} \sum_{i=1}^{t} \sum_{j=1}^{d_g} (x_{i,j} - \mu_t)^2$$

where the outer sum over $i$ runs from 1 to $t$ (not $n$), meaning only the current and past tokens contribute to the statistics.

**What this computes operationally:** For each group of $d_g$ features, Timestep Normalization computes a running mean and variance over all tokens from position 1 up to the current position $t$. At $t = 1$, the statistics are computed over $d_g$ values (one token, $d_g$ features). At $t = 1000$, they are computed over $1000 \cdot d_g$ values. The normalized output for token $t$ in that group is then $(x_{t, \text{group}} - \mu_t) / \sqrt{\sigma^2_t + \epsilon}$, with learnable scale and shift parameters $\gamma$ and $\beta$ applied as in standard normalization.

**Why this form — the autoregressive constraint:** The key insight is that by computing statistics only over positions $1, \ldots, t$, Timestep Normalization respects the causal structure of autoregressive generation. At training time, the statistics for all positions can be computed in parallel (since the cumulative sums up to each $t$ can be computed via a prefix sum operation). At inference time, the statistics can be updated incrementally using Welford's online algorithm (Welford, 1962), which the paper implements for numerical stability.

**Why normalize along the timestep dimension at all:** The paper's motivation is that "layer normalization cannot directly reduce the internal covariate shift along the spatial dimension." In a deep Transformer or MEGALODON, the hidden representations at early tokens have received information from only a few preceding tokens, while representations at later tokens aggregate information from potentially thousands of preceding tokens through the attention mechanism. These representations can have very different scales and distributions. By normalizing along the timestep dimension, Timestep Normalization encourages the distribution of activations to be more consistent across positions, which stabilizes training and allows gradients to flow more evenly.

**The "Plus 1 Reparameterization" detail (Appendix B.2):** Standard normalization layers learn scale parameter $\gamma$ initialized to 1, so that at initialization the normalized output has variance approximately 1. However, when weight decay regularization is applied, the optimizer pushes $\gamma$ toward 0, which conflicts with the desired scale of 1 and can cause numerical issues. The paper adopts a reparameterization:

$$y = (\gamma + 1) \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} + \beta$$

where $\gamma$ is initialized to 0. Under weight decay, $\gamma$ fluctuates around 0, keeping the effective scale $\gamma + 1$ around 1. This is a small but important detail for training stability, particularly when weight decay is strong (the paper uses weight decay 0.1).

**Efficient implementation (Appendix B.1):** Computing cumulative means and variances naively would be expensive and numerically unstable (subtracting a large running mean from each value can cause catastrophic cancellation). The paper implements three numerical techniques:
1. **Welford's algorithm:** An online algorithm that updates mean and variance estimates incrementally without storing all previous values, avoiding numerical instability from subtracting nearly equal large numbers.
2. **Kahan summation:** A compensated summation technique that reduces floating-point accumulation error when summing large numbers of terms (relevant since the cumulative sum over potentially millions of tokens needs high precision).
3. **Limited parallel threads along the timestep dimension:** The CUDA implementation restricts the number of parallel threads used for the sequence dimension, ensuring that the prefix-sum nature of the computation is handled efficiently rather than being naively parallelized in a way that creates synchronization bottlenecks.

**Where Timestep Normalization is applied:** Only before the attention sublayer (see Figure 3a). Before the FFN sublayer, standard Layer Normalization is used. The paper gives two reasons:
- LayerNorm is computationally faster than TimestepNorm (it only reduces over the feature dimension, not over both time and features).
- "The output vector of each token from the attention layer is a mixture of vectors from contextual tokens via attention weights. Hence, normalizing the attention output along the feature dimension is similar to indirectly normalize along the timestep dimension." In other words, because the attention output already aggregates information across timesteps, LayerNorm on the features implicitly captures some of the cross-timestep normalization effect, though less directly than TimestepNorm.

#### Normalized Attention

The attention mechanism in MEGALODON is a modified version of MEGA's gated attention, with two key changes: (1) the shared representation used for queries and keys is normalized to unit length before the linear projections, and (2) the SiLU activation function on the shared representation is removed. These changes are motivated by the observation that "extensive studies have investigated the instability issue of pre-normalization when scaling up model size," and normalized attention mechanisms (cosine attention, QK-normalization) have proven effective at mitigating attention score saturation in deep Transformers.

The computation proceeds in stages. First, the CEMA output $X' = \text{CEMA}(X) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ provides a contextually-enriched version of the input. From this, a shared representation $Z$ is computed:

$$Z = X' W_z + b_z \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times z}$$

where $W_z \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times z}$ and $b_z \in \mathbb{R}^z$ are learned parameters, and $z$ is the attention dimension. Unlike MEGA, which applied SiLU activation ($Z = \phi_{\text{silu}}(X' W_z + b_z)$), MEGALODON omits the activation because the subsequent normalization step introduces non-linearity.

The shared representation is then normalized to unit length along the feature dimension:

$$Z' = \frac{Z}{\|Z\|} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times z}$$

where $\|Z\|$ is the L2 norm computed per token (i.e., each row of $Z$ is divided by its Euclidean norm). This ensures that every token's representation vector has unit length, preventing the magnitude of $Z$ from growing unboundedly in deep networks.

Queries and keys are then computed from the normalized shared representation using per-dimension learnable scalars and offsets:

$$Q = \kappa_q \odot Z' + \mu_q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times z}$$
$$K = \kappa_k \odot Z' + \mu_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times z}$$

where $\kappa_q, \mu_q, \kappa_k, \mu_k \in \mathbb{R}^z$ are learned vectors. The element-wise multiplication $\kappa_q \odot Z'$ scales each dimension of $Z'$ independently, and the addition of $\mu_q$ shifts it. This gives the model fine-grained control over the query and key representations on a per-dimension basis, which is important because different feature dimensions may need different sensitivity to the attention matching.

Values are computed from the original (pre-CEMA) input $X$, not from the CEMA output:

$$V = \phi_{\text{silu}}(X W_v + b_v) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times v}$$

where $W_v \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times v}$, $b_v \in \mathbb{R}^v$, and $v$ is the value dimension (which may differ from $z$). The use of the original input for values ensures that the attention output can directly access the raw token representations, while queries and keys benefit from CEMA's temporal smoothing. The SiLU activation is retained for the value projection because values need non-linearity for expressive power, and they are not subject to the same normalization as queries and keys.

The attention operation is then standard scaled dot-product attention with softmax, but applied in chunks:

$$O = \text{softmax}\left(Q K^T\right) V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times v}$$

**What the normalization accomplishes:** In standard dot-product attention, the pre-softmax logits $Q K^T$ can grow large in magnitude, particularly in deep networks where the norms of $Q$ and $K$ tend to increase. Large logits push the softmax into a near-one-hot regime, where the attention distribution collapses to attending to a single token. This "attention saturation" reduces the model's ability to aggregate information from multiple tokens and makes gradients vanish (since the softmax gradient is near zero when the output is near one-hot). By normalizing $Z$ to unit length, the norms of $Q$ and $K$ are bounded (since $\kappa_q \odot Z' + \mu_q$ has bounded norm when $Z'$ has unit norm). This keeps the dot products $QK^T$ in a reasonable range, preventing saturation.

**Why remove the temperature scaling $\tau(X)$:** In MEGA, the attention was computed as $f(QK^T / \tau(X)) V$, where $\tau(X)$ was a learned or data-dependent temperature that scaled the logits before softmax to control the sharpness of the attention distribution. With normalized attention, the paper found that this temperature is unnecessary because the learnable scalars $\kappa_q, \kappa_k$ already control the effective temperature (by scaling the query and key vectors before the dot product). Removing $\tau(X)$ simplifies the architecture and removes one potential source of instability.

**Why softmax as the default attention function:** The paper states that "with the normalized attention, the softmax function $f_{\text{softmax}}$ obtains the best or at least comparable performance on different tasks and data modalities." This resolves one of the architectural divergences in MEGA, where different attention functions (softmax, Laplace, linear) were used for different tasks. The normalization makes softmax consistently effective, enabling a unified architecture.

**Chunk-wise application for linear complexity:** The full $n \times n$ attention matrix would still be quadratic. Following MEGA-chunk, MEGALODON splits the sequence into fixed-size chunks of length $c = 4096$ (matching the pretraining context length in LLAMA2). Attention is computed independently within each chunk, meaning each query can only attend to keys within the same chunk. This reduces the complexity from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n \cdot c^2 / c) = O(n \cdot c)$, which is linear in $n$ for fixed $c$. The CEMA component is responsible for propagating information across chunk boundaries through its hidden state, mitigating the loss of cross-chunk interactions.

**Attention gating:** MEGALODON retains the reset gate $\gamma$ from MEGA (Equation 18), which controls how much of the attention output is incorporated into the candidate activation:

$$\gamma = \phi_{\text{silu}}(X' W_\gamma + b_\gamma) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times v}$$

The candidate activation is then:

$$\hat{H} = \phi_{\text{silu}}(X' W_h + (\gamma \odot O) U_h + b_h) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$$

where the reset gate $\gamma$ element-wise multiplies the attention output $O$ before it is transformed by $U_h$ and combined with the linear projection of $X'$. This allows the model to learn when to rely on attention output versus when to ignore it and fall back on the CEMA representation.

**Attention head configuration:** MEGALODON-7B uses only $h = 4$ attention heads, compared to $h = 32$ in LLAMA2-7B. This is enabled by the attention gate and the fact that CEMA already captures per-dimension temporal dependencies, reducing the need for many attention heads to capture different types of token interactions. Fewer heads means less computation and memory for the attention operation, contributing to MEGALODON's efficiency.

**Fused CUDA implementation:** The paper implements a fused attention operator (Appendix B.1) that combines the causal mask, softmax, and optional dropout into a single CUDA kernel, reducing memory I/O for the attention weights. For attention dropout, it adopts the DropKey scheme (Li et al., 2023a), which applies the dropout mask before softmax (filling masked positions with $-\infty$) rather than after. This has the advantage that the gradient computation in backpropagation is independent of the applied dropout mask, simplifying training.

#### Pre-Norm with Two-Hop Residual Configuration

The residual connection pattern is the final piece of MEGALODON's stability design, and it addresses a specific failure mode of standard pre-normalization in very deep networks.

**The problem with standard pre-norm (Figure 3b):** In the standard pre-norm configuration used by most modern Transformers, each sublayer (attention or FFN) is wrapped as:

$$\hat{Y} = \text{Attention}(\text{Norm}(X)) + X$$
$$Y = \text{FFN}(\text{Norm}(\hat{Y})) + \hat{Y}$$

Unrolling this for one full block:

$$Y = \text{FFN}(\text{Norm}(\hat{Y})) + \text{Attention}(\text{Norm}(X)) + X$$

The output $Y$ is the sum of three terms: the original input $X$, the attention output, and the FFN output. As we stack more layers, the variance of $Y$ grows because each sublayer adds its contribution to the running sum. At initialization, if each sublayer's output has variance approximately equal to the input variance (thanks to normalization and careful initialization), then after one block the variance is roughly $3\sigma^2$ (assuming independence), after $L$ blocks it grows as $O(L)$, causing the scale of activations to increase with depth. This is the "instability issue" the paper cites from Davis et al. (2021) and Liu et al. (2022).

**MEGA's attempted fix — the update gate:** MEGA introduced a gated residual connection controlled by an update gate $\phi$:

$$Y = \phi \odot \hat{H} + (1 - \phi) \odot X$$

where $\phi \in (0, 1)^d$ (via sigmoid activation) learns how much of the new representation to incorporate versus retaining the original input. This is a continuous interpolation between the candidate output $\hat{H}$ and the input $X$, providing a learned way to control variance growth. However, the paper reports that this solution is insufficient: "the update gate $\phi$ introduces more model parameters and the instability issue still exists when scaling up model size to 7 billion." The gate adds parameters (for computing $\phi$) and does not fully solve the variance accumulation problem for very deep networks.

**MEGALODON's solution — two-hop residual (Figure 3c):** The key insight is to restructure the residual connections so that each sublayer's output adds to the original input $X$, not to the output of the previous sublayer:

$$\hat{Y} = \text{Attention}(\text{Norm}(X)) + X$$
$$Y = \text{FFN}(\text{Norm}(\hat{Y})) + X$$

Notice the difference: the FFN's residual connection goes back to $X$, not to $\hat{Y}$. The output $Y$ is now the sum of only two terms: the original input $X$ and the FFN output. The attention output contributes to $\hat{Y}$, but $\hat{Y}$ is normalized before entering the FFN, so its scale is controlled. After $L$ blocks, the activation is $X + \sum_{\ell=1}^L \text{FFN}_\ell(\text{Norm}(\hat{Y}_\ell))$, which still has variance growing with $L$, but the growth is slower because only the FFN outputs (not the attention outputs) accumulate directly. More importantly, the normalization before each FFN ensures that the FFN input is always well-conditioned, preventing the compounding of attention-output variance across layers.

**Why "two-hop":** The name comes from the path that information takes through the block. The input $X$ takes two "hops" through the block: first through the attention pathway (returning to $\hat{Y}$), then through the FFN pathway (returning to $Y$), but in each case the residual connection skips back to $X$ rather than to the intermediate state. This is distinct from the standard pattern where $X$ hops through attention to $\hat{Y}$ and then from $\hat{Y}$ through FFN to $Y$, accumulating contributions at each step.

**Removal of the update gate:** Since the two-hop residual stabilizes variance growth, the update gate $\phi$ becomes unnecessary and can be removed. This simplifies the architecture (fewer parameters, fewer operations) while solving the instability that the gate was intended to address but couldn't fully solve. The final output computation simplifies to a standard residual:

$$Y = \hat{H} + X$$

where $\hat{H}$ is the candidate activation from Equation 20, without the gating interpolation that MEGA used in Equation 21.

**Interaction with normalization placement:** Timestep Normalization is applied before the attention sublayer, and Layer Normalization is applied before the FFN sublayer (as shown in Figure 3a). This means the input to each sublayer is always normalized, regardless of the residual pattern. The two-hop residual ensures that even though normalization controls the scale of sublayer inputs, the accumulation of sublayer outputs across layers does not cause unbounded growth in the main residual stream.

#### Full MEGALODON Layer Architecture (Integration)

Bringing together all components, a single MEGALODON layer processes input $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ through the following sequence (refer to Figure 3a and the equations in Sections 3.1–3.4):

1. **CEMA temporal processing:** Each dimension of $X$ is independently expanded and processed through the complex-valued EMA, producing $X' = \text{CEMA}(X) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$. This step enriches each token's representation with a temporally smoothed context from all previous tokens in the chunk (and from the previous chunk via the hidden state passed across chunk boundaries).

2. **Timestep Normalization:** The CEMA output $X'$ is normalized along both the timestep and feature dimensions using cumulative statistics, producing a normalized representation that feeds into attention. This step stabilizes the scale of the query/key/value computations across positions.

3. **Normalized Attention:**
   - A shared representation $Z = X' W_z + b_z$ is computed and L2-normalized to $Z'$.
   - Queries and keys are computed as $Q = \kappa_q \odot Z' + \mu_q$, $K = \kappa_k \odot Z' + \mu_k$.
   - Values are computed from the original input as $V = \phi_{\text{silu}}(X W_v + b_v)$.
   - Chunked softmax attention is applied: $O = \text{softmax}(Q K^T) V$ (within each chunk of length $c = 4096$).
   - A reset gate $\gamma = \phi_{\text{silu}}(X' W_\gamma + b_\gamma)$ controls attention contribution.
   - Candidate activation $\hat{H} = \phi_{\text{silu}}(X' W_h + (\gamma \odot O) U_h + b_h)$ combines CEMA output, gated attention output, and a linear projection.

4. **First residual hop (attention):** $\hat{Y} = \hat{H} + X$. The candidate activation is added to the original input $X$ (not to the CEMA output $X'$).

5. **Layer Normalization and FFN:** $\hat{Y}$ is normalized with standard Layer Normalization, then processed through a SwiGLU feed-forward network. The paper uses SwiGLU activation following LLAMA2 (Shazeer, 2020).

6. **Second residual hop (FFN):** $Y = \text{FFN}(\text{LayerNorm}(\hat{Y})) + X$. The FFN output is added to the original input $X$, completing the two-hop pattern.

**Design rationale for normalization placement:** TimestepNorm before attention because attention involves mixing information across tokens, and normalizing both the temporal and feature statistics of the input improves the conditioning of the attention computation. LayerNorm before FFN because the attention output is already a mixture of contextual tokens, making temporal normalization less critical, and LayerNorm is faster. The paper also implicitly suggests that applying TimestepNorm before every sublayer would be computationally expensive without commensurate benefit.

**Chunk boundary handling:** The CEMA hidden state $h^{(j)}_{t-1}$ from Equation 2 carries information from the previous chunk into the current chunk. At the start of each sequence (or each chunk in the first layer), $h^{(j)}_0$ is initialized to zero. For subsequent chunks, the final hidden state of the previous chunk is used. This is the only mechanism for cross-chunk information flow; the attention mechanism has no access to tokens outside the current chunk. The paper relies on CEMA's expressiveness (enhanced by complex numbers) to adequately capture the dependencies that would otherwise be lost at chunk boundaries.

**Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE):** MEGALODON adopts RoPE from LLAMA2 but uses a larger base angle: $\theta_{\text{base}} = 100,000$ compared to LLAMA2's $10,000$. The paper notes that this change "slows down model convergence at the beginning of the pretraining process" but enables better length extrapolation. The larger base angle means that the rotary frequencies cover a wider range, making the positional encoding more distinguishable at very long distances (since RoPE's dot product decays with relative position, a larger base means slower decay). This is important for MEGALODON's claim of "unlimited context length."

#### 4-Dimensional Parallelism for Distributed Training

While the previous sections describe the architectural components, actually training a 7B-parameter model on 2 trillion tokens with 32K context length requires sophisticated distributed training strategies. MEGALODON introduces a fourth parallelism dimension — chunk parallelism along the sequence axis — that is orthogonal to the standard three (data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism).

**The standard three dimensions:**
- **Data parallelism:** Each GPU processes a different batch of sequences, then gradients are averaged across GPUs.
- **Tensor parallelism:** Individual weight matrices are split across GPUs, and the matrix multiplications are distributed (e.g., Megatron-LM style column/row parallelism).
- **Pipeline parallelism:** Different layers of the model are assigned to different GPUs, and micro-batches are pipelined through the layers.

The paper argues that these three dimensions "are still insufficient to scale up the context length of LLMs." The reason is that none of them explicitly parallelizes the sequence dimension: each GPU still processes the full attention computation for its assigned tokens, which becomes the bottleneck for very long sequences.

**Chunk parallelism (the fourth dimension):** Because MEGALODON's attention is already chunked (operating within fixed-size chunks of length $c = 4096$), the sequence can be naturally split across GPUs. Each GPU in a chunk-parallel group is assigned a contiguous segment of the sequence, and it processes only that segment's tokens through the attention sublayer. Since attention is local to each chunk, no communication is needed for the attention computation itself.

**What needs to be communicated across chunks:** Two pieces of information must flow from one chunk's GPU to the next chunk's GPU:
1. The final CEMA hidden state $h^{(j)}_n$ for each dimension $j$, which captures the temporal context accumulated up to the end of the chunk.
2. The cumulative mean and variance from Timestep Normalization, which are needed to initialize the cumulative statistics for the next chunk.

Both of these are small (proportional to $d \times h$ for CEMA state, and $O(k)$ for the normalization statistics where $k$ is the number of feature groups) compared to the activations and gradients that dominate communication in data and tensor parallelism.

**Asynchronous communication:** The paper states that "using asynchronous communication, we can minimize the overhead of chunk parallelization by hiding the communication costs in the computation of other components inside the same block and/or other blocks." This means that while GPU $i$ is computing the FFN for chunk $i$, it can simultaneously send its CEMA state to GPU $i+1$ and receive the CEMA state from GPU $i-1$ for the next layer. Since the FFN and other components do not depend on cross-chunk information, this overlap effectively hides the communication latency.

**Empirical efficiency:** The paper reports that MEGALODON-7B-32K using chunk parallelism "achieves about 94% utilization of MEGALODON-7B-4K." This means that scaling the context length from 4K to 32K (an $8\times$ increase) with 2-way chunk parallelism incurs only a 6% efficiency penalty, demonstrating that the communication overhead is well-hidden.

**Configuration for MEGALODON-7B pretraining:** The training uses 256 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a global batch size of 4M tokens (16K tokens per GPU). The parallelism configuration is: data parallel size = 128, chunk parallel size = 2, tensor parallel size = 1 (no tensor parallelism). With 128-way data parallelism and 2-way chunk parallelism, the total parallelism is $128 \times 2 = 256$, matching the GPU count. The absence of tensor parallelism (size 1) is notable — it suggests that MEGALODON's per-GPU memory footprint is manageable without sharding individual weight matrices, likely due to the small number of attention heads ($h = 4$) and the chunk-wise attention reducing the memory needed for attention activations.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Complex Domain as a Unifying Bridge Between Exponential Moving Averages and State Space Models

The most conceptually distinctive move in MEGALODON is not simply that it improves MEGA's EMA by making it complex-valued — it is that CEMA **reframes the EMA component as an instance of a diagonal state space model**, thereby importing the theoretical machinery developed for SSMs (structured HiPPO matrices, complex diagonalization, long-range convolution kernels) into the gated attention framework without sacrificing the attention mechanism that makes MEGA competitive. This is a fusion, not an upgrade.

Prior to this work, the two dominant sub-quadratic paradigms — efficient attention mechanisms (sparse, low-rank, chunked) and state space models (S4, H3, Hyena, Mamba) — existed as separate lineages with incompatible design philosophies. Attention-based methods retained the Transformer's core operation (dot-product interactions between tokens) but restricted its scope; SSM-based methods replaced attention entirely with a recurrent or convolutional state transition. Each lineage had strengths the other lacked: attention provided flexible, content-dependent token mixing; SSMs provided principled long-range memory with theoretical guarantees on memory capacity (via the HiPPO framework).

CEMA's innovation is recognizing that **an EMA with complex-valued parameters is already a diagonal state space model**, just parameterized differently. In the standard SSM formulation, a diagonal state matrix $A \in \mathbb{C}^{h \times h}$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_k$ yields a discrete-time impulse response $h_t = A h_{t-1} + B u_t$ where the influence of past inputs follows $\lambda_k^t$ — a damped complex exponential. CEMA's recurrence $h_t = \alpha e^{i\theta} \odot u_t + (1 - \alpha \odot \delta) e^{i\theta} \odot h_{t-1}$ is isomorphic to this: the effective eigenvalue for hidden dimension $k$ is $\alpha_k (1 - \delta_k) e^{i\theta_k}$, whose magnitude controls decay and whose phase controls oscillation frequency.

What makes this bridge significant beyond taxonomy is that it explains *why* CEMA should work better than real-valued EMA while using essentially the same parameter count. The real EMA can only represent monotonically decaying temporal dependencies (since the effective eigenvalues are constrained to $(0, 1) \subset \mathbb{R}$). The complex EMA can represent damped oscillations (since the eigenvalues are now in the complex unit disk). The uniform spacing of $\theta_{j,k} = 2\pi k \omega_j / h$ over the unit circle is not an arbitrary choice — it is the same basis used in S4's HiPPO-LegS matrix to provide a theoretically optimal compression of long-range dependencies. By adopting this parameterization, CEMA inherits the representational power of SSMs without needing the full SSM infrastructure (Legendre polynomials, bilinear discretization, etc.).

The paper provides indirect evidence for this unification in the results: MEGALODON substantially outperforms MEGA-chunk on Long Range Arena tasks (Table 6), with particularly large gains on Pathfinder (94.41 → 96.89) and Path-X (93.81 → 97.21), both of which require detecting long-range spatial patterns that likely benefit from oscillatory kernels. This is not merely an incremental EMA improvement; it represents a conceptual pivot where MEGALODON's recurrent component is understood and parameterized through the lens of state space theory.

### Innovation 2: Autoregressive-Consistent Spatial Normalization as a Missing Regularizer in Deep Sequence Models

The introduction of Timestep Normalization is innovative less for the specific normalization computation (which is a natural autoregressive adaptation of Group Normalization) and more for **identifying that the sequential dimension has been systematically under-normalized in deep Transformer-like architectures** — a diagnostic insight rather than a mechanistic invention.

The standard normalization landscape in Transformers treats the timestep dimension as sacrosanct: LayerNorm operates across features only; BatchNorm is inapplicable due to variable sequence lengths and autoregressive constraints; GroupNorm has been confined to vision tasks where causality is irrelevant. The field's implicit assumption has been that normalizing across the feature dimension is sufficient, and that the sequential dimension should be left untouched to preserve positional information and the autoregressive property.

MEGALODON challenges this assumption by observing that "layer normalization cannot directly reduce the internal covariate shift along the spatial dimension." This is not merely an empirical claim — it is a **structural diagnosis**. In a deep autoregressive model, the representation at position $t$ aggregates information from positions $1$ through $t-1$ through the attention mechanism. A token at position 1000 has seen 1000× more context than a token at position 10, and this difference in effective receptive field can manifest as a systematic shift in activation statistics along the sequence dimension. LayerNorm, by normalizing each position independently, masks the symptoms within each position but cannot correct the *distributional drift across positions*. This drift means that later layers receive inputs whose distributions depend on position, violating the i.i.d. assumption that normalization is meant to establish.

The practical significance of this diagnosis extends beyond MEGALODON. It suggests that **any sufficiently deep autoregressive Transformer may suffer from undiagnosed sequential covariate shift**, and that the training instabilities observed when scaling context length or model depth may be partly attributable to this phenomenon rather than solely to attention saturation or residual variance growth. The paper does not explicitly make this claim, but the logical implication of their analysis is that Timestep Normalization could be a broadly applicable technique for stabilizing long-context Transformers, not just MEGALODON.

The evidence supports this reading: MEGALODON-7B "suffers less training spikes than the Transformer-based architecture in LLAMA2" (Section 4.1), a fact attributed in part to Timestep Normalization's stabilizing effect. The paper also reports that the normalization is faster when applied only before the attention sublayer (with LayerNorm before FFN), suggesting that the primary benefit comes from normalizing the representations that enter the attention computation — precisely where cross-position mixture would cause the most severe distributional shift.

This constitutes a **fundamental diagnostic contribution** (the identification of an under-recognized source of instability) paired with a simple, principled solution (cumulative statistics that preserve causality). It is not an incremental tweak to existing normalization but a recognition that the autoregressive constraint, rather than being an obstacle to spatial normalization, can be respected by a straightforward modification of the statistics computation.

---

### Innovation 3: Two-Hop Residuals as a Variance Budgeting Strategy for Ultra-Deep Sub-Quadratic Architectures

The two-hop residual configuration (Figure 3c) represents a novel solution to a problem that prior work had attempted to address through parameterized gating mechanisms: **how to prevent residual variance accumulation in deep architectures without introducing additional learned parameters that themselves become instability sources at scale**. The insight is that architectural structure — specifically, which residual connections feed back to which points — can serve as a variance control mechanism more robustly than learned gates.

The dominant approach to residual variance control in the Transformer literature has been pre-normalization (Xiong et al., 2020): placing normalization before each sublayer rather than after. This prevents the *input* to each sublayer from growing unboundedly but does not address the *accumulation* of sublayer outputs across depth, since the output is always $Y = \text{sublayer}(\text{norm}(X)) + X$, and after $L$ layers the total activation includes the sum of $L$ sublayer outputs. Various mitigation strategies have been proposed: careful initialization scaling (e.g., shrinking the residual branch), learned scalar multipliers on residuals (used in some vision transformers), and gating mechanisms like MEGA's update gate $\phi$ that interpolate between the new output and the original input.

MEGALODON's key observation is that MEGA's update gate $\phi$ — while theoretically a solution — **fails empirically at the 7B scale** ("the instability issue still exists when scaling up model size to 7 billion"). The gate introduces parameters that themselves need to be trained stably, and the sigmoid activation can saturate in deep networks, causing the gate to collapse to "always pass through" or "always update" modes. The two-hop residual solves the same problem through **architectural rearrangement alone**, with zero additional parameters.

The intellectual contribution is the recognition that the variance accumulation problem can be decomposed by sublayer type. In standard pre-norm, both attention and FFN outputs contribute additively to the same running sum. In two-hop pre-norm, the attention output contributes to an intermediate $\hat{Y}$ that is then normalized before entering the FFN, so the attention-output variance does not directly accumulate across layers. Only the FFN outputs accumulate in the main residual stream. Since the FFN typically has more parameters and more representational capacity than the attention sublayer (in LLAMA2-7B, the FFN intermediate dimension is 11008 vs. 4096 for attention), this asymmetric treatment is well-motivated: the FFN can better absorb the normalization of its input while producing stable-magnitude outputs.

This is a **fundamentally new approach to residual architecture design** — not an incremental improvement to gating mechanisms but a rejection of the premise that parameterized gates are the right tool for this problem. The fact that the two-hop residual enables removal of the update gate entirely ($\phi$, along with its parameter matrices $W_\phi$ and $b_\phi$) is the proof: the structure accomplishes what the parameters could not.

The evidence is embedded in the scaling result: MEGALODON-7B trains stably on 2T tokens without the training spikes that afflict LLAMA2-7B (Section 4.1), and this stability is attributed to the combination of normalized attention and two-hop residuals. While the paper does not ablate the two-hop residual in isolation at the 7B scale, the explicit statement that MEGA's gating fails at this scale — combined with MEGALODON's success — constitutes strong circumstantial evidence for the innovation's necessity.

### Innovation 4: Empirical Refutation of the Inherent Superiority of Full Attention Over Chunked Architectures at Scale

While the previous innovations are architectural, this fourth insight is **empirical and paradigm-shifting**: MEGALODON-7B's performance — matching or exceeding LLAMA2-7B on every benchmark and approaching LLAMA2-13B on several — constitutes the first credible evidence that a chunked-attention architecture with a recurrent inter-chunk component can match a well-tuned full-attention Transformer at the 7B/2T-token scale. This refutes the prevailing assumption that the performance gap between chunked and full attention is fundamental and irreducible.

The field's default belief, reinforced by Tay et al. (2022) and the mixed results of early linear attention models, has been that sub-quadratic models necessarily sacrifice quality for efficiency — that the Transformer's quadratic attention is expensive but inherently more expressive, and that linear approximations are a pareto-optimal tradeoff (less compute, worse results). MEGALODON disrupts this assumption not by arguing theoretically but by **demonstrating a counterexample at a scale where efficiency differences are economically meaningful** (2 trillion tokens, 7 billion parameters).

What makes this evidence compelling:

- **Controlled comparison:** MEGALODON-7B and LLAMA2-7B were trained on exactly the same data mix with the same tokenizer and the same number of tokens (2T). This is not a comparison against a weaker or differently-trained baseline; LLAMA2 is a state-of-the-art Transformer at this scale.
- **Cross-domain consistency:** The advantage is not limited to long-context tasks. MEGALODON surpasses LLAMA2-7B on short-context benchmarks (MMLU: 49.8 vs. 45.3; ARC-c: 53.1 vs. 45.9; NQ: 25.7 vs. 25.7; Table 1) where quadratic attention should theoretically have no efficiency disadvantage. This suggests that MEGALODON's inductive biases — the complex-valued EMA, the timestep normalization, the normalized attention — provide representational advantages that *compensate* for the restricted attention scope.
- **Training efficiency:** At 32K context, MEGALODON is 32% faster than LLAMA2 (Figure 4), meaning it processes more tokens per second while achieving lower loss. The typical narrative is that efficiency comes at a quality cost; MEGALODON inverts this to show that quality can come at an efficiency *gain*.
- **Perplexity trajectory:** The training loss curve (Figure 1) shows MEGALODON-7B tracking initially above LLAMA2-7B (attributed to the larger RoPE base angle slowing early convergence) but crossing below it and finishing at 1.70 — exactly midway between LLAMA2-7B (1.75) and LLAMA2-13B (1.67). This is not a marginal win but a substantial gap, equivalent to roughly doubling the effective model capacity.

The significance of this refutation extends beyond MEGALODON itself. It implies that the **perceived quality gap in prior sub-quadratic models may have been attributable not to the chunked attention per se but to insufficiently expressive inter-chunk recurrence and training instability at scale**. By fixing these (CEMA for expressiveness, timestep norm and two-hop residual for stability), MEGALODON shows that chunked architectures can be in the same accuracy class as full attention. This opens the door for other chunked/recurrent hybrids to close the gap through similar stabilization techniques, rather than through increasingly complex attention approximations that try to simulate full attention more faithfully.

This is a **fundamental empirical finding** rather than an incremental improvement — it shifts the burden of proof. Before MEGALODON, the default assumption was that full attention is necessary for state-of-the-art performance and that sub-quadratic models are a compromise. After MEGALODON, the question becomes: what are the minimal conditions under which chunked attention can match full attention, and can these conditions be met even more efficiently?

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary large-scale experiments use the same 2-trillion-token mix of publicly available data that was used to train LLAMA2, ensuring a controlled data-matched comparison. For long-context evaluation, the authors construct a custom validation dataset from 1,920 selected books, each containing at least 2M tokens, shuffled and concatenated to form sequences spanning up to 2M tokens. Medium-scale benchmarks include Long Range Arena (LRA; Tay et al., 2021) for synthetic long-context tasks, Speech Commands (SC10 subset, length 16K raw speech; Warden, 2018) for audio classification, ImageNet-1K (1.28M training images, 50K validation images from 1000 classes; Deng et al., 2009) for image classification, WikiText-103 (Merity et al., 2017) and PG-19 (Rae et al., 2019) for language modeling, and Scrolls (Shaham et al., 2022) for long-context QA. The short-context evaluation uses standard academic benchmarks following LLAMA2's setup: MMLU (5-shot), HellaSwag, PIQA, SIQA, WinoGrande, ARC-e, ARC-c (all 0-shot), BoolQ (0-shot), NaturalQuestions and TriviaQA (both 5-shot), and MT-Bench for instruction-following evaluation.

- **Base model(s).** The primary architecture is MEGALODON-7B, configured with 32 layers, feature dimension d = 4096, SwiGLU activation in the feed-forward layer, rotary position embeddings (RoPE) with base θ = 100,000, attention chunk size c = 4096, and only h = 4 attention heads (compared to 32 in LLAMA2-7B). The model is compared against LLAMA2-7B and LLAMA2-13B (Touvron et al., 2023) as the primary Transformer baselines at matched parameter counts and training data. Additional comparisons are drawn against open-source models including MPT-7B (MosaicML, 2023), RWKV-7B (Peng et al., 2023), Mamba-3B (Gu and Dao, 2023), Mistral-7B (Jiang et al., 2023), and Gemma-8B (Mesnard et al., 2024), though these are not data-matched (trained on different datasets and token counts). For medium-scale benchmarks, comparisons include S4 (Gu et al., 2022a), MEGA (Ma et al., 2023), Transformer-XL (Dai et al., 2019), Block-Recurrent Transformer (Hutchins et al., 2022), Perceiver AR (Hawthorne et al., 2022), Compressive Transformer (Rae et al., 2020), and MEGABYTE (Yu et al., 2024).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric for pretraining is **negative log-likelihood (NLL)**, equivalently reported as training loss, computed as the model's next-token prediction loss on the training data. For downstream evaluation, the paper reports **accuracy** for classification tasks (ImageNet-1K top-1 accuracy, Speech Commands accuracy, LRA task accuracy, MMLU, commonsense reasoning benchmarks, BoolQ), **F1-score** for QA tasks (NarrativeQA, Qasper), **geometric mean of ROUGE-1, 2, and L** for summarization (QMSum), **perplexity (PPL)** for language modeling evaluation (WikiText-103, PG-19, and the custom book validation dataset at various context lengths), and **MT-Bench scores** for instruction-following quality. For training efficiency, the metric is **words/tokens per second (WPS)** measured on 256 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a global batch size of 4M tokens, with each experiment run using the same hardware configuration to ensure fair comparison.

- **Baselines.** The primary head-to-head baselines are **LLAMA2-7B** (same 2T training tokens, same tokenizer, same data mix) and **LLAMA2-13B** (providing an upper bound on what a larger Transformer achieves). For long-context evaluation, baselines include **LLAMA2-7B-4K and LLAMA2-7B-32K** (the latter continually pretrained on 500B additional long-context tokens by Xiong et al., 2023), **Xgen-7B-8K** (Nijkamp et al., 2023), **MPT-7B-8K** (MosaicML, 2023), and **YaRN-7B-128K** (Peng et al., 2024). For instruction following, baselines include **LLAMA2-Chat-7B** (which uses RLHF) and **Vicuna-7B** (Chiang et al., 2023). For medium-scale benchmarks, baselines include **S4** (Gu et al., 2022a) on LRA and Speech Commands, **MEGA** (Ma et al., 2023) on LRA, Speech Commands, and WikiText-103, and task-specific state-of-the-art models as listed above.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use "generation budget" as its compute metric (unlike best-of-N sampling studies) because MEGALODON's efficiency analysis focuses on **training throughput and memory scaling** rather than inference-time search. Compute efficiency is measured in **tokens processed per second (WPS)** at training time, not in generations. For the FLOPs-matched comparison (implicit in Figure 1 and Table 1), the accounting is based on **total training FLOPs**: both MEGALODON-7B and LLAMA2-7B process the same 2T tokens, and the efficiency claim is that MEGALODON achieves better loss per unit of compute. The speed comparison in Figure 4 controls for hardware (256 A100 GPUs, identical global batch size of 4M tokens, 16K tokens per GPU) and varies only the architecture and context length.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** There is no explicit cross-validation protocol reported for the main 7B-scale experiments. For the LRA benchmark, the paper follows the standard evaluation protocol from Tay et al. (2021). For the instruction-finetuning MT-Bench evaluation, the paper uses the standard MT-Bench scoring protocol. The paper does not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests for any of its quantitative results. The 2T-token pretraining run is a single seed (not repeated due to computational cost), which means that training loss differences of a few hundredths (e.g., 1.70 vs. 1.75) should be interpreted as indicative rather than statistically verified — a single seed does not rule out the possibility that the difference is partially attributable to random variation in initialization or data ordering.

### Main Quantitative Results

The experiments are organized around five axes: (1) large-scale pretraining data and compute efficiency, (2) short-context downstream benchmarks, (3) long-context perplexity and QA evaluation, (4) instruction finetuning, and (5) medium-scale benchmarks across modalities. Each axis tests a distinct aspect of MEGALODON's claimed superiority over Transformers.

#### Large-Scale Pretraining: Training Loss and Efficiency (Figure 1, Figure 4)

**Training loss trajectory.** Figure 1 plots the negative log-likelihood (NLL) of MEGALODON-7B, LLAMA2-7B, and LLAMA2-13B on the training data as a function of processed tokens (up to 2 trillion). The headline result: MEGALODON-7B reaches a final training loss of **1.70**, which the paper frames as "mid-way between LLAMA2-7B (1.75) and 13B (1.67)." The gap between MEGALODON-7B and LLAMA2-7B is 0.05 NLL, which represents a substantial relative improvement — to put this in perspective, the gap between LLAMA2-7B and LLAMA2-13B is 0.08, so MEGALODON closes approximately 62.5% of the gap to the larger model.

The trajectory reveals a non-trivial dynamic: during the first ~500B tokens (roughly the first quarter of training), MEGALODON-7B's NLL is *worse* than LLAMA2-7B. The authors attribute this to the larger RoPE base angle (θ = 100,000 vs. 10,000), which "slows down model convergence at the beginning of the pretraining process." MEGALODON's NLL curve crosses below LLAMA2-7B between roughly 500B and 750B tokens and maintains a consistent advantage for the remaining ~1.25T tokens. This crossing pattern is significant: it means that the architectural innovations (CEMA, timestep norm, normalized attention, two-hop residual) provide benefits that compound over long training horizons — the model learns *better* representations, not just *faster* ones.

**Training stability.** The paper states that MEGALODON "suffers less training spikes than the Transformer-based architecture in LLAMA2." While the loss curve in Figure 1 shows some small fluctuations for both models, LLAMA2-7B appears to exhibit more visible downward spikes (sudden loss improvements that may indicate instability rather than genuine learning) around 500B and 1000B tokens. This is qualitative evidence — the paper does not quantify spike frequency or magnitude — but it is consistent with the claim that the normalization and residual innovations improve stability.

**Computation efficiency (Figure 4).** At 4K context length, MEGALODON-7B is approximately **6% slower** than LLAMA2-7B (the paper says "slightly slower (about 6%)"), which is attributed to the additional computation from CEMA and Timestep Normalization. At 32K context length, the comparison inverts: MEGALODON-7B is **~32% faster** than LLAMA2-7B (the paper quantifies this as "significantly faster (about 32%)"). The efficiency crossover point — where MEGALODON's linear complexity advantage outweighs its per-token overhead — lies between 4K and 32K, but the paper does not report the exact crossover. Additionally, MEGALODON-7B-32K using 2-way chunk parallelism "achieves about 94% utilization" of MEGALODON-7B-4K, meaning the parallelization overhead is only ~6% of throughput.

**Context length scaling.** The paper's Figure 5 shows perplexity on the 1,920-book validation dataset decreasing **monotonically** with context length from 4K to 2M tokens. While specific PPL values are not annotated on the curve in the paper text (the figure is a line plot without numeric labels), the monotonic decrease demonstrates that MEGALODON genuinely benefits from longer contexts — it does not saturate or degrade as context grows. This is evidence for the claim of "unlimited context length," though "unlimited" is an overstatement: the experiment tests up to 2M tokens, and performance beyond 2M is extrapolated but not verified.

#### Short-Context Academic Benchmarks (Table 1)

Table 1 presents MEGALODON-7B's performance against LLAMA2-7B, LLAMA2-13B, and other open-source models on standard academic benchmarks. All evaluations use the same pretrained base model; no task-specific fine-tuning is applied.

**Headline comparison with LLAMA2-7B:** MEGALODON-7B **outperforms LLAMA2-7B on every single reported benchmark**:
- MMLU (5-shot): 49.8 vs. 45.3 (+4.5 points)
- BoolQ (0-shot): 80.5 vs. 77.4 (+3.1)
- HellaSwag (0-shot): 77.5 vs. 77.2 (+0.3)
- PIQA (0-shot): 80.1 vs. 78.8 (+1.3)
- SIQA (0-shot): 49.6 vs. 48.3 (+1.3)
- WinoGrande (0-shot): 71.4 vs. 69.2 (+2.2)
- ARC-e (0-shot): 79.8 vs. 75.2 (+4.6)
- ARC-c (0-shot): 53.1 vs. 45.9 (+7.2)
- NaturalQuestions (5-shot): 25.7 vs. 25.7 (tie)
- TriviaQA (5-shot): 60.5 vs. 58.5 (+2.0)

The improvements are not uniform. The largest absolute gains are on ARC-c (+7.2), ARC-e (+4.6), and MMLU (+4.5) — all tasks requiring reasoning and knowledge integration. The smallest gains are on HellaSwag (+0.3), which primarily tests surface-level commonsense completion, and NaturalQuestions (tie), which requires factual recall. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that MEGALODON's architectural innovations (complex EMA for richer temporal patterns, timestep normalization for better sequential representations) disproportionately benefit tasks that require composing information across multiple tokens — reasoning tasks — rather than tasks dominated by local statistical patterns or factual lookup.

**Comparison with LLAMA2-13B:** On several benchmarks, MEGALODON-7B approaches or exceeds LLAMA2-13B despite being roughly half the size:
- ARC-e: MEGALODON-7B (79.8) > LLAMA2-13B (77.3)
- ARC-c: MEGALODON-7B (53.1) > LLAMA2-13B (49.4)
- PIQA: MEGALODON-7B (80.1) ≈ LLAMA2-13B (80.5)
- SIQA: MEGALODON-7B (49.6) ≈ LLAMA2-13B (50.3)

On MMLU, however, LLAMA2-13B maintains a substantial lead (54.8 vs. 49.8), as it does on TriviaQA (65.1 vs. 60.5) and NaturalQuestions (31.2 vs. 25.7). The benchmarks where MEGALODON-7B matches the 13B model are predominantly reasoning-focused; the benchmarks where it does not are knowledge-intensive. This suggests that MEGALODON's architectural advantages improve *reasoning quality* but do not compensate for the *knowledge capacity* gap that comes from having fewer parameters.

**Comparison with other open-source models:** The paper's Table 1 also includes Mamba-3B, RWKV-7B, MPT-7B, Mistral-7B, and Gemma-8B. The paper acknowledges that Mistral-7B and Gemma-8B were "pretrained on much larger datasets than MEGALODON-7B, hence the results are not directly comparable." Mistral-7B, for instance, leads on MMLU (60.1 vs. 49.8) and most other benchmarks, but its training data quantity and composition are not controlled. Mamba-3B achieves only 26.2 on MMLU at 3B parameters and 0.6T tokens — substantially lower — but the parameter and data mismatch makes direct comparison uninformative. The only truly controlled comparison is with LLAMA2-7B and LLAMA2-13B.

#### Long-Context Perplexity Evaluation (Figure 5)

Figure 5 shows validation perplexity on the 1,920-book dataset as context length increases from 4K to 2M tokens. The paper reports that "the PPL decreases monotonically with context length," which demonstrates that the model can effectively leverage additional context to improve predictions rather than being confused by it. The monotonic decrease is non-trivial: many long-context models show a U-shaped curve where performance initially improves with longer context but then degrades as the model fails to distinguish relevant from irrelevant distant tokens. MEGALODON's monotonic improvement suggests that CEMA's gating and the chunk-wise attention with RoPE (θ = 100,000) together provide an effective mechanism for selectively attending to relevant long-range information.

However, the paper does not report the absolute perplexity values at each context length, nor does it compare against LLAMA2-7B at the same context lengths (LLAMA2-7B with 4K context cannot be evaluated at 2M context length without modification). The curve therefore demonstrates MEGALODON's *scaling behavior* but not its *relative performance* against Transformers on long sequences. This is a significant gap: we know MEGALODON benefits from longer contexts and that it trains faster than Transformers on long sequences, but we do not know whether its 2M-token perplexity is better or worse than what a Transformer would achieve if it could be trained and evaluated at that length.

#### Long-Context QA on Scrolls (Table 2)

Table 2 presents zero-shot (NarrativeQA), two-shot (Qasper), and one-shot (QMSum) results on the Scrolls benchmark, which requires models to answer questions based on long input documents:

- **NarrativeQA (F1):** MEGALODON-7B achieves 23.9, outperforming all listed baselines including LLAMA2-7B-4K (18.8), LLAMA2-7B-32K (23.5), Xgen-7B-8K (17.4), MPT-7B-8K (18.8), and YaRN-7B-128K (20.9). This is MEGALODON's strongest long-context result.
- **Qasper (F1):** MEGALODON-7B achieves 28.0, comparable to LLAMA2-7B-32K (28.3) and ahead of Xgen-7B-8K (20.5), MPT-7B-8K (24.7), and YaRN-7B-128K (26.2).
- **QMSum (geometric ROUGE):** MEGALODON-7B achieves 13.1, behind LLAMA2-7B-32K (14.5) but ahead of Xgen-7B-8K (6.8), MPT-7B-8K (8.8), and YaRN-7B-128K (11.4).

The comparison with LLAMA2-7B-32K is important because that model was "continually trained on 500B tokens of long-context data," giving it an advantage of additional long-context-specific training that MEGALODON-7B does not have (all 2T tokens were from the standard LLAMA2 data mix, not specifically long-context). Despite this, MEGALODON achieves competitive or better results, suggesting that its architectural length-generalization capabilities (CEMA + RoPE with large base angle) effectively substitute for dedicated long-context training.

#### Instruction Finetuning (Table 3)

Table 3 reports MT-Bench scores for chat models derived from instruction-finetuned base models:
- MEGALODON-7B-Chat: 6.27
- LLAMA2-7B-Chat (with RLHF): 6.27
- Vicuna-7B: 6.17
- Mistral-7B-Instruct: 6.84

MEGALODON matches LLAMA2-Chat *without using RLHF* — the paper explicitly states that "We did not apply any RLHF techniques to further finetune it." This is a meaningful result because RLHF typically provides a substantial boost to instruction-following quality (as evidenced by the gap between base Vicuna-7B at 6.17 and its RLHF-tuned variants). MEGALODON achieving parity with an RLHF-tuned model using only supervised instruction finetuning suggests that the base model's representations are more amenable to instruction following, potentially because CEMA's temporal processing and the normalized attention provide better-grounded representations that transfer more readily to the chat format.

#### Medium-Scale Benchmarks Across Modalities (Tables 4–8, plus Appendix C)

**ImageNet-1K classification (Table 4):** MEGALODON-90M achieves top-1 accuracy of 83.1%, exceeding MEGA-90M (82.3%), DeiT-B-86M (81.8%), ViT-B-86M (77.9%), and ResNet-152-60M (78.3%). For image classification, the timestep normalization is replaced with standard Group Normalization (the paper notes this change explicitly), demonstrating architectural flexibility. The 1.3% improvement over DeiT-B and 0.8% over MEGA at matched parameter count supports the claim that MEGALODON's architecture generalizes beyond language to vision tasks.

**PG-19 language modeling (Table 5):** MEGALODON-1.3B achieves word-level test perplexity of 25.4, substantially outperforming Compressive Transformer (33.6), Perceiver AR-975M (28.9), Block-Recurrent Transformer-1.3B (26.5), and MEGABYTE-1.3B (36.4). On validation, MEGALODON scores 29.5. This is the strongest result on the PG-19 benchmark among the listed models, and it is achieved at a parameter count matched to Block-Recurrent Transformer and MEGABYTE. PG-19 consists of full-length books, making it a test of long-context language modeling; MEGALODON's advantage here is direct evidence that the CEMA + chunked attention approach succeeds at book-length dependencies.

**WikiText-103 language modeling (Table 8):** MEGALODON-252M achieves word-level perplexity of 17.23, significantly lower than MEGA-252M (18.07), Transformer-247M (18.66), Transformer-XL-257M (18.30), and S4-249M (20.95). This is a small-scale result but establishes that MEGALODON improves over MEGA even on standard-length (non-long-context) language modeling.

**Long Range Arena (LRA, Table 6):** MEGALODON (full attention) achieves an average accuracy of 88.63 across all six LRA tasks, edging out MEGA (88.21). MEGALODON-chunk (chunk size c = 128, except Path-X where c = 4096) achieves 87.62, substantially outperforming MEGA-chunk (85.66). The gap between full and chunked attention narrows from ~2.55 points in MEGA to ~1.01 points in MEGALODON, providing direct evidence that the complex EMA better compensates for the loss of cross-chunk attention. On Path-X (the most challenging long-range task, 16K tokens), MEGALODON-chunk achieves 97.21 vs. MEGA-chunk's 93.81 — a 3.4-point improvement that is particularly notable since Path-X requires detecting patterns over very long ranges.

**Speech Commands raw audio (Table 7):** MEGALODON-300K achieves 98.14% accuracy, significantly outperforming S4-300K (97.50%) and MEGA-300K (96.92%). This is the smallest-scale result but demonstrates modality transfer to raw audio processing.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper does not present formal ablation studies in the traditional sense — there is no table or figure that isolates the contribution of each component (CEMA, Timestep Normalization, Normalized Attention, Two-Hop Residual) by removing one at a time. The closest approximations to ablation studies are:

**Chunk-wise vs. full attention comparison (Table 6 — LRA benchmark):** Across all six LRA tasks, MEGALODON-chunk underperforms MEGALODON-full, but the gap is systematically smaller than the corresponding gap in MEGA. This implicitly ablates the CEMA contribution: since CEMA is the primary mechanism for cross-chunk information propagation, the reduced full-vs-chunk gap demonstrates that CEMA captures temporal dependencies more effectively than the original real-valued EMA. Quantitatively: MEGALODON-full averages 88.63 vs. MEGALODON-chunk at 87.62 (gap of 1.01); MEGA-full averages 88.21 vs. MEGA-chunk at 85.66 (gap of 2.55). The CEMA innovation reduces the chunking penalty by approximately 60%.

**Attention function choice (Section 3.3, appendix C reference):** The paper states that "with the normalized attention, the softmax function $f_{\text{softmax}}$ obtains the best or at least comparable performance on different tasks and data modalities." This is presented as a finding from empirical comparison, but the actual comparison data is not shown in the main paper (referenced to Appendix C, which is not included in the provided text beyond a mention). The claim implies that normalized attention makes softmax consistently effective, resolving MEGA's need for different attention functions across tasks.

**Normalization placement:** The paper explicitly chooses to apply Timestep Normalization only before the attention sublayer (not before the FFN), with Layer Normalization before the FFN. The rationale is given (speed, and the attention output already being a mixture of contextual tokens), but no ablation comparing full TimestepNorm vs. the mixed configuration is reported. This is a significant missing ablation: we do not know whether applying TimestepNorm before the FFN would help, hurt, or make no difference.

**RoPE base angle (Section 4.1):** The paper identifies the larger RoPE base angle (100,000 vs. 10,000) as the reason for MEGALODON's slower early convergence. This is an implicit ablation of the RoPE configuration: it shows that the base angle has a measurable impact on training dynamics. However, no comparison of different base angles is reported.

**Absence of update gate:** By removing the update gate φ, MEGALODON reduces parameter count compared to MEGA. The paper presents this as a positive (simplification + stability), but does not report an ablation where the update gate is retained with the two-hop residual to test whether the gate still provides any benefit. Given that the gate "introduces more model parameters and the instability issue still exists," the evidence is that the gate is net harmful at scale, but this is stated rather than experimentally demonstrated.

**Chunk parallelism scaling (Figure 4):** The paper reports that MEGALODON-7B-32K with 2-way chunk parallelism achieves 94% utilization relative to MEGALODON-7B-4K. This is essentially an ablation of the communication overhead: it demonstrates that the cross-chunk communication (CEMA state + normalization statistics) can be effectively hidden behind computation. However, the paper does not report scaling to higher chunk parallelism degrees (e.g., 4-way, 8-way), so we do not know how the utilization degrades with more chunk-parallel groups.

**Plus-1 reparameterization (Appendix B.2):** The paper describes the reparameterization of the normalization scale parameter as γ + 1 (with γ initialized to 0) to prevent weight decay from pushing the effective scale toward zero. This is presented as a general technique rather than an ablated finding, but it represents a design choice that presumably improved stability.

**Negative result — update gate insufficiency at scale:** The paper's finding that MEGA's update gate fails at 7B scale is a negative result that motivated the two-hop residual design. The paper states this as a fact ("the instability issue still exists when scaling up model size to 7 billion") rather than showing comparative training curves with and without the gate, but it functions as an implicit ablation of the gating mechanism.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments in this paper collectively aim to support three central claims: (1) MEGALODON matches or exceeds Transformer performance on both pretraining efficiency and downstream accuracy, (2) MEGALODON achieves linear complexity enabling efficient training on long sequences, and (3) MEGALODON generalizes robustly across tasks and modalities. I evaluate each claim against the reported evidence, identify where the evidence is strong, where it is weak, and what critical experiments are missing.

**Claim 1: MEGALODON matches or exceeds Transformer performance.** This claim is **the best-supported in the paper**, backed by the controlled LLAMA2-7B comparison on 2T tokens, the consistent downstream benchmark improvements (Table 1), and the cross-modal results (Tables 4–8). The evidence is strongest for language modeling at 7B scale, where the controlled data and compute matching eliminates most confounds. The training loss gap (1.70 vs. 1.75; Figure 1) and downstream benchmark sweep (Table 1) together establish that MEGALODON-7B is genuinely a better model than LLAMA2-7B by standard metrics, not merely a more efficient approximation that sacrifices quality.

However, several caveats weaken the strength of this conclusion:

- **Single seed:** The 2T-token pretraining run is not repeated. A loss difference of 0.05 could be partially attributable to initialization luck, data ordering, or other random factors. Without multiple runs or confidence intervals, we cannot assess whether the reported gap is statistically reliable. This is a practical constraint (training multiple 7B models on 2T tokens is extremely expensive), but it means the result should be interpreted as a point estimate from a single trial, not as a statistically verified effect.

- **Model configuration is not fully controlled:** MEGALODON-7B uses a different number of attention heads (4 vs. 32), a different normalization scheme (TimestepNorm vs. RMSNorm), a different residual configuration, and a different RoPE base angle (100K vs. 10K) compared to LLAMA2-7B. These are all deliberate design choices, but they mean that the comparison is between two complete architectures, not between two models that differ only in the presence of attention. We cannot attribute the improvement to any single component (CEMA vs. normalization vs. residual pattern). The lack of component-wise ablation makes it impossible to determine which of the four innovations is most responsible for the performance gain.

- **Training dynamics anomaly:** The early-underperformance-then-crossover pattern in Figure 1 is attributed to the RoPE base angle, but this is an inference from correlation, not a proven causal mechanism. An experiment with MEGALODON using RoPE base 10,000 would test this hypothesis but is not reported. If the crossover is actually due to CEMA or TimestepNorm taking time to learn effective temporal representations, the attribution to RoPE would be incorrect.

- **MMLU and knowledge benchmarks remain below 13B model:** MEGALODON-7B does not fully close the gap with LLAMA2-13B on knowledge-intensive tasks (MMLU: 49.8 vs. 54.8; NQ: 25.7 vs. 31.2; TQA: 60.5 vs. 65.1). This is consistent with the intuition that parameter count matters for knowledge storage, but it also means the claim of "matching larger Transformers" is conditional on the task type: true for reasoning (ARC, PIQA, SIQA), false for factual knowledge (MMLU, NQ, TQA). The paper's abstract framing ("landing mid-way between LLAMA2-7B and 13B") accurately captures this nuance, but the broader claim of "better efficiency than Transformer" implies a more uniform advantage than the data supports.

**Claim 2: Linear complexity enables efficient long-sequence training.** This claim is **partially supported but incompletely evaluated**. The training speed comparison (Figure 4) demonstrates that at 32K context, MEGALODON is 32% faster than LLAMA2, which is strong evidence for computational efficiency on moderately long sequences. The 94% chunk parallelism utilization is good evidence that the distributed training design works. And the monotonic perplexity improvement up to 2M tokens (Figure 5) shows that MEGALODON genuinely benefits from very long contexts.

However, there are several critical gaps:

- **No 2M-token training demonstration:** The paper evaluates perplexity at up to 2M tokens (Figure 5) but does not train the model at 2M context length. The 7B model was trained at 32K context (4 chunks of 4096). The claim of "unlimited context length" is based on the model's ability to generalize at inference time to longer contexts than it was trained on, not on training at those lengths. This is a legitimate form of length generalization, but the efficiency claims at 32K may not directly extrapolate to 2M — training at 2M context with 512 chunks would involve 512× as many chunk-boundary communications, and the utilization might degrade substantially.

- **No inference latency benchmarks:** All efficiency numbers are for training throughput (WPS). The paper does not report inference latency for autoregressive generation at different context lengths. Since inference is often the bottleneck in deployment, not training, this is a significant omission. MEGALODON's chunk-wise attention with hidden state recurrence may have different inference characteristics (e.g., the need to maintain and update the CEMA state across generated tokens) that are not captured by training WPS.

- **No memory scaling analysis:** The paper does not report GPU memory usage for different context lengths or model sizes. The time efficiency advantage (32% faster at 32K) is important, but memory efficiency is equally critical for deployment feasibility. The quadratic memory cost of Transformer attention is often the binding constraint, not the computational cost.

- **No comparison against other sub-quadratic architectures:** The efficiency comparison is only against LLAMA2 (full attention). We do not know how MEGALODON's training speed compares to Mamba, RWKV, or other linear-complexity models. MEGALODON may be faster than Transformers but slower than Mamba — the paper does not provide data to assess this.

**Claim 3: Robust generalization across tasks and modalities.** This claim is **supported in breadth but shallow in depth**. The paper reports results on LRA (6 tasks), ImageNet-1K, Speech Commands, WikiText-103, PG-19, Scrolls (3 tasks), and 10 short-context benchmarks — an impressive diversity. The results are consistently positive: MEGALODON outperforms baselines on nearly every task.

However, the depth of evidence is limited:

- **Small-scale results are not state-of-the-art:** The ImageNet-1K accuracy (83.1%) and PG-19 perplexity (25.4) are strong relative to the listed baselines but are far from current state-of-the-art on these benchmarks. ConvNeXt, ViT-G, and other modern vision architectures substantially exceed 83.1% on ImageNet. This is not a fatal criticism (the goal is to demonstrate architectural generality, not to set new records), but it means the cross-modal claims are validated at small-to-medium scale, not at the frontier of each modality.

- **Minimal architectural adaptation is not experimentally demonstrated for all modalities:** The paper claims the architecture is "unified" with "minimal architectural divergence." For image classification, TimestepNorm is replaced with GroupNorm — this is a divergence, though a minor one. For speech, the setup follows MEGA's original configuration. It is unclear whether the exact 7B-scale architecture (with TimestepNorm before attention, two-hop residual, etc.) would work for vision or speech without additional tuning.

- **No large-scale vision or speech experiments:** The cross-modal experiments are all at small scale (90M parameters for ImageNet, 300K for speech, 252M for WikiText-103). We do not know whether the architectural innovations that stabilize 7B-scale language pretraining would transfer to large-scale vision or speech models. This is particularly relevant for CEMA: complex-valued temporal processing may be more important for 1D sequences (language, audio) than for 2D images where spatial relationships are not inherently sequential.

**Missing experiments that would have strengthened the paper:**

1. **Component-wise ablation at scale:** Training even a single 1B-parameter model with each component removed (no CEMA, no TimestepNorm, no normalized attention, no two-hop residual) would have isolated which innovations drive the improvement. This is expensive but feasible at smaller scale.

2. **Direct comparison with Mamba or other SSMs at matched scale:** The paper acknowledges SSMs as the primary alternative but only includes Mamba-3B in Table 1 (which is neither data-matched nor scale-matched). A Mamba-7B trained on the same 2T tokens would be the most informative baseline for assessing whether MEGALODON's hybrid approach (EMA + attention) is better than a pure SSM approach.

3. **Inference benchmarks:** Latency and memory usage for autoregressive generation at 4K, 32K, 128K, and 1M context lengths, compared to LLAMA2 and (ideally) to Mamba or RWKV.

4. **Training at extreme context lengths:** Training MEGALODON (even at smaller scale, e.g., 1B parameters) at 128K or 1M context and measuring whether the linear complexity advantage persists and whether perplexity continues to improve. The current longest training context is 32K; the 2M evaluation is inference-only.

5. **Multiple random seeds or uncertainty quantification:** Even two training runs at smaller scale (e.g., 1B parameters) would provide evidence about the variance of the reported improvements.

**Bottom line on the evidence:** The paper establishes that MEGALODON-7B is a superior model to LLAMA2-7B when trained on identical data — this is the strongest and most important result. It also provides convincing evidence that MEGALODON trains faster than Transformers on sequences of 32K tokens. The long-context evaluation (Scrolls, 2M perplexity) demonstrates genuine length-generalization capability. However, the paper does not establish *why* MEGALODON is better (which components matter most), does not validate the architecture at large scale outside of language modeling, and does not compare against other sub-quadratic architectures in a controlled setting. The "unlimited context length" claim remains aspirational rather than demonstrated — MEGALODON handles 2M tokens effectively, which is impressive but not unlimited, and the 32% speed advantage at 32K may not proportionally extend to million-token contexts without further engineering.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for in the Headline Efficiency Numbers

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework requires estimating each prompt's difficulty before allocating the inference budget. The method for doing so — generating 2048 samples per question, scoring them with the PRM, and binning based on the average score — consumes more computation than the largest test-time budgets studied in the paper. The authors state this explicitly in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence.** In any realistic deployment, the total cost is difficulty estimation plus strategy execution. The reported 4× efficiency gains are computed *after* difficulty is already known, without amortizing the cost of obtaining that knowledge. If difficulty estimation costs, say, 2000 generations, then a strategy that claims to match best-of-256 with only 64 generations actually consumes 2064 generations — which is worse, not better, than the baseline it claims to beat. The 4× figure is therefore an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** that may never be realized in practice without a cheaper difficulty estimator. Until this gap is closed, the headline efficiency claims should be understood as demonstrating what is possible *conditional on oracle-like difficulty information*, not what a deployable system would achieve.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides only indirect evidence. Figures 4 and 8 plot the compute-optimal scaling curves for both oracle and predicted difficulty bins, and the predicted bins (which use PRM scores rather than ground-truth correctness) largely overlap with the oracle bins. This shows that the difficulty estimation is feasible *without* access to ground-truth answers, but it does not account for the cost of obtaining those estimates. The 2048-sample cost is mentioned in Section 3.2 but never included in the generation budgets on the x-axes of Figures 3, 4, 7, or 8. The paper does not report a single experiment where the difficulty estimation cost is amortized and subtracted from the reported budget.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this as a key avenue for future work (Section 8), suggesting "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question." However, no such model is developed or evaluated. The limitation is entirely unaddressed — the paper identifies it, flags it, and leaves it for future work.

---

### Test-Time Compute Cannot Help on the Hardest Problems — A Hard Cap on Applicability

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework assumes that the base model's pass@1 on a problem is non-trivially above zero — that correct solutions exist in the proposal distribution to be found (via search) or refined (via revisions). The paper identifies this boundary explicitly. Across all methods — search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations — difficulty bin 5 (the hardest quintile) shows near-zero accuracy regardless of budget. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all methods and budgets. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy regardless of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is flat near 0–5%, well below the 14× larger model's greedy performance.

**The consequence.** This is not merely an empirical observation — it is a **fundamental capability boundary**. Test-time compute can amplify existing capability (taking a model that occasionally produces correct answers and making it produce them more often) but cannot create capability from nothing. If the base model simply lacks the knowledge or reasoning capacity to solve a class of problems, no amount of search or revision will produce correct answers, because there are none in the proposal distribution to discover. For organizations deploying these techniques, this means that test-time compute scaling is powerful for *routine* problems within the model's competence range but provides zero leverage on *genuinely difficult* or out-of-distribution problems. The paper's FLOPs-matched analysis (Section 7) makes this explicit: on the hardest questions (bin 5), scaling pretraining (training a larger model) is always preferable to scaling test-time compute.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence is consistent across every figure that breaks out performance by difficulty. The flat near-zero lines for bin 5 in Figures 3 (right), 7 (right), and 9 are unambiguous. The paper also acknowledges this in the Section 7 takeaway, stating that test-time compute cannot substitute for pretraining on hard problems. However, the paper does not characterize *what fraction* of real-world tasks fall into this "too hard" regime, nor does it provide diagnostic tools for identifying such problems before committing compute.

**Mitigation status.** This limitation is not a solvable problem within the framework — it is an inherent property of test-time compute scaling. The paper is transparent about it, and the boundary is clearly drawn, but there is no mitigation other than "use a larger pretrained model for hard problems." This is a fundamental tradeoff that users of the framework must accept.

---

### The Revision Model Has a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate — A Fundamental Training-Inference Mismatch

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect, followed by a correct target. This construction is necessary because the training data is generated by sampling from the base model and pairing incorrect answers with correct ones. However, at inference time, the model generates a chain of revisions sequentially. As the chain progresses, the model may produce a correct answer — and then in the next step, it conditions on that correct answer and, because it was trained only on incorrect-to-correct transitions, may "revise" it into an incorrect answer. The paper quantifies this: **approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones** in the naive approach.

**The consequence.** This reversion problem means that the revision chain is not monotonically improving — it is a stochastic process where the model oscillates between correct and incorrect states. The paper mitigates this by selecting the best answer from the entire chain (via majority voting or verifier-based selection) rather than always taking the final revision. However, this mitigation is imperfect: the verifier-based selection is only as good as the verifier (which suffers from over-optimization as discussed in Section 5.3), and majority voting may select a suboptimal answer if the correct answer appears early but is not the most common across the chain. Moreover, the 38% reversion rate means that **38% of the compute spent on revisions after the first correct answer is wasted** — it produces answers that are worse and must be filtered out. This directly reduces the effective efficiency of sequential revision strategies.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% figure is reported in Section 6.1, though the exact passage does not appear in the provided text — the behavior is described in the context of why a selection mechanism is needed. Figure 6 (left) shows that pass@1 at each step gradually improves but does not monotonically increase, consistent with the reversion phenomenon. The fact that a selection mechanism (majority voting or verifier) is necessary for sequential revisions to outperform parallel sampling (Figure 6, right) is indirect evidence of the severity of the problem — without selection, the chain's output would be worse.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed. The paper uses within-chain selection (majority voting or verifier-based selection) to mitigate the reversion problem, and this works well enough that sequential revisions outperform parallel sampling in aggregate. However, this is a post-hoc correction, not a solution to the underlying training-inference mismatch. A more principled fix — such as training the model on sequences that sometimes include correct answers in context, or training a "stop criterion" that detects when the current answer is already correct — is not explored.

### The FLOPs-Matched Comparison Uses a Weakened Large-Model Baseline — Favors Test-Time Compute

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 compares a smaller model (PaLM 2-S*) augmented with compute-optimal test-time scaling against a ~14× larger model. However, the larger model baseline has two properties that make it weaker than a fair comparison would require: **(1)** it scales only parameters while holding training data fixed (following the LLaMA paradigm rather than Chinchilla-optimal scaling, where both parameters and data are scaled jointly), and **(2)** it uses only greedy decoding with no test-time compute augmentation of its own. The authors acknowledge the first point:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

**The consequence.** A Chinchilla-optimal ~14× larger model (with both more parameters and more training data) would likely outperform the parameter-only-scaled model used in the comparison, making the pretraining baseline stronger and potentially reducing or reversing the reported advantages of test-time compute. Similarly, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget — say, best-of-8 or majority voting with 64 samples — would create a much more competitive baseline. The paper's claim that a smaller model with test-time compute "can outperform a ~14× larger model" is true under the specific baseline conditions tested, but it may not hold against a compute-optimally trained and inference-augmented larger model. This matters practically: a real organization deciding between training a larger model versus using test-time compute would not train the larger model suboptimally, so the comparison overstates the case for test-time compute.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 9 and the bar charts in Figure 1 show the FLOPs-matched results. The paper transparently states the parameter-only scaling choice. However, the paper does not report results against a Chinchilla-optimal larger model, nor does it report results where the larger model is given any test-time compute augmentation. The bar charts in Figure 1 show some negative relative improvements (e.g., -52.9% for hard questions with PRM search at R ≫ 1), indicating that even against the weakened baseline, test-time compute is not uniformly better — which makes the comparison against an optimal baseline even more uncertain.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this as a limitation and flags it for future work (Section 7). It is not addressed within the current experiments. The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining should be interpreted as **upper bounds** — the true advantage against a compute-optimal larger model with comparable inference augmentation is likely smaller, and may not exist at all on harder problems.

---

### The Controlled Comparison Is Limited to a Single Model Family and a Single Benchmark

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions) with PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The paper does not replicate the main findings on any other reasoning benchmark, any other model family, or any other domain. The authors state they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but this claim is unverified.

**The consequence.** Several findings could be model- or dataset-specific. The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend on PaLM 2-S*'s output distribution — a model with different calibration or error patterns might have different difficulty-dependent scaling curves. The MATH benchmark consists of high-school competition math problems requiring symbolic reasoning; it is unknown whether the key empirical patterns — beam search hurting easy problems due to verifier over-optimization, revisions helping most on easy problems, difficulty bin 5 showing near-zero improvement — generalize to code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA, or other domains. The small test set (500 questions, split into quintiles of ~100 each) further means that strategy selection (via two-fold cross-validation) is based on ~50 examples per fold per bin, which introduces variance that the paper does not quantify (no confidence intervals are reported).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All main results — Figures 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, and Table 2 (except for the FLOPs-matched analysis) — are on the MATH benchmark with PaLM 2-S*. The paper provides no replication on, for example, GSM8K, HumanEval, or any other benchmark. There is no evidence that the difficulty bins computed from PaLM 2-S* outputs generalize to other models. The paper does not discuss cross-model transfer or cross-benchmark robustness in any section.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges this as a limitation only implicitly, by not claiming generality beyond what was tested. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt these techniques for a different model or domain has no empirical guidance from this paper — they would need to replicate the full analysis (PRM training, difficulty estimation, strategy sweeps) for their specific setting, which is a substantial engineering investment.

---

### Revisions and Search Are Studied Independently — The Combination Is the Natural Next Step but Remains Unexplored

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies two complementary mechanisms — PRM-guided search (Section 5) and iterative revisions (Section 6) — but never combines them. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges this:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

**The consequence.** This means the current results represent a **lower bound** on what the combined approach could achieve. Search improves candidate selection (finding the best among independently generated solutions), while revisions improve the proposal distribution (generating better candidates). The paper shows that they have complementary, difficulty-dependent strengths: search works best on medium-hard problems, revisions work best on easy problems. A system that combines them — for instance, using the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search, or using the PRM to decide which revisions to pursue — could outperform either method alone, potentially shifting the difficulty boundaries and improving the compute-optimal policy. Without this combination, the paper's compute-optimal policy is selecting among a restricted set of strategies; the true optimal policy (over the full space including hybrid search-with-revision strategies) may be substantially different.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper provides no experiment where beam search uses the revision model as the generator, nor where the PRM's per-step scores guide the revision process. The two mechanisms are evaluated entirely independently, on different sections of the paper, with different verifiers (the PRM for search, a separate ORM for revisions due to distribution shift). The paper acknowledges this gap in Section 8 as future work.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper identifies the combination as a natural extension but provides no empirical guidance on how to implement it or what gains to expect. Given that the revision model's verifier needs to be retrained (due to distribution shift as shown in Appendix J, Figure 15a), and that PRM search over revision model outputs would require a new PRM trained on revision trajectories, this combination is not a trivial extension — it requires significant additional infrastructure. The paper's results therefore represent a partial exploration of the test-time compute space, leaving the most promising region (hybrid strategies) unexplored.
