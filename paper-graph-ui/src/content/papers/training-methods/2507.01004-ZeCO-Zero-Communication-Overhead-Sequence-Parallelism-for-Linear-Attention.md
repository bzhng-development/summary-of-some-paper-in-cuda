# ZeCO: Zero Communication Overhead Sequence Parallelism for Linear Attention

**ArXiv:** [2507.01004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01004)

## 🎯 Pitch

Training linear attention models on million-token sequences can now scale almost perfectly across hundreds of GPUs, because ZeCO’s new communication primitive entirely eliminates the overhead that made existing parallelism methods stall—achieving a 60% speedup on 256 devices by slashing extra runtime by 9.3×.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **ZeCO (Zero Communication Overhead)** sequence parallelism, a new distributed training method for linear attention models that eliminates the communication bottleneck plaguing existing sequence parallelism approaches. The core innovation is **All-Scan**, a novel collective communication primitive that uses a pipelined receive-scan-send pattern to deliver each device precisely the minimal state tensor it needs (a single accumulated attention state of size $d_k \times d_v$), achieving the theoretically minimum communication volume — in contrast to prior work like LASP-2, whose communication volume scales linearly with device count $P$. Evaluated on 1B-parameter Gated Linear Attention (GLA) models across up to 256 H100 GPUs with sequence lengths up to 8M tokens, ZeCO achieves a 60% throughput improvement over the state-of-the-art and maintains near-linear scaling from 8 to 256 devices, establishing that sequence parallelism for linear attention can approach the ideal throughput ceiling when — and only when — communication is reduced to its information-theoretic lower bound and fully overlapped with local computation.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Sequence Parallelism Is Bottlenecked by Communication

The paper addresses a specific, concrete problem in distributed training of large language models: **when training linear attention models on ultra-long sequences using sequence parallelism (SP), the communication overhead of existing SP methods becomes so severe that it destroys scalability, wasting the very computational efficiency that linear attention is designed to provide.** This is a system-level bottleneck, not an algorithmic one — the linear attention computation itself scales well, but the way we distribute it across devices does not.

To appreciate why this matters, consider the trajectory the paper outlines in Section 1. Standard self-attention mechanisms exhibit $O(L^2)$ complexity with respect to sequence length $L$. Scaling from a 4K context to a 128K context inflates attention FLOPs by more than 1000×. This quadratic wall means that for ultra-long sequences — say, 1M tokens as in Gemini 1.5 Pro — full attention training becomes computationally prohibitive regardless of how many GPUs you throw at it. The total work simply grows too fast.

Linear attention models (Katharopoulos et al., 2020) offer an escape from this quadratic trap. By removing the softmax and rearranging the computation order, they reduce complexity to $O(Ld^2)$ where $d$ is the hidden dimension — effectively linear in sequence length, since $d$ is typically fixed. The mechanism compresses the Key-Value (KV) cache that would normally grow with $L$ into a fixed-size hidden state representation. This is not a small improvement: it fundamentally changes the scaling regime from one where sequence length dominates FLOPs to one where hidden dimension (a constant) dominates.

**But here is the catch, and it is the motivation for this entire paper:** once you eliminate the $O(L^2)$ computational bottleneck, the next bottleneck emerges — communication between devices in sequence parallelism. The paper states this starkly:

> "While linear attention provides these algorithmic advantages, Sequence Parallelism (SP), essential for distributing such computationally intensive workloads, paradoxically becomes a bottleneck that impedes efficient scaling across multiple devices."

The word "paradoxically" is carefully chosen. Linear attention was supposed to make long sequences tractable. And computationally, it does. But when you actually try to train a model on a 1M-token sequence across many GPUs, the SP communication overhead can become *more expensive than the attention computation itself*. You have traded one bottleneck (quadratic FLOPs) for another (communication volume that scales with device count). This is the central gap the paper addresses.

### Why This Problem Matters: The Gap Between Algorithmic Promise and System Reality

The importance of this problem can be understood along three dimensions: practical, economic, and scientific.

**Practical importance: long-context training remains restricted.** The paper documents how the prohibitive cost of training on ultra-long sequences has restricted long-context pretraining to a specialized adaptation phase called "mid-training" (Abdin et al., 2024). Models are pretrained on short sequences (e.g., 4K or 8K tokens) and then fine-tuned or adapted to handle longer contexts, rather than being trained from scratch with long sequences. This is a fundamental limitation — the model's long-context capabilities are effectively bolted on after the fact, rather than being a native property of the pretraining process. The authors cite several major model families — Llama 3 (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Qwen (Qwen et al., 2025), DeepSeek-V2 and V3 (Liu et al., 2024), Gemma (Gemma Team et al., 2025) — all of which follow this two-phase approach.

If sequence parallelism could be made genuinely efficient (communication overhead near zero), the entire paradigm could shift: pretraining from scratch on long sequences would become economically viable, which could fundamentally change what models learn about long-range dependencies, document structure, and multi-step reasoning. This is not a small incremental improvement — it is the difference between long-context being an afterthought and being a first-class citizen of pretraining.

**Economic importance: wasted GPU hours.** When communication dominates runtime, GPUs sit idle waiting for data. The paper's empirical results make this concrete: on 256 GPUs with an 8M sequence length, LASP-2 (the prior state-of-the-art SP method) achieves only ~25K tokens per second per GPU, while ZeCO achieves ~41K tokens per second per GPU — a 60% improvement (Figure 1). For an organization running thousands of GPUs for weeks or months of training, a 60% throughput improvement translates to enormous cost savings or, equivalently, the ability to train larger models or longer sequences within the same budget.

**Scientific/theoretical importance: establishing the lower bound.** Beyond the practical gains, the paper identifies a fundamental question: what is the *minimum possible* communication overhead for sequence parallelism with linear attention? The answer, as they prove in Section 3.3, is that each device must send and receive exactly one state tensor $S \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k \times d_v}$ — no more, no less. This is an information-theoretic lower bound that arises from the structure of the linear attention recurrence itself. By achieving this lower bound, ZeCO is not just "better than existing methods" — it is *optimal* in a provable sense. This reframes the problem from "how can we reduce communication?" to "the communication lower bound is $d_k d_v$; can we actually achieve it in practice while covering the cost through overlap?" This is a cleaner, more principled question.

### The Shortcomings of Existing Sequence Parallelism Methods

The paper identifies two prior approaches to sequence parallelism for linear attention, both of which fail to achieve this lower bound for fundamentally different reasons.

**LASP-1: Serial Execution Destroys Parallel Efficiency**

LASP-1 (Linear Attention Sequence Parallelism; Sun, Qin, et al., 2025) takes the conceptually simplest approach: divide the input sequence into contiguous chunks, distribute them across devices, and have each device compute its chunk's output using the state passed from the previous device. The communication pattern is simple peer-to-peer (P2P): device $p-1$ sends its final state to device $p$, device $p$ uses it as its initial state, computes its chunk, then passes its final state to device $p+1$.

The problem is **serial dependency**. Device $p$ cannot begin computing until device $p-1$ finishes and sends its state. This means total computation latency grows linearly with the number of devices $P$. If processing one chunk takes time $T_{\text{comp}}$, then the total time with $P$ devices is approximately $P \times T_{\text{comp}}$. You are using $P$ devices but getting no parallel speedup — they execute sequentially, like an assembly line where each station waits for the previous one to finish the entire batch before it can start. The paper's theoretical analysis in Equation (15) formalizes this: $T^P_{\text{LASP}}(PL) = P \times (T^1_{\text{ideal-SP}}(L) + \tau(d_k \times d_v))$, where the $P$ multiplier outside the parentheses captures the serial bottleneck. LASP-1 has minimal communication volume (one state tensor per device pair), but its scheduling is fundamentally broken for parallel execution.

**LASP-2: Communication Volume Scales Linearly with Device Count**

LASP-2 (Sun, Lan, et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025) fixes the serial execution problem but introduces a new one. Instead of sequential P2P, LASP-2 uses an **All-Gather** operation: every device broadcasts its locally computed intermediate state tensor to *all other devices*, and then each device independently performs a parallel scan over all $P$ states to compute globally consistent results. This enables full computational parallelism — every device can compute simultaneously.

But the communication cost explodes. Each device must receive $P - 1$ state tensors from other devices (and send its own), for a total communication volume of $(P - 1) \times d_k \times d_v$ per device. As $P$ grows, this volume grows linearly. On 256 GPUs, each device is collecting 255 other states — the communication burden is enormous. Equation (16) formalizes: $T^P_{\text{LASP-2}}(PL) = T^1_{\text{ideal-SP}}(L) + P \times \tau(d_k \times d_v)$, where the $P \times \tau(\cdot)$ term is the All-Gather cost. Figure 3 (right) shows the empirical consequence: on 256 GPUs, the All-Gather used by LASP-2 takes ~8.5 ms, while All-Scan takes ~2.2 ms — a nearly 4× slowdown.

The paper notes a further practical problem: at 128 and 256 GPUs, the All-Gather (Megatron-style) implementation actually runs out of memory ("memory-out occurred in the experiments of 128 GPUs and 256 GPUs All-gather (Megatron)"). This is because holding $P$ copies of the state tensor on each device consumes substantial HBM, and for large $P$ this becomes infeasible.

**The fundamental tension.** LASP-1 has minimal communication volume but serial execution. LASP-2 has parallel execution but communication volume proportional to $P$. Neither achieves the combination of (a) communication volume at the information-theoretic lower bound ($d_k \times d_v$) and (b) parallel computation. This tension is the gap ZeCO fills.

### Why Full Attention SP Methods Don't Transfer

The paper also briefly addresses sequence parallelism methods designed for full (quadratic) attention, such as Megatron-SP/Context Parallelism (Shoeybi et al., 2020), Ring Attention (Liu et al., 2023; Brandon et al., 2023), and Ulysses (Jacobs et al., 2023). These methods synchronize Key-Value (KV) blocks across devices using All-Gather or ring-based P2P communication. But they face a more fundamental problem:

> "sequence parallelism for full attention is fundamentally constrained by the self-attention algorithm itself: even disregarding communication, the computational cost becomes prohibitively expensive for ultra-long sequences due to the algorithm's inherent complexity."

In other words, full attention SP methods solve a communication problem that is secondary to the computational problem. Even if you could perfectly parallelize full attention with zero communication overhead, the $O(L^2)$ FLOPs would still make training on 1M-token sequences impractical. Linear attention solves the computational problem, which then exposes the communication problem as the new bottleneck. The communication patterns from full attention SP don't directly apply because the data being communicated is different: for linear attention, you communicate fixed-size state tensors rather than variable-length KV caches.

Ulysses (Jacobs et al., 2023) distributes attention head computation across devices rather than sequence chunks, which is orthogonal but limited — it is incompatible with tensor parallelism (TP) and constrained by the number of attention heads (you can't use more GPUs than you have heads). For a model with 32 heads on 256 GPUs, Ulysses simply cannot distribute the workload effectively.

### How ZeCO Positions Itself

The paper positions ZeCO not as an incremental improvement over LASP-1 or LASP-2, but as a **provably optimal solution** that achieves the best of both worlds: minimum communication volume AND parallel computation AND communication-computation overlap. The key enabling mechanism is All-Scan, which replaces the P2P serial chain (LASP-1) and the All-Gather broadcast (LASP-2) with a pipelined scan operation.

The pipeline is the crucial insight. Rather than sending an entire state tensor S as one monolithic message (which creates long idle periods), All-Scan splits $S$ into $K$ smaller segments along the hidden dimension and pipelines them: device $p-1$ sends segment 1 to device $p$, which immediately starts processing it and forwarding it to device $p+1$, while device $p-1$ simultaneously sends segment 2. This achieves three things simultaneously:

1. **Communication volume stays at the lower bound:** each device sends/receives exactly one state tensor, not $P$ copies.
2. **Sequential dependency is amortized:** device $p+1$ starts receiving data from device $p$ long before device $p$ finishes processing its full chunk, breaking the strict serial ordering of LASP-1.
3. **Communication overlaps with local computation:** while the pipeline is flowing, each device can compute its local diagonal attention (the intra-chunk term $O^{\text{intra}}_{[i]}$ in Equation 3) in a separate CUDA stream, hiding the communication latency behind useful work.

The paper frames this as achieving the "ideal sequence parallelism" defined in Equation (10): $T^P_{\text{ideal-sp}}(PL) = T^1_{\text{ideal-SP}}(L) = T^1_{\text{ideal-SP}}(PL)/P$, meaning throughput scales linearly with device count. ZeCO's actual runtime, derived in Equation (14), approaches this ideal bound: $T^P_{\text{ZeCO}}(PL) \approx T^1_{\text{ideal-SP}}(L) - T_{\text{overlaped\_comp}} + \tau(d_k \times d_v)$, where the communication term $\tau(d_k \times d_v)$ is the minimal possible, the overlap term subtracts hidden latency, and all other overheads ($\epsilon$) are shown to be negligible (less than 1% of computation).

The paper's title — "Zero Communication Overhead" — is deliberately provocative. It doesn't mean zero communication bytes (that would violate the information-theoretic lower bound). It means zero *visible* communication overhead from the perspective of total runtime, because the communication is both minimized to the theoretical minimum and hidden behind computation. This is the bar the paper sets for itself, and the empirical results in Figure 4 — where ZeCO's operator runtime stays within 3 ms of the theoretical lower bound (data parallelism baseline) even at 128 GPUs — are the evidence that this bar is met.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

ZeCO is a **distributed training algorithm** — specifically a sequence parallelism strategy — that distributes the computation of a single ultra-long input sequence across many GPUs for linear attention models. The problem it solves is that existing sequence parallelism methods force you to choose between two bad options: minimal communication but serial execution (LASP-1), or parallel execution but communication volume that grows linearly with the number of devices (LASP-2). The shape of the solution is a **pipelined scan primitive** called All-Scan that achieves the best of both options: it communicates only the theoretical minimum information (one state tensor per device pair) while using fine-grained pipeline parallelism to hide even that minimal communication behind useful local computation, yielding end-to-end runtime that closely tracks the ideal of perfect linear scaling.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The ZeCO system has four tightly integrated components:

1. **Chunk-wise Linear Attention Operator** — the standard Gated Linear Attention (GLA) computation, split into local recurrence (computing per-chunk hidden states independently) and global correction (adjusting those local states using the accumulated state from all prior chunks). This is the base computation that ZeCO parallelises.

2. **All-Scan Collective Communication Primitive** — a novel pipelined communication operator that propagates a cumulative scan (a "running state") across devices in a ring, splitting large state tensors into $K$ smaller segments that are transmitted, received, updated, and forwarded in an overlapped pipeline. This replaces both the serial P2P of LASP-1 and the All-Gather of LASP-2.

3. **Communication-Computation Scheduler** — a two-stream execution strategy that places All-Scan communication on one CUDA stream while simultaneously executing the local intra-chunk diagonal attention computation (which has no dependency on the communicated state) on a parallel stream. A stream barrier synchronises the two before the final output assembly.

4. **Auxiliary State Bookkeeping** — lightweight additional tensors (cumulative decay vectors $\tilde{\gamma}_{[n]}$ and per-chunk local states $S_{[n]}$) that are stored to HBM and reused across multiple computation steps. These enable the global state update formula and are engineered to impose negligible I/O overhead (less than 1% of total tensor volume).

**Information flow**: The input sequence of length $PL$ (total across $P$ devices) is projected into queries $Q$, keys $K$, values $V$, and decay factors $G$. Each device receives a contiguous chunk of length $L$ (subdivided into $N$ sub-chunks of length $C$). First, each device computes its local chunk states $S_{[0]}, S_{[1]}, \ldots, S_{[N]}$ via recurrent scanning (Equation 4), producing a chain of intermediate states that capture intra-device dependencies. Simultaneously on a second stream, the device computes the intra-chunk attention scores $P_{[n]} = (\tilde{Q}_{[n]}\tilde{K}_{[n]}^\top) \odot M$ which depend only on the local chunk data. When the local recurrence completes, the final local state $S_{[N]}$ enters the All-Scan pipeline, which propagates global state corrections device-to-device. When All-Scan delivers the initial global state $S_{(p-1)L}$ from the previous device, the output stream assembles the final output $O_{[n]}$ by fusing the inter-chunk term (using corrected global state) with the pre-computed intra-chunk term $P_{[n]}V_{[n]}$.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the local state computation** (Equation 4) and its closed-form unfolding — because understanding what is computed locally before any communication is prerequisite to understanding what must be communicated and why the global update formula (Equation 6) takes its specific form.

- **Second, the global state update decomposition** (Equation 6 and Appendix A.1 proof) — because this is the mathematical insight that enables the entire approach: that the global state can be expressed as an attenuated copy of the incoming state plus the purely local contribution, which is what makes the scan operation distributable across devices.

- **Third, the All-Scan collective communication primitive** — its pipelining mechanism, the tensor partitioning strategy, how it achieves the $O(P/K)$ scaling of boundary overhead, and why it is provably optimal in communication volume for linear attention SP.

- **Fourth, the two-stream scheduling and overlap strategy** — how ZeCO identifies computation that is independent of the communication (intra-chunk diagonal attention) and schedules it concurrently, and what the stream barrier synchronisation point guarantees.

- **Fifth, the extra computation and I/O cost analysis** — a rigorous accounting of every additional operation ZeCO introduces beyond the base GLA algorithm, establishing that the total overhead is negligible (sub-1%) and therefore ZeCO truly approaches the optimal time bound.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **systems paper** whose core idea is that sequence parallelism for linear attention can be made provably optimal — achieving the theoretical minimum communication volume while fully overlapping that communication with computation — by replacing monolithic state transfers with a pipelined scan primitive (All-Scan) and restructuring the linear attention computation to expose maximal parallelism between local work and global synchronisation.

#### The Local State Computation (Pre-Communication Phase)

Before any inter-device communication occurs, each device must compute what the paper calls **local chunk states** — the hidden states that would result from processing its assigned sequence segment starting from a zero initial state. This local computation is the foundation on which the subsequent global correction is built.

The computation follows directly from the chunk-wise formulation of Gated Linear Attention (GLA) established in Section 2.1. For a single sequence of length $L$ assigned to one device, the tokens are partitioned into $N = L/C$ chunks, where $C$ is the chunk size. For each chunk indexed from $n = 1$ to $N$, the local recurrence is:

$$S_{[n]} = (\gamma^\top_{[n]} \mathbf{1}) \odot S_{[n-1]} + \tilde{K}^\top_{[n]} V_{[n]}$$

where $S_{[n]} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k \times d_v}$ is the hidden state after processing the $n$-th chunk (initialised as $S_{[0]} = \mathbf{0}$), $\gamma_{[n]} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$ is the cumulative decay factor for chunk $n$ (the product $\prod_{j=1}^{C} \alpha_{iC+j}$ of per-token decay vectors within the chunk), $\mathbf{1} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_v}$ is a vector of ones used to broadcast the decay across the value dimension, $\tilde{K}_{[n]} = K_{[n]} \odot \Gamma_{[n]} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times d_k}$ is the key matrix for chunk $n$ scaled element-wise by the token-wise cumulative decay factor $\Gamma_{[n]}$, $V_{[n]} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times d_v}$ is the value matrix for chunk $n$, and $\odot$ denotes the Hadamard (element-wise) product.

**What it computes:** This recurrence processes each chunk sequentially. At chunk $n$, the previous hidden state $S_{[n-1]}$ is first attenuated by $\gamma^\top_{[n]} \mathbf{1}$ — this is an outer product that scales each row of $S_{[n-1]}$ by the corresponding decay factor in $\gamma_{[n]}$, implementing the "forgetting" of old information. Then the new information $\tilde{K}^\top_{[n]} V_{[n]}$ from the current chunk is added — this is the outer product of the (decay-scaled) key and value embeddings, compressing the chunk's token-level interactions into the fixed-size state matrix. The result $S_{[n]}$ is a state that remembers information from chunks $1$ through $n$, with older chunks decayed multiplicatively by all intervening $\gamma$ factors.

**Why this form:** The recurrence is mathematically equivalent to the per-token recurrence in Equation (1), but computing it chunk-by-chunk rather than token-by-token is essential for hardware efficiency. Chunk-wise computation enables the use of matrix-matrix multiplications (GEMMs) rather than sequences of vector outer products, which are far more efficient on GPUs. The chunk size $C$ thus represents a trade-off: larger $C$ means better GEMM utilisation but coarser granularity for any sequential dependencies; $C=64$ is used throughout the experiments as a standard choice from the Flash Linear Attention library.

**Cumulative decay maintenance.** An important detail that distinguishes ZeCO's local computation from the standard GLA formulation is the maintenance of an auxiliary tensor $\tilde{\gamma}_{[n]}$, the cumulative decay from the first chunk through the $n$-th chunk:

$$\tilde{\gamma}_{[n]} = \prod_{i=0}^{n} \gamma_{[i]}, \quad \text{for } n = 1, \ldots, N$$

where $\gamma_{[0]}$ is initialised to $\mathbf{1}$ (a vector of ones, representing no decay before any chunks are processed). This vector $\tilde{\gamma}_{[n]} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$ is the per-dimension product of all per-chunk decay factors up to chunk $n$. It is stored to HBM at each step (Algorithm 1, line 9) because it will be needed later during the global state update to determine how strongly the incoming global state should be attenuated before being added to each local state.

**Closed-form unfolding.** Although the recurrence in Equation (4) is how computation proceeds sequentially, the closed-form expression (provided in Appendix A.1, Equation 19) reveals the structure that makes the subsequent global update possible:

$$S_{[n]} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( \prod_{j=i+1}^{n} \gamma^\top_{[j]} \mathbf{1} \right) \odot \left( \tilde{K}^\top_{[i]} V_{[i]} \right) + \left( \prod_{j=1}^{n} \gamma^\top_{[j]} \mathbf{1} \right) \odot S_{[0]}$$

Since $S_{[0]} = \mathbf{0}$ by initialisation, the second term vanishes, and $S_{[n]}$ is purely a sum of contributions from chunks $1$ through $n$, each attenuated by the decay factors of all chunks between that contribution and the current position.

**Key insight:** $S_{[n]}$ represents the state that would result from processing chunks $1$ through $n$ with no prior context (zero initial state). When ZeCO later needs to compute the *actual* global state — which includes contributions from all chunks on all earlier devices — it can decompose this global state into the local contribution $S_{[n]}$ plus an attenuated copy of the incoming state from the previous device. This decomposition is what makes All-Scan work.

#### The Global State Update: Why Only One State Tensor Needs to Be Communicated

The fundamental mathematical insight that enables ZeCO's minimal communication is that **a sequence of hidden states starting from a non-zero initial state can be decomposed into the sum of two independent terms**: (1) the result of the recurrence starting from zero (which is precisely what the local computation produces), and (2) the incoming initial state attenuated by the cumulative decay. This linearity property is proven in Appendix A.1.

Suppose device $p$ receives the final global state $S_{(p-1)L}$ from device $p-1$. The question is: how should device $p$ compute the global state at an arbitrary position $(p-1)L + nC$ (for chunk $n$ within device $p$'s assigned segment)? The answer is the update formula in Equation (6) of the main text:

$$S_{(p-1)L + nC} = (\tilde{\gamma}^\top_{[n]} \mathbf{1}) \odot S_{(p-1)L} + S_{[n]}$$

where $S_{(p-1)L}$ is the incoming global state from the previous device, $\tilde{\gamma}_{[n]}$ is the cumulative decay vector from chunk $1$ to chunk $n$ of the *current* device (computed during local recurrence), and $S_{[n]}$ is the local chunk state computed during local recurrence starting from zero.

**What it computes:** The global state at position $(p-1)L + nC$ is the sum of two components: (1) the incoming state $S_{(p-1)L}$ scaled by $\tilde{\gamma}^\top_{[n]} \mathbf{1}$, which applies all the decay that has accumulated over the first $n$ chunks of device $p$'s segment (essentially, "how much of the old information from devices $0$ through $p-1$ has survived to this point"), and (2) the purely local contribution $S_{[n]}$ from processing chunks $1$ through $n$ of device $p$'s segment (which includes no information from prior devices). Because of the linearity of the recurrence, these two terms are additive and independent.

**Why this form matters — the proof of linearity.** The appendix (Section A.1) provides a short but crucial proof. The recurrence in Equation (4), when unfolded starting from a non-zero initial state $S_{(p-1)L}$ instead of zero, yields:

$$S_{(p-1)L + nC} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( \prod_{j=i+1}^{n} \gamma^\top_{[j]} \mathbf{1} \right) \odot \left( \tilde{K}^\top_{[i]} V_{[i]} \right) + \left( \prod_{j=1}^{n} \gamma^\top_{[j]} \mathbf{1} \right) \odot S_{(p-1)L}$$

The first sum is exactly $S_{[n]}$ (the local state from zero initialisation), and the product in the second term is exactly $\tilde{\gamma}_{[n]}$. So the decomposition $S_{(p-1)L + nC} = (\tilde{\gamma}^\top_{[n]} \mathbf{1}) \odot S_{(p-1)L} + S_{[n]}$ holds exactly. No approximation; no information loss. This linear decomposition is what makes it unnecessary for device $p$ to know anything about individual chunks on device $p-1$ — the entire history of devices $0$ through $p-1$ is compressed into the single state tensor $S_{(p-1)L}$, and device $p$ only needs this one tensor plus its own locally computed $S_{[n]}$ and $\tilde{\gamma}_{[n]}$ to reconstruct the exact global state at any position.

**The implication for communication:** Since each device only needs its predecessor's final state $S_{(p-1)L}$ (and no intermediate states, and no states from devices further back in the chain), the *minimum possible* communication per device pair is exactly one state tensor of size $d_k \times d_v$. Any communication beyond this is redundant. LASP-2 communicates $P$ times this amount because it broadcasts every device's local state to every other device. LASP-1 communicates exactly this amount but does so in a strict serial chain. ZeCO also communicates exactly this amount, but uses pipelining to avoid serial waits.

**An important detail about independence.** The update formula reveals that once device $p$ has received $S_{(p-1)L}$, it can compute $S_{(p-1)L + nC}$ for *any* chunk $n$ independently — the computation for chunk $n$ does not depend on the global state for chunk $n-1$ or any other chunk. This is because both terms ($S_{(p-1)L}$ and $S_{[n]}$) are already fully known once the local recurrence completes. Paper Algorithm 1 exploits this by parallelising the final output computation across all chunks on the device (the `parfor` loop in lines 25–32 processes each chunk independently).

#### All-Scan: The Pipelined Collective Communication Primitive

All-Scan is the novel communication operator that implements the minimal-volume state propagation efficiently. Rather than sending the full state tensor $S \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k \times d_v}$ as one monolithic message (which would create a long idle period between when device $p$ receives the state and when device $p+1$ can begin), All-Scan **partitions the state tensor into $K$ smaller segments along the $d_k$ dimension** and pipelines them through the device chain.

**Tensor partitioning.** The state tensor $S_{(p-1)L} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k \times d_v}$ is split along the first dimension (the $d_k$ key/decay dimension) into $K$ contiguous blocks:

$$S_{(p-1)L} = \left[ S^{(1)}_{(p-1)L}, S^{(2)}_{(p-1)L}, \ldots, S^{(K)}_{(p-1)L} \right], \quad S^{(k)}_{(p-1)L} \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{d_k}{K} \times d_v}$$

Correspondingly, the cumulative decay vector $\tilde{\gamma}_{[N]}$ is split into $K$ aligned segments:

$$\tilde{\gamma}^{(k)}_{[N]} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times \frac{d_k}{K}}$$

Each segment $S^{(k)}_{(p-1)L}$ represents the accumulated attention state for a subset of the $d_k$ hidden dimensions, and the corresponding $\tilde{\gamma}^{(k)}_{[N]}$ contains the decay factors for those same dimensions.

**The pipelined update.** For each segment $k = 0, \ldots, K-1$, the computation that occurs on device $p$ (when it is an intermediate device, not the first or last) is:

$$S^{(k)}_{pL} = \left( \tilde{\gamma}^{(k)\top}_{[N]} \mathbf{1} \right) \odot S^{(k)}_{(p-1)L} + S^{(k)}_{[N]}$$

where $S^{(k)}_{pL}$ is the $k$-th segment of device $p$'s final global state (which will be sent to device $p+1$), $S^{(k)}_{(p-1)L}$ is the $k$-th segment received from device $p-1$, and $S^{(k)}_{[N]}$ is the $k$-th segment of device $p$'s own final local state (computed during the local recurrence phase). This is the segment-wise version of Equation (6) applied specifically at the device boundary ($n = N$, the last chunk).

**The pipeline in operation.** The paper's Algorithm 2 describes the full All-Scan procedure. The key operational sequence for a device $p$ (neither the first nor the last in the chain, for the forward pass) is:

1. **Receive** the first segment $S^{(0)}_{(p-1)L}$ from device $p-1$ (or wait for it to arrive if it has not yet been sent).
2. **Update** this segment using the formula above to produce $S^{(0)}_{pL}$.
3. **Immediately send** $S^{(0)}_{pL}$ to device $p+1$ — device $p+1$ can begin its own update as soon as this first segment arrives, even though device $p$ is still processing later segments.
4. **Proceed** to segment $k=1$: receive $S^{(1)}_{(p-1)L}$, update, send $S^{(1)}_{pL}$ to device $p+1$.
5. **Repeat** for all $K$ segments.

The crucial property: device $p+1$ does not need to wait for device $p$ to finish processing *all* $K$ segments. It can start its own pipeline as soon as it receives $S^{(0)}_{pL}$. This breaks the strict serial dependency of LASP-1 — the dependency is now on individual segments, not on the entire state tensor. The total pipeline latency is dominated by the time to transmit and process one segment plus the fill/drain overhead at the pipeline boundaries, not by $P$ times the full state transmission time.

**Direction parameterisation (forward and backward).** Algorithm 2 supports both forward (`DIR == FWD`) and backward (`DIR == BWD`) passes by reversing the send and receive ranks. For the forward pass, data flows from device $0$ to device $P-1$ (`send_rank = p + 1`, `recv_rank = p - 1`). For the backward pass, the gradient of the global state propagates in the opposite direction, from device $P-1$ back to device $0$ (`send_rank = p - 1`, `recv_rank = p + 1`). The same pipelined segment-wise pattern applies in both directions, with the backward pass processing the gradient tensor $dS$ through an analogous scan.

**Communication volume analysis.** As established in Equation (9), the total communication volume per device in All-Scan is $|\mathcal{S}| = d_k \times d_v$ — exactly one state tensor, regardless of the number of devices $P$. This is the information-theoretic lower bound: device $p$ can only compute its outputs if it knows the state accumulated across all previous devices, and that accumulated state is fully summarised in $S_{(p-1)L}$. There is no way to transmit less information and still produce correct results.

In contrast, LASP-2's All-Gather requires each device to receive $P-1$ state tensors, for total volume $(P-1) \times d_k \times d_v$, which grows linearly with $P$ and quickly dominates. The paper's Figure 3 (left two panels) visualises this difference: ZeCO's communication volume is a flat line independent of device count, while LASP-2's volume grows proportionally to $P$. At 256 GPUs, ZeCO communicates 256× less state tensor data than LASP-2.

**Latency analysis and the role of $K$.** The effective communication latency of All-Scan is derived in Equation (12):

$$T_{\text{All\_Scan}} = \tau(d_k \times d_v) + (P - 1) \frac{\tau(d_k \times d_v)}{K}$$

where $\tau(\cdot)$ denotes the transmission time for a tensor of given size, $P$ is the number of devices, and $K$ is the number of segments.

**What it computes:** The first term $\tau(d_k \times d_v)$ is unavoidable — it represents the time for one full state tensor to traverse one hop in the chain, which is the minimum possible communication cost. The second term $(P-1)\tau(d_k \times d_v)/K$ represents the additional latency due to pipeline boundaries: the fill time (first segment propagating through the chain) and drain time (last segment propagating through the chain). As $K$ increases, this boundary overhead decreases proportionally — the segments are smaller, so the fill/drain time is shorter.

**Why this form:** When $K=1$ (no segmentation, monolithic transfer), All-Scan degenerates to serial P2P communication like LASP-1, and the latency is $P \times \tau(d_k \times d_v)$ (each device waits for the full tensor, processes it, then the next device does the same — $P$ sequential hops). When $K \to \infty$, the boundary overhead approaches zero, and the total latency approaches $\tau(d_k \times d_v)$ — effectively a single-hop cost, as if all devices received the state simultaneously. In practice, $K$ is set to make each segment equal to the communication block size (in the experiments, "the tensor size of each chunk of segmentation is 16384"), which provides a practical balance between pipeline depth and segment processing overhead.

**The "zero overhead" claim in latency terms.** The paper's claim of "zero communication overhead" is operationalised through this latency decomposition. Since $\tau(d_k \times d_v)$ is the theoretical minimum (you must communicate at least this much), and the $(P-1)/K$ boundary term can be made arbitrarily small by increasing $K$, the *additional* latency beyond the theoretical minimum can be driven to zero. In practice, $K$ is constrained by the communication library's efficiency with small messages and the overhead of launching many small kernels, so the boundary term is reduced to a practical minimum rather than literally zero — but for the tensor sizes and device counts tested, the empirical results show this term is indeed negligible (Figure 3, right: All-Scan's latency grows only weakly with device count, from ~0.23 ms at 8 GPUs to ~2.16 ms at 256 GPUs, while All-Gather's grows from ~0.37 ms to ~8.5 ms).

**The first and last device special cases.** Algorithm 2 handles boundary conditions explicitly:
- **First device ($p = 0$):** has no predecessor to receive from, so it simply sends its local state $S^{(k)}_{\text{local}} = S^{(k)}_{[N]}$ to device 1 (no incoming attenuation needed since there is no prior context).
- **Last device ($p = P-1$):** receives from device $P-2$, updates its state, but does not forward to a successor. It only needs to produce its own global states for output computation and potential backward pass.

#### Two-Stream Overlap: Hiding Communication Behind Computation

ZeCO's design goes beyond minimising communication volume — it also **schedules communication to run concurrently with computation that does not depend on the communicated data**. This is what the paper means by "zero communication overhead" at the end-to-end runtime level: the time spent on communication is not *additional* time beyond what computation already requires.

**Identifying independent computation.** The linear attention output for each chunk (Equation 3) consists of two additive terms:

$$O_{[i]} = \underbrace{(Q_{[i]} \odot \Lambda_{[i]}) \cdot S_{[i-1]}}_{\text{inter-chunk (depends on global state)}} + \underbrace{\left[ ((Q_{[i]} \odot \Lambda_{[i]}) \cdot (K_{[i]} \odot \Gamma_{[i]})^\top) \odot M \right] \cdot V_{[i]}}_{\text{intra-chunk (does NOT depend on global state)}}$$

The inter-chunk term $O^{\text{inter}}_{[i]}$ involves $S_{[i-1]}$, which in turn depends on the incoming global state $S_{(p-1)L}$ from the previous device through the global update formula (Equation 6). This computation **cannot** begin until All-Scan delivers $S_{(p-1)L}$.

The intra-chunk term $O^{\text{intra}}_{[i]}$ involves only $Q_{[i]}$, $K_{[i]}$, $V_{[i]}$, $\Lambda_{[i]}$, $\Gamma_{[i]}$, and the causal mask $M$ — all of which are purely local to the current chunk on the current device. This computation has **no dependency whatsoever** on the incoming global state. It can begin immediately after the local projections are available, regardless of whether All-Scan has completed.

**The two-stream strategy.** Algorithm 1 (lines 13–32) implements this as a fork-join parallel pattern:

1. **Fork:** After the local state computation loop (lines 6–12) completes, two parallel execution streams are launched:
   - **Stream 1** (line 15): Execute the All-Scan communication primitive to obtain $S_{(p-1)L}$ and produce $S_{pL}$.
   - **Stream 2** (lines 17–23): In parallel across all chunks $n = 1, \ldots, N$, load $Q_{[n]}$, $K_{[n]}$, $G_{[n]}$ from HBM, construct the causal mask $M$, compute the decay-scaled queries $\tilde{Q}_{[n]} = Q_{[n]} \odot \Lambda_{[n]}$ and keys $\bar{K}_{[n]} = K_{[n]} / \Lambda_{[n]}$, compute the diagonal attention scores $P_{[n]} = (\tilde{Q}_{[n]} \bar{K}^\top_{[n]}) \odot M$, and write $P_{[n]}$ to HBM.

2. **Join (stream barrier, line 24):** Both streams must complete before proceeding. At this point, $S_{(p-1)L}$ is guaranteed to be available (from stream 1) and all $P_{[n]}$ are guaranteed to be available (from stream 2).

3. **Final assembly (lines 25–32):** For each chunk $n$, load all needed tensors ($Q_{[n]}$, $G_{[n]}$, $V_{[n]}$, $S_{(p-1)L}$, $S_{[n]}$, $\tilde{\gamma}_{[n-1]}$, $P_{[n]}$) from HBM, compute the inter-chunk output $O^{\text{inter}}_{[n]} = \tilde{Q}_{[n]}(S_{[n-1]} + (\tilde{\gamma}^\top_{[n-1]} \mathbf{1}) \odot S_{(p-1)L})$, compute the intra-chunk output $O^{\text{intra}}_{[n]} = P_{[n]} V_{[n]}$, sum them to produce $O_{[n]}$, and write the result to HBM.

**What makes the overlap effective.** The intra-chunk diagonal attention computation in stream 2 involves several GEMM operations per chunk (matrix multiplications for query-key products, mask application, and unit tests). For typical configurations ($C=64$, $d_k=128$ for 16-head models with hidden dimension 2048), this computation is substantial enough to hide the All-Scan latency. If the diagonal attention computation takes longer than All-Scan, the communication is *entirely hidden* — stream 1 finishes before stream 2, and the join barrier imposes no additional wait. This is the "zero overhead" scenario in practice.

**The worst-case bound in the analysis.** The paper's theoretical analysis in Equation (11) takes the conservative approach of assuming the worst case — that the diagonal attention computation does NOT fully cover the All-Scan latency:

$$T^P_{\text{SP}}(PL) = T^P_{\text{ideal-SP}}(PL) + T_{\text{extra\_comp\&I/O}} + (T_{\text{All\_Scan}} - T_{\text{overlaped\_comp}})$$

The term $(T_{\text{All\_Scan}} - T_{\text{overlaped\_comp}})$ represents the *uncovered* portion of communication latency — the part that extends the critical path beyond the ideal SP time. In the best case (overlap greater than or equal to communication time), this term is zero. In the worst case, it is bounded by $\tau(d_k \times d_v) + (P-1)\tau(d_k \times d_v)/K$ as derived above. The empirical results in Figure 4 show that even in the worst case on 128 GPUs, ZeCO's operator runtime exceeds the theoretical lower bound (data parallelism baseline) by only ~3 ms — a tiny fraction of the total forward-plus-backward pass time.

**Why this two-stream approach rather than overlapping the local recurrence itself?** The local recurrence (the loop in lines 6–12) must process chunks sequentially because each chunk's state depends on the previous chunk's state. This serial dependency makes overlap difficult — you cannot start computing $S_{[n+1]}$ until $\gamma_{[n]}$ and the key-value outer product for chunk $n$ are complete. However, the diagonal attention computation has no such intra-device dependency: $P_{[n]}$ for different chunks are completely independent. This makes it the ideal candidate for overlap. The paper's key design decision is recognising that the standard GLA computation can be reordered to expose this parallelism — computing all $P_{[n]}$ eagerly before the global state arrives, rather than computing each $P_{[n]}$ lazily as needed for the output assembly of chunk $n$.

#### Extra Computation and I/O Overhead: Why It's Negligible

ZeCO introduces additional operations beyond the base GLA algorithm: storing cumulative decay vectors $\tilde{\gamma}_{[n]}$ to HBM, loading them during output assembly, the element-wise multiplications for the global state update, and the segment-wise bookkeeping in All-Scan. The paper provides a careful accounting of why these constitute a negligible fraction of total runtime.

**I/O overhead.** Algorithm 1, line 9, writes the cumulative decay vector $\tilde{\gamma}_{[n]}$ to HBM at each chunk boundary. This vector is of size $d_k$ (e.g., 128 for 16-head models with $d=2048$). The state tensor $S_{[n]}$ written on line 11 is of size $d_k \times d_v$ (e.g., $128 \times 128 = 16384$ elements). The ratio of $\tilde{\gamma}$ I/O to $S$ I/O is therefore $1 / d_v$ — the paper states this directly:

> "the load and store operations for $\tilde{\gamma}[n]$ in lines 9 and 26 are vector, constituting only $\frac{1}{d_v}$ of the state tensor"

For a typical $d_v = 128$, this is less than 0.8% overhead. Furthermore, $\tilde{\gamma}$ is stored once per chunk and reused in the output assembly, where it is loaded once per chunk — this amortisation means the $\tilde{\gamma}$ I/O overhead is spread across all $N$ chunks. The paper notes:

> "The required additional state can be reused $N$ times, incurring just a $\frac{1}{N}$ overhead. For a sequence length of 8192 and a chunk size of 64 ($N = 128$), which is comparable to typical $d_v$, the added overhead is less than 1%."

The reasoning: the per-chunk I/O for $\tilde{\gamma}$ is $d_k$ elements per chunk, while the total tensor I/O per chunk (for $Q$, $K$, $V$, $S$, $P$, $O$, and their gradients) is on the order of $C \times d$ elements. For $C=64$ and $d=2048$, this is ~131K elements per chunk for the main tensors versus 128 elements for $\tilde{\gamma}$ — a factor of ~1000× difference. When summed across all chunks and all operations, the $\tilde{\gamma}$-related I/O is well below 1% of total memory traffic.

**Computation overhead.** The global state update in Equation (6) involves an outer product $(\tilde{\gamma}^\top_{[n]} \mathbf{1}) \odot S_{(p-1)L}$ followed by an element-wise addition with $S_{[n]}$. This is $O(d_k \times d_v)$ element-wise operations per chunk, compared to the $O(C \times d_k \times d_v)$ operations in the matrix multiplications for local state computation and diagonal attention. Since $C$ is typically 64 or larger, the element-wise update is 1–2 orders of magnitude cheaper than the matrix operations it accompanies. The paper categorises this as "negligible":

> "The cost of element-wise multiplications is negligible."

**Segment-wise All-Scan bookkeeping.** The partitioning of $S$ into $K$ segments and the loop over segments in Algorithm 2 introduces a small amount of indexing arithmetic and kernel launch overhead. However, since each segment operation involves $O(\frac{d_k}{K} \times d_v)$ element-wise operations and a communication send/receive, and the segment count $K$ is chosen to make each segment large enough for efficient communication (tensor size per segment is 16384 elements in the experiments), the overhead per segment is amortised over substantial work. The paper does not provide an explicit cost model for this overhead, but implicitly treats it as part of the $\epsilon$ term in Equation (14):

$$T^P_{\text{ZeCO}}(PL) \approx T^1_{\text{ideal-SP}}(L) - T_{\text{overlaped\_comp}} + \tau(d_k \times d_v)$$

where $\epsilon$ (dropped in the approximation) represents "a negligible computation and I/O cost."

**Comparison with other methods' extra costs.** The paper acknowledges that LASP-1 and LASP-2 could also optimise their extra computation and I/O costs to negligible levels:

> "We assume that the $T_{\text{extra\_comp\&I/O}}$ term in other methods can also be optimized to a negligible level. Even so, these methods remain suboptimal."

The suboptimality of LASP-1 and LASP-2 stems not from their extra computation overhead but from their fundamental communication patterns: LASP-1's serial dependency (the $P \times$ multiplier on the entire computation time in Equation 15) and LASP-2's communication volume scaling with $P$ (the $P \times \tau(d_k \times d_v)$ term in Equation 16). ZeCO's optimality comes from eliminating these fundamental bottlenecks, not from micro-optimising auxiliary operations.

#### Backward Pass Symmetry and Recompute Strategy

The backward pass of ZeCO (described in Algorithm 3 in Appendix A.4) mirrors the forward pass in structure, with one important difference: the handling of the hidden state tensor $S_{[n]}$ during gradient computation.

**Forward-like structure.** The backward pass begins by computing local gradient states $dS_{[n]}$ through a reverse recurrence (lines 1–7), starting from the last chunk and moving backward. The formula mirrors Equation (4) but with gradients and queries:

$$dS_{[n-1]} = (\gamma^\top_{[n]} \mathbf{1}) \odot dS + \tilde{Q}^\top_{[n]} dO_{[n]}$$

where $dS$ accumulates gradients from later chunks, $dO_{[n]}$ is the incoming gradient of the output, and $\tilde{Q}_{[n]} = Q_{[n]} \odot G_{[n]}$ is the decay-scaled query. This produces a chain of local gradient states $dS_{[0]}, dS_{[1]}, \ldots, dS_{[N]}$.

**All-Scan in the backward direction.** The gradient of the final local state, $dS_{[0]}$, must be propagated back through the device chain (from device $P-1$ to device $0$) because it carries information about how the initial state of device $p$ (which is the final state of device $p-1$) affects the loss. This uses the same All-Scan primitive but with `DIR == BWD`, reversing the communication direction:

$$dS_{(p-1)L}, dS_{pL} \leftarrow \text{All-Scan}(dS_{[0]}, \tilde{\gamma}_{[0]})$$

**The recompute optimisation.** The paper notes an important implementation detail:

> "in the official implementation of gated linear attention, $S_{[n]}$ needs to be recomputed during the backward pass. However, since the global initial state has already been obtained during the forward pass, there is no need for all-scan communication when recomputing $S_{[n]}$."

During the backward pass, the hidden states $S_{[n]}$ for each chunk are needed to compute gradients with respect to $Q$, $K$, and $V$ (they appear in the gradient of the inter-chunk term, lines 34–37 of Algorithm 3). Rather than storing all $S_{[n]}$ from the forward pass (which would consume substantial HBM), the official GLA implementation recomputes them on-the-fly during the backward pass. The key ZeCO optimisation: since $S_{(p-1)L}$ is already known from the forward pass All-Scan, the recomputation can start from $S_{[0]} = S_{(p-1)L}$ directly, without needing to re-execute All-Scan in the backward pass. This saves one round of inter-device communication during recomputation. The gradient All-Scan (for $dS_{[0]}$) is still needed, but the forward state All-Scan (for $S_{[N]}$) is not repeated.

**Parallelism in the backward pass.** Similar to the forward pass, the backward pass uses parallel streams: while All-Scan propagates $dS_{[0]}$ backward through the device chain, each device can compute gradient contributions that depend only on local data — specifically, the intra-chunk gradient terms involving the pre-computed attention scores $P_{[n]}$. The stream barrier at line 29 synchronises before the final gradient assembly (lines 30–40), which requires both the communicated gradient state $dS_{pL}$ and the locally computed partial gradients.

#### Unified Comparison with Prior Methods (Appendix A.2)

The paper provides a unified analysis of all SP methods in Appendix A.2, summarised in Table 1. This analysis formalises the comparison and makes clear why ZeCO is optimal.

**Assumptions and notation.** For fair comparison, the analysis uses a concrete model configuration: head count $H=32$, $d_k = d_v = D/H = e$ (so each head has dimension $e \times e$ for the state tensor). The computation cost per device for linear attention is $LDe$ — this is the inherent cost of processing a length-$L$ sequence, independent of the parallelism strategy.

**ZeCO's communication volume:** $De$ per device. Each device sends exactly one state tensor of size $e \times e$, and $De = H \times e \times e = D \times e$. This is independent of $P$.

**ZeCO's computation cost:** $LDe + NDe + Nd$. The term $LDe$ is the inherent GLA computation. $NDe$ is the extra cost for updating $N$ global states using the global recurrence (Equation 6, applied once per chunk per head). $Nd$ is the cost of maintaining $N$ cumulative decay vectors. Both extra terms are $O(Nde)$, while the base computation is $O(Lde)$. Since $N = L/C$ and $C$ is typically 64, $NDe$ is roughly $LDe/64$ — about 1.5% of the base cost. The $Nd$ term is even smaller ($d \ll De$). So ZeCO's total extra computation is approximately 1–2% beyond the base GLA computation.

**LASP-1:** Communication volume $PDe$, computation cost $PLDe$. The $P$ multiplier on computation reflects serial execution — each of $P$ devices spends time proportional to $PLDe$ sequentially, even though each device only processes $L$ tokens. The communication volume $PDe$ counts the P2P transfers across all device pairs.

**LASP-2:** Communication volume $PDe$, computation cost $LDe + \log(P)De + NDe$. The communication volume is the same as LASP-1 in aggregate but distributed differently (All-Gather vs. P2P). The computation has an extra $\log(P)De$ term from the parallel scan reduction over $P$ states, plus $NDe$ for state updates. The $\log(P)$ term grows with device count, though slowly.

**ZeCO's advantage in concrete numbers.** For the 1B-GLA model with $D=2048$, $H=32$, $e = d_k = d_v = 64$: each state tensor is $64 \times 64 = 4096$ elements, or 16 KB in FP32. On 256 GPUs, LASP-2 would communicate $256 \times 16 \text{ KB} \approx 4 \text{ MB}$ per device per layer per attention head — across 20 layers and 32 heads, this becomes ~2.5 GB per training iteration in communication volume alone. ZeCO communicates exactly 16 KB × 20 × 32 ≈ 10 MB total. The 250× difference in communication volume is what drives the empirical speedups in Figure 1.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Chunk-wise local recurrence + explicit cumulative decay storage** over pure token-wise recurrence: enables GEMM-accelerated local computation and provides the $\tilde{\gamma}_{[n]}$ vectors needed for the global state update decomposition. Without storing $\tilde{\gamma}_{[n]}$, the global update would require recomputing the cumulative decay on-the-fly.

- **Partitioning the state tensor along $d_k$ dimension for pipelining** over partitioning along $d_v$ or not partitioning at all: the $d_k$ dimension corresponds to independent decay channels — operations on different $d_k$ slices are independent (the decay for one channel does not affect another), making this the natural dimension for parallelism. The $d_v$ dimension, in contrast, is coupled through the outer products in the recurrence.

- **Two-stream scheduling with diagonal attention as the overlap candidate** over overlapping the local recurrence itself: the diagonal attention computation has no inter-chunk dependencies within a device, making it embarrassingly parallel and thus ideal for background execution. The local recurrence has strict sequential dependencies that make overlap difficult without speculative execution.

- **All-Scan as a new collective primitive** over adapting existing primitives (All-Reduce, All-Gather, Reduce-Scatter): existing collectives are designed for operations where all devices need a function of all inputs (All-Reduce) or all devices need all inputs (All-Gather). Sequence parallelism for linear attention has a different pattern: each device needs exactly the scan result from one neighbour, and the scan operation itself is a stateful recurrence, not a stateless reduction. All-Scan is purpose-built for this pattern.

- **Separate forward state All-Scan and backward gradient All-Scan** over a single bidirectional primitive: the forward and backward passes have different dependency directions and different tensor semantics (state tensors vs. gradient tensors). Keeping them separate allows each to use its own optimal segment size and overlap strategy.

- **Recomputation of $S_{[n]}$ in backward pass using cached $S_{(p-1)L}$** over storing all $S_{[n]}$ from forward: this trades a small amount of recomputation (re-running the local recurrence, which is fast) for a large HBM saving (avoiding storage of $N$ state tensors per layer per device). The cached $S_{(p-1)L}$ from the forward pass eliminates the need for re-communication during recomputation.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Sequence Parallelism for Linear Attention Has a Provable Communication Lower Bound, and ZeCO Achieves It

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is not the specific All-Scan mechanism, but the **identification and formalisation of the information-theoretic lower bound for sequence parallelism (SP) communication in linear attention models**, coupled with a proof that this bound is achievable in practice. Prior work on SP for linear attention (LASP-1, LASP-2) treated communication as an engineering cost to be reduced — a target for incremental optimisation. The paper reframes the problem entirely: there exists a *provable minimum* communication volume below which no correct SP algorithm can operate, and any method exceeding this minimum is fundamentally wasteful in a way that no amount of engineering optimisation can fix.

The lower bound is established in Equation (9): each device must send and receive exactly one state tensor $S \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k \times d_v}$, for a total communication volume of $d_k \times d_v$ elements per device. The argument is structural, not empirical. The linear attention recurrence compresses the entire history of tokens processed up to a given point into a single fixed-size hidden state — this is the defining property of linear attention. For device $p$ to compute correct outputs, it must know the accumulated state from all devices $0$ through $p-1$, and that accumulated information is fully and losslessly encoded in the final state $S_{(p-1)L}$ of device $p-1$. There is no alternative representation that is smaller — the state tensor is the minimal sufficient statistic for the prior sequence. Communicating anything less would discard information necessary for correctness; communicating anything more is redundant.

This framing is intellectually significant because it converts a vague optimisation goal ("reduce communication") into a precise, verifiable criterion ("does the communication volume equal $d_k \times d_v$?"). LASP-1 actually satisfies this volume bound — it communicates exactly one state tensor per device pair — but it fails on a separate dimension (serial execution). LASP-2 violates the bound fundamentally, communicating $P-1$ state tensors per device, with volume growing linearly in $P$. As Table 1 in Appendix A.2 shows, LASP-2's volume is $PDe$ while ZeCO's is $De$, where $D$ is hidden dimension, $e$ is per-head dimension, and $P$ is device count. At 256 GPUs, this is a 256× difference in communication volume — not an incremental improvement but a categorical one.

The paper reinforces this lower bound argument empirically in Figure 3 (left two panels): ZeCO's communication volume is a flat line independent of device count, while LASP-2's grows proportionally. The theoretical curve and the measured communication time (Figure 3, right) converge on the same conclusion: All-Scan (ZeCO's primitive) is not merely faster than All-Gather (LASP-2's primitive), but sits at the information-theoretic floor. You cannot go lower. This is a **fundamental advance**, not an incremental one — it establishes a ceiling for all future SP methods and proves that ceiling is attainable.

Why this reframing matters beyond ZeCO: any future SP method for linear attention can be immediately evaluated against this bound. If a method communicates more than $d_k \times d_v$ per device, it is provably suboptimal in communication volume, regardless of its other merits. This is analogous to how the Chinchilla scaling laws established compute-optimal training boundaries that subsequent work must respect. ZeCO does not just propose a better method; it defines the optimisation target.

---

### Innovation 2: Pipelining a Scan Operation Across Devices Reconciles Minimal Communication Volume with Parallel Computation

The tension that prior work failed to resolve was that minimising communication volume (LASP-1) seemed to require serial execution, while enabling parallel execution (LASP-2) seemed to require broadcasting state tensors to all devices. These two desiderata — minimal volume AND parallel computation — appeared contradictory. The paper's key diagnostic move is recognising that they are not contradictory at all: **the dependency chain exists at the granularity of individual tensor elements or segments, not at the granularity of the full state tensor.**

This is a conceptual insight about the *structure of the dependency*, not a mechanism detail. LASP-1's designers (Sun, Qin, et al., 2025) saw that device $p$ needs $S_{(p-1)L}$ to compute its outputs, and concluded that device $p$ must wait for device $p-1$ to finish entirely. The paper's counterargument is implicit but clear: device $p$ needs $S_{(p-1)L}$ *eventually*, but it does not need *all* of $S_{(p-1)L}$ *simultaneously* before it can begin useful work. By partitioning the state tensor along the $d_k$ dimension — which corresponds to independent decay channels that do not interact during the state update — each segment can flow through the device chain independently. Device $p+1$ can begin processing the first segment of $S_{pL}$ while device $p$ is still computing later segments. The serial dependency is *amortised across segments* rather than being a global barrier.

This insight is formalised in the latency equation (Equation 12): $T_{\text{All\_Scan}} = \tau(d_k \times d_v) + (P-1)\tau(d_k \times d_v)/K$, where $K$ is the number of segments. The first term is the unavoidable single-hop cost (the minimum communication time). The second term is the pipeline boundary overhead, which shrinks as $K$ increases. In the limit $K \to \infty$, the boundary overhead vanishes, and All-Scan's latency approaches the single-hop cost — meaning a state tensor can propagate through an arbitrary number of devices in roughly the time it takes to send it once. This is the insight that makes the paper's title ("Zero Communication Overhead") more than marketing: the communication cost, properly pipelined, is bounded by a constant independent of device count.

Crucially, this is NOT just an optimisation of LASP-1's P2P communication. LASP-1 sends one state tensor at a time, but the lack of segmentation means device $p+1$ waits for the *entire* tensor before starting. All-Scan's segmentation transforms a latency of $P \times \tau(d_k \times d_v)$ (Equation 15) into one approaching $\tau(d_k \times d_v)$ (Equation 14). The improvement factor is approximately $P$ — a fundamental algorithmic change, not a constant-factor tweak. Table 3 confirms this: at 128 GPUs with 16K sequence length, ZeCO's operator runtime is 9.88 ms versus LASP-1's 113.71 ms — an 11.5× speedup. This is not what incremental optimisation looks like (20% improvements). It is what happens when you remove a hard scaling bottleneck entirely.

The significance of this innovation extends beyond linear attention. The pattern — a scan operation over distributed data where the state is dense but the dependency between successive elements is a simple recurrence — appears in other settings (e.g., distributed prefix sums, certain RNN architectures). All-Scan demonstrates that pipelining the scan by partitioning the state along an *independently composable dimension* can recover near-ideal parallelism. This is a design pattern that other distributed systems can adopt.

---

### Innovation 3: Communication Overhead Is Not Just About Volume — Overlap with Computation Is the Second Necessary Condition for Optimality

The paper's optimality analysis (Section 3.3) identifies two conditions for an optimal SP strategy: (1) zero communication overhead (volume at the lower bound), and (2) optimal extra cost (communication overlapped with computation, and additional computation/I/O minimised). This second condition is often treated as an implementation detail in systems papers — a "nice to have" optimisation. ZeCO elevates it to a **necessary condition for provable optimality**, and then meets it through a specific identification of which computation can be overlapped and why.

The diagnostic move is in recognising that the linear attention output (Equation 3) decomposes into two terms with fundamentally different dependency structures: the inter-chunk term depends on the global state (and thus on communication), while the intra-chunk term depends ONLY on the local chunk's $Q$, $K$, and $V$. The intra-chunk term — the diagonal attention scores $P_{[n]}$ — is what the paper selects for overlap. This is not an arbitrary choice. The diagonal attention computation involves the most expensive local operations (query-key matrix products, causal masking), and it can be executed in parallel across all chunks within a device because there are no inter-chunk dependencies in the diagonal. This makes it the ideal candidate: maximising the amount of computation available to hide communication.

What makes this intellectually distinctive is that the paper treats overlap not as "run communication on a background thread and hope it finishes before the main computation," but as a scheduling problem with a **provable bound on the uncovered latency**. Equation (11) explicitly subtracts $T_{\text{overlaped\_comp}}$ from $T_{\text{All\_Scan}}$, and the empirical results verify that the uncovered portion — the gap between ZeCO's operator runtime and the data parallelism lower bound — is ~3 ms at 128 GPUs (Table 3). This is a tiny fraction of the ~15 ms total forward-plus-backward time per device.

The contrast with prior SP methods is sharp. LASP-1 cannot overlap anything because it is purely serial — computation IS communication in the sense that each device's computation directly depends on the previous device's completion. LASP-2 could theoretically overlap diagonal attention with its All-Gather communication, but its communication volume is $P$ times larger, making it much harder to hide fully behind the same amount of local computation. ZeCO's minimal volume makes overlap feasible; its identification of the diagonal attention as the overlap candidate makes it effective. Together, these two conditions define what "zero overhead" actually means in practice — not zero bytes communicated, but zero *additional* time on the critical path beyond what computation inherently requires.

This is a **fundamental** contribution to the design of distributed training systems. It establishes that communication minimisation and communication-computation overlap are not independent optimisation dimensions but coupled requirements: you must minimise volume first so that the remaining communication is small enough to fit inside the overlap window. Increasing overlap capacity (e.g., by making chunks larger) is an orthogonal strategy, but it has limits; reducing communication volume to its lower bound removes the need for heroic overlap engineering.

### Innovation 4: The Global State Update Decomposition as a Mathematical Insight That Eliminates Redundant Communication

While Section 3 covers the mechanics of the global state update formula (Equation 6 and its proof in Appendix A.1), the *intellectual significance* warrants separate treatment here. The decomposition $S_{(p-1)L + nC} = (\tilde{\gamma}^\top_{[n]} \mathbf{1}) \odot S_{(p-1)L} + S_{[n]}$ is the mathematical linchpin that makes minimal-volume communication possible. Without it, device $p$ would need more than just $S_{(p-1)L}$ — it might need intermediate states from device $p-1$, or per-chunk decay information from devices further back in the chain, or some other representation that grows with sequence position.

The paper proves (Appendix A.1) that this decomposition is exact — not an approximation, not a heuristic, but a direct algebraic consequence of the linearity of the GLA recurrence. The proof unfolds the chunk-wise recurrence (Equation 19) and shows that starting from a non-zero initial state simply adds an attenuated copy of that initial state to the result that would be obtained from zero initialisation. This property is what makes the entire prior sequence compressible into a single state tensor without information loss.

Why this is non-obvious: LASP-2's All-Gather strategy implicitly assumes that each device needs access to *all* intermediate states to compute global results. The paper demonstrates that this assumption is false — the global state at any position can be reconstructed from exactly two pieces of information: the incoming global state (which summarises everything before the current device) and the local state (which summarises everything within the current device up to that position). The cumulative decay vector $\tilde{\gamma}_{[n]}$ provides the correct attenuation factor for blending these two components. LASP-2's All-Gather communicates $P$ state tensors to each device when only one is needed — a $P$-fold redundancy that the paper's decomposition proves is mathematically unnecessary.

This is a **fundamental theoretical contribution**, not just an engineering trick. It establishes that the linear attention recurrence has a specific algebraic structure (linear decomposition under non-zero initial conditions) that any efficient distributed implementation must exploit. This structure is not present in full self-attention, where the softmax nonlinearity couples all tokens and makes state compression lossy. The fact that linear attention enables exact state compression is its algorithmic advantage; the paper's insight is that this advantage must be carried through to the distributed system design, not discarded by an All-Gather that treats states as opaque blobs to be broadcast.

The practical consequence is visible in the FLOPs-matched comparison between methods (Table 1): LASP-2's computation cost includes a $\log(P)De$ term from the parallel scan over all $P$ states, while ZeCO has no $\log(P)$ dependency because there is no multi-state reduction — only a single element-wise update per chunk. As device counts scale to hundreds or thousands, logarithmic terms become significant. ZeCO sidesteps them entirely by never creating the redundant states in the first place.

### Innovation 5: Verifier-Quality-Independent Throughput Scaling as a New Evaluation Paradigm for SP Methods

The paper's evaluation framework is itself a methodological contribution worth surfacing. Most SP papers evaluate throughput at fixed total sequence lengths and varying device counts, which confounds two effects: the parallel efficiency of the SP method (how well it uses additional devices) and the computational load changes (longer sequences mean more work, even with perfect parallelism). ZeCO's evaluation in Figure 4 and Section 4.2 uses a different and more diagnostic protocol: **fix the per-device sequence length** (16K or 32K tokens per GPU), scale the device count, and measure whether per-GPU throughput stays constant (linear scaling) or degrades (sublinear scaling). The data parallelism (DP) baseline — which has zero communication for sequence distribution — provides the theoretical upper bound.

This evaluation design isolates the quality of the SP method from the inherent computational scaling of the model. Perfect SP would show flat per-GPU throughput as device count increases, matching DP. Degradation below DP reveals exactly how much overhead the SP method introduces. Figure 4 shows that ZeCO's per-GPU throughput at 256 GPUs (32K sequence length) is ~40.9K tokens/sec versus the DP baseline of ~46.6K tokens/sec — a 12% degradation from ideal. LASP-2 degrades to ~25.4K tokens/sec — a 45% degradation. LASP-1 degrades so severely that at 128 GPUs it was not measured (likely due to memory or timeout constraints at the higher scale).

What makes this evaluation distinctive is its **diagnostic precision**. It separates the question "does the SP method scale?" (yes/no) from "why does it fail to scale?" (communication volume, serial dependency, or extra computation). For LASP-1, the answer is serial dependency: per-GPU throughput should decline approximately as $1/P$, and the data in Tables 5 and 6 roughly follow this trend (e.g., 16K throughput drops from ~26.4K at 8 GPUs to ~12.6K at 64 GPUs). For LASP-2, the answer is communication volume: the $\log(P)De$ term in its computation cost and the $PDe$ communication volume combine to create a gradual decline. For ZeCO, the near-flat throughput curve confirms that both conditions for optimality — minimal communication volume AND effective overlap — are satisfied simultaneously.

This evaluation protocol is a **methodological advance** for the field. It provides a template for how future SP methods should be evaluated: fix per-device work, scale devices, compare against the DP ceiling, and attribute any gap to specific components (communication latency, extra computation, I/O overhead). The paper's own analysis in Equation (11) provides the mathematical decomposition for such attribution. This is a lasting contribution independent of the specific numbers ZeCO achieves — it defines what "good enough" SP should look like and how to measure progress toward it.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on a 1B-parameter Gated Linear Attention (GLA) model during training, using synthetic training workloads rather than a benchmark dataset per se. The evaluation measures system-level performance metrics (runtime, throughput, scalability) under controlled training configurations with specified sequence lengths, model dimensions, and device counts. There is no "test set" or accuracy metric — this is purely a systems performance evaluation.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use a 1B-parameter GLA model (Section 4) with 20 layers, hidden dimension $d = 2048$, and $H = 32$ attention heads (so $d_k = d_v = 64$) for throughput experiments. For operator-level runtime experiments, $H = 16$ heads are used, giving $d_k = d_v = 128$. The model is implemented using the Flash Linear Attention (FLA) library (Yang and Zhang, 2024) for the chunk-wise GLA operator and trained within the Lingua distributed training framework (Videau et al., 2024). The model size (1B parameters) is chosen as representative of moderate-scale LLMs where sequence parallelism is a practical necessity for long-context training.

- **Metrics.** Three primary metrics are reported:
  - **Communication Runtime** (ms): wall-clock time for the collective communication operator to complete its data transfer and associated computation, averaged over 50 runs after 5 warm-up rounds (Section 4.1, Tables in Appendix A.3).
  - **SP Algorithm Runtime** (ms): wall-clock time for one forward and backward pass of the GLA attention operator equipped with a specific SP method, compared against a data parallelism (DP) baseline that serves as the theoretical lower bound (Section 4.2, Tables 3 and 4).
  - **Per-GPU Throughput** (tokens per second): number of tokens processed per second per GPU during end-to-end model training, measuring the macro-level efficiency of the full training pipeline including all layers, communication, and optimiser overhead (Section 4.2, Tables 5 and 6).

- **Baselines.** Three baselines are compared throughout:
  - **LASP-1** (Sun, Qin, et al., 2025): Serial P2P communication where each device passes its final state to the next device in sequence. Included in all experiments.
  - **LASP-2** (Sun, Lan, et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025): All-Gather-based communication where each device collects intermediate states from all other devices and performs a parallel scan. Included in all experiments where it does not run out of memory (noted as "—" for 128+ GPU configurations in Tables 5 and 6, and memory-out for All-Gather at 128 and 256 GPUs in Figure 3).
  - **GLA (DP baseline)**: Data parallelism — the model processes sequences using standard GLA operators without any sequence-level distribution, representing the theoretical upper bound for throughput and lower bound for operator runtime. Included in all experiments.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a "generation budget" in the LLM inference sense. Instead, it controls for **per-device sequence length** $L$ (fixed at 16K or 32K tokens per GPU across all experiments) and **number of devices** $P$ (varied from 8 to 256). This means the total global sequence length scales as $P \times L$ (from 128K tokens at 8 GPUs to 8M tokens at 256 GPUs with 32K per GPU). By fixing per-device work and varying device count, the evaluation isolates parallel efficiency: ideal SP would show constant per-GPU throughput regardless of $P$, while any throughput degradation directly measures communication and synchronisation overhead introduced by the SP method. The FLOPs for the GLA operator itself scale as $O(L d^2)$ per device — constant when $L$ and $d$ are fixed — so that any change in per-iteration time or throughput with $P$ is attributable to the SP communication pattern.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** All measurements use 5 rounds of warm-up followed by averaging over multiple iterations: 50 runs for communication runtime and operator runtime experiments, 100 steps for throughput experiments (Section 4.1, Section 4.2, Appendix A.3 Tables 2–6). This extensive averaging provides stable measurements on the HPC cluster, though no error bars or confidence intervals are reported. The experiments use a fixed hardware configuration (256× H100 80GB GPUs) and model configuration throughout; there is no cross-validation over hardware types, model scales, or sequence length distributions.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Communication Speed (Section 4.1)

The headline result: **All-Scan achieves the lowest communication latency among all tested collective primitives across all device scales, and the gap grows with device count** (Figure 3, right panel; Table 2 in Appendix A.3).

At 8 GPUs, the three methods are relatively close: All-Scan at 0.226 ms, All-Gather Linear at 0.375 ms, All-Reduce at 0.138 ms. However, All-Reduce is not a valid SP method — it computes a sum reduction, not a scan — so its lower latency is irrelevant for sequence parallelism correctness. Comparing only valid SP primitives, All-Scan is already 1.7× faster than All-Gather at 8 GPUs.

As device count increases, the gap widens dramatically:
- At 64 GPUs: All-Scan 0.739 ms vs. All-Gather 2.543 ms — **3.4× faster**.
- At 128 GPUs: All-Scan 1.272 ms vs. All-Gather 4.35 ms — **3.4× faster**.
- At 256 GPUs: All-Scan 2.165 ms vs. All-Gather 8.514 ms — **3.9× faster**, matching the claim in Figure 1.

The All-Gather primitive also encounters a practical failure at 128 and 256 GPUs for the Megatron implementation variant (Figure 3 text: "memory-out occurred in the experiments of 128 GPUs and 256 GPUs All-gather (Megatron)"), while All-Scan runs successfully across the full range.

The paper's theoretical analysis in Section 3.3 and Appendix A.2 predicts this scaling behaviour: All-Gather communicates $(P-1) \times d_k \times d_v$ elements per device, while All-Scan communicates exactly $d_k \times d_v$ — independent of $P$. With $d_k = d_v = e = 64$ and $H = 32$, each state tensor is $64 \times 64 = 4096$ elements (16 KB in FP32). At 256 GPUs, All-Gather transfers $255 \times 16$ KB ≈ 4 MB per device per communication call, while All-Scan transfers exactly 16 KB — a ~255× difference in volume, which the measured ~3.9× latency improvement reflects after accounting for fixed overheads and network topology effects.

A subtle but important detail in the communication experiment (Section 4.1): "Experiments are conducted with $P$ from 8 to 256 GPUs, and each GPU is assigned 8K sequence length." The tensor size per chunk segment is 16384 (from Section 4.1 and Appendix A.3), indicating $K$ — the number of pipeline segments — is chosen to balance pipeline depth against per-segment overhead. The paper does not report the exact $K$ value, but the tensor size of 16384 elements per segment in FP32 corresponds to 64 KB per segment, which is a reasonable granularity for NCCL-based communication on H100 GPUs with NVLink and InfiniBand interconnects.

#### SP Algorithm Runtime (Section 4.2, Operator-Level)

The headline result: **ZeCO's operator runtime stays within 3 ms of the theoretical lower bound (data parallelism baseline) even at 128 GPUs, while LASP-1 and LASP-2 degrade severely** (Figure 4, top half; Tables 3 and 4).

For 16K sequence length per GPU (Table 3):
- **DP baseline:** 6.39–6.55 ms across all device counts — the theoretical minimum, since DP has zero SP communication.
- **ZeCO:** 7.32 ms at 8 GPUs, 7.45 ms at 16, 7.65 ms at 32, 8.04 ms at 64, 9.88 ms at 128 GPUs. The gap above DP grows from ~0.9 ms at 8 GPUs to ~3.3 ms at 128 GPUs — a mere 50% increase in overhead for a 16× increase in device count.
- **LASP-2:** 19.39 ms at 8 GPUs, 24.48 ms at 16, 25.72 ms at 32, 29.17 ms at 64, 35.72 ms at 128 GPUs. The overhead over DP grows from ~13 ms to ~29 ms.
- **LASP-1:** 22.59 ms at 8 GPUs, 28.20 ms at 16, 39.03 ms at 32, 61.18 ms at 64, 113.71 ms at 128 GPUs. The serial dependency causes near-linear growth with device count.

For 32K sequence length per GPU (Table 4), the patterns hold with proportionally larger absolute times (DP baseline ~11.74–11.79 ms across device counts). At 128 GPUs: ZeCO 15.06 ms vs. DP 11.74 ms (3.32 ms gap), LASP-2 42.50 ms (30.76 ms gap), LASP-1 217.20 ms (205.46 ms gap). ZeCO's overhead relative to DP is nearly constant in absolute terms (~3 ms at 128 GPUs for both 16K and 32K), while LASP-1's overhead grows super-linearly and LASP-2's grows significantly.

These operator-level results are the micro-benchmark that validates the theoretical optimality analysis in Section 3.3. Equation (14) predicted $T^P_{\text{ZeCO}}(PL) \approx T^1_{\text{ideal-SP}}(L) - T_{\text{overlaped\_comp}} + \tau(d_k \times d_v)$. The ~3 ms residual at 128 GPUs represents the term $T_{\text{All\_Scan}} - T_{\text{overlaped\_comp}}$ (the uncovered portion of communication) plus the negligible extra computation/I/O overhead $\epsilon$. The paper's claim in Section 3.3 that "Textra_comp&I/O can be safely ignored in practice" is borne out — if there were substantial extra computation, the gap would grow with sequence length (doubling from 16K to 32K), but it remains roughly constant.

A notable data point: LASP-1 at 128 GPUs with 32K sequence length takes 217.20 ms — over 18× the DP baseline. This is the clearest illustration of why serial SP is fundamentally broken for large device counts. It is not a communication problem (LASP-1 communicates only one state tensor, same as ZeCO) — it is a scheduling problem where computation itself is serialised.

#### Model-Level Throughput (Section 4.2, End-to-End Training)

The headline result: **ZeCO achieves near-linear throughput scaling from 8 to 256 GPUs and delivers 60% higher per-GPU throughput than LASP-2 at 256 GPUs with 32K sequence length** (Figure 4, bottom half; Tables 5 and 6).

For 32K sequence length per GPU (Table 6; most relevant for ultra-long context training):
- **At 8 GPUs** (256K total sequence): ZeCO 47,369 tokens/sec/GPU, LASP-2 42,946, LASP-1 27,014, DP baseline 49,633. ZeCO achieves 95.4% of the DP ceiling.
- **At 128 GPUs** (4M total sequence): ZeCO 43,278 tokens/sec/GPU, LASP-2 33,327, LASP-1 not measured, DP baseline 47,848. ZeCO achieves 90.4% of DP; LASP-2 achieves 69.6%.
- **At 256 GPUs** (8M total sequence): ZeCO 40,967 tokens/sec/GPU, LASP-2 25,402, DP baseline 46,588. ZeCO achieves 87.9% of DP; LASP-2 achieves 54.5%.

The 60% improvement claim in the abstract and Figure 1 (right panel) corresponds to ZeCO's 40,967 vs. LASP-2's 25,402 tokens/sec at 256 GPUs — specifically, $(40967 - 25402) / 25402 \approx 61.3\%$. This is the most impactful practical result: at the scale where linear attention's algorithmic efficiency matters most (8M token sequences distributed across 256 GPUs), ZeCO extracts 60% more useful work from identical hardware than the prior SOTA.

The paper's "near-linear scalability" claim (Figure 1, left panel; Section 4) requires careful interpretation. Linear scaling would mean per-GPU throughput stays perfectly constant as device count increases. ZeCO's per-GPU throughput does decline — from 47,369 at 8 GPUs to 40,967 at 256 GPUs (32K setting), a 13.5% drop. However, total system throughput (the product of per-GPU throughput and device count) grows from ~379K tokens/sec at 8 GPUs to ~10.5M tokens/sec at 256 GPUs — a 27.7× increase for a 32× increase in devices, representing 86.6% parallel efficiency. The paper's "near-linear" claim is justified relative to the catastrophic degradation of LASP-1 and LASP-2, and relative to the practical reality that some efficiency loss at extreme scale is inevitable due to network topology, synchronisation, and load imbalance.

For 16K sequence length per GPU (Table 5), the patterns are similar but with lower absolute throughput (since less computation per communication step means the communication-to-computation ratio is higher). At 256 GPUs: ZeCO 34,400 tokens/sec/GPU (80.3% of DP baseline 42,838), LASP-2 15,196 tokens/sec/GPU (35.5% of DP). The gap between ZeCO and LASP-2 widens at shorter per-device sequences because communication cost is a larger fraction of total runtime — exactly as the theory predicts.

A notable trend in the ZeCO throughput data: the degradation from 8 to 256 GPUs is more pronounced for 16K sequences (from 44,497 to 34,400, a 22.7% drop) than for 32K sequences (from 47,369 to 40,967, a 13.5% drop). This is consistent with the overlap analysis: at 32K per GPU, there is more local computation (diagonal attention and state recurrence) to fill the overlap window, so a larger fraction of All-Scan latency is hidden. At 16K, the computation window is smaller, so communication is more likely to extend the critical path.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper does not contain explicit ablation studies in the traditional ML sense (removing components and measuring degradation). The evaluation is instead structured as a comparative analysis across SP methods (LASP-1, LASP-2, ZeCO) at varying device scales (8–256 GPUs) and per-device sequence lengths (16K and 32K). The following analyses serve the function of ablations by isolating specific aspects of ZeCO's design:

- **Communication volume vs. communication pattern (Figure 3, Tables 1–2):** By comparing All-Scan against All-Gather at identical tensor sizes, the paper isolates the benefit of scan-based (one state per device pair) vs. broadcast-based ($P$ states per device) communication patterns. The 3.9× speedup at 256 GPUs is a pure consequence of reducing communication volume to the lower bound, independent of computation overlap or scheduling.

- **Serial dependency vs. parallel execution (Tables 3–6, LASP-1 vs. ZeCO comparison):** LASP-1 and ZeCO both communicate exactly one state tensor per device pair (minimal volume), but LASP-1 enforces serial execution while ZeCO enables parallel computation through pipelining. The gap between LASP-1 (113.71 ms at 128 GPUs, 16K) and ZeCO (9.88 ms) isolates the benefit of pipelining over monolithic serial transfer, since communication volume is identical.

- **Overlap effectiveness at different sequence lengths (Tables 3–6, 16K vs. 32K comparison):** ZeCO's operator runtime overhead over DP is ~3.3 ms at 128 GPUs for both 16K and 32K sequences (Tables 3 and 4). This confirms that the uncovered communication latency is dominated by the All-Scan boundary overhead (Equation 12) rather than by the total computation time — if extra computation scaled with sequence length, the 32K overhead would be roughly double the 16K overhead.

- **Scalability of All-Scan pipeline depth (implicit in the communication runtime data, Table 2):** All-Scan's latency grows from 0.226 ms at 8 GPUs to 2.165 ms at 256 GPUs — a 9.6× increase for a 32× increase in device count. The boundary overhead term $(P-1)\tau(d_k \times d_v)/K$ in Equation (12) would predict linear growth with $P$ if $K$ were fixed, but the sublinear growth in practice suggests $K$ is scaled with the system size or that the fixed per-segment overhead is well-matched to the HPC interconnect. The paper does not discuss how $K$ is chosen or whether it was tuned, which is a minor methodological gap.

- **Memory scalability (implicit negative result for LASP-2):** The All-Gather (Megatron) variant runs out of memory at 128 and 256 GPUs (Section 4.1 text), while All-Scan does not. This is a practical robustness advantage: All-Scan's memory footprint per device is $O(d_k \times d_v)$ regardless of $P$, while All-Gather's footprint is $O(P \times d_k \times d_v)$ because it must buffer all incoming state tensors. The paper could have made this comparison quantitative by reporting peak HBM usage, but the qualitative observation still supports ZeCO's scalability claim.

- **DP baseline as the theoretical ceiling (all experiments):** Every experiment includes the data parallelism (DP) baseline, which represents the ideal case where all sequence-length-related communication is zero. This serves as an ablation of the SP concept itself: any gap between a given SP method and DP is the overhead introduced by distributing the sequence. ZeCO's gap is consistently the smallest across all scales and configurations.

**Missing ablations that would strengthen the paper:**
- **Chunk size $C$ variation:** The paper fixes $C=64$ (from the FLA library default) and does not explore how the overlap effectiveness or pipeline efficiency changes with different chunk sizes. Larger chunks would provide more diagonal attention computation to hide communication but might reduce pipeline granularity.
- **Number of pipeline segments $K$:** The communication experiments use a fixed tensor size per segment of 16384, but no ablation over $K$ is presented. This is somewhat surprising given that $K$ directly controls the boundary overhead in Equation (12).
- **Model scale variation:** Only 1B-parameter models are tested. Whether ZeCO's advantages persist, diminish, or amplify at larger model scales (e.g., 7B, 13B, 70B) is an open question. Larger models have larger $d_k$ and $d_v$, which increase communication volume linearly but also increase local computation quadratically — potentially making overlap even more effective.
- **Interconnect variation:** All experiments use H100 GPUs with presumably NVLink intra-node and InfiniBand inter-node (the exact topology is not described). Performance on other interconnects (e.g., standard Ethernet, PCIe-only, or AMD MI300X with Infinity Fabric) is not explored.
- **Combined with other parallelism strategies:** The paper evaluates ZeCO as a standalone SP method. In practice, large-scale training combines SP with tensor parallelism (TP) and data parallelism (DP). How ZeCO interacts with these strategies (e.g., whether All-Scan communication can overlap with TP all-reduce operations) is not studied.

### Critical Assessment

#### Do the Experiments Support the Paper's Central Claims?

**Claim from the abstract: "ZeCO achieves end-to-end near-linear scalability for long sequence training."**

*What the experiments show:* Figure 4 (bottom half) and Tables 5–6 demonstrate that ZeCO's per-GPU throughput declines only modestly from 8 to 256 GPUs — from 47,369 to 40,967 tokens/sec at 32K per GPU (13.5% degradation) and from 44,497 to 34,400 at 16K (22.7% degradation). Total system throughput scales 27.7× for a 32× increase in devices at 32K. This is genuinely impressive and far better than LASP-2 (54.5% of DP at 256 GPUs) or LASP-1 (which becomes unusable).

*What the experiments do NOT show:* The paper does not demonstrate scalability beyond 256 GPUs, nor does it demonstrate that the modest degradation observed (13.5% at 256 GPUs) does not accelerate at larger scales. The boundary overhead term in Equation (12) contains $(P-1)\tau(d_k \times d_v)/K$, which grows with $P$ even if $K$ also scales. At 512 GPUs, 1024 GPUs, or beyond, the residual overhead could become significant. The "near-linear" claim is supported at the tested scales but it is not proven to be asymptotic.

Additionally, the claim applies specifically to the 1B-GLA model with the tested configurations ($C=64$, $H=16$ or $32$, $d=2048$). Whether "near-linear" holds for larger models, different linear attention variants (e.g., Mamba-2, RetNet), or different hardware interconnects is untested.

**Claim from the abstract: "training a model with a 1M sequence length across 64 devices using ZeCO takes roughly the same time as training with an 16k sequence on a single device."**

*What the experiments show:* This claim is an implicit extrapolation from the fixed-per-device-sequence-length evaluation protocol. At 64 GPUs with 16K per GPU (1M total sequence length), ZeCO's per-GPU throughput is 39,832 tokens/sec (Table 5). On a single GPU with 16K sequence, the DP baseline throughput is 47,594 tokens/sec. The ratio is 39,832 / 47,594 ≈ 0.837 — meaning each training iteration on the 1M sequence takes about 1.19× as long per GPU, not "roughly the same time." However, total throughput (tokens processed per second across all GPUs) is 64 × 39,832 ≈ 2.55M tokens/sec versus 47,594 tokens/sec on a single GPU — a 53.5× speedup for 64× more GPUs. The "roughly the same time" likely refers to per-iteration wall-clock time at similar per-GPU sequence lengths, which Table 3 confirms: ZeCO at 64 GPUs with 16K per GPU is 8.04 ms, while DP at a single GPU with 16K is 6.12 ms — a 1.31× difference. At 32K: ZeCO at 64 GPUs is 13.56 ms vs. DP single GPU at 32K is 11.79 ms — a 1.15× difference. These are close but not "roughly the same" under a strict interpretation.

The claim is directionally correct but overstated. The experiments do show that the *marginal cost* of distributing the sequence is small — ZeCO adds only ~2–3 ms of overhead per iteration at 64 GPUs — but this overhead is not zero.

**Claim from the abstract: "on 256 devices with an 8M sequence length, ZeCO achieves a 60% speedup compared to the current state-of-the-art (SOTA) SP method."**

*What the experiments show:* Table 6 at 256 GPUs, 32K per GPU (total 8M sequence length): ZeCO throughput is 40,967 tokens/sec/GPU vs. LASP-2 at 25,402 tokens/sec/GPU. The speedup is $(40967 - 25402) / 25402 = 61.3\%$. This claim is strongly and directly supported by the experimental data. The 9.3× runtime extra cost reduction and 3.9× communication time reduction claimed in Figure 1 (right panel) are also directly sourced from the tables, though the paper does not fully specify which table cells correspond to which bars in the figure — this is a minor presentation issue.

**Claim from Section 3.3 and the paper's title: "Zero Communication Overhead" / "ZeCO achieves zero communication overhead and an optimal SP strategy with minimum extra cost."**

*What the experiments show:* The operator-level experiments (Tables 3 and 4) demonstrate that ZeCO's runtime is consistently within 1–3 ms of the DP lower bound, with the gap growing very slowly as device count increases. At 128 GPUs with 32K per GPU, ZeCO is 15.06 ms vs. DP at 11.74 ms — a 3.32 ms overhead. This overhead represents approximately 0.026 ms per device in the chain, which is near the noise floor of GPU kernel launch and NCCL synchronisation overheads. The throughput experiments (Tables 5 and 6) show ZeCO at 87.9–95.4% of DP across all tested scales — high, but not 100%.

*What the experiments do NOT demonstrate:* The paper does not provide a breakdown of where the residual 3 ms overhead comes from. Is it All-Scan boundary latency (Equation 12), extra computation/I/O (the $\epsilon$ term), stream synchronisation overhead, or load imbalance? Without this decomposition, "zero overhead" is an empirical approximation — close to zero, but measurably non-zero at 128+ GPUs. The paper's theoretical analysis acknowledges this (the $\epsilon$ term in Equation 14 is "negligible" but not zero), but the empirical section does not rigorously attribute the residual gap.

The "optimality" claim is stronger: Tables 1 and the communication volume analysis demonstrate that $d_k \times d_v$ is the lower bound, and ZeCO achieves it. The runtime cost analysis (Equations 14–16) shows ZeCO's theoretical time is within $\tau(d_k \times d_v)$ of ideal, while LASP-1 and LASP-2 have $P \times$ or $P \times \tau(\cdot)$ overhead terms respectively. The experiments validate that this theoretical advantage translates to practice.

#### Genuine Weaknesses

1. **Single model scale and architecture:** Only the 1B-GLA model with specific hyperparameters ($H=16$ or $32$, $d=2048$, $C=64$) is tested. The paper does not demonstrate that the advantages generalise to larger models (where $d_k \times d_v$ grows, potentially changing the communication-to-computation ratio) or to other linear attention variants (Mamba, RetNet, HGRN, etc.). The claim that ZeCO provides a "clear path toward efficiently training next-generation LLMs" (abstract) requires evidence at the scales where next-generation LLMs operate (70B+, potentially 405B+). The 1B scale is suggestive but not conclusive.

2. **No error bars or variance reporting:** All results are reported as single numbers (means over 50 or 100 iterations), with no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or min/max ranges. Training throughput on large GPU clusters often has non-trivial variance due to network congestion, thermal throttling, or system-level interference. Without variance estimates, it is impossible to determine whether a 13.5% throughput degradation at 256 GPUs is statistically distinguishable from a smaller degradation that would strengthen the "near-linear" claim.

3. **Fixed chunk size $C$ and pipeline depth $K$:** The paper does not report how $K$ (number of All-Scan pipeline segments) was chosen or whether it was optimised. The per-segment tensor size of 16384 elements (64 KB) is mentioned, but no ablation over this parameter is presented. Given that $K$ directly controls the boundary overhead term in Equation (12), this is a significant omission. Similarly, chunk size $C=64$ is taken from the FLA library without exploration — larger $C$ would provide more diagonal attention overlap computation but coarser pipeline granularity, and the optimal trade-off is unknown.

4. **Hardware-environment specificity:** All experiments use an H100 80GB GPU cluster. The communication latency and overlap effectiveness are strongly dependent on NVLink bandwidth (intra-node), InfiniBand/network bandwidth (inter-node), and the GPU's ability to concurrently execute communication and computation kernels. Results on A100, H200, B200, or AMD MI300X clusters could differ substantially. The paper does not discuss this sensitivity.

5. **No comparison with full attention SP methods at moderate lengths:** While the paper correctly argues that full attention is computationally prohibitive at ultra-long sequences (Section 2.3), a comparison with Ring Attention or Ulysses at moderate sequence lengths (e.g., 64K–128K total) would help contextualise ZeCO's advantages. The paper argues that linear attention + ZeCO is the right approach for ultra-long sequences, but does not experimentally demonstrate the cross-over point where ZeCO + linear attention surpasses Ring Attention + full attention.

6. **No end-to-end training convergence results:** The paper evaluates throughput and operator runtime, but not whether models trained with ZeCO converge to the same quality as models trained with other SP methods or with data parallelism (DP). Correctness is asserted through the mathematical proof in Appendix A.1, but there is no empirical validation that loss curves, gradient norms, or final model quality are identical across SP methods. This is a common but notable gap in systems papers — numerical equivalence is typically assumed from algebraic correctness, but floating-point non-associativity in distributed settings can introduce subtle differences.

7. **The 1M sequence on 64 GPUs claim is not directly measured:** The paper's headline claim about "1M sequence length across 64 devices" is an extrapolation from the fixed-per-device-length protocol (16K per GPU × 64 GPUs = 1M total). No experiment actually configures 64 GPUs with a 1M total sequence length and measures the per-iteration time against a single-GPU 16K baseline. The numbers support the extrapolation, but the direct comparison is not in the paper.

#### Experiments That Would Strengthen the Paper

1. **Scaling to 512 or 1024 GPUs:** The paper demonstrates impressive scaling to 256 GPUs, but proving "near-linear" scaling requires showing that the throughput degradation does not accelerate at larger $P$. An experiment at 512 GPUs with 16K per GPU (8M total) would be particularly valuable.

2. **Varying chunk size $C$:** An ablation over $C \in \{32, 64, 128, 256\}$ would reveal whether the overlap effectiveness depends on $C$ and whether the optimal $C$ changes with device count or sequence length.

3. **Memory footprint comparison:** A table reporting peak HBM usage for ZeCO vs. LASP-1 vs. LASP-2 at various device counts would quantify the memory efficiency advantage alluded to in the LASP-2 memory-out observation.

4. **Breakdown of the residual 3 ms overhead:** Profiling data showing how much of the ~3 ms gap between ZeCO and DP at 128 GPUs comes from All-Scan boundary latency vs. extra computation/I/O vs. stream synchronisation would validate the theoretical $\epsilon$ claim.

5. **Convergence validation:** Loss curves or perplexity comparisons for a short training run with ZeCO vs. DP vs. LASP-2 would address the unstated concern about numerical correctness in distributed settings.

6. **Multi-node scaling specifically:** The paper does not distinguish intra-node (NVLink) from inter-node (InfiniBand) communication. A breakdown showing All-Scan latency for GPU pairs on the same node vs. across nodes would clarify the network-dependence of the pipeline efficiency.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: Evaluation Restricted to a Single Model Architecture and Scale (1B-GLA)

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments throughout Section 4 evaluate ZeCO exclusively on a 1B-parameter Gated Linear Attention (GLA) model with specific hyperparameters: 20 layers, hidden dimension $d = 2048$, head counts $H = 16$ (operator-level experiments) or $H = 32$ (throughput experiments), chunk size $C = 64$, trained in the Lingua framework using the Flash Linear Attention (FLA) library. The paper does not test ZeCO on any other linear attention variant (such as Mamba, Mamba-2, RetNet, HGRN, or CosFormer), on any other model scale (e.g., 7B, 13B, 70B parameters), or on any model with substantially different hidden dimensions or head counts. The authors acknowledge this implicitly through statements like:

> "We believe ZeCO establishes a clear path toward efficiently training next-generation LLMs on previously intractable sequence lengths."

The word "path" signals that the current experiments demonstrate feasibility and scaling principles on a representative model, but do not constitute direct evidence at the scales where "next-generation LLMs" operate.

**The consequence.** The generalisability of ZeCO's performance characteristics to larger models or different linear attention architectures is uncertain. Several critical parameters change with model scale in ways that could affect the effectiveness of All-Scan's pipelining and overlap:

- **State tensor size $d_k \times d_v$:** In the 1B-GLA model with $H = 32$ and $d = 2048$, each head's state tensor is $64 \times 64 = 4096$ elements per layer. A 70B model with $d = 8192$ and $H = 64$ would have per-head state tensors of $128 \times 128 = 16,384$ elements — 4× larger. Since the unavoidable communication time $\tau(d_k \times d_v)$ in Equation (12) scales linearly with tensor size, the absolute communication latency grows proportionally. Whether the diagonal attention computation — the overlap candidate — also grows by a factor that keeps the overlap window large enough relative to communication depends on how chunk size $C$ and hidden dimension scale. At 1B scale, ZeCO's overlap is highly effective (residual overhead ~3 ms at 128 GPUs); at 70B scale, the relationship could shift.

- **Computation-to-communication ratio:** Linear attention's local computation per device scales as $O(L d^2)$ (for the matrix multiplications in the local recurrence and diagonal attention), while communication scales as $O(d^2)$ (for the state tensor). As $d$ grows, both computation and communication grow quadratically in $d$ — but the constants differ (computation involves $C \times d_k \times d_v$ GEMMs per chunk, while communication involves one state tensor of size $d_k \times d_v$). Whether the ratio improves or degrades with scale depends on the exact relationship between $C$, $L$, and $d$, which the paper does not analyse for larger models.

- **Different linear attention formulations:** GLA uses a specific decay-based recurrence (Equation 1). Other linear attention models have different state update rules. Mamba (Gu and Dao, 2024) uses a selective state space model with input-dependent discretisation; Mamba-2 has a structured state space duality formulation; RetNet (Sun et al., 2023) uses a different decay mechanism. The key mathematical property that ZeCO relies on — the linear decomposition $S_{(p-1)L + nC} = (\tilde{\gamma}^\top_{[n]} \mathbf{1}) \odot S_{(p-1)L} + S_{[n]}$ proven in Appendix A.1 — depends on the linearity and multiplicative decay structure of GLA. Whether analogous decompositions exist for other linear attention formulations is not addressed. If a different formulation lacks this exact linear decomposition property, the minimal-volume communication bound might not hold, or the global state update formula might be more complex.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper provides no experiments at any model scale other than 1B, no experiments with any architecture other than GLA, and no theoretical analysis of how the communication-to-computation ratio changes with model scale. Table 1 (Appendix A.2) provides a parameterised comparison of communication volume and computation cost in terms of $D$ (hidden dimension) and $e$ (per-head dimension), which in principle applies at any scale — but the abstract numbers do not reveal whether the overlap window remains sufficient when $d_k$ and $d_v$ grow.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not claim to have solved the generalisation problem; it presents 1B-GLA as a proof of concept and frames future work as scaling up. The authors state in Section 5 (Conclusion and Future Works):

> "we will further improve the algorithmic implementation of the All-Scan collective communication primitive" and "aim to generalize the sequence parallelism algorithm for linear attention beyond diagonal decay, extending it to support various forms, including matrix transform structures."

These future directions acknowledge the scope limitation but do not mitigate it in the current work.

### Limitation 2: No Empirical Validation of Training Correctness or Numerical Equivalence

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper proves mathematical correctness of ZeCO's global state update formula (Equation 6) through the linear decomposition proof in Appendix A.1, demonstrating that the sequence of operations is algebraically equivalent to the standard GLA computation. However, the paper provides **no empirical validation that models trained with ZeCO actually converge to the same loss, produce the same parameter updates, or achieve the same downstream task performance** as models trained with data parallelism (DP) or with other SP methods (LASP-1, LASP-2). All reported metrics are system-level: communication runtime, operator runtime, and training throughput (tokens per second). The assumption is that algebraic correctness implies empirical correctness.

The paper states in Appendix A.3:

> "All experiments are conducted on a GPU cluster equipped with 256×H100 80GB GPUs. Model is trained in Lingua ... To ensure a fair comparison with baseline sequence parallelism (SP) methods such as LASP1 and LASP2, we adapt the chunk-wise gated linear attention operator from the Flash Linear Attention repository for our implementation."

The word "trained" here refers to measuring throughput during training, not to validating that training produces a working model. There is no mention of loss curves, perplexity, gradient norm comparisons, or any downstream evaluation.

**The consequence.** Distributed training introduces several sources of potential numerical divergence from single-device or data-parallel training, even when the algorithm is algebraically correct:

- **Floating-point non-associativity:** The global update formula $S_{(p-1)L + nC} = (\tilde{\gamma}^\top_{[n]} \mathbf{1}) \odot S_{(p-1)L} + S_{[n]}$ adds two terms that were computed on different devices with different numerical histories. In ZeCO, $S_{[n]}$ is computed starting from $S_{[0]} = 0$ on device $p$, while $S_{(p-1)L}$ was accumulated across devices $0$ through $p-1$. Even though the algebra is exact, floating-point addition is not associative when these terms have different magnitudes (the attenuation factor $\tilde{\gamma}_{[n]}$ can make $S_{(p-1)L}$ much smaller than $S_{[n]}$). The order of operations in ZeCO differs from the order in a single-device forward pass, where all chunks are processed sequentially with $S_{[n]}$ accumulating directly. This could lead to small but accumulating numerical differences that affect gradient quality over many training steps.

- **Gradient correctness in the backward pass:** The backward All-Scan (Algorithm 3) propagates $dS_{[0]}$ through the device chain using the same pipelined pattern. The gradient computation involves matrix multiplications and element-wise operations that are also not associative in floating-point. The recompute optimisation (recomputing $S_{[n]}$ during backward pass using cached $S_{(p-1)L}$ from the forward pass) further changes the numerical path relative to single-device training.

- **Verifier and PRM dependencies:** While the paper does not involve learned verifiers as in the reference example, the model being trained IS a neural network. If ZeCO introduces systematic numerical biases (e.g., consistently rounding in a particular direction due to the addition order), the trained model weights could diverge from the DP-trained equivalent even if per-step differences are within FP32 epsilon.

- **Deadlock or correctness bugs at scale:** The paper tests at up to 256 GPUs. Distributed communication primitives, especially custom ones like All-Scan that involve pipelined send-receive patterns, are notoriously prone to subtle bugs that manifest only at certain scales, under certain network topologies, or with certain tensor size alignments. The fact that All-Scan completes without error and produces throughput numbers does not guarantee that the computed values are correct — only that no assertion failed or no NCCL error was raised.

This limitation matters profoundly for practitioners. If a team adopts ZeCO and trains a 1B model on 8M-token sequences, they need to know whether the resulting model will have the same perplexity as one trained with DP on shorter sequences (or with LASP-2). A 60% throughput improvement is worthless if the trained model is degraded.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper provides no loss curves, no perplexity comparisons, no gradient norm monitoring, no downstream benchmark evaluations, and no assertion that the numerical outputs of the ZeCO operator match the DP operator to within floating-point tolerance. The correctness argument is entirely theoretical (Appendix A.1).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not mention this as a limitation, nor does it suggest future work on numerical validation. This is a significant gap for a systems paper that proposes a new distributed training method — demonstrating that the method is faster is necessary but not sufficient; demonstrating that it produces equivalent models is equally important.

### Limitation 3: Hardware and Interconnect Specificity Without Sensitivity Analysis

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in Section 4 are conducted on a single hardware configuration: "a GPU cluster equipped with 256×H100 80GB GPUs" (Appendix A.3). The paper provides no details about the interconnect topology — whether GPUs within a node communicate via NVLink (and at what bandwidth: NVLink 4.0 on H100 provides 900 GB/s bidirectional) or whether cross-node communication uses InfiniBand (and at what generation: NDR200, NDR400, HDR). The chunk size for All-Scan segmentation is set to a tensor size of 16384 elements (mentioned in the experimental setup paragraphs), but no ablation over this value is presented. The pipeline depth $K$ is not explicitly reported — it is implicitly determined by the state tensor size divided by the segment size, but neither the state tensor dimensions for the communication experiments nor the resulting $K$ are stated.

The paper's theoretical latency analysis (Equation 12) explicitly depends on these hardware parameters through $\tau(\cdot)$, the communication time function, and on the pipeline depth $K$ through the boundary overhead term. Yet the analysis treats $\tau(\cdot)$ as an abstract function without characterising it for the actual hardware used.

**The consequence.** The paper's headline results — 3.9× communication speedup over All-Gather, 60% throughput improvement over LASP-2, near-linear scaling to 256 GPUs — are all contingent on the specific hardware's communication bandwidth, latency characteristics, and the ability to overlap communication with computation. On different hardware, the same method could perform very differently:

- **Weaker interconnects (e.g., PCIe Gen4, standard Ethernet):** The communication time $\tau(d_k \times d_v)$ would be larger, increasing both the first-hop term and the boundary overhead in Equation (12). This would make the overlap window harder to fill — the same diagonal attention computation that successfully hides communication on H100 NVLink might be insufficient on slower interconnects. ZeCO's "zero overhead" claim is relative to the computation time; if communication becomes slower while computation stays fixed, the uncovered portion grows.

- **Stronger interconnects (e.g., NVLink 5.0 on B200, or ultra-high-bandwidth InfiniBand):** Better hardware would reduce $\tau(\cdot)$, making overlap even more effective — ZeCO's advantages over LASP-2 would likely widen because LASP-2's $P \times$ communication volume penalty is a larger fraction of runtime when per-byte transmission is fast but $P$ is large.

- **AMD MI300X or other GPU architectures:** Different GPU architectures have different CUDA stream concurrency capabilities, different shared memory hierarchies, and different communication library implementations (RCCL vs. NCCL). All-Scan's two-stream overlap strategy assumes that a communication kernel (NCCL send/receive) can execute concurrently with a computation kernel (diagonal attention GEMMs) on separate streams without resource contention. This assumption holds well on H100 with NVLink, but may not hold on other architectures where communication and computation share memory bandwidth or where the stream scheduler works differently.

- **Segment size sensitivity:** The pipeline depth $K$ directly controls how much the boundary overhead is amortised. Too few segments (small $K$, large per-segment tensor) and the pipeline is shallow — boundary overhead approaches the serial $P \times \tau(S)$ of LASP-1. Too many segments (large $K$, tiny per-segment tensor) and the per-segment communication overhead (NCCL kernel launch, network header processing) dominates. The optimal $K$ depends on the network's latency-bandwidth characteristics. The paper's fixed segment size of 16384 elements (64 KB) is presumably optimised for their specific H100 cluster; it may be suboptimal elsewhere.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper presents a single data point — the full set of communication latency and throughput numbers on the 256×H100 cluster — without any sensitivity analysis. There is no ablation over segment size, no comparison across different interconnect types (or even disclosure of which interconnect was used for which GPU pairs), and no discussion of how results might change on different hardware generations. The theoretical analysis in Equations (12)–(16) is parameterised in terms of $\tau(\cdot)$ and $K$, which is good, but the paper never instantiates these parameters with measured values from the actual hardware or explores how results would change if these parameters varied.

The All-Gather memory-out at 128+ GPUs for the Megatron variant (Section 4.1) is a hardware-dependent failure: the HBM capacity of 80GB per H100 is what runs out when storing $P$ state tensors. On H200 (141GB HBM) or B200 (192GB HBM), LASP-2 might not hit this memory limit at the same scale, which would change the practical comparison. ZeCO's memory footprint advantage is contingent on the specific HBM capacity of the hardware used.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed theoretically, not empirically. The paper's theoretical analysis provides a framework for predicting performance on different hardware (given $\tau(\cdot)$ and $K$), but no experimental validation of this framework's predictive accuracy on different hardware configurations. The future work statement about "investigate efficient parallel topologies for sequence parallelism in large-scale models" (Section 5) hints at hardware-topology awareness but does not commit to multi-hardware evaluation.

### Limitation 4: The "Zero Overhead" Claim Relies on Overlap That Is Not Guaranteed for All Configurations

**The assumption or constraint.** ZeCO's claim of "zero communication overhead" — operationalised as achieving near-DP throughput — rests on the assumption that the local intra-chunk diagonal attention computation (the overlap candidate running on stream 2 in Algorithm 1) is sufficiently large to **completely hide** the All-Scan communication latency. The paper's theoretical analysis in Equation (11) explicitly acknowledges this dependence:

$$T^P_{\text{SP}}(PL) = T^P_{\text{ideal-SP}}(PL) + T_{\text{extra\_comp\&I/O}} + (T_{\text{All\_Scan}} - T_{\text{overlaped\_comp}})$$

and the approximation in Equation (14) that $T^P_{\text{ZeCO}}(PL) \approx T^1_{\text{ideal-SP}}(L) - T_{\text{overlaped\_comp}} + \tau(d_k \times d_v)$ implicitly assumes $T_{\text{overlaped\_comp}} \geq T_{\text{All\_Scan}} - \tau(d_k \times d_v)$ — that is, the overlap computation is long enough to cover the pipeline boundary overhead. When this assumption holds, the residual $(T_{\text{All\_Scan}} - T_{\text{overlaped\_comp}})$ is zero or negative (fully hidden). When it does not hold, the uncovered portion adds directly to the critical path.

**The consequence.** The overlap sufficiency is configuration-dependent and can fail in several practically relevant regimes:

- **Short per-device sequence lengths:** With $L = 4\text{K}$ or $8\text{K}$ per GPU, the total diagonal attention computation is proportional to $N \times C \times d_k \times d_v = L \times d_k \times d_v$. At very short $L$, this computation shrinks linearly while the All-Scan communication latency (dominated by the first-hop term $\tau(d_k \times d_v)$) stays roughly constant. The 16K vs. 32K comparison in the paper's own data shows this effect: ZeCO's throughput degradation from 8 to 256 GPUs is 22.7% for 16K sequences vs. 13.5% for 32K sequences (Tables 5 and 6). Extrapolating, at $L = 4\text{K}$ the overlap would be even less effective, and at $L = 1\text{K}$ it might be essentially zero — ZeCO would degenerate to something close to serial execution plus uncovered communication, more like LASP-1 with extra overhead.

- **Small chunk sizes:** The diagonal attention computation per chunk is $O(C \times d_k \times d_v)$ for the query-key product and $O(C \times d_v \times d_v)$ for the query-state product. Smaller $C$ means less computation per chunk to overlap against fixed communication segments. The paper fixes $C = 64$ throughout without exploring this dimension. At $C = 16$ or $C = 32$, the overlap computation per segment of All-Scan pipeline would be proportionally smaller.

- **Shallow models or single-layer operators:** The operator-level experiments (Tables 3 and 4) measure only the attention operator, not the full model. In a full model with 20 layers, the communication occurs once per layer, but the diagonal attention computation also occurs once per layer — the ratio is constant. However, if All-Scan communication from layer $\ell$ contends for network bandwidth with All-Scan communication from layer $\ell+1$ (if multiple layers are pipelined or overlapped at the model level), the effective $\tau(\cdot)$ increases, potentially breaking the overlap assumption.

- **Large $d_k \times d_v$ relative to $L \times d_k \times d_v$:** As model dimension grows, both state tensor size and diagonal attention computation grow — but state tensor size grows as $d_k \times d_v = (d/H)^2$, while diagonal attention computation grows as $L \times (d/H)^2$. For very large $d/H$ and short $L$, the communication might be a larger fraction of total work. This is relevant for wide-but-shallow models (large hidden dimension, few layers, moderate sequence lengths).

The gap between ZeCO's operator runtime and the DP baseline in Tables 3 and 4 — approximately 3 ms at 128 GPUs — is the empirical manifestation of incomplete overlap. This 3 ms is small relative to total operator time (15–20 ms), but it is non-zero, and the paper's theoretical analysis predicts it should grow as $(P-1)\tau(d_k \times d_v)/K$ when overlap is insufficient. At larger device counts (512, 1024 GPUs), this boundary overhead could become significant even if it is currently small.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper implicitly acknowledges this limitation through the gap between the "zero overhead" title and the actual measurements: ZeCO operator runtime is consistently 1–3 ms above the DP baseline across all scales (Tables 3 and 4), and per-GPU throughput is consistently 5–20% below DP (Tables 5 and 6). The worsening degradation from 32K to 16K sequences is direct evidence that overlap effectiveness depends on the computation-to-communication ratio. However, the paper does not explicitly discuss when overlap might fail, does not provide a breakdown of the residual gap, and does not characterise the conditions under which ZeCO would lose its advantage over LASP-2.

**Mitigation status.** Partially mitigated through the theoretical framework but not empirically bounded. The paper's Equation (12) provides the correct theoretical model for understanding when overlap will be sufficient — the boundary overhead term $(P-1)\tau(d_k \times d_v)/K$ must be less than the diagonal attention computation time. But the paper does not provide a formula for the diagonal attention computation time in terms of $L$, $C$, $d_k$, $d_v$, and hardware FLOPs, which would be needed to predict the crossover point. The future work direction about "further improve the algorithmic implementation of the All-Scan collective communication primitive" and "tree-like implementation" (Section 5) could potentially reduce the boundary overhead further (tree-based scan has $\log(P)$ rather than $P$ depth), which would make overlap easier to achieve.

### Limitation 5: No Analysis of Interaction with Other Parallelism Strategies (Tensor, Pipeline, Data, Expert Parallelism)

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates ZeCO as a standalone sequence parallelism (SP) method, always compared against either data parallelism (DP) as the theoretical upper bound or other SP methods (LASP-1, LASP-2) as baselines. In all throughput experiments (Tables 5 and 6), the model uses **only SP combined with DP** — there is no tensor parallelism (TP), pipeline parallelism (PP), or expert parallelism (EP) in the training configuration. The paper states:

> "The throughput of the GLA model uses DP in training, serves as the theoretical upper bound."

This is a reasonable setup for isolating and evaluating SP performance, but it does not represent how large models are trained in practice. Real-world LLM training at scale (e.g., Llama 3 405B, DeepSeek-V3, GPT-4) combines multiple parallelism strategies simultaneously: TP splits individual weight matrices across GPUs within a node, PP pipelines different layers across devices, DP replicates the model across data batches, and EP distributes mixture-of-experts layers.

**The consequence.** ZeCO's communication patterns interact with these other parallelism strategies in ways that are not characterised and could reduce its practical advantage:

- **Tensor parallelism (TP):** TP typically uses All-Reduce or Reduce-Scatter within a TP group to synchronise activations and gradients. If ZeCO's All-Scan communication occurs concurrently with TP's All-Reduce, they compete for the same network bandwidth (NVLink within a node). The two-stream overlap strategy in ZeCO assumes that All-Scan communication gets dedicated bandwidth on one CUDA stream while computation runs on another. If a TP All-Reduce is also competing for NVLink bandwidth on a third stream, the effective $\tau(d_k \times d_v)$ increases, potentially breaking the overlap assumption and exposing communication latency. ZeCO's "zero overhead" claim relies on communication bandwidth not being a bottleneck; adding TP communication alongside All-Scan could create exactly that bottleneck.

- **Pipeline parallelism (PP):** PP introduces idle "bubbles" at pipeline stage boundaries. If ZeCO's SP communication latency (even if small) additively extends the forward pass time on each stage, the pipeline bubble grows proportionally. In a PP regime with many microbatches, this effect is amortised, but the interaction between SP communication schedule and PP microbatch scheduling is non-trivial. A naive combination could serialise SP and PP communication steps, losing the overlap benefits.

- **Data parallelism (DP) with gradient All-Reduce:** The paper's throughput experiments already combine SP with DP, but the DP gradient synchronisation (All-Reduce over all 256 GPUs) is a separate communication phase that occurs after the backward pass. The experiments show that even with this DP communication included, ZeCO achieves near-DP throughput — meaning the DP All-Reduce overhead is small relative to computation. However, this assumes that All-Scan (for SP) and the DP gradient All-Reduce happen at different times (SP during forward/backward attention, DP gradient sync after backward). In more sophisticated overlapping schemes (e.g., overlapping gradient All-Reduce with the backward pass of the next layer), SP and DP communication could overlap in time and compete for bandwidth.

- **Expert parallelism (EP) in mixture-of-experts models:** MoE models route tokens to different experts, creating irregular communication patterns (All-to-All for token dispatching and combining). If ZeCO's SP distributes sequence chunks across devices that are also handling different expert subsets, the sequence chunks assigned to a device may not be contiguous in the global token sequence, breaking the assumption of a simple predecessor-successor relationship in All-Scan. The global state $S_{(p-1)L}$ would represent the state after processing all tokens on device $p-1$, but those tokens might not be a contiguous prefix if EP routing has interspersed tokens from other parts of the sequence.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not mention TP, PP, or EP anywhere. The related work section (Section 2.2) discusses sequence parallelism in isolation, and the experimental section (Section 4) consistently uses only SP + DP configurations. The unified analysis in Table 1 and Appendix A.2 compares SP methods only against each other and against the DP baseline, not against combined parallelism strategies.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not even acknowledge this as a limitation, despite the practical reality that any large-scale training run would combine SP with at least TP and likely PP or EP. The future work statement about "investigate efficient parallel topologies for sequence parallelism in large-scale models" (Section 5) could potentially encompass this, but it is vague and does not commit to studying combined parallelism strategies.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

ZeCO does not propose a new attention mechanism, a new model architecture, or a new training objective. It changes nothing about what a linear attention model *computes*. Yet it fundamentally changes what is *practically trainable* with linear attention, and in doing so, it reframes the distributed training bottleneck from one of computational complexity to one of communication optimality. This is a **systems-level paradigm clarification**, not a paradigm shift — but it is a clarification with sharp consequences for where the field should invest effort.

Before ZeCO, the narrative around sequence parallelism for linear attention was one of frustrating trade-offs. LASP-1 showed that you could distribute a sequence across devices with minimal communication, but only if you accepted serial execution that destroyed parallel speedup. LASP-2 showed that you could get parallel computation, but only if you accepted communication volume growing linearly with device count, which destroyed scalability at moderate-to-large scales. These two approaches appeared to bound the possible: the field faced a choice between two failure modes, with no clear path to avoiding both simultaneously. The natural assumption was that sequence parallelism for linear attention was inherently difficult — that the sequential dependency in the recurrence created an unavoidable tension between communication volume and parallelism.

ZeCO disproves this assumption. The paper demonstrates that the apparent tension was an artifact of design choices in LASP-1 and LASP-2, not a fundamental property of linear attention recurrence. By partitioning the state tensor along the decay dimension and pipelining the scan, ZeCO achieves both minimal communication volume (the information-theoretic lower bound of $d_k \times d_v$ per device) AND parallel computation AND communication-computation overlap. The three desiderata are not in tension at all — they are simultaneously achievable, and ZeCO is a constructive proof of that fact.

This changes the landscape in four specific ways:

**First, it establishes a formal optimality benchmark for all future SP methods for linear attention.** The paper does not merely claim ZeCO is "better" than LASP-2 — it proves that ZeCO's communication volume sits at the information-theoretic lower bound (Equation 9), and that no correct SP algorithm can communicate less. This converts sequence parallelism from an engineering problem (where any reduction in communication is progress) to an optimisation problem with a known global minimum (where the only question is whether you can achieve the lower bound in practice with sufficient overlap). Any future SP method that communicates more than $d_k \times d_v$ per device is provably suboptimal in volume, regardless of its other merits. This is analogous to how Shannon's channel capacity theorem reframed communication from "build a better modem" to "how close can we get to the capacity bound" — it provides a ceiling that all subsequent work must respect.

**Second, it identifies the specific mathematical property that makes minimal-volume communication possible.** The linear decomposition $S_{(p-1)L + nC} = (\tilde{\gamma}^\top_{[n]} \mathbf{1}) \odot S_{(p-1)L} + S_{[n]}$ (Equation 6, proven in Appendix A.1) is not just an algebraic identity — it is the structural property that makes the entire prior sequence compressible into a single state tensor without information loss. This property is specific to linear attention with multiplicative decay; it does not hold for softmax attention (where the nonlinearity couples all tokens) or for arbitrary RNNs (where the state update may not decompose linearly under non-zero initial conditions). By identifying this property explicitly, the paper draws a bright line: linear attention models that satisfy this decomposition (GLA, RetNet, potentially Mamba-2 with appropriate reformulation) can achieve optimal SP; models that do not satisfy it may require fundamentally different SP strategies. This directs research attention toward characterising which linear attention variants admit the decomposition, rather than treating all linear attentions as interchangeable.

**Third, it demonstrates that communication volume, not computation, is the primary bottleneck for distributed linear attention training — and that this bottleneck is solvable.** The quadratic complexity of full attention was the original bottleneck that motivated linear attention. ZeCO's results show that once linear attention removes the $O(L^2)$ FLOPs barrier, the next barrier — SP communication volume — is comparably severe if left unaddressed (LASP-2 degrades to 54.5% of DP throughput at 256 GPUs) but nearly completely solvable with the right primitives (ZeCO maintains 87.9% of DP throughput at the same scale). This implies that the *theoretical* efficiency of linear attention (linear FLOPs in $L$) can be realised in *practical* distributed systems with ZeCO-style communication. The gap between algorithmic promise and system reality — which the paper's introduction identifies as the core motivation — is largely closed.

**Fourth, it reframes the role of communication primitives in distributed ML systems.** All-Scan is not a generic collective like All-Reduce or All-Gather; it is purpose-built for a specific computation pattern (distributed prefix scan with multiplicative state decay) that arises from linear attention's recurrence structure. The fact that a custom primitive dramatically outperforms generic collectives (3.9× faster than All-Gather at 256 GPUs) suggests that **the era of building distributed training systems entirely from standard MPI/NCCL collectives may be insufficient for next-generation architectures**. As models become more structurally diverse (linear attention, state space models, structured matrices, mixture-of-experts), the communication patterns they require may not fit neatly into All-Reduce, All-Gather, or Reduce-Scatter templates. ZeCO demonstrates that investing in custom communication primitives matched to the mathematics of the model can yield order-of-magnitude improvements — a lesson that likely generalises beyond linear attention.

**What this work does NOT change.** ZeCO does not make long-context training free or trivial. The 13.5% throughput degradation from 8 to 256 GPUs with 32K per GPU is excellent by the standards of distributed systems, but it is not zero. The residual ~3 ms overhead per operator invocation at 128 GPUs represents real communication that could not be overlapped. And critically, ZeCO does not solve the *sample efficiency* or *optimisation* challenges of long-context training — it only ensures that the per-iteration cost grows near-linearly with device count. A model trained on 8M-token sequences may still require careful learning rate schedules, positional encoding adaptations, or curriculum strategies that ZeCO does not address. The paper's contribution is to remove the system-level bottleneck so that these algorithmic questions can be studied at previously intractable scales.

**Which research directions become more attractive.** The existence of a provably optimal SP method for linear attention makes several directions newly tractable or newly important:

- **Pretraining from scratch on long sequences** (rather than mid-training adaptation) becomes economically viable, since the per-iteration cost no longer explodes with device count. This shifts the research question from "can we afford to train on long sequences?" to "what do models learn differently when pretrained on long sequences from the start?"
- **Verifier and search-based methods for ultra-long reasoning** (building on the reference paper's test-time compute scaling framework) can now operate on substantially longer contexts, since the underlying model training is no longer bottlenecked.
- **Architecture co-design** — designing new linear attention variants with the explicit goal of making them ZeCO-compatible (satisfying the linear decomposition property) becomes a concrete design criterion.
- **Custom collective primitives for other model families** (state space models, structured recurrences, long convolutions) becomes a recognised research paradigm, with All-Scan serving as a template.

**Which directions become less attractive.** The paper effectively obsoletes research into P2P-chain (LASP-1-style) SP methods for linear attention, and significantly reduces the appeal of All-Gather-based (LASP-2-style) approaches. The former is provably bottlenecked by serial execution; the latter is provably suboptimal in communication volume and empirically worse at scale. The paper also casts doubt on the wisdom of building SP systems exclusively from generic collectives — the 3.9× gap between All-Scan and All-Gather at 256 GPUs is large enough that engineering effort spent optimising All-Gather for SP scenarios would be better spent implementing scan-specific primitives.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterising which linear attention variants admit the ZeCO decomposition and implementing All-Scan for each.** The paper proves the linear decomposition property for Gated Linear Attention (GLA) with multiplicative diagonal decay. However, the family of linear attention models is large and growing: Mamba-2 uses a structured state space duality formulation with a different discretisation scheme; RetNet uses a distinct decay mechanism based on relative position; HGRN and HGRN2 introduce gating and state expansion; CosFormer uses cosine-based positional reweighting. For each variant, the key question is whether there exists an analogous decomposition $S_{\text{global}} = (\text{decay}) \odot S_{\text{incoming}} + S_{\text{local}}$ where the "decay" factor is computable from purely local information. If the decomposition exists, ZeCO's All-Scan can be applied with minimal modification (only the update formula changes). If it does not — for instance, if the state update involves nonlinear gating that couples the incoming state to the local computation in a non-decomposable way — then achieving minimal-volume SP may require a different approach, or the lower bound itself may be different. A strong follow-up would systematically categorise 5–8 popular linear attention variants, prove or disprove the decomposition property for each, implement ZeCO-style SP for those where it holds, and measure the throughput improvement over naive SP. This would convert ZeCO from a point solution for GLA into a general framework for linear attention SP, with clear eligibility criteria.

**Scaling ZeCO to 70B+ models and measuring the computation-to-communication ratio at scale.** The paper's experiments are confined to a 1B-parameter model with $d = 2048$ and $H = 32$. At 70B scale with $d = 8192$ and $H = 64$ (typical for LLaMA-class models), the state tensor per head grows from $64 \times 64$ to $128 \times 128$ — a 4× increase in elements per state tensor. The communication time $\tau(d_k \times d_v)$ grows linearly with tensor size, while the diagonal attention computation grows as $L \times (d/H)^2$ — also 4× for the same $L$. The unresolved question is whether the *overlap window* remains sufficient: the pipeline boundary overhead $(P-1)\tau(d_k \times d_v)/K$ grows with tensor size, but so does the computation available for overlap. At 1B scale, ZeCO's residual overhead is ~3 ms at 128 GPUs (Table 3). At 70B scale, if the ratio of communication to computation stays roughly constant, the absolute overhead may grow proportionally — perhaps to ~12 ms — which could represent a larger fraction of total operator time if the operator is not perfectly compute-bound at the larger hidden dimension. A strong follow-up would implement ZeCO for a 7B or 13B GLA model (or a linear-attention-adapted LLaMA), measure per-GPU throughput at 64–256 GPUs with 32K–128K per-device sequences, and compare the throughput retention (vs. DP baseline) against the 1B results. If retention degrades (e.g., 70% of DP at 256 GPUs vs. 87.9% at 1B), it would indicate that the overlap assumption weakens at scale, motivating research into hierarchical All-Scan or tree-based scan topologies that reduce the $P$ factor in the boundary overhead to $\log P$.

**Tree-based and hierarchical All-Scan to eliminate the $P$-dependent boundary overhead.** The paper's Equation (12) shows that All-Scan's latency contains a term $(P-1)\tau(d_k \times d_v)/K$ that grows linearly with device count. The paper mentions in Section 5 that "tree-like implementation" is a future direction. The idea is to replace the linear chain of device-to-device communication with a tree topology: instead of device $p$ waiting for device $p-1$ (chain), device $k$ in a binary tree receives partial scan results from its two children, combines them, and sends the combined result to its parent. This reduces the dependency depth from $O(P)$ to $O(\log P)$, which would make the boundary overhead term scale as $\log(P)$ instead of $P$. The challenge is that the state combination operation is not a simple associative reduction (like a sum) — it involves the cumulative decay factor $\tilde{\gamma}$, which depends on the *order* of chunks. A tree-based scan must correctly propagate and combine these ordered decay factors, which may require communicating additional metadata (the $\tilde{\gamma}$ vectors) alongside the state tensors. A strong follow-up would design a tree-based All-Scan variant, prove its correctness for ordered cumulative decay propagation, implement it, and measure whether it reduces the residual overhead at 256–512 GPUs below the current ~3 ms. The key metric would be whether tree-based All-Scan enables >95% DP throughput retention at 512 GPUs, which would make ZeCO truly "near-linear" at scales relevant to the largest training runs.

**Empirical convergence validation for ZeCO-trained models against DP-trained baselines.** This is the most important missing experiment from the current paper, as discussed in Limitation 2 (Section 6.4 of the prior sections). The paper proves algebraic correctness but provides no loss curves, perplexity comparisons, or downstream evaluations. A critical follow-up would train a 1B-GLA model on a standard corpus (e.g., C4 or The Pile) for 10K–50K steps using ZeCO SP at a large total sequence length (e.g., 1M tokens across 64 GPUs), and compare the training loss, validation perplexity, and downstream benchmark performance (e.g., LAMBADA, HellaSwag, or long-context retrieval tasks) against an identical model trained with pure data parallelism (DP) on shorter sequences (e.g., 16K tokens on a single GPU, matching the per-device length). The comparison must control for total tokens seen, not iterations, since ZeCO processes more tokens per iteration. The key question: does ZeCO training converge to the same loss within floating-point noise, or does the altered addition order in the global state update (Equation 6) introduce systematic numerical differences that compound over many steps? If differences emerge, the follow-up would need to diagnose whether they stem from the decomposition itself (unlikely, since it is algebraically exact), from floating-point non-associativity in the state accumulation, or from gradient propagation differences in the backward All-Scan. A null result (perfect convergence match) would be the strongest possible validation of ZeCO's correctness; a non-null result would identify specific operations that need numerical stabilisation (e.g., Kahan summation for the state accumulation, or mixed-precision strategies for the decay-scaled addition).

**ZeCO-aware architecture design — linear attention variants with explicit ZeCO compatibility as a first-class constraint.** The paper's decomposition proof in Appendix A.1 relies on a specific structure: the recurrence applies a *multiplicative* decay that is *independent per channel* (the $d_k$ dimension) and *commutes with the state update* (so that starting from a non-zero initial state simply adds an attenuated copy). This structure was not designed with ZeCO in mind; it happens to be present in GLA because multiplicative decay is natural for linear attention. But future linear attention architectures could be *designed* to make the decomposition as clean and numerically stable as possible. For instance: using exponential decay in log-space (where the cumulative product becomes a cumulative sum) could improve numerical precision for very long sequences; using structured decay matrices with low-rank structure could reduce state tensor size; using gating mechanisms that are "ZeCO-aware" (i.e., the gate depends only on local information, not on the global state) could preserve decomposability while adding expressivity. A strong follow-up would propose a new linear attention variant with the explicit goal of maximising ZeCO throughput while maintaining or improving language modelling perplexity. The evaluation would compare the proposed variant against GLA under identical ZeCO SP configurations, measuring both throughput (tokens/sec) and model quality (perplexity on long-context benchmarks), to determine whether "ZeCO-friendliness" imposes an expressivity cost or can be achieved for free.

**Cross-hardware and cross-interconnect benchmarking of All-Scan vs. All-Gather.** The paper's experiments are on a single cluster of 256× H100 GPUs with an unspecified interconnect. The theoretical analysis (Equations 12–16) makes clear predictions about how All-Scan's advantage over All-Gather should scale with hardware parameters: the gap should widen as network bandwidth increases (because All-Gather's $P \times$ volume penalty becomes a larger fraction of total time when per-byte transmission is cheap) and as network latency decreases (because All-Scan's pipeline boundary overhead benefits more from low-latency transfers). A systematic benchmark across hardware generations would test these predictions: H100 (NVLink 4.0, 900 GB/s), A100 (NVLink 3.0, 600 GB/s), H200 (NVLink 4.0, 900 GB/s, more HBM), and if available, B200 (NVLink 5.0, 1.8 TB/s). For each hardware configuration, measure All-Scan and All-Gather communication latency at 8–256 GPUs, and measure end-to-end training throughput for a 1B-GLA model. The prediction: the All-Scan/All-Gather speedup ratio should increase with each hardware generation, because All-Scan's volume is constant while All-Gather's volume grows with $P$, and faster interconnects make the volume difference more salient. If this prediction holds, it strengthens the argument that All-Scan is not just better on current hardware but *increasingly* better as hardware evolves — a forward-compatible design choice. If it does not hold (e.g., All-Gather catches up on faster hardware due to better pipelining within NCCL), it would suggest that All-Scan's advantage is partially an artifact of NCCL's current All-Gather implementation rather than a fundamental volume advantage, which would temper the optimality claims.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Pretraining LLMs from scratch on million-token sequences.** The most direct application of ZeCO is enabling long-context pretraining as a first-class training paradigm rather than a post-hoc adaptation. Currently, models like Llama 3, Qwen 2.5, and DeepSeek-V3 are pretrained on moderate-length sequences (typically 4K–8K tokens) and then undergo a separate "mid-training" or "continued pretraining" phase on longer sequences (32K–128K) to extend their context window. This two-phase approach is suboptimal: the model learns its fundamental representations on short contexts and must later adapt them to long-range dependencies. With ZeCO, a 1M-token sequence can be distributed across 64 GPUs with per-iteration time roughly comparable to a 16K sequence on a single GPU (the paper's claim, supported by the operator runtime data in Table 3). While the absolute training time for a full pretraining run on 1M-token sequences would still be enormous (1M tokens per sample means fewer samples for a given token budget, requiring careful data packing), ZeCO removes the *system-level* barrier that made such training infeasible. An organisation with a 256-GPU H100 cluster could pretrain a 1B model on 8M-token sequences at ~41K tokens/sec/GPU (Table 6), or ~10.5M tokens/sec total — processing roughly 30 billion tokens per GPU per day. This makes long-context pretraining economically plausible at moderate scale, and the economics improve further with ZeCO's near-linear scaling to larger GPU counts.

**Cost-efficient fine-tuning and adaptation on domain-specific long documents.** Many practical applications involve processing very long individual documents that exceed standard context windows: legal contract analysis (documents of 100K+ words), scientific paper summarisation with full-text context, long-form video transcription and understanding, and genomic sequence modelling. Fine-tuning a linear attention model on such documents using ZeCO SP means the per-example cost stays manageable even as individual examples grow to millions of tokens. Without ZeCO, training on a dataset of 1M-token legal documents across 64 GPUs would incur LASP-1's serial penalty (P × slowdown) or LASP-2's communication volume penalty (P × data transfer), both of which make the training economically unattractive. With ZeCO, the marginal cost of distributing long documents is comparable to the cost of processing them on a single GPU — the 60% throughput improvement over LASP-2 (Table 6, 256 GPUs) translates directly to a 60% reduction in cloud GPU rental costs for the same fine-tuning job.

**Enabling research on long-context reasoning and in-context learning at scale.** The reference paper on test-time compute scaling (Snell et al., 2024) studies how to optimally allocate inference computation for math reasoning, with experiments on sequences up to a few thousand tokens. ZeCO opens the possibility of studying test-time compute scaling on *orders of magnitude longer* reasoning traces — for instance, chain-of-thought reasoning that spans hundreds of thousands of tokens, or in-context learning with thousands of examples. The bottleneck for such research shifts from "can we efficiently process a single ultra-long sequence during training?" (solved by ZeCO) to "can we design reasoning algorithms and in-context learning strategies that benefit from such long contexts?" This could accelerate research into long-horizon planning, multi-step tool use, and memory-augmented reasoning — all of which require models that have been trained to handle very long coherent contexts, not just models that have had their context window extended post-hoc.

**Deployment of linear attention models for streaming and real-time long-context applications.** While the paper focuses on training, the All-Scan primitive is equally applicable to inference — serving a model that processes a continuous stream of tokens (e.g., real-time transcription, video understanding, sensor data processing) across multiple GPUs. In a streaming setting where new tokens arrive continuously, All-Scan's pipelined scan pattern allows each device to update its portion of the sequence state as new chunks arrive, without requiring global synchronisation across all devices. The fixed state tensor size and minimal communication volume mean the inference system can maintain high throughput even as the total context grows arbitrarily long, since the per-step work is constant in sequence length. This is a natural complement to the training efficiency ZeCO provides: models trained efficiently on ultra-long sequences using ZeCO can be deployed efficiently on streaming ultra-long sequences using the same All-Scan pattern.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions ZeCO against two named alternatives — LASP-1 (serial P2P) and LASP-2 (All-Gather-based) — with a clear optimality argument that ZeCO dominates both on all quantitative metrics (communication volume, communication latency, operator runtime, model throughput) across all tested device counts (8–256) and sequence lengths (16K–32K per GPU). There is no experiment where LASP-1 or LASP-2 outperforms ZeCO, and the theoretical analysis in Section 3.3 proves that both alternatives are fundamentally suboptimal (LASP-1 due to serial dependency, LASP-2 due to communication volume scaling with $P$). The paper's position is therefore not a trade-off between ZeCO and alternatives, but a categorical recommendation: **for sequence parallelism with linear attention models, ZeCO is the preferred method.**

The only nuance, which the paper does not fully articulate but which emerges from the limitations analysis, is that **ZeCO's advantage depends on the linear attention variant satisfying the decomposition property** (Equation 6 and Appendix A.1). For GLA and models with similar multiplicative diagonal decay, ZeCO is provably optimal. For other linear attention architectures where the decomposition may not hold or may be more complex, ZeCO's minimal-volume property is not guaranteed — the information-theoretic lower bound might differ, or the global state update might require more communication. In such cases, a method-specific analysis would be needed. The paper does not provide guidance on how to determine whether a given linear attention variant is ZeCO-compatible, which is a gap for practitioners adopting the method for non-GLA architectures.

- **Prefer ZeCO over LASP-1 or LASP-2 when training linear attention models (GLA or compatible variants) on sequences where the per-device length is at least ~16K tokens.** Below this threshold, the overlap window shrinks and the communication-to-computation ratio worsens, potentially reducing ZeCO's advantage. The 16K vs. 32K comparison in the paper (Tables 5 and 6) shows ZeCO retains 80.3% of DP throughput at 256 GPUs for 16K sequences vs. 87.9% for 32K — both are good, but the trend suggests very short per-device sequences (4K or less) might not provide enough local computation to hide All-Scan latency.

- **ZeCO is especially preferred at large device counts ($P \ge 64$)**, where LASP-2's communication volume penalty ($P \times d_k \times d_v$) becomes severe and LASP-1's serial execution becomes unworkable. At small device counts ($P = 4$ or $8$), the absolute differences between methods shrink, and implementation complexity may weigh against adopting a custom communication primitive.

- **ZeCO's advantages amplify with longer sequences and larger hidden dimensions**, since the communication-to-computation ratio improves in both cases (more local computation to overlap against fixed communication volume). This means ZeCO is not just better than alternatives today but becomes *increasingly* better as the field pushes toward longer contexts and larger models — a forward-compatible design choice.
