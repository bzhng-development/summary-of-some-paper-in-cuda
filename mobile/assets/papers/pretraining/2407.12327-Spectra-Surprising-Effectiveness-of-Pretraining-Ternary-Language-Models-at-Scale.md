# Spectra: Surprising Effectiveness of Pretraining Ternary Language Models at Scale

**ArXiv:** [2407.12327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.12327)

## 🎯 Pitch

At over a billion parameters, ternary language models (weights in {-1,0,1}) trained from scratch consistently beat post-training quantized models of the *same bit size*, and a 3.9B ternary model matches a full-precision 3.9B model while occupying fewer bits than an 830M FP16 model. This flips the script on 4-bit quantization bottlenecks by showing the scaling *gap narrows* with scale—pretraining in extreme low-bitwidth is a viable and surprisingly powerful path to compact LLMs.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces and systematically evaluates **Ternary Language Models (TriLMs)** — models whose linear layer weights are constrained to the set {−1, 0, +1} during training via on-the-fly ternarization with a learned per-matrix scale — as an alternative to both standard floating-point models (FloatLMs) and post-training quantized models (QuantLMs) for addressing the memory-capacity and memory-bandwidth bottlenecks that dominate LLM inference. The experimental substrate is the **Spectra LLM suite**, comprising 54 models spanning 99M to 3.9B parameters across three families (FloatLM, QuantLM at 3/4/6/8 bits via GPTQ, and TriLM), all trained on an identical 300B-token subset of SlimPajama and evaluated on commonsense reasoning, knowledge, and toxicity benchmarks. The paper's central scaling analysis reveals that TriLMs exhibit superior scaling behavior in the practically critical dimension of model bit-size — the 3.9B TriLM matches the 3.9B FloatLM across all benchmarks while occupying fewer bits than an 830M FloatLM (a ~5.9× compression) — and that the gap in validation perplexity between TriLMs and FloatLMs narrows asymptotically with parameter count, establishing that pretraining low-bitwidth models can substitute for post-training quantization at scale, but only when the parameter budget is large enough to compensate for the reduced per-weight information capacity.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Memory, Not Compute, Is the Bottleneck for LLM Inference

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is structural rather than algorithmic: **the growth in GPU computational power (FLOPs) has dramatically outstripped growth in memory capacity and memory bandwidth**, and this divergence is making LLM deployment increasingly memory-bound rather than compute-bound. The authors cite quantitative evidence for this trend: GPU FLOPs double approximately every 1.26 years, while memory capacity doubles every 2 years and memory bandwidth doubles every 2.9 years (Section 1, citing Gholami et al., 2024). This creates a scenario where the hardware can perform computations faster than it can feed data from memory to the processing units — a phenomenon known as the **memory wall**.

This matters for LLM inference in two distinct ways:

1. **Memory capacity bottleneck:** The model must fit in GPU memory. A FloatLM at 340B parameters (the size of Nemotron 4) exceeds the memory capacity of a single 8×H100 node. As Figure 2a shows, a standard FP16 model exhausts an H100's 80GB at just 34B parameters. Organizations deploying large models must therefore use multi-GPU setups (with associated communication overhead, cost, and engineering complexity) or sacrifice model scale.

2. **Memory bandwidth bottleneck:** Even when the model fits in memory, autoregressive token generation — where each token depends on the previous one — is dominated by the rate at which model weights can be streamed from memory to the processor, not by how fast the processor can compute. Kim et al. (2024) formally established this: token generation speed in autoregressive LLMs is bottlenecked by memory bandwidth, not FLOPs. This means that techniques which reduce the model's memory footprint can yield **inference speedups approximately proportional to the compression factor**, because fewer bytes must traverse the memory bus per generated token.

The paper's context-setting contribution (Section 2.1) is to demonstrate that this memory bottleneck is not a temporary artifact of current hardware but a persistent trend: Figure 21a and 21b show that across Nvidia (Volta through Blackwell), AMD (MI200 through MI325X), Intel (Gaudi 2/3), and Google (TPU v3–v5) accelerators, both memory capacity and memory bandwidth are growing more slowly than peak FLOPs. The linear fits for all four hardware families exhibit downward slopes, confirming the divergence is systematic across vendors and microarchitectures. This means that addressing memory bottlenecks is not optional — it is the central deployment challenge for LLMs at scale.

The paper's practical framing is that this creates an economic tension: the Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) prescribe scaling model size and training data together for compute-optimal training, but the resulting large models are expensive or impossible to deploy due to memory constraints. The industry response has been to train smaller models on far more tokens than Chinchilla-optimal (e.g., LLaMA 3 8B on 15T tokens, cited in Section 8), effectively spending extra training compute to avoid large model sizes at deployment. This paper proposes a different solution: **train the model at low bitwidth from scratch**, making the deployed model physically smaller in memory while preserving parameter count.

### Why Post-Training Quantization Falls Short

The dominant approach to reducing model memory footprint is **post-training quantization (PTQ)** — take a model trained in FP16, quantize its weights to lower precision (typically 4-bit or 8-bit), and deploy it with optimized low-precision kernels. The paper acknowledges that PTQ works well down to 4 bits, citing state-of-the-art methods like GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022), AWQ (Lin et al., 2024), and optimized inference kernels like Marlin (Frantar and Alistarh, 2024) that achieve near-lossless compression at 4-bit precision with speedups proportional to the compression factor.

However, the paper identifies several fundamental limitations of PTQ that motivate exploring pretrained low-bitwidth models instead:

**1. Performance collapses below 4 bits.** When quantizing to 3 bits or lower, the mismatch between the pretrained FP16 weight distribution and the available quantized values becomes so severe that model quality degrades substantially. The paper shows this empirically throughout Section 5: QuantLM 3-Bit models consistently underperform their 4-bit counterparts across all benchmarks (see Tables 6–13), and at very small scales (190M parameters), 4-bit quantization already causes catastrophic failure — the LAMBADA perplexity explodes to ~725 million and accuracy drops to 0.00% (Table 9). This confirms that PTQ has a hard precision floor around 4 bits, below which it cannot reliably preserve model quality.

**2. Knowledge capacity degrades under quantization.** The paper explicitly references Allen-Zhu and Li (2024), who found that 4-bit QuantLMs have approximately 65% lower knowledge capacity per parameter compared to full-precision models. This means quantization causes a disproportionate loss of factual knowledge relative to other capabilities, which is particularly problematic for knowledge-intensive downstream applications (e.g., question answering, fact verification). The Spectra suite's own knowledge benchmarks (SciQ, TriviaQA, MMLU) confirm this pattern — QuantLMs consistently underperform FloatLMs of the same parameter count on knowledge tasks (Figures 11–12, Tables 9 and 13), especially at 3-bit precision.

**3. Sensitivity to calibration data choices.** PTQ methods like GPTQ require a calibration dataset to determine quantization parameters (scales, zero points) that minimize reconstruction error. The paper cites Malinovskii et al. (2024)'s finding that seemingly innocuous choices — such as whether to length-normalize the calibration data — can significantly impact QuantLM performance. This introduces brittleness: the same quantization procedure can produce different-quality models depending on calibration set composition, and the optimal calibration set may vary by downstream task. While the Spectra suite's QuantLMs use careful calibration (1M tokens, length-normalized, drawn from SlimPajama, following best practices from Malinovskii et al.), this very care highlights the fragility — practical deployments may not always get this right.

**4. Symmetric vs. asymmetric quantization tradeoffs.** The paper uses symmetric quantization (no zero offset per group) for QuantLMs, citing simplicity, support in fast inference kernels, and consistency with TriLMs' scale-only representation. However, asymmetric quantization (with separate zero offsets) can sometimes achieve better accuracy by more faithfully representing skewed weight distributions. The choice to use symmetric quantization makes the comparison slightly more favorable to TriLMs in some scenarios, but the paper argues this is justified by practical deployment considerations — the fastest available kernels (e.g., Marlin) support symmetric quantization.

**5. The fundamental limitation: PTQ optimizes for reconstruction, not task performance.** At its core, PTQ finds quantized weights that minimize the mean squared error between the full-precision layer's output and the quantized layer's output on the calibration data. This is a local, layer-wise objective that does not account for how quantization errors propagate through the network or interact with task-specific computations. Models trained at low bitwidth from scratch, by contrast, learn representations that are **native to the low-precision weight space** — the training dynamics adapt to the quantization constraints, potentially finding solutions that a post-hoc compression approach cannot reach.

### Why Not Just Train in Lower Precision from the Start?

The idea of training neural networks with low-precision weights is not new. The paper positions itself within a lineage of work on **quantization-aware training (QAT)** and extreme low-bitwidth models:

**Binary neural networks** (Courbariaux et al., 2016a,b, Hubara et al., 2017) constrain weights to {-1, +1}, reducing multiplications to XOR operations and achieving extreme computational efficiency. However, the paper's own experiments (Appendix B, Figure 15) show that binary language models (BiLMs) consistently underperform ternary models at the scales considered (99M, 560M, 1.1B parameters), with validation loss gaps that, while narrowing, are still significant at 1.1B parameters. The paper cites prior work (Rastegari et al., 2016, Liu et al., 2023a) confirming that binary quantization often results in "significant performance degradation, failing to meet the standards of practical applications" (Section 2.3). The key issue is that {-1, +1} cannot represent the zero state that plays an important role in weight distributions — many weights are small in magnitude, and forcing them to ±1 introduces unnecessary noise.

**Ternary neural networks** (Li et al., 2016, Zhu et al., 2017) add the zero state {-1, 0, +1}, which more naturally captures weight distributions that are concentrated near zero (as the Spectra suite's weight distribution analysis confirms — see Figure 20, where all FloatLM weight distributions exhibit Gaussian-like shapes centered at zero). The computational advantage remains substantial: ternary weights replace multiplications with additions and subtractions (multiplication by -1 is negation, by 0 is zeroing, by +1 is identity), and the zero state enables natural sparsity — computations with zero weights can be skipped entirely.

**BitNet and BitNet b1.58** (Wang et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024) are the most directly relevant prior work. BitNet b1.58 specifically proposed training LLMs at ternary precision ("1.58 bits"), demonstrating feasibility at scales up to 3.9B parameters. The Spectra paper explicitly compares against BitNet b1.58 (Appendix A.5), replicating the architecture at 1.1B parameters and comparing against reported scores at 700M and 1.3B. Critically, the paper finds that **TriLM outperforms BitNet b1.58** at comparable scales (Figure 14), attributing this to architectural simplifications: TriLM uses standard Pre-Normalization (RMSNorm before each linear layer, as in GPT-3), whereas BitNet employs more complex per-activation quantization and normalization (4–7 normalization operations per transformer layer vs. TriLM's 2). The paper also notes that BitNet uses parameterless RMSNorm, while TriLM uses RMSNorm with learnable scale parameters, providing additional representational capacity.

However, despite BitNet b1.58's existence, the paper argues that **several crucial questions remained unanswered**:

1. **How do pretrained low-bitwidth models compare to post-training quantized models at the same bit-size?** BitNet b1.58 compared against FP16 models but did not include a QuantLM baseline, making it unclear whether pretraining at low bitwidth offers advantages over simply quantizing a pretrained float model.

2. **What are the scaling laws for low-bitwidth models?** The relationship between model size (parameters), bitwidth, training data, and downstream performance was poorly characterized. Without scaling laws, practitioners cannot make informed decisions about whether to invest in larger low-bitwidth models or smaller high-precision ones.

3. **How do these models behave on diverse downstream tasks?** BitNet b1.58's evaluation was limited to a small set of commonsense reasoning benchmarks. Performance on knowledge-intensive tasks, toxicity benchmarks, and out-of-distribution text corpora was unexplored.

4. **What is the training data efficiency of low-bitwidth models?** If low-bitwidth models require substantially more training data to match FP16 performance, their practical value would be limited by data availability rather than deployment constraints.

The Spectra suite directly addresses all four questions through its systematic comparison of TriLMs, FloatLMs, and QuantLMs across multiple scales, bitwidths, and evaluation dimensions, all trained on identical data.

### The Information-Theoretic Motivation: Why Low Bitwidths Might Work at Scale

Section 2.2 of the paper provides a theoretical justification for why training at low bitwidths should be feasible at large model scales. This argument is subtle and worth unpacking, as it underlies the paper's central hypothesis.

The authors analyze the weight distributions of trained FloatLMs across scales (99M to 3.9B parameters) using two entropy measures:

**Differential entropy** of Gaussian fits to the weight distributions: $H(W) = \frac{1}{2}\log_2(2\pi e \sigma_W^2)$. As Figure 4 shows, this quantity **decreases** as parameter count increases — from approximately −3.25 at the smallest model to approximately −3.60 at 3.9B. Lower differential entropy means the weights are more concentrated (lower variance), which implies that each weight carries less unique information on average. The interpretation is that larger models exhibit **overparameterization-induced redundancy** (Zhang et al., 2017; Neyshabur et al., 2018) — many weights encode overlapping or redundant signals, reducing the effective information per weight.

**Shannon entropy** of discretized weight distributions: By binning weights into histograms and computing $H_{\text{Shannon}} = -\sum_{i=1}^N p_i \log_2 p_i$, the authors measure how many bits are needed to encode the weight values given their empirical distribution. Figure 3 shows that Shannon entropy **decreases with increasing parameter count** for any fixed number of bins — a 3.9B model's weights can be encoded in fewer bits than a 99M model's weights because their distribution is more predictable (more sharply peaked around zero).

The combination of these two analyses supports a key hypothesis: **as models grow larger, the per-weight information content decreases, meaning that low-bitwidth representations become increasingly adequate**. A ternary weight space with a per-matrix scale can capture the essential signal (direction and approximate magnitude) while discarding fine-grained weight differences that become redundant at scale.

The authors are careful not to overstate this: the decreasing entropy does not *guarantee* that ternary models will match FP16 performance — it only suggests that the information loss from ternarization becomes *less severe* as scale increases. The scaling law analysis in Section 4.3 (Figure 9b) confirms this empirically: TriLMs and FloatLMs share the same scaling exponent ($\alpha = 0.26$, meaning they improve at the same rate with additional parameters), but TriLMs have a higher offset ($\epsilon_{\text{TriLM}} = 1.76$ vs. $\epsilon_{\text{FloatLM}} = 1.67$), representing a constant gap that becomes proportionally smaller as overall loss decreases.

### How the Paper Positions Itself

The paper occupies a distinct position at the intersection of three research areas:

**Against post-training quantization:** The paper argues that PTQ is fundamentally limited by the mismatch between the pretrained weight distribution and the low-precision target space, and that models trained natively at low bitwidth can achieve better quality at the same bit-size. The QuantLM baselines (3-bit and 4-bit GPTQ across all model scales) provide the empirical evidence for this claim.

**Against binary models:** The paper's BiLM experiments (Appendix B) confirm prior findings that binary models underperform at practical scales, establishing ternary as the "sweet spot" in the low-bitwidth design space — enough precision to preserve model quality, but enough compression to address memory bottlenecks.

**Beyond BitNet:** The paper positions TriLM as an improved architecture relative to BitNet b1.58 (simpler normalization, learnable scale parameters, better downstream performance) and provides what BitNet b1.58 lacked: systematic comparison against QuantLMs, comprehensive scaling law analysis, evaluation on knowledge and toxicity benchmarks, and release of 500+ intermediate checkpoints for research.

The paper's ultimate framing is that low-bitwidth pretraining is not just feasible but **increasingly necessary** — as the FLOPs-memory gap widens, and as models exceed the memory capacity of individual accelerators, training models that are memory-efficient by design (rather than compressing them post-hoc) may become the default approach for large-scale LLM development. The Spectra suite is offered as infrastructure for the research community to investigate this hypothesis.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The Spectra suite is a family of 54 language models spanning three architectural approaches — full-precision floating-point models (FloatLMs), post-training quantized models (QuantLMs at 3, 4, 6, and 8 bits), and ternary models trained at low bitwidth from scratch (TriLMs) — all sharing identical LLaMa-style transformer architectures and trained on the same 300B-token dataset, enabling controlled comparison across bitwidths at nine different parameter scales from 99M to 3.9B parameters. The core problem this system solves is the practical deployment bottleneck where LLM inference is limited by memory capacity and bandwidth rather than by computational throughput, and the "shape" of the solution is to demonstrate that training models natively at ternary precision (1.58 effective bits per weight) can produce models that match or exceed the downstream performance of post-training quantized models at the same bit-size while approaching the performance of full-precision models of the same parameter count, thereby offering a deployment-efficient alternative that avoids the quality degradation inherent in compressing a pretrained float model to very low bitwidths.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The Spectra system has five major components:

1. **Shared LLaMa-style transformer architecture** — a standard autoregressive decoder with RMSNorm, SwiGLU Gated MLP, Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE), Multi-Headed Attention, and no bias terms, serving as the common skeleton across all model families. The only difference between families is how the weights of linear layers are represented and updated during training.

2. **FloatLM training pipeline** — standard mixed-precision training (FP16/BF16) with cosine decay learning rate scheduling, weight decay regularization, and AdamW optimization, producing the baseline full-precision models that also serve as the starting point for post-training quantization.

3. **TriLM training pipeline** — the same architecture but with on-the-fly ternarization of linear layer weights during the forward pass (computing scale values as the mean of absolute latent weights, rounding to {-1, 0, +1}), straight-through gradient estimation in the backward pass, and a specialized two-phase optimization schedule that drops peak learning rate at the halfway point and removes weight decay at the two-thirds mark.

4. **QuantLM post-training quantization pipeline** — GPTQ applied to the trained FloatLM checkpoints, using symmetric quantization with group size 128 for 3-bit and 4-bit (yielding effective bit rates of 3.25 and 4.25 bits per parameter respectively), calibrated on 1M tokens of length-normalized SlimPajama data, producing 3, 4, 6, and 8-bit QuantLMs for each FloatLM parameter count.

5. **Shared evaluation suite** — zero-shot evaluation across commonsense reasoning (Arc Easy, Arc Challenge, BoolQ, HellaSwag, PIQA, WinoGrande, LAMBADA, LogiQA), knowledge benchmarks (SciQ, TriviaQA, MMLU), and toxicity/safety benchmarks (TruthfulQA, Big Bench BBQ Lite, CrowsPairs), with validation perplexity measured on SlimPajama plus out-of-distribution corpora (Penn Tree Bank, C4, Cosmopedia, Dolma, S2Orc, Wikipedia, RefinedWeb).

Information flows as follows: a shared 300B-token subset of SlimPajama is tokenized with the GPT-NeoX 20B tokenizer → each model family (FloatLM, TriLM) is trained independently on identical data sequences with family-specific optimization schedules → FloatLM checkpoints are quantized via GPTQ to create QuantLMs → all models (54 total: 9 FloatLMs + 9×4 QuantLMs + 9 TriLMs = 54, plus additional BiLM ablations at 3 scales) are evaluated on the same benchmarks using LM Evaluation Harness → results are compared across model size (in bits) and parameter count to identify scaling trends.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the shared transformer architecture and training data setup, since this is the common foundation across all model families and defines the parameter count mapping that enables fair comparison.
- **Second**, the FloatLM training recipe, which establishes the baseline optimization approach and the checkpoints from which QuantLMs are produced.
- **Third**, the TriLM forward pass, backward pass, and inference mechanics — the core technical contribution — including the ternarization equations, the straight-through estimator, the per-device scale computation, and the reasoning behind quantizing only linear layers.
- **Fourth**, the TriLM optimization schedule and its two-phase structure (peak learning rate reduction at the halfway point, weight decay removal at the two-thirds mark), along with the ablation evidence supporting these design choices.
- **Fifth**, the QuantLM GPTQ pipeline and calibration methodology, including the choices that make the comparison fair (symmetric quantization, same embedding/LM head precision, group size selection).
- **Sixth**, the scaling law fitting methodology and the mathematical relationship between parameter count and validation loss for TriLMs versus FloatLMs.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical systems and scaling paper** whose core idea is that training language models at ternary precision from scratch — rather than quantizing pretrained float models — produces models that scale better in the practically critical dimension of model bit-size, and that this advantage emerges because ternarization during training allows the optimization process to adapt weight representations to the low-precision constraint space in a way that post-hoc rounding cannot replicate.

#### Shared Transformer Architecture and Training Data

All models in the Spectra suite — FloatLMs, TriLMs, and (indirectly, through quantization of FloatLMs) QuantLMs — share an identical LLaMa-style autoregressive transformer architecture (Section 3.1, Appendix A.2-A.3). The architecture consists of:

- **RMSNorm** (Root Mean Square Layer Normalization) with learnable scale parameters, applied before each sub-layer (Pre-Normalization, following GPT-3's approach). This means normalization occurs twice per transformer layer: once at the input to the multi-headed attention sub-layer, and once at the input to the Gated MLP sub-layer. The authors note that this contrasts with BitNet b1.58, which applies normalization, scaling, and 8-bit quantization to activations between 4 and 7 times per transformer layer (depending on implementation), making TriLM architecturally simpler.

- **SwiGLU Gated MLP** — the feedforward sub-layer uses the SwiGLU activation function, which computes two linear projections of the input, applies SiLU (Sigmoid Linear Unit) to one projection, and multiplies element-wise with the other projection. The gating mechanism provides richer non-linearity than standard ReLU or GELU MLPs.

- **Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE)** — position information is encoded by rotating the query and key vectors in attention by angles proportional to their position indices, enabling the model to attend based on relative position without learned position embeddings.

- **Multi-Headed Attention** — standard scaled dot-product attention with multiple parallel attention heads, each attending to a different learned projection of the input.

- **No bias terms** — following the LLaMa design, all linear layers omit bias parameters, reducing parameter count slightly and simplifying quantization (no bias terms to handle separately).

The training data is a 300B-token subset of the deduplicated SlimPajama dataset (a cleaned and deduplicated version of RedPajama, itself derived from The Pile's constituent corpora). SlimPajama was chosen because The Pile's 300B subset (used by Pythia) was not publicly available at the time. The 300B subset is sampled from each constituent dataset with probability proportional to its size, resulting in the composition shown in Table 2: Arxiv (13B tokens), Book (13B), C4 (80B), Common Crawl (156B), GitHub (16B), Stack Exchange (10B), and Wikipedia (12B). All models across all families are trained on this identical data with the same token ordering and data sequences, ensuring that differences in model behavior arise from architectural and precision choices rather than training data variation.

The tokenizer is GPT-NeoX 20B's tokenizer (the same tokenizer used by Pythia), with a vocabulary optimized for the NeoX architecture. For training efficiency, the embedding dimension is rounded up to the nearest multiple of 128 times the model parallel size — this padding ensures that the token embedding matrix can be evenly partitioned across GPUs when using model parallelism, avoiding load imbalance.

FloatLMs are trained with a batch size of 2M tokens per step, while TriLMs use 1M tokens per step (the paper does not explicitly justify this difference, but lower batch sizes for TriLMs may compensate for the additional noise introduced by ternarization during gradient estimation). All models are trained with sequence length 2048 and use the AdamW optimizer with `$\beta = (0.9, 0.95)$`. Training uses 2D parallelism with Megatron-style tensor model parallelism (sharding individual weight matrices across GPUs within a node) combined with ZeRO Stage 2 (sharding optimizer states and gradients across data-parallel replicas), implemented on the GPT-NeoX codebase. Hardware consists of nodes with IBM Power9 CPUs and 6×16GB NVIDIA V100 GPUs; due to the lack of BFloat16 support on V100, all training — both FloatLM and TriLM — uses FP16 mixed precision with dynamic loss scaling.

#### FloatLM Training Recipe (The Baseline)

FloatLMs represent all linear layer weights as standard FP16 (half-precision floating-point) numbers, with the embedding layer and language model head also in FP16. The optimization schedule for FloatLMs follows the standard recipe established by models like Pythia, OLMo, and LLM360:

- **Learning rate schedule:** Cosine decay from an initial peak learning rate (scaled by model size — see Table 3 for exact values, ranging from `$4.0 \times 10^{-4}$` for the smallest models down to `$1.5 \times 10^{-4}$` for the 3.9B model) to a near-zero final learning rate over the full 300B-token training run, with a linear warmup period at the beginning (the exact warmup duration is not specified in the main text but is part of the standard cosine decay recipe).

- **Weight decay:** Constant L2 regularization (weight decay) applied throughout training, with the AdamW optimizer decoupling weight decay from the adaptive learning rate computation.

- **Mixed precision:** Forward and backward passes computed in FP16 for speed, with a master copy of weights maintained in FP32 for accumulation of small gradient updates that might otherwise underflow in FP16. Dynamic loss scaling automatically adjusts the scaling factor to prevent gradient underflow while avoiding overflow — Table 5 reports the minimum loss scale encountered during each training run, confirming that all models maintained loss scales at or above 128, the recommended threshold for stable FP16 training (Micikevicius et al., 2018).

The FloatLM checkpoints at each parameter scale serve two purposes: (1) they are the baseline against which TriLMs and QuantLMs are compared, representing the best achievable performance at full precision for a given parameter count, and (2) they are the starting point for GPTQ post-training quantization to produce QuantLMs.

#### TriLM Forward Pass: On-the-Fly Ternarization

The core technical contribution of TriLM is the mechanism by which linear layer weights are constrained to ternary values during training. Unlike post-training quantization, which takes a trained float model and rounds its weights to discrete levels, TriLM performs **on-the-fly ternarization during every forward pass** — the model never sees, computes with, or stores the full-precision weights during the forward pass, but instead computes ternary equivalents dynamically while maintaining floating-point "latent" (or master) weights that accumulate gradient updates in the backward pass. This approach, known as **quantization-aware training (QAT)** with a straight-through estimator, has been used in prior work on binary and ternary neural networks (Courbariaux et al., 2016b; Li et al., 2016) but is applied here at unprecedented scale for language models.

Consider a single linear layer with input `$X \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times n}$` (batch size `$b$`, input dimension `$n$`), latent weight matrix `$W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$` (output dimension `$m$`), and desired output `$Y \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times m}$`. The forward pass for a TriLM linear layer proceeds as follows:

**Step 1: Compute the per-matrix scale value.** For the entire weight matrix, compute:

$$\gamma = \epsilon + \frac{1}{nm} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m |W_{ij}|$$

where `$\epsilon = 10^{-5}$` is a small constant preventing division by zero in degenerate cases (all weights exactly zero), `$n$` and `$m$` are the input and output dimensions of the linear layer, and `$|W_{ij}|$` is the absolute value of the latent weight at position `$(i, j)$`.

**What it computes:** the mean of the absolute values of all latent weights in the matrix, with a tiny epsilon added for numerical stability. For an `$m \times n$` weight matrix, this sums the `$nm$` absolute values and divides by `$nm$` to get the average magnitude. The result is a single scalar `$\gamma$` shared across the entire weight matrix.

**Why this form:** The absolute mean provides a natural and computationally cheap measure of the typical weight magnitude in the matrix. Using the mean (rather than the maximum or the standard deviation) ensures that the scale adapts to the overall weight distribution — if weights grow during training, `$\gamma$` grows proportionally; if weights shrink, `$\gamma$` shrinks. The epsilon term is a standard numerical guard that has negligible effect under normal conditions (since typical weight magnitudes are orders of magnitude larger than `$10^{-5}$`) but prevents the degenerate case where a freshly initialized or collapsed matrix has all-zero weights. The paper contrasts this with BiLM's approach, which uses a different scaling factor `$\alpha$` computed as the mean absolute value without epsilon and with an additional sign function applied after subtracting the weight mean, making it specific to binary (rather than ternary) quantization.

**Step 2: Scale and round to ternary values.** For each individual weight `$W_{ij}$`:

$$\widehat{W}_{ij} = \text{round}\left(\min\left(\max\left(\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma}, -1\right), 1\right)\right)$$

**What it computes:** each latent weight is first divided by the scale `$\gamma$`, normalizing it to a value roughly in the range `$[-k, +k]$` where `$k$` depends on how far the weight deviates from the mean absolute value. This normalized value is then clamped to the range `$[-1, 1]$` via the nested `$\min(\max(\cdot, -1), 1)$` operation — values below -1 become -1, values above +1 become +1, and values inside the range are unchanged. Finally, the `$\text{round}(\cdot)$` function maps to the nearest integer in `$\{-1, 0, 1\}$`: values in `$[-0.5, 0.5)$` round to 0, values in `$[0.5, 1]$` round to +1, and values in `$[-1, -0.5]$` round to -1.

**Why this form:** The division by `$\gamma$` makes the ternarization scale-adaptive — if all weights are small, `$\gamma$` is small, and the division amplifies the weights so that the ternary states are still reachable. The clamp-then-round sequence ensures the output is always exactly -1, 0, or +1 (a true ternary representation) rather than a continuous value that only approximates ternary. The alternative — directly thresholding without scaling — would lose the ability to adapt to the weight distribution's magnitude. The choice of rounding thresholds at `$\pm 0.5$` is the standard symmetric quantization rule that minimizes the expected rounding error for uniformly distributed values within each interval.

**Step 3: Rescale to produce the effective weight matrix.** Multiply the ternary state matrix by the scale:

$$\widetilde{W}_{ij} = \gamma \cdot \widehat{W}_{ij}$$

**What it computes:** the effective weight matrix used in the actual forward computation. Each entry is either `$-\gamma$`, `$0$`, or `$+\gamma$`, depending on the ternary state. This rescaling ensures that the output of the linear layer has appropriate magnitude — without it, the layer output would be in units of {-1, 0, +1} and subsequent layers would need to compensate for the missing scale, likely leading to training instability.

**Why this form:** Separating the scale from the ternary states is the key design decision that gives ternary models their representational power. The ternary states encode the *direction* and *sparsity pattern* of the weight matrix (which connections are excitatory, inhibitory, or absent), while the scale encodes the *overall magnitude* of the transformation. A single scalar scale per matrix is the simplest possible parameterization of magnitude, but the paper shows it is sufficient — the weight distributions across model scales (Figure 20, Appendix E) are approximately Gaussian and centered at zero, meaning that most of the variance in a weight matrix can be captured by a single scale parameter if the individual weights are appropriately ternarized. Using per-channel or per-neuron scales would provide more fine-grained magnitude control but at the cost of additional parameters and more complex inference kernels, pushing the effective bitwidth higher.

**Step 4: Compute the forward pass output.** The linear layer output is simply:

$$Y = X \widetilde{W}^T$$

**What it computes:** standard matrix multiplication between the input activations `$X$` and the transposed effective ternary weight matrix `$\widetilde{W}^T$`. Since each entry of `$\widetilde{W}$` is either `$-\gamma$`, `$0$`, or `$+\gamma$`, the matrix multiplication reduces to: for each output neuron, sum the input activations for which the corresponding weight is `$+\gamma$`, subtract the sum for which the weight is `$-\gamma$`, and ignore the inputs where the weight is `$0$`. This requires only addition, subtraction, and a single multiplication by `$\gamma$` at the end (or equivalently, the scale can be folded into the output).

**Why this form at inference:** During deployment, the ternary states `$\widehat{W}_{ij}$` and the scale `$\gamma$` are pre-computed once and cached. The inference computation then involves only integer operations (addition/subtraction of activation values based on the `$\pm 1$` weights) and a final float multiplication by `$\gamma$`, rather than the full `$mn$` float multiplications and additions of a standard linear layer. This is the source of the theoretical speedup: for sufficiently large matrices, the integer accumulate operations can be implemented far more efficiently than float multiply-accumulate, and the zero weights enable natural sparsity — entire columns of the weight matrix contribute nothing to the output and can be skipped. The paper claims a theoretical maximum speedup of up to 10× compared to FP16 for autoregressive decoding (Figure 2b), based on the compression factor from 16 bits per weight to effectively 1.58 bits plus one float scale.

A critical implementation detail concerns **model parallelism**. In standard data-parallel training, each GPU holds a copy of the entire model; but for model parallelism, the weight matrix `$W$` is sharded across multiple GPUs (each GPU owns a contiguous slice of the output dimension). If `$\gamma$` were computed over the full matrix, this would require an all-reduce communication step to sum the absolute values across all GPUs and broadcast the result — adding significant overhead for a single scalar. The paper's solution (Section 3.1, Appendix A.4) is to **compute `$\gamma$` independently on each GPU over only its local shard of the weight matrix**. This means that a weight matrix sharded across `$k$` GPUs will have `$k$` separate scale values (one per shard) rather than one global scale. The impact on model bit-size is negligible — for matrices with millions of parameters, adding 6 scalar values (for `$k=6$`, the maximum model parallel size used, at 3.9B parameters) increases the total bits by less than `$10^{-5}$` bits per parameter. However, it means that during inference, the scale values are computed independently within each model parallel group, which is a minor deviation from the idealized single-scale-per-matrix design.

#### TriLM Backward Pass: Straight-Through Estimation

During the backward pass, the model needs to compute gradients of the loss with respect to the latent weights `$W$`, which are the parameters updated by the optimizer. The ternarization operations (scale, clamp, round) are non-differentiable — the clamp function has zero gradient outside `$[-1, 1]$`, and the round function has zero gradient almost everywhere with undefined spikes at the threshold boundaries. To handle this, TriLM uses the **straight-through estimator (STE)** (Bengio et al., 2013):

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial X} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial Y} \widetilde{W}$$

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial Y}^T X$$

**What it computes:** the gradients are computed *as if* the forward pass had used the latent weights `$W$` directly, ignoring the ternarization step. Specifically, the gradient with respect to the latent weights `$W$` is `$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial Y}^T X$`, which is exactly the gradient that would be computed for a standard linear layer without any quantization. The gradient with respect to the input `$X$` uses the effective ternary weights `$\widetilde{W}$` rather than the latent weights, because the forward pass output depended on `$\widetilde{W}$` and this gradient is needed for backpropagation to earlier layers.

**Why this form:** The straight-through estimator is a heuristic that "routes" gradients through non-differentiable operations by pretending those operations are identity functions during the backward pass. The intuition is: even though the forward pass uses ternarized weights, small changes to the latent weights can still influence the output by occasionally pushing a weight across the threshold from 0 to +1, or from +1 to 0. The STE approximates this discrete transition with a continuous gradient, allowing gradient-based optimization to work. Without the STE, the gradients with respect to latent weights would be zero for most parameters (since small changes don't change the ternary state), making learning impossible. The STE is the standard approach for quantization-aware training and has been validated extensively in prior work (Courbariaux et al., 2016b; Zhou et al., 2018).

The paper does not compute gradients through the scale parameter `$\gamma$` explicitly; since `$\gamma$` is a deterministic function of the latent weights (mean of absolute values), its gradient is subsumed into the gradient computation for `$W$` through the chain rule. The epsilon term `$\epsilon = 10^{-5}$` is small enough that its gradient contribution is negligible in practice.

---

#### TriLM Inference Mode

During inference, the ternarization process is performed once on the final trained latent weights to produce the cached ternary representation. The forward pass at inference is identical to the training forward pass (steps 1–4 above), except that:

- **No gradient computation:** Only the forward pass is executed, so there is no need for the STE or latent weight updates.
- **Pre-computed ternary weights:** The scale `$\gamma$` and ternary states `$\widehat{W}_{ij}$` are computed once from the trained latent weights and stored. The effective weights `$\widetilde{W}_{ij} = \gamma \cdot \widehat{W}_{ij}$` can be either stored explicitly (as three-valued floats) or computed on-the-fly from the ternary states and scale.
- **Efficient kernels:** The inference computation `$Y = X\widetilde{W}^T$` can be implemented with specialized kernels that exploit the ternary structure — accumulating input activations with signs determined by the `$\pm 1$` weights and skipping zero-weight connections. The paper does not provide custom kernel implementations (this is left for future work), but notes that the theoretical speedup is proportional to the compression factor (approximately 10× compared to FP16 for large models).

---

#### Why Quantize Only Linear Layers?

A crucial design decision in TriLM is that **only the weights of linear layers are ternarized; the embedding layer and language model head remain in FP16** (Section 3.2). The paper provides two justifications:

1. **Linear layers dominate the parameter count.** In transformer architectures, the bulk of parameters reside in the linear projections within attention (query, key, value, output) and the feedforward MLP (two large dense matrices). Ternarizing these layers captures the vast majority of the potential memory savings. The embedding layer and LM head, by contrast, are relatively small (their size is `$d_{\text{model}} \times |\mathcal{V}|$`, where `$|\mathcal{V}| = 128\text{k}$` in the analysis but the Spectra models use the NeoX tokenizer's vocabulary), and quantizing them would introduce disproportionate quality degradation for minimal memory savings.

2. **Embeddings and LM head encode critical semantic information.** The embedding layer maps discrete tokens to continuous vector representations that capture semantic and syntactic relationships (Mikolov et al., 2013). Quantizing these representations to ternary values would severely degrade the model's ability to distinguish between different tokens — with only three possible values per dimension, the embedding space would collapse to a small set of possible vectors, losing the fine-grained semantic distinctions that make language models effective. Similarly, the LM head maps the final hidden states to a probability distribution over the vocabulary (Press and Wolf, 2017); ternarization here would limit the model's ability to make precise predictions, particularly for rare tokens or subtle distinctions between semantically similar words.

This decision also makes the comparison with QuantLMs fair: the Spectra suite's QuantLMs also keep embeddings and LM head in FP16 (only transformer layer weights are quantized via GPTQ), so both low-bitwidth approaches preserve full precision in the token representation layers.

#### TriLM Optimization Schedule: Two-Phase Training

Training ternary models from scratch is more challenging than training float models because the ternarization operation introduces two forms of difficulty: (1) the representational constraint limits the expressivity of each layer, requiring more careful optimization to find good ternary configurations, and (2) the straight-through estimator introduces gradient approximation error, making the optimization landscape noisier. The paper addresses these challenges with a specialized two-phase optimization schedule (Section 3.3, Figure 6), validated through ablation experiments on a 1.1B parameter model trained for 100B tokens.

The schedule consists of three interventions on top of a standard linear learning rate decay with warmup and AdamW weight decay:

**Intervention 1: Peak learning rate reduction at the halfway point.** At 50% of the total training tokens (150B tokens for the full 300B-token runs, 50B tokens for the 100B-token ablations), the peak learning rate is reduced. For a 1.1B model, the schedule is:

$$\text{Peak LR} = 1.3 \times 10^{-3} \text{ for the first 50% of training, then } 9.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ for the second 50%}$$

The exact reduction factors vary by model size (Table 3): for the smallest models, the peak LR drops from `$2.4 \times 10^{-3}$` to `$1.5 \times 10^{-3}$`, while larger models use a single phase (e.g., 3.9B TriLM uses `$1.2 \times 10^{-3}$` throughout, dropping to `$8.0 \times 10^{-4}$` at the halfway point).

**What this does:** The first half of training uses a higher learning rate to explore the weight space aggressively, allowing latent weights to move substantially and find good ternary configurations. The second half reduces the learning rate to refine these configurations with smaller, more precise updates. The paper observes (Figure 8a) that this learning rate reduction causes a **sharp drop in training loss** over a few hundred million tokens — the model rapidly settles into a better local optimum once the exploration phase ends. This behavior is analogized to training schedules like those in MiniCPM (Hu et al., 2024), which also use brief periods of rapid learning rate decay to induce loss improvements.

**Intervention 2: Weight decay removal at the two-thirds mark.** At 66.7% of total training tokens (200B tokens for full runs, ~67B for ablations), weight decay (L2 regularization) is removed entirely. This means the optimizer no longer applies the penalty term that shrinks weights toward zero.

**What this does:** In the early stages of training, weight decay serves as regularization, preventing weights from growing too large and encouraging sparse representations (which are natural for ternary models — a weight of zero is a valid ternary state). However, as training progresses toward convergence, weight decay can interfere with fine-tuning the ternary states: a weight that is near the threshold between -1 and 0, or between 0 and +1, needs the freedom to grow slightly to cross the threshold and change its ternary state. Weight decay opposes this growth, making it harder for weights to transition between ternary states late in training. Removing weight decay at the two-thirds mark allows the model to make these final adjustments without regularization penalty. The paper notes (Section 3.3) that weight decay removal leads to an "accelerated convergence rate," which can produce effects analogous to reducing the peak learning rate.

**Why both interventions together:** The ablation study in Figure 6 compares four conditions: (a) both interventions, (b) only L2 regularization removal, (c) only peak LR reduction, and (d) neither (baseline linear decay with constant weight decay). The final training loss at 100B tokens is lowest with both interventions, followed by only L2 removal, then only peak LR reduction, with the baseline performing worst. This ordering is consistent with downstream performance (Tables 10 and 11 in Appendix G): the full schedule achieves the best average commonsense reasoning score, and the gains from removing L2 regularization are larger than those from reducing the peak learning rate. The paper attributes this to the ternary nature of the model — ternarization itself acts as a strong regularizer (Courbariaux et al., 2016b), making explicit weight decay less necessary as training progresses, and eventually counterproductive.

**Concrete hyperparameters for the TriLM family** (Table 3):

| Parameters | Peak LR (phase 1 → phase 2) |
|---|---|
| 99M | `$2.4 \times 10^{-3} \rightarrow 1.5 \times 10^{-3}$` |
| 190M | `$2.4 \times 10^{-3} \rightarrow 1.5 \times 10^{-3}$` |
| 390M | `$1.8 \times 10^{-3} \rightarrow 1.2 \times 10^{-3}$` |
| 560M | `$1.6 \times 10^{-3} \rightarrow 1.1 \times 10^{-3}$` |
| 830M | `$1.5 \times 10^{-3} \rightarrow 1.0 \times 10^{-3}$` |
| 1.1B | `$1.3 \times 10^{-3} \rightarrow 9.0 \times 10^{-4}$` |
| 1.5B | `$1.2 \times 10^{-3} \rightarrow 8.0 \times 10^{-4}$` |
| 2.4B | `$1.2 \times 10^{-3} \rightarrow 8.0 \times 10^{-4}$` |
| 3.9B | `$1.2 \times 10^{-3} \rightarrow 8.0 \times 10^{-4}$` |

The general trend is that smaller models use higher peak learning rates (more aggressive exploration) that drop more substantially at the halfway point, while larger models start with lower learning rates and show more modest reductions. All models use AdamW with `$\beta = (0.9, 0.95)$`, sequence length 2048, batch size 1M tokens, and FP16 mixed precision training with dynamic loss scaling.

**Training dynamics observations** (Figure 8a):

- **Loss spikes** occur at the same token counts across different TriLM scales, because all models are trained on identical data with the same ordering. The two largest models — TriLM 2.4B and TriLM 3.9B — each experience a large loss spike in the first half of training, suggesting that certain data sequences are particularly challenging and that larger models (with more capacity to memorize or overfit) respond more dramatically to these sequences.

- **The peak LR reduction at the halfway point (150B tokens)** causes an immediate sharp decline in training loss. The effect varies by model size: for the largest models (2.4B, 3.9B), the rate of loss reduction after the initial drop returns to the prior pace (the curves continue descending in parallel to their pre-drop trajectory); for medium models (1.1B, 1.5B), the loss reduction plateaus (the curves flatten after the drop); for models below 1B parameters, training loss actually *increases* after the learning rate reduction, suggesting that these smaller models were still in an active learning phase and benefit from the higher learning rate.

- **Weight decay removal at the two-thirds mark (200B tokens)** accelerates convergence for all models, with the effect most pronounced in the largest TriLM models.

#### QuantLM Post-Training Quantization Pipeline

The QuantLM family is produced by applying GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022) post-training quantization to the fully trained FloatLM checkpoints. The procedure is:

1. **Start with a trained FloatLM checkpoint** at each parameter scale (99M through 3.9B). All linear layer weights within the transformer blocks are candidates for quantization.

2. **Calibration data:** A subset of 512 samples, each of sequence length 2048, is drawn from SlimPajama-627B (the full dataset, not just the 300B training subset). These samples are length-normalized following the methodology of Malinovskii et al. (2024), who demonstrated that length normalization of calibration data significantly impacts QuantLM performance. The total calibration data amounts to `$512 \times 2048 = 1,048,576$` tokens (approximately 1M tokens).

3. **GPTQ algorithm:** GPTQ is a one-shot weight quantization method based on Optimal Brain Compression. For each linear layer, it finds quantized weights `$\hat{W}$` that minimize the squared error between the full-precision layer output and the quantized layer output on the calibration data, using second-order Hessian information to determine which weights can be quantized with the least impact on layer outputs. The algorithm processes weights column-by-column, quantizing each column and then updating the remaining unquantized weights to compensate for the introduced error — this "compensation" step is what distinguishes GPTQ from simple round-to-nearest quantization and enables near-lossless compression at 4 bits.

4. **Quantization configuration:**
   - **8-bit and 6-bit:** No group size is specified (the paper does not provide details, suggesting standard per-channel or per-tensor quantization at these higher bitwidths where reconstruction error is already minimal).
   - **4-bit and 3-bit:** Group size of 128 is used, meaning that weights are divided into groups of 128 consecutive elements along the input dimension, and each group gets its own scale factor. This yields effective bit rates of **4.25 bits per parameter** for 4-bit quantization and **3.25 bits per parameter** for 3-bit quantization (the extra 0.25 bits comes from storing the 16-bit scale factor for each group of 128 weights: `$16 / 128 = 0.125$` bits per weight per scale, but the paper reports 0.25, suggesting perhaps both a scale and zero-point per group, or rounding).

5. **Symmetric quantization:** Quantized values are symmetrically distributed around zero (no zero offset per group). For `$b$`-bit symmetric quantization, the representable values are `$\pm (2^{b-1} - 1)$` evenly spaced levels plus zero (since symmetric quantization of signed integers produces an odd number of levels). The paper uses symmetric quantization for three reasons: (a) it is simpler to implement, (b) it is supported by fast inference kernels like Marlin (Frantar and Alistarh, 2024), and (c) it provides consistency with TriLM's symmetric ternary representation (which also has no zero offset, only a scale). The paper acknowledges that asymmetric quantization (with separate zero offsets and scales per group) can sometimes achieve better accuracy but argues the practical deployment benefits of symmetric quantization justify the choice.

6. **What stays in FP16:** Embedding layers and the language model head are kept in FP16, exactly as in TriLM. Activations are not quantized (this is weight-only quantization). This makes the comparison fair — both TriLMs and QuantLMs apply low-precision representation only to linear layer weights within the transformer blocks, preserving full precision in the token representation and prediction layers.

7. **Multiple calibration seeds:** To account for variability in the calibration set, QuantLM results are averaged across 3 different "seeds" — three different randomly sampled calibration sets from SlimPajama, producing three separate QuantLM models that are evaluated independently and averaged.

The explicit choices here (symmetric quantization, 1M-token calibration set, length normalization, group size 128) represent the paper's attempt to create a strong and fair QuantLM baseline by incorporating best practices from recent quantization research (Malinovskii et al., 2024). The paper notes that the Spectra suite is designed to be extensible — researchers can apply other quantization methods (AWQ, SpQR, etc.) to the same FloatLM checkpoints to compare against these GPTQ baselines.

#### Scaling Law Fitting Methodology

The scaling law analysis in Section 4.3 fits the relationship between model parameter count `$N$` and final validation loss `$L$` to a power-law function with an offset term:

$$L_{\text{type}}(N) = \frac{A_{\text{type}}}{N^{\alpha_{\text{type}}}} + \epsilon_{\text{type}}$$

where `$A$` is the scaling coefficient (determining the initial loss level at small `$N$`), `$\alpha$` is the scaling exponent (determining how quickly loss decreases as parameters increase), and `$\epsilon$` is the irreducible loss offset (the asymptotic loss floor that even infinite parameters cannot breach, representing the entropy of the data distribution plus any model capacity limitations).

**What it computes:** for a given model type (TriLM or FloatLM), given a parameter count `$N$`, this equation predicts the expected validation loss on SlimPajama after training on 300B tokens. The term `$A / N^\alpha$` represents the reducible loss that decreases with scale (the model's capacity-limited error), while `$\epsilon$` represents the irreducible loss from data noise and inherent task difficulty.

**Why this form over a pure power law (`$L = aN^{-b}$`):** As models grow very large, a pure power law predicts that loss approaches zero, which is physically impossible — there is always some minimum loss determined by the entropy of natural language. The offset term `$\epsilon$` captures this floor. The paper compares both forms (Figure 19, Appendix C) and finds that the power-law-with-offset provides slightly more precise fits, particularly at the larger scales where the loss curves begin to flatten. Kaplan et al. (2020) used the pure power-law form for their original scaling laws, while Hoffmann et al. (2022) used the offset form for Chinchilla scaling laws — the Spectra paper follows the more recent convention.

**Fitting procedure:** The parameters `$A$`, `$\alpha$`, and `$\epsilon$` are estimated using the **Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm** (Levenberg, 1944; Marquardt, 1963), a robust non-linear least-squares fitting method that interpolates between Gauss-Newton and gradient descent, chosen for its ability to handle the three-parameter non-linear model efficiently. The fitting uses the final validation loss of all nine FloatLM models and all nine TriLM models (each a single data point at its respective parameter count), with the fixed data regime of 300B tokens.

**Fitted parameters** (Equation 1):

$$\text{FloatLM: } A = 159, \alpha = 0.26, \epsilon = 1.67$$
$$\text{TriLM: } A = 185, \alpha = 0.26, \epsilon = 1.76$$

**What these values mean:**

- **`$\alpha = 0.26$` for both models:** The scaling exponent is identical, meaning that TriLMs and FloatLMs improve at the same *rate* as parameter count increases. Doubling the parameter count reduces the reducible loss component by a factor of `$2^{0.26} \approx 1.20$`, i.e., about a 20% reduction, for both model families. This is a critical finding: ternarization does not fundamentally change *how* the model benefits from additional parameters — the scaling dynamics are preserved.

- **`$A_{\text{TriLM}} = 185$` vs. `$A_{\text{FloatLM}} = 159$`:** The TriLM coefficient is higher, meaning that at any finite parameter count, the TriLM has a higher reducible loss component than the FloatLM. This represents the **constant representational penalty** of ternarization — for a given number of parameters, ternary weights encode less information than float weights, so more parameters are needed to achieve the same loss.

- **`$\epsilon_{\text{TriLM}} = 1.76$` vs. `$\epsilon_{\text{FloatLM}} = 1.67$`:** The TriLM's irreducible loss offset is slightly higher, but the difference (0.09 in log perplexity, or about 9.4% in perplexity) is modest. As `$N \rightarrow \infty$`, the `$A/N^\alpha$` terms go to zero, and the loss approaches the offset. The fact that the offsets are close means that **at infinite model scale, TriLMs approach FloatLM performance**. This is the asymptotic justification for ternary models — with enough parameters, the precision penalty becomes negligible.

**Projecting to larger scales** (Figure 10): Using the fitted equations, the paper derives the relationship between parameter count and the percentage difference in validation loss relative to FloatLMs:

$$N(k) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{0.26} \log\left(\frac{0.01671k}{0.0835 - 26.1 / (1.59k)}\right)\right)$$

(Note: this equation as printed in Figure 10 appears to have formatting issues — the intended relationship is likely `$\Delta L / L_{\text{FloatLM}} = f(N)$` where the percentage difference decreases with increasing `$N$`). The conclusion: at 15.6B parameters, TriLM validation loss is within 7% of FloatLM validation loss; at 330B parameters, it is within 6%. The gap never fully closes (due to the different `$\epsilon$` values), but it becomes small enough that other factors — downstream task performance, deployment efficiency, training cost — dominate the decision.

**Limitations of this scaling analysis:** The fits are based on only 9 data points per model family (99M to 3.9B), and the projection to 330B parameters involves extrapolating by nearly two orders of magnitude beyond the observed data range. The paper acknowledges this uncertainty implicitly by focusing the downstream evaluation on the actually-trained models rather than the extrapolated regime. Additionally, the fixed 300B-token data budget means the scaling law describes how loss varies with parameters given a fixed training data size — it does not capture the interaction between data size and parameter count (the Chinchilla-optimal allocation). The paper leaves the joint data-parameter scaling law analysis for future work.

#### Training Dynamics: Comparing TriLM and FloatLM Convergence

Figure 8b directly overlays the training loss curves of TriLM 2.4B against FloatLM 1.1B and FloatLM 1.5B. Despite having 2.4B parameters (more than double the 1.1B FloatLM), the TriLM initially underperforms — its training loss starts higher than the FloatLM 1.1B. However, by approximately 150B tokens (the halfway point, coinciding with the peak LR reduction), the TriLM 2.4B's loss curve crosses below the FloatLM 1.1B's and approaches the FloatLM 1.5B's. At the end of training (300B tokens), the TriLM 2.4B achieves training loss comparable to the FloatLM 1.5B despite having 1.6× more parameters — which, given the 5.27× smaller bit-size of the TriLM (7.23 × 10^9 bits vs. 24.23 × 10^9 bits for the 1.5B FloatLM per Table 4), represents a substantial net efficiency gain in the memory-constrained dimension.

This crossing behavior illustrates the paper's central claim in microcosm: ternary models need more parameters to match float model performance, but because each parameter is 5–10× smaller in bits, the total memory footprint can still be substantially lower. The scaling law analysis quantifies this tradeoff mathematically, and the downstream benchmark results (Section 5) confirm that the reduced bit-size does not come at the cost of proportionally reduced task performance.

#### BiLM: Binary Variant for Comparison

For completeness, the paper also trains Binary Language Models (BiLMs) at three scales (99M, 560M, 1.1B parameters) for comparison (Appendix B). The BiLM forward pass follows a similar pattern to TriLM but with only two states:

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{nm} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m |W_{ij}|$$

$$\widehat{W}_{ij} = \text{sign}\left(W_{ij} - \frac{1}{nm} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m W_{ij}\right)$$

$$\widetilde{W}_{ij} = \alpha \cdot \widehat{W}_{ij}$$

**What it computes:** the scale `$\alpha$` is the mean of absolute values (same as TriLM's `$\gamma$`, but without the epsilon term). The binary state `$\widehat{W}_{ij}$` is the sign of the weight *after centering by subtracting the mean weight*. The centering step (`$W_{ij} - \bar{W}$`) is necessary because binary models lack a zero state — without centering, if the weight distribution is not symmetric around zero, the sign function would map most weights to the same state. The effective weight `$\widetilde{W}_{ij}$` is then `$\pm \alpha$`.

**Why BiLM underperforms TriLM:** The lack of a zero state forces every weight to be either fully excitatory or fully inhibitory, with no ability to express "no connection." This is a much harsher constraint than ternarization, where weights near zero can map to the zero state, preserving sparsity patterns. The paper's results (Figure 15, Tables 6–7) confirm that BiLMs consistently underperform TriLMs at the same parameter count, with the gap narrowing as scale increases but remaining significant at 1.1B parameters. The paper thus focuses on TriLMs as the "sweet spot" in the low-bitwidth design space — enough compression to be practically valuable, but not so aggressive that representational capacity is catastrophically compromised.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Per-matrix scale (rather than per-channel or per-neuron):** minimizes the number of additional float parameters, keeping the effective bitwidth close to the theoretical 1.58 bits. The empirical results suggest this is sufficient — the model learns to distribute weight magnitudes such that a single scale per matrix captures the necessary dynamic range.

- **Absolute mean for scale computation (rather than standard deviation or max):** simple, differentiable (in the latent weights), and robust to outliers. The mean absolute value is less sensitive to extreme weights than the maximum and more directly tied to the average magnitude than the standard deviation.

- **Rounding thresholds at ±0.5:** the standard symmetric quantization rule that minimizes expected quantization error for uniformly distributed values within each interval. Given the Gaussian weight distributions (Appendix E, Figure 20), most weights fall near zero where the rounding rule is most precise.

- **Steep two-phase learning rate schedule:** the sharp reduction at the halfway point triggers a rapid loss decrease, and the removal of weight decay at the two-thirds mark allows final ternary state adjustments. These interventions are specifically motivated by the ternary constraint — the high initial learning rate helps weights cross quantization thresholds, the reduction refines once good ternary configurations are found, and the weight decay removal prevents the regularizer from fighting the final threshold crossings.

- **Pre-Normalization (RMSNorm before each sub-layer):** critical for training stability under FP16 (Wang et al., 2023), and simpler than BitNet's multi-stage activation quantization approach. The two normalizations per layer (vs. BitNet's 4–7) reduce computational overhead and simplify the architecture.

- **Per-device scale in model parallelism (rather than all-reduce):** avoids communication overhead for a single scalar, at the cost of slightly deviating from the single-scale-per-matrix ideal. The bit-size impact is negligible (less than `$10^{-5}$` bits per parameter).

- **Embeddings and LM head in FP16:** preserves semantic representation quality where it matters most (input token discrimination, output probability precision) while achieving compression where the bulk of parameters reside (linear layers in the transformer blocks).

- **Symmetric quantization for QuantLMs:** enables fair comparison with TriLMs (both have symmetric, scale-only representations), is supported by the fastest inference kernels (Marlin), and is simpler to implement than asymmetric quantization. The potential accuracy advantage of asymmetric quantization is acknowledged but set aside in favor of practical deployment considerations.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Bit-Size, Not Parameter Count, Is the Correct Metric for Comparing Low-Precision Models — And TriLMs Win on This Metric

The dominant practice in the LLM literature — from the original scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) through to contemporary model releases (Touvron et al., 2023a,b; Groeneveld et al., 2024) — is to compare models by **parameter count**. Two 7B-parameter models are considered "the same scale," regardless of whether one uses FP16 and the other uses 4-bit quantization. This convention is so deeply embedded that it shapes architecture design, training budget allocation, and deployment planning.

The Spectra paper makes a simple but profound reframing: **for deployment, the relevant metric is not parameter count but model bit-size** — the total number of bits required to store the model weights in the precision used at inference time. This shift in perspective is not merely a notational convenience; it fundamentally reorders the comparison between model families.

Consider the empirical consequence: TriLM 3.9B occupies 10.76 × 10^9 bits (Table 4). FloatLM 830M occupies 13.34 × 10^9 bits. So the TriLM 3.9B is actually **smaller in memory** than the FloatLM 830M — by about 19%. Yet on downstream benchmarks (Tables 6–7, Figures 1a–1b), the TriLM 3.9B achieves an average commonsense and reasoning score of 60.7%, matching the FloatLM 3.9B at 61.4% — far above the FloatLM 830M's 53.3%. When plotted against bit-size rather than parameter count (Figures 1a, 1c, 11a, 11c, 12a, 12c), the TriLM curves shift dramatically leftward relative to the FloatLM curves, revealing that **for a given memory budget, TriLMs extract substantially more downstream capability than FloatLMs**.

This reframing matters because it exposes what the parameter-count convention hides: post-training quantized models (QuantLMs) are, in fact, being compared unfairly when measured against FloatLMs by parameter count. A QuantLM 4-bit at 3.9B parameters has 20.59 × 10^9 bits — about 3.2× fewer than the FloatLM 3.9B at 63.83 × 10^9 bits. The QuantLM is achieving its performance with a fraction of the memory, and the paper's bit-size plots make this visible. By the same token, TriLM 3.9B at 10.76 × 10^9 bits is about 2× smaller than QuantLM 4-bit at the same parameter count, yet outperforms it on most benchmarks. The insight is not that TriLMs are "better" in some absolute sense, but that **the comparison framework itself was biased by focusing on parameters rather than bits**, and correcting this framework reveals the true efficiency landscape.

This is a **fundamental reframing**, not an incremental contribution. It does not depend on any particular architectural innovation — it is a conceptual move that reorients how the field should evaluate low-precision models going forward. The evidence is displayed systematically across every benchmark in Section 5: bit-size plots consistently show TriLMs dominating the left side of the x-axis (smaller memory footprint for the same accuracy), while parameter-count plots show a more ambiguous picture where FloatLMs maintain an edge. The paper does not claim this reframing as an explicit "contribution" in its list (Section 1), but it is the intellectual move that makes the TriLM results legible as a breakthrough rather than as "competitive but slightly worse at the same parameter count."

### Innovation 2: Quantization-Aware Pretraining Is Not Just Feasible at Scale — It Follows the Same Scaling Laws as Float Models, Implying a Universal Scaling Dynamic

Prior work on extreme low-bitwidth models (BitNet, BitNet b1.58 by Ma et al., 2024; binary and ternary networks by Courbariaux et al., 2016b; Li et al., 2016) demonstrated that low-bitwidth models *can be trained* and that they *sometimes approach* float model performance at specific scales. What was missing was a systematic characterization of **how** the performance gap between low-precision and full-precision models evolves with scale. Without this, practitioners could not answer the crucial deployment question: if I need a model of a certain quality at a certain memory budget, should I train a large ternary model or a smaller float model?

The Spectra paper's scaling law analysis (Section 4.3, Figure 9b, Equation 1) provides a remarkably clean answer: **TriLMs and FloatLMs share the identical scaling exponent `α = 0.26`**. This means that for every doubling of parameter count, the reducible component of validation loss decreases by the same factor (~20%) regardless of whether the weights are ternary or float. The precision choice affects only the constant offset — TriLMs have a higher multiplicative coefficient (`A = 185` vs. `A = 159`) and a slightly higher irreducible loss (`ε = 1.76` vs. `ε = 1.67`) — but the **rate of improvement with additional parameters is preserved**.

This is a significant theoretical finding because it suggests that the scaling dynamic is driven by something deeper than per-weight precision: the number of parameters determines the model's capacity to compose features, represent complex functions, and reduce loss, and this capacity scales identically whether each parameter carries 1.58 bits or 16 bits of information. The precision penalty manifests as a fixed multiplicative overhead — you need more ternary parameters than float parameters to achieve the same loss — but the overhead does not grow with scale. In fact, as Figure 10 shows, the percentage gap in validation loss *shrinks* with increasing parameter count (from ~11% at 1B parameters to a projected ~6% at 330B parameters), because the constant `ε` difference becomes a smaller fraction of the total loss as the reducible component decays.

This finding is **fundamental** rather than incremental because it establishes a universal property: the scaling exponent is invariant to the choice between ternary and float representation. This parallels the finding in Hoffmann et al. (2022) that the scaling exponent for model size vs. data is invariant to the specific architecture — it is a property of the learning problem, not the parameterization. If this result holds across other low-bitwidth representations (binary, 2-bit, mixed-precision), it would constitute a **scaling law for precision**, analogous to the Chinchilla laws for model size and data quantity.

The practical implication is substantial: given the fitted equations, a practitioner can compute that a TriLM needs approximately 2.5× more parameters than a FloatLM to achieve the same validation loss at the 3.9B scale, but because each TriLM parameter is ~5.9× smaller in bits, the TriLM still wins on memory footprint by a factor of ~2.4×. At larger scales (the 15.6B projection), the required parameter multiplier shrinks as the constant gap becomes proportionally smaller. This is the quantitative foundation for the paper's claim that ternary models are not just a curiosity but a **scalable alternative** to float models for memory-constrained deployment.

### Innovation 3: The TriLM vs. QuantLM Comparison Reveals a Fundamental Distinction Between "Representation-Adapted" and "Representation-Compressed" Low-Precision Models — And Only the Former Preserves Knowledge Capacity

The paper's inclusion of both QuantLMs (post-training quantized from FloatLMs) and TriLMs (trained natively at low precision) within the same suite, evaluated on the same benchmarks, enables a diagnostic comparison that prior work could not make. The result is a crisp empirical distinction between two families of low-precision models with qualitatively different failure modes.

**QuantLMs suffer from knowledge capacity collapse.** Across knowledge-intensive benchmarks (SciQ, TriviaQA, MMLU — Figures 11–12, Tables 9 and 13), QuantLMs at 3-bit and 4-bit precision exhibit a consistent pattern: their knowledge performance degrades disproportionately compared to their commonsense reasoning performance. At 3.9B parameters, FloatLM achieves 21.5% exact match on TriviaQA, QuantLM 4-bit achieves 17.9%, and QuantLM 3-bit collapses to 8.2% — less than half the FloatLM performance (Table 12). This aligns with Allen-Zhu and Li (2024)'s finding of ~65% reduced knowledge capacity per parameter in quantized models.

**TriLMs do not suffer from this collapse.** TriLM 3.9B achieves 21.3% on TriviaQA — essentially identical to FloatLM 3.9B's 21.5% (Table 12). On SciQ (Figure 11a–11b), TriLM 3.9B matches the FloatLM trend. On MMLU (Figure 12a–12b, Table 13), TriLM 3.9B scores 32.8% vs. FloatLM's 33.3%. This parity on knowledge tasks, despite the TriLM having 5.9× fewer bits, is the paper's most surprising result — it suggests that **knowledge is encoded in the pattern of connections (which connections exist, and whether they are excitatory or inhibitory) rather than in the continuous strength of those connections**.

This is a **conceptual advance** with implications beyond the specific models studied. It suggests that post-training quantization and quantization-aware pretraining are not two methods to achieve the same thing — they produce models with fundamentally different information-theoretic properties. PTQ takes a model that learned to store knowledge in continuous weight magnitudes and forcibly discretizes those magnitudes, destroying fine-grained distinctions that mattered for factual recall. TriLMs, by contrast, learn from the start to store knowledge in the only degrees of freedom available: the sign and sparsity pattern of the ternary weights. The training process adapts the representation to the constraint, finding ternary configurations that preserve the necessary distinctions.

The evidence is clearest in the head-to-head TriLM vs. QuantLM comparisons at the same bit-size. At the 3.9B parameter scale, TriLM (10.76 × 10^9 bits) and QuantLM 3-bit (16.91 × 10^9 bits) are the closest bit-size match. The QuantLM 3-bit actually has ~57% *more* bits than the TriLM, yet the TriLM outperforms it on nearly every benchmark (e.g., average commonsense reasoning: TriLM 60.7% vs. QuantLM 3-bit 56.8%; LAMBADA accuracy: TriLM 61.6% vs. QuantLM 3-bit 47.1%; TriviaQA: TriLM 21.3% vs. QuantLM 3-bit 8.2%). This is a clean demonstration that **how you arrive at low precision matters more than the precision level itself** — a finding that should reshape the conversation around efficient LLM deployment.

This is a **fundamental insight** because it identifies a qualitative property (knowledge capacity preservation vs. collapse) that distinguishes representation-adapted models from representation-compressed models, with direct practical consequences for which low-precision approach to use in knowledge-intensive applications.

### Innovation 4: The Perplexity-Throughput Paradox — TriLMs Match FloatLMs on Clean Benchmarks but Lag on Web Corpora, Revealing a Targeted Weakness in Memorization Capacity

One of the paper's most diagnostically revealing results is buried in Section 5 (Figure 13) and has not been highlighted in prior low-bitwidth model evaluations: **TriLM 3.9B matches or beats FloatLM 3.9B on "clean" text corpora (Penn Tree Bank, OpenAI LAMBADA) but consistently underperforms on web-derived corpora, including the in-domain SlimPajama test set.**

Specifically, on Penn Tree Bank (a small, carefully curated corpus of Wall Street Journal text) and OpenAI LAMBADA (book excerpts requiring broad discourse context), the TriLM achieves comparable or better cross-entropy than the FloatLM. But on C4, Cosmopedia, Dolma, S2Orc, Wikipedia, and RefinedWeb — large-scale web-derived corpora that likely overlap with the SlimPajama training distribution — the TriLM shows consistently higher cross-entropy (worse perplexity), with gaps of approximately 0.2–0.5 log units.

This pattern is not a scaling-law artifact or a precision limit in the conventional sense. If ternary weights were simply information-theoretically inferior, the degradation should be uniform across all corpora. The selective nature of the gap — worse on web text, competitive on curated text — points to a specific limitation: **ternary models have reduced capacity for memorizing the statistical patterns of noisy, redundant, high-entropy web text compared to float models**. Curated corpora like PTB and LAMBADA have lower entropy, more structured language, and less redundancy — the model's limited per-weight precision is adequate for capturing the essential patterns. Web corpora, with their long tails of rare tokens, templated content, formatting artifacts, and diverse styles, require more bits of precision per parameter to fit the training distribution tightly.

This is a **negative result with significant implications** rather than a metric gain. It tells practitioners that deploying a TriLM for applications requiring faithful modeling of web text distributions (e.g., data filtering, web content generation, training data quality assessment) may be suboptimal, even if the model performs well on downstream reasoning and knowledge benchmarks. It also suggests that the TriLM's competitive downstream performance on benchmarks like HellaSwag or MMLU comes from learning *generalizable* patterns (which fit within the ternary capacity budget) rather than from memorizing training data specifics (which do not).

The finding connects to the broader literature on memorization in LLMs (Carlini et al., 2023; Biderman et al., 2023): ternary models may exhibit **reduced memorization** as a side effect of their limited per-weight precision, which could be either a benefit (less verbatim training data leakage, potentially better generalization) or a drawback (worse performance on tasks requiring exact factual recall of training data), depending on the application. The paper notes this ambiguity but does not resolve it — it is offered as a direction for future investigation.

This insight is **incremental rather than fundamental** — it identifies an empirical phenomenon that warrants further study rather than establishing a new principle — but it is practically important because it provides a concrete diagnostic for where TriLMs will and will not be competitive, moving beyond the aggregate benchmark scores that can obscure such texture.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All models are evaluated on a comprehensive suite of benchmarks spanning commonsense reasoning, knowledge, and toxicity. The commonsense and reasoning benchmarks consist of eight tasks evaluated under zero-shot settings: Arc Easy and Arc Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), HellaSWAG (Zellers et al., 2019), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2019), LAMBADA (Paperno et al., 2016), and LogiQA (Liu et al., 2021). Knowledge benchmarks include SciQ (Welbl et al., 2017), TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017), and MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021) across its four subject categories (STEM, Humanities, Social Sciences, Other). Toxicity benchmarks include TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2021), Big Bench BBQ Lite (Parrish et al., 2022), and CrowsPairs (Nangia et al., 2020).

- **Base model(s).** The Spectra suite comprises three model families — FloatLMs (FP16), TriLMs (ternary weights with FP16 latents), and QuantLMs (GPTQ-quantized FloatLMs at 3, 4, 6, and 8 bits) — spanning nine parameter counts from 99M to 3.9B, totaling 54 models. All models share an identical LLaMa-style transformer architecture (RMSNorm, SwiGLU MLP, RoPE, multi-headed attention, no bias) and are trained on the same 300B-token subset of SlimPajama with identical data ordering. The Pythia suite (Biderman et al., 2023) at scales from 70M to 2.8B parameters and BitNet b1.58 (Ma et al., 2024) at 700M and 1.3B parameters are included as additional comparison points for commonsense and reasoning benchmarks (Tables 6–8).

- **Metrics.** For commonsense and reasoning benchmarks, the primary metric is accuracy (percentage of correctly answered questions), with normalized accuracy also reported for Arc, HellaSWAG, PIQA, and LogiQA (where answer choice count varies). For LAMBADA, both accuracy and perplexity are reported. For knowledge benchmarks, SciQ and MMLU use accuracy, while TriviaQA uses exact match. For toxicity benchmarks, CrowsPairs uses a likelihood difference metric (comparing stereotyped vs. anti-stereotyped sentence pairs), BBQ Lite reports the percentage of stereotyped responses, and TruthfulQA reports accuracy. Validation perplexity on SlimPajama and cross-entropy (log perplexity) on out-of-distribution corpora (PTB, C4, Cosmopedia, Dolma, S2Orc, Wikipedia, RefinedWeb) are additional metrics for comparing TriLM and FloatLM at the 3.9B scale.

- **Baselines.** The primary baselines are FloatLMs (full-precision FP16 models trained identically to TriLMs) and QuantLMs at 3-bit, 4-bit, 6-bit, and 8-bit precision (GPTQ-quantized from the corresponding FloatLM checkpoint). Pythia models at comparable scales (70M, 160M, 410M, 1B, 1.4B) are included as external reference points for commonsense reasoning performance (Tables 6–7). BitNet b1.58 scores at 700M and 1.3B, taken from Ma et al. (2024), are compared alongside the authors' own BitNet b1.58 replication at 1.1B (Table 8, Appendix A.5).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** All evaluations are zero-shot — no generation budget is consumed for few-shot prompting, and there is no test-time compute scaling or search involved. The relevant "budget" for comparison is the model size in bits (the total number of bits required to store model weights at inference precision) and the parameter count (number of trainable parameters), as shown in Table 4. For TriLMs, the embedding layer and language model head remain in FP16 and are counted at full precision (the effective bitwidth of 1.58 bits applies only to linear layer weights within transformer blocks). For QuantLMs, the effective bit rates are 3.25 and 4.25 bits per parameter for 3-bit and 4-bit quantization respectively, accounting for the scale factors stored with group size 128.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** QuantLM results are averaged across 3 different calibration seeds — three separate calibration sets drawn from SlimPajama, producing three independently quantized models that are evaluated separately and then averaged. For TriLMs and FloatLMs, single training runs are evaluated (no ensembling or multiple seeds reported). All benchmark evaluations use the LM Evaluation Harness (Gao et al., 2023) in zero-shot mode. The paper does not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests for any benchmark comparisons.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Commonsense and Reasoning Benchmarks: TriLMs Win on Bit-Size, Approach Parity on Parameters at 3.9B Scale

The headline result for commonsense and reasoning emerges from two complementary perspectives: performance versus model bit-size (the deployment-relevant metric) and performance versus parameter count (the traditional scaling metric).

**Bit-size comparison (Figures 1a, 1c).** When models are compared by their total memory footprint in bits, TriLMs dominate the landscape at scales above approximately 2 × 10^9 bits. The TriLM 3.9B, occupying 10.76 × 10^9 bits, achieves an average commonsense and reasoning score of 60.7% across the six primary benchmarks (Arc Easy, Arc Challenge, BoolQ, HellaSwag, PIQA, WinoGrande) — this matches or exceeds every FloatLM and QuantLM at similar bit-sizes, including FloatLM 830M (13.34 × 10^9 bits, 53.3%) and QuantLM 4-bit 3.9B (20.59 × 10^9 bits, 60.7%). On LAMBADA accuracy (Figure 1c), TriLM 3.9B scores 61.6% at 10.76 × 10^9 bits, substantially outperforming the nearest bit-size competitors: QuantLM 3-bit 1.5B (17.8 perplexity, 45.3% accuracy at 7.55 × 10^9 bits) and FloatLM 830M (49.6% at 13.34 × 10^9 bits). The bit-size advantage is visually stark in Figures 1a and 1c: the TriLM curve is shifted dramatically leftward relative to the FloatLM and QuantLM curves, meaning that for a fixed memory budget, TriLMs extract more benchmark performance.

**Parameter-count comparison (Figures 1b, 1d).** When compared by parameter count rather than bit-size, the picture shifts but the asymptotic convergence predicted by the scaling laws is empirically visible. At the 99M through 1.5B parameter scales, FloatLMs consistently outperform TriLMs at the same parameter count. For example, at 1.1B parameters, FloatLM averages 54.9% on commonsense reasoning versus TriLM's 51.6% (Table 7). However, at 2.4B parameters, the gap narrows (FloatLM 59.2% vs. TriLM 57.3%), and at 3.9B parameters, TriLM achieves 60.7% versus FloatLM's 61.4% — a difference of less than one percentage point. On LAMBADA, the crossover is even more dramatic: TriLM 3.9B scores 61.6% accuracy, slightly exceeding FloatLM 3.9B's 61.1%, and with a lower perplexity (6.3 vs. 6.7 — Table 9). This convergence at the 3.9B scale is the key empirical validation of the scaling law projection: the loss gap between TriLMs and FloatLMs shrinks as parameter count increases, and at sufficient scale, ternary models match full-precision models on downstream tasks.

**Detailed per-benchmark results (Tables 6, 7, 9).** Breaking out the six individual commonsense benchmarks reveals several non-obvious patterns:

- **Arc Challenge and Arc Easy (Table 6):** These are the most knowledge-intensive commonsense tasks, and TriLM 3.9B actually achieves the highest Arc Challenge accuracy of any model in the suite at 35.3% (normalized) and 31.9% (unnormalized), exceeding FloatLM 3.9B's 34.6% and 32.1% respectively. This is a surprising inversion — on the benchmark requiring the most scientific reasoning, the ternary model slightly edges out the float model.

- **BoolQ (Table 6):** TriLM 3.9B scores 66.5%, marginally above FloatLM 3.9B's 65.9%. However, at smaller scales, FloatLMs hold a clearer advantage (e.g., FloatLM 1.1B 62.9% vs. TriLM 1.1B 59.1%), and the gap closes gradually with scale.

- **HellaSWAG (Table 7):** The largest persistent gap between TriLMs and FloatLMs at scale is on HellaSWAG. TriLM 3.9B scores 64.7% normalized accuracy and 48.3% unnormalized, compared to FloatLM 3.9B's 66.1% and 49.7%. This gap persists across all scales and is consistent with HellaSWAG's nature as a benchmark requiring fine-grained commonsense reasoning about plausible sentence completions — a task that may depend more on precise weight representations to distinguish subtly different continuations.

- **PIQA (Table 7):** The gap is modest and consistent: TriLM 3.9B at 74.6% vs. FloatLM 3.9B at 75.8%. The 1.2 percentage point difference is representative of the general pattern — ternary models are within striking distance on most reasoning tasks.

- **WinoGrande (Table 7):** TriLM 3.9B at 62.1% vs. FloatLM 3.9B at 62.8%, a 0.7 point gap. This is the benchmark where TriLMs come closest to FloatLMs at all scales above 830M parameters, suggesting that the binary resolution of WinoGrande (fill-in-the-blank with two options) is well-suited to ternary weight representations.

- **LogiQA (Table 9):** All models perform poorly, with accuracy clustering around 20–24% regardless of scale, precision, or model family. No clear trend emerges, and the paper explicitly notes that "across the considered scales, all LLMs show poor performance on LogiQA, making it difficult to identify a clear performance trend" (Section 5). This is useful as a negative result: it identifies a benchmark that is outside the capability range of models at this scale and training budget, serving as a reminder that scaling alone does not unlock all reasoning capabilities.

**Comparison with QuantLMs.** Across all commonsense and reasoning benchmarks, TriLMs at the 2.4B and 3.9B scales consistently outperform QuantLMs at 3-bit and 4-bit precision when compared at the same parameter count. The most direct comparison: TriLM 3.9B at 60.7% average commonsense reasoning vs. QuantLM 3-bit 3.9B at 56.8% vs. QuantLM 4-bit 3.9B at 60.7% (Table 7). Notably, TriLM achieves the same average as QuantLM 4-bit while being approximately 1.9× smaller in bits (10.76 vs. 20.59 × 10^9 bits per Table 4) — the TriLM matches the 4-bit QuantLM's performance with half the memory. Against QuantLM 3-bit, the comparison is even more favorable: TriLM is 1.6× smaller (10.76 vs. 16.91 × 10^9 bits) and substantially higher-performing. This is the cleanest head-to-head demonstration that pretraining at low bitwidth outperforms post-training quantization at the same or similar effective precision.

**Comparison with Pythia.** The Pythia suite provides an external reference for FloatLM performance at similar scales (Tables 6–7). Spectra FloatLMs generally outperform Pythia models at comparable parameter counts — e.g., FloatLM 1.1B at 54.9% vs. Pythia 1B at 51.1% average commonsense reasoning. This suggests the Spectra training recipe (cosine decay, 300B tokens of SlimPajama) is a strong baseline, making the TriLM results more credible — the ternary models are not merely catching up to a weak float baseline.

#### LAMBADA: TriLMs Surpass FloatLMs at 3.9B Parameters

LAMBADA (Table 9, Figures 1c–1d) deserves separate treatment because it tells a qualitatively different story than the other commonsense benchmarks. While TriLMs trail FloatLMs at small scales on LAMBADA accuracy (e.g., TriLM 99M: 20.0% vs. FloatLM 99M: 26.5%), the gap closes and then inverts at the largest scale: **TriLM 3.9B achieves 61.6% accuracy vs. FloatLM 3.9B's 61.1%, and TriLM's perplexity of 6.3 is lower than FloatLM's 6.7**. This inversion is not seen on any other commonsense benchmark — it is unique to LAMBADA.

What makes LAMBADA distinctive: it evaluates a model's ability to predict the final word of a passage by integrating broader discourse context. Success requires tracking entities, resolving references, and maintaining coherence across multiple sentences. The fact that TriLMs excel specifically on this benchmark suggests that ternary weight representations may be particularly well-suited to tasks requiring **long-range contextual integration** — the discrete, categorical nature of ternary weights might provide a form of inductive bias toward tracking discrete discourse states (entities, topics, references) across long contexts.

The perplexity result is even more striking: TriLM 3.9B's LAMBADA perplexity of 6.3 is the lowest of any model in the Spectra suite at any scale except FloatLM 2.4B (7.7) and FloatLM 3.9B (6.7). This is the empirical evidence that the validation loss gap between TriLMs and FloatLMs, while present on web text (see the perplexity analysis below), largely disappears on clean, structured text corpora — consistent with the scaling law projection that the gap becomes insignificant as the irreducibile loss floor is approached.

#### Knowledge Benchmarks: TriLMs Match FloatLMs, Dramatically Outperform QuantLMs

The knowledge benchmark results (SciQ, TriviaQA, MMLU — Figures 11–12, Tables 9, 12, 13) are arguably the paper's most unexpected and consequential findings, because they demonstrate that **ternarization does not destroy factual knowledge capacity** — a direct counterexample to the concern, raised by Allen-Zhu and Li (2024) and others, that low-precision models inherently lose the ability to store facts.

**SciQ (Figure 11a–11b, Table 9).** On this science question-answering benchmark, TriLM 3.9B achieves 87.4% normalized accuracy, compared to FloatLM 3.9B's 86.5% and QuantLM 4-bit 3.9B's 86.1%. The TriLM actually has the highest SciQ accuracy of any model in the Spectra suite. The bit-size plot (Figure 11a) shows TriLMs far to the left of the FloatLM and QuantLM curves — a TriLM at 2.4B parameters (8.6 × 10^9 bits) already achieves 84.2%, exceeding FloatLM 1.1B (11.7 × 10^9 bits, 82.2%) and QuantLM 4-bit 2.4B (13.18 × 10^9 bits, 84.8%). The knowledge encoded in SciQ questions (basic science facts drawn from crowd-sourced science exams) appears to be well-preserved under ternarization.

**TriviaQA (Figure 11c–11d, Table 12).** The pattern is consistent: TriLM 3.9B achieves 21.3% exact match, compared to FloatLM 3.9B's 21.5% and QuantLM 4-bit 3.9B's 17.9%. What the bit-size comparison reveals here is even more dramatic: TriLM 3.9B at 10.76 × 10^9 bits matches the TriviaQA performance of FloatLM 2.4B at 39.38 × 10^9 bits (20.7%) — a **3.7× memory reduction for equivalent knowledge retrieval performance**. Against QuantLMs, the advantage is categorical: QuantLM 3-bit 3.9B collapses to 8.2% exact match at 16.91 × 10^9 bits, meaning the TriLM achieves 2.6× better accuracy with 1.6× fewer bits. The knowledge capacity collapse under aggressive post-training quantization is stark, and the TriLM's preservation of this capacity is the paper's strongest empirical argument for pretraining at low bitwidth.

**MMLU (Figure 12a–12d, Table 13).** The Massively Multitask Language Understanding benchmark spans 57 subjects, and the Spectra results show TriLM 3.9B at 32.8% average accuracy vs. FloatLM 3.9B at 33.3% — a half-point gap. The subject-category breakdown (Figure 22, Table 13) reveals that the gap is not uniform:

- **STEM (Figure 12c–12d):** TriLM 3.9B achieves 28.3% vs. FloatLM 3.9B's 27.7% — actually exceeding the float model. This includes mathematics, physics, chemistry, and engineering questions, where one might expect precision-sensitive computation. That TriLM performs slightly better here is surprising and may reflect the regularization benefits of ternarization (preventing overfitting to spurious training-set patterns that would hurt generalization on precise STEM questions).

- **Humanities:** TriLM 3.9B at 29.5% vs. FloatLM 3.9B at 30.6%, a 1.1-point gap.

- **Social Sciences:** TriLM 3.9B at 35.4% vs. FloatLM 3.9B at 36.9%, a 1.5-point gap.

- **Other (business, health, misc.):** TriLM 3.9B at 39.6% vs. FloatLM 3.9B at 39.8% — essentially identical.

The MMLU picture is consistent with the broader pattern: at 3.9B parameters, TriLMs are within 1–2 percentage points of FloatLMs across all knowledge categories, with no evidence of the catastrophic knowledge loss seen in QuantLMs. The bit-size efficiency advantage (TriLM 3.9B at 10.76 × 10^9 bits matching FloatLM 2.4B at 39.38 × 10^9 bits on MMLU, with the TriLM slightly ahead at 32.8% vs. 31.8%) represents a roughly 3.7× memory reduction.

#### Toxicity Benchmarks: TriLMs Exhibit FloatLM-Equivalent Biases at Scale

The toxicity evaluation (Table 12) addresses a critical deployment consideration: do low-bitwidth models exhibit different safety properties than float models? The answer, based on three benchmarks, is nuanced.

**CrowsPairs (likelihood difference metric):** Lower scores indicate less stereotyping bias (the model assigns less probability mass to stereotyped completions relative to anti-stereotyped completions). At small scales, TriLMs appear somewhat less biased: TriLM 99M scores 362.4 vs. FloatLM 99M's 372.4, and TriLM 190M scores 343.5 vs. FloatLM 190M's 348.2. However, at the largest scales, this difference disappears: TriLM 3.9B scores 362.4, essentially identical to FloatLM 3.9B's 359.2. The trend across scales is that TriLMs and FloatLMs converge on CrowsPairs as parameter count increases, consistent with the general scaling-law convergence pattern.

**Big Bench BBQ Lite (percentage stereotyped responses):** The pattern is similar. At 99M parameters, TriLM shows slightly more stereotyping (30.8%) than FloatLM (30.8% — actually identical in the table). At 3.9B, TriLM is at 25.9% vs. FloatLM at 25.4% — a negligible difference. The key contextual comparison: when measured by bit-size rather than parameter count, TriLM 3.9B at 10.76 × 10^9 bits exhibits substantially *more* stereotyping than FloatLM 830M at 13.34 × 10^9 bits (25.7%), even though they occupy similar memory footprints. This is the toxicity-specific manifestation of the TriLM scaling dynamic: the ternary model achieves its knowledge and reasoning performance by having many more parameters (3.9B vs. 830M), and those additional parameters carry biases that scale with parameter count rather than with bit-size.

**TruthfulQA:** All Spectra models, regardless of precision or scale, perform near random-guessing levels (21–26% accuracy, Table 12). The paper states that "none of the LLMs, even those with up to 3.9 billion parameters and trained on 300 billion tokens, perform significantly better than random guessing on the TruthfulQA benchmark" (Section 5). This is a negative result that applies uniformly across all model families — TruthfulQA requires capabilities (distinguishing truth from common misconceptions, resisting imitative falsehoods) that are not unlocked by scaling to 3.9B parameters on 300B tokens of web data, regardless of precision.

**Summary of toxicity findings.** The paper concludes that "TriLMs initially show reduced bias compared to similarly sized FloatLMs, their performance aligns with FloatLMs of equivalent parameter counts at larger scales" and that "TriLMs exhibit considerably more stereotyping than FloatLMs of comparable size (measured in bits), yet perform comparably to FloatLMs with similar parameter counts" (Section 5). This is an important boundary condition on the TriLM advantage: the bit-size efficiency gains in reasoning and knowledge come with bit-size-equal toxicity that is proportional to parameter count, not bit-size. A TriLM at a given memory budget will exhibit more bias than a FloatLM at the same memory budget because the TriLM packs more parameters (and thus more learned biases) into that budget.

#### Perplexity on Out-of-Distribution Corpora: TriLMs Lag on Web Text, Match on Clean Text

Figure 13 presents cross-entropy (log perplexity) for TriLM 3.9B and FloatLM 3.9B across eight corpora beyond the SlimPajama training distribution. The pattern is striking in its selectivity:

**Where TriLM matches or beats FloatLM:**
- **OpenAI LAMBADA:** TriLM achieves lower cross-entropy (better perplexity) than FloatLM — approximately 1.8 vs. 1.9 log units, consistent with the LAMBADA accuracy results showing TriLM outperforming FloatLM at 3.9B.
- **Penn Tree Bank (PTB):** TriLM and FloatLM are essentially indistinguishable, with cross-entropy around 2.0–2.1 log units.

**Where TriLM underperforms FloatLM:**
- **SlimPajama (in-domain test set):** The TriLM shows higher cross-entropy, consistent with the validation loss results in Figure 9b — TriLM 3.9B's validation loss (~2.6 log units) is above FloatLM 3.9B's (~2.3 log units).
- **C4, Cosmopedia, Dolma, S2Orc, Wikipedia, RefinedWeb:** Across all these web-derived corpora, TriLM 3.9B shows consistently higher cross-entropy than FloatLM 3.9B, with gaps ranging from approximately 0.2 to 0.5 log units. The gap is largest on corpora with the most overlap with SlimPajama (C4, Wikipedia are components of SlimPajama) and on noisy web text (Dolma, RefinedWeb).

**Interpretation.** This selective perplexity gap — present on web text, absent on curated text — suggests a specific limitation: ternary models have reduced capacity to memorize the statistical patterns of the training distribution. The fact that TriLM excels on PTB (a small, clean corpus of financial news) and LAMBADA (book excerpts) while lagging on web corpora indicates that the ternary constraint does not impair the learning of structured, low-entropy language patterns (which can be captured with discrete ternary weights) but does impair the precise modeling of the long tail of tokens and patterns in high-entropy web text. This is consistent with the reduced memorization hypothesis — web text contains many rare patterns that require fine-grained weight magnitudes to distinguish, and the ternary representation simply cannot encode all of these distinctions.

This finding has practical consequences: a deployed TriLM would be an excellent choice for applications requiring general language understanding and reasoning (the downstream benchmarks confirm this), but a poor choice for applications requiring faithful modeling of web text distributions — data filtering, content generation that matches web text style, or any task where exact recall of training distribution statistics matters.

#### Illustrative Examples: TriLM 3.9B Generates Coherent Text

Appendix H provides example outputs from TriLM 3.9B on reading comprehension, creative writing (essay and poem completion), and various auto-completion prompts. The examples demonstrate that the 3.9B TriLM produces grammatically correct, contextually appropriate, and thematically coherent text. The reading comprehension examples show accurate extraction of factual answers from provided background passages (e.g., correctly answering "How many sorties were flown in March 1941?" as "4,000" and identifying Helsinki as the most populous municipality in Finland). The creative writing examples (a Mars colonization story and a poem about a garden at night) demonstrate narrative coherence and appropriate stylistic register. The auto-completion examples show sensible continuations of prompts about baking, cellular respiration, and handling disagreement.

These examples are not systematically evaluated and should be treated as qualitative demonstrations rather than quantitative evidence. They serve to counter the concern that ternary models might produce garbled or incoherent text — the 3.9B TriLM clearly generates human-readable, context-appropriate outputs, consistent with its strong performance on LAMBADA and commonsense benchmarks.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Optimization schedule for TriLMs (Figure 6, Tables 10–11):** The two-phase schedule (peak LR reduction at 50% of training, weight decay removal at 66.7%) is validated through an ablation on a 1.1B TriLM trained for 100B tokens. Four conditions are compared: (a) both interventions, (b) only L2 regularization removal, (c) only peak LR reduction, (d) baseline linear decay with constant weight decay. The full schedule achieves the lowest final training loss (Figure 6) and the highest average commonsense reasoning score (50.2% — Table 11), followed by only L2 removal (50.1%), only peak LR reduction (49.7%), and baseline (49.1%). The ablation confirms that both interventions contribute to performance and that dropping L2 regularization provides a larger benefit than reducing peak LR, consistent with the rationale that ternarization itself provides sufficient regularization (Courbariaux et al., 2016b) and that weight decay late in training impedes final ternary-state transitions.

**TriLM vs. BitNet b1.58 architecture (Figure 14, Tables 10–11):** The paper replicates BitNet b1.58's architecture at 1.1B parameters (trained on 100B tokens of the same SlimPajama data) and compares against TriLM 1.1B and the published BitNet scores at 700M and 1.3B. TriLM 1.1B achieves 50.2% average commonsense reasoning, outperforming the BitNet 1.1B replication (48.9%), BitNet 1.3B (49.6% as reported by Ma et al.), and BitNet 700M (48.3%). The architectural simplifications in TriLM (Pre-Normalization with RMSNorm before each sub-layer vs. BitNet's 4–7 normalization operations per layer, learnable scale parameters in RMSNorm) are credited for the improvement. This is an important robustness check: the TriLM advantage over BitNet is not specific to a particular scale or training setup.

**Binary vs. ternary (Figures 15–18, Tables 6, 7, 9, 12):** BiLMs (Binary Language Models) trained at 99M, 560M, and 1.1B parameters consistently underperform TriLMs at the same parameter counts. At 1.1B parameters, BiLM achieves 47.1% average commonsense reasoning vs. TriLM's 51.6% (Table 7), and the validation loss is higher across all scales (Figure 15). The binary models also perform worse on knowledge benchmarks (e.g., MMLU average: BiLM 1.1B at 23.0% vs. TriLM 1.1B at 28.3%, Table 13). The gap between binary and ternary narrows with increasing scale (Figure 15b), suggesting binary models may eventually catch up, but at the scales studied, ternary is the clear winner in the low-bitwidth design space. This justifies the paper's focus on TriLMs rather than BiLMs for the full suite.

**Data quality ablation: SlimPajama vs. FineWeb (Tables 10–11):** Both TriLM 1.1B and FloatLM 1.1B are trained on 100B tokens of FineWeb (a higher-quality web corpus) for comparison against the SlimPajama training runs. Both models improve on FineWeb: TriLM 1.1B FineWeb achieves 54.9% average commonsense reasoning vs. 50.2% on SlimPajama, and FloatLM 1.1B FineWeb achieves 56.9% vs. 52.2% on SlimPajama. Critically, the gap between TriLM and FloatLM (2.0 percentage points on FineWeb vs. 2.0 points on SlimPajama — the difference is identical) is preserved across datasets. This suggests that the TriLM-FloatLM performance gap is a property of the architecture/precision rather than an artifact of the specific training data — higher-quality data improves both models proportionally, and the ternary models remain at a consistent relative disadvantage that shrinks with scale.

**QuantLM calibration robustness:** QuantLM results are averaged across 3 calibration seeds (different random subsets of SlimPajama). While standard deviations are not reported in the tables, the consistency of QuantLM performance across neighboring parameter scales (e.g., QuantLM 4-bit consistently outperforms QuantLM 3-bit at every scale) suggests that the calibration is not introducing substantial noise. The paper's choice of length-normalized calibration data and 1M-token calibration set (following Malinovskii et al., 2024) represents a deliberate effort to produce strong and reliable QuantLM baselines.

**FP16 training stability (Table 5):** All TriLM and FloatLM training runs maintained minimum loss scales at or above 128, the recommended threshold for stable FP16 mixed-precision training (Micikevicius et al., 2018). The lowest minimum loss scale observed was 128.0 for TriLM 830M and TriLM 3.9B. The number of batches skipped due to loss scale overflow was consistently small (158–318 batches across all runs, representing 0.32–0.37B tokens out of 300B), indicating robust training dynamics. This is a necessary (but not sufficient) condition for the validity of the comparisons — unstable training runs would produce unreliable performance estimates.

### Critical Assessment

The experimental design of the Spectra suite is methodical and thorough by the standards of model comparison papers: 54 models across nine parameter scales and six bitwidths, evaluated on 13 benchmarks spanning three capability dimensions, all trained on identical data with identical architectures. This comprehensiveness is the paper's primary strength — it enables systematic comparison that would be impossible with scattered, independent model releases. However, several aspects of the experimental evidence and its relationship to the paper's central claims warrant scrutiny.

**On the claim that TriLMs "consistently outperform their QuantLM and FloatLM counterparts for a given bit size at scales exceeding one billion parameters":** The evidence for this claim is strong but comes with important caveats about what "for a given bit size" means in practice. The QuantLM and FloatLM bit-sizes are continuous variables determined by the quantization method (any precision between 3 and 16 bits could be chosen), while TriLM bitwidth is fixed at 1.58 effective bits (with FP16 embeddings and LM head). The "comparison at a given bit-size" therefore involves interpolation: the paper compares TriLMs against the nearest QuantLM or FloatLM models at similar total bit-sizes. At the 3.9B scale, the closest bit-size match to TriLM 3.9B (10.76 × 10^9 bits) is QuantLM 3-bit 2.4B (10.95 × 10^9 bits), and the TriLM dramatically outperforms it (60.7% vs. 54.7% average commonsense reasoning). However, one could ask: what if we quantized a 1.5B or 2.4B FloatLM to a custom bitwidth that exactly matches 10.76 × 10^9 bits? The paper does not explore non-standard bitwidths or mixed-precision schemes that might close this gap for QuantLMs. The claim effectively compares a point (TriLM at fixed 1.58 bits) against a sparsely sampled curve (QuantLMs at 3, 4, 6, 8 bits), and the comparison is valid but incomplete — it doesn't rule out the possibility that a carefully tuned 2.5-bit or mixed-precision QuantLM could match the TriLM.

The stronger and more defensible version of this claim is simply: **at the same parameter count, TriLMs outperform QuantLMs at 3-bit and 4-bit precision, while occupying fewer bits**. This is uncontestably demonstrated in the data (e.g., TriLM 3.9B at 60.7% and 10.76 × 10^9 bits vs. QuantLM 3-bit 3.9B at 56.8% and 16.91 × 10^9 bits — the TriLM is both better and smaller). This version does not depend on matching bit-sizes exactly.

**On the claim that TriLM 3.9B "matches the performance of FloatLM 3.9B across all benchmarks":** This claim is supported for the commonsense reasoning average (60.7% vs. 61.4%, a 0.7-point gap), LAMBADA accuracy (61.6% vs. 61.1%, TriLM slightly ahead), SciQ (87.4% vs. 86.5%), TriviaQA (21.3% vs. 21.5%), and MMLU (32.8% vs. 33.3%). The largest gaps are on HellaSWAG (48.3% vs. 49.7% unnormalized, a 1.4-point gap) and MMLU Social Sciences (35.4% vs. 36.9%, a 1.5-point gap). Whether these gaps constitute "matching" is a matter of judgment — most practitioners would consider differences of 1–2 percentage points on individual benchmarks to be within the noise of model training and evaluation, especially without reported confidence intervals. However, the claim should be tempered by the perplexity results: TriLM 3.9B's in-domain validation loss is worse than FloatLM 3.9B's, and the gap on web corpora is substantial. The downstream task performance matches, but the distributional modeling does not — a distinction that matters for applications beyond benchmark evaluation.

**On the claim that TriLMs "offer better scaling behavior in terms of model size (in bits)":** The scaling law analysis (Figure 9b) provides the quantitative foundation for this claim, and it is the most rigorous part of the paper's empirical contribution. The identical scaling exponent (α = 0.26) for both TriLMs and FloatLMs, combined with the asymptotic convergence of validation loss, provides a mathematically precise statement: the TriLM penalty is a constant multiplicative factor in parameter count that translates to a net bit-size advantage because the ternary compression factor (~5.9× per linear layer weight) exceeds the parameter multiplier needed to close the loss gap. However, there are two limitations:

1. **Limited scale range for fitting:** The scaling laws are fit to only 9 data points spanning 99M to 3.9B parameters. The projection to 15.6B and 330B parameters (Figure 10) involves extrapolation by 4× to 85× beyond the observed range. Scaling laws are known to sometimes break or change slope at larger scales (the "scaling law break" phenomenon observed in some model families), and without training actual models at 10B+ parameters, the asymptotic convergence claim remains a projection rather than an empirical fact.

2. **Fixed data regime:** The scaling law describes how validation loss varies with parameters given a fixed 300B token budget. It does not address how the TriLM-FloatLM gap evolves with varying data quantities. If TriLMs are less data-efficient than FloatLMs — requiring more tokens to reach the same loss for a given parameter count — then the parameter-count advantage in bit-size would be partially offset by higher training costs. The current experiments cannot answer this question because all models are trained on the same 300B tokens.

**On the claim that the gap between TriLM and FloatLM validation loss "becomes insignificant" at larger scales:** The paper projections (Figure 10) suggest that at 15.6B parameters, TriLM validation loss is within 7% of FloatLM validation loss, and at 330B parameters, within 6%. Whether a 6–7% gap is "insignificant" depends on the application. For downstream reasoning benchmarks, the 3.9B results suggest that a 6–7% validation loss gap translates to sub-percentage-point differences in accuracy, which is indeed negligible. But the perplexity gap on web corpora (Figure 13) is substantial at 3.9B (roughly 0.3–0.5 log units, corresponding to 35–65% higher perplexity), and the scaling projections do not directly address how this specific gap evolves with scale. It is possible that the web-text perplexity gap persists or even widens at larger scales if it reflects a fundamental memorization capacity limit rather than a precision limit that shrinks with scale.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

1. **Training a FloatLM at the bit-size of a TriLM.** The cleanest test of the "bit-size efficiency" claim would be to train a FloatLM at approximately 10.76 × 10^9 bits (the size of TriLM 3.9B). This would be a FloatLM with roughly 670M parameters (10.76 × 10^9 bits / 16 bits per parameter). The paper does not train a 670M FloatLM, but FloatLM 560M (9.11 × 10^9 bits) and FloatLM 830M (13.34 × 10^9 bits) bracket this point. Interpolation suggests such a model would achieve approximately 48–53% average commonsense reasoning — substantially below TriLM 3.9B's 60.7%. While the interpolation is straightforward, actually training and evaluating this model would provide a direct bit-size-matched comparison without relying on interpolation.

2. **Training TriLMs with varying data budgets.** The paper's scaling law is one-dimensional (varying parameters at fixed data). A two-dimensional scaling analysis (varying both parameters and data) would reveal whether TriLMs have different data efficiency than FloatLMs, which directly impacts the practical tradeoff between training cost and deployment efficiency.

3. **Measuring actual inference speedup, not just theoretical maximum.** The paper claims up to 10× theoretical speedup for TriLMs (Figure 2b) but does not implement optimized ternary inference kernels or measure actual throughput. The deployment efficiency argument rests entirely on memory footprint reduction and theoretical bandwidth-proportional speedup; without wall-clock measurements, the practical benefit remains speculative. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by not including latency benchmarks.

4. **Evaluation on generative tasks beyond multiple-choice.** All downstream benchmarks are multiple-choice (except LAMBADA, which is next-word prediction, and the qualitative examples in Appendix H). Performance on open-ended generation tasks (summarization, translation, dialogue) is unexplored. The perplexity gap on web corpora raises the possibility that TriLM-generated text, while coherent, might be detectably different in style or quality from FloatLM-generated text.

5. **Confidence intervals or statistical significance.** The paper reports point estimates for all benchmarks without confidence intervals. For a single training run per TriLM/FloatLM scale, the variance due to random seed is unquantified. For QuantLMs, averaging across 3 calibration seeds provides some robustness but standard deviations are not reported. The small differences at 3.9B (TriLM vs. FloatLM gaps of 0.5–1.5 percentage points) may or may not be statistically significant — without variance estimates, the reader cannot judge.

6. **Analysis of emergent capabilities.** At 3.9B parameters, TriLM catches up to FloatLM on most benchmarks. Is this a smooth asymptotic convergence, or does something qualitatively change at a specific scale threshold? The paper's 9 model scales provide reasonably fine granularity, but the analysis focuses on aggregate trends rather than identifying specific capabilities that emerge or remain absent in TriLMs at particular scales.

**On the fairness of the FloatLM baseline for the bit-size comparison.** The FloatLM uses 16-bit precision for all weights, while the TriLM uses 1.58 bits for linear layers and 16 bits for embeddings/LM head. This means the TriLM's effective bitwidth is not uniform — it's a mixed-precision model. The FloatLM could, in principle, also use mixed precision (e.g., 16-bit embeddings, 8-bit linear layers) to achieve a lower total bit-size while maintaining quality. The paper does not explore mixed-precision FloatLM configurations, which would be a natural and potentially strong baseline for the bit-size efficiency claim. QuantLMs partially fill this role (using GPTQ to reduce linear layer precision), but they are post-training quantized rather than trained at reduced precision, and the paper's own results show they underperform TriLMs at the same parameter count.

**On the generalizability beyond the Spectra training setup.** All TriLMs and FloatLMs are trained on 300B tokens of SlimPajama using exactly the same architecture (LLaMa-style), tokenizer (GPT-NeoX 20B), and training infrastructure (GPT-NeoX codebase on V100 GPUs with FP16 mixed precision). It is unknown whether the TriLM advantage would persist with different architectures (e.g., non-LLaMa designs, mixture of experts), different tokenizers, larger training budgets (trillions of tokens), or different hardware precision (BF16 training, which is standard for many LLM training runs and which the V100 GPUs used here do not support). The paper acknowledges this scope limitation implicitly — the Spectra suite is offered as a research infrastructure for the community to explore these questions.

**Bottom line on the experimental evidence.** The paper convincingly demonstrates that pretraining ternary language models is feasible at scale and that TriLMs offer substantial bit-size efficiency advantages over both FloatLMs and QuantLMs at parameter counts up to 3.9B on a diverse set of downstream benchmarks. The scaling law analysis provides a plausible asymptotic picture of convergence between TriLM and FloatLM performance, and the knowledge benchmark results are genuinely surprising in showing that ternary models preserve factual knowledge capacity that post-training quantization destroys. The primary limitations are the modest scale ceiling (3.9B parameters is small by contemporary standards), the lack of generative task evaluation beyond multiple-choice, the absence of actual inference speedup measurements, and the unexplored interaction between data quantity and precision in the scaling laws. These limitations do not undermine the paper's core contributions but define the boundaries of what has been demonstrated versus what is projected.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 Scaling Laws Are Extrapolated From a Narrow Parameter Range With a Fixed Data Budget

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's scaling law analysis in Section 4.3 fits the relationship between parameter count `$N$` and validation loss to a power-law-with-offset function using only 9 data points spanning 99M to 3.9B parameters, all trained on a fixed 300B-token budget. The projection that TriLMs will be within 6–7% of FloatLM validation loss at 15.6B to 330B parameters (Figure 10) extrapolates 4× to 85× beyond the observed range. The paper does not train any models beyond 3.9B parameters to validate this extrapolation, and the 300B-token data budget is held constant rather than varied — meaning the scaling law describes how loss varies with parameters *at a fixed data quantity*, not how the optimal parameter-data allocation (Chinchilla-style) differs between TriLMs and FloatLMs.

**The consequence.** Two distinct failure modes are possible at larger scales that the current data cannot rule out. First, scaling laws are known to sometimes exhibit "breaks" — changes in the power-law exponent `$\alpha$` or the irreducible loss `$\epsilon$` at specific scale thresholds — and the TriLM-FloatLM scaling relationship could diverge rather than converge if such a break occurs at, say, 7B or 10B parameters. Second, because the data budget is fixed, the analysis conflates the effects of parameter count and data insufficiency. At 3.9B parameters, 300B tokens may already be below the Chinchilla-optimal data quantity for FloatLMs, meaning the FloatLMs could be data-starved while TriLMs (being less expressive per parameter) might be closer to their compute-optimal allocation. Under a joint parameter-data scaling analysis, the TriLM penalty might be larger or smaller than the current estimate. A practitioner considering training a 70B TriLM cannot confidently use the paper's scaling projections to decide whether to invest in ternary vs. float pretraining at that scale.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The entire scaling law analysis is confined to Section 4.3 and Appendix C. Figure 9b shows the 9-point fit for each model family, and Figure 10 extrapolates the percentage validation loss difference to 330B parameters. The paper explicitly acknowledges the fixed data regime limitation in a footnote: "derived using a fixed data regime of 300B tokens" (Section 4.3), and the related work section notes that Chinchilla-optimal scaling of both data and parameters is "left to future work" (Section 8). The training dynamics in Figure 8a do show that TriLM loss curves at the largest scales (2.4B, 3.9B) are still declining meaningfully at 300B tokens, suggesting these models are not yet at convergence — which is inconsistent with the scaling law's assumption that final validation loss represents the asymptotic loss for that parameter count.

**Mitigation status.** Not mitigated. The paper acknowledges the fixed data regime explicitly but does not provide any sensitivity analysis (e.g., fitting the scaling law using only the first 200B or 250B tokens to check whether the exponent `$\alpha$` is stable across training duration) or train even a single model at a higher data budget to test extrapolation validity. Future work on "scaling laws across different data regimes for various low-bitwidth architectures" is suggested in Section 7.

### 6.2 Difficulty Estimation Cost for Compute-Optimal Allocation Is Not Accounted For

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal test-time scaling framework in this paper (Sections 3.2 and 4 of the underlying methodology) requires estimating each prompt's difficulty before allocating the inference budget. The primary difficulty estimation method involves generating **2048 samples per question** from the base model and computing either ground-truth pass@1 (oracle difficulty) or the average PRM final-answer score (predicted difficulty), then binning questions into five quintiles. The paper explicitly states: "our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity" (Section 3.2).

**The consequence.** The headline efficiency gains of up to 4× over best-of-N — for instance, matching best-of-N at 64 generations with only 16 generations using compute-optimal search (Figure 4) — are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the 2048-sample estimation cost. In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be `$C_{\text{estimation}} + C_{\text{strategy}}$`. For a single question, 2048 samples for estimation dwarfs any test-time budget studied (which tops out at 256–512 generations). Even amortizing difficulty estimation across many questions from the same distribution (e.g., assuming difficulty bins are stable for a given benchmark), the initial cost is substantial and must be recovered through efficiency savings over many queries. The paper's quoted 4× figure is therefore an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** — real deployments would see lower net gains, potentially negative if the question distribution shifts and difficulty must be re-estimated frequently. Furthermore, the cost of difficulty estimation could exceed the cost of simply running a larger test-time budget (e.g., best-of-256) for a single query, making the adaptive approach net-negative for low-volume inference.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 3.2 describes the difficulty estimation procedure and explicitly acknowledges the exploration-exploitation tradeoff: "compute spent assessing difficulty versus compute spent solving the problem." Figures 4 and 8 show that predicted difficulty bins (using PRM scores) track oracle bins closely, confirming that ground-truth labels are not needed — but the 2048-sample cost applies to both. The paper provides no measurement of how the difficulty estimation cost compares to the strategy execution cost, no amortization analysis, and no experiment testing whether fewer than 2048 samples suffice for reliable difficulty binning. The cross-validation protocol (two-fold within each difficulty bin on the 500-question test set) further masks the deployment cost, because difficulty is pre-computed on the entire test set before the cross-validation splits are created.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation explicitly and flags it as "a key avenue for future work" (Section 3.2), suggesting that "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" could eliminate the sampling cost. No such model is developed or evaluated. The limitation remains entirely unaddressed in the reported results.

### 6.3 All Results Are on a Single Benchmark (MATH) With a Single Model Family (PaLM 2-S*)

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire experimental analysis — the behavior of PRM search, revision models, compute-optimal scaling, and the FLOPs-matched pretraining comparison — is performed on the MATH benchmark (500 test questions) using PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The paper states that the model is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this claim is unverified within the paper itself.

**The consequence.** The paper's central finding — that test-time compute effectiveness is highly difficulty-dependent, with beam search hurting easy problems (due to verifier over-optimization) while helping medium problems, and with revisions dominating on easy problems while balanced parallel-sequential ratios dominating on hard ones — may be specific to the interaction between PaLM 2-S*'s output distribution and the MATH benchmark's problem structure. Several aspects of this finding could fail to generalize:

1. **Model-specific verifier behavior.** The PRM is trained via Monte Carlo rollouts from PaLM 2-S* (Section 5.1). A base model with different error patterns — e.g., one that makes more subtle errors requiring finer-grained verification, or one with better calibration — might produce a PRM with different over-optimization characteristics. The over-optimization threshold (the budget at which beam search starts degrading easy-problem performance, Figure 3 right) could shift substantially with a different base model.

2. **Domain-specific difficulty structure.** MATH consists of competition-level mathematics problems requiring multi-step symbolic reasoning. The difficulty bins are defined by the base model's pass@1 rate — a narrow, task-specific notion of difficulty. Problems requiring factual recall, common-sense reasoning, or code generation might exhibit entirely different difficulty-dependent scaling behavior. The paper's finding that hardest problems (bin 5) see near-zero improvement regardless of budget (Figure 3 right, Figure 7 right) may be a property of math problems where the base model's pass@1 is essentially zero, rather than a universal statement about hard problems.

3. **Revision model training depends on base model quality.** The revision model is fine-tuned on trajectories constructed by pairing incorrect and correct solutions from PaLM 2-S*, using edit distance to ensure structural similarity (Section 6.1). If the base model's incorrect solutions are qualitatively different — e.g., more diverse, less structured, or containing different error types — the revision model may learn a different (and possibly less effective) revision strategy.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no cross-model or cross-domain evaluation. All figures, tables, and scaling analyses reference MATH test accuracy or validation loss on MATH training data. The revision model training procedure acknowledges dependence on the base model's output distribution (Section 6.1) but does not test whether a revision model trained on one base model's outputs transfers to another. The FLOPs-matched comparison (Section 7) uses a single larger PaLM 2 model as the pretraining-scaled baseline, with no test of whether the tradeoff changes with different model families or architectures.

**Mitigation status.** The authors acknowledge the single-benchmark limitation implicitly by framing the results as specific to the MATH setting and PaLM 2 models (Section 4). No cross-domain or cross-model experiments are performed, and no claims of universality are made — but the paper also does not discuss how the findings might change under different conditions. The limitation is inherent to the scope of the study rather than a methodological flaw.

### 6.4 Verifier Over-Optimization Is a Hard Ceiling That the Compute-Optimal Policy Mitigates But Does Not Solve

**The assumption or constraint.** The PRM verifier is trained via Monte Carlo rollout supervision: for each step of each training solution, 16 rollouts are sampled and the fraction reaching the correct answer becomes the soft label (Section 5.1, Appendix D). This training procedure produces a verifier that is calibrated on solutions drawn from the base model's *natural* (i.i.d.) sampling distribution, but not on solutions found through aggressive search — which can be adversarial to the verifier. The compute-optimal policy addresses this by routing easy problems away from aggressive search (using best-of-N instead of beam search), but the underlying verifier remains vulnerable to over-optimization whenever search *is* deployed.

**The consequence.** On medium-difficulty problems (bins 3–4, where beam search is deployed per the compute-optimal policy), performance still plateaus and sometimes declines at high budgets — the beam search curves in Figure 3 (right) flatten well before the 256-generation budget is exhausted, and qualitative examples in Appendix M (Figure 29) show search producing degenerate outputs (repetitive low-information steps, overly short solutions) that score highly under the PRM but are incorrect. This means that **the compute-optimal approach cannot arbitrarily scale test-time compute on medium problems** — it hits a verifier-quality ceiling that is determined by the PRM's training procedure and calibration, not by the search algorithm. The paper's most powerful search method, lookahead search (which gives the PRM more context by simulating `$k$` additional steps forward), paradoxically performs *worst* overall (Figure 3 left), precisely because it is a stronger optimizer of the verifier signal and thus over-optimizes more aggressively.

The practical consequence is that a practitioner cannot simply increase the inference budget and expect continued improvement — the verifier ceiling imposes a maximum useful budget that varies by problem difficulty and base model quality. The paper provides no method for estimating this ceiling ahead of time or for improving verifier robustness.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 (right) directly shows beam search accuracy flattening or declining at high budgets on easy problems (verifier exploitation) and plateauing on medium problems. Figure 3 (left) shows lookahead search (the most powerful optimizer) underperforming simpler methods. Qualitative failure modes from verifier over-optimization are documented in Appendix M (Section 5.3). The compute-optimal policy's mitigation is visible in Figure 4, where compute-optimal scaling continues improving at budgets where individual methods stall — but even compute-optimal scaling shows signs of plateauing at the highest budgets (~256 generations), particularly with predicted difficulty bins.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies verifier over-optimization explicitly as a central limiting factor (Section 5.3: "degradation at high budgets is attributed to over-optimization of the PRM") and the compute-optimal policy is, in part, designed to mitigate it by routing easy problems to weaker optimization methods. However, the paper does not propose or evaluate any method for improving verifier robustness — such as adversarial training of the PRM on search-generated solutions, ensemble verification, or KL-penalized search that prevents the proposal distribution from deviating too far from the base model's output distribution. Future work on "improving verifier robustness is the key bottleneck for further scaling test-time compute" is suggested in Section 8 but not addressed experimentally.

### 6.5 The FLOPs-Matched Pretraining Baseline Is Weakened by Non-Compute-Optimal Training and No Test-Time Compute

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 compares PaLM 2-S* with compute-optimal test-time strategies against a model with approximately 14× more parameters. However, the larger model's training is not compute-optimal: following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023), the paper scales parameters while holding training data fixed, rather than scaling both data and parameters equally as prescribed by Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022). The paper acknowledges this explicitly: "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work" (Section 7). Additionally, the larger model is evaluated with **greedy decoding only** — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search, and no revision mechanism.

**The consequence.** The comparison is tilted in favor of test-time compute in two distinct ways:

1. **Non-Chinchilla-optimal pretraining weakens the larger model.** Under Chinchilla-optimal scaling, an equal percentage increase in total FLOPs is allocated to both model size and data quantity. By scaling only parameters while holding data constant, the 14× larger model likely receives fewer training tokens than is optimal for its parameter count, making it perform worse than a properly compute-optimal model of equivalent total FLOPs would. The paper's reported advantages — e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on easy questions at `$R \ll 1$` using revisions (Figure 1, top-right bar chart) — would likely shrink against a stronger pretraining baseline.

2. **No test-time compute for the larger model.** The comparison is between "small model + optimized test-time compute" and "large model + greedy decoding." A fairer comparison would give the larger model at least a modest test-time compute budget (e.g., best-of-8 or best-of-16) to account for the fact that any deployed model can benefit from simple test-time strategies. Since best-of-N provides substantial gains at low budgets (Figure 3 left), even a small test-time allocation would close some of the gap the paper reports. The question "is it better to invest in pretraining or in test-time compute?" is more accurately phrased as "what is the optimal split of a total budget between pretraining and per-query test-time compute for the resulting model?" — and the current experiments answer only the extreme version where test-time compute is allocated entirely to the small model.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 7 describes the FLOP accounting and acknowledges the LLaMA-style scaling departure from Chinchilla optimality. Figure 9 and the bar charts in Figure 1 present the results. The paper does not include any experiment where the larger model receives test-time compute, nor does it estimate how the FLOPs-matched comparison would change if the larger model were Chinchilla-optimally trained.

**Mitigation status.** The limitation is acknowledged explicitly (the quote about "canonical approach" above), but the paper treats this as a scope choice rather than a weakness. Future work on "compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally" is mentioned. No sensitivity analysis is provided — e.g., estimating what fraction of the reported advantage would disappear if the larger model used best-of-8, or if a Chinchilla-optimal larger model were substituted.

### 6.6 The Revision Model Suffers From a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate and Is Sensitive to Training Methodology

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained on sequences consisting of 0–4 incorrect answers followed by a correct answer, where the last incorrect answer is selected to have minimum character-level edit distance to the correct answer (Section 6.1). This construction teaches the model to transform incorrect solutions into correct ones, but it never exposes the model to correct answers in the context — the model is trained to always produce a correct answer given incorrect context, with no signal for what to do when the current answer is already correct. The paper reports that "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones" when the revision chain encounters a correct answer (Section 6.1).

**The consequence.** During inference with long revision chains (the paper tests up to 64 sequential revisions), the model can "undo" its own correct work — a correct revision at step `$t$` may be followed by an incorrect revision at step `$t+1$` because the model has never learned to recognize and preserve correctness. The mitigation — using majority voting or verifier-based selection across the entire chain rather than taking the final revision — adds computational overhead and does not prevent the wasted computation of generating revision steps that degrade quality. Moreover, the 38% reversion rate means that even with a perfect answer selector, some fraction of the chain is spent unproductively. For a chain of length `$N$`, the expected number of correct answers in the chain is reduced by the reversion process, potentially requiring longer chains than would be necessary if the model could reliably preserve correctness.

The broader issue is that the revision model's training data construction is fragile. The ReST$^{EM}$ experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) shows that attempting to optimize the revision model with RL-style training causes performance to *degrade substantially* under sequential revisions — the ReST$^{EM}$ revision model performs *worse* with more sequential steps, dropping to approximately 33.5% accuracy at 256 generations vs. 38.5% at the optimal ratio. The authors hypothesize that "the on-policy data collection in ReST$^{EM}$ exacerbates spurious correlations in revision data," suggesting that the revision approach is highly sensitive to the data generation procedure.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% reversion rate is stated in Section 6.1. Figure 6 (left) shows the per-step pass@1 trajectory — while overall pass@1 improves (from ~18% to ~24% by step 15–20), the curve is noisy and does not monotonically increase, consistent with occasional steps undoing prior progress. The ReST$^{EM}$ ablation (Figure 16) demonstrates sensitivity to training methodology. The edit-distance-based pairing and offline data construction are described in Section 6.1 as deliberate design choices to improve revision training stability.

**Mitigation status.** The paper mitigates the reversion problem at inference time via within-chain selection (majority voting or verifier-based selection across the full revision chain rather than taking the final output), but this is a post-hoc patch rather than a solution — it does not address the root cause (the model never learning to preserve correctness) and adds selection overhead. The paper does not propose or evaluate architectural changes to the revision training that would teach the model when to stop revising (e.g., including correct answers in the training context with a "no revision needed" target, or training a separate halting classifier). The sensitivity to training methodology is documented but not resolved — the paper's positive revision results depend on the specific offline data construction and edit-distance pairing described in Section 6.1, and it is unclear whether these choices would transfer to other domains or base models without substantial tuning.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper forces a reorientation in how the field evaluates and compares low-precision language models. The conventional wisdom — dominant since the original scaling laws papers (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) and embedded in every model release from GPT-3 through LLaMA 3 — is that models are compared by **parameter count**. A 7B model is a 7B model, regardless of whether its weights are FP16, BF16, 4-bit, or 1.58-bit. The Spectra paper demonstrates that this convention systematically obscures the true deployment efficiency landscape, and that the correct metric for memory-constrained inference is **model bit-size** — the total number of bits required to store the model weights at deployment precision.

This reframing is not merely a notational preference. When plotted against bit-size rather than parameter count, the TriLM results shift from "competitive but slightly behind at the same parameter count" to **"dramatically more efficient at the same memory budget."** TriLM 3.9B occupies 10.76 × 10^9 bits and achieves 60.7% average commonsense reasoning, matching FloatLM 3.9B (63.83 × 10^9 bits, 61.4%) and dramatically exceeding FloatLM 830M (13.34 × 10^9 bits, 53.3%) — its closest bit-size match among float models. This is a 5.9× compression for equivalent downstream performance. The paper does not announce this reframing as its primary contribution — it is listed as a finding rather than a methodological intervention — but it is the intellectual move that makes the TriLM results legible as a breakthrough and that should reshape how model releases report efficiency metrics going forward.

Beyond the metric reframing, the paper establishes three empirical findings that collectively change the conversation around low-precision LLMs:

**1. Quantization-aware pretraining and post-training quantization are qualitatively different approaches with qualitatively different failure modes.** The Spectra suite's systematic comparison reveals that QuantLMs (GPTQ at 3–4 bits) suffer a catastrophic collapse in knowledge capacity — at 3.9B parameters, TriviaQA exact match drops from FloatLM's 21.5% to QuantLM 4-bit's 17.9% to QuantLM 3-bit's 8.2% (Table 12), confirming the knowledge capacity degradation identified by Allen-Zhu and Li (2024). TriLMs, by contrast, preserve factual knowledge: TriLM 3.9B achieves 21.3% on TriviaQA and matches or exceeds FloatLM on SciQ and MMLU. This is not a small quantitative difference — it is a **qualitative distinction** between two families of low-precision models. PTQ takes a model that learned to store knowledge in continuous weight magnitudes and forcibly discretizes those magnitudes, destroying fine-grained distinctions that matter for factual recall. TriLMs learn from the start to store knowledge in the only degrees of freedom available — the sign and sparsity pattern of ternary weights — and the training process adapts the representation to the constraint. This finding should redirect research attention from PTQ at very low bitwidths (which the Spectra results suggest has a hard precision floor around 4 bits) toward native low-precision training as the more promising path for extreme compression.

**2. The scaling exponent for validation loss is invariant to precision choice.** The fitted power-law exponents for TriLMs and FloatLMs are identical (`α = 0.26`, Section 4.3, Equation 1). This means the rate at which additional parameters reduce loss is the same regardless of whether each parameter carries 1.58 bits or 16 bits of information. The precision penalty manifests as a constant multiplicative factor (`A_TriLM = 185` vs. `A_FloatLM = 159`) and a slightly higher irreducible loss offset (`ε_TriLM = 1.76` vs. `ε_FloatLM = 1.67`), but these are fixed costs that become proportionally smaller as scale increases. This is the first empirical evidence that the scaling exponent may be a property of the learning problem rather than the parameterization — analogous to Hoffmann et al. (2022)'s finding that the scaling exponent for model size vs. data is invariant to architecture. If this result generalizes to other low-bitwidth representations (binary, 2-bit, mixed-precision), it would constitute a **scaling law for precision** that could guide resource allocation as concretely as the Chinchilla laws guide pretraining.

**3. Ternary models preserve knowledge capacity that post-training quantization destroys, but at the cost of reduced memorization of web text distributions.** The perplexity analysis (Figure 13) reveals a nuanced capability profile: TriLM 3.9B matches or beats FloatLM 3.9B on curated text (Penn Tree Bank, LAMBADA) but consistently underperforms on web-derived corpora (C4, Dolma, RefinedWeb), including the in-domain SlimPajama test set. This is a diagnostically rich finding — it suggests that ternary models learn the generalizable patterns needed for downstream reasoning and knowledge benchmarks (which are curated) while having reduced capacity for the statistical memorization of noisy, redundant web text. Whether this reduced memorization is a feature (less verbatim training data leakage, potentially better generalization) or a bug (worse in-domain modeling, potential quality degradation on web-text-like generation tasks) depends on the application, but the finding provides a concrete diagnostic for where TriLMs will and will not be competitive.

The paper also reconciles a latent tension in the low-precision training literature. BitNet b1.58 (Ma et al., 2024) demonstrated the feasibility of ternary LLMs but did not compare against post-training quantized baselines, leaving unclear whether pretraining at low bitwidth offered advantages over simply quantizing a pretrained float model. The Spectra suite's QuantLM baselines close this gap definitively: at 3.9B parameters, TriLM (60.7% commonsense reasoning, 10.76 × 10^9 bits) substantially outperforms QuantLM 3-bit (56.8%, 16.91 × 10^9 bits) while being 1.6× smaller, and matches QuantLM 4-bit (60.7%, 20.59 × 10^9 bits) while being 1.9× smaller. The answer to "should we quantize post-hoc or train at low precision from scratch?" is now empirically grounded: for extreme compression (below 4 effective bits), **pretrain at low bitwidth** — the representation-adapted approach avoids the knowledge capacity collapse that plagues representation-compressed approaches.

Research directions that become more attractive: native low-precision pretraining at scales beyond 3.9B parameters, scaling law analysis jointly varying parameters and data for low-bitwidth models, and investigation of ternary-specific inductive biases (e.g., reduced memorization as a regularizer). Research directions that become less attractive: pushing post-training quantization below 4 bits without architectural changes to mitigate knowledge collapse — the Spectra results suggest this is a fundamentally limited approach for knowledge-intensive applications.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Scaling TriLMs to 10B+ parameters to validate the asymptotic convergence hypothesis.** The paper's scaling law projection (Figure 10) predicts that TriLMs will be within 6–7% of FloatLM validation loss at 15.6B to 330B parameters, but this extrapolates 4× to 85× beyond the observed 3.9B ceiling. A direct test would train TriLM and FloatLM at 7B, 13B, or 30B parameters on a fixed data budget and measure whether the validation loss gap continues to shrink as projected, and — critically — whether the downstream benchmark parity observed at 3.9B parameters holds at these larger scales. The 3.9B results show that TriLM matches FloatLM on most benchmarks but with a persistent gap on HellaSWAG and web-text perplexity; determining whether these specific gaps close or persist at 10B+ parameters would reveal whether they represent a fundamental precision limit or a scale-dependent phenomenon. This experiment is newly tractable because the Spectra suite provides the exact training recipe, optimization schedule, and architecture needed to reproduce and extend the TriLM approach.

**Joint parameter-data scaling laws for ternary vs. float models.** The current scaling analysis (Section 4.3) varies parameters while holding data fixed at 300B tokens, preventing analysis of data efficiency. A two-dimensional scaling study would train TriLMs and FloatLMs across multiple parameter counts (e.g., 500M, 1B, 3B, 7B) and multiple data budgets (e.g., 50B, 100B, 300B, 1T tokens), fitting a Chinchilla-style loss function `L(N, D) = A/N^α + B/D^β + ε`. This would answer: (1) do TriLMs require more training data than FloatLMs to reach the same loss for a given parameter count? (2) does the Chinchilla-optimal parameter-data allocation ratio differ between ternary and float models? and (3) at fixed total training FLOPs (which accounts for both the per-step cost and the number of steps), is it ever optimal to train a ternary model rather than a float model, or does the potential training-time overhead (from the STE gradient approximation and the need for more parameters) outweigh the deployment memory savings? The Spectra suite's infrastructure (identical architectures, tokenizer, data pipeline) makes this extension straightforward.

**Measuring actual inference throughput with optimized ternary kernels.** The paper claims up to 10× theoretical speedup for autoregressive decoding (Figure 2b) based on memory bandwidth analysis, but provides no wall-clock measurements because optimized ternary inference kernels are not yet implemented. A critical follow-up would implement and benchmark a custom CUDA/Triton kernel that exploits ternary weight structure — accumulating activations with sign-dependent addition/subtraction, skipping zero-weight connections, and using the per-matrix scale as a post-accumulation multiplication — and measure actual tokens-per-second throughput on standard hardware (A100, H100) compared to FP16 and INT4 kernels at batch sizes ranging from 1 (latency-critical) to 32 (throughput-optimized). This would convert the paper's theoretical efficiency argument into a practical deployment metric and reveal whether the 10× theoretical maximum is approachable in practice or whether overheads (e.g., irregular memory access patterns from zero-weight sparsity, kernel launch overhead for small matrices) significantly reduce the realized speedup. The Spectra suite's 3.9B TriLM checkpoint provides a concrete model to benchmark.

**Testing whether ternary models exhibit reduced training data memorization, and whether this improves or harms generalization.** The perplexity gap in Figure 13 — TriLM 3.9B matches FloatLM on curated text but underperforms on web corpora — suggests that ternary models may have reduced capacity for memorizing training distribution statistics. A controlled memorization study would measure verbatim training data regurgitation rates (following Carlini et al., 2023's methodology: test whether the model can complete infrequent training sequences when given a prefix) for TriLM vs. FloatLM at matched parameter counts, and correlate memorization rates with downstream performance on tasks that benefit from memorization (closed-book QA on rare facts, code completion of specific API calls) versus tasks that benefit from generalization (synthetic reasoning problems with held-out patterns). If TriLMs genuinely memorize less, this could be either a deployment benefit (reduced risk of training data leakage, lower susceptibility to membership inference attacks) or a limitation (worse performance on long-tail knowledge tasks), and the result would guide whether ternary pretraining is appropriate for privacy-sensitive or knowledge-intensive applications.

**Combining ternary pretraining with state-space architectures for compound efficiency gains.** The paper mentions (Section 7) that "combining these models with state-space architectures like Mamba could further enhance efficiency and performance without sacrificing accuracy." A concrete experiment would replace the attention mechanism in the TriLM architecture with a Mamba-2 selective state-space layer (Dao and Gu, 2024), which also benefits from efficient hardware implementations. The hypothesis is that ternary weights reduce the memory footprint of the linear projections in the SSM, while the SSM's recurrent computation (which already has lower memory bandwidth requirements than attention) sidesteps the KV-cache bottleneck entirely. Training a TriMamba model at 1-3B parameters on the Spectra data pipeline, and evaluating both perplexity and throughput against TriLM and FloatLM baselines, would test whether the efficiency gains from ternarization and state-space models are additive or overlapping. This is newly tractable because the Spectra suite provides the TriLM training recipe and evaluation framework against which the hybrid architecture can be compared.

**Adversarial evaluation of the knowledge capacity claim.** The paper's finding that TriLMs preserve factual knowledge better than QuantLMs (TriviaQA: TriLM 3.9B 21.3% vs. QuantLM 3-bit 8.2%) is based on standard zero-shot evaluation. A stress-test would construct a synthetic knowledge benchmark where facts are injected into the training data in a controlled way (e.g., a set of artificially constructed "rare facts" about fictional entities, with varying frequency in the training corpus) and measure how many of these facts are retained by TriLM vs. FloatLM vs. QuantLM at matched bit-sizes. This would disentangle whether the TriLM advantage on TriviaQA reflects genuinely preserved factual recall or simply better generalization on a multiple-choice task that can be solved via partial knowledge and reasoning. If TriLMs match FloatLMs on rare-fact recall at the same parameter count, this would strengthen the claim that ternary weights can encode factual associations as effectively as float weights. If they underperform on rare facts but match on frequent facts, this would refine the knowledge capacity claim to "TriLMs preserve frequent factual knowledge but lose long-tail knowledge" — an important boundary condition.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-device LLM deployment for consumer hardware.** A TriLM at 3.9B parameters occupies approximately 1.3 GB of memory (10.76 × 10^9 bits = ~1.34 GB) for the linear layer weights, plus embedding and LM head overhead. This fits comfortably within the RAM budget of a modern smartphone (8–16 GB) or laptop, whereas a FloatLM of equivalent reasoning capability (3.9B parameters, ~8 GB FP16) would be marginal or infeasible on the same hardware. The downstream benchmark parity — 60.7% average commonsense reasoning, 61.6% LAMBADA accuracy, 32.8% MMLU — puts TriLM 3.9B in a performance tier that is useful for real applications (chat, summarization, coding assistance, question answering) at a memory footprint that enables on-device inference with acceptable latency, particularly if the theoretical 4–10× bandwidth-proportional speedup is realized in practice. For deployment scenarios requiring air-gapped or privacy-preserving inference (medical, legal, personal assistant applications), where data cannot leave the device, TriLMs offer a path to capable local models without relying on cloud offloading.

**Cost-efficient batch inference at scale.** For organizations running large-scale batch inference — evaluating millions of documents, generating training data, or powering retrieval-augmented pipelines — the memory footprint of deployed models directly determines the number of model replicas that can fit on available GPU memory, and thus the throughput of the batch inference system. A single H100 (80 GB) can hold approximately 5–6 replicas of TriLM 3.9B (~1.3 GB each plus KV cache and activation overhead) versus 1–2 replicas of FloatLM 3.9B (~8 GB). At 4× higher replica density, the TriLM deployment can process approximately 4× more queries per unit time (assuming bandwidth-bound inference where per-replica throughput is similar). For a batch processing job that would take 24 hours with FloatLM replicas, the TriLM deployment could complete in ~6 hours on the same hardware, or use 4× fewer GPUs for the same latency target. The downstream benchmark parity at 3.9B parameters (TriLM matches FloatLM within 1–2 percentage points on most tasks) means this throughput gain comes with negligible quality degradation for most applications.

**Scaling LLMs beyond GPU memory capacity without model parallelism.** The most striking deployment implication of Figure 2a is that TriLMs can, in principle, fit over 300B parameters on a single H100 (80 GB) with appropriate weight packing, while FloatLM exceeds a single GPU's capacity at just 34B parameters. Even accounting for embedding overhead and KV cache, a 70B TriLM (~9 GB of ternary weights + embedding overhead) would fit on a single consumer GPU (24 GB RTX 4090) or a single datacenter GPU (80 GB H100), whereas a 70B FloatLM requires multi-GPU tensor parallelism or pipeline parallelism — with associated communication overhead, engineering complexity, and cost. This capability is not yet realized (the largest TriLM trained is 3.9B), but the scaling laws and the 500+ intermediate checkpoints released with the Spectra suite provide the foundation for the community to train and deploy much larger ternary models. For research labs and startups with limited GPU budgets, the ability to experiment with 30B–70B-parameter models on a single GPU — rather than requiring an 8-GPU node — could dramatically lower the barrier to entry for large-model research.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not explicitly frame a decision rule or conditional guidance for practitioners choosing between TriLMs, FloatLMs, and QuantLMs. The following is therefore a synthesis of the paper's results into a practical decision rule, grounded in the empirical benchmarks and scaling analyses presented.

**Prefer TriLMs when:**
- Deployment memory is the binding constraint, and a FloatLM of equivalent capability would not fit on the target hardware. The bit-size efficiency advantage (~5.9× compression for linear layers) means a TriLM can achieve benchmark scores matching a FloatLM 3.9B while occupying fewer bits than a FloatLM 830M (Figure 1a, Table 4).
- The application requires factual knowledge recall (QA over documents, knowledge-grounded dialogue, educational applications). TriLMs preserve knowledge capacity that post-training quantized models at 3–4 bits catastrophically lose (TriviaQA: TriLM 3.9B 21.3% vs. QuantLM 3-bit 8.2%, Table 12).
- The problem distribution features structured, curated text rather than noisy web text. TriLMs match FloatLMs on Penn Tree Bank and LAMBADA perplexity but lag on web corpora (Figure 13). Applications involving clean-domain text (scientific QA, code generation, structured reasoning) are in TriLM's strength zone.
- The deployment scenario requires high throughput at fixed hardware (batch inference, API serving). Higher replica density from reduced memory footprint translates to proportionally higher throughput under bandwidth-bound inference.
- Parameter count can be scaled beyond 2–3B parameters. The TriLM-FloatLM performance gap is large at small scales (e.g., 1.1B: 51.6% vs. 54.9% commonsense reasoning) but shrinks to near-parity at 3.9B (60.7% vs. 61.4%). TriLMs are most compelling at scales where the constant precision penalty is proportionally small.

**Prefer FloatLMs when:**
- The model must faithfully reproduce web text distributions (data filtering, content generation matching web style, training data quality assessment). The perplexity gap on web corpora (Figure 13) persists at 3.9B and may not close at larger scales — the paper provides no evidence that TriLMs can match FloatLMs on high-entropy web text modeling.
- The parameter budget is small (<1B parameters). At 99M–830M parameters, FloatLMs consistently and sometimes substantially outperform TriLMs at the same parameter count (e.g., FloatLM 560M: 50.5% vs. TriLM 560M: 48.4% average commonsense reasoning), and the bit-size advantage is not yet large enough to outweigh the precision penalty for most deployment scenarios.
- The application requires HellaSWAG-level fine-grained commonsense reasoning specifically. This is the benchmark where the TriLM-FloatLM gap is largest and most persistent across scales (TriLM 3.9B: 48.3% vs. FloatLM 3.9B: 49.7% unnormalized accuracy).
- Training stability or infrastructure constraints make the TriLM optimization schedule difficult to implement. The two-phase schedule (peak LR reduction, weight decay removal) and the need for straight-through estimation during training add complexity relative to standard FloatLM training, and the V100 GPU constraint (no BF16 support) means the current recipe cannot be directly applied on newer hardware without adaptation.

**Prefer QuantLMs at 4–8 bits when:**
- The deployment constraint is moderate (2–4× compression relative to FP16 is sufficient), and the application does not involve knowledge-intensive tasks. QuantLM 4-bit at 3.9B matches TriLM 3.9B on average commonsense reasoning (60.7% vs. 60.7%, Table 7) while being simpler to produce (no retraining, just GPTQ on a standard FloatLM checkpoint). For applications where 4-bit compression is adequate and knowledge capacity is not critical, the engineering simplicity of PTQ outweighs the TriLM advantage.
- The FloatLM checkpoint already exists, and retraining from scratch is not feasible. PTQ is a post-hoc compression step; TriLM requires pretraining the model from scratch with a specialized optimization schedule.

**Prefer QuantLMs at ≤3 bits only when:**
- The compression ratio is absolutely necessary (e.g., deploying on extremely memory-constrained hardware where even 4× compression is insufficient), and the application can tolerate the knowledge capacity collapse documented in the paper. TriLM is the better approach for extreme compression in most scenarios because it preserves knowledge capacity while achieving higher compression ratios than 3-bit QuantLMs at the same parameter count.
