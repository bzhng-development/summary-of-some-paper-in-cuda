Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (a Staff Engineer sharing how owning “boring” infrastructure work earned trust and unlocked Staff-level scope):

**Volunteering for the work nobody wants**

In his first year at Instagram, Ryan volunteered to help with his team’s holiday preparations—the unglamorous, high-stakes work of ensuring no reliability issues during peak holiday usage. “As you can imagine, no one was eager to take holiday oncall shifts so it was easy for me to get involved.” The following year, confident he could drive the entire process for his team, he volunteered again and took full ownership. Because the work was unattractive to most, it was effectively uncontested. As he puts it: “this work was unattractive but impactful, it was mine for the taking.” He frames this as a broader principle: **“boring” work is often an opportunity**—where there is impact, there is opportunity for career growth.

Under his leadership, the team had its quietest holiday season yet in terms of SEVs. Ryan is careful to credit his teammates—many contributed to the effort—but he drove and owned the overall preparations. Afterward, he made a point of sharing both the process and the strong results with his broader org. That deliberate communication of success would prove pivotal.

**How visible results create their own opportunities**

At the same time, other areas outside Ryan’s team—across an org of 70+ engineers—suffered poor reliability during the holidays. A year later, his director was looking for someone to prevent a repeat across the entire org. “Guess who he reached out to? Because I had solved a similar problem and had a good track record, I was an obvious choice to ask.” The opportunity arrived not because Ryan had lobbied for it, but because his director already knew about his results. This is his core principle here: **visible results build trust and credibility**. He frames it as increasing your luck—doing good work and making sure people know about it so opportunities start coming to you.

**Scaling through coordination, not domain expertise**

His director trusted him with Staff-level scope before he was at that level—a clean example of how such opportunities can happen naturally. Ryan was handed an org-wide infrastructure preparation initiative despite not being a deep expert in every team’s domain. “I wasn’t an expert in these new areas but I had been around long enough to know who was.” He created a proposal based on his own experience, then ran it by a few domain experts to stress-test it and ensure the plan was sound. Once there was alignment on the high-level plan, he drove a weekly meeting with the relevant people and made sure everything got executed.

The work paid off: no issues during the holiday season, this time for the whole org. The experience cemented a lesson he now champions: **you don’t need to know everything**. If you can build alignment and coordinate work, you can lean on others to fill the gaps—the core skills of a “tech lead,” which “scale well.” This infrastructure-leadership role was just one small part of his eventual Staff promotion, but it’s a crisp demonstration of how unglamorous, high-impact work—paired with visible results and deliberate coordination—can earn trust and unlock scope before you technically “deserve” it on paper.

Ryan’s **“boring” work is often an opportunity** stance and his **visible results build trust and credibility** principle form a tight flywheel: taking on unattractive-but-impactful work creates a track record, and deliberately sharing that track record attracts bigger opportunities. The whole arc also embodies his **you don’t need to know everything** playbook—leaning on domain experts and driving alignment let him scale far beyond his own expertise. Together, they show that earning trust is less about asking for scope and more about making your results impossible to ignore.
