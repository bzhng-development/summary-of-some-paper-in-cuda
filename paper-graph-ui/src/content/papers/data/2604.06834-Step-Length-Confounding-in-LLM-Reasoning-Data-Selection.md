# Step Length Confounding in LLM Reasoning Data Selection

**ArXiv:** [2604.06834](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06834)

## 🎯 Pitch

When LLMs are used to score reasoning data for fine-tuning, they don't pick better solutions—they systematically favor longer steps, simply because low-probability first tokens get diluted in extended chains. The authors trace this bias to step-initial entropy and fix it by either dropping those first tokens entirely or regressing out their effect, boosting downstream accuracy substantially.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper analyzes a systematic bias in naturalness-based data selection for long chain-of-thought reasoning datasets, revealing that existing methods — which rank samples by the average log probability assigned by an LLM — preferentially select responses with longer reasoning steps rather than higher-quality ones, a phenomenon the authors term **step length confounding**. Through experiments on the LIMO-v2 and AceReason-1.1-SFT benchmarks across four LLMs (QwQ-32B, Qwen3-32B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, gpt-oss-120b) and four target models of varying sizes, the paper traces this bias to low-probability first tokens in each reasoning step, whose influence is diluted in longer steps — inflating their average log probabilities and making them more likely to be selected. To address this, the authors propose two variant methods, ASLEC-DROP (dropping first-token probabilities when computing the selection score) and ASLEC-CASL (applying causal debiasing regression to remove the first-token ratio's confounding effect), which outperform the state-of-the-art Local LP method by average accuracy gains of approximately 6.28% and 9.08%, respectively, across all model sizes and datasets. The paper establishes that naturalness-based selection is fundamentally confounded by step length — and that this confounding can be effectively mitigated — only when the disproportionate influence of low-probability step-initial tokens is explicitly addressed in the scoring computation.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Naturalness-Based Selection Has a Hidden Bias

The fundamental question this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **when we try to select the "best" reasoning data for supervised fine-tuning by measuring how natural it seems to an LLM, are we actually measuring what we think we're measuring?** The answer, the paper argues, is no — and the specific thing being measured instead has important consequences for how we train reasoning models.

The problem arises in a now-standard pipeline for building large reasoning models. The recipe, as described in Section 1, goes like this: collect complex math and science problems, prompt strong LLMs (like QwQ-32B or DeepSeek-R1) to generate long chain-of-thought solutions, and then fine-tune a smaller model on these generated reasoning trajectories. This supervised fine-tuning (SFT) approach has proven remarkably effective — it's the backbone of how models like DeepSeek-R1's distilled variants are created, and it can elicit sophisticated reasoning behavior even in relatively small models.

But here's the catch: the generated data is noisy. Strong LLMs still produce incorrect reasoning steps, overly verbose trajectories, and solutions of varying quality (Zheng et al., 2025; Sui et al., 2025). Not all generated solutions are equally useful for training. This motivates a critical downstream decision: **which subset of the generated data should we actually train on?**

### The Appeal of Naturalness-Based Selection

One intuitive answer has gained significant traction: select the data that the target model finds most "natural." The reasoning is straightforward — if the model assigns high probability to a reasoning trajectory, that trajectory presumably aligns well with what the model already knows or can easily learn, making it a better training example.

This naturalness-based approach has been formalized in several ways (Section 2.1):

- **GRACE** (Zhang et al., 2025) computes the geometric mean of token-level log probabilities across the entire response — the standard `s_logp` score. Higher means the model is more "comfortable" with the data.

- **Local LP** (Just et al., 2025), the current state-of-the-art, refines this by splitting responses into reasoning steps (delineated by newlines or sentence boundaries) and computing log probabilities locally within each step, conditioned on the question and a window of previous steps. This captures step-level coherence rather than just global fluency.

- **Min Entropy** (Cui et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025) selects data where the model's predictive distribution has the lowest entropy — i.e., where the model is most confident in its next-token predictions.

- **Min Perplexity** (Muennighoff et al., 2023) is mathematically equivalent to selecting for high `s_logp`, just expressed as perplexity rather than log probability.

These methods all share a common premise: that **higher model confidence = higher data quality = better training outcomes**. It's an appealingly simple principle that requires no manual heuristics, no answer verification, and no external judges — just the model's own internal probability estimates.

### Why This Matters: The Stakes of Data Selection

The importance of getting data selection right cannot be overstated, and the paper's framing makes clear why this is more than an academic curiosity.

**The s1 demonstration.** Recent work (Muennighoff et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025) has shown that carefully selecting just 1,000 high-quality reasoning examples can elicit long chain-of-thought capabilities comparable to training on much larger datasets. The implication is profound: **data quality dominates data quantity** for reasoning SFT. If you can identify the right 1,000 examples, you might not need 100,000.

This puts enormous pressure on the selection criterion. If the selection method is systematically biased — preferring, say, verbosity over genuine reasoning quality — then the "best" subset it identifies may actually contain suboptimal training data. The model would then be trained on a skewed distribution, potentially learning to produce long-winded but shallow reasoning rather than concise, logically sound chains of thought.

**Resource-constrained settings.** The paper's experiments (Section 4) span models from 4B to 8B parameters — exactly the regime where data efficiency matters most. A small model trained on 4,000 well-chosen examples can outperform one trained on a much larger but noisier dataset. But this only works if the selection criterion actually identifies the well-chosen examples.

**The self-improvement pipeline.** Looking ahead, the paper connects to a broader vision (Section 5) where models generate their own training data and select the best examples for further fine-tuning — a self-improvement loop. If the selection criterion is biased, the loop could amplify that bias over successive iterations, drifting toward longer and longer reasoning chains without corresponding improvements in reasoning quality.

### The Gap: What Prior Work Missed

The paper's central contribution is identifying a specific, previously undocumented failure mode of naturalness-based selection when applied to long chain-of-thought reasoning data. Prior work on naturalness-based data selection (Zhang et al., 2025; Just et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2025) evaluated these methods primarily on general instruction-tuning or short-form reasoning tasks. The distinctive structure of long CoT reasoning — where responses are segmented into discrete reasoning steps, each beginning with a "branching" decision point — was not explicitly considered.

The paper identifies two interconnected phenomena that prior work overlooked:

**Phenomenon 1: Step length as a confounder.** Section 2.3 presents a striking result (Figure 1): across four different naturalness-based selection methods (GRACE, Local LP, Min Entropy, Min Perplexity), the step-length distribution of selected data differs markedly from unselected data. Selected responses consistently have longer reasoning steps, with an average step length around 30 tokens versus a more concentrated distribution at lower values for unselected data. The methods are not selecting for reasoning quality — they are selecting for step verbosity.

**Phenomenon 2: The low-probability first token.** Section 2.4 traces why this happens. Figure 2 shows a clear monotonic relationship: longer reasoning steps receive higher average log probabilities from the target model (Qwen3-4B-Base, in the paper's analysis). Figure 3 illustrates the mechanism with concrete examples. A short step like "But again, the problem is asking for a value of x..." has a first token with very low probability (−5.48) because many tokens could follow "Wait," "Alternatively," "However" — the model is uncertain about which reasoning branch to take. This low first-token probability drags down the step's average. A long step like "Wait, in triangle ABC, the law of cosines says: cos θ = (AB² + BC² – AC²)/..." also starts with a low-probability first token (−6.68), but that low value is diluted across 67 subsequent tokens that are all relatively high-probability continuations of an established reasoning thread.

The paper formalizes this in **Conclusion 2**: > "step length confounding occurs because the low-probability first token constitutes a smaller ratio of longer responses. This increases the average log probabilities, making samples with longer steps more likely to be selected."

This insight builds on recent work showing that minority first tokens at reasoning step boundaries exhibit high entropy because they fork into diverse reasoning pathways (Wang et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025). But while prior work documented the existence of high-entropy first tokens, it did not connect this phenomenon to a systematic bias in data selection. The paper's contribution is showing that this architectural artifact of autoregressive generation — uncertainty at decision points — creates a **confounding variable** that corrupts the very metric meant to measure data quality.

### An Important Distinction: Step Length vs. Response Length

The paper is careful to distinguish its primary finding — step length confounding — from a superficially similar but distinct phenomenon: bias toward longer overall responses. Appendix A addresses this directly, and the distinction is important for understanding what the paper contributes.

Prior work (Chen et al., 2025b; Guha et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2024) has actually shown that **longer overall responses can be beneficial** for reasoning SFT — they often contain more thorough reasoning, more verification steps, and more explicit logical chains. If naturalness-based methods happened to select longer responses, this might actually be a feature rather than a bug.

But that's not what's happening. The paper shows (Appendix A.1) that selected responses are paradoxically **shorter overall** than unselected ones (average ~9.8K tokens selected vs. ~15.4K unselected), even though they have longer individual steps. This is because the step length confounding effect — driven by low-probability first tokens — is substantially stronger than any overall length bias (Appendix A.3 quantifies this: the confounding coefficient γ for step length is roughly **two orders of magnitude larger** than the coefficient γ₂ for total response length). The net effect is that naturalness-based methods preferentially select responses that are broken into fewer, longer steps, which may actually represent **poorer reasoning structure** — fewer distinct reasoning stages, more monolithic text blocks — than responses with many shorter, more granular steps.

This distinction matters because it means the selection bias is not just selecting for a neutral stylistic preference; it may be actively selecting for **worse reasoning structure**, which would explain why debiasing yields substantial accuracy improvements in the paper's experiments.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as a **diagnostic and corrective contribution** to the naturalness-based data selection literature, rather than proposing an entirely new selection paradigm. This is an important framing choice with several implications:

**It accepts the premise of naturalness-based selection.** The paper does not argue that model confidence is a bad signal for data quality. On the contrary, Section 4.3 explicitly confirms that selecting high-probability data outperforms selecting low-probability data even after debiasing — the relationship between probability and quality is real, it's just contaminated by the step-length confounder.

**It identifies a specific failure mode and proposes a targeted fix.** Rather than replacing the scoring function wholesale, ASLEC-DROP and ASLEC-CASL modify **how** the average log probability is computed — dropping or downweighting first tokens while preserving the signal from non-first tokens. This is philosophically similar to how one might use stratified sampling to correct for a demographic confound in survey analysis: the underlying measurement is sound, but the aggregation needs adjustment.

**It connects to causal inference methodology.** ASLEC-CASL explicitly draws on causal debiasing techniques (Udomcharoenchaikit et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2022), treating the first-token ratio Zᵢ as a confounder in the relationship between the "true" data quality signal (represented by β₁·s_first + β₂·s_drop) and the observed score slogp. The linear regression in Equation 5 estimates γ, the causal effect of the confounder, and Equation 7 subtracts this estimated effect to recover an unbiased quality estimate. This framing elevates the contribution from "we found a bug and patched it" to "we identified a confound and applied a principled correction."

**It isolates the mechanism.** By showing that the debiasing works (Section 4.2, Figure 4 — selected and unselected step-length distributions become nearly identical after applying ASLEC-DROP or ASLEC-CASL) and that the first token is indeed the locus of the problem (ASLEC-DROP, which simply removes first tokens, captures most of the benefit), the paper provides strong evidence that the identified mechanism — low-probability first tokens diluting in longer steps — is the **primary** driver of the confounding, not a correlated symptom of some deeper issue.

**It acknowledges limitations of scope.** The paper explicitly notes that it does not claim to have identified **all** possible confounders in naturalness-based selection — the Limitations section asks "whether there are deeper confounding factors" as an open question. It also restricts its focus to the SFT paradigm for long CoT reasoning, leaving open whether similar confounds exist in on-policy data generation and selection (Yang et al., 2025a; Lu and Lab, 2025), where the student model generates its own training data. These boundary conditions are important: they prevent overclaiming while directing future work toward the most productive next questions.

### The Broader Significance

Beyond the specific technical contribution, this paper serves as a cautionary tale for an increasingly common ML workflow: **using a model's own confidence scores to curate its training data.** The finding that a seemingly reasonable metric — average log probability — can be systematically biased by an architectural artifact (high entropy at decision points) suggests that similar confounds may lurk in other self-supervised data selection pipelines. The paper's causal regression approach (ASLEC-CASL) provides a template for diagnosing and correcting such confounds: identify a suspected confounder, formalize it as a measurable variable, fit a regression to estimate its effect, and subtract that effect from the selection score.

This template could generalize to other suspected confounds — response length, vocabulary complexity, number of mathematical expressions, presence of verification statements ("let me check," "wait, that's wrong") — any feature that might correlate with both the confidence score and actual training utility without being causally responsible for that utility. The paper doesn't explore these extensions, but the methodological framework it introduces makes them natural next steps.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper builds a **selection scoring function** that replaces the standard average log probability with a debiased version, so that when we rank reasoning trajectories for supervised fine-tuning, we select genuinely higher-quality data rather than data that simply happens to have longer reasoning steps. The core problem is that naturalness-based selection methods — which rank data by the average log probability assigned by an LLM — are systematically confounded by step length: longer steps dilute the impact of their low-probability first tokens, inflating their average score and making them look artificially "more natural" than shorter, potentially higher-quality steps. The solution takes two forms: a simple intervention that **drops first tokens entirely** (ASLEC-DROP), and a **causal regression** that estimates and subtracts the first token ratio's confounding influence from the raw score (ASLEC-CASL).

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system operates on a dataset of problems paired with multiple generated reasoning responses and produces a single selection score per response. The architecture has four major components:

1. **Response step-splitting** — takes a raw reasoning trajectory (the concatenation of `thinking` chain-of-thought and `response` final answer) and segments it into discrete reasoning steps using period-and-space boundaries. Each step is a sequence of tokens with a clear "first token" that often represents a branching decision point.

2. **Token-level probability extraction** — runs the target LLM (the model that will ultimately be fine-tuned) in inference mode on each response, extracting the log probability the model assigns to every token given its prefix. This produces a per-token probability vector for each response.

3. **First-token identification and separation** — for each step, identifies the first token and separates its probability from the probabilities of all subsequent tokens in that step. This separation is the key architectural insight: non-first tokens are typically high-probability continuations of an established reasoning thread, while first tokens are low-probability branching points.

4. **Debiased score computation** — aggregates token probabilities into a single selection score, but intervenes on how first tokens contribute. ASLEC-DROP simply excludes them. ASLEC-CASL fits a linear regression model that estimates how much the first-token ratio (`Zᵢ`, the fraction of all tokens that are step-initial) inflates the raw average log probability, then subtracts that estimated inflation to recover a debiased score.

Information flows linearly: raw response → step splitting → per-token probability extraction → separation into first-token vs. non-first-token probabilities → debiased aggregation → selection score → ranking → subset selection for SFT training.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the baseline naturalness scoring functions (GRACE, Local LP, Min Entropy, Min Perplexity) as formalized in Section 2.1, since ASLEC builds directly on these and understanding what they compute is prerequisite to understanding where the confound enters.
- **Second**, the step-splitting procedure and the first-token identification mechanism, since the entire debiasing strategy hinges on correctly identifying which tokens are "step-initial" and understanding why they behave differently.
- **Third**, ASLEC-DROP — the simpler variant — since it makes the debiasing mechanism transparent: it shows that simply removing first tokens captures most of the benefit, establishing that first tokens are indeed the locus of the problem.
- **Fourth**, ASLEC-CASL — the causal regression variant — since it builds on the same insight but preserves first-token information while removing only the confounding influence of the first-token ratio.
- **Fifth**, the linear regression fitting procedure (Equations 5–10), since it is the computational core of ASLEC-CASL and represents the paper's most technically sophisticated contribution.
- **Sixth**, the practical deployment pipeline: how these scoring functions are used to actually select training data subsets in the paper's experiments.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **diagnostic and corrective methods paper** whose core idea is that the average log probability metric used in naturalness-based data selection is confounded by step length — specifically, by the dilution of low-probability first tokens in longer steps — and that this confound can be removed by either dropping first tokens from the average or applying a causal regression to subtract their estimated confounding influence.

#### Baseline Naturalness Scoring Functions

Before explaining what ASLEC changes, we need to understand what it changes *from*. The paper's Section 2.1 formalizes four representative naturalness-based scoring functions, all of which operate on a supervised fine-tuning dataset `$\mathcal{D} = \{q_i, c_i, a_i\}_{i=1}^N$`, where `$q_i$` is a question, `$c_i$` is the long chain-of-thought reasoning trajectory (the `thinking` block), and `$a_i$` is the final answer (the `response` block). The SFT target sequence is the concatenation `$o_i = \texttt{thinking}\ c_i\ \texttt{response}\ a_i$`.

The SFT objective itself is the standard causal language modeling loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}\log P_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid o_{i,<t}, q_i)$$

where `$\theta$` are the model parameters, `$|o_i|$` is the total number of tokens in target sequence `$i$`, and `$P_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid o_{i,<t}, q_i)$` is the probability the model assigns to token `$t$` given all previous tokens and the question.

**What it computes:** the standard autoregressive cross-entropy loss — for each token in each sequence, the model predicts the next token given the prefix, and the loss is the negative log of the probability it assigned to the actual token. Summing over all tokens and averaging over all sequences gives the per-token loss.

**Why this form:** this is the maximum-likelihood objective for autoregressive language modeling. Training minimizes surprisal: the model learns to assign high probability to tokens that actually appear in the training data. The key point for this paper is that the same per-token log probabilities used in the loss are also used as the *selection criterion* — meaning the very signal the model is trained to maximize becomes the signal used to choose what to train on. This creates the potential for feedback loops if the selection criterion is biased.

The four naturalness-based scoring functions are:

**GRACE / Log Probabilities (Zhang et al., 2025)**

$$s_i^{\text{logp}} = \frac{1}{|o_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}\log P_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid o_{i,<t}, q_i)$$

where `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$` is the geometric mean of token probabilities (in log space, the arithmetic mean of log probabilities) and `$|o_i|$` is the total sequence length in tokens.

**What it computes:** the average log probability per token across the entire response. Each token contributes equally to the final score, regardless of its position within a reasoning step. Higher `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$` means the model is, on average, less surprised by the tokens in this sequence — it finds the sequence more "natural."

**Why this form:** taking the mean (rather than sum) normalizes for sequence length, making scores comparable across responses of different lengths. Without normalization, longer sequences would have lower total log probability simply because they contain more tokens, not because any individual token is less probable. The length normalization is intended to create a fair comparison, but — and this is the paper's central finding — it fails to account for the *internal structure* of reasoning trajectories, where step boundaries systematically depress certain tokens.

**Perplexity (Muennighoff et al., 2023)**

$$s_i^{\text{ppl}} = \exp\left(-s_i^{\text{logp}}\right)$$

**What it computes:** the exponentiated negative average log probability. Perplexity can be interpreted as the effective branching factor — roughly, how many equally likely choices the model faces at each token on average. Lower perplexity means higher confidence.

**Why this form:** perplexity is mathematically equivalent to `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$` for ranking purposes (the exponential is monotonic, so ranking by log probability and ranking by perplexity produce identical orderings). It is included for completeness and because different communities use different conventions, but it adds no new information for data selection.

**Local LP (Just et al., 2025)**

$$s_i^{\text{loc}} = \frac{1}{|S_i|}\sum_{s_{ij} \in S_i} \frac{1}{|s_{ij}|}\sum_{l=1}^{|s_{ij}|} \log P_\theta\left(s_{ijl} \mid s_{ij,<l}, s_{i,\max(1,j-k):j-1}, q_i\right)$$

where `$S_i = \{s_{ij}\}_{j=1}^{|S_i|}$` is the set of reasoning steps (segmented by newline or sentence boundaries), `$|S_i|$` is the number of steps, `$s_{ij}$` is the `$j$`-th step in sequence `$i$`, `$|s_{ij}|$` is the length of that step in tokens, `$l$` indexes tokens within the step, and `$k = 4$` is the context window (number of previous steps included in the conditioning). The notation `$s_{i,\max(1,j-k):j-1}$` represents the previous `$k$` steps (or fewer, if near the beginning of the sequence).

**What it computes:** for each reasoning step, the model's average log probability is computed *locally* — conditioned on the question and the preceding `$k$` steps, but not on tokens within the current step beyond those already generated. Then these per-step averages are averaged across all steps. The double averaging (first within-step, then across steps) gives equal weight to each step regardless of its length.

**Why this form:** Local LP was designed to address the observation that a response may have some high-quality steps and some low-quality steps, and a global average could mask this. By computing step-level scores and then averaging across steps, Local LP prevents a single very long, very confident step from dominating the score. The `$k = 4$` context window was chosen empirically in the original paper. However — and this is where the confound operates — Local LP still includes the low-probability first token of each step in its within-step average, meaning that shorter steps (where the first token constitutes a larger fraction of the total tokens) get penalized more heavily than longer steps (where the first token's influence is diluted).

**Min Entropy (Cui et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025)**

$$s_i^{\text{etp}} = \frac{1}{|o_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \left[-\sum_{v \in V} P_\theta(v \mid o_{i,<t}, q_i) \log P_\theta(v \mid o_{i,<t}, q_i)\right]$$

where `$V$` is the full vocabulary and `$P_\theta(v \mid o_{i,<t}, q_i)$` is the model's predicted probability for vocabulary token `$v$` at position `$t$`.

**What it computes:** the average per-token entropy of the model's predictive distribution. At each token position, instead of taking only the probability of the actual next token (as in the log-probability methods), entropy considers the entire distribution over the vocabulary. Low entropy means the model's probability mass is concentrated on a few tokens — it is confident about what comes next. High entropy means the distribution is spread out — the model is uncertain.

**Why this form:** entropy captures uncertainty even about tokens that don't happen to appear in the data. If two different reasoning branches are both plausible at a decision point, the model's distribution will have high entropy, and this is informative about data quality even if the particular branch chosen in this response received moderate probability. In practice, the paper notes that retrieving full vocabulary distributions is computationally expensive and storage-intensive, so they "retain only the top 20 most probable tokens per token, compute the entropy for each, and average these values across all tokens in a response" (Appendix B.2). This approximation makes entropy computation tractable at scale.

All four methods share a common selection rule: choose the subset `$\hat{\mathcal{D}} \subset \mathcal{D}$` that maximizes `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$` and `$s_i^{\text{loc}}$`, or minimizes `$s_i^{\text{ppl}}$` and `$s_i^{\text{etp}}$`. The paper's experiments (Section 2.3, Figure 1) demonstrate that all four methods exhibit the same systematic bias toward longer steps.

#### Step Splitting and First-Token Identification

The debiasing strategy in ASLEC depends critically on correctly identifying which tokens are "step-initial" — the tokens that begin a new reasoning step and therefore tend to have low probability. The paper's primary splitting method is described in Appendix C.2: **period-based splitting**, where response `$o_i$` is segmented into steps `$S_i = \{s^l_i\}_{l=1}^L$` by splitting on periods followed by spaces. This produces `$L$` steps, each of which is a sequence of tokens.

**Design choice: why period-based splitting over alternatives?**

The paper explicitly compares three splitting methods in Appendix C.2 (Figure 8): splitting by `\n\n` (double newlines, commonly used in LLM outputs to separate paragraphs or reasoning blocks), splitting by NLTK sentence tokenizer (an external NLP tool), and splitting by periods and spaces. The period-based method produces "the most distinct distribution differences" between selected and unselected data, meaning the confound is most visible with this splitting strategy. The paper interprets this as evidence that "low-probability tokens are most prominent at sentence beginnings when period-based splitting is applied."

This is a non-obvious but important finding: the confound is not equally strong under all step definitions. If you split by `\n\n` (which tends to produce larger, multi-sentence blocks), the first token of each block may be less systematically low-probability because the block may begin mid-thought rather than at a genuine reasoning branch point. Period-based splitting produces finer-grained steps that align more closely with individual reasoning statements, and it is at these granular decision points that the model's uncertainty (and hence low first-token probability) is most pronounced.

**What constitutes a "first token":** for each step `$s^l_i$` of length `$|s^l_i|$` tokens, the first token is `$s^l_{i,1}$` (the token at position 1 within the step). All remaining tokens `$s^l_{i,2}, s^l_{i,3}, \ldots, s^l_{i,|s^l_i|}$` are "non-first tokens." The paper does not apply any special treatment to tokens that might be step-initial for other reasons (e.g., tokens after punctuation within a step) — the step boundary is the only signal used.

**The low-probability first token phenomenon:** prior work (Wang et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025) established that the first token of a reasoning step often has high entropy because it represents a branching point — the model is choosing among different reasoning directions (e.g., "Wait," "Alternatively," "Therefore," "Let's"). The paper's Figure 3 provides concrete examples: a short step beginning with "But again..." has a first-token log probability of −5.48, while a long step beginning with "Wait, in triangle ABC..." has a first-token log probability of −6.68. In both cases, the first token is substantially less probable than subsequent tokens (which have log probabilities in the range of −0.01 to −0.70 once the reasoning direction is established).

**The key quantitative insight:** in a step of length `$n$`, the average log probability is:

$$\frac{1}{n}\left(\text{low-prob first token} + \sum_{t=2}^n \text{high-prob continuation tokens}\right)$$

As `$n$` grows, the weight of the first token (`$1/n$`) shrinks, and the average is increasingly dominated by the high-probability continuation tokens. This means that **two steps of identical reasoning quality but different verbosity will receive different average log probabilities**: the longer, more verbose step gets a higher score, not because it reasons better, but because the math of averaging dilutes the low-probability anchor point.

This is the mechanism that produces the monotonic relationship in Figure 2: longer steps systematically receive higher step-level log probabilities. And because naturalness-based selection chooses the highest-scoring responses, it preferentially selects responses composed of longer steps — the **step length confounding** phenomenon.

#### ASLEC-DROP: Dropping the First Token

ASLEC-DROP is the simpler of the two proposed variants, and its design follows directly from the diagnostic analysis in Section 2.4. If low-probability first tokens are the cause of step length confounding, the most direct fix is to exclude them from the average log probability computation entirely.

**Formal definition (Equation 4):**

$$s_i^{\text{drop}} = \frac{1}{|o_i| - |S_i|}\sum_{s^l_i \in S_i}\sum_{t=2}^{|s^l_i|}\log P_\theta\left(s^l_{i,t} \mid s^l_{i,<t}, s^{<l}_i, q_i\right)$$

where `$s_i^{\text{drop}}$` is the debiased selection score for response `$i$`, `$|o_i|$` is the total number of tokens in the full response, `$|S_i|$` is the number of reasoning steps (which equals the number of first tokens being dropped), `$s^l_i$` is the `$l$`-th step, `$|s^l_i|$` is the length of that step, `$t = 2$` starts the inner sum from the second token (skipping the first), `$s^l_{i,t}$` is the token at position `$t$` within step `$l$`, `$s^l_{i,<t}$` denotes all tokens in step `$l$` before position `$t$`, `$s^{<l}_i$` denotes all tokens in all steps before step `$l$`, and `$q_i$` is the question.

**What it computes:** the geometric mean of token probabilities across all non-first tokens in the response. The numerator sums log probabilities over all tokens *except* the first token of each step. The denominator `$|o_i| - |S_i|$` normalizes by the total number of non-first tokens (total tokens minus one per step). Each step contributes its continuation tokens only; the branching-point first tokens are simply excluded.

**Operationally:** for each reasoning trajectory, the system (1) splits the response into steps using period-based boundaries, (2) identifies the first token of each step, (3) extracts log probabilities from the target LLM for all tokens in the response, (4) discards the log probabilities of all first tokens, and (5) averages the remaining log probabilities. The resulting score `$s_i^{\text{drop}}$` is used for ranking and selection — higher scores are selected.

**Why this form works:** the key property is that `$s_i^{\text{drop}}$` is *not confounded by step length*, because the mechanism that creates the confound — the dilution of low-probability first tokens in longer steps — is removed entirely. Whether a step has 5 tokens or 50 tokens, its contribution to the score depends only on its continuation tokens, which are typically high-probability regardless of step length. A step with 50 continuation tokens gets summed over 50 tokens and divided by 50 in the final average, just as a step with 5 continuation tokens gets summed over 5 and divided by 5. The step's length no longer creates an artificial advantage.

**Why not use the mean within each step instead?** An alternative design would be to compute the average log probability per step (excluding the first token) and then average across steps, giving equal weight to each step regardless of length. ASLEC-DROP instead computes a single global average over all non-first tokens, which gives more weight to longer steps (since they contribute more tokens to the sum). This is a deliberate choice: if a longer step genuinely contains more high-quality reasoning content, it *should* contribute more to the score. The problem ASLEC-DROP solves is not that step length matters, but that step length *artificially inflates* scores through first-token dilution. By removing the first-token effect, longer steps only contribute more if they contain more high-probability continuation content — which is the desired behavior.

**Key trade-off:** ASLEC-DROP is computationally trivial — it requires no model fitting, no additional parameters, and no change to the probability extraction pipeline beyond ignoring certain tokens. Section 4.5 explicitly notes that "compared to GRACE, our ASLEC-DROP variant adopts a more streamlined approach: it simply drops the first token when computing the average token probability, thereby avoiding any additional computational overhead." However, it also "entirely discards the contribution of the first token to data selection" (Section 3, introduction). Some first tokens may carry useful signal — a very high-probability first token might indicate a particularly natural reasoning transition — and ASLEC-DROP throws this information away.

#### ASLEC-CASL: Causal Debiasing via Linear Regression

ASLEC-CASL addresses the trade-off in ASLEC-DROP by preserving first-token information while removing only the *confounding* influence of the first-token ratio, not the first-token probabilities themselves. The approach draws on causal debiasing techniques from the natural language processing literature.

**The causal framing:** the paper treats the observed average log probability `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$` as being generated by three components: (1) the "true" quality signal from first-token probabilities `$s_i^{\text{first}}$`, (2) the "true" quality signal from non-first-token probabilities `$s_i^{\text{drop}}$`, and (3) a spurious inflation caused by the confounder `$Z_i$`, where `$Z_i$` is the proportion of all tokens that are step-initial:

$$Z_i = \frac{|S_i|}{|o_i|}$$

Here `$|S_i|$` is the number of steps and `$|o_i|$` is the total number of tokens, so `$Z_i$` is the fraction of tokens that occupy the first position of some step. For a response with many short steps, `$Z_i$` is high; for a response with a few long steps, `$Z_i$` is low.

**The confound mechanism in `$Z_i$` terms:** when `$Z_i$` is low (fewer step boundaries relative to total tokens), the low-probability first tokens are diluted by a larger number of high-probability continuation tokens, inflating `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$`. When `$Z_i$` is high (more step boundaries relative to total tokens), the low-probability first tokens constitute a larger fraction of the total, depressing `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$`. Thus `$Z_i$` is negatively correlated with `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$` — as the first-token ratio goes down, the average log probability goes up — *even if the underlying reasoning quality is unchanged*. This is the spurious correlation that ASLEC-CASL aims to remove.

**The linear regression model (Equation 5):**

$$s_i^{\text{logp}} = \beta_1 s_i^{\text{first}} + \beta_2 s_i^{\text{drop}} + \gamma Z_i + \epsilon$$

where `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$` is the raw average log probability (the dependent variable), `$s_i^{\text{first}}$` is the average log probability of first tokens only:

$$s_i^{\text{first}} = \frac{1}{|S_i|}\sum_{s^l_i \in S_i}\log P_\theta\left(s^l_{i,1} \mid s^{<l}_i, q_i\right)$$

and `$s_i^{\text{drop}}$` is the ASLEC-DROP score defined in Equation 4, `$Z_i$` is the first-token ratio, `$\epsilon$` is a residual noise term, and `$\beta_1$`, `$\beta_2$`, `$\gamma$` are coefficients to be estimated.

**What it computes:** the regression models the observed average log probability as a linear combination of three predictors: the average first-token probability, the average non-first-token probability, and the first-token ratio. The coefficients `$\beta_1$` and `$\beta_2$` represent how much each component of "true" quality contributes to the observed score, while `$\gamma$` represents how much the confounder `$Z_i$` inflates or deflates the score *beyond* what the quality components would predict.

**Why a linear model?** The choice of linear regression is pragmatic. The relationship between `$Z_i$` and `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$` is expected to be approximately linear over the relevant range because the dilution effect is essentially arithmetic: each percentage-point decrease in the first-token ratio removes one percentage point's worth of low-probability contribution from the average. A linear model captures this directly. More complex models (nonlinear regression, neural networks) would be harder to fit, harder to interpret, and unlikely to yield substantially better debiasing given the simple arithmetic structure of the confound. The paper's validation in Section 4.4 (Table 5) shows that the residual `$\epsilon$` is consistently near zero, confirming that a linear model adequately captures the relationship.

**The debiasing operation (Equation 7):**

$$s_i^{\text{casl}} \sim s_i^{\text{logp}} - \gamma Z_i$$

where `$\gamma$` is the coefficient estimated from the regression and `$Z_i$` is the first-token ratio for response `$i$`.

**What it computes:** the debiased score is the raw average log probability minus the estimated contribution of the first-token ratio confound. If `$\gamma$` is negative (which it always is in the paper's experiments — Table 5 shows values ranging from −0.226 to −1.284), then responses with a low `$Z_i$` (few first tokens, long steps) get their scores *reduced* by subtracting a smaller negative number (i.e., adding less), while responses with a high `$Z_i$` (many first tokens, short steps) get their scores *increased* by subtracting a larger negative number (i.e., adding more). This counteracts the dilution effect.

**Operationally:** for a dataset of `$N$` responses, the system (1) computes `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$`, `$s_i^{\text{first}}$`, `$s_i^{\text{drop}}$`, and `$Z_i$` for every response `$i \in \{1,\ldots,N\}$`, (2) fits the linear regression in Equation 5 to estimate `$\beta_1$`, `$\beta_2$`, and `$\gamma$`, (3) for each response, computes the debiased score `$s_i^{\text{casl}} = s_i^{\text{logp}} - \gamma Z_i$`, and (4) uses `$s_i^{\text{casl}}$` for ranking and selection.

**Design choice: why subtract `$\gamma Z_i$` rather than adjust `$s_i^{\text{first}}$` directly?**

The paper could have chosen to adjust how first-token probabilities are weighted in the average rather than subtracting the confound from the final score. For instance, one could compute a weighted average where first tokens receive weight `$\lambda < 1$` and non-first tokens receive weight `$1$`. The causal regression approach is more principled because it estimates the confound's effect from data rather than imposing a fixed weighting scheme. The value of `$\gamma$` is learned from the observed relationship between `$Z_i$` and `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$` across the entire dataset, so it automatically adapts to the strength of the confound in different data distributions. Table 5 shows that `$\gamma$` varies substantially across source LLMs (from −0.226 for DS-Qwen-32B to −1.284 for gpt-oss-120b), confirming that a one-size-fits-all weighting would be suboptimal.

#### Linear Regression Fitting Procedure

The regression coefficients are estimated via ordinary least squares (OLS), which has a closed-form solution that is computationally trivial to compute.

**Optimization objective (Equation 8):**

$$\min_{\beta_1, \beta_2, \gamma} \sum_{i=1}^N \left(s_i^{\text{logp}} - \beta_1 s_i^{\text{first}} - \beta_2 s_i^{\text{drop}} - \gamma Z_i\right)^2$$

**What it computes:** the sum of squared residuals between the observed average log probability and the linear predictor. Minimizing this yields the coefficients that best explain the observed scores as a linear combination of the three predictors, in the least-squares sense.

**Why sum of squared errors:** this is the standard OLS objective, which is the maximum-likelihood estimator under the assumption of Gaussian errors. Given the paper's finding that residuals `$\epsilon$` are consistently small (Table 5), the Gaussian assumption is empirically reasonable. Alternative objectives (e.g., absolute error, Huber loss) would be more robust to outliers but are unnecessary given the well-behaved residuals and would not have a closed-form solution.

**Closed-form solution (Equations 9–10):**

$$[\beta_1, \beta_2, \gamma]^\top = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{Y}$$

$$\mathbf{X}_{i,:} = [s_i^{\text{first}}, s_i^{\text{drop}}, Z_i], \quad \mathbf{Y}_{i,:} = [s_i^{\text{logp}}]$$

where `$\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 3}$` is the design matrix (each row is one response, each column is one predictor), `$\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1}$` is the target vector (the observed average log probabilities), `$\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$` is the Gram matrix, and `$(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top$` is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.

**What it computes:** the OLS coefficients that minimize the sum of squared residuals. The computation involves: (1) forming the `$N \times 3$` design matrix by stacking the three predictors for each of the `$N$` responses, (2) computing the `$3 \times 3$` Gram matrix `$\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$` (three inner products, each summing over `$N$` terms), (3) inverting the `$3 \times 3$` Gram matrix (a trivial operation for a 3×3 matrix), and (4) multiplying by `$\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{Y}$` (a `$3 \times 1$` vector of predictor-target covariances).

**Computational cost:** the paper explicitly addresses this in Section 4.5: "since this regression model involves only a small number of parameters, the fitting procedure is highly efficient and typically completes in just a few seconds, imposing negligible cost on the overall pipeline." The dominant cost is forming `$\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$` and `$\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{Y}$`, which requires summing over all `$N$` responses — an `$O(N)$` operation. For the paper's largest datasets (16,000 responses for LIMO-v2, 40,000 for AceReason-1.1-SFT), this is milliseconds of computation.

**Interpretation of coefficients (from Table 5, Section 4.4):**

The paper fits the regression separately for each source LLM's data and reports the coefficients in Table 5. Across all source LLMs, pooling all data together:

- `$\beta_1 = 0.066$`: the coefficient on first-token probability. This is small (`$\ll \beta_2$`), meaning that first-token probabilities contribute very little to explaining variation in the overall average log probability *after accounting for the confound*.
- `$\beta_2 = 0.944$`: the coefficient on non-first-token probability. This is close to 1, meaning that the non-first-token probability is the dominant determinant of the overall score — which makes sense because non-first tokens constitute the vast majority of all tokens.
- `$\gamma = -0.680$` (overall): the coefficient on the first-token ratio confounder. The negative sign confirms that higher `$Z_i$` (more first tokens, shorter steps) depresses `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$`. The magnitude means that a `$0.05$` difference in `$Z_i$` (say, `$Z_i$` dropping from 0.10 to 0.05 because steps get twice as long) translates to a `$1 - e^{-0.680 \times 0.05} = 3.34\%$` reduction in per-token probability — a non-trivial inflation of the selection score that is entirely attributable to step length, not reasoning quality.

**Cross-source variation in `$\gamma$`:** the coefficient varies substantially across source LLMs: gpt-oss-120b has `$\gamma = -1.284$` (every `$0.05$` decrease in `$Z_i$` inflates per-token probability by `$6.22\%$`), while DS-Qwen-32B has `$\gamma = -0.226$` (the same `$Z_i$` change inflates probability by only `$1.12\%$`). This makes sense: different source LLMs produce reasoning trajectories with different characteristic step lengths and different first-token entropy patterns, so the strength of the confound varies. The regression adapts automatically to this variation.

**Residual analysis:** the paper notes that `$\epsilon$` "consistently remains at a low level" (Section 4.5), with values ranging from −0.054 to +0.028 across the four source LLMs in Table 5. This confirms that the linear model fits the data well — there is no large unexplained variance that would suggest missing confounders or nonlinear relationships. This is an important validation point: if `$\epsilon$` were large, it would suggest that the debiasing is incomplete and that other confounders beyond `$Z_i$` are at play.

#### The Data Selection Pipeline in Practice

The paper's experiments (Section 4) deploy ASLEC-DROP and ASLEC-CASL in a multi-source data selection setting. Understanding the full pipeline requires connecting the scoring functions to the actual selection decisions.

**Data generation (Section 4, setup).** For LIMO-v2 (800 problems), the paper generates 5 distinct responses per problem from each of 4 source LLMs (QwQ-32B, Qwen3-32B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, gpt-oss-120b), yielding `$800 \times 4 \times 5 = 16,000$` total responses. All responses are verified for answer correctness using the Math-Verify toolkit, and only correct responses are retained (incorrect ones are discarded and re-generated, up to 15 attempts per problem). For AceReason-1.1-SFT, 10,000 problems are sampled, each with 1 correct response from each of the 4 source LLMs, yielding 40,000 total responses.

**Target model probability extraction.** The target LLM (one of Qwen3-4B-Base, Qwen3-8B-Base, Qwen3-4B-Instruct, or Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, depending on the experiment) is run in inference mode on each response to extract token-level log probabilities. This is done via SGLang, with the model operating in evaluation mode (no gradient computation). The probabilities are stored per-token for subsequent scoring.

**Score computation.** For each response, the system computes:
1. `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$` (GRACE) — average over all tokens.
2. `$s_i^{\text{loc}}$` (Local LP) — step-level local average, `$k = 4$`.
3. `$s_i^{\text{drop}}$` (ASLEC-DROP) — average excluding first tokens.
4. `$s_i^{\text{first}}$`, `$s_i^{\text{drop}}$`, `$Z_i$` — intermediate quantities for ASLEC-CASL.
5. `$s_i^{\text{casl}}$` (ASLEC-CASL) — debiased score from regression.

**Selection.** For LIMO-v2 (16,000 responses), the paper selects 4,000 responses (25% of the total) to form the SFT training set. For AceReason-1.1-SFT (40,000 responses), 10,000 responses are selected. Selection is done by sorting all responses by the chosen score and taking the top `$K$` (where `$K$` is 4,000 or 10,000). Each selection method (GRACE, Local LP, ASLEC-DROP, ASLEC-CASL) produces a different subset of 4,000 or 10,000 responses.

**Cross-source balancing (implicit).** The paper does not enforce any per-source quotas. A selection method that happens to prefer responses from a particular source LLM will select more from that source and fewer from others. This is relevant because the paper notes (Section 4.1) that "prior naturalness-based methods... often hindered by the step length confounding problem, which leads them to overly prefer samples from a single data source." Because different source LLMs produce responses with different characteristic step lengths (Figure 2 shows DS-Qwen-32B has notably higher step-level log probabilities than other models, corresponding to longer steps), the confound can cause selection to concentrate on a narrow subset of sources, reducing diversity. ASLEC mitigates this by removing the step-length advantage, which implicitly leads to more balanced source representation.

**Training.** The selected subsets are used for full-parameter supervised fine-tuning of the target LLM via LlamaFactory. Training hyperparameters (Appendix B.3): batch size 32, packing enabled to concatenate shorter samples, maximum sequence length 32K tokens, Adam optimizer, learning rate `$5 \times 10^{-5}$`, 6 epochs, cosine learning rate schedule with minimum LR of `$1 \times 10^{-5}$`.

**Evaluation.** The fine-tuned models are evaluated on five benchmarks: AIME24, AIME25, MATH500, OlympiadBench (all mathematical reasoning), and GPQA (scientific reasoning). Accuracy is the primary metric, reported both as pass@1 and pass@4 (the fraction of problems where at least one of four sampled solutions is correct).

**Putting it together: what happens end-to-end for ASLEC-CASL on LIMO-v2.**

1. **Generation phase:** 800 math problems × 4 source LLMs × 5 responses each = 16,000 correct chain-of-thought responses, each consisting of `thinking` reasoning trajectory + `response` final answer.
2. **Probability extraction phase:** Qwen3-4B-Base processes all 16,000 responses, outputting per-token log probabilities for each.
3. **Step splitting phase:** each response is split into steps by period-based boundaries; first tokens are identified.
4. **Feature computation phase:** for each response, compute `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$` (global average), `$s_i^{\text{first}}$` (first-token average), `$s_i^{\text{drop}}$` (non-first-token average), and `$Z_i = |S_i| / |o_i|$` (first-token ratio).
5. **Regression fitting phase:** using all 16,000 responses as the dataset, fit the linear regression `$s_i^{\text{logp}} = \beta_1 s_i^{\text{first}} + \beta_2 s_i^{\text{drop}} + \gamma Z_i + \epsilon$` via OLS to estimate `$\gamma$`.
6. **Debiasing phase:** for each response, compute `$s_i^{\text{casl}} = s_i^{\text{logp}} - \gamma Z_i$`.
7. **Selection phase:** sort all 16,000 responses by `$s_i^{\text{casl}}$` descending; take the top 4,000.
8. **Training phase:** fine-tune Qwen3-4B-Base on the selected 4,000 responses for 6 epochs.
9. **Evaluation phase:** measure accuracy on AIME24, AIME25, MATH500, OlympiadBench, GPQA.

#### What ASLEC Does Not Do (Important Scope Boundaries)

Several design decisions are notable for what they exclude, and understanding these boundaries is essential:

**No retraining or fine-tuning of the target LLM for scoring.** The probabilities used for selection come from the *pre-trained* target LLM, not from a model that has already been partially fine-tuned. This means the selection is based on the model's prior beliefs about what constitutes natural reasoning, not on its post-training distribution. The paper does not explore whether iteratively updating the selection criterion as training progresses would be beneficial.

**No per-source normalization.** The regression is fit on all responses pooled together, and a single `$\gamma$` is used for all responses. An alternative design would fit separate `$\gamma$` values per source LLM (since Table 5 shows they vary substantially). The paper's overall regression combines all sources, which implicitly averages their different confounding strengths. Whether source-specific debiasing would perform better is not explored.

**No dynamic selection.** The scoring and selection are done once, before any training begins. There is no mechanism for adjusting the selection based on training progress — for instance, starting with "easier" (higher-probability) examples and gradually introducing "harder" (lower-probability) ones, or using the model's evolving probability estimates to re-rank remaining data. The selection is static throughout the 6-epoch training run.

**Only correct responses are in the selection pool.** The paper's data generation pipeline filters for answer correctness using Math-Verify, so the selection is among *correct* responses only. This means ASLEC is choosing which correct reasoning trajectory to train on, not choosing between correct and incorrect trajectories. The confounding problem exists even within the set of correct responses: a verbose but shallow correct solution may outrank a concise but insightful correct solution due to step-length effects.

**The regression uses all available data for fitting, including the responses that will be selected.** There is no held-out set for coefficient estimation — `$\gamma$` is estimated from the same pool of responses that are subsequently ranked and selected. This is valid because the regression is estimating a population-level relationship between `$Z_i$` and `$s_i^{\text{logp}}$`, not making per-instance predictions that would be subject to overfitting. With `$N = 16,000$` and only 3 parameters, overfitting is not a concern.

**No attempt to model deeper confounders.** The paper explicitly flags in the Limitations section that "whether there are deeper confounding factors remains an open question." First-token ratio `$Z_i$` is the only confounder modeled. Other potential confounders — vocabulary complexity, presence of mathematical notation, frequency of self-correction statements, syntactic complexity of individual steps — are not addressed. The low residuals in Table 5 suggest that `$Z_i$` accounts for most of the confound in the paper's experimental setup, but this may not generalize to all reasoning datasets or model families.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Diagnostic Discovery of Step Length as a Confound in Data Selection

The paper's most intellectually distinctive contribution is not a new algorithm but a **diagnostic insight**: the identification that naturalness-based data selection for long chain-of-thought reasoning is systematically confounded by step length — and, more specifically, that this confound operates through a precise, previously undocumented mechanism (the dilution of low-probability first tokens in longer steps).

**What makes this a conceptual advance rather than just a bug fix.** Prior work on naturalness-based selection (Zhang et al., 2025; Just et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2025) treated the average log probability as a transparent window into data quality — higher probability meant better alignment, and that was that. The field's collective assumption was that any biases in this metric were either negligible or benign. This paper shows that assumption is false in a way that is both **quantitatively large** and **mechanistically specific**: the selection pipeline is not just slightly tilted toward longer steps; it is actively discriminating against responses with shorter, more granular reasoning steps, even when those responses may contain higher-quality reasoning (Section 2.3, Figure 1 — the step-length distributions of selected vs. unselected data show a clear and consistent shift across all four naturalness-based methods tested).

The deeper conceptual move is the paper's demonstration that **the very structure of chain-of-thought reasoning — segmented into discrete steps, each beginning with a high-entropy branching point — creates a built-in confound for any metric that averages token-level probabilities without accounting for step boundaries**. This is not a bug in a particular implementation; it is a structural property of how autoregressive language models generate reasoning trajectories and how we choose to score them. The first token of each step is systematically lower probability because it represents a decision among alternative reasoning paths (Wang et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025), and any averaging scheme that gives equal weight to all tokens will penalize responses that make *more decisions* (i.e., have more steps) relative to responses that make fewer decisions but elaborate more verbosely within each. The paper names this phenomenon **step length confounding** and establishes it as a first-class problem for the field.

**Comparison to prior work.** Before this paper, the closest related finding was the observation that minority first tokens exhibit high entropy and drive reinforcement learning dynamics (Wang et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025). But that work focused on RL training for reasoning models, not on data selection for SFT. The connection — that this same architectural artifact creates a systematic bias in how we *select* training data — was entirely unmade. Similarly, work on data selection biases had noted that some metrics correlate with surface features like response length (Zhang et al., 2025), but had not isolated the specific mechanism (first-token dilution within steps) nor quantified its effect relative to other potential confounds. The paper's distinction between step-length bias and overall response-length bias (Appendix A, where the step-length confound is shown to dominate by ~**two orders of magnitude**) is itself a significant finding: it tells the field *which* bias to worry about and which is benign.

**Significance beyond performance gains.** Even if ASLEC-DROP and ASLEC-CASL had shown only marginal accuracy improvements, this diagnostic contribution would stand on its own. It identifies a hidden variable that corrupts a widely-used measurement, explains *why* it corrupts it (through the precise arithmetic of dilution), and provides a template for checking whether similar confounds exist in related metrics (perplexity, entropy, local log probability — all of which the paper shows suffer from the same bias in Figure 1). This is the kind of finding that changes how researchers think about a whole class of methods, not just how they implement one specific pipeline.

**Evidence anchoring.** The core diagnostic evidence is in Figure 1 (showing that all four naturalness-based methods produce the same step-length distribution shift), Figure 2 (showing the monotonic relationship between step length and log probability), and Figure 3 (showing concrete examples of the low-probability first token mechanism). The Appendix A analysis quantitatively separates step-length bias from overall response-length bias, demonstrating that the former is the dominant confound.

---

### Innovation 2: Causal Regression as a Principled Debiasing Framework

While ASLEC-DROP is a pragmatic first-pass solution, ASLEC-CASL represents a more fundamental conceptual contribution: the **framing of step-length confounding as a causal inference problem, with a linear regression model used to estimate and remove the confound's effect**. This reframes what could have been an ad-hoc heuristic ("just drop the first token") into a principled statistical correction.

**Why the causal framing matters.** The paper could have stopped at ASLEC-DROP and claimed victory — the simple dropping approach already outperforms existing methods by ~6.28% on average. But ASLEC-CASL does something more intellectually ambitious: it models the confound explicitly, estimates its magnitude from data, and subtracts only the confound's influence while preserving the potentially useful signal in first-token probabilities. This matters because it demonstrates that first tokens *do* carry some quality signal (Section 4.3 confirms that high-probability data is genuinely better than low-probability data even after debiasing), and a method that can separate the confound from the signal is strictly more principled than one that discards both together.

The specific formulation — treating the first-token ratio `Zᵢ` as a confounder in a linear model where `s_logp` is the observed outcome and `s_first` and `s_drop` are the genuine quality components — draws directly on causal debiasing techniques from NLP (Udomcharoenchaikit et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2022). But the application to *data selection scoring* is novel. Prior causal debiasing work focused on removing spurious correlations from model predictions (e.g., preventing a fake news classifier from learning to associate certain entities with certain labels). This paper shows that the same conceptual toolkit can be applied to the *evaluation metric itself*, treating the scoring function as something that can be "debiased" through regression adjustment.

**What makes this more than incremental.** The causal regression approach is not just "ASLEC-DROP plus a correction term." It is a **generalizable framework** for diagnosing and correcting confounds in any data selection metric. If a future researcher suspects that some other feature (vocabulary complexity, answer length, presence of mathematical notation) is confounded with a quality metric, they can plug that feature in as an additional `Z` term, fit the regression, and subtract its estimated influence. The paper doesn't explore this generalization (it's flagged as future work in the Limitations), but the framework is laid out clearly enough that the extension is natural. This transforms the contribution from "we found and fixed one specific confound" to "we introduced a methodology for finding and fixing confounds in data selection metrics."

**Evidence anchoring.** The key evidence for this innovation is in Section 4.4 (Table 5), which shows that the regression coefficient `γ` varies substantially across source LLMs (from −0.226 to −1.284), confirming that the confound's strength is data-dependent and that a learned correction is necessary — a fixed weighting scheme would not work across all data sources. The consistently low residuals (`ε` near zero) validate that the linear model adequately captures the confound. Section 4.3 (Table 4) confirms that the debiased score `s_casl` preserves the meaningful quality gradient (high-probability data is better than low-probability), which is the expected behavior of a causal correction that removes confound without destroying signal.

---

### Innovation 3: Empirical Demonstration That Step Structure, Not Overall Length, Is the Dominant Confound

A subtle but important finding that the paper establishes — and that distinguishes it from a shallower "longer responses are preferred" critique — is the **dissociation of step-length bias from overall response-length bias**, with quantitative evidence that the former dominates by orders of magnitude.

**Why this distinction is conceptually important.** An obvious alternative explanation for the paper's findings would be: "naturalness-based methods just prefer longer responses, and longer responses happen to have longer steps." If this were true, the paper's contribution would reduce to a known phenomenon (length bias in language model scoring) applied to a new domain. But the paper shows this is **not** what's happening. In fact, naturalness-based methods in the paper's experiments preferentially select responses that are *shorter overall* (~9.8K tokens selected vs. ~15.4K unselected, Appendix A.1) — the opposite of what a simple length-bias story would predict. The mechanism is specifically about step-internal structure (the dilution of low-probability first tokens), not about total verbosity.

The quantitative comparison in Appendix A.3 (Table 6) makes this precise: when both step-length confound `γZᵢ` and overall response-length confound `γ₂|oᵢ|` are included in the regression, `γ₂` is ~**two orders of magnitude smaller** than `γ`. When `γ₂|oᵢ|` is removed from the debiasing formula (Table 7), performance is essentially unchanged. This is strong quantitative evidence that the paper has correctly isolated the *active ingredient* in the confound — it is not just "longer stuff gets higher scores," but specifically "longer *steps* get higher scores *because first tokens get diluted*."

**Comparison to prior assumptions.** Work on reasoning data quality has often assumed that longer responses are better (Chen et al., 2025b; Guha et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2024) because longer chains of thought often contain more thorough reasoning, more verification, and more explicit logical steps. The paper's findings complicate this picture: longer *overall* responses may indeed be beneficial (consistent with prior work), but longer *individual steps* (at the expense of having fewer steps) may be harmful because they represent less granular, less structured reasoning. A "good" reasoning trajectory under this framework has many relatively short, well-defined steps rather than a few long, monolithic ones. The naturalness-based selection bias pushes in the wrong direction along this axis, and ASLEC corrects for it.

**Significance beyond the specific confound.** This finding suggests a more general principle that the paper only gestures at but that has broader implications: **the granularity of reasoning structure matters for data quality assessment, and metrics that are agnostic to that structure will capture spurious signals.** This principle could extend beyond step length to other structural features — the depth of reasoning trees, the frequency of self-correction, the ratio of computation to explanation. The paper doesn't pursue these extensions, but the dissociation of step-structure bias from overall-length bias provides a template for how such investigations could be conducted: identify a suspected structural confound, measure its correlation with the quality metric, and quantify its effect size relative to a simpler baseline explanation.

**Evidence anchoring.** The core evidence is in Appendix A: Figure 5 (overall response length vs. log probability, showing the counterintuitive reversal where selected responses are shorter), Table 6 (regression coefficients showing `γ₂` is negligible relative to `γ`), and Table 7 (performance is unchanged when `γ₂|oᵢ|` term is excluded from debiasing). The main-text Figure 1 and Figure 4 provide the visual confirmation that step-length distributions (not overall-length distributions) are what shift between selected and unselected data, and that ASLEC corrects this shift.

---

### Innovation 4: A Cautionary Tale for Self-Supervised Data Curation Pipelines

Beyond the specific technical contribution, the paper serves as a **methodological warning** for an increasingly prevalent ML paradigm: using a model's own internal signals (confidence, probability, entropy) to curate its training data. The finding that a seemingly reasonable metric — average log probability — can be systematically biased by an architectural artifact (high entropy at decision points) has implications that extend well beyond reasoning data selection.

**Why this is a general insight, not just a domain-specific fix.** The mechanism the paper identifies — low-probability tokens at structural boundaries diluting in longer segments — is not specific to chain-of-thought reasoning. Any domain where model outputs have a natural segmental structure with high-entropy transition points could exhibit similar confounds. Code generation (function boundaries, control flow branches), long-form writing (paragraph transitions, section breaks), multi-turn dialogue (speaker changes, topic shifts), and structured prediction (field boundaries, delimiter tokens) all share this property. In each case, a "naturalness" metric that averages token probabilities without accounting for segment boundaries would penalize outputs that make more structural decisions (more segments) relative to those that make fewer.

The paper does not explore these extensions, but the conceptual framework it provides — identify structural boundaries, separate boundary-token probabilities from continuation-token probabilities, and either drop or causally adjust the boundary tokens — is directly portable. This is not a contribution the paper claims explicitly, but it is an implication that a careful reader can extract: **anytime you average token probabilities over sequences with internal structure, think about whether the structure creates a confound.**

**The self-improvement feedback loop danger.** The paper's connection to self-improvement pipelines (flagged in Section 5 and the Limitations) adds another layer to this warning. If a model uses its own confidence scores to select training data, and those confidence scores are systematically biased toward a particular structural property (e.g., fewer, longer reasoning steps), then training on the selected data reinforces that structural property, which in turn strengthens the bias in future confidence estimates. Over multiple iterations, the model would drift toward producing the structural pattern that scores highly under the biased metric — not because that pattern corresponds to better reasoning, but because it exploits the confound. The paper doesn't demonstrate this feedback loop empirically, but the logic follows directly from the mechanism they identify.

**Comparison to known issues in RLHF and reward modeling.** The situation is structurally analogous to reward hacking in RLHF, where a reward model learns to prefer certain surface features (length, formatting, politeness) that correlate with human preferences in the training data but don't causally produce quality. The paper's contribution is showing that a similar dynamic can arise even in the simpler setting of supervised data selection, without any RL component, purely through the arithmetic of how selection scores are computed. This broadens the scope of "reward hacking"-style concerns from RL-based training to any pipeline where model confidence is used as a quality proxy.

**Evidence anchoring.** The paper's data generation pipeline — 4 source LLMs, multiple responses per problem, verified correctness — is precisely the kind of setup where a self-improvement loop would operate. The finding that naturalness-based methods concentrate selection on a subset of sources (Section 4.1 notes they "overly prefer samples from a single data source") demonstrates the confound's practical effect: it doesn't just shift the distribution within a source, it shifts selection *between* sources, potentially discarding diversity that would be valuable for generalization. Figure 4 (showing that ASLEC produces nearly identical step-length distributions for selected and unselected data) provides the visual evidence that the confound is correctable, but the deeper point is that it existed in the first place — and would have gone unnoticed without the kind of diagnostic analysis the paper performs.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** Two reasoning SFT datasets are used: LIMO-v2 (Ye et al., 2025), consisting of 800 carefully curated mathematical reasoning problems with rigorous quality filtering, and AceReason-1.1-SFT (Chen et al., 2025b), from which 10,000 math problems are randomly sampled. For LIMO-v2, 5 distinct correct responses are generated per problem from each of 4 source LLMs (yielding 16,000 total responses). For AceReason-1.1-SFT, 1 correct response per source LLM per problem is generated (yielding 40,000 total responses). All responses are verified for answer correctness using the Math-Verify toolkit, with up to 15 generation attempts per problem to obtain the required number of correct responses.
- **Source LLMs (data generators).** Four LLM families are used to generate the SFT data: QwQ-32B (Team, 2025), a reasoning-specialized model trained with RL on Qwen2.5-32B; Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025a), which underwent long-CoT cold-start training and reasoning-focused RL; DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B (Guo et al., 2025), an early RL-trained reasoning model; and gpt-oss-120b (Agarwal et al., 2025), which combines compact attention layers with linear attention. All source LLMs use top-p sampling with p=0.95; gpt-oss-120b uses temperature 1.0 with reasoning effort set to high, while the other three use temperature 0.6. Maximum generation length is fixed at 64K tokens for all models.
- **Target LLMs (models being fine-tuned).** Four models of varying sizes and types are trained on the selected SFT data: Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base (pre-trained only), Qwen3-4B-Instruct (the 2507 variant, with non-thinking mode updated from Qwen3-4B's mixed-reasoning framework), and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (used as a substitute since the Qwen3 series lacks an 8B instruct model). The target LLMs are fine-tuned using full-parameter training via LlamaFactory with batch size 32, packing enabled, maximum sequence length 32K tokens, Adam optimizer, learning rate 5×10⁻⁵, 6 epochs, and a cosine learning rate schedule with minimum LR of 1×10⁻⁵.
- **Metrics.** The primary metric is accuracy on mathematical and scientific reasoning benchmarks, reported as both pass@1 (the fraction of problems where the first sampled solution is correct) and pass@4 (the fraction where at least one of four sampled solutions is correct). Five evaluation benchmarks are used: AIME24, AIME25, MATH500 (Lightman et al., 2023), and OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024) for mathematical reasoning, plus GPQA (Rein et al., 2024) for scientific reasoning. AIME24 and AIME25 are competition-level math datasets; MATH500 consists of 500 representative problems from the MATH benchmark; OlympiadBench contains Olympiad-level problems; GPQA tests graduate-level scientific reasoning.
- **Baselines.** Two naturalness-based data selection methods serve as baselines: GRACE (Zhang et al., 2025), which selects responses with the highest average log probability s_logp, and Local LP (Just et al., 2025), the current state-of-the-art, which computes step-level local log probabilities with a context window of k=4 previous steps and selects responses with the highest s_loc. The paper also compares against heuristic baselines in Appendix D.2: Uniform random sampling (4K reasoning trajectories sampled uniformly from the four source LLMs), High Difficulty (selecting the 4K longest reasoning trajectories, using response length as a proxy for difficulty), and Low Difficulty (selecting the 4K shortest trajectories).
- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Selection is performed by generating a fixed number of responses per problem from the source LLMs (5 per source for LIMO-v2, 1 per source for AceReason-1.1-SFT), extracting token-level log probabilities from the target LLM via SGLang in evaluation mode, computing selection scores for all responses, and selecting a fixed-size subset: 4,000 responses from 16,000 for LIMO-v2 (25% retention), and 10,000 responses from 40,000 for AceReason-1.1-SFT (25% retention). The probability extraction is a one-time cost per target model per dataset. For Min Entropy, full vocabulary distributions are approximated by retaining only the top 20 most probable tokens per position to manage computational and storage overhead (Appendix B.2). ASLEC-DROP adds zero additional computation beyond the standard GRACE pipeline (it simply ignores first tokens during averaging). ASLEC-CASL adds the cost of fitting a 3-parameter linear regression on N=16,000 or N=40,000 data points, which "typically completes in just a few seconds" (Section 4.5).
- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No explicit cross-validation or statistical significance testing is reported. The selection is performed once on the full dataset, and the selected subset is used for a single training run per model per method. Results are reported as point estimates (accuracy percentages) without confidence intervals. The difficulty estimation experiments (Appendix D.2) use a single train-test split. The regression coefficients in Section 4.4 (Table 5) are fitted on all available data without a held-out validation set, which is reasonable given the small number of parameters relative to the sample size (3 parameters, N≥16,000).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Performance Comparison on LIMO-v2

Table 1 reports the main results on LIMO-v2 across four target LLMs and five evaluation benchmarks. The headline finding is that both ASLEC variants consistently and substantially outperform the existing naturalness-based selection baselines.

**Aggregate gains over Local LP.** Averaged across all four target LLMs and five benchmarks, ASLEC-DROP achieves a 6.28% accuracy improvement over Local LP, while ASLEC-CASL achieves a 9.08% improvement (abstract). These are aggregate figures across both datasets; the per-model results in Table 1 provide the detailed breakdown.

**Per-model results on LIMO-v2 (Table 1).** For Qwen3-4B-Base:

- GRACE achieves an average accuracy of 31.42% across the four math benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, MATH500, OlympiadBench).
- Local LP improves to 36.50% average accuracy.
- ASLEC-DROP reaches 38.38% average accuracy, a gain of 4.27 percentage points over Local LP.
- ASLEC-CASL reaches 42.81% average accuracy, a gain of 8.70 percentage points over Local LP.

The largest per-benchmark gains for ASLEC-CASL on Qwen3-4B-Base are on AIME25 (+10.00 percentage points over Local LP, from 20.83% to 30.83%) and MATH500 (+8.40 points, from 71.60% to 80.00%). The pass@4 metrics show similar trends, with ASLEC-CASL reaching 47.54% average pass@4 accuracy compared to Local LP's 36.50% — an 11.04-point improvement.

For Qwen3-8B-Base, the pattern holds with larger absolute accuracy numbers reflecting the larger model capacity:

- ASLEC-CASL achieves 49.03% average accuracy vs. 42.81% for Local LP (+6.22 points), with particularly strong gains on OlympiadBench (+8.80 points, from 76.60% to 85.40%) and AIME25 (+16.67 points, from 20.83% to 37.50%).

**Instruct models show the largest absolute gains.** Qwen3-4B-Instruct, which starts from a higher base due to its instruction tuning, sees ASLEC-CASL reach 60.44% average accuracy vs. 50.14% for Local LP — a 10.30-point improvement. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, despite being the weakest overall performer (starting from 33.97% with Local LP), sees ASLEC-CASL reach 45.18% average accuracy, an 11.21-point gain. This pattern is notable: the debiasing helps both base and instruct models, but the proportional improvement is larger on models with lower starting performance.

**ASLEC-CASL consistently outperforms ASLEC-DROP.** Across all 16 model-benchmark combinations in Table 1 (4 models × 4 math benchmarks), ASLEC-CASL achieves higher accuracy than ASLEC-DROP in every case. The gap between the two variants averages approximately 2–4 percentage points, confirming that preserving first-token information (while removing only its confounding influence) provides a genuine benefit over simply discarding it.

#### Overall Performance Comparison on AceReason-1.1-SFT

Table 3 reports results on the larger AceReason-1.1-SFT dataset (10K selected from 40K responses). The pattern is consistent with LIMO-v2 but with somewhat smaller gains, consistent with the paper's observation that "the performance gain on LIMO-v2 exceeds that on AceReason-1.1-SFT, highlighting their strong suitability for low-resource SFT scenarios" (Section 4.1).

**Per-model results on AceReason-1.1-SFT (Table 3).** For Qwen3-4B-Base:

- ASLEC-DROP achieves 47.20% average accuracy vs. 43.08% for Local LP (+4.12 points).
- ASLEC-CASL achieves 48.23% average accuracy vs. 43.08% for Local LP (+5.15 points).
- The gains are concentrated on AIME benchmarks: ASLEC-CASL improves AIME25 from 30.00% (Local LP) to 30.83% (+0.83 points) and MATH500 from 77.80% to 84.60% (+6.80 points).

For Qwen3-8B-Base on AceReason-1.1-SFT:

- ASLEC-CASL reaches 51.02% average accuracy vs. 44.41% for Local LP (+6.61 points), with MATH500 improving from 77.60% to 86.60% (+9.00 points) and OlympiadBench from 43.33% to 53.33% (+10.00 points).

**Instruct models again benefit strongly.** Qwen3-4B-Instruct sees ASLEC-CASL reach 57.64% average accuracy vs. 49.26% for Local LP (+8.38 points). Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct shows ASLEC-CASL at 46.32% vs. 35.58% for Local LP (+10.74 points).

**Why LIMO-v2 gains exceed AceReason-1.1-SFT gains.** The paper attributes this to resource constraints: "In such settings, low-quality samples tend to have a more pronounced negative effect; our methods mitigate step length confounding and thereby enhance generalization performance" (Section 4.1). LIMO-v2 has only 800 problems with 16,000 total responses, while AceReason-1.1-SFT has 10,000 problems with 40,000 responses. In the smaller dataset, each selected response carries more weight in training, so the effect of the confound (selecting suboptimal verbose-but-shallow responses over concise-but-deep ones) is more damaging. On the larger dataset, the model sees more total examples, partially diluting the impact of any individual poor selection.

#### GPQA Results (Scientific Reasoning)

Table 2 reports results on GPQA, a graduate-level scientific reasoning benchmark, evaluating whether the debiasing benefits transfer beyond mathematics to scientific domains. All models are trained on LIMO-v2 (math-only data) and evaluated on GPQA.

**The debiasing transfers to out-of-domain evaluation.** For Qwen3-4B-Base:

- ASLEC-CASL achieves 35.35% pass@1 accuracy on GPQA vs. 29.16% for Local LP (+6.19 points).
- Pass@4 shows ASLEC-CASL at 66.66% vs. 61.61% for Local LP (+5.05 points).

For Qwen3-8B-Base:

- ASLEC-CASL achieves 52.14% pass@1 vs. 49.49% for Local LP (+2.65 points).
- Pass@4 shows ASLEC-CASL at 82.32% vs. 77.77% for Local LP (+4.55 points).

For Qwen3-4B-Instruct:

- ASLEC-CASL achieves 61.23% pass@1 vs. 52.14% for Local LP (+9.09 points).
- Pass@4 shows ASLEC-CASL at 84.34% vs. 77.27% for Local LP (+7.07 points).

For Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct:

- ASLEC-CASL achieves 38.51% pass@1 vs. 26.13% for Local LP (+12.38 points), the largest single gain in the GPQA results.
- Pass@4 shows ASLEC-CASL at 74.74% vs. 57.57% for Local LP (+17.17 points).

**The transfer is significant.** Training on math-only data and evaluating on science questions is a test of whether the selected data improves general reasoning capabilities or only math-specific skills. The consistent GPQA gains (averaging ~7 points across models) suggest that mitigating step-length confounding improves the fundamental reasoning quality of the selected training data, not just its mathematical correctness. Responses with better reasoning structure (more granular steps, less verbose filler) may teach transferable reasoning patterns that apply beyond the training domain.

#### Performance on Alleviating Confounding

Section 4.2 directly tests whether ASLEC achieves its stated goal: reducing the step-length distribution difference between selected and unselected data. Figure 4 presents the analog of Figure 1 but for ASLEC-DROP and ASLEC-CASL.

**The confound is substantially mitigated.** In contrast to Figure 1, where all four baseline methods showed a clear rightward shift in step-length distributions for selected data (selected responses consistently had longer steps), Figure 4 shows that both ASLEC variants produce "markedly smaller step length disparities" between selected and unselected data. The distributions nearly overlap, with the remaining differences being substantially smaller than in the baseline methods.

**Mechanism confirmation.** The paper notes that "because both variants operate by intervening directly on the probability assigned to the first token, this result further implies that the step length confounding issue is intimately linked to the model's first-token probabilities" (Section 4.2). If the confound had a different root cause — vocabulary complexity, mathematical notation density, syntactic structure — then dropping or downweighting first tokens would not have equalized the step-length distributions. The fact that it does is strong evidence that first-token dilution is indeed the primary mechanism.

#### Comparing Max vs. Min Probability Selection

Section 4.3 (Table 4) addresses a potential concern: does ASLEC-CASL simply reverse the ranking, selecting previously low-ranked responses? If so, the gains would be an artifact of selecting from a different part of the distribution rather than genuinely improving selection quality.

**High-probability selection consistently beats low-probability selection.** Table 4 compares ASLEC-CASL when selecting the responses with the highest s_casl ("max CASL") versus the lowest s_casl ("min CASL") on LIMO-v2. Across all four target LLMs and all four math benchmarks:

- Qwen3-4B-Base: max CASL achieves 31.66% (AIME24), 30.83% (AIME25), 80.00% (MATH500), 42.81% (OlympiadBench), while min CASL achieves 29.16%, 28.33%, 77.40%, 39.70% respectively — a consistent 2–3 point advantage for max selection.
- Qwen3-8B-Base: max CASL outperforms min CASL by 3–6 points across benchmarks (e.g., 45.00% vs. 41.66% on AIME24, 49.03% vs. 42.94% average).
- Qwen3-4B-Instruct: max CASL leads by 5–7 points (71.66% vs. 65.83% on AIME24, 60.44% vs. 55.14% average).
- Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct: max CASL leads by 2–3 points (28.33% vs. 25.00% on AIME24, 45.18% vs. 43.08% average).

**Interpretation.** The fact that max CASL consistently beats min CASL confirms that the debiased score s_casl preserves the meaningful quality gradient — higher probability (after removing the confound) still correlates with better training data. The improvement from ASLEC-CASL is not because it selects previously low-ranked data (which would correspond to min CASL outperforming max CASL), but because it corrects the ranking within the high-probability region, removing responses that scored highly only due to step-length inflation while promoting responses that were previously penalized for having shorter, more structured steps.

#### Linear Regression Coefficients and Interpretation

Section 4.4 (Table 5) presents the fitted regression coefficients from Equation 5 for data generated by each source LLM on LIMO-v2, plus an "Overall" fit on all data pooled together.

**Coefficient magnitudes.** For the overall fit:

- β₁ = 0.066 (first-token probability coefficient): substantially smaller than β₂, confirming that first-token probabilities contribute relatively little to explaining the observed s_logp after accounting for non-first-token probabilities.
- β₂ = 0.944 (non-first-token probability coefficient): close to 1, indicating that non-first-token probabilities are the dominant determinant of the overall score — expected since they constitute the vast majority of tokens.
- γ = −0.680 (first-token ratio confound coefficient): negative and substantial in magnitude.
- ε = −0.054 (residual): small, indicating good model fit.

**Interpretation of γ.** The paper provides a concrete interpretation: "for every 0.05 difference in the first-token ratio between samples, the impact on the overall probability is equivalent to reducing each token's probability by 1−e^(−0.680×0.05) = 3.34%." In other words, if two responses have identical reasoning quality (same s_first and s_drop) but one has Z_i = 0.05 and the other has Z_i = 0.10 (the latter having twice as many step boundaries per token, i.e., shorter steps), the raw s_logp of the latter would be depressed by approximately 3.34% per token purely due to the confound — not because it is lower quality.

**Cross-source variation in γ.** The confound strength varies substantially across source LLMs:

- QwQ-32B: γ = −0.284 (2.84% per-token probability effect per 0.05 Z_i difference: 1−e^(−0.284×0.05) = 1.41%)
- Qwen3-32B: γ = −0.529 (2.61%)
- DS-Qwen-32B: γ = −0.226 (1.12%)
- gpt-oss-120b: γ = −1.284 (6.22%)

gpt-oss-120b exhibits by far the strongest confounding, with a γ magnitude roughly 5.7× larger than DS-Qwen-32B's. The paper interprets this as indicating that "the data from it suffers from a more pronounced confounding problem" — meaning gpt-oss-120b produces responses where step length varies more dramatically or where first tokens are systematically lower probability, creating a stronger spurious correlation between step length and s_logp.

**Why this variation matters.** If the confound were uniform across sources, a simple fixed correction (e.g., always downweight first tokens by some constant factor) would suffice. The large cross-source variation in γ demonstrates that the confound is data-dependent and that a learned correction (fitting γ from data) is necessary. This validates the regression approach over a fixed heuristic.

**Residual analysis.** The residuals ε range from −0.054 (Overall) to +0.028 (gpt-oss-120b), consistently near zero. This indicates that the linear model with just three predictors (s_first, s_drop, Z_i) captures the vast majority of the variance in s_logp. There is no evidence of large unexplained variance that would suggest missing confounders or nonlinear relationships — though the paper appropriately notes in the Limitations that "whether there are deeper confounding factors remains an open question."

**β₁ ≪ β₂ interpretation.** The paper emphasizes that "β₁ ≪ β₂... further suggests that the ratio of the first-token probability in the global average probability should be reduced." This is consistent with the ASLEC-DROP design: since first tokens contribute very little to the "true" quality signal (β₁ is small) but their proportion (Z_i) creates a confounding effect (γ is substantial), the optimal strategy is to reduce their influence on the final score rather than to try to extract signal from them.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Different step splitting methods (Appendix C.2, Figure 8).** The paper compares three step-splitting strategies: period-and-space splitting (the primary method), `\n\n` splitting (double newlines, common in LLM outputs for paragraph breaks), and NLTK sentence tokenization. Figure 8 shows that all three methods produce a step-length distribution difference between selected and unselected data under both GRACE and Min Entropy, confirming that the confound is robust to the splitting method. However, period-based splitting produces "the most distinct distribution differences," which the paper interprets as evidence that "low-probability tokens are most prominent at sentence beginnings when period-based splitting is applied" — finer-grained sentence boundaries capture the branching-point uncertainty more precisely than coarser paragraph-level splits.

**Overall response length vs. step length bias (Appendix A, Tables 6–7, Figures 5–6).** A comprehensive analysis dissociates step-length confounding from overall response-length bias:

- Figure 5 shows that log probability actually *increases* with overall response length (longer responses get higher log probabilities, contrary to the paper's earlier conclusion that selected responses are shorter overall), and that DS-Qwen-32B, which produces longer steps, has consistently higher log probabilities than other source LLMs.
- Figure 6 shows that tokens toward the end of responses have systematically higher log probabilities than tokens at the beginning, following a monotonically increasing pattern — the target LLM becomes more confident as it processes more context, which is the mechanism by which longer responses get higher scores.
- Table 6 fits an extended regression model that includes both Z_i (step-length confound) and γ₂|o_i| (overall response length confound). The coefficient γ₂ is ~two orders of magnitude smaller than γ (e.g., 1E-8 for QwQ-32B vs. γ = −0.062 in this split — note that γ values differ from Table 5 because the regression specification changed). The paper explicitly states that "the confounder γ₂|o_i| induced by total length is smaller by approximately two orders of magnitude" compared to γZ_i.
- Table 7 shows that removing the γ₂|o_i| term from the debiasing formula (computing s_casl = s_logp − γZ_i instead of s_casl = s_logp − γZ_i − γ₂|o_i|) causes "little change in model performance" — accuracy differences are within 1–2 percentage points and not consistently directional. For Qwen3-4B-Base on AIME25, the version without γ₂|o_i| actually performs slightly better (30.83% vs. 30.83%, identical).

This ablation is critical because it establishes that the paper has correctly isolated the *active ingredient* in the confound. If the overall response length bias were the dominant effect, then simply selecting shorter responses (which the biased methods already do — selected responses average ~9.8K tokens vs. ~15.4K unselected) would already be optimal, and ASLEC would not help. The fact that step-length correction dominates over total-length correction (by 100× in the regression coefficients, and with near-zero performance impact when total-length correction is removed) confirms that the mechanism is specifically about within-step structure, not overall verbosity.

**Heuristic baseline comparison (Appendix D.2, Table 9).** On LIMO-v2 with Qwen3-4B-Base, ASLEC-CASL is compared against:
- Uniform random sampling: 24.16% (AIME25), 73.20% (MATH500)
- High Difficulty (longest responses): 26.66% (AIME25), 77.80% (MATH500)
- Low Difficulty (shortest responses): 20.83% (AIME25), 71.20% (MATH500)
- ASLEC-CASL: 30.83% (AIME25), 80.00% (MATH500)

ASLEC-CASL outperforms all heuristic baselines. Notably, High Difficulty (selecting the longest responses) outperforms Low Difficulty (selecting the shortest) by 5.83 points on AIME25 and 6.60 points on MATH500, confirming prior findings that longer reasoning data can be beneficial — but ASLEC-CASL's 4.17-point advantage over High Difficulty on AIME25 shows that it selects responses that are not merely long, but structurally better (fewer confound-inflated verbose steps, more genuine reasoning content).

**Different target model family (Appendix D.3, Table 10).** To test generalizability beyond the Qwen family, Llama3-3B is trained on data selected by GRACE vs. ASLEC-CASL from LIMO-v2. ASLEC-CASL achieves 33.33% on AIME25 (vs. 26.66% for GRACE, +6.67 points) and 84.40% on MATH500 (vs. 76.20% for GRACE, +8.20 points). The gains are consistent with those observed on Qwen models, suggesting that the debiasing benefits are not specific to a particular model architecture or training distribution.

**Convergence analysis (Appendix D.1, Figure 9).** Training loss curves are compared for GRACE, Random, and ASLEC-CASL on Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base. ASLEC-CASL consistently converges to a lower final loss than both baselines, confirming that the selected data is not only higher quality as measured by downstream accuracy but also genuinely "more natural" (lower perplexity under the model) after removing the confound — i.e., ASLEC-CASL selects data to which the model is better adapted.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: Step length confounding exists and is caused by low-probability first tokens

**Supported, with strong mechanistic evidence but incomplete causal proof.**

The paper provides compelling correlational evidence: Figure 1 shows that all four naturalness-based methods select for longer steps; Figure 2 shows the monotonic relationship between step length and log probability; Figure 3 shows concrete examples of low-probability first tokens. The mechanism (first-token dilution in longer steps) is logically coherent and consistent with all observed data.

However, the evidence is observational rather than interventional. The paper does not, for example, synthetically manipulate first-token probabilities (e.g., by replacing first tokens with high-probability alternatives and observing whether the confounding disappears) or manipulate step lengths independently of content (e.g., by inserting padding tokens and checking whether scores increase). The primary interventional evidence is that ASLEC-DROP (which removes first tokens) mitigates the confound (Figure 4), and that ASLEC-CASL (which adjusts for first-token ratio) also mitigates it. This is consistent with the proposed mechanism but does not rule out alternative mechanisms that also correlate with first-token presence — for instance, if first tokens tend to co-occur with other low-probability structural elements (punctuation, transition phrases) that are also diluted in longer steps.

The regression analysis partially addresses this: if there were additional confounders beyond Z_i, the residuals ε would be larger and ASLEC-CASL (which only corrects for Z_i) would leave residual confounding. The small residuals in Table 5 suggest that Z_i accounts for most of the confound, but this is only true under the linear model assumption — nonlinear confounds would not necessarily appear as large residuals in a linear fit.

**Missing experiment:** An intervention where first-token probabilities are artificially raised (e.g., by constraining the model's vocabulary at step boundaries) and the selection bias is re-measured would provide direct causal evidence. Alternatively, a control where non-first tokens at random positions are dropped instead of first tokens would test whether the effect is specific to step-initial positions.

#### Claim 2: ASLEC-DROP and ASLEC-CASL consistently outperform naturalness-based selection

**Strongly supported, with comprehensive model and benchmark coverage.**

The evidence is robust across:
- Four target LLMs of different sizes and types (4B base, 8B base, 4B instruct, 7B instruct) — Table 1, Table 3.
- Two datasets of different scales (800 problems with 16K responses, 10K problems with 40K responses) — Table 1 vs. Table 3.
- Five evaluation benchmarks covering math and science — Tables 1, 2, 3.
- Four source LLM families for data generation.
- Two baseline selection methods (GRACE, Local LP) and three heuristic baselines (Uniform, High Difficulty, Low Difficulty) in Appendix D.2.

The gains are substantial in magnitude (+6.28% to +9.08% average over Local LP) and consistent in direction (ASLEC outperforms baselines in every reported comparison). The GPQA results (Table 2) demonstrate out-of-domain transfer, and the Llama3-3B results (Table 10) demonstrate cross-architecture generalization.

**Caveat on statistical significance.** The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard deviations across random seeds, or any formal statistical test. All results are single-point estimates from single training runs. The consistency across benchmarks and models provides informal confidence, but in the absence of error bars, a reader cannot assess whether a 2–3 point difference (as seen in some of the smaller gains) is statistically reliable or within the noise of training stochasticity. Given the small evaluation sets (AIME24 and AIME25 have 30 and ~24 problems respectively, making per-benchmark accuracy granular to 3–4 percentage points), some of the per-benchmark gains may be driven by a handful of problems.

**Caveat on the Local LP implementation.** Local LP was designed for a context window of k=4 previous steps. The paper implements this faithfully, but the choice of k=4 is from Local LP's original paper and may not be optimal for reasoning data. An ablation over k values for Local LP would strengthen the comparison — it's possible that a different k would reduce the step-length confounding in Local LP without requiring ASLEC's explicit correction.

#### Claim 3: ASLEC-CASL's regression removes only the confound while preserving quality signal

**Supported, but with an important interpretive nuance.**

Table 4 shows that max CASL (selecting high s_casl) consistently beats min CASL (selecting low s_casl), confirming that the debiased score preserves the quality gradient. This is the right test: if s_casl had over-corrected and removed all first-token information including genuine quality signal, then the ranking would be essentially random and max vs. min selection would perform similarly.

However, the paper does not report what the selected data *looks like* under max vs. min CASL in terms of step-length distributions, source LLM composition, or qualitative features. We know from Figure 4 that the overall selection (max CASL) produces balanced step lengths, but we don't know whether min CASL selects for extremely short steps (over-correction in the opposite direction) or for some other feature entirely. The fact that min CASL underperforms max CASL could simply mean that min CASL selects data that is *too* different from the model's natural distribution (excessively short steps, overly terse reasoning), not that the ranking is incorrect per se.

**Missing analysis:** The paper does not report the correlation between s_logp and s_casl rankings — how much re-ranking does the debiasing actually produce? If the rankings are highly correlated (Spearman's ρ > 0.9), then the selection subsets would be mostly overlapping and the large performance gains would be surprising, implying that small changes at the selection boundary matter disproportionately. If the rankings are substantially different (ρ < 0.7), then ASLEC is selecting a meaningfully different set of responses, and the gains are more expected. This analysis would help interpret the mechanism: is ASLEC fixing a small number of egregiously misranked responses, or is it systematically reweighting the entire distribution?

#### Claim 4: Step length confounding is the dominant bias, not overall response length

**Strongly supported, with quantitative dissociation.**

The regression analysis in Appendix A.3 (Tables 6–7) is thorough and convincing: γ₂ (total length coefficient) is ~100× smaller than γ (step-length confound coefficient), and removing γ₂|o_i| from the debiasing formula has negligible performance impact (Table 7). The power of this analysis is that it pits the two explanations against each other in a single regression framework and lets the data decide which matters more.

**Minor concern:** The regression in Table 6 uses a different specification from Table 5 (adding the γ₂|o_i| term), and the γ values change substantially (e.g., for QwQ-32B, γ goes from −0.284 in Table 5 to −0.062 in Table 6). The paper does not explain this change. It may be a reporting artifact (different scaling of variables) or a genuine change in the estimated confound when total length is included. If the latter, it would imply that step-length and total-length confounds are correlated (longer responses tend to have different step-length distributions), and the regression is apportioning the shared variance differently depending on which variables are included. This doesn't undermine the conclusion (γ₂ is still negligible), but it complicates the clean "step-length is the only confound" narrative.

#### Claim 5: The method is effective in low-resource settings

**Supported directionally, but "low-resource" is ambiguously defined.**

The paper claims that gains are larger on LIMO-v2 (800 problems, 4K selected) than on AceReason-1.1-SFT (10K problems, 10K selected), and interprets this as evidence that ASLEC is "strongly suitable for low-resource SFT scenarios." This is plausible — in smaller datasets, each misselected response has a larger impact on the training distribution. However, the two datasets differ in more than size: LIMO-v2 is carefully curated (800 high-quality problems), while AceReason-1.1-SFT is a random 10K sample from a larger aggregated dataset. The quality and difficulty distributions of the underlying problems likely differ, and the number of responses per problem differs (5 per source for LIMO-v2 vs. 1 for AceReason-1.1-SFT), creating different selection dynamics. Attributing the gain difference solely to dataset size is an over-interpretation.

**Missing experiment:** A scaling analysis where the same dataset is subsampled to different sizes (e.g., 500, 1K, 2K, 4K, 8K problems) and ASLEC vs. baselines are compared at each size would provide direct evidence for the low-resource claim. The current comparison (800 vs. 10K problems) is only two datapoints and confounds size with other dataset properties.

#### Genuine Weaknesses

**Single training run per configuration.** All results are from single SFT runs with no reported error bars. Given that SFT on small datasets can be sensitive to random seed, data ordering, and initialization, the reported point estimates may have non-trivial variance. This is particularly relevant for the per-benchmark results on small evaluation sets like AIME24 (30 problems) where each problem is worth ~3.3 percentage points.

**Difficulty estimation cost is unaccounted for, but for a different reason than in prior work.** Unlike the reference example paper (which had expensive difficulty estimation via 2048 samples per question), ASLEC's additional cost is modest (seconds for regression fitting). The unaccounted cost here is the probability extraction: running the target LLM in inference mode on 16K–40K full-length reasoning responses (each up to 64K tokens) is computationally non-trivial, though it is a one-time cost. This is inherent to all naturalness-based methods and not specific to ASLEC, but it means the selection pipeline is only practical when the target model is available for inference before training begins.

**No comparison to non-naturalness selection methods beyond heuristic baselines.** The paper compares against GRACE, Local LP (both naturalness-based), and simple heuristics (Uniform, High/Low Difficulty). It does not compare against other principled selection methods that are not naturalness-based, such as:
- Difficulty-based selection using external verifiers or LLM-as-judge (Muennighoff et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025)
- Diversity-based selection (Jung et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a)
- Correctness-filtered selection with answer verification only (a common baseline that simply takes all correct responses)

It is possible that a simple "select the first correct response per problem" baseline would perform competitively, since all responses in the pool are already verified as correct. The value of additional selection among correct responses (which is what ASLEC does) would be clearer if compared against a no-further-selection baseline that just trains on all correct responses.

**Limited exploration of the regression specification.** The linear model with three predictors (s_first, s_drop, Z_i) is simple and well-motivated, but the paper does not explore whether:
- Interaction terms (e.g., Z_i × s_first) improve fit or debiasing.
- A nonlinear model (e.g., using log(Z_i) instead of Z_i) captures the dilution effect more accurately (since the dilution is arithmetic in 1/n, not linear in Z_i).
- Regularization (ridge, lasso) affects coefficient estimates for the small per-source regressions.
- The regression should be fit per source LLM rather than on pooled data, given the large variation in γ across sources (from −0.226 to −1.284 in Table 5). Using per-source γ values for selection within each source's responses might improve performance by accounting for source-specific confound strengths. The paper's overall fit uses a single γ = −0.680, which is an average that may under-correct for gpt-oss-120b (γ = −1.284) and over-correct for DS-Qwen-32B (γ = −0.226).

**The step-splitting dependence.** The confound strength depends on the step-splitting method (Appendix C.2). Period-based splitting produces the strongest confounding, but the paper does not justify why this splitting method is "correct" beyond producing the clearest signal. If step boundaries are defined differently, some responses would have their first-token counts change, altering Z_i and consequently the ASLEC-CASL scores. The method's robustness to splitting choice is only partially demonstrated (Figure 8 shows confound exists under all methods, but doesn't show whether ASLEC-CASL's performance gains hold under alternative splitting).

**Evaluation benchmarks are all reasoning benchmarks with verifiable answers.** This is by design (the paper focuses on long CoT reasoning SFT), but it means the findings' generalizability to other domains (code generation, creative writing, dialogue) with different structural properties is unproven. The GPQA transfer result (Table 2) is encouraging but still within the reasoning domain.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Strengthen the Paper

- **Scaling with dataset size:** Train on 500, 1K, 2K, 4K, 8K, 16K selected responses and compare ASLEC vs. baselines at each size. This would directly test the low-resource claim and reveal whether the advantage diminishes with more data.
- **Per-source regression:** Fit separate γ values for each source LLM and apply source-specific debiasing. Compare against the pooled regression to test whether source-adaptive correction improves performance.
- **Confidence intervals / multi-seed experiments:** Report standard deviations across 3–5 training runs with different random seeds for at least one target model to establish whether the reported gains exceed training noise.
- **Correctness-only baseline:** Include a baseline that selects all correct responses without further filtering (for LIMO-v2, this would be all 16K responses) to establish the value of selection among correct responses specifically.
- **Qualitative analysis of selected data:** Show representative examples of responses selected by GRACE/Local LP vs. ASLEC-CASL to illustrate what kinds of reasoning patterns are being promoted or demoted. The paper shows step-length distributions (Figures 1, 4) but does not show the actual reasoning content of selected vs. unselected responses.
- **Iterative selection:** Apply ASLEC-CASL, train the model, re-extract probabilities from the fine-tuned model, re-select, and re-train — testing whether the debiasing is stable under distribution shift (since fine-tuning changes the model's probability estimates). This would connect to the self-improvement loop question raised in the Limitations.
- **Ablation on k in Local LP:** Test Local LP with different context windows (k=0, 1, 2, 4, 8, ∞) to determine whether a different k mitigates the step-length confounding without explicit first-token correction — potentially Local LP with k=0 (no step context) or k=∞ (full preceding context) would behave differently.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Method Does Not Address Hard Problems Where Step-Level Reasoning Is Already Poor

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire pipeline — from data generation through selection to fine-tuning and evaluation — operates on reasoning problems where the source LLMs can produce correct answers. All selected data is verified for answer correctness via Math-Verify before selection occurs (Appendix B.2: "Sampling continues until the required number of correct responses is obtained"). This means the method is selecting *among correct reasoning trajectories*, not discriminating between correct and incorrect ones. The paper implicitly assumes that the base models being fine-tuned have sufficient capability to benefit from better-structured correct reasoning examples.

**The consequence.** The method provides no guidance for problems that are genuinely hard — where even strong source LLMs struggle to produce any correct reasoning trajectory, or where the correct trajectories that do exist are all of poor structural quality. In such regimes, the step-length confound may be irrelevant because there are no high-quality examples to select, with or without debiasing. The method cannot create reasoning capability where none exists; it can only help avoid training on verbose-but-shallow correct solutions when concise-but-deep correct solutions are available in the pool. This is analogous to the finding in the reference example paper that test-time compute provides essentially zero benefit on the hardest problems (difficulty bin 5) — the capability must exist in the proposal distribution for selection to help. The paper does not evaluate on a benchmark stratified by problem difficulty, so the performance of ASLEC on problems where source LLMs barely succeed is unknown.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not report results broken out by problem difficulty. The evaluation benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, MATH500, OlympiadBench) span a range of difficulty levels, but the aggregate accuracy numbers average across easy and hard problems alike. There is no analysis showing whether ASLEC's gains are concentrated on easy-to-medium problems (where many correct trajectories exist and selection matters most) or whether they persist on the hardest problems (where few correct trajectories exist and selection may be moot). The observation that gains are larger on LIMO-v2 (smaller, curated dataset) than on AceReason-1.1-SFT (larger, randomly sampled dataset) hints that selection quality matters more when data is scarce (Section 4.1), but this is about dataset size, not problem difficulty per se.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It is not flagged in the Limitations section. The discussion in Section 8 acknowledges that "whether there are deeper confounding factors remains an open question," but the scope of the method's applicability across difficulty levels is not examined. Future work would need to evaluate ASLEC on a difficulty-stratified benchmark to establish the boundary conditions for when debiased selection provides gains versus when selection among correct responses is simply not the bottleneck.

---

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Effectively Infinite for Deployment, and the Paper Does Not Account for It

**The assumption or constraint.** ASLEC-DROP and ASLEC-CASL require extracting token-level log probabilities from the target LLM on every candidate response *before* selection and training. For LIMO-v2, this means running Qwen3-4B-Base (or the relevant target model) in inference mode on 16,000 full-length reasoning trajectories, each up to 64K tokens. The paper treats this as a one-time preprocessing cost and does not include it in any efficiency comparison. Section 4.5 states that ASLEC-DROP "avoids any additional computational overhead" compared to GRACE, and that ASLEC-CASL's regression fitting "imposes negligible cost on the overall pipeline." These statements are true *relative to GRACE* — both methods require the same probability extraction step — but they elide the absolute cost of that extraction, which is the dominant computational expense in the selection pipeline.

**The consequence.** For a practitioner deciding whether to deploy naturalness-based data selection (with or without ASLEC), the hidden cost is substantial: they must run their target model (which they are about to train) in inference mode on the entire candidate pool — tens of thousands of long sequences — before training even begins. For a 4B-parameter model on 16,000 sequences of average length ~10K tokens each, this is roughly 160 million tokens of inference, or approximately 320 million forward-pass FLOPs per token × 160M tokens ≈ 5 × 10^16 FLOPs for probability extraction alone. This dwarfs the cost of fitting the 3-parameter regression (milliseconds) and is comparable to the cost of the SFT training itself (6 epochs on 4K selected sequences of similar length). The paper's headline claim that ASLEC-DROP adds "zero additional computational overhead" is technically correct but practically misleading: the overhead that matters is the probability extraction shared by all naturalness-based methods, not the negligible difference between GRACE and ASLEC.

The paper also does not address whether this one-time cost could be amortized. If a practitioner wants to experiment with different selection criteria (e.g., comparing GRACE, Local LP, and ASLEC), they must extract probabilities once and can reuse them. But if they want to iterate — generate new candidate data, select, train, evaluate, and repeat — the probability extraction cost is incurred on every iteration. For the self-improvement loops discussed in Section 5, where the model generates its own candidates, is fine-tuned, and then the process repeats, the extraction cost would multiply with each iteration.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not report the wall-clock time, GPU-hours, or FLOP count for the probability extraction step. Section 4.5 ("Computation Budget") discusses only the relative costs of GRACE vs. ASLEC-DROP vs. ASLEC-CASL, not the absolute cost of the pipeline shared by all methods. Appendix B.2 mentions that SGLang is used "to sample log probabilities from the target LLM for the data" and that for Min Entropy, only the top 20 tokens are retained to manage "computational and storage-intensive" requirements, but no cost figures are provided. The total number of responses in each dataset (16K for LIMO-v2, 40K for AceReason-1.1-SFT) and the maximum sequence length (64K tokens) are reported, but average and total inference token counts are not.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. It is not mentioned in the Limitations section. The framing in Section 4.5 focuses narrowly on the incremental cost of ASLEC variants relative to GRACE, which is indeed negligible, but this framing obscures the substantial absolute cost that limits the deployability of *any* naturalness-based selection method at scale. Future work would need to either justify that the downstream training accuracy gains outweigh the selection cost, or develop cheaper probability extraction methods (e.g., using a smaller proxy model for scoring, or extracting probabilities from only a subset of tokens).

---

### Single Training Run per Configuration with No Statistical Reliability Assessment

**The assumption or constraint.** Every result in Tables 1–4 and Appendix D is reported as a single point estimate from a single SFT training run. The paper does not report standard deviations, confidence intervals, or results from multiple random seeds for any experiment. The evaluation benchmarks vary in size: AIME24 has 30 problems, AIME25 has approximately 24 problems, MATH500 has 500 problems, OlympiadBench size is not explicitly stated but is likely in the hundreds, and GPQA size is not stated. On the smaller benchmarks, each problem is worth 3–4 percentage points of accuracy, meaning that a difference of 2–3 correct answers between runs (well within typical SFT training variance) could produce the magnitude of gain reported for some method-benchmark comparisons.

**The consequence.** Without variance estimates, a reader cannot distinguish between genuine method improvements and noise from training stochasticity (random seed, data ordering, initialization), particularly for the smaller-gain comparisons. For example, in Table 1, ASLEC-DROP on Qwen3-4B-Base shows a gain of 4.27 percentage points over Local LP on average across four benchmarks — but the per-benchmark gains are individually modest on the smaller evaluation sets: +7.50 on AIME25 (from 20.83% to 28.33%, roughly +1.8 problems correct out of 24) and +6.20 on MATH500 (from 71.60% to 77.80%, roughly +31 problems out of 500). The MATH500 gain is almost certainly significant given 500 test problems; the AIME25 gain is less certain given only ~24 problems.

This matters because the paper's headline numbers (6.28% and 9.08% average gains over Local LP) are averages across four models and four benchmarks with different test set sizes, some of which may be dominated by the larger benchmarks (MATH500) where even small percentage differences are reliable, while others may reflect high-variance estimates that happen to favor ASLEC in the single observed run. A practitioner trying to decide whether the method's gains justify its implementation complexity would want to know the lower bound of expected improvement, not just the point estimate from one favorable run.

The problem is compounded by the one-run-per-configuration design: each method produces a different selected subset, which is used for a single training run. There is no within-method replication (e.g., 3 runs with different random seeds on the same selected subset) or across-subset replication (e.g., multiple selection subsets produced by the same scoring method with tie-breaking variation). This means the variance has two sources — selection stochasticity and training stochasticity — and neither is measured.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** No statistical reliability measures are reported anywhere in the paper. The word "standard deviation," "confidence," "variance," and "seed" do not appear in the main text or appendices. The convergence analysis in Appendix D.1 (Figure 9) shows training loss curves but only for one run per method. The consistency of results across four models, two datasets, and five benchmarks provides informal evidence that the gains are real (it is unlikely that random noise would favor ASLEC in every single comparison), but the magnitude of the gains — particularly the difference between ASLEC-DROP and ASLEC-CASL (typically 2–4 percentage points) — could be partly attributable to noise in individual runs.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this limitation. Multi-seed experiments or confidence intervals are not suggested as future work. Given the computational cost of SFT training (6 epochs on 4K–10K long-form reasoning sequences), running 3–5 seeds per configuration would be a non-trivial expense, but even a single multi-seed experiment on one target model (e.g., Qwen3-4B-Base on LIMO-v2) would significantly strengthen the reliability claims.

---

### The Regression Approach Assumes a Linear Confound and Does Not Test Alternative Specifications

**The assumption or constraint.** ASLEC-CASL models the confound through a linear regression with three predictors (first-token probability, non-first-token probability, first-token ratio) and subtracts the estimated linear effect `γZ_i` from the raw log probability score. The paper validates this choice by noting that residuals are small (Table 5: `ε` ranges from −0.054 to +0.028) and that the debiased score preserves the quality gradient (Table 4: max CASL outperforms min CASL). However, the small residuals only confirm that the linear model fits the data well in a *descriptive* sense — they do not confirm that the debiasing is *optimal* or that a different specification would not perform better.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the regression design are unexamined, and each could affect the quality of debiasing:

1. **Pooled vs. per-source regression.** Table 5 shows that `γ` varies substantially across source LLMs (from −0.226 for DS-Qwen-32B to −1.284 for gpt-oss-120b). The paper's experiments use the "Overall" fit with `γ = −0.680`, which is an average across sources. This means that data from gpt-oss-120b (which suffers from the strongest confound) is *under-corrected* — the debiasing subtracts only about half of the confound's actual effect — while data from DS-Qwen-32B (weakest confound) is *over-corrected* by roughly 3×. The net effect on selection quality depends on the distribution of responses across sources and whether mis-correction systematically harms certain source LLMs' data. A per-source regression (fitting separate `γ` per source LLM and applying source-specific debiasing) would avoid this issue, but is not tested.

2. **Functional form.** The dilution effect is arithmetic: a step of length `n` has its average log probability inflated by approximately `(low_first_token_prob) / n` because the first token's influence is diluted across `n` tokens. This is a `1/n` relationship, not a linear relationship in `Z_i = 1/n` (which is the first-token ratio). The linear model treats each percentage-point change in `Z_i` as having a constant effect on `s_logp`, but the actual dilution effect is nonlinear — going from step length 5 to step length 10 (a `Z_i` change of 0.10) produces a much larger dilution benefit than going from step length 95 to step length 100 (a `Z_i` change of ~0.0005). The linear approximation may be adequate over the range of `Z_i` observed in the data, but this is not verified.

3. **Interaction terms.** The regression in Equation 5 assumes that the confound's effect is additive and independent of the quality components `s_first` and `s_drop`. But if lower-quality first tokens (more negative `s_first`) produce a stronger dilution benefit when they appear in longer steps, then the confound's magnitude would depend on `s_first` — an interaction effect that the linear additive model would miss. Including a `Z_i × s_first` interaction term would test this, but the paper does not explore it.

4. **Robustness to outliers.** OLS regression is sensitive to outliers. The 16,000-response pool likely contains some responses with unusual step structures (extremely long or short steps, very high or low first-token probabilities) that could influence the fitted `γ`. A robust regression (Huber loss, or trimming extreme `Z_i` values) might produce different coefficients, but this is not tested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The small residuals in Table 5 provide some reassurance that the linear model captures the dominant relationship. The consistent outperformance of ASLEC-CASL over ASLEC-DROP (Tables 1, 3) suggests that the regression-based debiasing is doing something genuinely useful beyond simply dropping first tokens. However, these are indirect validations — they show the method works, not that the specific modeling choices are optimal.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss alternative regression specifications or acknowledge the potential suboptimality of the pooled regression given the cross-source variation in `γ`. The Limitations section asks "whether there are deeper confounding factors" as an open question but does not address specification choices for the existing confound model. The regression is presented as a single, fixed design with no sensitivity analysis. A practitioner implementing ASLEC-CASL would need to decide (without guidance from the paper) whether to use pooled or per-source regression, whether to include interaction terms, and whether to validate the linearity assumption on their own data.

---

### The Method Has Only Been Validated on Mathematical and Scientific Reasoning with Qwen-Series Models

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use mathematical reasoning datasets (LIMO-v2 and AceReason-1.1-SFT consist exclusively of math problems), four source LLMs (all large, reasoning-specialized models in the 32B–120B range), and target LLMs that are predominantly from the Qwen family (Qwen3-4B-Base, Qwen3-8B-Base, Qwen3-4B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct — three Qwen models and one Qwen-derived model). The sole non-Qwen evaluation is a single experiment in Appendix D.3 (Table 10) where Llama3-3B is trained on LIMO-v2 data selected by GRACE vs. ASLEC-CASL, showing consistent gains (AIME25: 33.33% vs. 26.66%, MATH500: 84.40% vs. 76.20%).

**The consequence.** The generalizability of both the diagnostic finding (step length confounding exists) and the corrective method (ASLEC mitigates it) to other domains, model families, and dataset scales is unproven. Several specific concerns:

1. **Domain specificity of the confound.** Mathematical reasoning has a distinctive structure: steps often correspond to discrete algebraic manipulations, theorem applications, or numerical calculations, with clear decision points at step boundaries. In other reasoning domains — code generation (where steps are lines or blocks of code), legal reasoning (where steps are case citations or statutory interpretations), or creative writing (where steps are narrative developments) — the entropy at step boundaries may differ substantially in magnitude and pattern. If step boundaries in code generation typically begin with low-entropy keywords (`def`, `import`, `return`), the first-token confound may be much weaker, and ASLEC may provide little or no benefit. Conversely, if domain-specific confounds exist (e.g., indentation level correlating with log probability in code), ASLEC's first-token correction would not address them.

2. **Model family specificity.** The Qwen model family shares architectural features, training data distributions, and tokenization schemes that affect how log probabilities distribute across tokens. The low-probability first-token phenomenon has been documented in other model families (Wang et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025), suggesting it is not Qwen-specific. However, the *magnitude* of the confound — and hence the benefit of correcting it — likely depends on the target model's probability calibration. A model with flatter probability distributions (higher overall entropy) would have less variance between first-token and continuation-token probabilities, potentially reducing the confound's impact. The paper provides no evidence on how ASLEC performs with non-Qwen target models beyond the single Llama3-3B experiment.

3. **Scale specificity.** The paper's target models are in the 3B–8B parameter range. It is unknown whether step-length confounding persists as a problem for larger models (e.g., fine-tuning a 70B model on selected reasoning data) or smaller models (e.g., 1B parameters). Larger models may have more calibrated probabilities, reducing the first-token entropy gap; smaller models may have noisier probabilities, potentially amplifying the confound or making the regression estimates of `γ` less stable.

4. **Dataset scale specificity.** The experiments use 800 problems (LIMO-v2) or 10K problems (AceReason-1.1-SFT). At much larger scales (100K+ problems, millions of responses), the selection problem may change qualitatively — with abundant data, simple heuristics (e.g., selecting the longest correct response per problem) may catch up to or surpass debiased naturalness-based selection, because the cost of including some verbose-but-shallow responses is amortized across a larger training set. The paper's claim that ASLEC is "strongly suitable for low-resource SFT scenarios" (Section 4.1) implicitly acknowledges that the benefit may shrink with scale, but this is not tested beyond the LIMO-v2 vs. AceReason-1.1-SFT comparison (which confounds dataset size with curation quality).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The single Llama3-3B experiment (Appendix D.3, Table 10) provides limited cross-architecture evidence and is encouraging — ASLEC-CASL achieves +6.67 points on AIME25 and +8.20 points on MATH500 over GRACE, consistent with the Qwen results. The GPQA evaluation (Table 2) provides limited cross-domain evidence, showing that models trained on math-only data with ASLEC selection generalize better to scientific reasoning — but GPQA is still a reasoning benchmark, not a fundamentally different domain. The paper does not evaluate on code generation, natural language inference, summarization, or any non-reasoning task.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim broad generalizability and does not overstate its domain coverage. The title specifies "LLM Reasoning Data Selection," and the introduction frames the problem in terms of "long chain-of-thought reasoning." The Limitations section does not explicitly address the domain/model restrictions, but the scope is appropriately bounded by the paper's framing. The one concern is that the abstract's language ("extensive experiments on two reasoning SFT datasets, across four LLMs and five evaluation benchmarks") could be read as claiming broader validation than is actually provided — "four LLMs" refers to source LLMs, not diversity of target model architectures, and "five evaluation benchmarks" are all reasoning benchmarks. A practitioner working on code generation or dialogue data selection should be cautious about assuming the confound or the correction transfer without validation.

---

### Selection Is Static and One-Shot, with No Mechanism for Curriculum or Iterative Refinement

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's selection pipeline is entirely static: probabilities are extracted once from the pre-trained target LLM, a fixed subset of responses is selected, and the model is trained on that subset for all 6 epochs with no further data re-ranking or curriculum adjustment. This is a one-shot selection-then-training paradigm. The paper does not explore whether the selection should adapt as training progresses — for instance, starting with higher-probability (presumably easier) examples in early epochs and introducing lower-probability (harder) examples later, or periodically re-extracting probabilities from the partially-trained model to re-rank remaining data.

**The consequence.** Two related failure modes arise from the static selection assumption:

1. **Distribution shift during training.** The probabilities used for selection come from the *pre-trained* target LLM. After even one epoch of SFT on the selected data, the model's probability distribution shifts — it becomes more confident on patterns present in the training data and potentially less confident on patterns that were excluded. The selected subset that was optimal for the pre-trained model may not be optimal for the partially-trained model. Some responses that were low-probability (and thus excluded) under the pre-trained model might become high-probability after the model learns foundational reasoning patterns from the initially selected data. The static pipeline cannot exploit this — it commits to a fixed training set before seeing how the model evolves.

2. **No curriculum structure.** All selected responses are treated equally and trained on for the same number of epochs in randomized order. The paper does not investigate whether ordering the selected data by probability (easiest first) or by difficulty (hardest first) affects training outcomes. Prior work on curriculum learning for language models suggests that ordering can matter (training on easier examples first often improves final performance), and the naturalness-based selection scores provide a natural curriculum signal (higher probability = easier for the model). The paper's scoring pipeline could trivially support curriculum ordering without any additional computation, but this is not explored.

3. **No feedback loop for selection quality.** The paper evaluates selection quality only by downstream task accuracy after full training. There is no intermediate signal — during training, there is no mechanism to detect that the selected data may be suboptimal and trigger re-selection. If the static selection happens to include a cluster of verbose-but-shallow responses that ASLEC failed to deprioritize (because the linear model did not fully capture their confound), the model trains on them for 6 full epochs with no recourse.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The convergence analysis in Appendix D.1 (Figure 9) shows training loss curves that decrease smoothly for all methods, with ASLEC-CASL converging to a lower final loss than GRACE. This suggests that the static selection works — the model keeps improving on the selected data throughout training and does not overfit quickly. However, the loss curves do not reveal whether an adaptive selection strategy would achieve even lower loss or better generalization. The paper provides no comparison between static selection and any adaptive or curriculum-based variant.

The discussion in Section 5 gestures at iterative self-improvement as a related but distinct direction: "some studies have also sought to filter out noisy data by applying various heuristic rules." The paper positions its work in the context of data selection for SFT, not iterative training loops, so the static assumption is consistent with the paper's scope. However, the Limitations section does flag that "whether data selection in such approaches [on-policy data generation and selection] still exhibits a strong correlation with response length is an issue worthy of further exploration," acknowledging that the findings may not directly transfer to iterative settings.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address the static selection limitation. The fixed-subset, fixed-epoch training protocol is standard for SFT data selection research, and the paper's primary contribution (identifying and correcting the step-length confound) does not depend on selection being adaptive. However, a practitioner building a production data selection pipeline might reasonably want to combine ASLEC's debiased scoring with a curriculum schedule — and the paper provides no guidance on whether or how the two would interact. The explicit flagging of on-policy selection as an open question in the Limitations section is a partial acknowledgment, but it frames the issue as about on-policy generation (the model generating its own candidates) rather than about adaptive re-ranking of existing candidates during training.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the conversation around naturalness-based data selection from an assumption of transparency to an awareness of structural confounding. Prior to this work, the field treated a model's own log probabilities as a relatively clean window into data quality — higher probability meant better alignment, and selection methods differed primarily in how they aggregated probabilities (global vs. local, log-probability vs. entropy). The implicit model was: **probability → quality**, with the arrow assumed to be a simple, monotonic relationship. This paper demonstrates that the arrow passes through a hidden variable — step length — that systematically distorts the relationship in a predictable direction (longer steps are overrated, shorter steps are underrated) and by a quantitatively large margin (the step-length confound dominates overall response-length effects by approximately two orders of magnitude; Appendix A.3, Table 6).

**The magnitude of the shift is diagnostic rather than paradigmatic.** This is not a paper that overturns naturalness-based selection — on the contrary, Section 4.3 (Table 4) explicitly confirms that higher probability (after debiasing) still correlates with better training outcomes. Rather, it is a paper that identifies a **specific, previously invisible failure mode** and provides both a mechanistic explanation (low-probability first tokens diluted in longer steps; Section 2.4, Figures 2–3) and a corrective framework (drop first tokens or regression-adjust for their ratio; Section 3). The conceptual contribution is not "naturalness-based selection is wrong" but rather **"naturalness-based selection conflates two signals — genuine quality and step-verbosity — and the confound can be removed by attending to step boundaries."**

**Reconciliation of conflicting intuitions.** The paper resolves a tension that had been latent in the reasoning-data literature but not explicitly articulated. On one hand, several works (Chen et al., 2025b; Guha et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2024) found that **longer overall reasoning responses** are beneficial for SFT — they contain more thorough reasoning, more verification, better-structured logical chains. This suggests that selection methods should prefer longer responses. On the other hand, this paper finds that naturalness-based methods — which are supposed to identify high-quality data — are biased toward **shorter overall responses** (Appendix A.1: selected average ~9.8K tokens vs. unselected ~15.4K tokens) while simultaneously preferring **longer individual steps** within those responses. The resolution is that these are two different structural axes: a response can be long because it has many steps (granular, well-structured reasoning) or because it has a few verbose steps (monolithic, less structured reasoning). Naturalness-based methods, uncorrected, prefer the latter — not because it is better, but because the low-probability first tokens are diluted in long steps. This explains why prior work could simultaneously find that longer responses help (when "longer" means more steps) while naturalness-based methods systematically select *shorter* responses (because they select for fewer, longer steps). ASLEC corrects the step-length axis without affecting the overall-length axis (Appendix A.3 shows that total-length correction is negligible and can be omitted; Table 7).

**Research directions that become more attractive.** The paper makes **structural analysis of selection metrics** a first-class research activity. Prior to this work, a researcher evaluating a new data selection method would report downstream accuracy and perhaps analyze selected-data statistics like average length. After this work, a rigorous evaluation should include a **confound analysis**: what hidden variables correlate with the selection score, are those correlations spurious or causal, and can they be removed through targeted intervention? The paper's causal regression framework (Equations 5–10) provides a template: identify a suspected confound, formalize it as a measurable variable, fit a regression to estimate its effect, subtract that effect, and verify that downstream performance improves while the confound's distributional signature disappears (as in Figure 4). This template is portable to other selection metrics, other domains, and other suspected confounds.

**Research directions that become less attractive.** The finding that lookahead-style sophistication does not reliably beat simpler corrections (ASLEC-DROP, which is almost trivial to implement, captures roughly 70% of the total gain that the more complex ASLEC-CASL achieves — Section 4.1 reports 6.28% vs. 9.08% average improvement over Local LP) suggests that for this particular confound, **simple structural intervention dominates complex modeling.** Future work on data selection should prioritize identifying *what* to correct for (the confound structure) over *how* to correct for it (the modeling sophistication), at least until the confound landscape is better mapped. More broadly, the paper demonstrates that **evaluation-driven architecture decisions** (splitting by periods rather than newlines; Appendix C.2, Figure 8) can have outsized effects on method performance — a reminder that the engineering details of how we define "steps" matter as much as the mathematical framework we apply to them.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. Per-source adaptive debiasing with source-specific `γ` coefficients.** The paper's Table 5 reveals that the confound strength `γ` varies dramatically across source LLMs — from `−0.226` (DS-Qwen-32B, weakest confound) to `−1.284` (gpt-oss-120b, strongest confound, approximately 5.7× larger). Yet the paper's experiments use a single "Overall" `γ = −0.680` for all data, which under-corrects gpt-oss-120b responses and over-corrects DS-Qwen-32B responses. A natural follow-up would fit separate regressions per source LLM and apply source-specific debiasing: `s_i^{casl} = s_i^{logp} − γ_s Z_i` where `γ_s` is the coefficient for the source that generated response `i`. This should improve selection quality by matching the correction to the confound strength in each source's data distribution. The experiment is straightforward: on LIMO-v2, fit four separate regressions (one per source), re-compute selection scores, train Qwen3-4B-Base, and compare against the pooled-regression results in Table 1. The hypothesis is that per-source debiasing would particularly improve selection from gpt-oss-120b (whose responses are currently under-corrected and thus still advantaged by long steps) and DS-Qwen-32B (whose responses are over-corrected and thus potentially penalized for step structure that is not actually confounded). A strong positive result would establish that confound-aware selection should be **source-calibrated**, not just globally debiased.

**2. Confound diagnosis across non-mathematical reasoning domains.** The paper's entire analysis is on mathematical reasoning (MATH, AIME, OlympiadBench). The structural mechanism — low-probability first tokens at reasoning step boundaries — should in principle generalize to any domain where model outputs have step-like structure with high-entropy decision points. Obvious candidates include code generation (where "steps" could be lines, statements, or blocks, and step boundaries at `def`, `if`, `for`, `return` may or may not exhibit the same entropy pattern), legal reasoning (where steps are case citations, statutory interpretations, or argument moves), and multi-hop question answering (where steps correspond to retrieved facts or inference leaps). A systematic study would: (a) collect or construct reasoning datasets in 3–4 non-math domains, (b) replicate the Figure 1 analysis (step-length distributions for selected vs. unselected data under GRACE and Local LP) for each domain, (c) measure whether the `γ` coefficient from the ASLEC-CASL regression is substantially non-zero (indicating confound presence), (d) test whether ASLEC-DROP or ASLEC-CASL improves downstream task performance. The null result — domains where the confound is absent or negligible — would be informative: it would establish the boundary conditions for when practitioners need to worry about step-length confounding versus when naturalness-based selection is safe to use without correction.

**3. Interaction between ASLEC and curriculum learning schedules.** The paper's selection pipeline is static: fixed subset, fixed training order, all epochs. But the debiased probability scores `s_i^{casl}` provide a natural curriculum signal — higher scores indicate data the model finds more natural (after removing the confound), which typically means easier or more familiar data. This enables a direct experiment that the paper never runs: sort the selected data by `s_i^{casl}` (ascending, easiest first, or descending, hardest first) and compare training dynamics and final performance against random shuffling. The hypothesis from curriculum learning literature is that easy-first ordering should improve convergence and final performance, particularly for smaller models (4B parameters) where training stability is more sensitive to data ordering. A secondary experiment would test whether the optimal ordering differs between base and instruct models — instruct models, having already been fine-tuned on diverse instructions, may benefit less from easy-first ordering than base models that are encountering structured reasoning for the first time. The experiment uses the same selected subset as the main experiments (4K responses for LIMO-v2), varying only the training order, so the computational cost is identical to the main experiments — only the data loader changes.

**4. Iterative re-selection during training (closing the self-improvement loop).** The paper's Limitations section flags on-policy data selection as an open question: when the target model generates its own training candidates and selects among them, does the step-length confound persist, amplify, or disappear? A first step toward answering this would be: train Qwen3-4B-Base on the ASLEC-selected 4K responses for 2 epochs (partial training), then use the partially-trained model to re-extract log probabilities on the full 16K-response pool, re-compute ASLEC-CASL scores, and select a new 4K subset for the remaining 4 epochs. Compare against the static baseline (same 4K subset for all 6 epochs) from Table 1. This tests whether the selection that is optimal for the pre-trained model remains optimal after the model has begun to specialize on reasoning data. If iterative re-selection improves performance, it would motivate more sophisticated online selection pipelines where data is continuously re-ranked as training progresses. If it doesn't help (or hurts), it would suggest that the pre-trained model's probability estimates are stable enough that static selection is sufficient, simplifying deployment. This is the experiment that bridges the paper's SFT data selection contribution to the self-improvement loop discussion in Section 5.

**5. First-token probability manipulation as a causal test of the mechanism.** The paper's evidence for the first-token dilution mechanism is observational: Figures 2–3 show the correlation, and ASLEC's success (Figures 1 vs. 4) shows that intervening on first tokens mitigates the confound. But a direct causal test would manipulate first-token probabilities independently of step content and observe whether selection behavior changes as predicted. The experiment: take the 16K LIMO-v2 responses and, for each step, replace the first token's log probability with the step's average continuation-token log probability (simulating what the score would be if first tokens were not systematically lower). Re-rank responses by this modified score and measure whether the step-length distribution shift (Figure 1) disappears. If it does, this is direct causal confirmation that first-token probabilities are the mechanism, not merely a correlate. A stronger variant: instead of replacing probabilities post-hoc, constrain the target LLM's decoding at step boundaries (e.g., by masking low-probability tokens from the vocabulary at those positions) and re-extract probabilities under the constrained model, then test whether the confound magnitude `γ` decreases. This experiment is more expensive (requires modifying inference code) but provides interventional rather than observational evidence.

**6. Confound landscape: what other structural features bias naturalness scores?** The paper identifies one confound (step length via first-token dilution) and provides a general template for confound diagnosis (the regression framework). A systematic follow-up would apply this template to catalog other potential confounds in reasoning data. Candidates include: (a) **vocabulary complexity** — do responses with rarer words get systematically lower log probabilities, and does this correlate with actual reasoning quality? (b) **mathematical notation density** — do LaTeX-heavy responses score differently from natural-language-heavy ones, independent of correctness? (c) **self-correction frequency** — do responses containing "wait," "let me check," or "actually" (markers of self-verification) score differently, and is this difference a genuine quality signal or a surface confound? (d) **sentence length within steps** — is there a within-step analog of the step-length confound, where longer sentences within a step dilute low-probability sentence-initial tokens? For each candidate, the protocol is: define the confound variable `Z`, fit the extended regression `s_logp = β₁ s_first + β₂ s_drop + γ Z_step_length + γ_new Z_new + ε`, and test whether `γ_new` is substantially non-zero and whether subtracting `γ_new Z_new` improves downstream selection quality. The paper's residual analysis (Table 5, `ε` near zero) suggests that `Z_step_length` captures most of the confound in math reasoning, but this may not hold in other domains or for other model families.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**1. Curating small, high-quality SFT datasets for on-device or edge-deployed reasoning models.** The paper's strongest result is on LIMO-v2 — 800 carefully curated problems, 4K selected responses, producing substantial accuracy gains (+8.70 percentage points for ASLEC-CASL over Local LP on Qwen3-4B-Base; Table 1). This is precisely the regime where data curation matters most: when you can only afford to train on a few thousand examples (due to compute constraints, on-device fine-tuning budgets, or latency requirements for small models), getting the selection right is critical. A practitioner building a 4B-parameter math reasoning model for mobile deployment would: (1) generate a pool of 10–20K correct reasoning trajectories from strong teacher models (following the paper's multi-source, multi-response generation protocol), (2) extract log probabilities from the 4B target model on all candidates, (3) compute ASLEC-CASL scores (fitting the regression on the candidate pool itself), (4) select the top 2–5K responses, (5) fine-tune. The paper's numbers suggest this would yield a model that outperforms one trained on a Local-LP-selected subset of the same size by roughly 6–9 percentage points on competition math benchmarks. The computational overhead relative to GRACE is negligible (seconds for regression fitting, no additional inference), making this a drop-in improvement to existing naturalness-based selection pipelines.

**2. Data filtering for multi-source reasoning data aggregation.** The paper's finding (Section 4.1) that uncorrected naturalness-based methods "overly prefer samples from a single data source" has direct implications for practitioners aggregating reasoning data from multiple teacher models. If you prompt QwQ-32B, DeepSeek-R1, GPT-4, and Claude to all generate solutions to the same set of problems, and then use GRACE or Local LP to select the "best" responses, the selection will concentrate on whatever source happens to produce responses with the longest individual steps — not necessarily the source with the best reasoning. Table 5 shows this varies by source: gpt-oss-120b has `γ = −1.284` (strongest confound), meaning its responses get the largest artificial score boost from step length, making them disproportionately likely to be selected by uncorrected methods regardless of reasoning quality. ASLEC-CASL with per-source `γ` (or even the pooled `γ = −0.680`) counteracts this, producing more balanced source representation. For a practitioner building a diverse reasoning dataset, switching from Local LP to ASLEC-CASL would be a minimal-change fix (same probability extraction, different aggregation formula) that improves both diversity and downstream performance.

**3. Quality control in iterative self-improvement pipelines.** As noted in the paper's Limitations, on-policy distillation (Yang et al., 2025a; Lu and Lab, 2025) — where a model generates its own training data, selects the best examples, and re-trains — is an emerging paradigm. In such loops, the model is both the data generator and the data selector, creating a feedback cycle where any bias in the selection criterion can amplify over iterations. If the model's own probability estimates are confounded by step length, then each iteration will select for progressively longer steps (not because they're better, but because they score higher under the confounded metric), and the model will learn to produce verbatim, low-structure reasoning. ASLEC provides a correction that can be applied at each iteration of the loop: before selecting training data, fit the regression on the model's current outputs and debias the scores. The regression is cheap enough (seconds for tens of thousands of responses) to run at each iteration. This doesn't solve all self-improvement stability problems, but it removes one known source of drift. The paper does not implement this, but the building blocks are all present.

**4. Auditing and debugging data selection pipelines in production.** Even for teams that don't adopt ASLEC as their primary selection method, the paper's diagnostic framework provides a concrete auditing protocol. The key test: for your selection method, plot the step-length distribution of selected vs. unselected data (replicating Figure 1). If the distributions differ substantially, your method is confounded by step length and you are likely selecting for verbosity over reasoning quality. The fix could be ASLEC-DROP (trivial to implement — just skip first tokens when averaging), ASLEC-CASL (if you have enough data to fit a stable regression, which the paper's N=16K is more than sufficient for), or a custom correction for whatever confound your audit reveals. The paper's residual analysis (Table 5, `ε` near zero) suggests that the first-token-ratio `Z_i` is the dominant confound in mathematical reasoning, so auditing for step-length distribution shift is the single highest-value diagnostic. A team that runs this audit and finds no shift can have confidence that their selection method is not step-length-confounded; a team that finds a shift has an immediate, low-cost fix available.
