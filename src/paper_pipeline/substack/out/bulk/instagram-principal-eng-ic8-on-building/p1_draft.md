Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Olson interview (went from IC4 to Principal Engineer/IC8 at Instagram, built iOS for IG Stories and Whiteout redesign, now co-founder of Retro):

**Failing the Facebook interview**

Ryan Olson’s journey into Big Tech nearly ended before it started. In 2011, interviewing for Facebook, he was so nervous the keys literally rattled under his shaking hands. The interviewer gave him a question—one later banned at the company—about “gram buckets.” The clean dictionary-based solution didn’t come to him; he wrote a “horrendous triple nested for loop.” The interviewer cut the interview short and told him, “Look, you’re just not gonna be able to make it at Facebook,” a line Ryan calls “a knife to the heart.” He never got the interviewer’s name, but the sting lasted. Later, after a successful career at Instagram, he could reflect: “I managed to prove him wrong.” He notes he’s always struggled with Leetcode-style interviews and tried to change the process while at the company, but “it’s the interview process”—a machine too big to steer. One perk of having his own company now: “I don’t have to do those things that I don’t agree with.”

**Interning at Flipboard with future billionaires**

After failing Facebook, Ryan got an exploding offer from Amazon, but an investor friend (who founded Insight Partners) connected him on a Sunday to Evan Dahl, co-founder of Flipboard. Dahl had written `UIViewController` at Apple and taught the Stanford iOS course (CS 193P) that Ryan had used to learn development. Ryan joined Flipboard’s ~50-person team as one of four interns, in what turned out to be an absurdly talent-dense environment. The intern sitting next to him had written the push notifications framework at Apple and told stories about running WWDC demos off his own Mac Mini and shipping a catastrophic time-zone bug in the clock app that caused people’s alarms to fail on New Year’s. Two of the other interns went on to build multi-billion-dollar companies: Dylan Field (Figma) and Devin Finzer (OpenSea). Ryan noticed they were “determined to start companies” in a way his university peers weren’t; the highest aspiration for most classmates was a Big Tech job, but Dylan and Devin were constantly talking about ideas. Dylan’s early insight was that **WebGL was a new technology that would enable new things on the web**. Figma wasn’t Figma yet—Dylan later said they were “a meme generator app for a week”—but they stuck with the technology thesis until they found the use case. Ryan almost didn’t go back for his final year of school, feeling he’d “miss out on something really critical” if he left.

During his Flipboard internship, he built an internal debugging tool called FLTweaker (later open-sourced as **FLEX**), driven by his fascination with Objective-C’s runtime: you could inspect classes, methods, properties, and state dynamically while the app ran. It became a nights-and-weekends project that showed network requests, file system contents, and let you tweak app state. People inside Flipboard found it so useful he asked to open-source it, and it became one of the most popular iOS debugging tools—later used at Facebook and Instagram. Ryan hoped showing interviewers his open-source code would prove his ability, but “none of my interviewers had any interest in looking at it.” Still, an engineer named Jonathan Dan saw FLEX, got him into the interview loop, and likely advocated for him in candidate review when his loop was mixed.

**Interview nerves tip: beta blockers**

Ryan offers a concrete, unusual piece of advice for people who freeze in interviews: **beta blockers** block adrenaline, preventing the physical feedback loop where a pounding heart and sweaty palms cascade into mental panic. “I got a prescription for that for my interview—interview performance-enhancing drugs, I guess.” He’s only used them a handful of times, but says the physical nervous effects disappear almost completely, though you can still be in your head. He notes performers like comedians sometimes use the same approach.

**Early Instagram experiences**

Ryan joined Instagram as an IC4—under-leveled, he believes—on an iOS team of about 10 people. The team had recently been decimated by an internal civil war: two factions had battled over whether to adopt AsyncDisplayKit (a framework built by Scott Goodson for Facebook Paper) or stick with vanilla iOS UIKit. “These two factions fought each other, and each side destroyed each other. Everyone just left, and nobody won.” The result was a new team full of low-hanging fruit. Ryan put the app in Time Profiler and found cold-start work for the profile tab running even if users never tapped it—shaving 20% off cold start time by deferring that work. He also discovered the networking library AFNetworking had assertions turned on in production, crashing the app on benign network failures. A one-line change (`NSBlockAssertions`) cut the crash rate by 80%, earning him the **ax**: Instagram’s tradition of giving a mounted physical ax to the person who’d done the week’s highest-impact work (originating from a joke holiday gift guide where founders suggested a bespoke ax, and an investor actually sent one—Facebook security made them mount it to a plaque). Ryan was initially excited to take it home, then realized “there was just one ax.” The tradition was eventually killed as exclusionary, which saddened him as a piece of early Instagram culture swept away after the founders left.

Ryan’s playbook throughout his career: **find the amazing designer and be the engineer they want to work with**. “Your impact can be multiplied so much by finding a good designer and creating a really good working relationship with them.” He distinguishes between product engineers (pair with a designer) and infrastructure engineers (pair with a senior IC whose ideas you can implement).

**Whiteout: redesigning Instagram**

A few months in, Ryan maneuvered himself onto the Whiteout redesign project. He spent two to three months in a war room with designer Joy Vincent (“an incredible talent and the nicest guy ever,” who sadly passed away while at Instagram), passing an iPhone back and forth: Joy would sketch changes, Ryan would implement, repeat. The goal was to remove color from the chrome—taking the app’s dark blue and black heavy UI and making it the clean, white, content-first design Instagram still uses today. Mike Krieger initially told him, “Don’t worry about trying to A/B test this thing. Just ship it.” Instagram at the time was skeptical of testing. But Ryan, scarred by a nightmare merge of a long-lived Direct Messaging branch, put Whiteout behind a feature flag with semantic color abstractions so he could constantly merge. This architecture accidentally enabled testing. The night before launch, an executive from Facebook above Instagram—spooked by a previous failed Facebook redesign—blocked the launch and demanded A/B tests. Ryan was up at 1:00 AM backing things out and resubmitting. It tested well, but the launch moment was lost. The icon generated public backlash: “I went on Twitter and it was supposed to be the celebratory day… we were just getting raked.” Yet the data told a different story—the new icon materially improved daily opens because it was more visible on people’s home screens. Ryan’s nuanced take on testing: A/B tests are “extremely useful” for things like onboarding conversions where you get counterintuitive results, but “for high-level product direction and what you want the thing to be, I prefer to come in with a stronger opinion.” Too much experimentation risks **incrementalism**—“you’re never going to get that 50% jump.”

**Building Instagram Stories**

After Whiteout, Ryan briefly landed on Search and Explore, but his manager pointed him toward the Creation team that was building what would become Stories. At that moment, despite Instagram’s size, it “actually felt in some ways like Instagram was dying”—everyday sharing from real friends was evaporating, replaced by creators and influencers, with the intimate sharing flowing to Snapchat. The task was: “How do we get normal people to feel comfortable sharing on Instagram again?” Ryan took over iOS for Stories and immediately cut the team size significantly; high churn and too many people working on misaligned directions had created drag. The core iOS team was just Ryan and one other engineer, with part-time help from Android and no dedicated server engineer. “It seems kind of crazy, but it really allowed us to move quickly.” His principle: **if you want to go fast, go small**. “There was never a question of am I working on something that someone else is working on? If there’s a bug, it’s like, oh, okay, that’s my bug.”

For two to three months, Ryan worked 16-18 hour days, seven days a week, commuting from San Francisco to Menlo Park and back at 1-2 AM, refusing to sleep in the office. He gave up his identity as a serious rock climber (he’d been on the U.S. team, competing in World Cups). But he describes the experience as a bonding crucible: the PM on the project is now his co-founder at Retro. They felt like underdogs against Snapchat, even though Instagram was the incumbent—“We had a Ghostbusters poster up in our war room.” Instagram’s unfair advantage was that users already had their friend graph and wanted to share with those people—they just lacked the right outlet. But the team also pushed the format forward: Snapchat didn’t have backward navigation by tapping left, and the **hold to pause** gesture came from Ryan using an early build, putting his thumb on the screen to pause an auto-advancing story, and realizing the feature didn’t exist yet. “When I mentor engineers, you have this power as an engineer to just build your ideas. If you are using something and you think it should work a different way, you can just go do that.” On launch, he thought the whole thing would crash and burn—he could see all the cracks and crash reports—but the public reception was overwhelmingly positive, calling it polished. That surprised him: “Really? Are we using the same thing?”

A deeper insight from the feed-ranking change that shipped around the same time: Ryan was initially skeptical of algorithmic ranking, but the metric that changed his mind was that ranked-feed users *created more content themselves*—they shared more, because ranking surfaced friends’ content and sparked mutual connection. “That’s kind of hard to dispute. That’s probably a good thing.” He connected with Instagram’s original mission: “to capture and share the world’s moments,” encouraging creativity.

**One promo per half to Staff (IC6)**

Ryan started at IC4 and believed he’d been mis-leveled. His Android counterpart on Stories, Will Bailey, was an IC8 with a similar role, which made the gap obvious. In the middle of the Stories crunch, he told his manager—a director—“I think you should promote me from four to six in this cycle.” To the manager’s credit, he checked with HR; they’d done a double promotion twice in company history and both times “it worked out terribly, the people left very quickly.” Ryan didn’t get it, but he’d seeded the idea: his correct level was a high IC6. He knew that if he took a normal single promotion one cycle, the next cycle they’d say “well, he just got promoted last half.” His framing: promotions are ultimately **an incentive system**, “the carrot that’s being dangled in front of you,” and sometimes it’s rational for the company to delay even when performance is there. He got IC5 for Whiteout and IC6 for Stories—a promotion every half. The real career dividend wasn’t the level, but the **freedom to work on whatever projects he wanted**: “It just demonstrated that if you put me on a project, I’m gonna do a good job with it.”

**Senior staff promo (IC7) via IGTV**

Kevin Systrom and designer Ian Silber unveiled IGTV at an all-hands: a vision for long-form, vertical, mobile-native video. Ryan immediately thought, “I gotta work on this thing.” The small team—Ryan, Will Bailey, Thomasson as iOS/Android/server leads—moved fast with one or two engineers per platform. IGTV doesn’t exist today; in hindsight, it was a product mismatch: people weren’t producing high-quality long-form vertical content. They tried AI to dynamically crop landscape video to vertical, which Ryan patented, but creators were “horrified—‘you’re destroying my content.’” There was “maybe a little bit of hubris: Instagram can just change the industry.” The project informed what later became Reels, but on its own it didn’t work. Still, executing it well earned him IC7.

Ryan notes that internal tensions between Facebook and Instagram—now public via antitrust litigation—shaped that era. Instagram had been the popular newcomer, Stories outperformed Facebook’s version, and there were concerns about cannibalization. Ryan’s philosophy: “The best way to get the best outcome for the overall company was actually to have these things compete with each other because you own both. They’re gonna make each other better and one of them will win. If you stifle that competition, somebody from the outside is more likely to come up with something better.”

**IG Labs and the IC8 principal promo**

After IGTV, Ryan spent time “wandering the impact desert,” close to leaving. The new big initiative was Reels, but he didn’t want to work on passive video consumption. Instagram’s culture was shifting—founders gone, Facebook leadership incoming, old Instagram energy fading. Ryan pitched the new head of engineering on an idea he’d developed with designer Vivian Wong: **IG Labs**, a small, high-talent-density group working on new product initiatives that didn’t slot cleanly into the existing org structure. Instagram had grown so large that cross-org projects were under-invested; IG Labs would span across them. They tried many things, most didn’t ship, but one lasting feature was collaborative posts (multiple authors on a single post)—a small-seeming feature Ryan notes actually gets heavy use and yielded one of his more interesting patents. IG Labs also served as a concentration of talent that could be deployed for big new bets like Threads.

To get the IC8 promo, Ryan’s approach was **build consensus ahead of time**: he identified champions among VPs and a critical D2, set up monthly check-ins to course-correct, so that by the time the promo packet went to calibration, the key decision-makers were already bought in. He’s clear this isn’t just a social game—you need the underlying impact too—but alignment before the calibration room prevents debate there.

**Trying management as a Tech Lead Director**

When IG Labs grew, it made more sense for Ryan to manage the people in the group rather than have them report to a manager in the Reels org. Facebook’s orthodoxy is that IC and management tracks are separate; Ryan disagrees: “I don’t subscribe to that.” His wife is a Senior Staff Engineer at Tesla where ICs have direct reports and managers code—a model he prefers. His controversial opinion: **senior engineers should be involved in coding** because it gives them skin in the game. “You’re not gonna come up with some architecture that you just hand off to somebody else and it’s their problem now.” He also found that a higher title made cross-functional conversations easier in a company that had grown so large he was constantly re-establishing credibility with new PMs. Facebook keeps levels private, which Ryan likes for meritocratic engineering discussions, but he’d support some form of senior designation for external interactions. The tradeoff was real: “My ratings were probably lower than they would’ve been if I was just an IC. It was like, okay, you’re exceeding as an IC but you’re meeting as a manager, so you’re just meeting.” He wasn’t optimizing for career at that point—he’d never put more than one year on the internal “how long do you intend to stay” survey. “It was always like, okay, this is my last year. I’m gonna get out soon.”

**Starting Retro and leaving Big Tech**

Ryan and Nathan (his PM from the Stories team, later at Facebook Dating and VR) had spent years talking at a bar in San Francisco’s Mission district about wanting to build products differently. Timing finally aligned, and Ryan’s investor mentor told him, “It’s time; you should really take a bet on yourself.” They left to create a product studio—Retro is the first product, but the company’s goal is to build an environment where great product builders can do the best work of their lives, creating products that feel good to use.

Retro is intentionally different from modern social media: it’s actual-social, focused on friends (not creators), with a mutual-friending model and a 250-friend limit. There’s no ad-based business model because Ryan believes **ads combined with an algorithmic feed are “a little bit evil”** —the incentive is to keep users consuming past what they wanted. “I show up to see what a friend is doing; it’s gonna try to keep me there for the next hour, two hours, whatever.” Monetization is a premium subscription for features like video sharing. The engagement pattern is strikingly different: nearly half of daily active users post something, far higher than Instagram. The app hit critical mass in Taiwan, reaching #1 on the App Store last year, validating the model when it works. Ryan sees Retro coexisting alongside Instagram—he still has it, but uses it less, and it’s helped him break a phone addiction he didn’t feel good about.

Compared to Big Tech: compensation is much lower (Ryan and his co-founder pay themselves less than all their employees), but satisfaction and learning are higher. “A lot of my work became convincing layers above me that we should do something… political wrangling and how do I make this person feel like this is their idea, not my idea. I’d spend none of my time on that now.” He has one meeting a day—a one-hour standup—and spends the rest building.

**The small-teams hypothetical**

Ryan is emphatic that smaller teams move faster, and he’d often discuss the thought experiment: if you snapped your fingers and randomly removed half the people, would things go better? “Frequently we felt like, yeah, maybe they would.” He acknowledges that at Instagram’s scale, a 0.1% improvement is hundreds of millions of dollars, so incremental hires can justify themselves. But the **hidden cost of each person** is underestimated: organizational overhead, more stakeholders, more meetings, and more code in the app itself. Before he left, a tool showed he was spending over four hours a day waiting for the app to compile. “With a smaller team, when we were 10 people and the app was tiny, it was so much easier to get things done.” He sees Twitter’s post-Elon cuts as an interesting case study: buggy but still running, and now profitable for the first time.

**Examples of talented individuals**

Mike Krieger was a hero who didn’t disappoint: “They say don’t meet your heroes because you’ll be disappointed, but I think you should meet Mike Krieger.” Ryan’s engineering philosophy—**the simple thing first**, attention to craft, **leading from the front**—came from him. At the end of Stories, when the drawing-tools engineer got pulled back by his own team, Mike personally jumped in, coded the neon brush, and was reviewing Ryan’s diffs at 2:00 AM alongside the team. “Seeing that and how that felt as a team really has informed the way I think about leading teams.”

Will Bailey, his IC8 Android counterpart on Stories, wrote a full vision document for the product before they’d shipped anything—five years later, Ryan looked back and realized it was essentially the roadmap for everything they built. Will modeled how to have a successful career as a **product-focused engineer** in a Facebook culture that often saw product teams as junior and infrastructure as “real engineering.” Instagram’s culture valued product more, and that was reflected in Stories’ success.

Thomas Dimson (feed ranking at Instagram, later had his company bought by OpenAI) was “probably just the smartest person I’ve ever met” but also had great social skills. He built ranking infrastructure while maintaining a product mindset, thinking about human psychology and how features make people feel. Ryan says he, Thomas, and Will all shared this orientation: “I’ve always been interested in technology as a means to create things for people. I’ve never been that interested in the technology itself.”

**Advice to his younger self**

Ryan’s advice to new grads: **invest in the tools of your time to build things for people**. New grads have a unique advantage—they aren’t locked into a legacy technology stack, so they can pick up the latest thing natively, while senior engineers are still defaulting to what they already know. Just as he stood out by learning iOS when it was new while senior folks wrote Java backends, today’s grads can stand out by mastering AI tools. “I think with these AI tools that are coming out, you can do incredible things with them and you’re probably gonna be more skilled at those than the senior engineers because it’s just not the thing that they learned natively.” Even as demand for junior engineers shrinks, “if you can get really good at using those tools, there will definitely be roles for you.”
