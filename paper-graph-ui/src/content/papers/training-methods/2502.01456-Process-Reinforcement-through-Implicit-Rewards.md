# Process Reinforcement through Implicit Rewards

**ArXiv:** [2502.01456](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01456)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper proposes **PRIME** (Process Reinforcement through Implicit rEwards), a scalable framework for online reinforcement learning of LLMs with token-level dense rewards that bypasses the prohibitive cost of collecting step-level process labels.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **PRIME** (Process Reinforcement through Implicit rEwards), a scalable framework for online reinforcement learning of LLMs with token-level dense rewards that bypasses the prohibitive cost of collecting step-level process labels. Training Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Base on competition-level math and coding benchmarks, PRIME employs **implicit process rewards** (deriving token-level rewards from a language model trained with only outcome labels via the representation $r_\phi(y_t) := \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t|y_{<t})}$) and updates the implicit process reward model (PRM) online using policy rollouts and outcome verifier signals, which fundamentally mitigates reward hacking while maintaining the same computational cost as standard outcome reward models. Compared to RL using outcome rewards only, PRIME achieves a 2.5× sample efficiency gain and a 6.9% improvement in training rewards, yielding a final model—Eurus-2-7B-PRIME—that surpasses Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct on seven reasoning benchmarks while using only 10% of its training data, establishing that dense reward signals from an online-updated implicit PRM substantially accelerate and improve RL for LLM reasoning, and that the method generalizes across diverse advantage estimators including RLOO, REINFORCE, GRPO, and PPO.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Dense Rewards in Online RL for LLMs Remain Inaccessible

The paper addresses a specific, practical gap in the reinforcement learning (RL) pipeline for large language models: while dense, token-level process rewards offer clear theoretical advantages over sparse outcome-level rewards, **no existing method enables scalable, online training of process reward models (PRMs) during RL**. This is not a hypothetical limitation — it is a documented bottleneck that has prevented major industry efforts from incorporating PRMs into their RL training pipelines.

The significance of this gap is best understood through the lens of what dense rewards *promise* versus what sparse rewards *actually deliver*. When an LLM generates a multi-step reasoning solution to a competition math problem, an outcome reward model (ORM) provides a single scalar signal at the final token — correct or incorrect. This is the "bandit setting" described in Section 2.1, where $r(y_t) = 0$ for all intermediate steps $t < T$ and only the terminal reward $r(y_T)$ is non-zero. The authors identify three concrete harms of this sparsity:

**1. Spurious solutions with incorrect processes but correct answers.** The policy can learn to produce answers that happen to match the ground truth through faulty reasoning, because the verifier signals no penalty for intermediate errors. On competition math problems where multiple solution paths exist, this means the model can internalize reasoning patterns that are statistically correlated with correct answers but logically unsound — a brittle capability that fails under distribution shift.

**2. Drastically reduced sample efficiency.** The policy receives no gradient signal about *which tokens* in a correct solution contributed to success versus which were incidental. Every token in a correct trajectory gets reinforced equally, and every token in an incorrect trajectory gets penalized equally, regardless of whether the error occurred at step 2 or step 15. This is the **credit assignment problem** that Sutton & Barto (2018) identified as fundamental to RL: without intermediate feedback, the policy must average over many trajectories to disentangle causal contributions of early versus late decisions. The empirical consequence is that RL with sparse rewards requires substantially more samples to converge, a cost that compounds with problem difficulty as solution lengths grow.

**3. Amplified training variance on complex tasks.** As reasoning tasks require longer chains of thought (a trend accelerated by o1-style models that generate thousands of tokens of internal reasoning), the variance of Monte Carlo returns explodes because each additional step introduces independent sampling noise that accumulates over the trajectory. Dense rewards, by providing a signal at every step, partially decompose the long-horizon credit assignment into a sequence of shorter-horizon sub-problems, which reduces variance without introducing bias.

This gap has immediate practical urgency. The paper is published in early 2025, at a moment when the field is undergoing a paradigm shift toward RL-based post-training for reasoning. OpenAI's o1 (Jaech et al., 2024) demonstrated that large-scale RL can produce dramatic reasoning improvements, and DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025) provided an open verification of this scaling effect. However, as the paper explicitly notes in Section 2.2, DeepSeek-AI et al. (2025) "shares similar conclusions and thus is impeded from incorporating PRMs into large-scale RL training." The implication is clear: even teams with substantial compute resources have concluded that process rewards are too expensive to deploy online, and have settled for outcome-only RL despite recognizing its limitations.

### Why This Problem Is Fundamentally Difficult

The paper carefully diagnoses *why* dense rewards have resisted online deployment, decomposing the challenge into three interconnected obstacles (C1–C3 in Section 2.2):

**C1: Process rewards are hard to define.** The first obstacle is definitional. What constitutes a "correct" intermediate step? In formal verification domains like Lean proofs, intermediate correctness is well-defined. But in natural language mathematical reasoning, steps vary in granularity, logical dependency, and optionality. An intermediate claim might be mathematically true but strategically irrelevant; it might be false but serve as a useful exploratory hypothesis that gets corrected later; it might be partially correct but imprecise. The paper notes that "some incorrect steps can also positively contribute to the final answer by pruning searching branches" — a reference to how o1-style models use exploration, backtracking, and error correction as integral parts of their reasoning process rather than as failures. Defining dense rewards therefore requires making contestable normative judgments about what constitutes "good" intermediate reasoning.

Moreover, even if one decides on a definition, the annotation burden is extreme. The PRM800k dataset (Lightman et al., 2023) used human annotators to label individual solution steps, but this pipeline is expensive, slow, and specific to the annotators' mathematical expertise. Scaling it to diverse reasoning domains (coding, scientific reasoning, legal analysis) or to the massive volume of on-policy rollouts generated during online RL is infeasible.

**C2: PRM online updates are not scalable.** This is the central obstacle. Static reward models — whether ORMs or PRMs — inevitably suffer from **overoptimization** or **reward hacking** (Gao et al., 2022) because the policy distribution shifts during training. As the policy model improves, its outputs drift away from the distribution on which the reward model was trained, causing the reward model's scores to become increasingly miscalibrated. The RL optimization then exploits these calibration errors, producing outputs that score highly under the reward model but are actually poor — a phenomenon the authors explicitly discuss in Section 5.1.

The standard solution in RL literature is to update the reward model online alongside the policy (Leike et al., 2018; Schulman et al., 2017). But for PRMs, this creates a circular dependency: online updating requires fresh process labels on the policy's latest rollouts, but generating those labels requires the expensive annotation pipeline that online updating is meant to avoid. Existing estimation-based methods for PRM training (Wang et al., 2023; Kazemnejad et al., 2024) use Monte Carlo rollouts — for each step in a solution, sample many completions and compute the ratio that reaches the correct answer — but this requires "about 10× more rollouts for each step than sampling only the response-level trajectories." Running this estimation at every RL iteration on every policy rollout would multiply the already-substantial generation cost by an order of magnitude, making it impractical for any but the smallest-scale experiments.

**C3: Explicit reward modeling brings extra cost.** Even setting aside online updates, the *offline* training phase for reward models is a substantial development tax. Training a PRM typically requires:
- Curating a dataset of diverse solution trajectories with step-level annotations or rollout estimates.
- Training a separate model (often of comparable size to the policy model) on this data.
- Validating that the PRM transfers adequately to the policy's output distribution before RL begins.

This is not merely a compute cost — it is a **development overhead** that adds complexity to the training pipeline, requires separate data collection and filtering pipelines, and introduces an additional potential failure point before RL can even begin. The paper contrasts this with its approach, which "eliminates the dedicated reward modeling stage" entirely (Section 1, C3).

### Prior Approaches and Their Limitations

The paper situates its contribution against several lines of prior work, each of which addresses part of the problem but leaves the core gap unresolved.

**Outcome-only RL for reasoning.** The dominant paradigm at the time of this paper's publication — exemplified by DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025) and related efforts — uses verifiable outcome rewards (ground-truth answer matching for math, unit test pass rates for code) in combination with REINFORCE-style policy gradient algorithms. These approaches are scalable because they require no learned reward model at all — the environment provides a binary or continuous correctness signal at the terminal step. However, they inherit all the credit assignment and sample efficiency problems of sparse rewards. The paper empirically validates this limitation in Figure 3, showing that RLOO with outcome verifier only (sparse rewards) plateaus at lower training rewards than PRIME and takes 2.5× more steps to reach equivalent performance.

Some outcome-reward approaches attempt to mitigate sparsity through majority voting or best-of-N sampling at inference time, but these are post-hoc patches that do not address the training-time credit assignment problem. Others use value models (critics) trained alongside the policy to provide intermediate value estimates, as in PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). However, the paper notes in Section 2.1 that "previous work showed that value models may not be able to solve the reward sparsity issue effectively due to training challenges, despite the additional computation overhead." The experimental results in Section 5.4 confirm this: using an Implicit PRM as a value model (predicting expected returns at each state) underperforms using it as a reward model (providing immediate per-token rewards), and a standard linear-head value model provides no benefit over REINFORCE.

**Process reward models with static training.** The seminal work of Lightman et al. (2023) demonstrated that PRMs — which score each intermediate solution step rather than only the final answer — substantially outperform ORMs for best-of-N selection at inference time. Math-Shepherd (Wang et al., 2023) showed that Monte Carlo rollout-based supervision could train PRMs without human labels, reducing the annotation bottleneck. VinePPO (Kazemnejad et al., 2024) attempted to incorporate these pre-trained PRMs into RL training by using averaged returns across trajectories as value estimates.

However, all of these approaches train the PRM **offline, before RL begins** and keep it **static during RL**. This creates an inherent ceiling: no matter how good the initial PRM is, it will be overoptimized as the policy distribution shifts. The paper's Figure 5 provides direct evidence of this phenomenon, showing that an offline PRM's classification accuracy on policy rollouts steadily degrades during training, while online PRMs show the opposite trend. The static PRM also introduces a chicken-and-egg problem: the PRM is trained on data from a weaker model, so it may not accurately judge the more sophisticated reasoning patterns that emerge during RL.

**Implicit rewards in preference optimization.** A separate line of work in the alignment literature uses **implicit rewards** — rewards derived from the log-ratio of a policy model and a reference model — as the theoretical foundation for direct preference optimization (DPO; Rafailov et al., 2023) and its variants. These methods show that the optimal policy under a KL-constrained RL objective can be expressed in terms of an implicit reward function without explicitly training a reward model. Rafailov et al. (2024) further showed that optimizing the DPO objective implicitly learns a Q-function.

Yuan et al. (2024b) — a direct precursor to this work — extended the implicit reward concept to **process rewards**, demonstrating that a language model trained with only outcome-level preference labels (using any loss function that optimizes the implicit reward representation $r_\phi(y) := \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}$) can be repurposed at inference time as a PRM that provides token-level scores via $r_\phi(y_t) := \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t|y_{<t})}$. This was a significant advance because it eliminated the need for step-level labels entirely — the PRM is trained exactly like an ORM, with only outcome supervision, but produces dense scores at inference. However, prior to PRIME, this approach had only been applied in the context of **inference-time search** (e.g., scoring candidate solutions for best-of-N selection), not for **online RL training**.

**Reward shaping theory.** From a theoretical perspective, the paper notes in Appendix C.3 that PRIME can be viewed through the lens of **potential-based reward shaping** (Ng et al., 1999). If the Q-value (expected cumulative reward from a state) is treated as a potential function, then the difference between successive Q-values — exactly the process reward — constitutes a shaping reward. Potential-based reward shaping is guaranteed not to change the optimal policy (when $\gamma = 1$), but can dramatically accelerate learning by providing denser feedback. This theoretical connection is important because it explains *why* PRIME should preserve the same asymptotic behavior as outcome-only RL while improving sample efficiency — the process rewards reshape the reward landscape without altering the optimal solution.

### How PRIME Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

PRIME makes a single strategic move that distinguishes it from all prior approaches: **it applies implicit process rewards not as a static inference-time tool but as a continuously updated online reward signal during RL training**. This elegantly resolves the three challenges identified in Section 2.2:

**Against C1 (definition difficulty):** Implicit process rewards are derived automatically from the log-ratio of two language models — there is no need to define what constitutes a "correct" intermediate step. The reward at each token is the contribution of that token to the overall likelihood ratio, which is a natural measure of how much the token "steers" the trajectory toward outcomes preferred under the PRM's training objective. This sidesteps entirely the philosophical problem of step-level correctness.

**Against C2 (scalability of online updates):** Because the Implicit PRM is trained with only outcome labels — the same outcome labels already collected to update the policy — updating it online requires exactly zero additional annotation. The training signal for the PRM is the binary outcome verifier signal $r_o(y) \in \{0, 1\}$, and the loss is standard cross-entropy: $\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y,r_o(y))\sim\mathcal{T}}[r_o(y) \cdot \log \sigma(r_\phi(y)) + (1 - r_o(y)) \cdot \log(1 - \sigma(r_\phi(y)))]$, where $\mathcal{T}$ is the set of on-policy rollouts. This is the same computational cost as training an ORM online, which is standard in PPO pipelines. The paper quantifies this: PRIME requires 24% more wall-clock time per step than outcome-only RLOO (Table 2), but achieves equivalent performance in 40% of the steps, yielding a net 2× speedup.

**Against C3 (dedicated reward modeling overhead):** Since the Implicit PRM is simply a language model initialized from the same checkpoint as the policy model (the SFT model or even the base model), there is no separate reward modeling phase. The paper shows in Section 5.1 and Figure 4 that initializing the PRM from the SFT model *outperforms* a dedicatedly trained PRM (EurusPRM) trained on 500K additional examples. This is a striking finding: the distribution alignment from starting at the same initialization appears more valuable than the additional data used to train a specialized reward model. The paper conjectures that "initializing policy model and PRM from the same model largely alleviates the distribution shift issue."

The paper positions PRIME not as a new RL algorithm but as a **general plug-in component** compatible with diverse advantage estimators. Section 5.3 demonstrates this explicitly by integrating PRIME's dense rewards into REINFORCE, GRPO, PPO, and RLOO, showing consistent improvements across all four. This is critical because it means PRIME does not require practitioners to adopt a specific RL framework — it can be dropped into existing GRPO pipelines (widely used in the open-source community post-DeepSeek-R1) or standard PPO implementations with minimal modification.

Finally, the paper frames its contribution in terms of a broader historical trajectory: as Sutton (2019) argued in "The Bitter Lesson," general methods that leverage computation tend to outperform approaches that rely on human knowledge. The paper extends this argument to reward modeling — implicit process rewards, which are computed automatically from model outputs without human-designed step definitions, represent a more scalable path than manually specified process reward criteria. Section 7 makes this explicit: "as the fuel of LLMs, data, will be depleted in the near future, we are entering a new era of experience, which is exemplified by RL." PRIME is positioned as infrastructure for this transition — a way to generate rich training signals from model interactions rather than human annotations.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

PRIME is a method for training language models to reason better through reinforcement learning, where the reward signal at every single token is automatically computed from an **implicit process reward model** (a second copy of the language model that is continuously updated during training) rather than only receiving a binary "correct/incorrect" signal at the very end of each solution. The core problem it solves is that dense, token-level rewards are theoretically superior for RL credit assignment and sample efficiency, but existing approaches require prohibitively expensive step-level human labels or massive Monte Carlo rollouts that make online updating infeasible — PRIME resolves this by using a reward parameterization that requires only outcome labels (the same binary correct/incorrect signals already available from ground-truth answer verification) and allows the reward model to be updated online at the same cost as a standard outcome reward model, thereby preventing the reward hacking that plagues static reward models while providing rich intermediate feedback throughout each reasoning trajectory.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The PRIME system has five major components that operate in a closed loop during RL training:

1. **Policy Model (`$\pi_\theta$`)** — the primary language model being trained to solve reasoning tasks. It generates complete solution trajectories (prompt + chain-of-thought reasoning + final answer) by sampling autoregressively. Initialized from either an SFT model or directly from a base model.

2. **Implicit Process Reward Model (Implicit PRM, `$\pi_\phi$`)** — a language model with identical architecture and tokenizer to the policy model, initialized from the same checkpoint, that serves as both an **outcome reward model** (when scoring complete solutions) and a **process reward model** (when decomposing its per-token log-probability ratios into dense rewards). It is updated online using outcome labels collected from the policy's rollouts.

3. **Reference Model (`$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`)** — a frozen copy of the initial model (either the SFT model or the policy's old parameters) that provides the baseline log-probabilities against which the Implicit PRM's outputs are compared. The ratio `$\pi_\phi / \pi_{\text{ref}}$` is the core mechanism for deriving rewards.

4. **Outcome Verifier (`$r_o$`)** — a deterministic, rule-based function that provides ground-truth binary correctness labels for completed solutions. For math problems, `$r_o^{\text{math}}(y) = 1$` if the extracted answer matches the ground truth exactly, and `$0$` otherwise. For coding problems, `$r_o^{\text{code}}(y) = \frac{\text{\# passed test cases}}{\text{\# total test cases}}$`. This is the only source of supervision for the entire pipeline.

5. **Advantage Estimator and Policy Update** — the RL algorithm component that computes per-token advantages from the combined process rewards (from the Implicit PRM) and outcome rewards (from the verifier), then applies a PPO-style clipped surrogate loss to update the policy model.

Information flows as follows at each RL iteration: prompts are sampled from the training dataset → the policy model generates `$K = 4$` complete responses per prompt → the outcome verifier assigns correctness labels to each response → an accuracy filter removes prompts whose responses are all correct or all incorrect → the Implicit PRM and reference model perform forward passes on all retained (prompt, response) pairs → the Implicit PRM is updated via cross-entropy loss against the outcome labels → token-level implicit process rewards are computed as `$r_\phi(y_t) := \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t|y_{<t})}$` → advantages are computed by combining discounted returns from process rewards with a leave-one-out baseline from outcome rewards → the policy model is updated via the PPO clip loss → the cycle repeats.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal definition of implicit process rewards and the Implicit PRM — how a language model trained with outcome labels produces token-level rewards, what the mathematical representation is, and why this representation enables online updating.
- **Second**, the online PRM training procedure — the loss function, the data it trains on, and exactly how it avoids the distribution shift problem that plagues offline PRMs.
- **Third**, the advantage estimation mechanism — how token-level process rewards and sparse outcome rewards are combined into a single per-token advantage signal using a leave-one-out baseline, and why this specific combination is chosen.
- **Fourth**, the policy update mechanism — the PPO clip loss and why it is preferred over simpler policy gradient estimators for stability.
- **Fifth**, the supporting techniques — SFT model initialization for the PRM, online prompt filtering, and alternative reference model choices — that make the core algorithm work reliably in practice.
- **Sixth**, the implementation details — hyperparameters, hardware configuration, training data preparation, and the RL algorithm variants tested.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methods paper** whose core idea is that implicit process rewards — derived from a language model trained with only outcome supervision via the representation `$r_\phi(y_t) := \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t|y_{<t})}$` — enable scalable online updating of process reward models during RL, thereby providing dense, token-level feedback that dramatically improves sample efficiency and final policy performance while preventing the reward hacking that occurs with static reward models.

#### The Implicit Process Reward Model: Deriving Dense Rewards from Outcome Supervision

The foundational mathematical object in PRIME is the **Implicit PRM**, which is simply a causal language model `$\pi_\phi$` trained to predict outcome-level correctness, but whose per-token log-probability ratios serve as dense process rewards at inference time.

**The reward representation.** The Implicit PRM is parameterized using a specific reward function that maps complete sequences to scalar scores:

$$r_\phi(y) := \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}$$

where `$\beta > 0$` is a scaling hyperparameter (set to `$\beta = 0.05$` in all experiments), `$\pi_\phi(y)$` is the probability assigned to the complete response `$y$` by the Implicit PRM (computed as `$\pi_\phi(y) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\phi(y_t|y_{<t})$`, the standard autoregressive factorization), and `$\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)$` is the probability assigned by a frozen reference model (initialized from the same checkpoint as the policy model and held fixed throughout training).

**What this representation computes:** for a complete response `$y$`, `$r_\phi(y)$` is the log-ratio of two models' probabilities, scaled by `$\beta$`. If the Implicit PRM `$\pi_\phi$` assigns higher probability than the reference model to a response, `$r_\phi(y)$` is positive (the response is "rewarded"); if it assigns lower probability, `$r_\phi(y)$` is negative (the response is "penalized"). The magnitude captures how much more or less likely the response is under the trained model versus the initial model.

**Why this form:** this parameterization is the key to scalability. The log-ratio of two language models is a **self-normalizing** representation — because both `$\pi_\phi$` and `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` are probability distributions that sum to 1 over all sequences, the ratio automatically calibrates for sequence length and token frequency without requiring an explicit normalization constant. This is in contrast to standard reward model training, where a scalar head on top of a language model produces unnormalized scores that can drift arbitrarily during training. Additionally, this representation has a theoretical connection established in Yuan et al. (2024b): for any loss function that optimizes `$r_\phi(y)$` against outcome labels, the expected cumulative reward from state `$(y_{<t}, y_t)$` (the Q-value) has the closed-form solution:

$$q_t^\phi(y_{<t}, y_t) = \sum_{i=1}^t \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y_i|y_{<i})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|y_{<i})}$$

This means that the **token-level decomposition** of the sequence-level reward — `$r_\phi(y_t) := \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t|y_{<t})}$` — naturally emerges as the one-step contribution to the cumulative Q-value. In plain terms: the reward at each token is exactly the token's contribution to the overall log-ratio, and the sum of all per-token rewards up to step `$t$` equals the Q-value at that state. This is not an approximation or an assumption — it follows directly from the autoregressive factorization of the two models.

**Critical distinction from DPO-style implicit rewards.** The paper is careful to note (Appendix C.1) that PRIME's implicit process rewards, despite the superficial resemblance, are **not derived from the optimal policy of entropy-regularized RL** as in DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023). In DPO, the implicit reward `$\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$` emerges from solving the KL-constrained RL objective analytically and represents the reward under which the current policy `$\pi_\theta$` would be optimal. In PRIME, the Implicit PRM `$\pi_\phi$` is a **separately trained model** whose log-ratio with the reference model serves as a learned reward function — there is no assumption that the policy model is optimal under this reward. The Implicit PRM is trained discriminatively to predict outcome correctness, not derived from the policy's parameters. This distinction is what makes online updating possible: the PRM can be trained on new data without changing the policy's relationship to the reward, because the PRM and policy are separate models.

**How token-level rewards are obtained at inference.** During RL training, after a response `$y = (y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_T)$` is generated by the policy model, the Implicit PRM `$\pi_\phi$` and the reference model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` both perform a forward pass over the entire sequence to compute their per-token log-probabilities. The process reward at step `$t$` is then:

$$r_\phi(y_t) := \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t|y_{<t})}$$

This produces a dense reward signal — one scalar per token — at no additional annotation cost beyond the forward passes, which are standard operations in any transformer-based training pipeline.

**Why this addresses C1 (process rewards are hard to define).** There is no human judgment about whether an intermediate step is "correct" or "incorrect." The reward at each token simply measures how much that token contributes to making the overall sequence look more like a "correct" response (as determined by the Implicit PRM's training on outcome labels) versus a generic response from the reference model. Tokens that steer the trajectory toward high-probability regions for correct solutions receive positive rewards; tokens that steer toward low-probability regions receive negative rewards. This is a purely statistical property of the two models' distributions, requiring no definition of step boundaries or step-level correctness.

#### Online Training of the Implicit PRM

The Implicit PRM `$\pi_\phi$` is not static — it is updated at every RL iteration using the policy model's latest rollouts, which is the mechanism that prevents reward hacking.

**Training data.** At each iteration, the policy model `$\pi_\theta$` samples `$K = 4$` complete responses for each prompt in a batch of 256 prompts, producing `$256 \times 4 = 1024$` response trajectories. Each response is then scored by the outcome verifier `$r_o$` to get a binary label: `$r_o(y) = 1$` if the final answer matches the ground truth, and `$r_o(y) = 0$` otherwise. This label is the **only supervision signal** for the PRM — there are no step-level annotations, no Monte Carlo rollouts, and no human labels.

The training data for the PRM at iteration `$i$` consists of all (prompt, response, outcome label) triples from that iteration's rollouts that pass the accuracy filter (described later). Critically, these are **on-policy** rollouts — they are generated by the current policy model, not by a separate data collection process. This means the PRM is always trained on exactly the distribution of responses that the policy is currently producing, which is what prevents the distribution shift that causes offline PRMs to become increasingly miscalibrated.

**Loss function.** The Implicit PRM is trained via standard binary cross-entropy:

$$\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y, r_o(y)) \sim \mathcal{T}}\left[r_o(y) \cdot \log \sigma(r_\phi(y)) + (1 - r_o(y)) \cdot \log(1 - \sigma(r_\phi(y)))\right]$$

where `$\mathcal{T}$` is the set of on-policy rollouts that passed the accuracy filter, `$r_o(y) \in \{0, 1\}$` is the binary outcome label, `$\sigma(\cdot)$` is the sigmoid function `$\sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$`, and `$r_\phi(y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}$` is the Implicit PRM's scalar score for the complete response (summed over all tokens).

**What this loss computes:** the Implicit PRM's sequence-level score `$r_\phi(y)$` is passed through a sigmoid to produce a probability `$\sigma(r_\phi(y)) \in (0, 1)$`, which is then compared to the binary ground-truth label `$r_o(y)$` using cross-entropy. If the response is correct (`$r_o(y) = 1$`), the loss penalizes the model when `$\sigma(r_\phi(y))$` is low (i.e., when the PRM assigns a low score to a correct response). If the response is incorrect (`$r_o(y) = 0$`), the loss penalizes the model when `$\sigma(r_\phi(y))$` is high (i.e., when the PRM assigns a high score to an incorrect response). The expectation is taken over the training batch.

**Why cross-entropy:** binary cross-entropy is the standard maximum-likelihood objective for binary classification, which is the right formulation because the outcome verifier provides binary labels. The sigmoid function maps the unnormalized reward `$r_\phi(y)$` (which can be any real number, positive or negative) to a valid probability. Alternative losses like mean squared error would be improper scoring rules for probability estimation.

**A subtlety about the loss (Appendix C.2).** Because both `$\pi_\phi$` and `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` are language models that are inherently self-normalized (their probabilities sum to 1), the minimum of the cross-entropy loss is not 0. The optimal solution satisfies `$\beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi^*(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} = r_o + c$` for some constant `$c$`, rather than `$\beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi^*(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} = r_o$`. The constant `$c$` appears because the self-normalization constraint prevents the log-ratio from perfectly matching the binary label. However, this bias term `$c$` **does not affect RL training** because PRIME uses **relative rewards** — the advantage computation subtracts a baseline (the leave-one-out average) from the process reward at each token, which cancels out any prompt-dependent additive constant. If the bias were not canceled, it could shift all advantages by a constant offset, which would bias the policy gradient. The paper also experimented with using DPO loss instead of cross-entropy, which eliminates this bias issue by construction (since DPO is a pairwise loss that depends only on relative scores), and found that "DPO and CE achieve similar results" (Table 8), so cross-entropy was chosen for memory efficiency.

**Training hyperparameters.** The Implicit PRM is updated using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`, batch size 256, and micro batch size 8. The PRM update occurs once per RL iteration, immediately after rollouts are generated and graded.

**Initialization of the Implicit PRM.** A critical design choice is how to initialize `$\pi_\phi$` at the start of RL training. The paper explores three strategies (Section 5.1):
- **SFT initialization:** use Eurus-2-7B-SFT (the supervised fine-tuned model that also serves as the starting policy) as `$\pi_\phi$`. This means the PRM initially produces zero reward for all tokens (since `$\pi_\phi = \pi_{\text{ref}}$`, so `$\log(1) = 0$`), and all learning comes from online updates.
- **EurusPRM offline:** train a specialized reward model from Eurus-2-7B-SFT on 500K additional examples generated by Llama-3.1 and Qwen2.5 models, then use this as the initial `$\pi_\phi$`.
- **EurusPRM online:** same as EurusPRM offline but additionally updated online during RL.

The surprising result (Figure 4) is that **SFT initialization substantially outperforms EurusPRM**, despite EurusPRM having been trained on more data. The authors conjecture that "initializing policy model and PRM from the same model largely alleviates the distribution shift issue, as the PRM is only trained on the online rollouts from the policy model." In other words, the benefit of perfect initial distribution alignment (the PRM's reference model is exactly the model that generated the SFT training data) outweighs the benefit of additional training data on a different distribution.

**Why online updates are essential (Figure 5).** The paper provides direct evidence that online PRM updates prevent reward hacking. By pairing correct and incorrect samples from each batch and measuring the PRM's classification accuracy (the fraction of pairs where `$r_\phi(y_{\text{correct}}) > r_\phi(y_{\text{incorrect}})$`), Figure 5 shows:
- **Offline PRM:** starts with high accuracy (~0.75) but steadily degrades to ~0.60 as the policy distribution shifts.
- **Online PRM (SFT initialization):** starts at chance level (0.50, since `$r_\phi = 0$` for all tokens initially) but rapidly improves to ~0.70 and continues to climb.
- **Online PRM (EurusPRM initialization):** starts high (~0.68) and further improves to ~0.75.

The offline PRM's degradation is the signature of reward overoptimization — the PRM becomes increasingly miscalibrated on the policy's outputs, and the policy learns to exploit these calibration errors. The online PRMs show the opposite curve because they are continuously trained on the policy's latest distribution.

#### Advantage Estimation: Combining Process and Outcome Rewards

After the Implicit PRM is updated, the system computes **advantages** — per-token, per-response estimates of how much better or worse each action (token choice) was compared to alternatives. The advantage function is what the policy gradient optimizes: positive advantages increase the probability of the corresponding tokens, negative advantages decrease it.

**The challenge of combining two reward sources.** PRIME has access to two distinct reward signals:
1. **Dense implicit process rewards** `$r_\phi(y_t)$` — a scalar at every token, derived from the Implicit PRM.
2. **Sparse outcome rewards** `$r_o(y)$` — a single scalar at the final token, derived from the ground-truth verifier.

The naive approach of simply adding them together (`$r(y_t) = r_\phi(y_t) + r_o(y_t)$`) would cause numerical instability because the two rewards have different scales and variances. The paper follows the approach of Shao et al. (2024): compute the **returns** separately for each reward type, then sum the returns to produce the final advantage.

**The leave-one-out baseline for outcome rewards.** For outcome rewards, PRIME uses the **leave-one-out (LOO) baseline** from RLOO (REINFORCE Leave-One-Out; Ahmadian et al., 2024; Kool et al., 2019). For a prompt where the policy generates `$K$` responses `$y^1, y^2, \ldots, y^K$`, the outcome-reward advantage for response `$i$` is:

$$A_o(y^i) = r_o(y^i) - \frac{1}{K-1} \sum_{j \neq i} r_o(y^j)$$

where `$r_o(y^i) \in \{0, 1\}$` is the binary outcome label for the `$i$`-th response, and the second term is the average outcome reward of the other `$K-1$` responses (the "leave-one-out" baseline).

**What this baseline does:** instead of using a learned value function (as in standard PPO) or a constant baseline (as in basic REINFORCE) to reduce the variance of the policy gradient, RLOO uses the empirically observed average reward of other responses to the same prompt as the baseline. If the `$i$`-th response is correct (`$r_o = 1$`) and most other responses are incorrect (baseline close to 0), the advantage is strongly positive, encouraging the policy to produce tokens similar to this response. If the `$i$`-th response is incorrect (`$r_o = 0$`) and most other responses are correct (baseline close to 1), the advantage is strongly negative, discouraging this response. If all responses are correct or all are incorrect, the advantage is close to zero, providing no learning signal — which is why the accuracy filter (described later) removes these prompts.

**Why leave-one-out rather than a learned baseline:** the paper empirically finds that "MC estimators, despite being simpler, are strong enough to produce stable results" (Section 3.2), and that "an additional value model does not benefit policy performance" (Section 5.4). The LOO baseline has the advantage of requiring no additional model or training, and it adapts naturally to the current prompt's difficulty (if a prompt is hard, the baseline will be low; if easy, the baseline will be high).

**The leave-one-out baseline adapted for process rewards.** For implicit process rewards, the baseline calculation must account for the fact that different responses have different lengths. The paper computes the baseline as the **average per-token process reward** across all tokens in the other `$K-1$` responses:

$$\bar{r}_\phi^{-i} = \frac{1}{K-1} \sum_{j \neq i} \bar{r}_\phi(y^j)$$

where `$\bar{r}_\phi(y^j) = \frac{1}{|y^j|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y^j|} r_\phi(y_t^j)$` is the average process reward per token in response `$y^j$`. This average-per-token baseline is then used to normalize the process reward at each individual token:

$$\tilde{r}_\phi(y_t^i) = r_\phi(y_t^i) - \bar{r}_\phi^{-i}$$

**The combined advantage.** The final per-token advantage for response `$y^i$` at timestep `$t$` is the sum of two components:

$$A_t^i = \underbrace{\sum_{s=t}^{|y^i|} \gamma^{s-t} \cdot \tilde{r}_\phi(y_s^i)}_{\text{discounted return of normalized process rewards}} + \underbrace{\left(r_o(y^i) - \frac{1}{K-1} \sum_{j \neq i} r_o(y^j)\right)}_{\text{leave-one-out outcome advantage}}$$

where `$\gamma \in [0, 1]$` is a discount factor (implicitly set to `$\gamma = 1$` in the paper — the discussion of discounting is formal, and the practical implementation uses no discounting since the process rewards already provide per-step signals), and `$|y^i|$` is the length of response `$y^i$` in tokens.

**What this advantage computes, operationally:**
1. For each token `$t$` in response `$i$`, compute the **discounted sum** of all future normalized process rewards from that token onward. This is the "return" of the process reward signal — a scalar indicating how much future reward the trajectory accumulated after taking action `$y_t^i$`.
2. Add the **outcome advantage** — a single scalar that is the same for all tokens in the response, indicating whether the final answer was correct relative to other responses to the same prompt.
3. The result is a per-token scalar `$A_t^i$` that feeds into the policy gradient: tokens that lead to higher cumulative process rewards and correct final answers receive positive advantages; tokens that lead to lower process rewards and incorrect answers receive negative advantages.

**Why separate returns and then sum:** directly mixing token-level process rewards and sequence-level outcome rewards at the reward level (`$r(y_t) = r_\phi(y_t) + r_o(y_t)$`) would cause two problems. First, the outcome reward would need to be spread across all tokens (e.g., uniformly), which would arbitrarily assign credit to tokens that may have had no causal role in the outcome. Second, the scales of the two rewards can differ — the process reward is a per-token value that accumulates over the sequence, while the outcome reward is a single terminal value. Computing returns separately and then summing is mathematically equivalent to using a reward function of `$r(y_t) = r_\phi(y_t)$` for `$t < T$` and `$r(y_T) = r_\phi(y_T) + r_o(y)$` for the final token (with no discounting), but the separate computation avoids numerical issues from mixing scales.

**Why this specific advantage formulation:** the leave-one-out baseline for outcome rewards is an unbiased estimator of the advantage (assuming responses are i.i.d. given the prompt) and has been shown effective in prior work (Ahmadian et al., 2024). The average-per-token baseline for process rewards accounts for the fact that different prompts have different inherent difficulty — a prompt where all responses receive low process rewards (because the problem is hard) should not penalize tokens in the same way as a prompt where all responses receive high process rewards (because the problem is easy). By subtracting the per-prompt average, the normalized process reward `$\tilde{r}_\phi$` captures how much better or worse a specific token is relative to the average token in other responses to the same prompt.

**Alternative advantage formulations studied (Section 5.4).** The paper compares four variants of advantage estimation to validate the choice of using Implicit PRMs as reward models rather than value models:
- **REINFORCE:** `$A_t = r_o(y)$` — standard sparse outcome advantage, no dense signal.
- **PPO with value model:** `$A_t = r_o(y) - V(y_{<t})$` — uses a learned value function (a linear head on the policy model) as the baseline.
- **Implicit PRM as value model:** `$A_t = r_o(y) - v_\phi(y_{<t})$` — uses the Implicit PRM's Q-value `$v_\phi(y_{<t}) = \sum_{i=1}^t r_\phi(y_i)$` as the baseline (equivalent to PPO with the value model replaced by the Implicit PRM's cumulative reward).
- **PRIME (Implicit PRM as reward model):** `$A_t = r_o(y) + \sum_{s=t}^T r_\phi(y_s)$` — uses the Implicit PRM's token-level rewards to compute the return directly, with no separate value baseline.

The results (Figure 8) show that PRIME (reward model usage) substantially outperforms all three baseline variants, and that both value model variants (the learned value model and the Implicit PRM as value model) provide no benefit over basic REINFORCE. The paper's interpretation is that "PRMs work better than value models in RL for LLMs" — providing dense per-step rewards is more effective for policy optimization than providing state values for baseline subtraction, likely because the reward signal directly shapes the policy gradient at each step rather than only affecting it indirectly through the baseline.

#### Policy Update via PPO Clip Loss

Once advantages are computed, the policy model `$\pi_\theta$` is updated using a **clipped surrogate objective** from Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO; Schulman et al., 2017). The core idea is to prevent the updated policy from deviating too far from the old policy in a single update, which would invalidate the importance sampling assumptions underlying the policy gradient.

**The PPO clip loss.** For each timestep `$t$` in each response, the loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \min\left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|y_{<t})} A_t, \text{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|y_{<t})}, 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon\right) A_t \right) \right]$$

where `$\pi_\theta(y_t|y_{<t})$` is the probability the current policy assigns to token `$y_t$` given the preceding context, `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|y_{<t})$` is the probability assigned by the policy at the start of the current iteration (frozen during the update), `$A_t$` is the computed advantage, `$\epsilon$` is the clipping parameter (set to `$\epsilon = 0.2$`, the standard value from the PPO paper), and `$\text{clip}(r, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)$` constrains the ratio `$r$` to the interval `$[0.8, 1.2]$`.

**What this loss computes, step by step:**
1. The **probability ratio** `$\rho_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|y_{<t})}$` measures how much more or less likely the current policy is to produce token `$y_t$` compared to the old policy. A ratio of 1.0 means no change; a ratio of 2.0 means the current policy is twice as likely to produce this token.
2. The **unclipped objective** is `$\rho_t A_t$` — if the advantage is positive, increasing the probability ratio increases this term (encouraging the token); if the advantage is negative, decreasing the ratio increases this term (discouraging the token).
3. The **clipped objective** is `$\text{clip}(\rho_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A_t$` — the ratio is capped so it cannot exceed `$1+\epsilon$` or fall below `$1-\epsilon$`. This means the policy cannot increase the probability of a token by more than 20% or decrease it by more than 20% in a single update.
4. The **min** of the clipped and unclipped objectives is taken. This implements a **pessimistic** update: if the advantage is positive and the ratio is large, the clipped (lower) value is used to prevent the policy from moving too aggressively toward a potentially noisy advantage estimate. If the advantage is negative and the ratio is small, the clipped (higher) value is used to prevent the policy from moving too aggressively away.

**Why PPO clip loss:** in standard policy gradient (REINFORCE), the update is proportional to `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t|y_{<t}) A_t$`, which has no constraint on how much the policy can change per step. In practice, this can lead to **destructive updates** where a single batch with high-variance advantages causes the policy to collapse (e.g., assigning near-zero probability to tokens that were previously frequent). The PPO clip loss acts as a **trust region** — it allows the policy to improve, but only in small, controlled steps. The paper notes that it uses the clip loss "for more stable policy updates" (Section 3.2). Notably, they apply the same clip loss across all RL algorithm variants (RLOO, REINFORCE, GRPO, PPO), only modifying the advantage estimation component while keeping the surrogate loss constant.

**Why `$\epsilon = 0.2$`:** this is the original PPO value and was not tuned. The paper states hyperparameters are set to "constant" values without extensive sweeps.

**Policy hyperparameters.** The policy model is updated using the AdamW optimizer with a constant learning rate of `$5 \times 10^{-7}$`, batch size 256, micro batch size 8. The KL penalty coefficient (a common PPO addition that penalizes divergence from a reference model) is set to 0 in all experiments, meaning there is no explicit KL regularization — the clip loss alone provides sufficient constraint.

#### Online Prompt Filtering

A supporting technique that the paper introduces is **online prompt filtering**, which selectively removes prompts from the training batch based on the outcome distribution of their sampled responses.

**Motivation.** When the policy model generates `$K = 4$` responses for a given prompt, three scenarios can occur:
1. **All responses are correct** — the prompt is too easy for the current policy, and the Implicit PRM would see only positive examples, providing no discriminative signal for what distinguishes correct from incorrect reasoning.
2. **All responses are incorrect** — the prompt is too hard for the current policy, and the Implicit PRM would see only negative examples, again providing no discriminative signal.
3. **Mixed correctness** — some responses are correct and some are incorrect, providing a balanced training signal for the PRM and meaningful variance in the advantage estimates.

**The filtering mechanism.** The accuracy filter removes prompts where `$\text{mean}(\{r_o(y^1), \ldots, r_o(y^K)\})$` is exactly 0 or exactly 1 — i.e., where all sampled responses have the same outcome. Only prompts with at least one correct and one incorrect response are retained for both PRM training and policy updates.

**Why this filter helps.** The paper presents an ablation study in Figure 2 using RLOO with outcome rewards only, showing that the filter "largely lowers the variance of RL training." The training reward curve with the filter is smoother and shows less oscillation than without it. The authors note that the filter "(1) preserves only the prompts within a certain median-level difficulty range (Yang et al., 2024b) and (2) balances data distribution for the Implicit PRM online training." By removing prompts that provide no contrast between correct and incorrect responses, the filter ensures that every batch contains meaningful learning signals for both the PRM (which learns to discriminate correct from incorrect) and the policy (which learns to shift probability mass from incorrect to correct reasoning patterns).

**Connection to the leave-one-out baseline.** The filter also addresses a failure mode of the LOO baseline: when all `$K$` responses are correct, the baseline is 1 and every advantage is 0, so the policy receives no update signal regardless. When all responses are incorrect, the baseline is 0 and every advantage is 0 or negative, providing no contrast. By removing these cases, the filter ensures that every update step uses prompts where the LOO baseline provides meaningful discrimination.

**Practical note.** In the PRIME experiments, this filter is applied at every iteration. The fraction of prompts retained varies with the policy's current capability — early in training, when the policy generates mostly incorrect responses, more prompts are filtered out; later in training, as the policy improves, the proportion of mixed-correctness prompts increases.

#### Reference Model Choice

The Implicit PRM's reward definition `$r_\phi(y) := \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}$` requires a reference model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`. The paper explores two strategies (Appendix B.1, Figure 9):

**SFT reference (default).** The reference model is the initial SFT model (Eurus-2-7B-SFT), frozen at the start of RL training and never updated. This means the reference model's log-probabilities are constant throughout training, and all variation in `$r_\phi(y_t)$` comes from updates to the Implicit PRM `$\pi_\phi$`. This is the default setting used in the main experiments.

**Policy reference.** The reference model is the policy model's old parameters `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$` (the policy checkpoint from the start of the current iteration). This is the same `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$` used in the PPO clip loss ratio denominator. Under this strategy, the reference model changes at each iteration as the policy improves.

**Comparison (Figure 10).** The training reward curves for the two strategies are "close" — they produce similar outcomes with slight variations. The paper notes a trade-off: using the policy as reference means "the Q value calculated by implicit PRM is the expectation under the distribution of the reference model, so the updating policy could naturally serve as the reference," providing a cleaner theoretical connection. However, using the SFT model as reference is necessary if one wants to compute a KL divergence penalty (though the paper does not use one, setting the KL coefficient to 0). The flexibility of either choice is presented as a practical advantage.

#### Initialization and the "Zero" RL Setting

The paper explores two initialization strategies for the entire pipeline:

**SFT warmup (default for main experiments).** The policy model is first fine-tuned on 230K instruction-response pairs to learn basic reasoning patterns before RL begins. The SFT data uses an **action-centric chain-of-thought framework** (Appendix D) where the model is trained to structure its reasoning into discrete actions: ASSESS, ADVANCE, VERIFY, SIMPLIFY, SYNTHESIZE, PIVOT, and OUTPUT. The SFT model, Eurus-2-7B-SFT, achieves moderate performance (e.g., 3.3% on AIME 2024) and serves as the starting point for RL.

**"Zero" RL from base model (Appendix B.4).** Following the DeepSeek-R1-Zero paradigm (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025), the paper also experiments with starting RL training directly from Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Base, skipping the SFT phase entirely. The results (Figure 13) show that:
- "RL from the base model converges way faster than the SFT model, surpassing the instruct version within 32 steps."
- However, performance "quickly saturated at a very early stage (about 50 steps), which hinders further improvement like in DeepSeek-AI et al. (2025)."
- The 32B base model variant (Figure 14) shows even more dramatic initial gains, surpassing Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct within 16 steps, consistent with the finding in DeepSeek-AI et al. (2025) that larger models benefit more from RL.

The saturation in the zero setting is attributed to "the decrease of response diversity" — as the policy becomes more deterministic, the LOO baseline loses variance, and the PRM receives less diverse training data.

#### Implementation and Hardware Details

**Hardware.** All experiments are conducted on 8×A800 GPUs (80GB each), using the veRL framework (Sheng et al., 2024) for distributed RL training.

**Training loop timing (Table 2).** Each RL iteration for PRIME takes approximately 680.3 seconds, broken down as:
- Rollout (generating 1024 responses): 281.7 seconds (41%)
- Policy update (forward + backward through policy model): 156.6 seconds (23%)
- PRM update (forward + backward through Implicit PRM): 150.9 seconds (22%)
- Other overhead (data transfer, synchronization): 91.1 seconds (13%)

In comparison, RLOO with outcome rewards only (no PRM) takes 530.7 seconds per iteration, with the same rollout and policy update times but no PRM update. PRIME thus requires 24% more time per iteration. However, PRIME achieves equivalent training rewards in 40% of the iterations (Figure 17, which compares wall-clock time directly), meaning the net wall-clock time to reach a given performance level is approximately half that of outcome-only RL — a 2× speedup in practice.

**Memory management.** The veRL framework uses a single-controller design where the policy model, rollout engine, and other components are offloaded to CPU during the PRM update phase, meaning "no extra GPU memory" is required for the additional PRM model beyond what is already provisioned for the policy.

**Training data for RL.** The RL training dataset (Appendix E) consists of 457K math problems and 27K coding problems, sourced from NuminaMath-CoT (Li et al., 2024), APPS (Hendrycks et al., 2021a), CodeContests (Li et al., 2022), TACO (Li et al., 2023), and Codeforces. The data undergoes extensive preprocessing: problems with figures or diagrams are removed, proof questions are excluded (due to difficulty of answer verification), and multiple-choice questions are converted to direct question-answer format using a combination of rule-based filtering and LLM-based reformatting (via Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct). Final validation uses QwQ-32B-Preview and Qwen2.5-Math-72B-Instruct with self-consistency (5 independent attempts) to verify solvability and answer correctness.

**Evaluation benchmarks.** The model is evaluated on 7 reasoning benchmarks: AIME 2024, AMC (Li et al., 2024), MATH-500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021b), Minerva Math (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024), LeetCode (Guo et al., 2024), and LiveCodeBench v2 (Jain et al., 2024). All evaluations use pass@1 (greedy decoding, temperature 0) unless otherwise noted, with additional avg@16 results (temperature 0.3, 16 samples, best-of-16) reported in Table 12.

#### Scaling Behavior with Compute

The paper investigates how PRIME's benefits scale with additional computation along two axes (Section 5.2):

**Longer training (Figure 6, left).** Training PRIME for 800 rollout steps (3200 gradient steps, since each rollout step performs one policy and one PRM update) versus the default 240 steps shows that PRIME "consistently exhibits stable growth and outperforms the baseline with an improvement of 3.7%." There is no sign of plateauing or degradation, suggesting the online PRM update successfully prevents the overoptimization that would otherwise cause performance to decline with extended training.

**More samples per prompt (Figure 6, right).** Increasing the number of responses sampled per prompt from `$K = 4$` to `$K = 16$` yields "non-trivial improvement of approximately 4.4% compared to RLOO." Larger `$K$` provides two benefits: a more accurate LOO baseline (since it averages over more comparison responses) and more diverse training data for the Implicit PRM. However, the generation cost scales linearly with `$K$`, so this represents a direct compute-performance trade-off.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Implicit process reward representation** `$r_\phi(y) := \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}$`: enables training a PRM with only outcome labels, solving C1 (no need to define step-level correctness) and C2 (online updates require only the same outcome labels already collected for policy updates).
- **Online PRM update via cross-entropy on policy rollouts:** prevents the distribution shift and reward overoptimization that plagues offline PRMs, as evidenced by the diverging accuracy curves in Figure 5.
- **SFT initialization for the PRM (rather than a dedicatedly trained reward model):** exploits distribution alignment between policy and PRM at initialization, which empirically outperforms a reward model trained on 500K additional examples.
- **Leave-one-out baseline for both outcome and process rewards:** provides a simple, hyperparameter-free variance reduction mechanism that requires no learned value model (which Section 5.4 shows provides no benefit) and naturally adapts to prompt difficulty.
- **Separate return computation for process and outcome rewards:** avoids numerical instability from mixing rewards with different scales and temporal structures.
- **PPO clip loss** `$\epsilon = 0.2$` **on top of various advantage estimators:** provides stability while being compatible with RLOO, REINFORCE, GRPO, and PPO advantage formulations, making PRIME a general plug-in.
- **Online prompt filtering** retaining only prompts with mixed-correctness responses: reduces training variance and ensures discriminative training data for the Implicit PRM.
- **No KL penalty** (`$\beta_{\text{KL}} = 0$`): the PPO clip loss alone provides sufficient regularization, simplifying the loss function and hyperparameter space.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Online PRM Updates Are the Linchpin — Not Just an Optimization Detail

The dominant assumption in the field prior to PRIME was that the bottleneck for dense rewards is **acquisition**: if only we could affordably obtain high-quality process labels, we could train better reward models and feed them into RL. This assumption motivated enormous investment in PRM data collection — human annotation pipelines (Lightman et al., 2023), Monte Carlo rollout estimation requiring 10× more compute (Wang et al., 2023), and reward model distillation from stronger models. PRIME fundamentally reframes the problem. The bottleneck is not label acquisition but **reward model staleness**. The paper provides direct evidence for this reframing in Figure 5: an offline PRM trained with high-quality data (EurusPRM, 500K examples) starts with strong discriminative accuracy but **steadily degrades** during RL as the policy distribution shifts, dropping from ~0.75 to ~0.60 accuracy. Meanwhile, an online PRM initialized identically but continuously updated on policy rollouts **improves** from ~0.68 to ~0.75. The conclusion is stark: a weaker PRM that is kept current outperforms a stronger PRM that is allowed to go stale.

This is not an incremental observation about training dynamics. It is a diagnosis that shifts the research agenda. If staleness is the primary failure mode, then the field's focus on ever-larger PRM training datasets — the "better labels" strategy — is attacking the wrong problem. What matters is the **update frequency and distribution alignment** of the reward model, not its absolute discriminative power at initialization. The paper shows this most clearly in Figure 4, where initializing the PRM from the SFT model (which starts with zero discriminative ability — it assigns the same reward to all tokens since `$\pi_\phi = \pi_{\text{ref}}$`) **outperforms** initializing from a dedicatedly trained EurusPRM. The explanation the authors offer — "initializing policy model and PRM from the same model largely alleviates the distribution shift issue" — is a statement about the primacy of distribution alignment over data volume. This is a conceptual contribution that generalizes beyond implicit rewards: any online RL system using learned reward signals should prioritize keeping the reward model synchronized with the policy, even at the cost of starting with a weaker signal.

The practical implication is equally significant. DeepSeek-AI et al. (2025) explicitly cited the infeasibility of online PRM updates as the reason they abandoned process rewards entirely, settling for outcome-only RL. PRIME demonstrates that this abandonment was premature — not because PRIME solves label acquisition (it circumvents it via implicit rewards), but because the online update mechanism it enables is the critical missing piece that makes dense rewards viable at scale. This reframes the conversation from "how do we get process labels?" to "how do we keep reward models fresh during training?", which is a fundamentally different — and more solvable — engineering challenge.

**Evidence anchor:** Figure 4 (SFT initialization outperforms EurusPRM), Figure 5 (offline PRM accuracy degrades while online PRM accuracy improves), and the trajectory of DeepSeek-AI et al. (2025) abandoning PRMs due to online update infeasibility (cited in Section 2.2).

---

### Innovation 2: Implicit Process Rewards as a Bridge Between Outcome Supervision and Dense Feedback

The paper's second conceptual contribution is demonstrating that the representation `$r_\phi(y) := \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}$` — previously studied in the context of inference-time search (Yuan et al., 2024b) and preference optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023) — is not merely a convenient trick for avoiding process labels, but a **bridge between two previously separate regimes**: the outcome-only supervision that is cheap and scalable, and the dense token-level feedback that is theoretically superior for RL. What makes this bridge distinctive is that it operates without any lossy compression or heuristic credit assignment. The per-token decomposition `$r_\phi(y_t) := \beta \log \frac{\pi_\phi(y_t|y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t|y_{<t})}$` is an exact mathematical identity that follows from the autoregressive factorization of the two models — it is not an approximation of a step-level value or a learned credit-assignment heuristic.

Prior work attempted to bridge this gap through intermediate constructions, each with significant drawbacks. Value models (critics) in PPO-style actor-critic setups try to learn expected future returns at each state, but the paper shows empirically that they "may not be able to solve the reward sparsity issue effectively due to training challenges" (Section 2.1), and Section 5.4 confirms that both a standard linear-head value model and an Implicit PRM used as a value model provide no benefit over basic REINFORCE. Monte Carlo rollout-based PRMs (Wang et al., 2023; Kazemnejad et al., 2024) produce dense labels but require sampling many completions per step, making them too expensive for online use. Human-annotated PRMs (Lightman et al., 2023) are high-quality but prohibitively expensive to collect at the scale and freshness required for online RL.

The implicit reward representation sidesteps these trade-offs by making the **training phase** operate in the outcome-supervision regime (the PRM sees only `$r_o(y) \in \{0, 1\}$` as its training target) while the **inference phase** produces dense per-token rewards as a natural byproduct of the autoregressive architecture. This decoupling — cheap outcome labels at training time, dense reward signals at inference time — is what makes online updating scalable. The PRM's training cost is identical to that of an outcome reward model (one forward pass, one cross-entropy computation per sequence), but its outputs are token-level. This is not an incremental efficiency improvement; it is a qualitative change in what information can be extracted from the same supervision budget.

The paper also contributes a clarifying negative result about loss functions in Appendix C.2: the constant-offset bias introduced by using cross-entropy with self-normalized language models (since the minimum loss 0 is unreachable when the log-ratio must sum to a value over all sequences) does **not** harm RL training because the relative advantage computation subtracts it out. This is a subtle but practically important observation that removes a potential objection to the cross-entropy formulation and shows that DPO loss — which eliminates the bias by construction — provides no empirical advantage (Table 8).

**Evidence anchor:** Section 3.1 (the implicit reward representation), Appendix C.2 (bias cancellation in relative advantages), Table 8 (CE vs. DPO equivalence), and the contrast with value models in Section 5.4 (Figure 8).

---

### Innovation 3: PRIME as a Universal Adapter for Dense Rewards — Not a New RL Algorithm

The paper makes a deliberate strategic choice that distinguishes it from most RL-for-LLMs contributions: rather than proposing a new advantage estimator or policy update rule, it treats dense rewards as a **modular component** that can be inserted into diverse existing RL algorithms with minimal modification. This is demonstrated concretely in Section 5.3: the same implicit process reward signal is integrated into REINFORCE, GRPO, PPO, and RLOO, and in all four cases, the addition of PRIME's dense rewards yields consistent improvements in both training efficiency and final performance (Figure 7, Table 4).

This modularity is not accidental — it follows from the paper's design decision to compute returns from process rewards and outcome rewards **separately** before summing them into a single advantage signal. By keeping the reward sources decoupled, PRIME can be injected into any advantage estimator that operates on a scalar reward sequence: for RLOO, the process reward return uses a leave-one-out baseline; for GRPO, it uses group normalization (mean and standard deviation across responses); for basic REINFORCE, it is added directly to the outcome advantage without any baseline. The paper provides the exact adaptation formulas (Eq. 7 and Eq. 8) showing how straightforward the integration is.

This is a significant architectural contribution because it lowers the barrier to adoption. The open-source community post-DeepSeek-R1 has largely converged on GRPO as the default RL algorithm for reasoning. If PRIME required switching to a completely different training framework, adoption would be slow and error-prone. By demonstrating that it works as a drop-in enhancement to GRPO — and RLOO, and PPO, and REINFORCE — the paper positions implicit process rewards as **infrastructure** rather than a competitor to existing methods. This design philosophy echoes successful prior contributions like AdamW (which improved weight decay handling across optimizers rather than proposing a new optimizer) and FlashAttention (which accelerated attention without changing the model architecture).

The paper also contributes a practical negative finding that supports this modularity: **value models are unnecessary** when dense process rewards are available. Section 5.4 shows that both learned value models (PPO's critic) and Implicit PRMs used as value models underperform the simpler approach of using the Implicit PRM directly as a reward model. This is a useful simplification: it means practitioners can drop the critic network entirely and use the same Implicit PRM for both reward generation and (if desired) value estimation, reducing the number of trainable components from three (policy, value, reward) to two (policy, reward).

**Evidence anchor:** Section 5.3 (Figure 7, Table 4 — consistent improvements across all four algorithms), Section 5.4 (Figure 8 — reward model usage outperforms value model usage), and the adaptation formulas in Eqs. 7–8.

### Innovation 4: Diagnosing the "Zero" RL Saturation Problem Through the Lens of Reward and Diversity

The paper's "Zero" RL experiments (Appendix B.4) — where RL is applied directly to a base model without SFT warmup, following DeepSeek-R1-Zero — produce results that are superficially impressive but reveal a deeper diagnostic contribution. Starting from Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Base, PRIME-Zero converges dramatically faster than the SFT-initialized variant, surpassing Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct within 32 steps. However, performance then **quickly saturates** — after approximately 50 steps, further training produces minimal improvement. This saturation pattern is consistent with observations in DeepSeek-AI et al. (2025) but was not explained there.

The paper's diagnostic contribution is to attribute this saturation to "the decrease of response diversity" — a plausible mechanism that connects the saturation phenomenon to the leave-one-out baseline. As the policy becomes more capable and deterministic (producing similar responses for each prompt), the variance of the LOO baseline collapses because all `$K$` responses to a prompt receive similar outcome labels. When all responses are correct (or all incorrect), the advantage is zero regardless of reasoning quality, and the policy receives no gradient signal. The online prompt filter (Section 3.3) mitigates this by removing all-correct and all-incorrect prompts, but as the policy's pass@1 increases, the fraction of prompts with mixed outcomes shrinks — the filter removes more and more training data, eventually starving the RL process of meaningful updates.

This diagnosis is significant because it identifies a **structural limitation** of group-based advantage estimators (RLOO, GRPO) when applied to strong policies, independent of the reward signal quality. Dense process rewards from PRIME help by providing per-token signals that remain informative even when outcome labels are uniform (since process rewards capture *how* a correct answer was reached, not just *whether* it was reached), but they cannot fully compensate for the collapse of the outcome-based contrast. The paper's suggestion that larger models benefit more from Zero RL (comparing 7B and 32B in Figures 13–14) and that saturation occurs at higher performance levels for larger models aligns with this diagnosis: larger models have more capacity to explore diverse reasoning paths before collapsing to a deterministic policy.

This is not a solved problem — the paper explicitly leaves it as future work — but the diagnostic framing is valuable because it suggests concrete research directions: adaptive `$K$` that increases as diversity decreases, explicit entropy regularization, or alternative advantage estimators that do not rely on within-batch contrast. The framing also explains why SFT warmup (which establishes a diverse initial policy through imitation learning on many distinct reasoning patterns) delays saturation compared to Zero RL — the SFT model starts with higher entropy in its output distribution, providing more contrast for the LOO baseline to exploit.

**Evidence anchor:** Appendix B.4 (Figures 13–14 — saturation at ~50 steps for 7B, ~80 steps for 32B), Section 3.3 (online prompt filter mechanism), and the discussion of response diversity decrease.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The RL training dataset (Appendix E) comprises 457K math problems sourced from NuminaMath-CoT (Li et al., 2024) and 27K coding problems from APPS (Hendrycks et al., 2021a), CodeContests (Li et al., 2022), TACO (Li et al., 2023), and Codeforces. Math problems undergo extensive preprocessing: problems with figures or diagrams are excluded, proof questions are removed (due to difficulty of answer verification), and multiple-choice questions are converted to direct question-answer format via rule-based filtering, LLM-based classification (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), and LLM-based reformatting (QwQ-32B-Preview and Qwen2.5-Math-72B-Instruct validate solvability and answer correctness through 5 independent self-consistency attempts).
- **Base model(s).** The primary model is Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Base (Yang et al., 2024b), a 7B-parameter model pretrained specifically for mathematical reasoning. For SFT warmup experiments, the model is first fine-tuned into Eurus-2-7B-SFT using 230K instruction-response pairs with an action-centric chain-of-thought framework. The "Zero" RL experiments (Appendix B.4) additionally use Qwen2.5-32B-Base to test scaling behavior. The paper also includes a 1.5B-scale experiment using RhoMath-1.1B (Lin et al., 2024) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B to compare against VinePPO and DeepScaleR respectively.
- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is **pass@1 accuracy** on each benchmark, measured with greedy decoding (temperature 0). For training dynamics, the paper reports **outcome training rewards** (the mean outcome verifier score `$r_o(y)$` across sampled responses, smoothed with a 10-step moving average) and **PRM classification accuracy** (the fraction of correctly ranked pairs where the PRM assigns higher sequence-level reward to the correct response). A secondary metric, avg@16 (Table 12), reports best-of-16 accuracy with temperature 0.3 sampling. The paper also tracks **wall-clock time** (Table 2) and **total gradient steps** (where one rollout step = one policy update + one PRM update for PRIME; for RLOO baselines, one rollout step = one policy update only).
- **Baselines.** The central comparison is against **RLOO with outcome verifier (OV) only** (Section 4.3) — identical architecture, data, and hyperparameters but with only the sparse outcome reward term in the advantage (Eq. 4 without the process reward component). The paper also compares against several RL algorithm variants without dense rewards: **REINFORCE** (Williams, 1992), **GRPO** (Shao et al., 2024), and **PPO** (Schulman et al., 2017), all implemented with the same policy update (PPO clip loss) but using only outcome rewards in their respective advantage formulations (Section 5.3). For the SFT baseline, **Eurus-2-7B-SFT** represents the model after supervised fine-tuning but before any RL. External model baselines include **GPT-4o-2024-08-06**, **Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct**, and **Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct** (the official instruction-tuned variant of the same base model). In the VinePPO comparison (Appendix B.6), **VinePPO** (Kazemnejad et al., 2024) serves as the baseline, and in the DeepScaleR comparison (Appendix B.7), **DeepScaleR-1.5B-Preview** (Luo et al., 2025) serves as the comparison point.
- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Training compute is measured in two ways: **rollout steps** (one complete iteration of sampling, reward computation, and model update) and **wall-clock time** (Table 2, Figure 17). For sample efficiency comparisons, the paper reports the number of gradient steps needed to reach a given training reward threshold. For PRIME vs. RLOO, cost accounting includes: PRIME requires 680.3s per step (281.7s rollout + 156.6s policy update + 150.9s PRM update + 91.1s overhead) versus 530.7s for RLOO (282.4s rollout + 157.9s policy update + 0s PRM update + 90.4s overhead), representing a 24% per-step overhead. However, PRIME achieves equivalent training rewards in ~40% of the steps, yielding a net 2× wall-clock speedup. For the DeepScaleR comparison, GPU hours are reported: PRIME consumed 446.7 A800 GPU hours versus DeepScaleR's ~600 A100 GPU hours for stage 1 (and 3800 total). For the VinePPO comparison, PRIME takes 1.22 hours total vs. 13.94 hours for VinePPO — an 11× efficiency advantage. The default configuration samples `$K = 4$` responses per prompt at each iteration with a batch of 256 prompts (1024 total generations per rollout). The scaling experiments test `$K = 16$` responses per prompt.
- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation in the traditional sense—there is no held-out validation set for hyperparameter selection described in the main RL experiments. The evaluation is conducted on separate benchmark datasets (the 7 reasoning benchmarks listed above) that are distinct from the RL training data, providing a form of out-of-distribution evaluation. The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical significance tests for any results. The primary statistical control is the online prompt filter (Section 3.3), which filters prompts within a batch to retain only those with mixed-correctness responses, reducing training variance.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### PRIME vs. Outcome-Only RL: Training Dynamics and Final Performance

The central quantitative comparison establishes that PRIME's dense implicit process rewards substantially improve both sample efficiency and final policy quality relative to outcome-only RL. Training Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Base via the SFT warmup path, **RLOO with outcome verifier only achieves a final training reward of ~0.47 after 240 steps, while PRIME reaches ~0.50 at 240 steps — a 6.9% improvement** (Figure 3, left). The sample efficiency gain is more dramatic: PRIME achieves the same training reward that RLOO reaches at step 240 after only ~96 steps, representing a **2.5× sample efficiency advantage** (the dotted vertical line projection in Figure 3a). The PRIME training curve also exhibits visibly lower variance—the 10-step moving average oscillates less, especially after step 60—which the authors attribute to the combination of dense rewards and online prompt filtering.

These training dynamics translate directly to downstream benchmark performance. At step 240, PRIME achieves 41.0% average accuracy across the 7 benchmarks versus 36.9% for RLOO with OV only (Table 1). The per-benchmark breakdown reveals where the gains concentrate:

- **AIME 2024:** Both methods achieve 20.0% pass@1 at step 240, suggesting that competition-level problems remain challenging even with dense rewards.
- **AMC:** PRIME achieves 50.6% vs. 47.0% for RLOO — a 3.6 percentage point gain.
- **MATH-500:** PRIME achieves 78.2% vs. 73.2% — a 5.0 percentage point gain.
- **Minerva Math:** PRIME achieves 39.3% vs. 36.4% — a 2.9 percentage point gain.
- **OlympiadBench:** PRIME achieves 40.3% vs. 35.4% — a 4.9 percentage point gain, the largest absolute improvement.
- **LeetCode:** PRIME achieves 31.1% vs. 28.3% — a 2.8 percentage point gain.
- **LiveCodeBench:** PRIME achieves 27.5% vs. 26.7% — a modest 0.8 percentage point gain.

Coding benchmarks (LeetCode, LiveCodeBench) show the smallest relative improvements, which may reflect the different nature of coding verification (partial credit via test case pass rates yields already-denser outcome signals) or the smaller proportion of coding data in the RL training mix (27K coding vs. 457K math problems).

Extended training to **592 steps** (the final Eurus-2-7B-PRIME checkpoint) further widens the gap (Table 1): the model reaches 43.9% average accuracy, with AIME 2024 climbing to 26.7% (surpassing GPT-4o's 9.3%, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct's 20.0%, and Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct's 13.3%), AMC reaching 57.8%, MATH-500 at 79.2%, Minerva Math at 38.6%, OlympiadBench at 42.1%, LeetCode at 33.3%, and LiveCodeBench at 28.6%.

The **avg@16 results** (Table 12) with temperature 0.3 sampling show a similar pattern: Eurus-2-7B-PRIME at step 240 achieves 17.3% on AIME 2024 (vs. 15.4% for RLOO) and 49.2% on AMC (vs. 43.8%). At step 592, these climb to 24.2% on AIME and 54.5% on AMC. The consistent improvement in the sampling-based metric suggests PRIME's gains are not limited to greedy decoding behavior—the model generates better candidate solutions overall.

**Comparison against the SFT starting point.** Eurus-2-7B-SFT (the model before any RL) achieves 28.8% average accuracy (Table 1), with particularly weak performance on competition math (3.3% on AIME 2024, 30.1% on AMC). PRIME at step 240 represents a **12.2 percentage point absolute improvement** in average accuracy over this SFT baseline, with gains concentrated on the hardest benchmarks (AIME: +16.7 percentage points; AMC: +20.5 percentage points). This confirms that RL with PRIME is not merely recovering SFT-level performance but substantially improving it, particularly on problems requiring multi-step reasoning.

**Comparison against Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct.** The final Eurus-2-7B-PRIME model outperforms the official instruction-tuned variant on 5 of 7 benchmarks (Table 1): AIME 2024 (26.7% vs. 13.3%), AMC (57.8% vs. 50.6%), OlympiadBench (42.1% vs. 40.7%), LeetCode (33.3% vs. 11.7%), and LiveCodeBench (28.6% vs. 11.3%). It trails slightly on MATH-500 (79.2% vs. 79.8%) and Minerva Math (38.6% vs. 34.6%). The paper emphasizes (Table 3) that this is achieved with dramatically less data: Eurus-2-7B-PRIME uses 230K SFT examples + 150K RL prompts × 4 samples (600K total RL responses) versus Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct's 2.5M SFT examples + 618K RM training examples + 66K RL prompts × 32 samples (~2.1M RL responses), representing roughly **10% of the total training data**.

#### Scaling Behavior with Additional Compute

The paper investigates two dimensions of scaling (Section 5.2, Figure 6):

**Extended training steps.** Training PRIME to 800 rollout steps (3200 gradient steps) rather than the default 240 shows that "PRIME consistently exhibits stable growth and outperforms the baseline with an improvement of 3.7%" (Figure 6, left). The test accuracy curve for PRIME continues to rise throughout the 800-step period with no visible plateau, while RLOO with OV only appears to flatten earlier. This suggests that the online PRM update successfully prevents the overoptimization-induced stagnation that would cause performance to saturate or decline with extended RL training.

**Scaling the number of samples per prompt.** Increasing `$K$` from 4 to 16 responses per prompt yields "non-trivial improvement of approximately 4.4% compared to RLOO" (Figure 6, right). The test accuracy curves at `$K = 16$` are visibly higher for PRIME than for RLOO across all steps plotted (0–600 steps). Larger `$K$` provides two reinforcing benefits: a more accurate LOO baseline (averaging over 15 comparison responses instead of 3) and more diverse training data for the Implicit PRM (the PRM sees both more positive and more negative examples, improving its discriminative training signal). However, the generation cost scales linearly with `$K$` — 4× more forward passes per rollout — so this represents a direct compute-performance trade-off. The paper does not explore whether further increasing `$K$` (e.g., to 32 or 64) would provide additional gains or whether diminishing returns set in.

#### PRIME as a General Plug-In Across RL Algorithms

Section 5.3 tests whether PRIME's benefits are specific to the RLOO advantage formulation or generalize across RL algorithms. The experiments train all variants for 240 steps and report both training rewards (Figure 7) and benchmark accuracy (Table 4). The key finding is that **PRIME improves performance regardless of the underlying advantage estimator**:

- **RLOO:** PRIME achieves 41.0% vs. 36.9% for RLOO-only (+4.1 percentage points)
- **REINFORCE:** PRIME achieves 37.8% vs. 36.0% for REINFORCE-only (+1.8 percentage points)
- **GRPO:** PRIME achieves 37.8% vs. 36.1% for GRPO-only (+1.7 percentage points)
- **PPO:** PRIME achieves 39.4% vs. 35.8% for PPO-only (+3.6 percentage points)

The training reward curves (Figure 7) show that PRIME variants consistently reach higher final rewards and converge faster than their outcome-only counterparts. The improvements are not uniform: RLOO benefits most from PRIME (both in absolute terms and relative improvement), while GRPO and REINFORCE show smaller but still positive gains. PPO with PRIME benefits substantially (39.4%) but the paper notes that "the PPO variant of PRIME provides no performance gain" relative to using a standard value model — the gain comes from the process rewards in the advantage, not from the critic architecture (which is redundant when process rewards already provide intermediate signals).

The paper also tests an alternative variant: **Implicit PRM as value model** (Section 5.4, Table 4), which uses the Implicit PRM's Q-values `$v_\phi(y_{<t})$` as a baseline for outcome rewards rather than as a direct reward signal. This achieves only 36.6% — worse than basic RLOO (36.9%) and substantially worse than PRIME (41.0%). The finding confirms that the Implicit PRM is more effective when used to **provide rewards** (which directly shape the policy gradient at each step) rather than **estimate values** (which only affect the baseline subtraction in the advantage).

#### Model Scale Experiments: "Zero" RL and 32B

The "Zero" RL experiments (Appendix B.4) — applying PRIME directly to base models without SFT warmup — reveal scale-dependent behaviors:

**7B scale (Figure 13).** Starting from Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Base, PRIME-Zero achieves rapid initial gains, surpassing Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct on math benchmarks within 32 steps. However, performance **saturates quickly**: after approximately 50 steps, the test accuracy curve flattens around 41-42% (Figure 13b), comparable to PRIME-from-SFT at step 240 but achieved much faster. The training reward curve (Figure 13a) similarly rises steeply then plateaus.

**32B scale (Figure 14).** Starting from Qwen2.5-32B-Base, the gains are even more dramatic: PRIME-Zero surpasses Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct within 16 steps, and the test accuracy curve reaches approximately 50% by step 64 versus ~47% for the instruct model (Figure 14b). The training reward curve (Figure 14a) shows a rapid initial climb from ~0.30 to ~0.55 in 20 steps, followed by continued but slower improvement to ~0.58 by step 80. The saturation is less severe than at 7B — the 32B model continues to improve (albeit slowly) throughout the 96-step experiment — consistent with the observation in DeepSeek-AI et al. (2025) that larger models benefit more from RL.

The 32B experiment used only 8×A800 GPUs and 96 steps (a fraction of the compute used for DeepSeek-R1's 671B training), suggesting that PRIME's sample efficiency gains become more pronounced at larger scales where the cost of outcome-only RL's credit assignment inefficiency is amplified.

#### Comparison Against External Methods: VinePPO and DeepScaleR

**VinePPO (Appendix B.6, Table 6, Figure 15).** Training RhoMath-1.1B on the MATH dataset with identical hyperparameters, PRIME achieves consistently higher validation accuracy than VinePPO (Kazemnejad et al., 2024) at every checkpoint: at step 96, PRIME reaches 18.8% vs. 18.4% for VinePPO. The efficiency advantage is the headline: PRIME completes training in **1.22 hours versus 13.94 hours for VinePPO**, an 11× reduction in wall-clock time. This efficiency gap arises because VinePPO's Monte Carlo value estimation requires computing average returns across multiple trajectories for each advantage calculation, which entails additional forward passes that PRIME avoids by using the Implicit PRM's direct token-level rewards.

**DeepScaleR (Appendix B.7, Table 7, Figure 16).** Training DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B with PRIME under DeepScaleR's stage-1 settings (8K maximum response length, same base model and data), PRIME achieves **32.1% on AIME 2024 after 330 steps** versus DeepScaleR-Stage1's 33.9% after 1040 steps (estimated from their blog figure). On the broader average across 5 benchmarks, PRIME reaches 52.0% versus 48.9% for the un-fine-tuned DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B base model, an improvement of 3.1 percentage points. The compute cost is favorable: PRIME consumed 446.7 A800 GPU hours versus DeepScaleR's ~600 A100 GPU hours for stage 1 alone (and 3800 total A100 GPU hours for the full three-stage pipeline). The paper notes that on long-response models, the overhead from PRM updates becomes proportionally smaller because the rollout phase dominates the per-step time: "the overhead would be attributed more to the generation phase, narrowing down the extra time PRIME brings to about 18%." This suggests PRIME is particularly well-suited for training long-chain-of-thought reasoning models where generation cost dominates.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Online vs. offline PRM update:** Online PRM updates are essential for preventing reward overoptimization; Figure 5 shows that an offline PRM's discriminative accuracy degrades from ~0.75 to ~0.60 during training, while an online-updated PRM (initialized from SFT) improves from chance-level (0.50) to ~0.70, and an online-updated EurusPRM improves from ~0.68 to ~0.75. This ablation (Section 5.1) validates the paper's central claim that reward model staleness, not initial quality, is the primary bottleneck. The corresponding policy performance (Figure 4) shows that the offline EurusPRM variant achieves substantially lower training rewards and test accuracy than both online variants.

**SFT initialization vs. trained PRM initialization:** Initializing the Implicit PRM from the SFT model (Eurus-2-7B-SFT) outperforms initializing from a dedicatedly trained EurusPRM; Figure 4 shows the "online SFT PRM" variant achieving the highest training rewards and test accuracy (~41% at step 240) compared to "online EurusPRM" (~39%) and "offline EurusPRM" (~37%). This is a non-obvious result: the EurusPRM was trained on 500K additional examples with explicit reward modeling, yet the zero-shot SFT initialization (which initially outputs zero reward for all tokens) learns to be more effective through online updates alone. The paper attributes this to superior distribution alignment at initialization (Appendix E.5, Section 5.1).

**Single-forward vs. double-forward PRM computation:** At each iteration, the PRM can be used either before updating (single-forward, using the previous iteration's PRM weights) or updated first and then used to re-compute rewards (double-forward, using freshly updated PRM weights). Appendix B.2 (Figure 11) shows that double-forward increases PRM classification accuracy on the current batch (since the PRM has just been trained on that batch), but training rewards "remain close between the two methods." The paper defaults to single-forward for simplicity and memory efficiency, since double-forward requires an additional forward pass without providing clear policy improvement.

**Reference model choice (SFT vs. policy):** The Implicit PRM's reference model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` can be either the frozen initial SFT model or the policy model's old log-probabilities (`$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$`). Appendix B.1 (Figure 10) shows that both strategies produce similar training reward curves with "pros and cons in different aspects": using the policy as reference provides a cleaner theoretical connection (the Q-value is the expectation under the reference distribution), while retaining the SFT model as reference is necessary for computing KL penalties (though the paper does not use KL regularization in any experiment). The flexibility to use either is noted but not explored in depth.

**PRM loss function (CE vs. DPO):** Appendix C.2 (Table 8, Figure 18) compares training the Implicit PRM with binary cross-entropy versus DPO loss. At step 96, CE achieves 32.3% average test accuracy vs. 32.4% for DPO — statistically indistinguishable. Training reward curves (Figure 18a) and test accuracy trajectories (Figure 18b) are nearly identical. The paper chooses CE for memory efficiency, since DPO requires maintaining pairwise preference data structures.

**Online prompt filtering:** Figure 2 shows the effect of the accuracy filter (removing prompts where all K responses are correct or all incorrect) on RLOO with outcome rewards only. The filtered training curve shows "largely lower variance" — the oscillations in the 10-step moving average of training rewards are visibly reduced. The filter's mechanism (Section 3.3) ensures that every retained prompt provides both correct and incorrect examples, giving the PRM discriminative training data and ensuring the LOO baseline produces non-zero advantages.

**Reward model size:** Appendix B.5 (Table 5) fixes the policy model as Qwen2.5-7B-Base and varies the Implicit PRM among Qwen2.5-3B-Base, Qwen2.5-7B-Base, and Qwen2.5-14B-Base. The 7B PRM achieves the best average at 32.6%, compared to 32.0% for 3B and 31.8% for 14B. The differences are small, suggesting "reward model size has limited influence." This is practically important: practitioners can use the same-sized model for both policy and PRM without needing larger reward models, and smaller PRMs do not substantially degrade performance.

**Extended training and scaling of K:** The extended 800-step training (Figure 6 left, Section 5.2) shows PRIME maintains stable improvement versus RLOO throughout, with no overoptimization-induced degradation. The `$K = 16$` experiment (Figure 6 right) shows that scaling the number of responses per prompt provides additional gains (~4.4% improvement over RLOO), confirming that PRIME benefits from increased sample budgets at each iteration. Neither experiment shows signs of diminishing returns at the tested scales.

**VinePPO comparison (Appendix B.6):** Beyond the headline 11× efficiency gain, the per-step validation accuracy (Table 6) shows PRIME consistently ahead: at step 16 (16.4% vs. 15.7%), step 48 (17.5% vs. 17.2%), step 96 (18.8% vs. 18.4%). The gap is small in absolute terms (~1 percentage point) but consistent, and the efficiency advantage (1.22 hours vs. 13.94 hours) makes PRIME substantially more practical for resource-constrained settings.

**DeepScaleR comparison (Appendix B.7):** PRIME under DeepScaleR settings achieves comparable training accuracy within 330 steps (~1/3 of DeepScaleR's stage-1 steps), demonstrating that PRIME's sample efficiency transfers to settings with long response lengths (8K tokens). The per-benchmark results (Table 7) show PRIME improving on MATH-500 (85.1% vs. 82.8%), AMC (68.1% vs. 62.9%), and Minerva Math (30.1% vs. 26.5%) over the base DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, with a slight regression on OlympiadBench (44.6% vs. 43.3%).

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: "PRIME achieves a 2.5× sample efficiency gain and a 6.9% performance improvement over outcome-only RL."** 
Supported with qualifications. The 2.5× figure (Figure 3a) refers to the number of steps needed to reach equivalent *training rewards*, not downstream accuracy. The downstream accuracy gap at the matched step count (step 240) is +4.1 percentage points absolute (41.0% vs. 36.9%), which represents a relative improvement of ~11% over the RLOO baseline, not 6.9%. The 6.9% figure specifically refers to the improvement in final training reward (0.50 vs. 0.47). These are different metrics being conflated in the abstract—a reader could reasonably interpret "6.9% performance improvement" as downstream accuracy improvement, which would be incorrect.

The efficiency claim is further qualified by the 24% per-step overhead of PRIME relative to RLOO (Table 2). The paper properly accounts for this (Figure 17 shows wall-clock time parity at ~25 hours), demonstrating net efficiency gains, but the "2.5×" is a step-count ratio, not a wall-clock ratio. The net wall-clock improvement is closer to 2× (PRIME reaches RLOO's step-240 performance in ~40% of the steps, each taking 1.24× longer, yielding ~50% of the total time).

**Claim 2: "Eurus-2-7B-PRIME surpasses Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct on five key mathematical benchmarks while using only 10% of its training data."**
Supported, but the "10% of training data" framing requires careful interpretation. Table 3 compares total data volumes: Eurus-2-7B-PRIME uses 230K SFT examples + 150K RL prompts × 4 samples versus Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct's 2.5M SFT examples + 618K RM examples + 66K RL prompts × 32 samples. The 10% claim is approximately correct in terms of total training instances, but it compares apples-to-oranges in several dimensions. The Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct pipeline includes a dedicated reward model training phase with 618K examples that PRIME eliminates entirely—but PRIME replaces this with online PRM updates that consume compute (the 150.9s per step shown in Table 2) not captured in data volume metrics. Additionally, Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct uses 32 samples per RL prompt versus PRIME's 4, meaning it performs 8× more rollouts per prompt—a compute cost not reflected in the "10% of data" framing. The claim is technically accurate on its own terms but overstates the resource efficiency advantage by focusing on a narrow data metric while ignoring compute differences.

**Claim 3: "PRIME serves as a general method that works with broad RL algorithms including RLOO, REINFORCE, GRPO, and PPO."**
Supported. Table 4 and Figure 7 provide consistent evidence that adding PRIME's dense rewards improves performance across all four algorithms tested. The improvements are not uniform—RLOO (+4.1 pp) and PPO (+3.6 pp) benefit more than REINFORCE (+1.8 pp) and GRPO (+1.7 pp)—but the direction is consistently positive. The adaption formulas (Eqs. 7–8) demonstrate that the integration is straightforward. 

However, the "general" claim is tested only on these four specific advantage formulations, all within the policy-gradient family. The paper does not test integration with off-policy algorithms (DQN-style), actor-critic methods beyond PPO, or more recent innovations like GRPO with token-level advantages (which would require a different decomposition). The claim that PRIME works "with broad RL algorithms" is supported for the algorithms tested but should not be extrapolated to all RL paradigms without evidence.

**Claim 4 (implicit): "Online PRM updates are the key mechanism preventing reward hacking, and SFT initialization outperforms dedicated reward model training."**
Strongly supported by Figures 4, 5. The ablation comparing SFT initialization to EurusPRM initialization (both online and offline) is well-designed and the results are striking: a zero-signal PRM that learns from scratch on policy rollouts outperforms a PRM pre-trained on 500K examples. Figure 5 provides a mechanistic explanation—offline PRM accuracy degrades as the policy distribution shifts, while online PRM accuracy improves—that directly supports the paper's narrative about the primacy of distribution alignment over data volume.

A missing experiment would strengthen this claim: training the EurusPRM on the SFT model's output distribution (rather than on data from Llama-3.1 and Qwen2.5 series models, as described in Appendix E.5) would test whether the offline PRM's poor performance is due to distribution mismatch at initialization (different model families) or due to the inherent staleness problem (any static training set, regardless of source). The current setup confounds model-family mismatch with the staleness variable. If a EurusPRM trained on Eurus-2-7B-SFT outputs still degrades during RL, the staleness argument is strengthened; if it performs comparably to SFT initialization, then model-family alignment is the dominant factor.

**Claim 5 (implicit from the DeepScaleR and VinePPO comparisons): "PRIME is more efficient than existing dense reward methods."**
Supported for VinePPO (11× wall-clock reduction, direct reproduction under matched settings) but preliminary for DeepScaleR. The DeepScaleR comparison uses only stage 1 of a 3-stage pipeline, runs for 330 steps (vs. DeepScaleR's 1040 for stage 1), and the training curves (Figure 16) for DeepScaleR are "estimated from the figure in the blog" rather than reproduced—the paper explicitly notes that "the provided training logs are broken." This makes the comparison suggestive rather than definitive. The 3.1 percentage point improvement over the base model (52.0% vs. 48.9%) is meaningful but smaller than the gap between DeepScaleR-1.5B-Preview (57.0%) and PRIME (52.0%), suggesting that the full three-stage pipeline with iterative context lengthening provides benefits that PRIME's stage-1-only run does not capture.

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

1. **No confidence intervals or standard deviations.** All results in Table 1, Table 4, and Figures 3–7 are reported as point estimates without any measure of statistical reliability. Given the 500-question test sets for MATH-500 and similar sizes for other benchmarks, differences of 1-2 percentage points may fall within sampling error. The paper's central efficiency claims would be strengthened by reporting variance across multiple random seeds, particularly for the RL training runs which are known to be high-variance.

2. **Single training run per configuration.** The paper does not state that experiments were repeated with different random seeds. RL training is sensitive to initialization and data ordering; the observed differences between PRIME and RLOO (4.1 points at step 240) could be partially attributable to seed effects. The consistency of PRIME's advantage across four RL algorithms (Figure 7) partially mitigates this concern but does not eliminate it.

3. **Benchmark selection favors math-heavy evaluation.** Six of the seven evaluation benchmarks are mathematical reasoning tasks; only LeetCode and LiveCodeBench test coding, and these show the smallest PRIME improvements (2.8 and 0.8 percentage points respectively at step 240 in Table 1). The RL training data is predominantly math (457K math vs. 27K coding problems), so the smaller coding gains could reflect data imbalance rather than a fundamental limitation of PRIME for coding. However, the paper does not investigate this by rebalancing the training data or evaluating on additional coding benchmarks.

4. **The SFT baseline is weak by design.** Eurus-2-7B-SFT achieves only 28.8% average (Table 1) versus Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct's 34.6%, despite being derived from the same base model. The paper notes this is intentional—SFT data was selected to avoid overlap with the RL training data ("we consider ground-truth more essential in RL than in SFT" in Appendix D)—but it means the PRIME gains over the SFT baseline (+12.2 pp) include both the effect of RL training and the effect of now using the held-back high-quality training data. The comparison against Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct (which had access to that high-quality data in SFT) is a fairer reference point, and PRIME's advantage there is narrower: 43.9% vs. 34.6% average, a 9.3 percentage point gain.

5. **No comparison to standard PPO with a separately trained ORM that is also updated online.** The RLOO baseline uses only the outcome verifier, not a learned reward model. A stronger baseline—PPO with an online-updated standard ORM (not implicit, using a scalar head)—would isolate whether the benefit comes from dense rewards specifically or simply from having any online-updated reward signal beyond the binary verifier. The paper compares against value models (Section 5.4) but not against online ORMs, leaving open the possibility that an online ORM with standard architecture would capture some of PRIME's gains.

6. **The "Zero" RL saturation is observed but inadequately analyzed.** Appendix B.4 identifies rapid saturation as a limitation—performance plateaus after ~50 steps for the 7B model—but the investigation is limited to observing the phenomenon and attributing it to "decrease of response diversity." No experiments vary entropy regularization strength, sampling temperature, or the number of responses per prompt to test whether these interventions delay saturation. This is a natural follow-up experiment that would strengthen the diagnostic contribution.

7. **The Implicit PRM's per-token reward quality is never directly evaluated.** All evaluations of the PRM are at the sequence level (classification accuracy in Figure 5). The paper provides no evidence that the token-level decomposition `$r_\phi(y_t)$` actually identifies which tokens contributed to correct versus incorrect outcomes—it may simply distribute the sequence-level signal uniformly or concentrate it on trivial tokens. An experiment correlating per-token rewards with actual error locations (e.g., on MATH problems where the step of the first mistake can be identified) would validate that the implicit process rewards capture genuine credit assignment rather than serving as a smoothed version of the outcome signal.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **PRM quality evaluation at the token level:** As noted above, there is no validation that token-level rewards correspond to actual reasoning quality. A simple experiment: on problems with known solution steps, check whether the PRM assigns lower rewards after an incorrect step is introduced versus before it.

- **Ablation on the separate return computation:** The paper computes returns from process and outcome rewards separately before summing (Eq. 5). An ablation comparing this to directly mixing the rewards (e.g., `$r(y_t) = r_\phi(y_t) + \lambda \cdot r_o(y)$` with `$\lambda$` tuned) would validate whether the separate computation is necessary or merely convenient.

- **Interaction between online prompt filtering and PRIME:** Figure 2 shows the filter's effect on outcome-only RL. Does the filter provide similar variance reduction in PRIME, or does the dense reward signal already provide sufficient stability? An ablation of PRIME with and without the filter would clarify whether this component remains important when process rewards are available.

- **Transfer to non-math reasoning domains:** All experiments are on math and coding. Testing PRIME on scientific reasoning (e.g., GPQA), logical reasoning, or multi-step planning tasks would test the claim that it is a "general" method.

- **Scaling to larger models:** The 32B experiment (Figure 14) is limited to 96 steps. Training to convergence at 32B or 70B scale would test whether the gains observed at 7B scale proportionally with model size, and whether the saturation problem in Zero RL can be overcome with sufficient scale (as DeepSeek-R1-Zero's results at 671B suggest).

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Implicit PRM's Token-Level Credit Assignment Quality Is Never Validated

**The assumption or constraint.** PRIME's entire value proposition — that dense, token-level process rewards improve credit assignment and sample efficiency — rests on the assumption that the per-token decomposition `r_phi(y_t) = beta * log(pi_phi(y_t|y_{<t}) / pi_ref(y_t|y_{<t}))` actually identifies which tokens contribute to correct versus incorrect outcomes, rather than simply distributing the sequence-level signal in some mathematically convenient but semantically arbitrary way. The paper provides extensive evidence that the sequence-level reward `r_phi(y)` discriminates correct from incorrect responses (Figure 5 shows PRM classification accuracy reaching ~0.70–0.75), but **zero evidence that the token-level decomposition localizes credit to causally relevant tokens**. The Implicit PRM is trained with only outcome labels via cross-entropy loss on the aggregated sequence score — there is no supervision signal that encourages the per-token rewards to align with actual error locations, reasoning quality, or step-level correctness.

**The consequence.** If the token-level rewards are not meaningfully localized, PRIME may function as little more than a variance-reduction technique that smooths the sparse outcome signal across tokens in a way that happens to stabilize training — useful, but not addressing the credit assignment problem in the causal sense the paper claims. The policy could be learning from rewards that are high at tokens that are merely correlated with correct answers (e.g., common phrases in correct solutions) rather than tokens that causally produce correctness. This would mean PRIME's gains come from statistical regularization rather than genuine credit assignment, and the method would not generalize to domains where surface-level statistical correlations between tokens and outcomes are weaker (e.g., tasks with more diverse reasoning paths or less formulaic solution structures). It would also mean that PRIME's process rewards would be less useful for downstream applications that require trustworthy step-level confidence estimates, such as interpretability, process supervision, or selective revision of reasoning steps.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper evaluates the Implicit PRM exclusively at the sequence level: Figure 5 shows PRM classification accuracy (whether `r_phi(y_correct) > r_phi(y_incorrect)` for matched pairs), Section 5.1 discusses PRM accuracy as a binary discriminator, and all downstream evaluations measure final-answer accuracy. There is no experiment that introduces a controlled error at a specific step in a solution and measures whether the Implicit PRM's per-token rewards drop at the error location. There is no analysis of the distribution of per-token rewards within correct solutions (do they concentrate on semantically important tokens like mathematical operators and final answers, or are they distributed uniformly?). There is no comparison of the token-level reward assignments to an external process supervision signal (e.g., human step-level labels or rollout-based PRM scores from an independently trained PRM). This is a notable gap because the paper's central theoretical motivation — that dense rewards address the credit assignment problem (Section 2.1, Point 3) — explicitly invokes causal credit assignment, not just variance reduction.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The closest the paper comes to discussing token-level reward quality is Appendix C.3, which frames PRIME through potential-based reward shaping theory and notes that "PBRS does not affect the optimal policy, but does speed up learning, which is aligned with our results." This framing is consistent with PRIME providing a speedup through reward shaping (which can work even with imperfect shaping rewards) rather than through accurate causal credit assignment, but the distinction is not discussed. A practitioner who cares about whether the model is receiving temporally accurate feedback on its reasoning process — as opposed to merely a statistically helpful smoothing of the outcome signal — has no evidence either way from this paper.

---

### 6.2 The Computational Cost of PRM Updates Reduces, But Does Not Eliminate, the Scalability Barrier for Dense Rewards

**The constraint.** The paper positions PRIME as a solution to the scalability challenge of dense rewards in online RL (C2 in Section 2.2). While PRIME eliminates the annotation bottleneck — requiring no step-level labels or Monte Carlo rollouts beyond the policy's own generations — it introduces a **persistent per-iteration compute overhead** from training and running the Implicit PRM. Table 2 quantifies this: each PRIME iteration takes 680.3 seconds versus 530.7 seconds for RLOO with outcome verifier only, a **24% per-step increase**. The PRM update alone consumes 150.9 seconds per iteration (22% of the total), and the Implicit PRM requires its own forward passes to compute per-token log-probabilities for every generated response.

**The consequence.** The paper correctly argues that PRIME's sample efficiency gains (reaching equivalent performance in ~40% of the steps) yield a net wall-clock speedup of approximately 2× (Figure 17). However, this cost calculus omits a crucial variable: **the cost of the PRM relative to the policy depends on model size**. At larger scales (70B, 405B, or beyond), the Implicit PRM is typically the same architecture as the policy model, meaning the PRM update costs approximately as much as one policy update. The paper's 7B experiments amortize this cost across 4× fewer steps, but this assumes the 2.5× sample efficiency ratio holds at larger scales — an assumption the paper does not test beyond a 32B pilot (Figure 14, Appendix B.4) limited to 96 steps, far short of convergence.

If the sample efficiency ratio degrades at larger scales (e.g., if sparse outcome rewards become more effective as model capacity increases, reducing the relative benefit of dense rewards), the 24% per-step overhead could become a net negative. The DeepScaleR comparison (Appendix B.7) provides a partial data point: with long-response models (8K context), the PRM overhead drops to ~18% of per-step time because generation dominates, but this is 1.5B scale. At 70B scale with similar response lengths, generation time would grow superlinearly (due to memory constraints and communication overhead), and it is unclear whether the PRM overhead fraction shrinks or grows.

Additionally, the PRM requires **maintaining a separate model copy in memory** (or offloading/loading it from CPU, as described in Section 4.3: "all other components (policy model, rollout engine) would be offloaded to CPU during PRM update"). The paper states this incurs "no extra GPU memory," but this is true only with the veRL framework's single-controller design and CPU offloading strategy. In frameworks without this infrastructure (which describes most open-source RL-for-LLMs implementations), running PRIME would require either doubling the GPU memory to hold both the policy and PRM simultaneously, or implementing custom offloading that introduces additional latency. The paper does not discuss these implementation requirements.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2 provides the per-step timing breakdown for the 7B experiments. Figure 17 shows the wall-clock training curve comparison. The 32B experiment (Appendix B.4, Figure 14) uses only 96 steps and does not report per-step timing. The DeepScaleR comparison (Appendix B.7) mentions that at 1.5B scale with 8K responses, the overhead "narrowed down to about 18%." No experiments report memory usage, GPU utilization, or offloading latency. No experiments test PRIME at scales beyond 32B.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges this by quantifying the per-step overhead in Table 2 and providing the wall-clock comparison in Figure 17. However, the scalability discussion (Section 5.2) focuses only on the positive finding that PRIME's gains persist with longer training and more samples per prompt, without discussing whether the overhead scales acceptably. The paper frames the cost as solved ("no extra GPU memory") without acknowledging the infrastructure assumptions. The explicit suggestion that future work could explore "larger models" (conclusion) implicitly acknowledges that the current experiments are at a scale where the overhead has been demonstrated manageable but not proven minimal.

### 6.3 The Method Is Validated Only on Mathematical and Coding Reasoning with Verifiable Ground-Truth Answers

**The constraint.** PRIME relies on a **rule-based outcome verifier** `r_o` that provides deterministic binary or continuous correctness labels for completed solutions. For math, this verifier checks exact string match against ground-truth answers; for coding, it computes the fraction of test cases passed. The paper's entire training pipeline — the Implicit PRM's cross-entropy loss, the policy's outcome advantage term, the online prompt filter, and the evaluation benchmarks — assumes access to such a verifier. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by stating that it uses "rule-based ground truth outcome verifiers" (Section 4.1) following the convention of DeepSeek-AI et al. (2025) and related work, but does not discuss the implications of this requirement for broader applicability.

**The consequence.** This constraint excludes a vast range of reasoning tasks where ground-truth answers are not verifiable programmatically. Scientific reasoning (e.g., generating hypotheses, interpreting experimental results), legal analysis, medical diagnosis, creative problem-solving, and most forms of open-ended generation lack deterministic correctness functions. In these domains, one would need to either use a learned reward model (which reintroduces the reward hacking problem that PRIME is designed to avoid, since learned reward models can be overoptimized) or rely on human evaluation (which is too slow and expensive for online RL at scale). PRIME is therefore currently restricted to the narrow class of tasks with automatic verification — a class that is important (math, coding, formal theorem proving) but represents a fraction of the reasoning problems LLMs are deployed for.

Even within math and coding, the verifier requirement imposes constraints. The paper's data preprocessing pipeline (Appendix E) explicitly removed proof questions "due to difficulties in answer verification" and excluded problems with figures or diagrams "since they require visual processing capabilities." Multiple-choice questions were converted to direct question-answer format, potentially altering problem difficulty and reasoning structure. These exclusions mean the training data distribution is systematically skewed toward problems with clean, verifiable final answers — a distribution that may not align with the full space of mathematical reasoning.

More subtly, the verifier's binary nature (for math) limits the richness of the training signal. The Implicit PRM is trained to distinguish completely correct from completely incorrect solutions, but there is no signal for partially correct reasoning — a solution with a minor arithmetic error in step 7 that is otherwise sound receives the same `r_o = 0` label as a solution with completely nonsensical reasoning. This means the Implicit PRM cannot learn to assign intermediate rewards that reflect degrees of correctness, even though its per-token decomposition might in principle support this. The policy similarly receives no gradient signal distinguishing near-misses from complete failures, which may slow learning on problems where the model's reasoning is almost correct but the final answer is wrong.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All experiments use rule-based outcome verifiers. The paper does not experiment with learned reward models, human evaluation, or any form of partial-credit scoring beyond the coding verifier's test-case pass rate (which is inherently continuous). The 7 evaluation benchmarks are either math (AIME 2024, AMC, MATH-500, Minerva Math, OlympiadBench) or coding (LeetCode, LiveCodeBench) — there are no evaluations on natural language reasoning, multi-step planning, scientific QA, or any domain without automatic verification. The paper does not discuss this scope limitation.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not frame this as a limitation or discuss extensions to non-verifiable domains. The broader vision articulated in the conclusion — "we are entering a new era of experience, which is exemplified by RL" — suggests ambition beyond verifiable tasks, but no path is provided for getting there. A reader interested in applying PRIME to dialogue, creative writing, or open-ended reasoning would need to solve the verification problem independently before PRIME's training pipeline could be used.

### 6.4 The Method Is Tested on a Single Model Family with a Single Generalization Pattern

**The constraint.** All primary experiments use Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Base (Yang et al., 2024b) as the starting point, with SFT warmup producing Eurus-2-7B-SFT. The smaller-scale comparisons use RhoMath-1.1B (Appendix B.6) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (Appendix B.7) — all models from the same architectural family (Qwen) with similar pretraining recipes. The paper's central claims about PRIME's effectiveness, sample efficiency gains, and compatibility with multiple RL algorithms are supported only on these Qwen-derived models. The paper states in Section 4 that it uses Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Base "for its great mathematical capabilities" and that "we believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" — a claim made without supporting evidence.

**The consequence.** The Implicit PRM's effectiveness depends on the log-ratio between two language models, which is sensitive to both models' calibration and output distributions. Models from different families (e.g., Llama, Mistral, DeepSeek, Gemma) have different pretraining data mixtures, tokenizers, architectural choices (e.g., attention mechanisms, normalization layers), and calibration properties. The finding that the Implicit PRM works well when initialized from the same SFT checkpoint as the policy model (Section 5.1) is particularly architecture-dependent: it relies on the SFT model being a good initialization for a reward model, which in turn depends on the SFT model's ability to represent outcome-level correctness in its log-probability space — a property that may vary substantially across model families and SFT recipes.

Similarly, the paper's key finding about PRM initialization — that SFT initialization outperforms a dedicatedly trained EurusPRM (Figure 4) — may not generalize. EurusPRM was trained on data generated by Llama-3.1 and Qwen2.5 series models (Appendix E.5), so its poor performance could be due to distribution mismatch between the training data generators and the policy model, rather than an inherent advantage of same-model initialization. On a model family where the SFT checkpoint is poorly calibrated as a reward model, PRIME might require the dedicated reward model training phase that the paper claims to eliminate. The paper's finding that reward model size has "limited influence" (Appendix B.5, Table 5) is also tested only on Qwen2.5 variants (3B, 7B, 14B), and different model families might show different scaling patterns for reward model capacity.

The "Zero" RL experiments use Qwen2.5-7B-Base and Qwen2.5-32B-Base — again, only Qwen models. The saturation pattern observed (rapid initial improvement followed by plateau) might be specific to Qwen's pretraining distribution or the relationship between Qwen's base model capabilities and the MATH dataset's difficulty distribution. Other model families might saturate at different rates, at different performance levels, or not at all.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The Qwen2.5 model family is the only family tested in the main experiments. The smaller-scale comparisons use Qwen-derived models (RhoMath-1.1B is based on Qwen2.5, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B is a Qwen distillation). No experiments use Llama, Mistral, Gemma, DeepSeek-V2, or any non-Qwen architecture. The paper acknowledges "due to resource constraints, we only conducted experiments on models up to 32B" (Appendix A) but does not discuss model family generalizability.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not claim or test generalizability across model families. A practitioner using Llama-3-8B or Mistral-7B as their base model has no evidence from this paper about whether PRIME would work comparably, require different hyperparameters, or fail entirely. The Appendix B.6 and B.7 experiments at least test at 1.1B and 1.5B scales, providing some evidence that PRIME works on smaller Qwen variants, but this is within-family scaling, not cross-family transfer.

### 6.5 The "Zero" RL Setting Saturates Quickly, and the Paper Does Not Resolve This

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper explicitly explores a "Zero" RL setting (Appendix B.4) where PRIME is applied directly to a base model without SFT warmup, following the DeepSeek-R1-Zero paradigm. This is a practically appealing setup because it eliminates the SFT data curation and training phase, enabling a pure RL-driven improvement loop. However, the experiments reveal a sharp limitation: the 7B model "quickly saturated at a very early stage (about 50 steps), which hinders further improvement" (Appendix B.4). The 32B model also saturates, though less severely and at a higher performance level (Figure 14). The paper attributes this to "the decrease of response diversity" — as the policy improves and becomes more deterministic, the leave-one-out baseline loses variance because all sampled responses to a given prompt tend to be either all correct or all incorrect, providing no discriminative advantage signal.

**The consequence.** The saturation means PRIME-Zero cannot sustain long training runs, which limits the total performance gain achievable through this path. In the 7B experiment, test accuracy reaches approximately 41-42% (Figure 13b) and then flattens — comparable to the SFT-initialized PRIME result at step 240 but achieved much faster, suggesting the "Zero" path trades asymptotic performance for training speed. For practitioners, this means the Zero setting is suitable for rapid initial gains but cannot replace SFT warmup if higher final performance is the goal, unless additional techniques to maintain diversity are introduced.

More fundamentally, the saturation highlights a **structural tension** in PRIME's design: the advantage function (Eq. 5) relies on within-batch contrast — the leave-one-out baseline requires variance in outcome labels across responses to the same prompt to produce non-zero advantages. The online prompt filter (Section 3.3) explicitly removes prompts where all responses have the same label, but as the policy's accuracy increases, the proportion of such prompts grows, reducing the effective training data per iteration. This is not a bug but a direct consequence of the LOO baseline design: as the policy converges, the baseline's variance collapses, and the signal-to-noise ratio of the advantage estimates degrades. Dense process rewards from the Implicit PRM partially mitigate this by providing per-token signals that remain informative even when outcome labels are uniform, but they cannot fully compensate — if all responses to a prompt are correct, the process rewards still provide within-response variation (some tokens in a correct solution are "more responsible" for correctness than others), but the LOO baseline for process rewards (averaging across responses) also loses contrast when all responses are similar in quality.

The paper's extended training experiment (Figure 6, left) uses the SFT-initialized variant and shows continued improvement through 800 steps — apparently avoiding the saturation seen in the Zero setting. This suggests SFT warmup provides enough initial diversity to sustain longer training, but the paper does not investigate *why* or at what point the SFT-initialized variant would saturate if trained even longer.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 13 (7B Zero) shows the training reward curve rising steeply to step ~40 and then flattening with "quickly saturated at a very early stage" explicitly noted. Figure 14 (32B Zero) shows saturation at a higher level and later, but still evident by step ~80. The paper's diagnostic discussion ("this is possibly attributed to the decrease of response diversity") is speculative — no experiments measure response diversity (e.g., entropy of the policy's output distribution, n-gram diversity, number of distinct final answers per prompt) during training to confirm this mechanism. The extended 800-step training (Figure 6, left) uses the SFT-initialized variant and shows no saturation, but this is a different setting from Zero RL and the comparison between them is not analyzed.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies this as an open problem ("we leave this as future work") and suggests response diversity decrease as the mechanism. No interventions are tested — no experiments with entropy bonuses, adaptive temperatures, increased `K` (samples per prompt) to maintain diversity, or alternative advantage estimators that do not rely on within-batch contrast. The observation that larger models saturate less severely (comparing Figures 13 and 14) is noted but not investigated further. A practitioner wanting to use PRIME-Zero at scale would need to solve the diversity collapse problem independently.

### 6.6 The Resource Comparison Against Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct Overstates PRIME's Efficiency by Using Incomparable Metrics

**The constraint.** The paper's headline comparison states that Eurus-2-7B-PRIME "surpasses Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct on five key mathematical benchmarks while using only 10% of its training data" (Section 1, Table 3, and discussion throughout). Table 3 breaks this down: Eurus-2-7B-PRIME uses 230K SFT examples + 150K RL prompts × 4 samples (600K total RL responses) and no separate reward model data, while Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct uses 2.5M SFT examples + 618K RM examples + 66K RL prompts × 32 samples (~2.1M RL responses). The paper treats "number of training examples" as the relevant resource metric, framing PRIME as dramatically more data-efficient.

**The consequence.** This comparison conflates data volume with total compute in a way that **systematically favors PRIME**. The key asymmetry is that Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct uses 32 rollouts per RL prompt versus PRIME's 4 — a factor of 8× more generations per prompt, which dominates the RL compute cost. PRIME replaces the dedicated reward model training phase (618K examples) with online PRM updates that consume 150.9 seconds per iteration (Table 2) — a compute cost that accumulates over hundreds of iterations and is not captured in any "data volume" metric. A fair comparison would account for **total FLOPs or GPU hours**, not number of training examples. The paper does not report the GPU hours used to train Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct, so a reader cannot assess whether PRIME is actually more compute-efficient or simply trades one form of compute (more rollouts, dedicated RM training) for another (online PRM updates, more RL iterations).

More subtly, the 10% figure treats all training examples as equivalent, but the quality and difficulty of the data differ. Eurus-2-7B-PRIME's SFT data (Appendix D) was deliberately curated to exclude "many datasets with ground-truth answers" (the paper reserves these for RL training), making the SFT phase intentionally weaker — Eurus-2-7B-SFT achieves only 28.8% average accuracy (Table 1). Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct's SFT data (2.5M examples) was designed to produce the strongest possible SFT model, and it includes the high-quality ground-truth data that PRIME holds back for RL. Comparing PRIME's SFT+RL pipeline (which gets the high-quality data during RL) against Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct's SFT pipeline (which gets it during SFT) conflates data **volume** with data **allocation strategy**. The superior final performance of PRIME could be partly attributable to the decision to use high-quality data in RL rather than SFT, not to any property of implicit process rewards.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 provides the data volume comparison. The paper does not report GPU hours for Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct's training. The Qwen2.5-Math technical report (Yang et al., 2024b) is cited but its training compute details are not discussed in this paper. The paper does not attempt to construct a FLOPs-matched or GPU-hour-matched comparison against Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct, which would be the appropriate baseline for an efficiency claim.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation or attempt to control for compute in the efficiency comparison. The word "data" is used throughout to refer to training examples, never to total FLOPs or GPU hours. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt PRIME over a stronger SFT pipeline cannot answer the question "which uses less total compute?" from the evidence provided — they can only conclude that PRIME uses fewer training examples, a metric of limited practical relevance when generation cost (which scales with rollouts per prompt) is the dominant compute factor in RL training.
