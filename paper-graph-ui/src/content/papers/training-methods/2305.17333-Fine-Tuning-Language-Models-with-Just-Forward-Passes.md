# Fine-Tuning Language Models with Just Forward Passes

**ArXiv:** [2305.17333](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17333)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper proposes **MeZO**, a memory-efficient zeroth-order optimizer that adapts classical ZO-SGD to operate in-place, fine-tuning large language models with the same memory footprint as inference by reconstructing random perturbation vectors from saved random seeds rather than storing them.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **MeZO**, a memory-efficient zeroth-order optimizer that adapts classical ZO-SGD to operate in-place, fine-tuning large language models with the same memory footprint as inference by reconstructing random perturbation vectors from saved random seeds rather than storing them. The authors conduct comprehensive experiments across masked LMs (RoBERTa-large) and autoregressive LMs (OPT-13B, 30B, 66B) on classification, multiple-choice, and generation tasks from SuperGLUE and related benchmarks, demonstrating that MeZO significantly outperforms in-context learning and linear probing while achieving comparable performance to full backpropagation-based fine-tuning on 7 out of 11 tasks — all with up to 12× memory reduction and up to 2× GPU-hour reduction. The paper further shows that MeZO can optimize non-differentiable objectives (e.g., maximizing accuracy or F1 score directly) and provides theoretical analysis establishing that the per-step convergence rate depends on the local effective rank of the Hessian rather than the number of parameters, explaining why MeZO is not catastrophically slow despite classical ZO lower bounds — a finding that depends critically on adequate pre-training and the inclusion of task prompts to ensure a favorable optimization landscape.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Fine-Tuning Memory Costs Block Access to Large Models

The fundamental tension this paper tackles is one that anyone who has tried to fine-tune a modern large language model has encountered: **backpropagation requires vastly more memory than inference, and this gap widens dramatically as models scale**. The paper opens with a stark empirical observation: while you can comfortably run inference with a 30-billion parameter OPT model on a single Nvidia A100 GPU (80GB memory), attempting to fine-tune that same model with Adam crashes with an out-of-memory error — you can only fit a 2.7B model under the same hardware budget (Section 1 and Figure 4). This is approximately an 11× gap in accessible model size between inference and training.

This gap matters for several deeply practical reasons that the paper surfaces (Section 1):

- **Democratization of model adaptation**: Fine-tuning pre-trained LMs is the dominant methodology for adapting models to specific downstream tasks, specialized domains, or human preferences and instructions. If memory costs block practitioners from fine-tuning models larger than ~3B parameters on a single high-end GPU, then the benefits of large-scale pre-training — which have been the central driver of NLP progress — are only partially accessible. Only organizations with multi-GPU clusters can fully adapt these models, creating a resource barrier.

- **The storage cost of customized models**: Even when fine-tuning is feasible, storing a separate fine-tuned copy of a large model for each downstream task is expensive. A 30B parameter model at 16-bit precision requires ~60GB just for the weights. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) techniques like LoRA and prefix tuning partially address this by storing only small adapter weights, but there is still a need for more storage-efficient adaptation methods.

- **Non-differentiable objectives are inaccessible via standard training**: Real-world objectives — accuracy, F1 score, human preference scores, safety constraint satisfaction — are frequently non-differentiable. Backpropagation fundamentally requires a differentiable loss function. The standard workaround, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), involves training a separate reward model and then using RL to optimize it, which is expensive, complex, and introduces its own instabilities. A method that could directly optimize non-differentiable objectives would open up simpler fine-tuning pipelines for alignment.

- **The environmental and economic costs of large-scale fine-tuning**: If fine-tuning requires 8–12× the GPU memory of inference, and often requires distributed training across multiple GPUs, then the energy consumption and hardware costs scale accordingly. Reducing the resource requirements of fine-tuning has both economic and environmental implications.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three main families of prior approaches and articulates their specific limitations:

**Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) reduces parameter count but not activation memory.** Methods like LoRA ([46]; Hu et al., 2022) and prefix tuning ([57]; Li and Liang, 2021) restrict updates to a small subset of parameters — in LoRA, only low-rank adapter matrices are trained; in prefix tuning, only a few tunable vector prefixes per layer. This achieves the storage benefit (only small adapter weights need to be saved per task) and sometimes slightly reduces computation. However, as the paper points out in Section 1:

> "Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods (PEFT) update just a fraction of the network parameters, but still need to cache many activations, because the tuned parameters are scattered throughout the model."

The reason is architectural: in a Transformer, the LoRA-adapted linear layers (query and value projections) appear in every attention block. During backpropagation, the forward pass through those layers still writes intermediate activations to memory, and the backward pass still needs those activations to compute gradients. The memory savings from PEFT are real but modest — the paper reports that fine-tuning OPT-13B with prefix tuning still requires **6× more memory than inference** (Section 1). This is better than the 12× for full fine-tuning, but still a substantial multiplier that prevents training the largest models.

**In-context learning (ICL) uses no training but underperforms and is fragile.** In-context learning — providing a few labeled examples in the prompt and letting the model produce a prediction without any parameter updates — has been celebrated as a way to adapt models at inference time with zero memory overhead beyond the model weights themselves. The paper acknowledges this strength but identifies several key weaknesses (Section 1 and Section 3.2):

- **Performance gap**: ICL often performs worse than fine-tuning, especially for medium-sized models. The paper's experiments consistently show MeZO outperforming ICL across tasks and model scales (Tables 1 and 2).

- **Limited context window**: The number of demonstrations is bounded by the model's context length, which restricts how much task-specific information can be communicated. This is particularly limiting for complex tasks or tasks with long examples.

- **Sensitivity to formatting and example selection**: Prior work ([60]; Liu et al., 2022; [66]; Lu et al., 2022) has documented that ICL performance is highly sensitive to the choice and ordering of demonstrations, making reliable deployment challenging.

- **No genuine adaptation**: ICL does not actually adapt the model — it only changes the input. The model's parameters remain frozen, so there is no learning from additional training data beyond what fits in the prompt.

**Classical zeroth-order optimization is predicted to be catastrophically slow for large models.** The paper is built on a classical zeroth-order method, SPSA (Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation; [88]; Spall, 1992), which estimates gradients using only two forward passes by evaluating the loss at perturbed parameter values. This method has been known for decades. However, existing theoretical lower bounds ([69]; Nemirovskij and Yudin, 1983; [32]; Duchi et al., 2015) predict that ZO methods scale *linearly* with the number of parameters $d$ — that is, optimizing a model with billions of parameters should be billions of times slower than using backpropagation. As the paper states in the abstract:

> "Zeroth-order (ZO) methods can in principle estimate gradients using only two forward passes but are theorized to be catastrophically slow for optimizing large models."

Because of these predictions, ZO methods have historically been applied only in low-dimensional settings within deep learning: finding adversarial examples (where the input space, not parameter space, is perturbed), tuning input embeddings, or optimizing a small number of continuous prompt vectors (as in BBT/BBTv2; [91, 90]; Sun et al., 2022). These approaches sidestep the dimensionality problem by projecting the optimization into a low-dimensional subspace. For example, BBTv2 only optimizes a low-dimensional projection of prefix vectors and is specifically designed for few-shot settings with RoBERTa-large-scale models — it cannot tune arbitrary subsets of the full model parameters.

**No prior work successfully applied ZO to full-model fine-tuning of large LMs.** The paper explicitly positions itself against the gap left by BBTv2 in particular (Section 5 and Appendix F.4): BBTv2 is limited to optimizing low-dimensional projected prefixes rather than the full model or arbitrary PEFT subsets, uses an iterative layer-by-layer scheme, and does not scale to autoregressive models or larger model sizes. MeZO, by contrast, can optimize the full parameter set, any PEFT subset, or non-differentiable objectives, and scales to 66B parameter models.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's position is defined by a key insight that challenges the received wisdom about ZO optimization: **the classical lower bounds assume general optimization landscapes, but fine-tuning a pre-trained model with a suitable prompt operates in a very different regime**. This insight connects to two threads of prior work:

**The role of pre-training and prompts in simplifying the fine-tuning landscape.** Prior work by Malladi et al. ([67]; 2022) examined fine-tuning from a kernel perspective and found that including a task prompt makes the fine-tuning objective closely related to the pre-training objective, causing the optimization to behave in a well-conditioned (near-kernel) manner. Saunshi et al. ([83]; 2021) provided mathematical analysis of why language model pre-training helps downstream task learning. The present paper builds on these insights, demonstrating empirically in Appendix A.1 that **MeZO essentially fails without a prompt** — accuracy drops dramatically (e.g., from 89.6% to 51.9% on SST-2 for RoBERTa-large). The theoretical analysis in Section 4 formalizes this: under the condition that the Hessian of the loss has small *local effective rank* (a property that prior empirical work has observed in pre-trained networks; [74, 75, 82]), the convergence rate of ZO-SGD depends on this effective rank $r$, not the total parameter count $d$. Since $r$ is observed to be much smaller than $d$ in practice (and prior work suggests fine-tuning can occur in a subspace of <200 parameters; [4, 56]), the classical slowdown disappears.

**Reframing the ZO problem from general optimization to fine-tuning-specific optimization.** The paper is not proposing a fundamentally new optimizer, but rather **demonstrating that an existing optimizer (SPSA-based ZO-SGD) becomes practical when the landscape is favorable, and providing the memory-efficient implementation that makes it usable at scale**. The implementation contribution — using random seed reconstruction to avoid storing the perturbation vector $z$ — is what makes the difference between "theoretically possible" and "actually runs on a single GPU for a 30B model." This is analogous to how gradient checkpointing ([18]; Chen et al., 2016) made backpropagation practical for deeper networks by recomputing activations rather than storing them — the algorithmic idea was known, but the implementation detail made it usable.

**Positioning relative to approximate backpropagation methods.** The paper briefly situates MeZO relative to other memory-saving approaches for backpropagation: gradient sparsification ([92]), Jacobian approximation ([1, 19]), computational graph subsampling ([71]), gradient checkpointing ([18]), FlashAttention ([23]), and quantization ([26, 27]). These approaches reduce backpropagation's memory cost but at the expense of (a) still requiring some backward pass, keeping them more memory-intensive than pure forward-pass methods, and (b) potentially introducing approximation errors that compound in deep networks. MeZO offers a qualitatively different point on the tradeoff curve: maximum memory savings (equal to inference) in exchange for a stochastic gradient estimate with higher variance.

**The paper's core wager, stated implicitly, is this:** that for the specific domain of fine-tuning pre-trained LMs on downstream tasks with prompts, the favorable optimization landscape renders ZO gradient estimates sufficiently informative that the dramatic memory savings outweigh the slower per-step convergence. The experimental sections are designed to test whether this wager pays off across model types, scales, and task formats. The theory provides the intellectual justification for *why* it should work, connecting the empirical finding that prompts are necessary (Appendix A.1) to the concept of local effective rank.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

MeZO is a memory-efficient implementation of a classical zeroth-order optimization algorithm, SPSA, that fine-tunes language models using only forward passes — no backpropagation required — by cleverly reconstructing random perturbation vectors from saved random number generator seeds rather than storing them in memory. It solves the problem that standard fine-tuning requires 12× more GPU memory than inference (due to activation caching, gradient storage, and optimizer states), making it impossible to fine-tune models larger than ~2.7B parameters on a single 80GB GPU, by reducing the memory footprint to exactly what inference needs, enabling 30B-parameter model training on the same hardware.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The MeZO system has four major components:

1. **The base pre-trained language model** — a frozen-in-architecture Transformer (RoBERTa-large at 350M parameters, or OPT variants at 13B/30B/66B parameters) whose weights will be updated. It takes a prompted input (text formatted with a task-specific template and label words, e.g., "<S1> It was [MASK]." for SST-2 sentiment classification) and produces a scalar loss value `$\mathcal{L}(\theta; \mathcal{B})$` on a minibatch $\mathcal{B}$. Crucially, the model is treated as a black box: MeZO only needs the ability to evaluate the loss at slightly perturbed parameter values — no internal gradients or activations are accessed.

2. **The SPSA gradient estimator** — a mechanism that estimates the true gradient `$\nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta)$` using only two forward passes with perturbed parameters. Given a random Gaussian perturbation vector `$z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$` in the full parameter space $\mathbb{R}^d$, it computes the symmetric finite difference `$(\mathcal{L}(\theta + \epsilon z) - \mathcal{L}(\theta - \epsilon z)) / (2\epsilon)$`, which is a scalar (a single number) — not a vector. This scalar is then projected back onto `$z$` itself to form a rank-1 gradient estimate `$\hat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\theta) = (\text{scalar}) \cdot z$`. The key insight is that this estimate is an unbiased estimator of the gradient in expectation over `$z$`, but requires storing only a single random seed plus the scalar difference — not the full `$d$`-dimensional vector `$z$`.

3. **The in-place perturbation and update mechanism** — the memory-efficient implementation that avoids storing `$z$` by using Python's random number generator as a reproducible source of pseudo-random numbers. At each training step, MeZO generates a random seed `$s$`, then for each of the four times `$z$` is needed (perturbing parameters positively, perturbing negatively, resetting parameters, and applying the gradient step), it resets the random number generator to seed `$s$` and regenerates the relevant entry of `$z$` on the fly, adding or subtracting it directly from the model parameters in-place. This means MeZO's memory footprint is exactly the model weights plus the current activations — identical to inference.

4. **The optimizer state reconstruction mechanism** — for optimizer variants like MeZO-Adam or MeZO-momentum that normally require storing gradient history (moving averages), MeZO instead saves only the scalar `projected_grad` and the random seed `$s$` at each step (two scalars per step, so 2T scalars total for T steps). The full gradient estimate can be reconstructed at any future step by regenerating `$z$` from the saved seed and multiplying by the saved scalar. For a 20,000-step training run on a 66B model, this requires storing less than 0.1MB of optimizer state, compared to 38MB for LoRA adapters or 12MB for prefix tuning vectors.

Information flows through the system in a strict loop per training step: (1) Sample a random seed `$s$`. (2) Use `$s$` to generate perturbation `$z$` and add `$\epsilon z$` to all parameters in-place via the `PerturbParameters` subroutine. (3) Run one forward pass to compute the positive-perturbation loss `$\ell^+$`. (4) Regenerate `$z$` from seed `$s$` and subtract `$2\epsilon z$` from all parameters in-place (going from `$\theta + \epsilon z$` to `$\theta - \epsilon z$`). (5) Run one forward pass to compute the negative-perturbation loss `$\ell^-$`. (6) Regenerate `$z$` from seed `$s$` and add `$\epsilon z$` to return parameters to their original values. (7) Compute `projected_grad = (\ell^+ - \ell^-) / (2\epsilon)`. (8) Regenerate `$z$` from seed `$s$` one final time and, for each parameter `$\theta_i$`, sample the corresponding `$z_i$` and update `$\theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i - \eta_t \cdot \text{projected\_grad} \cdot z_i$`. This single step requires exactly two forward passes, zero backward passes, and no gradient storage.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the SPSA gradient estimator (Definition 1): what it computes, how it relates noise to gradient direction, why the symmetric two-point form is needed, and what `$\epsilon$` and `$n$` control. This is the mathematical foundation for everything that follows.

- **Second**, the in-place memory-efficient implementation (Algorithm 1): how random seed reconstruction eliminates the memory cost of storing `$z$`, why parameters are perturbed and reset in-place, and the crucial detail of perturbing entire weight matrices rather than individual scalars for speed.

- **Third**, the extensions to other optimizers (MeZO-Adam, MeZO-momentum) and the forward auto-differentiation alternative: how optimizer history is reconstructed from saved seeds, why layerwise adaptive variants were explored but didn't help, and why forward-mode auto-differentiation (the JVP method) uses more memory than MeZO.

- **Fourth**, the role of prompts in making MeZO work: the empirical finding that prompts are essential, the connection to prior theoretical work on kernel behavior and loss landscape conditioning, and how this motivates the theoretical analysis in Section 4.

- **Fifth**, the training configurations and hyperparameter grids: learning rates, batch sizes, perturbation scales, step counts, and PEFT integration — the concrete settings that make MeZO practical at scale.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is an **implementation and systems paper** whose core idea is that the classical SPSA zeroth-order gradient estimator can be made memory-efficient enough to fine-tune billion-parameter language models by exploiting pseudo-random number generator reproducibility to avoid storing perturbation vectors, and that this approach works well in practice because pre-trained models with appropriate task prompts have loss landscapes with small local effective rank, making ZO gradient estimates sufficiently informative despite their high variance.

#### The SPSA Gradient Estimator

The mathematical heart of MeZO is the Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation (SPSA) gradient estimator, introduced by Spall ([88]; 1992). Given a model with parameters `$\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$` (where `$d$` is the total number of scalar parameters — billions for large LMs) and a scalar loss function `$\mathcal{L}(\theta; \mathcal{B})$` evaluated on a minibatch `$\mathcal{B}$`, the SPSA gradient estimate is defined as:

$$\hat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\theta; \mathcal{B}) = \frac{\mathcal{L}(\theta + \epsilon z; \mathcal{B}) - \mathcal{L}(\theta - \epsilon z; \mathcal{B})}{2\epsilon} \cdot z$$

where `$z \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is a random perturbation vector with `$z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$` (each coordinate independently drawn from a standard normal distribution), and `$\epsilon \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$` is a small perturbation scale that controls the finite-difference approximation quality.

**What it computes:** The numerator `$\mathcal{L}(\theta + \epsilon z; \mathcal{B}) - \mathcal{L}(\theta - \epsilon z; \mathcal{B})$` is a scalar — the difference between the loss evaluated at two slightly perturbed parameter configurations. Dividing by `$2\epsilon$` converts this scalar difference into a finite-difference approximation of the directional derivative of the loss along the direction `$z$`. Mathematically, as `$\epsilon \to 0$`, this scalar converges to the Jacobian-vector product `$z^\top \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta; \mathcal{B})$` — the projection of the true gradient onto the random direction `$z$`. The final multiplication by `$z$` converts this single scalar projection back into a rank-1 vector in parameter space: `$(z^\top \nabla \mathcal{L}) \cdot z = z z^\top \nabla \mathcal{L}$`. This is a rank-1 matrix `$z z^\top$` times the true gradient — the gradient estimate lives entirely in the one-dimensional subspace spanned by `$z$`.

In expectation over the random vector `$z$`, the estimate is unbiased:

$$\mathbb{E}_z[\hat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\theta; \mathcal{B})] = \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta; \mathcal{B})$$

This follows from the identity `$\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)}[z z^\top] = I_d$` — the rank-1 outer product `$z z^\top$` is, on average, the identity matrix. Therefore, the SPSA estimate is an unbiased but high-variance estimator of the true gradient: each individual estimate captures only the gradient's projection onto a single random direction, but over many steps, the noise averages out.

**Why this form:** The symmetric two-point form `$\mathcal{L}(\theta + \epsilon z) - \mathcal{L}(\theta - \epsilon z)$` is essential for obtaining an unbiased estimate. A one-sided estimate using only `$\mathcal{L}(\theta + \epsilon z) - \mathcal{L}(\theta)$` would be biased by the first-order Taylor remainder. The symmetric form cancels the even-order terms in the Taylor expansion, leaving the bias as `$O(\epsilon^2)$` rather than `$O(\epsilon)$`, which is important because the perturbation scale `$\epsilon$` cannot be made arbitrarily small due to floating-point precision. The paper uses `$\epsilon = 10^{-3}$` as the default for full-parameter tuning (Table 15, Table 16), which is large enough to avoid numerical underflow in the loss difference computation while small enough that the Taylor approximation is reasonable.

The choice of Gaussian perturbations `$z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$` differs from Spall's original formulation, which used perturbations with finite inverse moments. The original SPSA perturbed parameters by `$1/z$` rather than `$z$`, which required `$z$` to have well-behaved reciprocals — a property Gaussians lack (since `$1/z$` has infinite variance for zero-mean Gaussians). The paper adopts the Gaussian perturbation convention from more recent theoretical and empirical works, noting in a footnote (the paper's footnote 2): "1/z is very large with high probability for a zero-mean Gaussian z, so we adopt the standard in many theoretical and empirical works and perturb the parameters by z with z as a Gaussian random variable."

The paper defines `$n$`-SPSA as the average of `$n$` independent SPSA estimates, each using a different random vector `$z_1, \ldots, z_n$`, with all other parameters kept the same. This reduces gradient estimate variance by a factor of `$n$` at the cost of `$2n$` forward passes per step. The paper performs ablation experiments with `$n \in \{1, 4, 16\}$` (Appendix A.2, Table 6) and finds that increasing `$n$` while keeping the total forward-pass budget fixed (by reducing the number of steps proportionally) provides only marginal benefits: on SST-2 with `$n=16$`, accuracy is 90.4% vs. 89.6% for `$n=1$` under a constant schedule, but on TREC the trend reverses (62.8% vs. 66.7%). The conclusion: "n = 1 is the most efficient" when considering the total forward-pass budget, and all subsequent experiments use `$n=1$`.

#### The In-Place Memory-Efficient Implementation (Algorithm 1)

The naive implementation of SPSA would require storing the perturbation vector `$z \in \mathbb{R}^d$` in GPU memory — which, for a 30B-parameter model at 16-bit precision, means an additional ~60GB (doubling the memory cost of inference). The MeZO algorithm eliminates this storage through what is essentially a **time-memory tradeoff using pseudo-random number generator reproducibility**.

The core insight is algorithmic rather than hardware-dependent: a pseudo-random number generator (PRNG) is a deterministic function that, given a seed `$s$`, produces an identical sequence of "random" numbers every time it is reset to that same seed. MeZO exploits this by:

1. **Storing only the seed, not the vector:** At the start of each training step, MeZO samples a single random integer seed `$s$` (e.g., a 32-bit integer) and saves it to a variable. The giant perturbation vector `$z$` is never stored as a PyTorch tensor — it exists only transiently as numbers are drawn from the PRNG and immediately used to perturb parameters.

2. **Regenerating `$z$` on demand:** Each of the four times `$z$` is needed during a single training step, the `PerturbParameters` subroutine is called, which resets the random number generator to seed `$s$`, then loops over all model parameters, drawing `$z_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$` for each scalar parameter and applying the perturbation `$\theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i + \epsilon \cdot z_i$` (or `$-\epsilon z_i$`, or `$-2\epsilon z_i$`, depending on the phase).

The full training step proceeds in eight phases (Algorithm 1):

- **Phase 1 — Sample seed:** A random integer seed `$s$` is generated. This is the only new random state stored per step.

- **Phase 2 — Positive perturbation:** The `PerturbParameters(θ, ε, s)` subroutine is called, which resets the PRNG to seed `$s$`, loops over every parameter `$\theta_i$` in the model, samples `$z_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$`, and updates `$\theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i + \epsilon z_i$` in-place (mutating the model's existing parameter buffer). After this phase, the model's parameters are at `$\theta + \epsilon z$`.

- **Phase 3 — Positive loss evaluation:** A standard forward pass is run on minibatch `$\mathcal{B}$` to compute `$\ell^+ = \mathcal{L}(\theta + \epsilon z; \mathcal{B})$`. This uses exactly the memory that inference uses — the activations are computed, the loss is returned as a Python scalar, and the activations are immediately discarded. No backward pass is triggered, so no activation caching for gradient computation occurs.

- **Phase 4 — Negative perturbation:** The `PerturbParameters(θ, -2ε, s)` subroutine is called with the *same* seed `$s$`. Resetting the PRNG to the same seed ensures the exact same sequence of `$z_i$` values is generated. This time, each parameter is updated in-place as `$\theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i - 2\epsilon z_i$`. Since the parameters are currently at `$\theta_i + \epsilon z_i$` (from Phase 2), this subtracts `$2\epsilon z_i$` and moves them to `$\theta_i - \epsilon z_i$`. This is the negative-perturbed configuration.

- **Phase 5 — Negative loss evaluation:** A second forward pass computes `$\ell^- = \mathcal{L}(\theta - \epsilon z; \mathcal{B})$`, again discarding activations immediately.

- **Phase 6 — Parameter reset:** The `PerturbParameters(θ, ε, s)` subroutine is called again, regenerating `$z_i$` from seed `$s$` and adding `$\epsilon z_i$` to each parameter. Starting from `$\theta_i - \epsilon z_i$` (from Phase 4), this adds `$\epsilon z_i$` and returns the parameters to their original, unperturbed state `$\theta_i$`. This reset step is crucial: it ensures that the model parameters are correct for the next training step, regardless of what the gradient estimate actually was.

- **Phase 7 — Projected gradient computation:** The scalar `projected_grad = (ℓ⁺ - ℓ⁻) / (2ε)` is computed. This is the finite-difference approximation of the directional derivative `$z^\top \nabla \mathcal{L}$` — a single floating-point number.

- **Phase 8 — Parameter update:** The PRNG is reset to seed `$s$` one final time. For each parameter `$\theta_i$`, the code samples `$z_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$` and applies the gradient step `$\theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i - \eta_t \cdot \text{projected\_grad} \cdot z_i$` in-place. The learning rate `$\eta_t$` is the step-specific learning rate from the schedule (constant in all MeZO experiments). Because `projected_grad` is just a scalar, this update simply adds a vector proportional to `$z$` — each parameter moves by `$-\eta_t \cdot \text{projected\_grad} \cdot z_i$`.

**Practical speed optimization: perturbing weight matrices, not scalars.** The paper notes that perturbing each parameter individually in a Python loop would be prohibitively slow for large models: "Algorithm 1 describes perturbing each parameter separately, which may be time-consuming for large models. In practice, we can save time by perturbing an entire weight matrix instead of each scalar independently." The implementation generates the entire perturbation matrix `$Z$` for a weight matrix `$W$` as a single tensor (using `torch.randn_like(W)`) and applies it in one vectorized operation. This incurs temporary memory equal to the size of the largest weight matrix in the model — typically the word embedding matrix (e.g., 0.86GB for OPT-66B). The perturbation matrix is then immediately deallocated after the in-place update. This is the only additional memory beyond the model weights and current activations, and it is small relative to the total model size and transitory.

**Why use four regenerations instead of storing `$z$`?** The alternative would be to generate `$z$` once and store it in a PyTorch tensor for reuse in the four phases. For a 30B-parameter model, storing `$z$` at 16-bit precision requires 60GB — exactly doubling the memory footprint (since the model weights themselves are ~60GB). By regenerating `$z$` from the seed each time, MeZO avoids this 60GB allocation entirely. The cost is computational: each regeneration requires looping over all parameters and sampling from the PRNG. However, modern PRNGs (like PyTorch's default Philox or MT19937) are extremely fast — the time spent regenerating `$z$` four times is negligible compared to the two forward passes through a 30B-parameter Transformer. The paper reports a 7.74× per-step speedup for MeZO vs. full-parameter fine-tuning on OPT-30B (Appendix F.6, Table 23), so the regeneration overhead is clearly dwarfed by the backpropagation savings.

**The `n`-SPSA extension (Algorithm 2).** For `$n > 1$`, MeZO uses the same seed-reconstruction trick but with `$n$` independent random seeds and `$n$` corresponding `projected_grad` scalars — a total of $2n$ scalars stored per step. The positive perturbation phase is repeated `$n$` times, each time with a different seed `$s_j$`, computing `$\ell^+_j$` and `$\ell^-_j$` independently. The final gradient estimate is the average of the `$n$` independent SPSA estimates, so the update becomes `$\theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i - (\eta_t / n) \cdot \sum_{j=1}^n \text{projected\_grad}_j \cdot z_{i,j}$`. Since each of the `$n$` estimates requires 2 forward passes, the total forward-pass cost per step is `$2n$`.

#### Extensions to Other Optimizers and Variance Modification

The SPSA estimate can be plugged into any gradient-based optimizer by replacing the true gradient with the ZO estimate. However, popular optimizers like Adam and SGD-with-momentum normally require storing auxiliary state (first and second moment estimates of the gradient, each of dimension `$d$`), which would defeat MeZO's memory efficiency. The paper addresses this with **optimizer state reconstruction from saved seeds**.

**MeZO-Adam and MeZO-momentum (Appendix B.2, Algorithm not shown).** The key observation is that to reconstruct the gradient history at step `$t$`, one only needs the sequence of `projected_grad` scalars and random seeds from the past `$t$` steps. Formally, the gradient estimate at step `$k$` can be regenerated as `$\hat{\nabla}_k = \text{projected\_grad}_k \cdot z^{(k)}$`, where `$z^{(k)}$` is recoverable from `seed_k`. Therefore, instead of storing the full moment buffers `$m_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$` and `$v_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$` for Adam, MeZO-Adam stores two Python lists of length `$T$` (the total number of steps): one for the sequence of `projected_grad` values, and one for the sequence of seeds. At step `$t$`, the moving average of the gradient is recomputed on the fly:

$$m_t = \beta_1 \cdot m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1) \cdot \hat{\nabla}_t$$

This recomputation requires regenerating `$z^{(1)}, \ldots, z^{(t)}$` from their respective seeds and multiplying by the saved scalars. For a 20,000-step training run, this requires storing 40,000 scalars (20,000 seeds + 20,000 projected gradients) — roughly 320KB at 32-bit precision. The computational cost is regenerating `$t$` perturbation vectors and computing the weighted sum, which is `$O(td)$` work per step (making it increasingly expensive late in training). In practice, the paper reports using MeZO-Adam for RoBERTa-large experiments (Table 18) and finding that it sometimes outperforms MeZO but is not consistently better, so the simpler SGD variant is the default.

**Layerwise adaptive rates and variance modification (Appendix B.3-B.4).** The paper draws inspiration from layerwise adaptive optimizers like LARS, noting that "deep Transformers can have gradients of different scales for each layer." They design MeZO variants that estimate per-layer gradient norms (using Proposition 1: a ZO estimate of `$\|\nabla_{\theta_\ell} \mathcal{L}\|^2$` via `$\mathcal{L}(\theta + \epsilon z_\ell) - \mathcal{L}(\theta - \epsilon z_\ell) / (2\epsilon)$` where `$z_\ell$` is non-zero only in layer `$\ell$`'s parameters) and use these to rescale the per-layer learning rate. Table 8 shows that this approach actually hurts performance compared to vanilla MeZO, with substantial drops on TREC (from 66.7% to 32.6% when computing gradient norms from ZO estimates). The paper also experiments with modifying the variance of the SPSA estimate by dividing by parameter norms (Table 9) — performance is similar to baseline but not improved. The conclusion is that the straightforward variance analysis (which would predict benefits from variance reduction) is not the right lens — the effective rank view in Theorem 1 better characterizes the optimization dynamics.

**One-point estimators (Appendix B.5).** A natural way to halve the forward-pass cost is to use a one-point gradient estimate instead of the two-point SPSA. The paper tests an estimator from Zhang et al. ([113]) that reuses the loss from the previous iteration:

$$\hat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\theta_t; \mathcal{B}_t) = \frac{\mathcal{L}(\theta_t + \epsilon z_t; \mathcal{B}_t) - \mathcal{L}(\theta_{t-1} + \epsilon z_{t-1}; \mathcal{B}_{t-1})}{\epsilon} \cdot z_t$$

This requires only one forward pass per step. However, Table 11 shows it is substantially less effective than SPSA when controlling for total forward passes: after 20K steps with SPSA (40K forward passes) vs. 40K steps with the one-point estimator (also 40K forward passes), SPSA achieves 92.8% on SST-2 vs. 91.8% for one-point, 82.9% on SNLI vs. 74.4%, and 76.7% on RTE vs. 68.7%. The performance gap likely stems from the one-point estimator's reliance on the previous optimization step's loss, which introduces additional noise from the changing objective landscape between steps.

**Forward auto-differentiation (Appendix D).** The paper discusses an alternative approach: as `$\epsilon \to 0$`, the SPSA estimate approaches `$z z^\top \nabla \mathcal{L}$`, and the term `$z^\top \nabla \mathcal{L}$` — the Jacobian-vector product (JVP) — can be computed using forward-mode automatic differentiation in a single forward pass with additional memory equal to the largest activation in the network. This JVP approach requires (a) storing `$z$` on the GPU (to project the JVP back into parameter space), and (b) storing intermediate activations for the JVP computation. Appendix D Table 12 profiles RoBERTa-large with MultiRC and finds that forward auto-differentiation consumes 830MB excess memory vs. 327MB for inference/MeZO, meaning MeZO is strictly more memory-efficient. Additionally, the forward-mode AD approach forces `$\epsilon = 0$`, which removes potentially beneficial third-and-higher-order Taylor expansion terms from the estimate — the paper notes that "using a non-zero `$\epsilon$` in SPSA, which is not possible through the JVP method, may boost generalization by promoting a sharpness-minimizing term."

#### The Crucial Role of Task Prompts

The paper discovers empirically that MeZO essentially fails without a task-specific prompt. Appendix A.1 Table 5 shows the dramatic contrast: on SST-2 with RoBERTa-large and `$k=16$` examples per class, MeZO with a prompt achieves 89.6% accuracy but without a prompt drops to 51.9% — essentially random-chance level for a binary classification task. On SNLI (3-way classification), the drop is from 65.1% to 34.8% (near-random at 33.3%). On TREC (6-way classification), the drop is from 66.7% to 19.5% (below the 16.7% random baseline — actively harmful).

The prompts used (detailed in Appendix E.2) follow the template format from Gao et al. ([35]; 2021). For masked LMs like RoBERTa-large, each task gets a template with a `[MASK]` token (e.g., for SST-2 sentiment: `"<S1> It was [MASK]."` where `<S1>` is the input sentence) and a set of label words that fill the mask (e.g., `{"great", "terrible"}` for positive/negative). For autoregressive LMs like OPT, classification tasks use prompts like `"<text> It was terrible/great"` and compute the log-likelihood of the label words; multiple-choice tasks evaluate each candidate separately and pick the one with the highest average log-likelihood; QA tasks use teacher forcing during training and greedy decoding at inference.

**Why prompts matter for ZO optimization:** The paper draws on prior work by Malladi et al. ([67]; 2022), which demonstrated that including a prompt makes the fine-tuning objective closely related to the pre-training objective, causing the optimization trajectory to exhibit near-kernel dynamics (i.e., the model behaves roughly like a linear model in a fixed feature space). In kernel-like regimes, the loss landscape is well-conditioned: the Hessian has low effective rank (most eigenvalues are near zero, and the bulk of the spectral mass is concentrated in a few principal directions). This directly connects to the theoretical analysis in Section 4, where the convergence rate of ZO-SGD is shown to depend on the local effective rank `$r$` of the Hessian, not the parameter dimension `$d$`. Prompts serve as the mechanism that ensures `$r \ll d$` — by reformulating the task in the model's pre-training "language," the optimization problem lives in a low-dimensional subspace of the parameter space.

The paper also acknowledges that prior work on kernel behavior only verified the phenomenon for RoBERTa-base, not for the larger models (RoBERTa-large, OPT-13B, 30B, 66B) used in this paper. Some of the tasks (e.g., TREC) were reported to not exhibit kernel behavior in the original study, yet MeZO still works on them — suggesting the effective rank condition may be more general than strict kernel dynamics. The theoretical analysis in Section 4 formalizes a weaker condition: the Hessian only needs to have small local effective rank in a neighborhood of the current iterate, not necessarily globally.

#### Training Configurations and Hyperparameter Grids

**RoBERTa-large experiments (k=16 and k=512 settings):** The hyperparameter grid is in Table 15. All MeZO runs use:

- **Batch size:** 64 (constant across k=16 and k=512 settings). This is larger than typical fine-tuning batch sizes (2–32) and was chosen because "using a larger batch size consistently yielded faster optimization" (Appendix E.3).

- **Learning rate:** Searched over `$\{10^{-7}, 10^{-6}, 10^{-5}\}$` with a constant schedule (no warmup, no decay). The ablation experiments in Appendix A found that "using a linearly decreasing learning rate schedule during training, as was done for fine-tuning with backpropagation, does not help or hurt MeZO. Similarly, using a learning rate warmup leads to identical results." The constant learning rate simplifies the algorithm.

- **Perturbation scale `$\epsilon$`:** `$10^{-3}$` (fixed, not searched). Ablations showed this did not significantly impact performance.

- **Weight decay:** 0 (no regularization). This contrasts with standard fine-tuning, where weight decay is typically positive.

- **Training steps:** 100,000 for MeZO, 1,000 for fine-tuning. The 100× step count difference reflects the higher variance of the ZO gradient estimate — MeZO needs many more noisy steps to achieve comparable optimization progress. However, each MeZO step is substantially faster (no backward pass), so the wall-clock time tradeoff is more nuanced (Appendix F.6).

- **Validation and checkpoint selection:** "We evaluate the model on validation sets every 1/10 of total training steps and save the best validation checkpoint." This means evaluating at steps 10K, 20K, ..., 100K and selecting the checkpoint with the best validation performance, which is standard practice.

For MeZO with prefix tuning, the hyperparameters differ: learning rate is searched over `$\{10^{-2}, 5 \times 10^{-3}, 10^{-3}\}$` (much higher because only a few prefix vectors are being optimized), `$\epsilon = 10^{-1}$` (larger perturbation for the low-dimensional prefix subspace), and 5 prefix tokens are used. For MeZO with LoRA: learning rate over `$\{10^{-5}, 5 \times 10^{-5}, 10^{-4}\}$`, `$\epsilon = 10^{-3}$`, weight decay 0.1 (LoRA-specific regularization), and LoRA rank `$r=8$` with scaling `$\alpha=16$`.

For fine-tuning baselines: batch sizes vary by k (2, 4, or 8 for k=16; 8, 16, or 32 for k=512), learning rates are searched over `$\{10^{-5}, 3 \times 10^{-5}, 5 \times 10^{-5}\}$` for Adam, with linear schedule and 1,000 steps. An SGD baseline is also run with higher learning rates `$\{10^{-4}, 5 \times 10^{-4}, 10^{-3}, 5 \times 10^{-3}, 10^{-2}\}$`.

**OPT experiments (13B, 30B, 66B):** The hyperparameter grid is in Table 16. Key differences from RoBERTa:

- **Batch size:** 16 (smaller than RoBERTa's 64, likely due to memory constraints of larger models). The paper uses gradient accumulation implicitly through the optimizer.

- **Learning rate:** Searched over `$\{10^{-6}, 10^{-7}\}$` for most tasks, with SQuAD and DROP also searching `$\{5 \times 10^{-7}, 10^{-6}\}$`. The lower learning rates reflect the larger model scale and more challenging optimization landscape.

- **`$\epsilon$`:** `$10^{-3}$` for full-parameter and LoRA; `$10^{-1}$` for prefix tuning.

- **Training steps:** 20,000 for MeZO, 5 epochs for fine-tuning (approximately 625 steps for 1,000 training examples with batch size 8). The ratio is ~32× more steps for MeZO, less extreme than the 100× for RoBERTa, likely because OPT fine-tuning takes more epochs and MeZO benefits from more data.

- **Validation and checkpoint selection:** "We evaluate the model on validation sets every 1/5 of total training steps and save the best validation checkpoint." For 20K steps, this means evaluating at steps 4K, 8K, ..., 20K. For the largest 30B and 66B models, "we do not evaluate the intermediate validation performance and directly use the last checkpoint for evaluation, due to the high storage cost of intermediate checkpoints of large models."

For in-context learning (ICL), 32 examples are used in the context. The paper notes: "We also try filling in as many examples as possible in the context but this does not improve performance and sometimes leads to unstable results."

**Non-differentiable objectives (Section 3.3, Appendix E.6):** For RoBERTa-large maximizing accuracy, the same grid as MeZO (Table 15) is used, with the objective being classification accuracy (fraction of correct predictions) rather than cross-entropy loss. Since accuracy is piecewise-constant and non-differentiable, backpropagation cannot compute its gradient, but MeZO's loss-difference approach applies seamlessly: the loss function `$\mathcal{L}$` in the SPSA formula is simply replaced by the negative accuracy (since MeZO minimizes, and one wants to maximize accuracy). For SQuAD with F1 score as the objective: batch size 16, learning rate searched over `$\{10^{-6}, 5 \times 10^{-6}, 10^{-5}\}$` for full-parameter and `$\{10^{-1}, 5 \times 10^{-2}, 10^{-2}\}$` for prefix, with `$\epsilon = 10^{-3}$` (full) or `$10^{-1}$` (prefix).

#### The Memory-Proportional Perturbation Trick and Its Implementation Details

A subtle aspect of the in-place perturbation mechanism is the tradeoff between memory and speed when perturbing parameters. The paper's Algorithm 1 describes perturbing each scalar parameter individually: for each `$\theta_i$`, sample `$z_i$`, compute `$\theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i + \epsilon z_i$`. This loop, implemented naively in Python over billions of parameters, would be extremely slow due to Python's interpreter overhead and the inability to leverage GPU parallelism.

The practical solution is to **generate perturbation tensors matching each parameter tensor's shape, apply them in one vectorized operation, and then immediately deallocate**. For a weight matrix `$W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$`, the implementation:
1. Generates `$Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)^{m \times n}$` as a single tensor using `torch.randn_like(W)`.
2. Updates `$W \leftarrow W + \epsilon Z$` in one GPU kernel.
3. Deletes the tensor `$Z$` (or lets it be garbage-collected if it goes out of scope).

The memory cost of storing `$Z$` temporarily is the size of the largest parameter tensor in the model. In Transformers, the largest tensors are typically the word embedding matrix `$W_{\text{emb}} \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d_{\text{model}}}$` where `$V$` is the vocabulary size and `$d_{\text{model}}$` is the hidden dimension. For OPT-66B, with vocab size 50,272 and hidden dimension 9,216, the embedding matrix has size 50,272 × 9,216 ≈ 463M parameters, requiring 0.86GB at 16-bit precision. This 0.86GB is the maximum temporary memory overhead of MeZO — well within the margin of fitting a 30B model (~60GB) into an 80GB GPU.

The paper notes: "This incurs an additional memory cost as large as the largest weight matrix; usually, this is the word embedding matrix." All other weight matrices (attention projections, feed-forward layers) are smaller than the embedding matrix, so the temporary perturbation tensor never exceeds this bound. Since this tensor is allocated and freed within the scope of a single `PerturbParameters` call, the peak memory usage is the model weights + current activations + one copy of the largest weight matrix — still dramatically less than the 12× overhead of backpropagation.

#### Storage Efficiency of MeZO Checkpoints

The paper makes an underappreciated point about the storage cost of MeZO fine-tuning. Because the entire training trajectory can be reconstructed from (a) the initial pre-trained weights, (b) a single initial random seed that spawns per-step seeds, and (c) the sequence of `projected_grad` scalars, the stored checkpoint for a fine-tuned model is effectively just these scalars plus metadata.

For a 66B model fine-tuned for 20,000 steps with MeZO, the stored information is:
- One master seed (e.g., 8 bytes for a 64-bit integer).
- 20,000 step-specific seeds (20,000 × 8 bytes = 160KB).
- 20,000 `projected_grad` scalars (20,000 × 2 bytes at half-precision, or 4 bytes at float = 80KB).
- Total: less than 0.1MB.

By contrast:
- LoRA with rank 8 adapts 19M parameters, each stored at half-precision → 38MB.
- Prefix tuning with 5 prefix tokens per layer stores 6M parameters → 12MB.
- Full model checkpoint → ~132GB.

The reconstruction process requires iterating through the entire training procedure again — regenerating `$z$` at each step from the saved seeds and applying the gradient updates — but this requires no data (the `projected_grad` scalars implicitly encode the loss information from the original training minibatches) and no additional forward passes. The paper does not claim this reconstruction is fast, only that it is possible and dramatically more storage-efficient than alternatives.

#### Theoretical Time-Memory Tradeoff vs. Backpropagation (Appendix C)

The paper includes a brief theoretical comparison between MeZO and backpropagation-informed algorithms. Drawing on Griewank and Walther's ([40]) analysis of the time-memory tradeoff for automatic differentiation:

For a network of size `$N$` (in some appropriate units), backpropagation with a memory-time tradeoff hyperparameter `$c = O(1)$` can run in time `$O(cN)$` while using memory `$O(N^{1/c})$`. Popular gradient checkpointing implementations choose `$c=2$`, yielding `$O(\sqrt{N})$` memory and `$O(2N)$` computation. In the extreme, one can use `$O(N \log N)$` computation and `$O(\log N)$` memory.

MeZO always consumes `$2N$` computation (two forward passes) and `$O(1)$` additional memory (one seed). At the same memory cost, this is more compute-efficient than gradient checkpointing (which would require `$O(N \log N)$` computation to achieve `$O(\log N)$` memory, or is fundamentally unable to achieve `$O(1)$` memory without approximation).

The tradeoff: gradient checkpointing computes the exact gradient (no approximation error), while MeZO computes a stochastic rank-1 approximation. The paper frames this as appropriate for fine-tuning (where the landscape is well-conditioned) but acknowledges that "MeZO is not as broadly useful as gradient checkpointing" — for general training tasks where exact gradients are needed, gradient checkpointing remains superior.

**Multi-GPU MeZO:** For models too large to fit on a single GPU's memory even for inference (e.g., 66B requiring 132GB at 16-bit + activation memory > 80GB), MeZO is compatible with model parallelism without modification, since it only requires forward passes. The paper uses Hugging Face's `transformers` multi-GPU inference for large models (Appendix E.7), which distributes model layers across GPUs and handles the cross-device communication for activations during forward passes. No special gradient synchronization is needed because there are no gradients to synchronize.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing ZO Optimization from "Dimension-Bound" to "Effective-Rank-Bound" Through the Lens of Pre-Training

The most intellectually distinctive contribution of this paper is not the memory-efficient implementation itself (which is a systems contribution) but rather the **diagnostic reframing of why zeroth-order optimization suddenly becomes practical for fine-tuning, despite decades of theory predicting it should be catastrophically slow**. This is fundamentally a theoretical insight that changes the way one should think about ZO methods in the context of modern deep learning.

**What the field believed before.** Classical theoretical lower bounds for ZO optimization, dating back to Nemirovskij and Yudin (1983) and refined by subsequent work (Duchi et al., 2015; Jamieson et al., 2012; Shamir, 2017), established that the query complexity of ZO methods scales linearly with the ambient dimension `$d$` — the number of parameters being optimized — in both convex and non-convex settings. The intuition is straightforward: ZO methods estimate the gradient by probing random directions, and in high dimensions, the signal-to-noise ratio of a single random projection degrades proportionally to `$1/\sqrt{d}$`. For a 30-billion-parameter model, this predicts that ZO-SGD should be approximately 30 billion times slower than SGD — which would make the approach entirely hopeless. This theoretical prediction was so strong that ZO methods in deep learning were confined exclusively to low-dimensional subspaces: adversarial example generation (perturbing inputs, not parameters), embedding tuning (small vectors), or projected prefix optimization as in BBTv2 (Sun et al., 2022), where the effective dimension is reduced by design.

**What MeZO's theory reveals.** The paper's Theorem 1 (Section 4.1) proves that the slowdown factor of ZO-SGD relative to SGD is not `$d$` but rather `$\gamma = \Theta(r/n)$`, where `$r$` is the **local effective rank** of the Hessian — defined as `$\text{tr}(H) / \|H\|_{\text{op}}$`, a quantity bounded by the number of dominant eigenvalue directions in the loss curvature — and `$n$` is the number of perturbation vectors averaged in n-SPSA. Under the assumption that the Hessian of the fine-tuning loss has small local effective rank (Assumption 1), the per-step loss decrease (Corollary 1) and eventual global convergence rate (Lemma 3) scale with `$r$`, not `$d$`. This is not a small refinement — it is a fundamental conceptual shift from "dimension is the bottleneck" to "landscape conditioning is the bottleneck."

**Why this insight is novel, not obvious.** It has long been known empirically that deep network Hessians exhibit low effective rank (Papyan, 2018; Ghorbani et al., 2019; Sagun et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2020) and that fine-tuning adaptations occur in low-dimensional subspaces (Aghajanyan et al., 2021; Li et al., 2018, reporting intrinsic dimensions as low as <200 parameters). However, nobody had previously connected this empirical observation to the specific algorithmic question of ZO optimization feasibility. The paper's contribution is to identify that **pre-training plus a suitable prompt** is the mechanism that ensures `$r \ll d$`: pre-training shapes the loss landscape so that only a few directions in parameter space meaningfully affect the task loss, and the prompt aligns the fine-tuning objective with this pre-trained structure (drawing on insights from Malladi et al., 2022 and Saunshi et al., 2021). The dramatic experiment in Appendix A.1 — MeZO accuracy dropping from 89.6% to 51.9% on SST-2 without a prompt — is not presented as mere engineering advice but as **empirical validation of the theoretical claim**: removing the prompt destroys the low-effective-rank property, at which point the classical ZO lower bounds reassert themselves and optimization fails.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond MeZO itself. It suggests that **the entire ZO optimization subfield may have been evaluating its methods on the wrong problems** — general-purpose optimization where the landscape is assumed to be adversarial to ZO methods — and that a much larger class of ZO methods may be practical for fine-tuning, transfer learning, and other settings where a pre-trained model provides a well-conditioned starting point. It also provides a diagnostic lens for when ZO methods will succeed or fail: if the effective rank is low (as it is for fine-tuning with prompts), ZO works; if the landscape is generic high-dimensional, the classical bounds apply.

### Innovation 2: The Seed-Reconstruction Trick as an Enabling Systems Contribution That Changes the Storage Economics of Model Adaptation

While the theoretical reframing explains *why* ZO-SGD can converge, the seed-reconstruction mechanism in Algorithm 1 is what makes it *actually run* on a single GPU for 30B-parameter models. This is a systems innovation rather than an algorithmic one, but it has a qualitative impact on the storage economics of model adaptation that deserves recognition as a distinct insight.

**What distinguishes this from prior memory-saving methods.** Prior approaches to reducing fine-tuning memory consumption fall into two categories: (a) reducing what backpropagation stores (gradient checkpointing, FlashAttention, quantization, activation offloading), which brings memory down but never to the level of pure inference because some backward-pass state must always be retained; or (b) reducing what is trained (PEFT methods like LoRA, prefix tuning, adapters), which reduces the number of trainable parameters but still requires backpropagation through the adapted layers, keeping memory well above inference. MeZO's seed-reconstruction mechanism sits in a qualitatively different category: it **eliminates the need to store any gradient-related information whatsoever**, bringing memory to exactly the inference footprint plus a single random seed and a scalar `projected_grad`.

The key conceptual move is recognizing that a pseudo-random number generator is a **deterministic function from seed to sequence**, and therefore the perturbation vector `$z$` — which would be a 60GB tensor for a 30B model — can be replaced by the original seed and four regenerations. This is not an approximation or a compression; it is an exact representation, just encoded in a different form (algorithmic regeneration rather than storage). The memory cost drops from `$O(d)$` to `$O(1)$`.

**Why this matters beyond the stated memory savings.** The storage efficiency of MeZO checkpoints — less than 0.1MB for a 66B model training run of 20,000 steps, compared to 38MB for LoRA or 132GB for a full checkpoint — reveals a more subtle implication: **model adaptation can now be separated from model storage**. A service provider could store a single copy of a large pre-trained model and apply hundreds of task-specific MeZO checkpoints (each <0.1MB) to reconstruct task-adapted models on the fly, without ever duplicating the base model weights. This changes the scaling economics of model customization from linear in the number of tasks (each fine-tuning requiring a full or LoRA-adapted copy) to near-constant (one base model plus tiny adaptation seeds).

The paper does not fully explore this implication — it focuses on training-time memory rather than deployment-time storage — but the checkpoint analysis in Section 2.1 makes the point clearly: "For fine-tuning a 66B model, MeZO requires saving the seed plus 20,000 (steps) × 2 bytes, which is less than 0.1MB. LoRA fine-tunes 19M parameters and requires 38MB storage." This is a **fundamental shift in the deployment model for adapted models**, not just an incremental memory improvement during training. It enables use cases where models are adapted per-user, per-session, or per-document, with negligible storage overhead.

**Comparison to approximate backpropagation methods.** The paper's comparison to gradient checkpointing in Appendix C is illuminating: both techniques trade computation for memory, but they operate in different regimes. Gradient checkpointing recomputes activations (intermediate values that are deterministic functions of the model parameters and input); MeZO recomputes perturbations (random numbers that are deterministic given a seed). The difference is that gradient checkpointing's recomputation cost grows with model depth (each activation must be recomputed for the backward pass), while MeZO's recomputation cost is constant per parameter (just resampling from a PRNG). This makes MeZO's computation-memory Pareto frontier genuinely more favorable in the extreme low-memory regime.

### Innovation 3: The Dual Compatibility with Full-Parameter Tuning, PEFT, and Non-Differentiable Objectives as a Unified Adaptive Framework

A third insight, subtler but practically significant, is that MeZO's gradient-estimation approach **decouples the optimization method from both the parameterization and the objective function**, creating a unified framework that handles full-parameter tuning, PEFT (LoRA, prefix tuning), and non-differentiable objectives through the same simple interface — forward passes with perturbed parameters — without any architectural or algorithmic specialization.

**Why this is non-trivial.** In standard backpropagation-based fine-tuning, switching between full-parameter tuning and PEFT involves changing which parameters receive gradients, but the fundamental mechanism (backprop through the computational graph) remains the same. However, the memory savings from PEFT are limited because activations through the adapted layers must still be cached. With MeZO, the memory footprint is **identical regardless of how many parameters are being optimized** (Figures 3 and 5; Section 3.4), because the perturbation vector `$z$` is regenerated on the fly from the seed whether it is applied to all parameters or only a subset. This means the choice between full-parameter and PEFT becomes purely about **storage of the final checkpoint** and **optimization efficiency**, not about training-time memory — a decoupling that doesn't exist in backpropagation-based methods.

Even more striking is the convergence behavior: Figure 5 (Appendix F.3) shows that MeZO optimizes at essentially the same rate whether tuning all 350M parameters of RoBERTa-large, only the LoRA adapters (~1M parameters), or only the prefix vectors (~0.1M parameters). This directly contradicts the classical ZO analysis which predicts a linear slowdown with dimension — if dimension were the bottleneck, tuning 350M parameters should be orders of magnitude slower than tuning 0.1M parameters. The fact that it isn't provides additional empirical support for the effective-rank theory (Innovation 1) and distinguishes MeZO from prior ZO-PEFT hybrids like BBTv2, which explicitly project the optimization into a low-dimensional subspace to avoid the dimensionality problem.

**Non-differentiable objectives as a first-class capability.** The paper's demonstration that MeZO can optimize accuracy and F1 score directly (Table 3; Section 3.3) is not presented as a major empirical achievement — the results are weaker than cross-entropy optimization, as expected — but as a **proof of concept for a fundamentally different class of fine-tuning objectives**. Backpropagation requires a differentiable loss; RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022) works around this by training a differentiable reward model and then optimizing it with PPO, introducing a complex multi-stage pipeline with its own instabilities. MeZO sidesteps all of this: any objective that can be evaluated as a scalar for a given model output can be optimized directly, with no surrogate, no reward model, and no RL.

The accuracy/F1 results are preliminary (e.g., on SST-2 with k=512, cross-entropy MeZO achieves 93.3% while accuracy-based MeZO achieves 92.7% — only a 0.6% gap — but on TREC the gap is substantial: 94.3% vs. 68.6%). The paper does not claim these results are competitive with surrogate-based optimization, but they establish the **conceptual possibility** of direct non-differentiable optimization, which matters for objectives like human preference scores, safety constraints, or task-specific metrics where good differentiable surrogates don't exist. Tang et al. (2024) concurrently explored ZO optimization for human feedback with ranking oracles, suggesting this direction has legs.

**Unified API for adaptation.** The combination of these properties — same memory regardless of parameter count, no differentiation requirement, seed-based checkpoint reconstruction — means MeZO provides a **single, unified mechanism for model adaptation** that handles classical fine-tuning, PEFT, and non-differentiable objective optimization through the same interface: (model, loss_function, seeds). This unification is not just aesthetic; it means practitioners can switch between these modes without changing infrastructure, memory provisioning, or optimization code. If this unification holds at larger scales and on more complex tasks (e.g., RLHF-style preference optimization), it could simplify the currently fragmented landscape of model adaptation methods.

### Innovation 4: The Wall-Clock Speed Paradox — More Steps but Less Total Time — as a Practical Reframing of Efficiency

The paper reports a finding that initially seems paradoxical: MeZO requires 32× more optimization steps than backpropagation fine-tuning (20,000 vs. ~625 for OPT), yet the total GPU-hours for MeZO is only **half** that of full-parameter fine-tuning (Appendix F.6). This is not a theoretical contribution but a **practical reframing of what "efficiency" means for large-model optimization** that challenges the field's conventional focus on step count or convergence rate.

**Deconstructing the efficiency claim.** Table 23 provides the breakdown: on OPT-30B, a single MeZO step takes 5.9 seconds on 1 GPU, while a single backpropagation step takes 45.6 seconds on 8 GPUs. The per-step speedup is 7.74×, but more importantly, **MeZO uses 8× fewer GPUs**, so the per-GPU-step advantage is much larger. Since MeZO takes 32× more steps but uses 8× fewer GPUs and runs each step 7.74× faster, the total GPU-hours end up being roughly half: (32 × 1 × 5.9) / (1 × 8 × 45.6) ≈ 0.52.

What makes this insight distinctive is that it **separates algorithmic efficiency from systems efficiency**. The classical ZO analysis predicts poor algorithmic efficiency (many more steps needed due to high-variance gradient estimates), and this prediction holds — MeZO does indeed take many more steps. But the systems efficiency (per-step wall-clock time and hardware requirements) more than compensates. This distinction has been underappreciated in the ZO literature, which typically focuses on query complexity (number of function evaluations) as the sole efficiency metric, implicitly assuming each query has the same cost. For large Transformer models, where backpropagation is dramatically more expensive per step than a forward pass, this assumption is false.

**The implication for resource allocation.** This finding suggests that the dominant paradigm for fine-tuning — use as many GPUs as needed to fit backpropagation, minimize step count, and accept the wall-clock time — may be suboptimal from a total-cost perspective. Trading step efficiency for hardware efficiency (fewer GPUs, simpler communication patterns, no gradient synchronization) can reduce total cost even when the algorithm is "slower" by classical measures. This is not a universal claim (the paper is careful to note that the advantage is implementation-dependent and varies with model size and hardware), but it opens a line of reasoning where **optimization algorithms are evaluated on total GPU-hours rather than convergence rate**.

The caveat is important: MeZO's per-step speed advantage is largest for larger models (7.74× for 30B vs. ~1× for 1.3B, where backpropagation fits comfortably on a single GPU) and relies on the fact that MeZO avoids multi-GPU communication overhead entirely. For models small enough that backpropagation fits on a single GPU, the wall-clock advantage diminishes or disappears — at 1.3B, MeZO (0.815s/step, batch size 16) is comparable to FT (0.784s/step, batch size 8), and MeZO's higher step count would make it slower in total. This threshold-dependence is itself a useful diagnostic: MeZO's systems advantage is specifically for models at the boundary of what backpropagation can handle, which is precisely the regime where memory-efficient methods are most needed.

### Assessing Whether These Are Incremental or Fundamental

Innovation 1 (effective-rank reframing) is **fundamental theoretical work** that changes how the field should think about ZO optimization in the context of pre-trained models. It does not invent a new algorithm but provides the intellectual justification for why an old one suddenly works, and in doing so opens a new research direction (ZO methods for fine-tuning transfer-learned models). Innovation 2 (seed-reconstruction) is an **enabling systems contribution** that converts a theoretical possibility into a practical tool — incremental in the sense that it implements a known algorithm efficiently, but fundamental in its impact on the storage economics of adaptation. Innovation 3 (unified framework) is an **architectural insight** arising from the properties of the ZO approach rather than from any additional mechanism — it is a consequence of Innovations 1 and 2 rather than an independent contribution, but it has practical significance that merits separate recognition. Innovation 4 (wall-clock reframing) is an **empirical diagnostic** rather than a conceptual breakthrough, but it challenges an important implicit assumption in the optimization literature and provides a more nuanced efficiency metric for practitioners.

Together, these four insights form a coherent intellectual arc: the paper shows that ZO optimization is not just possible but often **preferable** for large-model fine-tuning, not because ZO algorithms have improved, but because (a) pre-trained landscapes are favorable in a way classical theory didn't account for, (b) implementation tricks eliminate the memory penalty that made ZO impractical at scale, and (c) the systems-level efficiency of forward-only computation more than compensates for slower algorithmic convergence in the large-model regime.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on a diverse set of tasks drawn primarily from SuperGLUE (Wang et al., 2019) and augmented with several others. For medium-sized masked LM experiments (RoBERTa-large), six classification datasets are used: SST-2 (Socher et al., 2013), SST-5 (Socher et al., 2013), TREC (Voorhees and Tice, 2000), MNLI (Williams et al., 2018), SNLI (Bowman et al., 2015), and RTE (Dagan et al., 2005; Bar Haim et al., 2006; Giampiccolo et al., 2007; Bentivogli et al., 2010). Test sets are limited to 1,000 examples each following Malladi et al. (2022), and training data is sampled in two few-shot/many-shot configurations: `k=16` and `k=512` examples per class, with matching validation set sizes. For large autoregressive LM experiments (OPT-13B, 30B, 66B), the paper selects 11 tasks spanning three formats: classification tasks (SST-2, RTE, CB (De Marneffe et al., 2019), BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), WSC (Levesque et al., 2012), WiC (Pilehvar and Camacho-Collados, 2019)), multiple-choice tasks (MultiRC (Khashabi et al., 2018), COPA (Roemmele et al., 2011), ReCoRD (Zhang et al., 2018)), and generation tasks (SQuAD (Rajpurkar et al., 2016), DROP (Dua et al., 2019)). For each task, 1,000 examples are randomly sampled for training, 500 for validation, and 1,000 for testing.

- **Base model(s).** The experiments span two model families and four scales. For masked language models, the paper uses RoBERTa-large (Liu et al., 2019) with 350M parameters, chosen as a representative medium-sized bidirectional Transformer that allows comparison to prior ZO work like BBTv2 (Sun et al., 2022). For autoregressive models, the paper uses the OPT family (Zhang et al., 2022) at 13B, 30B, and 66B parameters, chosen because the scale progression from 13B (barely fits fine-tuning on a single A100) to 66B (requires 16 GPUs for fine-tuning) sharply illustrates the memory bottleneck that MeZO addresses. The largest model tested is OPT-66B with MeZO, while fine-tuning with Adam is only feasible up to OPT-13B on 4 A100s under the paper's hardware configuration.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric for all classification and multiple-choice tasks is **accuracy** (% of test examples where the model's predicted label matches the ground truth). For RoBERTa masked LM experiments, prediction uses the logits of label words filling the `[MASK]` token; for OPT classification tasks, prediction uses the average per-token log-likelihood of each candidate label and selects the highest; for OPT multiple-choice tasks, each candidate is scored similarly and the highest-scoring candidate is chosen; for OPT generation tasks (SQuAD, DROP), greedy decoding is used at inference and the generated text is compared to the reference answer(s). For generation tasks, the paper evaluates using standard exact match or F1 metrics (SQuAD uses F1; DROP uses F1). Standard deviations are reported over multiple random seeds for RoBERTa experiments (typically 5 seeds for few-shot settings), while OPT experiments report single-run results (due to computational cost) except where noted.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares MeZO against five distinct baselines, each representing a different point on the memory-performance tradeoff curve:
  - **Zero-shot**: The pre-trained model is evaluated on each task with the prompt template but no task-specific training examples and no parameter updates. This represents the memory floor (inference only) and the performance floor for adaptation.
  - **In-context learning (ICL)**: The model sees 32 labeled examples in its context window (prompt) before making a prediction, but no parameters are updated. For OPT experiments, the paper also tested filling the context window with as many examples as possible but found this did not improve performance and sometimes caused instability. This matches MeZO's memory footprint (inference only) but uses the prompt mechanism differently — demonstrations instead of training.
  - **Linear probing (LP)**: For classification tasks, a logistic regression classifier is trained on top of the frozen model's output features using scipy's solver. For multiple-choice and generation tasks where the output space is the full vocabulary, "head-tuning" is used instead: only the LM projection head is trained with backpropagation while the rest of the model is frozen. This requires backpropagation through the head only, making it slightly more memory-intensive than inference but substantially less than full fine-tuning.
  - **Fine-tuning with Adam (FT)**: Standard backpropagation-based fine-tuning using the Adam optimizer (Kingma and Ba, 2015). The learning rate is linearly scheduled, and models are trained for 1,000 steps (RoBERTa) or 5 epochs (OPT), with the best validation checkpoint selected. This represents the performance ceiling but costs 12× the memory of inference for full-parameter tuning on OPT-13B.
  - **Fine-tuning with PEFT (FT prefix, FT LoRA)**: For RoBERTa experiments, the paper also compares against fine-tuning with prefix tuning (Li and Liang, 2021) and LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) as baselines that reduce the number of trainable parameters while still using backpropagation. These cost ~6× the memory of inference for OPT-13B.

  Additionally, for RoBERTa experiments, **MeZO-Adam** is compared to MeZO (SGD) as an algorithmic variant (Table 18), and **LP-MeZO** (linear probing followed by MeZO fine-tuning, following the suggestion of Kumar et al., 2022) is explored (Table 19). For comparison to prior ZO work, **BBTv2** (Sun et al., 2022) is evaluated on mutually assessed tasks (SST-2, SNLI, RTE) with RoBERTa-large (Table 21).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures computational cost in terms of **forward passes** through the model. Each MeZO step with n-SPSA costs 2n forward passes (two loss evaluations per perturbation vector, n perturbation vectors). All main experiments use n=1 (2 forward passes per step). Fine-tuning with backpropagation costs 1 forward pass + 1 backward pass per step; the paper treats one backward pass as roughly comparable in cost to one forward pass for wall-clock timing purposes (Appendix F.6), though the memory implications are vastly different. When comparing MeZO to FT, the total number of optimization steps differs by 32–100× (MeZO uses 20K steps for OPT, 100K steps for RoBERTa; FT uses ~625 steps for OPT, 1,000 steps for RoBERTa), so total forward-pass budgets are substantially larger for MeZO. The paper additionally measures wall-clock time per step and total GPU-hours (Appendix F.6, Table 23) and GPU memory consumption (Figures 3 and 4, Table 22) as complementary efficiency metrics, since forward-pass count alone does not capture the multi-GPU communication overhead of backpropagation or the memory savings of MeZO's in-place updates.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For RoBERTa-large experiments, the paper reports mean accuracy and standard deviation across 5 random seeds (for both data sampling and optimization randomness) in the `k=16` and `k=512` settings (Table 18). For OPT experiments, due to the computational cost of large models, results are reported from single training runs. Hyperparameter selection is done via grid search over learning rates, batch sizes, and perturbation scales as detailed in Tables 15 and 16, with the best configuration selected based on validation set performance. For RoBERTa, validation is performed every 1/10 of total training steps (every 10K steps for 100K-step runs) and the best checkpoint is used for test evaluation. For OPT, validation is performed every 1/5 of total training steps (every 4K steps for 20K-step runs). For the largest models (OPT-30B, 66B), the paper dispenses with intermediate validation and uses the final checkpoint, "due to the high storage cost of intermediate checkpoints of large models" (Appendix E.3). This means the reported results for 30B and 66B may not reflect the best-possible checkpoint performance, biasing MeZO results downward relative to what early stopping could achieve.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### MeZO on Medium-Sized Masked LMs (RoBERTa-large)

**Headline results (Table 18, Figure 2).** On RoBERTa-large with `k=16` examples per class, MeZO achieves accuracy within striking distance of full Adam fine-tuning across all six tasks while using dramatically less memory. For example, on SST-2, MeZO reaches 90.5% (std 1.2) vs. FT at 91.9% (std 1.8) — a gap of only 1.4 percentage points. On the harder RTE task, MeZO achieves 64.0% vs. FT at 66.4% — a 2.4 point gap. The largest gap in the `k=16` setting is on SNLI: 68.5% (MeZO) vs. 77.5% (FT), a 9-point gap. For `k=512`, the gaps narrow substantially: on SST-2, 93.3% (MeZO) vs. 93.9% (FT); on SNLI, 83.0% vs. 88.7% (5.7 point gap); on TREC, 94.3% vs. 97.3% (3.0 point gap). The paper states: "With enough data, MeZO achieves comparable performance (up to 5% gap) to FT" (Section 3.1). The "up to 5%" claim is accurate for `k=512` across all six tasks when comparing the best MeZO variant (MeZO, MeZO LoRA, or MeZO prefix) to FT: the gaps are SST-2: 0.6%, SST-5: 2.3%, SNLI: 4.7%, MNLI: 4.6%, RTE: 3.5%, TREC: 3.0%.

**MeZO vs. zero-shot and linear probing.** MeZO comprehensively outperforms both baselines across all tasks and both data regimes (Table 18). On `k=16`, MeZO's advantage over zero-shot ranges from 12.6 points on RTE (64.0% vs. 51.4%) to 44.9 points on TREC (76.9% vs. 32.0%). The advantage over linear probing is similarly large: 14.5 points on TREC (76.9% vs. 51.3%), though narrower on SST-2 (90.5% vs. 76.0%). On `k=512`, linear probing closes some gaps but MeZO still leads by 4.9 points on TREC (94.3% vs. 89.4%) and 6.8 points on MNLI (78.3% vs. 71.5%).

**MeZO with PEFT vs. full-parameter MeZO.** Across both `k=16` and `k=512` settings, the three MeZO variants — full-parameter, LoRA, and prefix — achieve broadly comparable performance (Table 18, Figure 2). On `k=512` SST-2: MeZO 93.3%, MeZO (LoRA) 93.4%, MeZO (prefix) 93.3%. On `k=512` SNLI: MeZO 83.0%, MeZO (LoRA) 84.0%, MeZO (prefix) 84.8%. The paper notes that "MeZO (prefix) sometimes outperforms MeZO" (Section 3.1) — for example, 84.8% vs. 83.0% on SNLI and 79.8% vs. 78.3% on MNLI — but the differences are typically within 1–2 points and not consistently in one variant's favor. The convergence rates of the three variants are also similar (Appendix F.3, Figure 5): the training loss curves for MeZO, MeZO (LoRA), and MeZO (prefix) on SST-2 and SNLI are nearly overlapping for the first 5,000 steps. This is a non-obvious and theoretically significant result: tuning 350M parameters vs. tuning ~1M (LoRA) vs. tuning ~100K (prefix) parameters takes roughly the same number of steps, contradicting classical ZO theory which predicts a d-dependent slowdown.

**MeZO-Adam vs. MeZO (SGD).** Table 18 includes MeZO-Adam results which show small and inconsistent improvements over vanilla MeZO: on `k=512` SST-2, both achieve 93.3%; on `k=512` SNLI, MeZO-Adam reaches 85.3% vs. MeZO's 83.0%; on `k=512` RTE, 79.2% vs. 78.6%. For `k=16`, MeZO-Adam shows a meaningful boost on SNLI (74.1% vs. 68.5%) but degrades on RTE (59.2% vs. 64.0%). The paper concludes that "ZO-Adam sometimes outperforms ZO-SGD but is not consistent across tasks" (Appendix F.1).

**LP-MeZO results (Table 19).** Performing linear probing before MeZO fine-tuning (following Kumar et al., 2022) can sometimes help: on SST-2 `k=16`, LP-MeZO achieves 91.4% vs. MeZO's 90.5%. However, on TREC, LP-MeZO severely degrades performance to 54.0% vs. MeZO's 76.9%, suggesting LP initialization can be harmful.

#### MeZO on Large Autoregressive LMs (OPT-13B, 30B, 66B)

**OPT-13B overall results (Table 1, Figure 1).** The headline claim is that "MeZO outperforms or performs comparably to fine-tuning on 7 out of 11 tasks" despite using roughly 12× less memory. Examining the table: MeZO matches or exceeds FT on SST-2 (91.4% MeZO vs. 92.0% FT — 0.6 point gap), RTE (66.1% vs. 70.8%), COPA (88.0% vs. 79.0% — MeZO leads by 9 points), ReCoRD (81.7% vs. 74.1% — MeZO leads by 7.6 points), SQuAD (84.7% vs. 84.9% — 0.2 point gap), and DROP (30.9% vs. 31.3% — 0.4 point gap). On CB, MeZO achieves 67.9% vs. FT at 83.9% — a 16-point gap that is the largest disparity. On WIC, MeZO reaches 61.1% vs. FT at 70.1% — a 9-point gap. On MultiRC, MeZO reaches 60.1% vs. FT at 71.1% — an 11-point gap. The "7 out of 11" count includes the cases where the best MeZO variant (among MeZO, MeZO LoRA, and MeZO prefix) is within 1% of FT (SST-2, SQuAD, DROP) or beats FT outright (COPA, ReCoRD, BoolQ via MeZO prefix at 73.8% vs. FT at 77.1% is not within 1%, but MeZO LoRA at 73.8% is also not within 1% — the "7 out of 11" claim appears to count BoolQ as a win for MeZO prefix at 73.1% vs. FT at 77.1%, which is a 4-point gap, and WSC at 63.5% (MeZO LoRA at 64.4% is closer), which is equal to FT). The counting is somewhat generous, but the overall pattern is clear: MeZO achieves strong performance on classification and generation tasks while falling further behind on the more complex multiple-choice reasoning tasks (CB, MultiRC, WIC). Note that on CB, FT achieves a dramatically higher 83.9% vs. MeZO's best of 69.6% (prefix) — a 14.3-point gap that is the clearest failure case.

**MeZO vs. ICL and zero-shot on OPT-13B.** MeZO and its variants outperform ICL on 8 of the 11 tasks (Table 1). The ICL wins are on RTE (62.1% ICL vs. 66.1% MeZO — ICL loses), CB (57.1% vs. 69.6% MeZO prefix — ICL loses), BoolQ (66.9% vs. 73.8% MeZO LoRA — ICL loses), MultiRC (53.1% vs. 63.7% MeZO prefix — ICL loses), COPA (87.0% ICL vs. 88.0% MeZO — ICL loses), and ReCoRD (82.5% vs. 81.7% — ICL wins narrowly). The exceptions where ICL beats MeZO are SST-2 (87.0% ICL vs. MeZO prefix at 90.7% — MeZO wins), WSC (39.4% ICL vs. 63.5% MeZO — MeZO wins decisively), WIC (50.5% ICL vs. 61.1% MeZO — MeZO wins), SQuAD (75.9% ICL vs. 84.7% MeZO — MeZO wins), and DROP (29.6% ICL vs. 31.4% MeZO — MeZO wins). Overall, MeZO consistently outperforms ICL, often substantially. Zero-shot performance is far below both ICL and MeZO across all tasks.

**OPT-13B task-type breakdown.** The paper emphasizes that "MeZO exhibits strong performance across classification, multiple-choice, and generation tasks" (Section 3.2). On classification tasks: MeZO achieves 91.4% (SST-2), 66.1% (RTE), ranging up to 70.8% for MeZO prefix on RTE. On multiple-choice: best results are 88.0% (COPA, MeZO), 81.7% (ReCoRD, MeZO), and 60.1% (MultiRC, MeZO). On generation: 84.7% (SQuAD, MeZO) and 31.4% (DROP, MeZO LoRA). The generation results are particularly noteworthy because they require the model to produce free-form text, not just select from a closed set of labels — MeZO's ZO gradient estimate must guide the model toward producing coherent and correct answer spans. The near-parity with FT on SQuAD (84.7% vs. 84.9%) is impressive, though DROP's low absolute numbers (31.4% vs. 31.3%) reflect the dataset's difficulty rather than a MeZO-specific failure.

**Scaling to OPT-30B and OPT-66B (Table 2, Table 20).** MeZO successfully optimizes models up to 66B parameters, where fine-tuning with Adam is completely infeasible on the available hardware. On OPT-30B, MeZO achieves 90.6% (SST-2), 72.6% (RTE, via prefix), 73.5% (BoolQ, via prefix), 63.5% (WSC), 59.1% (WIC, via prefix), and 85.2% (SQuAD). Comparing to 30B zero-shot (SST-2: 56.7%, RTE: 52.0%, SQuAD: 46.5%), the improvements from MeZO are dramatic — e.g., +33.9 points on SST-2, +20.6 on RTE, +38.7 on SQuAD. Comparing to 30B ICL (SST-2: 81.9%, RTE: 66.8%, SQuAD: 78.0%), MeZO still provides meaningful gains on most tasks. On OPT-66B, MeZO achieves 93.6% (SST-2, prefix), 66.4% (RTE, prefix), 73.7% (BoolQ, prefix), 63.5% (WSC, full), 58.9% (WIC, full), and 85.0% (SQuAD, prefix). Comparing to 66B ICL (SST-2: 89.3%, SQuAD: 81.3%), MeZO still provides gains, though the margin narrows on SST-2 (93.6% vs. 89.3%). The paper notes a curious failure: "MeZO requires further tuning to successfully optimize" on 66B SQuAD with full-parameter tuning (Table 20 footnote), though prefix tuning works.

#### Non-Differentiable Objectives (Table 3, Section 3.3)

**RoBERTa-large classification with accuracy objective.** On SST-2 `k=512`, optimizing accuracy directly with MeZO achieves 92.7% vs. 93.3% for cross-entropy MeZO — a 0.6-point gap. On SST-5, the gap is larger: 48.9% (accuracy objective) vs. 53.2% (cross-entropy). On SNLI, 82.7% vs. 83.0% — nearly identical. On TREC, the gap is dramatic: 68.6% vs. 94.3%. The TREC result is the clearest failure of the non-differentiable objective approach: the 6-way classification task sees a 25.7-point drop compared to cross-entropy optimization.

**OPT-13B SQuAD with F1 objective.** Using MeZO (prefix) with F1 score as the objective achieves 78.5% F1, compared to 84.2% for cross-entropy fine-tuning with Adam and 84.7% for cross-entropy MeZO. This is a 6.2-point gap to cross-entropy MeZO, larger than the classification gaps, suggesting that optimizing sequence-level metrics like F1 directly is harder than optimizing token-level cross-entropy for generation tasks.

**Interpretation.** The paper presents these results as "preliminary findings" that "highlight the promising potential of applying MeZO to optimize non-differentiable objectives" (Section 3.3). The results are mixed: on some tasks (SST-2, SNLI), direct accuracy optimization nearly matches cross-entropy; on others (TREC, SQuAD F1), the gap is large. The paper does not investigate why TREC is so much harder than SST-2 for accuracy-based optimization, though the higher number of classes (6 vs. 2) and the resulting sparser accuracy signal may contribute.

#### Memory Usage and Efficiency Profiling (Figures 3-4, Tables 22-23)

**GPU memory consumption (Figure 3, Table 22).** On the MultiRC task with OPT models, MeZO's peak GPU memory is identical to zero-shot inference: 4GB for OPT-1.3B, 7GB for OPT-2.7B, 14GB for OPT-6.7B, 26GB for OPT-13B, 58GB for OPT-30B, and 128GB for OPT-66B (2 GPUs). Full-parameter FT with Adam requires: 27GB for 1.3B, 55GB for 2.7B, 156GB for 6.7B (2 GPUs), 316GB for 13B (4 GPUs), 633GB for 30B (8 GPUs), and 16 GPUs for 66B (estimated). The memory ratio FT/MeZO ranges from 6.75× (1.3B) to 12.15× (13B). Prefix tuning FT falls between: 19GB for 1.3B, 29GB for 2.7B, 46GB for 6.7B, 158GB for 13B (2 GPUs), with ratios to MeZO of roughly 4.75× to 6.08×. ICL uses slightly more memory than MeZO due to the longer context (6GB vs. 4GB for 1.3B, 29GB vs. 26GB for 13B).

**Largest trainable model size (Figure 4).** The practical consequence is stark: on a single A100 80GB GPU, MeZO can train a 30B model, while FT can only train a 2.7B model (11× larger). With 2 A100s, MeZO trains 66B while FT trains 6.7B. The paper projects that with 8 A100s (640GB total), MeZO could train a 175B model while FT maxes out at 30B — nearly a 6× advantage in model scale.

**Wall-clock time (Table 23, Appendix F.6).** On OPT-30B with MultiRC, MeZO (batch size 16) takes 5.896s per step on 1 GPU, while FT (batch size 8) takes 45.608s per step on 8 GPUs — a 7.74× per-step speedup for MeZO. At smaller scales, the advantage shrinks: 1.3B (0.815s MeZO vs. 0.784s FT, roughly 1.04×), 2.7B (1.400s vs. 1.326s, 1.06×). The paper notes that "the wall-clock benefit of MeZO is not inherent to the algorithm and is highly dependent on the implementation" but that overall, "MeZO only requires half as many GPU-hours to fine-tune a 30B model compared to full-parameter fine-tuning" — for 20K MeZO steps × 1 GPU × 5.9s vs. 625 FT steps × 8 GPUs × 45.6s, the ratio is approximately (20,000 × 1 × 5.9) / (625 × 8 × 45.6) ≈ 0.52.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper conducts ablations primarily in Appendix A (algorithmic ablations) and Appendix B (MeZO variants), with further robustness checks scattered through the main results and appendices.

**Prompt necessity (Appendix A.1, Table 5).** MeZO without a prompt fails catastrophically: on SST-2, accuracy drops from 89.6% (with prompt) to 51.9% (without prompt) — near the 50% random baseline for binary classification. On SNLI (3-way), the drop is from 65.1% to 34.8% (near the 33% random baseline). On TREC (6-way), the drop is to 19.5%, above the 16.7% random baseline but far below prompted performance. This finding is robust and forms the empirical foundation for the theoretical analysis in Section 4 — prompts induce the favorable landscape conditioning that makes ZO optimization feasible.

**SPSA noise sample count `n` and scheduling (Appendix A.2, Table 6).** Under a fixed budget of 10,000 forward passes, increasing `n` (using n-SPSA with proportionally fewer steps) does not consistently improve performance. For constant schedules: `n=1` achieves 89.6% SST-2, 65.1% SNLI, 66.7% TREC; `n=4` achieves 89.5%, 68.6%, 62.3%; `n=16` achieves 90.4%, 67.0%, 62.8%. For linearly increasing schedules (starting from `n=1` and ramping up): `n=4` linear achieves 89.6%, 65.3%, 66.1%; `n=16` linear achieves 88.9%, 62.8%, 64.2%. The pattern is noisy, with minor gains on some tasks and minor losses on others, leading the paper to conclude "n=1 is the most efficient" and use it as default. This is an important finding because it means variance reduction through averaging is not beneficial when accounting for the increased forward-pass cost — each additional noise sample halves the number of optimization steps, and the tradeoff is roughly neutral.

**Perturbation scale `ε`.** The paper states in Appendix E.3 that "the choice of `ε` seemed to not significantly impact performance" for RoBERTa-large, using `ε=10⁻³` as the fixed default. For OPT (Table 16), `ε` is fixed at `10⁻³` for full-parameter and LoRA, and `10⁻¹` for prefix tuning. For non-differentiable objectives (Appendix E.6), `ε` remains at `10⁻³` for full-parameter and `10⁻¹` for prefix. The paper does not present an `ε` sensitivity sweep, so robustness to this hyperparameter is asserted but not quantitatively demonstrated.

**Batch size (Appendix E.3).** The paper states that "using a larger batch size consistently yielded faster optimization" for RoBERTa-large, settling on batch size 64 versus the smaller sizes used in FT baselines. For OPT, batch size 16 is used (Table 16). The larger batch size likely helps by reducing the variance of the loss evaluation itself, which compounds with the already-high variance of the SPSA gradient estimate — with a small batch, the loss difference `(ℓ⁺ − ℓ⁻)` would be dominated by minibatch noise rather than the perturbation signal.

**Learning rate schedule (Appendix A).** The paper finds that "using a linearly decreasing learning rate schedule during training, as was done for fine-tuning with backpropagation, does not help or hurt MeZO. Similarly, using a learning rate warmup leads to identical results." All MeZO experiments use a constant learning rate schedule with no warmup. This simplifies the optimizer but removes a potential source of improvement — well-tuned schedules are often critical for SGD-based optimization in deep learning.

**Variance reduction through gradient norm scaling (Appendix B.3, Table 8).** Modifying the SPSA estimate to scale perturbation sizes inversely by per-layer gradient norms (estimated via ZO, Proposition 1) substantially hurts performance: on TREC, accuracy drops from 66.7% (baseline) to 32.6% (ZO-estimated gradient norms) or to 38.7% (recomputed norms). Even with cheat access to exact backpropagation-computed gradient norms, performance degrades: 87.0% SST-2, 49.6% SNLI, 32.6% TREC. This negative result is theoretically informative — it suggests that the ZO gradient estimate's variance structure is not the bottleneck the classical analysis would predict, aligning with the effective-rank theory.

**Variance reduction through parameter norm scaling (Appendix B.3, Table 9).** Using parameter norms to scale per-layer perturbation magnitudes yields performance close to baseline: 89.2% SST-2, 65.4% SNLI, 64.8% TREC vs. baseline 89.6%, 65.1%, 66.7%. The paper concludes that parameter-norm-based scaling "does not substantially impact the performance of MeZO."

**Normalized gradient estimation (Appendix B.4, Table 10).** Estimating the normalized gradient (dividing each coordinate's SPSA estimate by its absolute value) degrades performance: 88.0% SST-2 (vs. 89.6%), 60.0% SNLI (vs. 65.1%), and a severe 44.0% TREC (vs. 66.7%). This suggests that preserving the magnitude information in the gradient estimate is important, even though the SPSA estimate has a uniform scale across coordinates.

**One-point gradient estimators (Appendix B.5, Table 11).** Testing an estimator from Zhang et al. (2022) that requires only one forward pass per step (reusing the previous step's loss), the paper finds it substantially less effective than the two-point SPSA when controlling for total forward passes. After 40K steps (40K forward passes, matching SPSA's 20K steps at 40K forward passes), the one-point estimator achieves 91.8% SST-2 (vs. 92.8% SPSA), 74.4% SNLI (vs. 82.9%), and 68.7% RTE (vs. 76.7%). The performance gap is particularly large on the more complex tasks (SNLI, RTE). The paper attributes this to the additional noise from the changing loss landscape between consecutive steps.

**MeZO-Adam vs. MeZO-SGD (Table 18, discussed in Main Results above).** MeZO-Adam sometimes improves performance (e.g., +2.3 points on `k=512` SNLI) but is not consistently better, and each step requires recomputing the optimizer state from saved seeds, which incurs additional computation.

**Linear probing followed by MeZO (Appendix F.1, Table 19).** LP-MeZO can improve performance (SST-2: 91.4% vs. 90.5%) but can also severely hurt (TREC: 54.0% vs. 76.9%). The inconsistency makes this approach unreliable without task-specific tuning.

**MeZO across PEFT methods — convergence speed comparison (Appendix F.3, Figure 5).** The training loss curves for MeZO full-parameter, MeZO LoRA, and MeZO prefix on SST-2 and SNLI are nearly overlapping for the first 5,000 steps, despite the different numbers of parameters being optimized (350M, ~1M, and ~100K respectively). This is direct evidence against the classical ZO theory that predicts a d-dependent slowdown.

**BBTv2 comparison (Appendix F.4, Table 21).** On RoBERTa-large `k=16`, MeZO outperforms BBTv2 (Sun et al., 2022) by 0.2 points on SST-2 (90.5% vs. 90.3%), 11.2 points on SNLI (68.5% vs. 57.3%), and 7.3 points on RTE (64.0% vs. 56.7%). BBTv2 is a ZO method limited to optimizing low-dimensional projected prefixes, while MeZO can tune the full model or any PEFT subset — the performance gap is largest on the harder tasks (SNLI, RTE), suggesting the flexibility of MeZO matters most when the task requires more substantial adaptation.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: "MeZO significantly outperforms in-context learning and linear probing."** This claim is strongly and consistently supported across all model scales, task types, and data regimes. Table 1 (OPT-13B) shows MeZO besting ICL on 8 of 11 tasks, often by large margins (e.g., +24.1 points on WSC, +8.8 on SQuAD). Table 18 (RoBERTa-large) shows large gaps over linear probing in the low-data regime. There is no exception or qualification needed — MeZO is uniformly better than these baselines in the experiments presented. However, the paper uses only 32 ICL examples, which may not be optimal. The paper notes: "We also try filling in as many examples as possible in the context but this does not improve performance and sometimes leads to unstable results" (Appendix E.3) — this suggests the ICL baseline is reasonably tuned for the available context, but the possibility that more sophisticated demonstration selection (Liu et al., 2022) or ordering (Lu et al., 2022) could close some gaps is not explored.

**Claim 2: "MeZO achieves comparable performance to fine-tuning with backpropagation across multiple tasks, with up to 12× memory reduction."** This claim requires unpacking "comparable." On RoBERTa-large with `k=512`, the MeZO-FT gap is within 5% across all tasks (Table 18), which is a reasonable definition of "comparable" for a method using 12× less memory. On OPT-13B, the picture is more nuanced: MeZO is within 1% of FT on 4 tasks (SST-2, COPA, SQuAD, DROP), within 4-5% on 3 more (RTE, BoolQ, WSC), and 9-16% behind on 4 tasks (CB, MultiRC, WIC, plus RTE at 4.7% is borderline). The "7 out of 11" framing counts the borderline cases generously. A more precise characterization would be: MeZO matches or beats FT on 4 tasks, is close (within 5 points) on 3 more, and falls substantially short on 4 tasks. The substantially-short cases are concentrated in multiple-choice tasks requiring complex multi-sentence reasoning (CB, MultiRC) or fine-grained word sense disambiguation (WiC). The paper does not analyze *why* these particular tasks are hard for MeZO — whether it's a landscape conditioning issue (Hessian effective rank might be higher for these tasks), a gradient estimation variance issue, or a step count issue (maybe MeZO would catch up with more steps). This gap in analysis is significant because it limits the diagnostic value of the experimental results: knowing *when* MeZO fails is as important as knowing that it usually works.

The memory reduction claim (12×) is measured specifically for OPT-13B on MultiRC (Table 22, Figure 3). This is a single task with an average of 400 tokens per example — memory consumption depends on sequence length and batch size, so the 12× figure may not generalize to other configurations. The paper does provide a systematic sweep across model sizes (Table 22), showing ratios from 6.75× (1.3B) to ~12× (13B, 30B). The ratio increases with model size because FT's activation memory grows with hidden dimension and layer count, while MeZO's stays constant. The claim is therefore well-supported for the core scenario the paper targets (large autoregressive models), but the specific multiplier is task- and batch-size-dependent.

**Claim 3: "MeZO is compatible with both full-parameter and parameter-efficient tuning techniques such as LoRA and prefix tuning."** This claim is directly demonstrated: Tables 18 and 1 include MeZO, MeZO (LoRA), and MeZO (prefix) columns. Figure 5 shows compatible training dynamics. The finding that all three converge at similar rates is robust. However, "compatible" is a minimal claim — the more interesting question is whether MeZO + PEFT offers advantages over MeZO full-parameter tuning, and the answer from the experiments is "not really": performance is similar, convergence speed is similar, and the storage advantage of PEFT is partially moot because MeZO full-parameter checkpoints are already tiny (<0.1MB). The value of MeZO + PEFT appears to be in scenarios where one wants to distribute only adapter weights rather than the full model — but this is not explored.

**Claim 4: "MeZO can effectively optimize non-differentiable objectives."** This claim is supported with qualifications. Table 3 shows that accuracy/F1 optimization works (beats zero-shot substantially) but generally underperforms cross-entropy optimization, sometimes dramatically (25.7-point gap on TREC). The paper presents this as proof-of-concept rather than a competitive method, and the claim should be understood in that spirit: MeZO *can* optimize non-differentiable objectives, but whether it does so *effectively* depends on the task and objective. The experiments are preliminary — only two objectives (accuracy, F1), a handful of tasks, no comparison to RL-based methods for non-differentiable optimization, no investigation of why TREC fails so badly.

**Claim 5 (theoretical): "The convergence rate depends on the local effective rank of the Hessian, not the number of parameters."** This is a theoretical claim whose empirical support is primarily the observation that MeZO works at all on billion-parameter models, and that PEFT and full-parameter tuning converge at similar rates (Figure 5). The paper does not directly measure the effective rank of the Hessian for any of the models or tasks — such measurement would be extremely expensive for large models. Instead, the paper relies on prior empirical work showing that deep network Hessians have low effective rank (Papyan, 2018; Ghorbani et al., 2019; etc.) and on the indirect evidence that if the classical d-dependent bound applied, MeZO would fail completely (which it doesn't). This is a reasonable inference but falls short of direct validation. The prompt ablation (Table 5) provides circumstantial evidence: removing the prompt causes MeZO to fail, consistent with the prompt being necessary for the low-effective-rank property. However, the paper does not show that prompt removal actually increases effective rank — it could fail for other reasons (worse initialization, different loss geometry).

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

- **Single model family for large-scale experiments.** All autoregressive results use the OPT family. Transformer architectures differ in ways that could affect ZO optimization — normalization scheme, activation function, weight tying, attention variant — and the paper provides no evidence that MeZO transfers to other families (LLaMA, GPT-NeoX, PaLM, BLOOM). The authors acknowledge this implicitly by not claiming universality, but the paper would be stronger with even one additional model family at the 7–13B scale.

- **Test set sizes are small.** For OPT experiments, each task uses 1,000 test examples. For tasks like CB (CommitmentBank) which has only 250 examples in the original SuperGLUE test set, this is adequate, but for larger datasets like SQuAD and DROP, 1,000 is a subsample. The paper does not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests for the OPT results (since they are single-run), making it difficult to assess whether a 1–2 point difference is meaningful. For RoBERTa, 5-seed standard deviations are reported, but these capture only optimization randomness, not data sampling randomness (the same `k=16` examples are presumably used across seeds).

- **The 12× memory comparison is to an unoptimized FT baseline.** The paper states: "We did not turn on any advance memory-saving options, e.g., gradient checkpointing" (Appendix E.7). Gradients checkpointing can reduce FT memory by roughly 2× at the cost of recomputation, which would bring the FT/MeZO ratio from 12× to ~6×. The paper acknowledges this in Section 5: "The above analyses also do not consider recent advances (e.g., gradient checkpointing, FlashAttention, and quantization)." While MeZO would likely still have an advantage, the specific 12× figure is an upper bound on the memory savings over a reasonably optimized FT baseline. The paper does not run experiments with gradient checkpointing + FT to quantify the actual gap.

- **No comparison to other forward-only training methods.** The forward-forward algorithm (Hinton, 2022) is mentioned as related work but not compared. Admittedly, forward-forward was designed for different purposes and has not been scaled to large LMs, but its absence means MeZO's advantage over *other* forward-only approaches is unquantified.

- **The OPT-30B and 66B results are from final checkpoints, not best-validation.** As noted in Appendix E.3, "we do not evaluate the intermediate validation performance and directly use the last checkpoint for evaluation, due to the high storage cost of intermediate checkpoints of large models." If MeZO overfits or if the optimal checkpoint occurs earlier than step 20K, the reported results underestimate MeZO's potential performance. Given that the paper reports no overfitting for RoBERTa ("the performance of MeZO improves monotonically with the number of steps, and there does not appear to be any overfitting," Appendix A), this may not be a large effect, but it is untested at the 30B/66B scale.

- **Limited exploration of the non-differentiable objective space.** The paper tests accuracy and F1, but the motivating example in the introduction is RLHF and human preference optimization (Ouyang et al., 2022; Stiennon et al., 2020). No experiment connects MeZO to preference-based fine-tuning, even on a small scale. The concurrent work by Tang et al. (2024) explores ZO for ranking-based human feedback, but this paper presents no such results.

- **Wall-clock time measurements are implementation-dependent and not the main claim.** The paper is careful to note: "The wall-clock benefit of MeZO is not inherent to the algorithm and is highly dependent on the implementation" (Appendix F.6). The 2× GPU-hour reduction is measured on their specific hardware with their specific PyTorch/FSDP configuration. A highly optimized FT implementation (e.g., with FlashAttention-2, tensor parallelism, better gradient checkpointing strategies) could narrow or reverse this gap. The paper would benefit from a more detailed breakdown of where FT's time is spent (computation vs. communication vs. memory management) to clarify which aspects are fundamental and which are implementation artifacts.

**Missing experiments that would have strengthened the paper:**

- **Ablation on number of MeZO training steps.** For OPT, MeZO uses 20K steps. The paper does not show learning curves (accuracy vs. steps) to indicate whether performance is still improving at 20K or has plateaued. If MeZO is still improving, then the reported gaps to FT may overstate MeZO's disadvantage; if it has plateaued, the gaps reflect fundamental limitations of the ZO approach. This is particularly important for the tasks where MeZO substantially underperforms FT (CB, MultiRC, WiC) — would more steps close the gap?

- **Difficulty-stratified analysis.** The paper does not break down results by example difficulty or task complexity within a dataset. For instance, on MultiRC, are MeZO's errors concentrated on questions requiring the most complex multi-sentence reasoning? Such analysis would connect the empirical results to the theoretical framework (effective rank may be higher for harder reasoning steps) and provide a more precise diagnostic of when MeZO fails.

- **Gradient estimate quality analysis.** How well does the SPSA estimate correlate with the true gradient? For a small model, one could compute the exact gradient via backpropagation and measure the cosine similarity to the SPSA estimate at various points during fine-tuning. This would directly quantify the signal-to-noise ratio of the ZO estimate and its evolution during optimization — a key quantity that the theory depends on but the experiments never measure.

- **Combination with FlashAttention and quantization.** The paper mentions these as future work but includes no experiments. Since these methods reduce inference memory, they would also reduce MeZO's memory footprint, potentially enabling even larger models. Quantifying the combined memory savings would strengthen the practical case for MeZO.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: Difficulty Estimation for Compute-Optimal Allocation Is Prohibitively Expensive and Not Accounted For

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal scaling framework in Section 3.2 rests on the ability to estimate prompt difficulty *before* deciding how to allocate the inference budget. The paper's method for doing so — generating 2048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness (oracle) or PRM final-answer scores (predicted) — is extraordinarily expensive. At 2048 samples per question, the difficulty estimation step alone consumes more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations). The paper acknowledges this explicitly in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence.** The reported 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution, and the former could dominate the latter. A practitioner trying to deploy compute-optimal scaling would face a painful upfront cost: run 2048 forward passes per query just to decide how to spend the remaining budget. For low-volume, high-stakes applications this might be acceptable, but for high-throughput production systems (the very setting where test-time compute allocation matters most), it is likely prohibitive. The efficiency numbers in Figures 4 and 8 should therefore be understood as **upper bounds on achievable efficiency** rather than realized deployment gains.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no measurement of total cost including difficulty estimation — not even a single data point showing what the effective efficiency multiplier would be if the 2048-sample cost were amortized. The difficulty estimation method is described in Section 3.2, and the predicted (non-oracle) variant is validated in Figures 4 and 8 (showing that PRM-based bins track oracle bins well), but the cost of generating those 2048 samples is never factored into any budget calculation. Appendix C mentions the exploration-exploitation tradeoff but does not quantify it.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly flags this as a key avenue for future work (Section 8): "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question." It also sketches the idea of adaptive difficulty estimation — start with a few samples, assess difficulty, then allocate the remaining budget — but no such method is implemented or evaluated. This is acknowledged but completely unresolved. Until a cheap difficulty estimator exists, the compute-optimal framework is an analysis tool, not a deployable system. The paper's decision to exclude the cost "largely for simplicity" is understandable for a research contribution establishing the conceptual framework, but a practitioner reading the paper should not mistake the unamortized efficiency numbers for realized savings.

---

### Limitation 2: Single Benchmark, Single Model Family — with No Evidence of Transfer

**The assumption or constraint.** All experimental results in this paper use the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021) — specifically the Lightman et al. (2022) split of 12,000 training and 500 test questions — with PaLM 2-S* (Codey) as the base model. The paper states in Section 4:

> "We believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs"

but provides no cross-model or cross-benchmark results to support this belief. The 500-question test set is split into five difficulty quintiles (100 questions each), and two-fold cross-validation within each bin means strategy selection is based on approximately 50 questions per fold — a remarkably small sample for claims about optimal allocation policies.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or benchmark-specific. The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend intimately on PaLM 2-S*'s output distribution — a model with different calibration, different error patterns, or different pre-training data might exhibit qualitatively different difficulty-dependent scaling curves. The revision model's ability to learn from in-context incorrect examples depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families. The MATH benchmark consists exclusively of competition-level math problems requiring symbolic multi-step reasoning. It is entirely unclear whether the core findings — beam search hurts easy problems but helps medium ones (Figure 3, right), sequential revisions dominate on easy problems but balanced ratios are optimal on hard ones (Figure 7, right), compute-optimal allocation yields 4× efficiency gains (Figures 4 and 8) — generalize to other reasoning domains (code generation, logical inference, scientific QA) or to tasks requiring factual knowledge rather than deductive reasoning.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper reports no experiments on any benchmark other than MATH, and no experiments with any model other than PaLM 2-S* (plus the 14× larger PaLM variant for FLOPs-matched comparisons). The 500-question test set, with ~100 questions per difficulty bin, is never supplemented with confidence intervals for the compute-optimal scaling curves in Figures 4 and 8, so it is impossible to assess whether the observed differences between strategies are statistically significant at this sample size. The paper's claim that PaLM 2-S* is "representative" is an assertion, not an empirical finding.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges none of these generalizability concerns explicitly. The single-model, single-benchmark scope is not framed as a limitation in Section 8 (which focuses on difficulty estimation, search-revision combination, and self-improvement loops as future work). The representative-model claim in Section 4 is the paper's only gesture toward generalizability. For a practitioner considering adopting this approach, the absence of any replication across model families or task domains is the single largest source of uncertainty about whether the findings — particularly the difficulty-dependent strategy selection — will transfer to their setting. A replication on, say, LLaMA-2 on code generation or logical reasoning tasks would substantially increase confidence in the framework's applicability.

---

### Limitation 3: The 14× Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained and Uses Greedy Decoding

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm rather than Chinchilla-optimal training where both data and parameters are scaled equally. The paper acknowledges this explicitly:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

Furthermore, the 14× larger model uses only **greedy decoding** — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search, no revisions. The smaller PaLM 2-S* model gets the full benefit of sophisticated compute-optimal test-time strategies, while the larger model gets none.

**The consequence.** The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining — e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on easy questions at low inference-to-pretraining ratios (R ≪ 1) for revisions — are measured against a baseline that is weaker than it could reasonably be. A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with 14× more total FLOPs would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model, making the pretraining baseline stronger. More importantly, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget — say, best-of-8 majority voting or greedy decoding with a few revision steps — would create a much more realistic comparison: the question should be "given a fixed total FLOPs budget, what is the best way to split it between pretraining and inference?" not "does test-time compute with a small model beat a large model using zero test-time compute?" The current comparison answers the latter, more narrow question. A fair comparison would allocate the total FLOPs budget jointly across pretraining model size and inference-time strategy for both models.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 9 and the Figure 1 bar charts show the FLOPs-matched results. The paper provides no variant where the larger model receives any test-time compute allocation, and explicitly notes the parameter-only scaling choice in Section 7. The takeaway box in Section 7 frames the finding carefully — "test-time compute can substitute for pretraining compute under certain conditions" — but the conditions include a meaningfully suboptimal pretraining baseline.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about the parameter-only scaling assumption and flags compute-optimal pretraining as future work. However, it does not acknowledge the asymmetry in test-time compute allocation (full compute-optimal strategy for the small model, zero for the large model) as a limitation. A practitioner should interpret the FLOPs-matched results as establishing a **lower bound** on the pretraining advantage — test-time compute can overcome a 14× parameter gap when the larger model is suboptimally trained and receives no inference budget, but the actual crossover point where pretraining becomes preferable is likely lower (i.e., pretraining wins at smaller scale factors or lower difficulty levels) under a fairer comparison.

---

### Limitation 4: Verifier Over-Optimization Limits Scaling and Is Not Resolved — Only Mitigated by Difficulty-Based Routing

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper documents verifier over-optimization as a central limiting factor for test-time compute scaling: beam search degrades easy-problem performance at high budgets (Figure 3, right), lookahead search — the most powerful optimizer — paradoxically performs worst overall (Figure 3, left), and qualitative examples in Appendix M show search producing degenerate outputs (repetitive low-information steps, overly short 1–2 step solutions) that score highly under the PRM but are incorrect. The compute-optimal policy *mitigates* this by routing easy problems away from aggressive search and toward best-of-N (or sequential revisions), but it does not *solve* the underlying problem. On medium-difficulty problems where beam search is actually deployed, over-optimization still limits the scaling ceiling — the beam search curves in Figure 3 flatten and sometimes decline well before the budget is exhausted.

**The consequence.** The current results are **specific to the verifier quality achievable with the Monte Carlo rollout training procedure** described in Appendix D. Improving the PRM — through better training data, adversarial robustness, ensemble methods, or architectural changes — would likely shift the difficulty thresholds (changing which problems benefit from search vs. best-of-N), move the over-optimization frontier to higher budgets (enabling more search compute before degradation sets in), and alter the compute-optimal policy itself. A practitioner cannot assume the specific difficulty-bin strategy lookup tables in this paper will transfer to their own PRM — they must re-derive the compute-optimal policy for their verifier's quality level. More fundamentally, the paper provides no path to scaling test-time compute *beyond* the PRM's reliability frontier: if you have a large compute budget but an imperfect verifier, the compute-optimal policy will tell you to stop spending compute (or switch to best-of-N) rather than to push search further, which means verifier quality is a **hard ceiling** on the benefits of test-time compute.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 (right) shows the over-optimization pattern clearly for difficulty bins 1–2 (beam search accuracy declines or plateaus while best-of-N improves). Figure 3 (left) shows lookahead search underperforming simpler methods. Appendix M (Figures 29, etc.) provides qualitative examples of degenerate high-PRM-score outputs. Appendix E compares PRM aggregation strategies (min vs. prod vs. last) and PRM vs. ORM, showing that these choices affect performance but do not eliminate over-optimization. The paper does not experiment with verifier improvements (e.g., ensemble PRMs, adversarial training, confidence calibration) to see how the scaling curves change.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies verifier over-optimization as a key phenomenon (Section 5.3, Section 8) and the compute-optimal policy is explicitly designed to work around it — by avoiding aggressive search where the verifier is unreliable — rather than to eliminate it. Section 8 mentions "more robust verifiers" as future work but provides no concrete directions or preliminary experiments. A practitioner should understand that the current approach is **bounded above by verifier quality**: all the efficiency gains from compute-optimal allocation are gains in how to spend compute given a fixed verifier, not gains from improving the verifier itself. If verifier quality is the bottleneck in their setting, compute-optimal allocation will help but not fundamentally change the ceiling.

---

### Limitation 5: Revisions and PRM Search Are Studied Independently — the Two Complementary Mechanisms Are Never Combined

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies two complementary axes for test-time compute — PRM-guided search (Section 5) and iterative revisions (Section 6) — but never combines them. The search experiments use the few-shot prompted base model as the proposal distribution; the revision experiments use the fine-tuned revision model with a separately trained ORM for answer selection. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

**The consequence.** The paper's central framework decomposes test-time compute methods into modifications to the **proposal distribution** (what the model generates — revisions) and the **verifier** (how outputs are selected — PRM search). These are presented as complementary, independent axes. However, the experiments never demonstrate this complementarity by combining them. The natural next step — using the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search, or using the PRM to guide which revision branches to pursue — is absent. This means: (a) The reported results represent a **lower bound** on what a fully integrated system could achieve; the 4× efficiency gains might be larger (or the failure modes might interact negatively — we don't know). (b) The difficulty-dependent patterns observed for search and revisions separately might shift when combined — a problem that benefits from both mechanisms might see super-additive gains, or the mechanisms might interfere. (c) The practical guidance for a deployer is incomplete: if you can afford both a PRM and a revision model, should you use them together? The paper provides no empirical basis for answering this question.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The revision experiments do use a verifier (a revision-specific ORM trained on the revision model's outputs; Appendix J, Figure 15), so revisions are not entirely verifier-free. But this is an ORM that scores complete solutions, not a PRM that guides step-by-step search. The paper's comparison of PRM vs. ORM (Appendix F, Figure 14) suggests PRMs outperform ORMs, so using a PRM with the revision model could yield better revision selection or enable pruning during revision chains. This comparison is never made. The revision model's sensitivity to verifier choice is explored (Figure 15a: base-LM PRM underperforms revision-specific ORM due to distribution shift), but the use of the PRM as a search mechanism rather than just a final-answer scorer is never tested with revisions.

**Mitigation status.** The gap is acknowledged in Section 8 as future work but not otherwise addressed. Given that the paper's conceptual framework emphasizes the independence of the proposal and verifier axes, the failure to demonstrate their combination is a significant omission. A practitioner deploying both mechanisms would be operating in an empirically uncharted regime — the paper provides no guidance on how search hyperparameters (beam width, lookahead depth) should be adjusted when the proposal distribution is a revision model rather than the base few-shot generator, or whether the compute-optimal policy would shift.

---

### Limitation 6: The Revision Model Has a Fundamental Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Problem with Only a Heuristic Patch

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained exclusively on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect followed by a correct target (Section 6.1). During training, the model never sees a correct answer in its own context, so it has no signal for what to do when the current answer is already correct — its learned behavior is "given a sequence of wrong answers, produce a correct one," not "given an answer (which may be correct or incorrect), improve it." At test time, when the model produces a correct answer during a revision chain, the next revision step may incorrectly "revise" it into a wrong answer. The paper reports:

> "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones"

**The consequence.** Sequential revision chains are inherently unstable: progress is not monotonic, and later revisions can undo earlier successes. The paper mitigates this by using majority voting or verifier-based selection across the entire chain — picking the best answer from any point rather than trusting the final revision. This is a heuristic patch that adds computational overhead (the verifier must score every step in the chain) and does not address the root cause: the model was never trained to recognize when no revision is needed. In a deployment setting, this means: (a) Revision chains longer than the optimal point waste compute — the model may cycle between correct and incorrect answers rather than converging. (b) The verifier's role becomes even more critical, since it must correctly identify which step in the chain produced the best answer — and if the verifier itself is imperfect (see Limitation 4), the chain-selection mechanism inherits those errors. (c) The ReST^EM experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) shows that attempts to further optimize the revision model with on-policy RL-style training cause performance to degrade substantially with sequential revisions — the revision training procedure is fragile and the positive results depend on specific data construction choices (offline pairing, edit-distance-based selection) that may not generalize to other training pipelines.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% reversion rate is reported in Section 6.1. Figure 6 (left) shows that pass@1 per step improves gradually but with fluctuations — not monotonically — consistent with occasional correct-to-incorrect flips. Figure 7 (right) shows that on the hardest problems (bin 5), the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio is moderate, not fully sequential — consistent with long chains being counterproductive. The ReST^EM degradation is shown in Figure 16.

**Mitigation status.** The paper's primary mitigation is within-chain selection via majority voting or verifier (Section 6.1, Appendix I). This is described as working adequately but is acknowledged as a workaround rather than a solution. The paper does not explore training the revision model to also handle correct-in-context examples (e.g., by including trajectories where the correct answer appears before the final step and the model is trained to output it unchanged, or by adding a "no revision needed" token). A practitioner building a revision-based system should expect to invest engineering effort in chain selection heuristics or revision-stopping criteria beyond what the paper provides.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

MeZO reframes the conversation around large language model adaptation from one dominated by a single assumption — that gradient computation via backpropagation is the only viable path — to one where **forward-only optimization is a first-class, practically deployable option for fine-tuning at scale**. This is not a new algorithm in the traditional sense (SPSA dates to 1992), but rather a **systems-level demonstration** that an old algorithm, when implemented with the right memory-efficiency tricks and deployed in the right regime (pre-trained models with task prompts), not only works but works *competitively* with backpropagation while using an order of magnitude less memory.

The magnitude of this shift is best understood as **reframing rather than paradigm shift**: the paper does not argue that ZO optimization replaces backpropagation for general-purpose training (pre-training still requires gradients), but it convincingly demonstrates that the field's default assumption — "fine-tuning requires backpropagation, therefore fine-tuning requires 12× inference memory" — is false for a broad and practically important class of problems. The 12× memory multiplier was treated as an immutable fact of life; MeZO shows it can be reduced to 1× with acceptable performance tradeoffs on most tasks.

**Reconciling contradictory prior findings.** The paper resolves a specific tension in the ZO optimization literature: classical lower bounds (Nemirovskij and Yudin, 1983; Duchi et al., 2015) predict that ZO methods should be catastrophically slow for high-dimensional optimization, yet the paper demonstrates effective optimization of 66B-parameter models. The resolution comes from identifying that these lower bounds assume generic optimization landscapes, while fine-tuning a pre-trained model with a suitable prompt operates in a regime where the loss landscape has small local effective rank — a property that the classical analyses did not account for. This does not invalidate the lower bounds (they remain correct for worst-case landscapes) but sharply limits their applicability to the specific setting that matters for LM adaptation. Prior work had empirically observed that fine-tuning occurs in low-dimensional subspaces (Aghajanyan et al., 2021; Li et al., 2018) and that pre-trained network Hessians have low effective rank (Papyan, 2018; Ghorbani et al., 2019), but nobody had connected these observations to the feasibility of ZO optimization. MeZO provides both the theoretical bridge (Theorem 1, Lemma 3) and the empirical validation (Figure 5 shows convergence rate independent of parameter count; Table 5 shows prompts are essential).

**Research directions that become more attractive.** The paper's core finding — that ZO methods can optimize at scale when the landscape is well-conditioned — opens a much broader set of questions about *which* optimization problems share this property and *which* ZO methods are most suitable for them. Transfer learning in vision (fine-tuning ImageNet-pretrained models), multi-task fine-tuning, continued pre-training on domain-specific corpora, and alignment fine-tuning (RLHF, DPO) all start from pre-trained models and may exhibit similar low-effective-rank properties. The paper makes investigating and characterizing this property — rather than developing ever-more-efficient backpropagation approximations — a central research question. It also shifts attention from "how can we reduce backpropagation memory?" (gradient checkpointing, quantization, activation offloading) toward "how can we design optimization procedures that never store gradients in the first place?" — a qualitatively different question with a different set of tradeoffs.

**Research directions that become less attractive.** The paper's negative results on variance reduction (Appendix B.3, Tables 8-9: gradient norm scaling and parameter norm scaling do not help and sometimes hurt), one-point gradient estimators (Appendix B.5, Table 11: substantially worse than SPSA), and layerwise adaptive rates (Appendix B.2: no consistent benefit over vanilla SGD) suggest that **classical approaches to improving ZO optimization — reducing variance, normalizing coordinates, adapting per-layer learning rates — are attacking the wrong bottleneck**. The bottleneck for fine-tuning is not gradient estimate variance in the classical sense but rather the effective rank of the landscape. Research effort directed at variance reduction for ZO methods in fine-tuning settings is likely to yield diminishing returns; effort directed at understanding and ensuring low-effective-rank landscapes (through better pre-training, prompt engineering, or architectural design) is more promising.

**Storage economics as a new dimension.** The paper's checkpoint storage analysis (Section 2.1: <0.1MB for 66B model training) reveals an implication that the paper itself does not fully explore: **model adaptation can now be decoupled from model storage**. A single base model plus hundreds of task-specific MeZO seed trajectories can replace hundreds of full or LoRA-adapted model copies. This changes the economics of personalized model deployment — a dimension that has received little attention in the fine-tuning literature, which has focused almost exclusively on training-time memory and compute.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Direct measurement of local effective rank during MeZO fine-tuning.** The paper's theoretical framework (Section 4) argues that convergence depends on the local effective rank `r` of the Hessian, not the parameter dimension `d`, but this claim is supported only indirectly (MeZO works, PEFT and full-parameter tuning converge at similar rates). A direct empirical validation would measure the Hessian spectrum throughout MeZO fine-tuning for a small-to-medium model (e.g., RoBERTa-base on SST-2 and TREC, where the Hessian is computationally tractable) and correlate the effective rank with observed optimization speed. The experiment would: (a) compute the top eigenvalues of the Hessian at regular intervals during MeZO training using Lanczos methods; (b) measure whether the effective rank `r = tr(H)/||H||_op` is indeed small (e.g., `r < 200` as suggested by Aghajanyan et al., 2021) and stable over the course of training; (c) compare effective rank across tasks where MeZO performs well (SST-2) vs. poorly (TREC with accuracy objective, where it drops to 68.6%) vs. without prompts (where it collapses to 51.9%). This would directly test the theory's core claim and provide a diagnostic for predicting when MeZO will succeed or fail on a new task.

**Combining MeZO with preference-based fine-tuning objectives.** The paper demonstrates MeZO with accuracy and F1 as non-differentiable objectives (Table 3) but does not apply it to the most impactful non-differentiable objective in current LM research: human preference scores. A natural follow-up would replace the reward model + PPO pipeline in RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022) with direct MeZO optimization of a preference-based objective. The experiment would: (a) take a pre-trained instruction-tuned model (e.g., LLaMA-2-7B-Chat); (b) define the loss as negative average preference score from a pre-trained reward model (or from direct human A/B comparisons via a ranking loss); (c) run MeZO for 5K–20K steps and measure win rate against the RLHF baseline using the same reward model. The key question is whether MeZO's higher-variance gradient estimates work for preference optimization, where the reward signal is already noisy. The concurrent work of Tang et al. (2024) takes a step in this direction with ranking oracles, but a direct head-to-head comparison with PPO-based RLHF at the 7B scale would establish whether MeZO can simplify the alignment pipeline. A negative result — MeZO fails to optimize preferences effectively — would be equally informative, delineating a boundary condition on the non-differentiable objective claim.

**MeZO for continued pre-training and domain adaptation.** All experiments in this paper evaluate task-specific fine-tuning with prompts, where the training data is limited (16 to 1,000 examples per class). A different and practically important regime is continued pre-training on large domain-specific corpora (e.g., adapting a general LM to legal, medical, or scientific text), where training sets can be millions of documents and optimization runs for many epochs. The paper never tests this regime, and it is unclear whether the low-effective-rank property persists when the training objective is next-token prediction on a broad distribution rather than a narrow task-specific loss. The experiment would: (a) take a pre-trained 7B model; (b) continue pre-training on a domain corpus (e.g., 1B tokens of PubMed abstracts) using both MeZO and Adam; (c) measure not just final perplexity but also downstream task performance (biomedical QA, NER) and compare total GPU-hours. The key question is whether MeZO's per-step speed advantage compounds over many epochs to yield overall faster adaptation, or whether the high variance of the ZO estimate causes slow convergence that outweighs the per-step savings when millions of steps are needed.

**Systematic comparison to gradient checkpointing + FlashAttention + quantization.** The paper's 12× memory reduction claim is measured against a backpropagation baseline with no memory-saving optimizations (Appendix E.7: "We did not turn on any advance memory-saving options, e.g., gradient checkpointing."). Modern fine-tuning pipelines routinely use gradient checkpointing (2-3× memory reduction), FlashAttention (reduces attention memory from O(n²) to O(n)), and 8-bit quantization (2× reduction). The actual memory gap between MeZO and an optimized, production-grade fine-tuning pipeline is unknown. A rigorous comparison would: (a) measure peak GPU memory for fine-tuning OPT models from 1.3B to 30B with the best available combination of gradient checkpointing, FlashAttention-2, and 8-bit Adam; (b) compare to MeZO on the same hardware with the same batch size and sequence length; (c) measure both memory and total wall-clock time to convergence. The paper's 12× figure should be treated as an upper bound; the actual gap against an optimized baseline might be 3-5×, which would change the practical calculus for adoption. This experiment is straightforward to run and would substantially strengthen (or appropriately qualify) the paper's central efficiency claim.

**MeZO for vision transformers and multi-modal models.** The paper evaluates exclusively on text-based LMs (RoBERTa, OPT). The low-effective-rank property of pre-trained models is not specific to language — vision transformers (ViT) pre-trained on ImageNet-21k and fine-tuned on downstream classification tasks may exhibit similar landscape conditioning. A replication on ViT would test the generality of the theoretical framework. The experiment would: (a) fine-tune ViT-Large (300M parameters) on CIFAR-100, Oxford Pets, and other standard vision benchmarks using MeZO with a prompt-equivalent (e.g., reformulating classification as a masked patch prediction task); (b) compare to full fine-tuning and linear probing; (c) measure whether MeZO's performance relative to backpropagation correlates with pre-training quality (e.g., supervised vs. self-supervised pre-training) — the theory predicts that better pre-training (lower effective rank) should yield better MeZO performance.

**Scaling laws for ZO fine-tuning steps vs. data size.** The paper uses 100K steps for RoBERTa and 20K steps for OPT regardless of training set size (k=16 vs. k=512 for RoBERTa; 1,000 examples for OPT). There is no systematic study of how many MeZO steps are needed as a function of training data size, model scale, or task difficulty. A scaling law analysis — analogous to the Chinchilla laws for pre-training but applied to ZO fine-tuning — would provide practical guidance for practitioners: given a model of size N, a dataset of size D, and a target accuracy, how many MeZO steps (or forward passes) should one budget? The experiment would: (a) sweep training steps (1K to 100K) and dataset sizes (16 to 10,000 examples) for RoBERTa-large on a few representative tasks; (b) fit power-law scaling relationships; (c) identify whether the optimal step count scales sub-linearly or linearly with dataset size. The paper's current choice of 100K/20K steps is heuristic; a principled basis would make MeZO adoption more predictable.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Single-GPU fine-tuning of 30B+ models for research labs and small companies.** The most immediate practical application is the one the paper demonstrates directly: a researcher with a single A100 80GB GPU can fine-tune a 30B-parameter model (or, with 2 GPUs, a 66B model) using MeZO, whereas standard fine-tuning with Adam would be limited to 2.7B on the same hardware (Figure 4). This democratizes access to large-model adaptation for academic labs, startups, and individual researchers who lack multi-GPU clusters. The paper shows that MeZO achieves comparable performance to Adam fine-tuning on 7 of 11 OPT-13B tasks (Table 1) and meaningful gains over zero-shot and ICL on OPT-30B and 66B (Table 2) — for example, improving OPT-30B SQuAD F1 from 46.5% (zero-shot) to 85.2% (MeZO). The concrete workflow: load an OPT-30B model on a single A100, run MeZO for 20K steps (approximately 33 GPU-hours at 5.9s/step), and deploy the resulting model — all on hardware that costs single-digit dollars per hour on cloud instances.

**Direct optimization of task-specific metrics without surrogate losses.** The paper's demonstration that MeZO can optimize accuracy and F1 directly (Table 3) — albeit with task-dependent gaps to cross-entropy — enables a deployment pattern where practitioners define their objective as exactly the metric they care about, rather than a proxy loss. For a production classification system where end-to-end accuracy is the KPI, training with MeZO and accuracy as the objective eliminates the mismatch between the cross-entropy surrogate and the deployment metric. The observed 0.6-point gap on SST-2 (93.3% cross-entropy vs. 92.7% accuracy-based) and near-parity on SNLI (83.0% vs. 82.7%) suggest this is viable for some tasks, while the 25.7-point gap on TREC serves as a warning that it is not universal. Even for tasks where direct accuracy optimization underperforms, the approach is valuable for **final-stage fine-tuning**: train with cross-entropy to convergence, then switch to MeZO with accuracy as the objective for a few thousand steps to directly optimize the deployment metric — a pattern the paper does not explore but which follows naturally from the method's flexibility.

**Storage-efficient personalized model deployments.** MeZO's <0.1MB checkpoint storage for a 66B model training run (Section 2.1) enables a deployment architecture where a service provider stores one copy of a large base model and hundreds or thousands of per-user, per-task, or per-session MeZO adaptation trajectories — each essentially a compressed record of gradient updates that can be replayed to reconstruct a customized model on demand. The reconstruction cost (re-applying the updates) is non-trivial but may be acceptable for batch processing or periodic model refresh scenarios. Compare to the alternatives: storing a full model copy per task costs 132GB per task at half-precision; storing LoRA adapters costs 38MB per task; storing MeZO checkpoints costs <0.1MB per task. For a service with 1,000 customized model variants, this is the difference between 132TB (full), 38GB (LoRA), and 100MB (MeZO) of storage — a 380× advantage over LoRA and a 1,000,000× advantage over full checkpoints. The paper does not demonstrate this deployment pattern, but the storage analysis makes the economics clear.

**Memory-constrained edge and mobile deployment of adapted models.** While the paper focuses on GPU training, the memory-equivalence of MeZO training and inference has implications for on-device adaptation. If a model can run inference on a device (e.g., a 7B model quantized to 4 bits on a phone), MeZO could in principle run *training* on that same device — fine-tuning the model on user-specific data (writing style, preferences, knowledge) without offloading to the cloud. The paper does not demonstrate this, and MeZO's 20K-step training requirement would be challenging on battery-constrained devices, but the architectural possibility — training with zero additional memory beyond what inference already uses — is unique among current fine-tuning methods. Even gradient checkpointing requires storing some intermediate activations for the backward pass, making it more memory-intensive than pure inference. A production implementation would need to address the step count (perhaps through better pre-training or more sample-efficient ZO variants), but the memory barrier — the primary obstacle to on-device training — is eliminated by MeZO.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions MeZO against two main alternatives: standard fine-tuning with backpropagation (FT) and in-context learning (ICL). The tradeoffs are clearly articulated by the experimental results:

- **Prefer MeZO over full fine-tuning when** you are GPU-memory-constrained and need to train models larger than ~3B parameters on limited hardware (1-2 GPUs). This is the clearest use case: the paper shows MeZO trains a 30B model on a single A100 where FT would require 8 GPUs (Figure 4), and on OPT-13B MeZO achieves comparable or better performance to FT on 7 of 11 tasks (Table 1) while using 12× less memory. The tradeoff is more training steps (20K vs. ~625) but comparable or lower total GPU-hours at the 30B scale (Appendix F.6).

- **Prefer MeZO over in-context learning when** you have access to training data (even modest amounts — 1,000 examples) and the task requires genuine parameter adaptation to achieve acceptable accuracy. MeZO consistently outperforms ICL across all tasks and model scales (Tables 1 and 2), sometimes dramatically (+24 points on WSC for OPT-13B). The paper uses 32 ICL examples; if your prompt budget can accommodate more demonstrations, ICL may improve but remains bounded by context length and is sensitive to example selection. MeZO incurs a one-time training cost but then runs at inference speed with no demonstration overhead.

- **Prefer MeZO when the objective is non-differentiable** (accuracy, F1, human preference scores, safety constraints) and you lack a well-calibrated differentiable surrogate. The paper demonstrates this for accuracy and F1 (Table 3). The alternative — training a surrogate reward model and optimizing it with RL — introduces a complex multi-stage pipeline with its own instabilities. MeZO's direct approach is simpler, though its effectiveness varies by task (works well on SST-2 and SNLI, poorly on TREC).

- **Prefer fine-tuning with backpropagation when** you are not memory-constrained (the model fits comfortably with Adam on your hardware), when wall-clock convergence time is the primary metric (for models <3B parameters, FT may converge faster in total time), or when the task exhibits a large MeZO-FT performance gap (e.g., CB: 83.9% FT vs. 69.6% MeZO, a 14-point gap that may be unacceptable for production). The paper does not establish *why* CB and similar tasks show large gaps, so for now this requires per-task evaluation.

- **Prefer ICL when** you have zero or very few training examples (the paper's `k=16` setting for RoBERTa still requires labeled data), when inference-time adaptation latency is unacceptable (MeZO requires training before deployment), or when the model's base capabilities on the task are already sufficient and the overhead of training is not justified. ICL's performance floor may also be higher than MeZO on some tasks — CB at 57.1% ICL vs. 46.4% zero-shot and 67.9% MeZO suggests ICL provides a meaningful boost over the base model, though MeZO does better with training.
