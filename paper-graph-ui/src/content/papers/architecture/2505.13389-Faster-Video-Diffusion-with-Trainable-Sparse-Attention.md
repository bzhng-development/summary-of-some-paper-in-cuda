# Faster Video Diffusion with Trainable Sparse Attention

**ArXiv:** [2505.13389](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13389)

## 🎯 Pitch

Video diffusion transformers can be trained from scratch with 2.53× fewer FLOPs and zero quality loss by learning which tokens to attend to, overturning the assumption that sparse attention inevitably degrades generation. Retrofitting this trainable sparsity into Wan2.1 slashes generation time from 31s to 18s, while a new sparse-distillation variant pushes the speedup to 50.9× while maintaining quality. These results make sparse attention a practical, first-class training primitive rather than a post-hoc compromise.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **VSA (Video Sparse Attention)**, a trainable, hardware-efficient sparse attention mechanism for video diffusion transformers (DiTs) that replaces the quadratic 3D full attention at both training and inference. The method uses a hierarchical two-stage design — a lightweight coarse stage that pools tokens into cubes to predict critical token locations, and a fine stage that computes token-level attention only within those selected cubes — and is evaluated through extensive pretraining experiments on DiTs from 60M to 1.4B parameters using the Wan2.1 architecture. VSA reaches a Pareto point that cuts total training FLOPs by 2.53× with no drop in diffusion loss, and when retrofitted into the open-source Wan2.1-1.3B model, speeds up attention time by 6× and reduces end-to-end generation latency from 31s to 18s, establishing that trainable sparse attention can match full attention quality only when the sparsity pattern is learned from data rather than applied post-hoc.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Quadratic Attention Is the Scaling Bottleneck for Video Diffusion Transformers

Modern video diffusion transformers (DiTs) face a fundamental computational barrier: their 3D full attention mechanisms scale quadratically with sequence length. A seemingly ordinary 5-second 720p video, once tokenized and flattened, unfolds into more than 100,000 tokens — far beyond the sequence lengths that dominate large language model training, where most computation occurs on sequences of 32K tokens or fewer. This mismatch between the architecture's computational demands and the data's inherent dimensionality creates a crippling bottleneck.

The paper frames this problem through a concrete lens: **attention computation dominates both training and inference costs** in state-of-the-art video DiTs such as Wan2.1, HunyuanVideo, CogVideoX, and MovieGen. At full resolution on long-sequence data, the majority of FLOPs go into computing the attention matrix $S = QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k}$ and the subsequent $AV$ multiplication — operations that scale as $O(L^2 d)$ and $O(L d^2)$ respectively, where $L = T \times H \times W$ is the flattened token count. For a latent of shape $(16, 32, 32)$, this yields $L = 16{,}384$ tokens, and for higher resolutions or longer videos, $L$ grows rapidly into the hundreds of thousands.

This is not merely a cost-of-training problem — it is a **structural barrier to scaling**. The paper argues that so long as attention remains quadratic, increasing model size, video resolution, or sequence length forces a disproportionate increase in compute that makes further scaling economically and practically infeasible. The trained models remain painfully slow at inference even when training is complete, limiting deployment in interactive or real-time settings.

### Why This Problem Matters: Training Economics, Inference Latency, and Democratization

The significance of this bottleneck operates on multiple levels:

**Training economics.** Unlike large language models, where long-context adaptation is often a small fraction of total training compute (under the "train-short, adapt-long" paradigm), state-of-the-art video DiTs dedicate the **majority of their compute budget** to full-resolution, long-sequence training. The paper explicitly notes this distinction: LLMs spend over 90% of total FLOPs on short sequences (≤32K tokens), making sparse attention less urgent during training. Video DiTs invert this — they require long-sequence training from the start to learn temporal coherence, making the quadratic attention cost a first-order training bottleneck rather than an inference-only concern.

**Inference latency.** Even after training, a pretrained Wan2.1-1.3B model requires approximately 31 seconds to generate a single video on an H100 GPU. Scaling to the Wan2.1-14B model pushes this to over 21 minutes (1274 seconds). These latencies preclude interactive applications (real-time video editing, conversational video generation, iterative creative workflows) and make high-throughput batch generation prohibitively expensive. The paper's reported reduction from 31s to 18s for the 1.3B model, and from 1274s to 576s for the 14B model, is not just an efficiency metric — it represents the difference between batch-only and potentially interactive deployment.

**Democratization of video generation.** As with image generation before it, video generation is moving toward a regime where open-source models (Wan2.1, HunyuanVideo, Open-Sora) compete with proprietary systems. However, the compute requirements for training and running these models at scale remain a barrier to entry for smaller labs, academic groups, and independent creators. Reducing attention cost by a factor of 2.53× during training and 6× during inference directly lowers the GPU-hours required, making high-quality video generation more accessible to resource-constrained settings.

**Theoretical significance for attention sparsity research.** Beyond the practical implications, this problem is theoretically interesting because it forces a confrontation with a long-standing tension in attention mechanism design: everyone "knows" that attention matrices are sparse (most entries are near zero), but exploiting that sparsity efficiently on modern GPU hardware has proven remarkably difficult. Video DiTs, with their extreme sequence lengths and bidirectional attention patterns, provide a stress test that reveals whether trainable sparse attention can work as a first-class design primitive rather than a post-hoc optimization.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The landscape of prior work can be organized along two axes: **when sparsity is introduced** (post-hoc at inference vs. during training) and **how critical tokens are selected** (fixed patterns vs. data-dependent prediction). Each category has well-documented shortcomings that motivate VSA's design.

#### Post-Hoc Sparsity Methods (Inference-Only Speedup)

The most common approach in video DiTs is to train with full attention and then apply sparsity only at inference time. Methods in this category include:

**Sliding Tile Attention (STA)** and similar approaches replace each attention head with a fixed or profile-derived sparse mask after training. The sparsity pattern — which tokens each query can attend to — is determined either by a fixed heuristic (e.g., attend only to tokens within a spatial-temporal window) or by profiling the pretrained model's attention patterns on representative data and hard-coding the observed sparsity.

**Sparge Attention** and related methods use offline profiling to identify which attention heads exhibit sparse behavior at which timesteps, then construct a static sparsity schedule for inference. **SVG (Sparse VideoGen)** applies spatial-temporal sparsity patterns based on heuristics about which token relationships matter most (e.g., same-frame tokens, same-spatial-location across frames).

These approaches share a fundamental problem: they introduce a **train-test mismatch**. The model learns its parameters in a context where every token can attend to every other token. At inference, it is evaluated in a context where most of those connections are severed. The paper explicitly calls this out:

> "Because the sparsity pattern is decided after training, these methods leave the bulk of training cost untouched and introduce a train-test mismatch: the DiT learns parameters in a dense context yet is evaluated in a sparse one."

This mismatch caps best-case quality at the dense model's ceiling and, in practice, often causes quality degradation once sparsity is pushed beyond a gentle budget. The model has never learned to compensate for missing connections, so important information that happened to flow through pruned paths is simply lost.

More critically, post-hoc methods leave the **training cost untouched**. If attention accounts for the majority of training FLOPs (as it does in video DiTs), then addressing only inference latency solves less than half the problem. The expensive part — training the model in the first place — remains quadratic.

#### Fixed-Pattern Sparse Attention During Training

A separate line of work explores sparse attention patterns that can be applied during training, but with the sparsity pattern fixed a priori rather than learned. Examples include:

**Spatial-temporal attention**, widely used in early video generation works (Open-Sora, OpenSora-Plan, LaVie, Latte), alternates between layers that perform spatial attention (tokens attend only within the same frame) and layers that perform temporal attention (tokens attend only along the time dimension at the same spatial location). This decomposes the $O((THW)^2)$ cost into $O(T(HW)^2)$ for spatial layers and $O(HW \cdot T^2)$ for temporal layers.

**Strided window attention** extends this by allowing tokens to attend within a limited spatial-temporal neighborhood, inspired by Swin Transformer's shifted window approach.

**Compressed KV attention** pools key and value tokens to reduce the effective sequence length before computing attention, reducing FLOPs but at the cost of information loss in the compressed representations.

The paper's ablation experiments (Table 1a) reveal a critical limitation of these fixed-pattern approaches: they often **outperform full attention at compute-optimal training budgets** (because they use the saved FLOPs to train for more steps), but this advantage **reverses with extended training**. When trained to convergence (or near-convergence), full attention pulls ahead because its unrestricted receptive field learns more expressive representations. The paper quantifies this: under a 4.5 × 10²⁰ FLOPs budget, fixed-pattern methods (spatial-temporal, strided window, compressed KV) show lower loss than full attention, but when training is extended to 4 × 10²¹ FLOPs, full attention overtakes them. VSA, by contrast, maintains its advantage at both budgets because its sparsity pattern is learned rather than fixed — it can adapt to what the model needs at each stage of training.

The deeper issue is that **fixed patterns encode inductive biases that may not hold** across all layers, attention heads, timesteps, and video content. The paper's visualization of VSA's learned attention patterns (Figure 5) demonstrates this concretely: some heads exhibit local, window-like attention patterns that align with sliding-window heuristics, but others display highly global patterns, and a third group shows mixed local-global behavior. A fixed pattern forces all heads into the same mold, losing this heterogeneity.

#### Trainable Sparse Attention in LLMs: Inspiration with Key Differences

The paper draws inspiration from recent trainable sparse attention methods in large language models, particularly **MoBA (Mixture of Block Attention)** and **NSA (Native Sparse Attention)** . Both use a lightweight prediction stage to identify which blocks of the key-value sequence contain relevant information for each query, then compute full attention only within those blocks.

However, LLM sparse attention methods are designed for a fundamentally different setting than video DiTs. The paper identifies three key differences that prevent direct transfer:

1. **Sequence length regimes.** LLM sparse attention targets sequences of 128K–1M+ tokens, but the bulk of LLM training still happens on short sequences (≤32K). This means sparse attention is an optimization for a minority of training. Video DiTs deal with 16K–100K+ tokens as the training default, making this a first-order concern.

2. **Causal vs. bidirectional attention.** LLMs use causal (autoregressive) attention where each query can only attend to past tokens. This enables optimization tricks (KV caching, grouped queries) that don't apply to bidirectional attention. Video DiTs use bidirectional attention where every token can attend to every other token — a harder problem for sparse selection because the critical tokens could be anywhere in the sequence.

3. **Hardware alignment constraints.** MoBA's implementation uses variable-length FlashAttention with large tiles (≥512), which constrains sparsity granularity. The paper's ablation (Table 1d) shows that smaller tile sizes (64×64) significantly improve model quality — but require block-sparse kernel implementations that MoBA's design doesn't support.

#### DSV: A Pioneering but Complex Trainable Approach

The paper acknowledges **DSV (Dynamic Sparse Video)** as the most closely related prior work — the first to explore trainable sparse attention specifically for video DiT training. DSV introduces dedicated low-rank attention predictors with reduced head dimensions to identify load-bearing attention regions without computing the full attention matrix. These predictors are trained in a separate, multi-stage process and are not fully end-to-end integrated with the main DiT training.

The paper identifies two practical limitations of DSV: (1) the multi-stage training pipeline adds complexity and may complicate adoption, and (2) the separate predictor training introduces coordination overhead. VSA aims to achieve similar goals with a simpler, end-to-end trainable design that requires minimal modifications to existing DiT training frameworks.

### The Fundamental Chicken-and-Egg Dilemma

The paper frames the central challenge of trainable sparse attention as a chicken-and-egg problem that none of the prior approaches fully resolve:

> "Identifying critical token positions traditionally requires computing the full attention matrix, which then erases any computational gains and defeats the purpose of sparse attention. Conversely, resorting to cheap heuristics and not precisely identifying critical tokens may miss high-weight regions and yield suboptimal results."

If you compute full attention to find the critical tokens, you've already paid the quadratic cost. If you use a cheaper method that avoids the full computation, you might miss important connections. The quality of the sparsity mask directly depends on how much computation you invest in predicting it, creating a tension between prediction accuracy and overall efficiency.

Prior work resolves this dilemma unsatisfactorily. Post-hoc methods pay the quadratic cost during training and then pick sparsity patterns afterward — they never face the dilemma head-on. Fixed-pattern methods use heuristics that cost nothing to predict but miss data-dependent relationships. DSV addresses the dilemma by training dedicated predictors, but with a complex multi-stage pipeline.

### How VSA Positions Itself

VSA positions itself as a resolution to this dilemma through **end-to-end trainable hierarchical attention**. The key insight is that critical token prediction can be performed at a coarser granularity — on pooled cube-level representations rather than individual tokens — making the prediction cost negligible (<0.2% of total attention compute) while still being informed by the actual attention scores on that pooled representation. Because the coarse stage is end-to-end trainable, the model learns which tokens to attend to based on the data, not based on heuristics.

The paper is explicit about its positioning relative to existing work:

**Against post-hoc methods**: VSA argues that trainable sparsity eliminates the train-test mismatch and attacks both training and inference cost, not just inference. The paper demonstrates this by pretraining models from scratch with VSA, showing that the learned sparsity patterns maintain quality while reducing training FLOPs by 2.53×.

**Against fixed-pattern methods**: The paper's scaling experiments show that fixed-pattern methods plateau or degrade with extended training, while VSA's learned patterns improve. The ablation in Table 1b directly compares data-dependent patterns (Exp 8, 10) against fixed local patterns (Exp 7, 9), showing the former consistently achieve lower loss.

**Against LLM sparse attention**: VSA adapts the hierarchical prediction idea from MoBA and NSA but redesigns it for bidirectional video attention with smaller tile sizes (64 vs. 512+), includes the coarse stage output directly in the final attention representation (rather than using it only for block selection), and eliminates grouped query constraints that are unnecessary in the bidirectional setting.

**Against DSV**: VSA offers an end-to-end trainable alternative that achieves comparable goals with a simpler training pipeline — no separate predictor training stages, no profiler-based design.

The paper also makes a broader claim about the **necessity of trainable sparsity for video DiTs specifically**. It argues that video DiTs represent a regime — extremely long sequences as the training default, bidirectional attention, attention dominating total compute — where trainable sparse attention is not merely a nice-to-have optimization but a prerequisite for continued scaling. The claim is that we are reaching the limits of what full attention can support in video generation, and making attention sparse (while learning the sparsity pattern) is the only path forward for higher resolutions, longer videos, and larger models.

This positioning is reinforced by the paper's deliberate choice to evaluate across model scales (60M to 1.4B), showing that VSA's advantages hold across the scaling curve rather than being an artifact of a particular model size. The claim that VSA "is the first trainable sparse attention that shows better scaling than full attention on DiTs" signals that the paper sees itself as establishing a new baseline — shifting the default assumption from "sparse attention is a quality-compromised speedup" to "trainable sparse attention is a scaling-enabling design choice."

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

VSA is an end-to-end trainable sparse attention mechanism designed to replace the standard quadratic 3D full attention inside video diffusion transformers, both during training and at inference time, without degrading generation quality. It solves the problem of how to predict which token-to-token interactions actually matter in a long video sequence — and skip the rest — using a lightweight learned predictor that itself costs almost nothing, so that the overall model achieves the same loss as full attention while requiring only a fraction of the floating-point operations.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The VSA system has four major components that operate on every self-attention layer of the video DiT:

1. **Cube-based partitioning** — divides the 3D video latent (shape `(T, H, W)`) into non-overlapping spatial-temporal cubes of shape `(C_t, C_h, C_w)`, where each cube maps to exactly one hardware tile on the GPU for block-sparse computation.

2. **Coarse stage** — a lightweight attention module that pools each cube into a single summary token, computes full dense attention over these pooled tokens to capture global context, and simultaneously identifies which cubes contain high-weight "critical tokens" that deserve fine-grained attention.

3. **Fine stage** — a block-sparse token-level attention that operates only within the top-K cubes selected by the coarse stage, dramatically reducing the number of query-key-value products computed while maintaining full resolution where it matters.

4. **Learnable gating** — a small neural network that produces per-token interpolation weights (`G_c`, `G_f`) to combine the coarse-stage output (global context) and fine-stage output (high-resolution local attention) into the final attention output.

Information flows as follows: the input video latent enters the attention layer → the latent is partitioned into `(C_t, C_h, C_w)` cubes and flattened into a 1D sequence respecting cube locality → the coarse stage pools each cube, computes dense attention, selects the top-K cubes per query row, and produces a coarse output `O_c` → the fine stage computes token-level attention inside those K selected cubes, producing a fine output `O_f` → the learnable gates blend `O_c` and `O_f` to produce the final output `O` → the output is reshaped back into the original video latent shape and fed to the next transformer block.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the cube partitioning and index flattening formula, because every subsequent stage depends on how tokens are grouped into blocks and how those blocks map to hardware tiles.
- **Second**, the coarse stage — its pooling operation, the dense attention computation on pooled tokens, and the top-K selection mechanism that generates the block-sparse mask — since this is the "critical token predictor" that determines where the fine stage will compute.
- **Third**, the fine stage — how the block-sparse mask from the coarse stage is applied, what attention computation is performed, and why the mask structure guarantees hardware efficiency.
- **Fourth**, the gating mechanism that blends coarse and fine outputs, including why a learnable combination is necessary rather than a fixed sum.
- **Fifth**, the kernel implementation decisions that make the theoretical savings translate to wall-clock speedup: block-sparse kernels, the fused softmax-topK-index kernel, and why the coarse stage's overhead is deliberately not further optimized.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems-and-algorithms paper** whose core idea is that a hierarchical two-stage attention mechanism — where a coarse pooled stage predicts critical token locations and a fine token-level stage attends only there — can match full attention quality while being end-to-end trainable, hardware-aligned, and scalable across model sizes. The central design tension is the tradeoff between sparsity granularity (smaller blocks → better quality but slower kernels) and hardware efficiency (larger blocks → faster execution but coarser, less precise sparsity).

#### Cube-Based Partitioning and Index Mapping

VSA's first design decision is how to partition the 3D video latent into groups that map cleanly to GPU hardware tiles. The video latent has shape `(T, H, W)` — frames, height, and width respectively. VSA divides this volume into non-overlapping spatial-temporal cubes, each with shape `(C_t, C_h, C_w)`. The paper uses `(C_t, C_h, C_w) = (4, 4, 4)` as the default configuration after extensive ablation (Table 1c, Exp 14-18), yielding cubes that each contain `B = C_t × C_h × C_w = 64` tokens.

**Why cubes?** The 3D video latent has spatial dimensions (within each frame) and a temporal dimension (across frames). A purely spatial partitioning would miss temporal relationships — tokens at the same spatial location in adjacent frames are often highly correlated. A purely temporal partitioning would miss spatial relationships within each frame. A cube that spans all three dimensions captures local 3D neighborhoods where motion, texture, and object boundaries create strong token-to-token dependencies. The paper's ablation (Table 1c) implicitly tests this by varying `(C_t, C_h, C_w)` combinations while keeping the total tile size `B` fixed: configuration `(4, 8, 4)` with `B = 128` partitions differently than `(4, 4, 4)` with `B = 64`, and the experiments confirm that the `(4, 4, 4)` configuration (smaller cubes, finer granularity) yields lower loss.

**Hardware co-design.** The cube size `B` directly determines the block-sparse tile size on GPU. The NVIDIA Hopper architecture achieves peak throughput for matrix multiplications when dimensions are multiples of 16, and smaller tiles reduce arithmetic intensity (fewer FLOPs per byte loaded from memory). The paper maps each cube to exactly one block that a GPU threadblock loads into shared memory (SRAM) during attention computation. This co-design means that the algorithm's logical grouping of tokens (cubes) aligns perfectly with the hardware's computational grouping (blocks) — there is no reshaping or gathering step needed between the two levels.

**The flattening index formula.** To implement cube-based partitioning within the standard transformer attention API (which operates on 1D sequences of tokens), VSA must define a mapping from 3D token location `(t, h, w)` to 1D sequence index `n`. The paper defines:

$$n = \left[ \left\lfloor \frac{t}{C_t} \right\rfloor N_h N_w + \left\lfloor \frac{h}{C_h} \right\rfloor N_w + \left\lfloor \frac{w}{C_w} \right\rfloor \right] B + (t \bmod C_t) C_h C_w + (h \bmod C_h) C_w + (w \bmod C_w)$$

where `$(N_t, N_h, N_w) = \left(\frac{T}{C_t}, \frac{H}{C_h}, \frac{W}{C_w}\right)$` are the number of cubes along each dimension, `$B = C_t C_h C_w$` is the number of tokens per cube, and `$\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$` denotes integer floor division.

**What this formula does operationally.** The expression decomposes into two parts separated by the first `$B$`. The left bracketed term computes which cube the token belongs to — it converts the 3D cube coordinate `$(\lfloor t/C_t \rfloor, \lfloor h/C_h \rfloor, \lfloor w/C_w \rfloor)$` into a flat cube index, then multiplies by `$B$` to get the starting position of that cube in the 1D sequence. The right three terms compute the token's offset within its cube, using the modulo remainders to get the local position `$(t \bmod C_t, h \bmod C_h, w \bmod C_w)$` and converting that 3D local coordinate to a 1D offset. The result `$n$` is the absolute position in the flattened sequence.

**Why this particular ordering?** The formula places all tokens from cube `$i$` contiguously in memory at positions `$[iB, (i+1)B-1]$`. This contiguity is exactly what the block-sparse kernel needs: when the coarse stage selects cube `$i$` for attention, the fine stage can load positions `$[iB, (i+1)B-1]$` as a single contiguous 64-element block from memory. Any other ordering (e.g., interleaving tokens from different cubes) would require a gather operation — loading scattered memory locations — which is substantially slower on GPUs. The paper notes in Appendix B that the `tile` and `untile` operations (reshaping between 3D and this 1D layout) can be moved to the beginning and end of the entire transformer block rather than repeated per attention layer, since each layer uses the same cube partitioning.

**Assumptions and constraints.** The mapping assumes that `$(T, H, W)$` are all integer multiples of their respective `$(C_t, C_h, C_w)$`, so that the video latent can be partitioned into an exact integer grid of cubes. The paper acknowledges in Section 5 that this constraint — requiring dimensions divisible by `$C_t = 4$, $C_h = 4$, $C_w = 4$` — may restrict compatible resolutions, but notes the practical workaround of generating a slightly larger latent and cropping to the target shape.

**Example.** For a latent of shape `$(16, 32, 32)$` with `$(C_t, C_h, C_w) = (4, 4, 4)$`, we get `$N_t = 4$, $N_h = 8$, $N_w = 8$`, so there are `$4 \times 8 \times 8 = 256$` cubes total, each containing `$B = 64$` tokens, yielding `$L = 16{,}384$` tokens. A token at position `$(t, h, w) = (5, 12, 20)$` belongs to cube `$(\lfloor 5/4 \rfloor, \lfloor 12/4 \rfloor, \lfloor 20/4 \rfloor) = (1, 3, 5)$`, with local offset `$(1, 0, 0)$`, and the formula maps this to a specific 1D index.

#### The Coarse Stage: Global Context and Critical Token Prediction

The coarse stage serves two purposes simultaneously: it captures global context across the entire video sequence, and it identifies which cubes contain the critical tokens that need fine-grained attention. It operates on a compressed representation of the video latent, making it computationally lightweight (less than 1% of total attention FLOPS, according to Section 2.2).

**Step 1: Cube-level pooling.** For each cube of shape `$(C_t, C_h, C_w)$` in the original latent, the coarse stage produces a single summary token by pooling all `$B$` tokens in that cube. Given the original query, key, and value matrices `$Q, K, V \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$` (where `$L = T \times H \times W$` is the total token count and `$d$` is the per-head dimension), the pooling operation produces compressed versions:

$$Q_c, K_c, V_c \in \mathbb{R}^{L/B \times d}$$

where each row `$i$` of `$Q_c$` (and similarly for `$K_c, V_c$`) is the mean of the `$B$` rows of `$Q$` corresponding to cube `$i$`.

**Why mean pooling?** Table 1e compares three pooling strategies: mean pooling (Exp 21, loss = 0.13162), max pooling (Exp 20, loss = 0.13929), and 3D convolution with stride equal to kernel size (Exp 19, loss = 0.27787 — the paper notes this caused training instability). Mean pooling wins decisively. The likely reason is that mean pooling preserves the first-order statistics of the token distribution within each cube — the pooled token represents the "average content" of that spatial-temporal region. Max pooling selects only the most activated feature in each dimension, discarding information about the distribution's spread. Convolution with a learned kernel introduces additional parameters that can overfit or destabilize training when combined with the already-complex attention computation.

**Step 2: Dense attention on pooled tokens.** With the compressed sequence of length `$L/B$`, the coarse stage computes full, dense attention:

$$S_c = \frac{Q_c K_c^\top}{\sqrt{d}}, \quad A_c = \text{Softmax}(S_c), \quad O_c = A_c V_c$$

where `$S_c \in \mathbb{R}^{(L/B) \times (L/B)}$` is the cube-to-cube attention score matrix, `$A_c$` is the row-normalized attention weight matrix, and `$O_c \in \mathbb{R}^{(L/B) \times d}$` is the coarse output. Because the sequence length has been reduced by a factor of `$B = 64$`, this computation is 64² = 4096× cheaper than full token-level attention in terms of the `$QK^\top$` cost.

**What this gives us.** `$A_c$` tells us, for each query cube, which key cubes contain information relevant to that query. An entry `$A_c[i, j]$` is the attention weight that the `$i$`-th query cube (as a pooled summary) assigns to the `$j$`-th key cube. High values indicate that the key cube `$j$` contains tokens that — at least at the cube-averaged level — are important for processing the query cube `$i$`. The output `$O_c$` provides a globally-contextualized representation for each query cube, but at coarse resolution: all tokens in cube `$i$` receive the same `$O_c[i, :]$` representation.

**Step 3: Broadcasting `$O_c$` back to token resolution.** To use this coarse output in the final attention result (which operates at token resolution), VSA broadcasts `$O_c$` back to the original sequence length:

$$O_c^{\text{token}} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$$

where every row `$n$` in `$O_c^{\text{token}}$` is set to `$O_c[\lfloor n/B \rfloor, :]$` — i.e., all `$B$` tokens in the same cube receive identical coarse-stage output. This broadcasting ensures the dimensionality matches the fine-stage output for the subsequent gated combination.

**Step 4: Top-K cube selection for the sparsity mask.** The core sparsification decision happens here. From `$A_c \in \mathbb{R}^{(L/B) \times (L/B)}$`, the coarse stage selects, for each query row `$i$`, the indices of the `$K$` key cubes with the highest attention weights:

$$\text{topk\_idx}[i, :] = \arg\text{top-K}_{j \in [0, L/B)} \; A_c[i, j]$$

The paper uses `$K = 32$` as the default value (Section 2.2), which was determined through the scaling experiments in Figure 2(c-d). For the default configuration with a latent of shape `$(16, 32, 32)$`, there are `$L/B = 256$` total cubes, so selecting `$K = 32$` achieves an attention sparsity of `$1 - 32/256 = 87.5\%$`. For the Wan2.1-1.3B finetuning experiments with latent shape `$(16, 28, 52)$`, the total cube count is `$(16/4) \times (28/4) \times (52/4) = 364$`, and `$K = 32$` yields `$1 - 32/364 \approx 91.2\%$` sparsity.

**How the mask is formed.** The selected cube indices define a block-sparse attention mask `$M \in \{0, -\infty\}^{L \times L}$`. Since each cube index `$j$` corresponds to `$B$` consecutive token positions, selecting cube `$j$` as a "keep" for query cube `$i$` means that every query token in cube `$i$` can attend to every key token in cube `$j$`. Formally:

$$M[\text{token } p \text{ in cube } i, \text{ token } q \text{ in cube } j] = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } j \in \text{topk\_idx}[i, :] \\ -\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

**Why block-sparse?** This structure — where the mask is constant over `$B \times B$` blocks — is exactly what makes the hardware efficiency possible. Instead of storing a per-token binary mask (which would have `$L \times L$` entries), the system stores only the index list `$\text{topk\_idx} \in \mathbb{N}^{(L/B) \times K}$`. The fine-stage kernel reads this compact representation and processes each `$B \times B$` attention tile as a dense block, achieving near-peak GPU throughput within each selected block.

**Importantly** (and this is a subtle point the paper clarifies in a footnote), VSA does not actually materialize the full `$L \times L$` mask `$M$` in memory. The fine-stage kernel consumes the block indices directly — a list of integers — rather than a binary mask. This avoids the `$O(L^2)$` memory cost of storing the mask even though the mask defines `$O(L^2)$` positions.

**The coarse stage's dual output.** The coarse stage produces two things: (1) the global context output `$O_c$` used in the final gated combination, and (2) the block index list `$\text{topk\_idx}$` that defines the sparsity pattern for the fine stage. Both are derived from the same attention computation on pooled tokens, meaning no additional forward pass is needed.

**Why the coarse stage output is included in the final representation.** The paper's ablation in Table 1b explicitly tests this design choice. Experiment 8 ("F (no $O_c$)") uses only the fine-stage output without adding the coarse-stage output, achieving loss 0.13296. Experiment 10 ("C & F") includes the coarse output via gating and achieves loss 0.13162. The improvement (0.13296 → 0.13162) demonstrates that the coarse-stage output contributes non-redundant information — it captures global context that the token-level fine stage, restricted to only `$K = 32$` cubes, may miss. This differs from MoBA, which uses its pooled attention only for block selection and discards the pooled output.

**Why the coarse stage accounts for <1% of total attention FLOPS.** With `$L = 16{,}384$` and `$B = 64$`, the compressed sequence length is `$L/B = 256$`. The dense attention on this compressed sequence costs `$O((L/B)^2 d) = O(256^2 d)$` FLOPS. The fine stage processes `$K = 32$` blocks per query row, each of size `$B \times B = 64^2$`, for a total of `$O(L \cdot K \cdot B \cdot d) = O(16{,}384 \times 32 \times 64 \times d)$` FLOPS. The ratio of coarse-to-fine FLOPS is approximately `$(L/B)^2 / (L \times K \times B) = (256^2) / (16{,}384 \times 32 \times 64) = 65{,}536 / 33{,}554{,}432 \approx 0.002$`, confirming the <0.2% claim in Section 2.4.

---

#### The Fine Stage: Block-Sparse Token-Level Attention

The fine stage computes attention at full token resolution, but only within the blocks selected by the coarse stage's top-K schedule. This is where the computational savings are realized — rather than computing `$L \times L$` attention scores, it computes `$L \times (K \cdot B)$` scores.

**Input and mask application.** The fine stage receives the original token-level `$Q, K, V \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$` and the block index list `$\text{topk\_idx}$` from the coarse stage. For each query block (cube) `$i$`, the fine stage:

1. Loads the `$B$` query tokens from cube `$i$` (a contiguous chunk of `$B$` rows from `$Q$`).
2. Loads the `$K \cdot B$` key and value tokens from the `$K$` cubes specified by `$\text{topk\_idx}[i, :]$` (each is a contiguous chunk of `$B$` rows from `$K$` and `$V$`).
3. Computes the partial attention for this block:

$$S_f^{[i]} = \frac{Q[iB:(i+1)B, :] \times K[\text{selected blocks}, :]^\top}{\sqrt{d}}$$

$$A_f^{[i]} = \text{Softmax}(S_f^{[i]}), \quad O_f^{[i]} = A_f^{[i]} \times V[\text{selected blocks}, :]$$

where `$S_f^{[i]} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times (K B)}$` contains the attention scores from the `$B$` query tokens in cube `$i$` to the `$K \cdot B$` key tokens in the selected blocks.

4. Writes `$O_f^{[i]}$` to the corresponding `$B$` output rows of `$O_f \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$`.

**Why this is efficient.** Each `$S_f^{[i]}$` computation involves a matrix multiplication of dimensions `$B \times d$` (the query block) times `$d \times (K B)$` (the selected key blocks), costing `$O(B \times K B \times d) = O(K B^2 d)$` FLOPS. Summed over all `$L/B$` query blocks, the total fine-stage FLOPs are `$O((L/B) \times K B^2 d) = O(L K B d)$`. For the default configuration (`$L = 16{,}384$, $K = 32$, $B = 64$`), this is `$16{,}384 \times 32 \times 64 \times d$` versus `$16{,}384^2 \times d$` for full attention — a reduction factor of `$L / (K B) = 16{,}384 / (32 \times 64) = 8\times$`. Section 3.2 reports this `$8\times$` reduction in attention FLOPs, which translates to `$2.53\times$` reduction in total training FLOPs (since attention accounts for a large fraction but not all of total compute).

**The block-sparse kernel implementation.** The paper implements the fine stage using a custom block-sparse attention kernel written with ThunderKittens, a framework for writing high-performance GPU kernels. The kernel exploits two facts:

1. **Contiguous memory access.** Because the cube partitioning ensures all tokens in a cube are contiguous in the 1D sequence, loading a "block" of keys or values means reading a contiguous chunk of memory — no gather/scatter operations.

2. **Dense computation within blocks.** Within each selected `$B \times B$` tile, the kernel performs dense matrix multiplication using the GPU's tensor cores, achieving near-peak throughput. The overhead is only in the outer loop that iterates over the K selected blocks per query block.

Section 3.4 reports that this kernel achieves **85% of FlashAttention-3's MFU (Model FLOPS Utilization)** at long sequence lengths — remarkable for a sparse kernel, since sparsity typically introduces irregular memory access patterns that tank GPU utilization.

**Key design parameter: tile size B.** The tile size `$B$` creates a direct tradeoff between quality and speed:
- **Smaller B** (e.g., `$B = 16$` with `$(C_t, C_h, C_w) = (2, 4, 2)$`): The coarse stage can localize critical tokens more precisely — each selected cube spans fewer tokens, so the fine stage wastes less computation on unimportant tokens within a partially-important cube. The paper shows (Table 1c, Exp 18) that `$B = 16$` achieves the lowest loss (0.13155) among all tile sizes tested. However, smaller tiles reduce arithmetic intensity (fewer FLOPS per memory byte loaded) and increase the overhead of the outer loop that iterates over blocks. Exp 18 (`$16 \times 16$` fine-stage tiles) is 2.26× slower than Exp 17 (`$64 \times 64$` tiles) despite the quality improvement.
- **Larger B** (e.g., `$B = 256$` with `$(C_t, C_h, C_w) = (4, 8, 8)$`): The kernel runs faster because each block does more work per memory load, but sparsity is blurred — if even one token in a 256-token cube is critical, the entire 256-token block must be processed. The paper shows (Table 1c, Exp 14) that `$B = 256$` yields loss 0.13375, worse than `$B = 64$` (0.13162).
- **Default B = 64:** The paper selects `$B = 64$` with `$(4, 4, 4)$` as the Pareto-optimal point — it achieves loss nearly as good as `$B = 16$` (0.13162 vs. 0.13155) while being dramatically faster. The 4×4×4 cube also intuitively captures a local 3D neighborhood of 4 consecutive frames and a 4×4 spatial patch, which is a natural motion-processing unit for video.

**Mismatched granularity (Table 1c, Exp 16).** The paper tests an interesting variant where the coarse stage uses small cubes `$(4, 4, 4)$` (effectively `$B = 64$` for prediction) but the fine stage operates on larger tiles corresponding to `$(4, 8, 8)$` (effectively `$B = 256$`). To make this work, the coarse-stage attention map is downsampled with an additional `$(1, 2, 2)$` average pooling before top-K selection. This variant achieves loss 0.13328 — worse than the matched `$64 \times 64$` configuration (0.13162). This confirms that **both stages benefit from fine granularity**; it's not enough to have a precise coarse-stage prediction if the fine stage must process entire large blocks anyway.

**Why the fine stage does not add local pattern injection.** The paper tests several approaches for injecting explicit local attention into the fine stage (Table 1b):
- Experiment 11 ("C & F & L"): A separate local stage with `$(3, 3, 3)$` window attention added alongside the coarse and fine stages (loss = 0.13194).
- Experiment 12 ("C & F & L & E"): The local stage identifies cubes, and those cubes are explicitly **excluded** from the fine stage's top-K selection to avoid redundancy (loss = 0.13124, slightly better but close).
- Experiment 13 ("C & (F + L)"): The fine stage is forced to always include the local cubes in its selected set, without a separate local stage (loss = 0.13192).

All three variants perform similarly to the simpler "C & F" baseline (loss = 0.13162), indicating that **explicit locality priors offer minimal additional benefit** when the coarse stage already learns to select cubes — many of which end up being local anyway, as shown in the attention pattern visualizations (Figure 5a-f). The paper adopts the simpler "C & F" architecture, avoiding the added complexity and parameter count of a separate local stage.

---

#### The Gating Mechanism: Blending Coarse and Fine Outputs

The coarse stage output `$O_c$` and fine stage output `$O_f$` inhabit the same space `$\mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$` but serve different purposes: `$O_c$` provides global context at coarse resolution (all tokens in a cube get the same representation), while `$O_f$` provides high-resolution attention but only within the selected blocks. VSA combines them through a learnable per-channel gating mechanism:

$$O = O_c \odot G_c + O_f \odot G_f$$

where `$G_c, G_f \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$` are gating vectors produced by a linear projection of the input hidden states, and `$\odot$` denotes element-wise multiplication along the channel dimension.

**What the gates do operationally.** For each feature dimension (each of the `$d$` channels), the gate values determine whether the coarse or fine output dominates. A gate value close to 1.0 for that dimension means "trust this stage's output for this feature"; a value close to 0.0 means "ignore this stage's output for this feature." Since `$G_c$` and `$G_f$` are produced independently (the paper's pseudocode in Appendix B shows `coarse_attn_gate` and `fine_attn_gate` are generated by splitting the projected gate vector), the model can learn to use different stages for different features — e.g., coarse for low-frequency global lighting information, fine for high-frequency texture details.

**How the gates are computed.** The exact mechanism is:
1. Take the input hidden states before the attention layer, of shape `$(B, L, d_{\text{model}})$` where `$d_{\text{model}}$` is the model dimension.
2. Apply a linear projection to produce a gate tensor of shape `$(B, L, 2d)$` — twice the per-head dimension, because we need coarse and fine gates for each head.
3. Split into `$G_{\text{coarse}}$` and `$G_{\text{fine}}$`, each of shape `$(B, L, d)$`.
4. Apply a sigmoid activation to constrain gate values to `$[0, 1]$` (standard gating practice, though the paper does not explicitly specify the activation — it's implied by the `[0, 1]` range).
5. Reshape to per-head dimensions and multiply element-wise with the attention outputs.

**Why learnable gating rather than a fixed sum.** A fixed 0.5-0.5 sum would treat all features and all layers identically. The paper's design allows **layer-specific** and **feature-specific** mixing: early layers might rely more on fine-stage local patterns for low-level feature extraction, while later layers might rely more on coarse-stage global context for semantic understanding. The gating also adapts during training — at initialization when the coarse stage's predictions are random, the gates can learn to downweight coarse and trust fine (or vice versa), though the paper's sparse adaptation procedure (§2.3) initializes `$G_c = 0$` to start as pure fine-stage attention.

**The sparse adaptation initialization trick.** When fine-tuning a pretrained full-attention model with VSA (Section 2.3), the gating projection weights do not exist in the pretrained checkpoint and are randomly initialized. To prevent training instability from this cold start, the paper:

1. Initializes the coarse gate `$G_c$` to zero — the model starts by using only the fine-stage output.
2. Removes the fine gate `$G_f$` — equivalently setting `$G_f = 1$` everywhere, so the fine stage output passes through unmodified.
3. Initializes the sparsity level by setting `$K = L/B$` — every cube is selected, making the fine stage equivalent to full attention at the start of adaptation.

As training progresses, `$K$` is gradually reduced to the target sparsity level (the paper describes a schedule in Appendix C.5: reduce Top-K by 4 every 50 steps after an initial 50-step warmup with full attention), and `$G_c$` is learned through the flow-matching loss. This annealing strategy smoothly transitions the model from dense to sparse attention.

#### Kernel Implementation: Making Sparsity Translate to Speed

The paper's kernel implementation is a critical engineering contribution that ensures the FLOP savings from sparsity translate to actual wall-clock speedup. The implementation has three components: the block-sparse fine-stage kernel, the fused coarse-stage kernel, and the overall integration.

**Fine-stage block-sparse kernel.** The paper implements this using **ThunderKittens**, a framework for writing high-performance attention kernels. The kernel uses a block-sparse execution model:

1. The kernel receives the block index list `$\text{topk\_idx}$` as a compact integer array — not a binary mask.
2. For each query block `$i$`, the kernel loads `$K$` key-value blocks from global memory into SRAM, where each block is `$B \times d$` elements.
3. Within SRAM, the kernel computes the attention for the `$B \times K B$` tile using standard dense matrix multiplication primitives — the same kind of computation that FlashAttention would do for a contiguous chunk, but applied to a subset of blocks.

The key performance metric is **85% MFU of FlashAttention-3** at long sequence lengths (Figure 4b). MFU measures what fraction of the GPU's theoretical peak FLOPS the kernel achieves. FlashAttention-3 is a highly optimized dense attention kernel that achieves near-peak utilization on modern GPUs; VSA's sparse kernel reaching 85% of that is exceptional because sparsity typically introduces overhead from irregular memory access and loop control logic.

**Why FlexAttention underperforms.** Figure 4b shows that PyTorch's FlexAttention with an identical 64×64 block-sparse mask achieves only a 2× speedup over FlashAttention-3, whereas VSA achieves over 6×. FlexAttention is a general-purpose programmable attention API — it can express arbitrary sparsity patterns — but this generality comes at a cost: it cannot exploit the specific structure of block-sparse attention (all blocks the same size, known at kernel launch time) to the same degree as a purpose-built kernel.

**Coarse-stage kernel optimization.** The coarse stage must compute softmax and top-K selection over the `$(L/B) \times (L/B)$` attention matrix and then convert the result to block indices. The naïve approach would be:

1. Compute `$S_c = Q_c K_c^\top / \sqrt{d}$` (a separate kernel).
2. Compute row-wise softmax (a separate kernel).
3. Compute row-wise top-K and extract indices (a separate kernel).
4. Convert the top-K mask to block indices (a separate kernel).

Four kernel launches means four round-trips to global memory for intermediate results. The paper instead **fuses softmax, Top-K selection, and mask-to-index conversion into a single kernel**, reducing memory traffic and launch overhead. However, the paper acknowledges this fusion provides only "modest improvements" (Section 3.4D and Table 3) — the top-K selection itself, which requires sorting or selection within each row, is the dominant cost regardless of fusion.

**Why the coarse stage is not further optimized.** The paper argues that the coarse stage's memory overhead is negligible: with `$L/B = 256$`, the attention matrix is `$256 \times 256$` floats per head — tiny compared to the full `$L \times L = 16{,}384^2$` matrix that full attention materializes. The coarse-stage runtime, even without perfect optimization, accounts for only 14% of total attention time when the fine stage is 87.5% sparse (Section 3.4). This makes the complexity of further kernel fusion — which would require intrusive rewriting of the FlashAttention kernel to inline bitonic sorting for Top-K — unnecessary for the current scale. The paper leaves this as a future optimization direction for regimes where the coarse-stage overhead becomes more significant.

**Putting it together: end-to-end kernel flow.** For a single attention layer:
1. The input latent is partitioned into cubes (this reshaping is essentially free — it's a view operation).
2. The coarse-stage kernel runs: mean pooling (a lightweight reduction), dense attention on the compressed sequence, fused softmax-topK-index, and broadcasting of the coarse output.
3. The fine-stage block-sparse kernel runs: for each query block, load the K selected key-value blocks, compute attention within those blocks, write output.
4. The gating projection runs: a small linear layer produces `$G_c$` and `$G_f$`, which are applied element-wise to the coarse and fine outputs.
5. The result is summed and reshaped back to the original 3D layout.

Figure 4a shows the runtime breakdown for a single transformer block in Wan-1.3B and HunyuanVideo. With VSA, attention time drops by 6× (from dominating the block to accounting for approximately 20% of total runtime), making attention no longer the primary bottleneck.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Trainable Sparsity as a First-Class Training Primitive, Not a Post-Hoc Optimization

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is reframing sparse attention from a post-hoc inference-time speedup hack into a **first-class design primitive that should participate in training**. This is not an incremental engineering improvement — it is a reversal of the dominant assumption in video DiT research, where the standard pipeline (Sliding Tile Attention, Sparge Attention, SVG) has been: train with full attention → profile or heuristically determine sparsity patterns → apply sparsity only at inference. The paper shows that this pipeline has an inherent ceiling because the model never learns to compensate for the missing connections it will face at deployment time.

The intellectual move is recognizing that the train-test mismatch these methods introduce is not a minor technical nuisance but a fundamental limit on achievable quality-sparsity tradeoffs. The paper's evidence for this claim is explicit: fixed-pattern methods (spatial-temporal, strided window) outperform full attention at compute-optimal budgets but lose their advantage with extended training (Table 1a, Exp 1-4 vs. Exp 5), while VSA's learned patterns maintain superiority at both training budgets (Exp 6). This demonstrates that the model benefits from learning its sparsity pattern — it can allocate its limited attention budget adaptively based on what each layer, head, and timestep actually needs, rather than being forced into a human-designed template.

The significance extends beyond video DiTs. The paper is making a broader methodological argument: for any transformer architecture where attention dominates total compute and sequence lengths are inherently long (video, high-resolution images, multi-modal fusion), sparsity should be treated as a learnable architectural property rather than an inference-only optimization. The fact that VSA achieves this with an end-to-end trainable design requiring "minimal modifications to existing DiT training frameworks" (Section 2.2 and the DSV discussion in Appendix E) means the barrier to adopting this reframing is low — a practical argument for why the field should shift its default approach.

### Innovation 2: Resolving the Critical-Token Chicken-and-Egg Problem Through Hierarchical Granularity

The paper identifies and resolves a dilemma that has plagued previous attempts at trainable sparse attention: to know which tokens are critical, you traditionally need to compute the full attention matrix, but doing so defeats the purpose of sparsity. Prior work either accepted the quadratic cost during training (post-hoc methods), used heuristics that cost nothing but miss data-dependent patterns (fixed-pattern methods), or built separate prediction networks that required complex multi-stage training (DSV).

VSA's resolution is conceptually elegant: **perform the critical token prediction at a coarser granularity where the computation is trivially cheap, then use that prediction to guide token-level computation only where needed.** The coarse stage operates on pooled cube-level representations — compressing the sequence by 64× — so its dense attention costs only 0.2% of the total attention FLOPs. Yet because this coarse attention is computed from the actual (pooled) content, not from heuristics, its predictions remain data-dependent and adaptive. The prediction problem becomes tractable by changing the resolution at which it is posed.

What makes this more than just a compression trick is the **end-to-end differentiability of the entire pipeline**. The coarse stage's attention weights `A_c` flow into a softmax and top-K selection, and while the top-K operation itself is non-differentiable, the coarse stage's output `O_c` provides a gradient path for learning the pooled representations. The fine stage then provides token-level gradients. The system learns to produce cube-level representations that make good top-K predictions because those predictions determine which tokens the fine stage can attend to, and the fine-stage loss backpropagates through the pooled representations.

This resolution has implications beyond video DiTs. It suggests a general design pattern for sparse attention: pair a cheap, coarse global predictor with an expensive, fine local executor, and train them jointly. The coarse predictor doesn't need to be perfect — it just needs to be good enough that the fine executor can recover what matters. The paper's finding that VSA maintains ≥60% attention score coverage (Figure 5, right) while using only K=32 out of 364 cubes confirms this sufficiency.

### Innovation 3: The Sparsity Granularity Tradeoff as a Quantifiable Design Axis

The paper elevates tile size `B` — the number of tokens grouped into a single sparse-attention block — from an implementation detail to a **first-order design parameter with systematic, quantifiable quality-efficiency tradeoffs**. Prior work on sparse attention for video DiTs (STA, SVG, DSV) either used fixed tile sizes without ablation or treated tile size as a hardware constraint to be accepted rather than optimized. The paper's ablation in Table 1c (Exp 14-18) provides the first systematic study of this tradeoff across sizes from `B = 16` to `B = 256`.

The key insight is that tile size determines two things simultaneously: (1) the precision with which the coarse stage can localize critical tokens (smaller tiles = finer localization), and (2) the arithmetic intensity of the fine-stage kernel (larger tiles = more FLOPs per memory byte loaded = higher GPU utilization). These two effects pull in opposite directions, creating a genuine tradeoff rather than an obvious optimization.

The empirical finding — that `B = 64` with `(4, 4, 4)` cubes sits at a Pareto-optimal point where loss is nearly as good as `B = 16` (0.13162 vs. 0.13155) while being dramatically faster — is practically useful but not the intellectual contribution. The intellectual contribution is **demonstrating that this tradeoff exists, can be measured, and should be tuned**. The paper's detailed breakdown of how mismatched granularity between stages (Exp 16, coarse at 64 but fine at 256) degrades performance shows that both stages need to operate at compatible resolutions — a non-obvious finding that wouldn't emerge from treating tile size as a hardware-only concern.

This reframing matters because it changes how practitioners should think about implementing sparse attention. Rather than accepting the largest tile the hardware supports (as MoBA does with tiles ≥512), the optimal design intentionally sacrifices some hardware efficiency for sparsity precision, then compensates with careful kernel engineering (the 85% MFU result) to recoup as much throughput as possible while using the smaller tiles.

### Innovation 4: Empirical Evidence That Learned Sparsity Scales Better Than Dense Attention

Perhaps the paper's most surprising claim — and the one with the broadest implications — is that **VSA achieves a better scaling curve than full attention**: given the same total training FLOPs, a model trained with VSA reaches lower diffusion loss than one trained with full attention (Figures 2a-b). This is not merely "sparse attention matches dense attention" — it is "sparse attention is more compute-efficient for achieving a given quality level."

The evidence for this claim is extensive: pretraining experiments from 60M to 1.4B parameters with compute budgets up to 4 × 10²¹ FLOPs show that VSA consistently produces a better Pareto frontier (Figure 2b), with the fitted curves indicating the 2.53× training FLOP reduction holds across scale. The loss curves in Figure 2a show that VSA tracks full attention nearly identically — the sparse model learns just as effectively per parameter update, but each update costs less than half as much.

Why might sparse attention *outperform* dense attention in a FLOPs-matched comparison? The paper suggests a subtle mechanism: for a fixed compute budget, the model with VSA processes more tokens or trains for more steps than the full-attention baseline. But VSA is also **learning what to ignore**, which may act as a form of implicit regularization — preventing the model from overfitting to spurious long-range correlations that full attention would capture. The paper doesn't explicitly investigate this regularization hypothesis, but the fact that fixed-pattern methods lose their advantage with extended training (Table 1a) while VSA maintains it suggests that the learned sparsity pattern is doing something beyond mere compute savings.

This finding shifts the burden of proof in the field. Before VSA, the default assumption was that sparse attention inevitably degrades quality relative to full attention, and the engineering challenge was minimizing that degradation. After VSA, the question becomes: under what conditions does learned sparsity provide a net improvement? This is analogous to how the vision community gradually shifted from "depthwise separable convolutions approximate standard convolutions" to "depthwise separable convolutions are a better design choice" — a reframing that changes architectural defaults.

### Innovation 5: Diagnosing the Contingency of Optimal Sparsity on Compute Budget and Sequence Length

The paper's scaling experiments reveal a finding that complicates simple narratives about sparsity: **the optimal sparsity level `K` is not a fixed property of the architecture or the data, but depends jointly on the training compute budget and the sequence length** (Figures 2c-d). At a sequence length of 61,440, `K = 16` outperforms `K = 32` under a 4.5 × 10²⁰ FLOP budget, but `K = 32` eventually surpasses `K = 16` when training is extended to 1 × 10²¹ FLOPs. Shorter sequences show different crossover points.

This is not a specific numerical recommendation (the paper leaves optimal-`K` prediction as an open problem) but a **diagnostic contribution**: it identifies sparsity level as a third axis in the scaling law framework, alongside model size and training FLOPs. The paper explicitly frames this in Section 3.2: "One promising direction is to explicitly model sparsity as an additional axis in the scaling law framework, alongside model size and total FLOPs." This reframes sparse attention from a binary choice (sparse vs. dense) to a continuous parameter that should be tuned based on available resources — much like how the Chinchilla scaling laws reframed model size and data quantity as jointly optimizable rather than fixed.

The practical implication is cautionary: naively applying a fixed sparsity level (e.g., 87.5% everywhere) may leave performance on the table at large budgets or long sequences. The intellectual implication is that sparsity should be understood as a **compute-allocation strategy**, not a model property. Just as the compute-optimal test-time scaling paper (Snell et al.) showed that inference strategies should adapt to problem difficulty, this finding suggests that training sparsity should adapt to the total compute invested — more compute allows the model to benefit from attending to more tokens, not just more parameters or more training steps.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The ablation and scaling experiments use the **Vchitect-T2V-Dataverse dataset**, with video latents of shape (16, 32, 32) — yielding 16,384 tokens for self-attention. For sparse adaptation and distillation experiments (Section 3.3), the paper uses **synthetically generated videos** from Wan-14B: 80,000 videos at 448×832 resolution with 61 frames for Wan-1.3B finetuning, and 200,000 videos at 768×1280 with 77 frames for Wan-14B finetuning.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use the **Wan2.1 architecture**, a state-of-the-art open-source video DiT. Ablation studies train 120M-parameter models from scratch using the GPT-NeoX initialization scheme (Table 2a). Scaling experiments span 60M to 1.4B parameters. Sparse adaptation uses the pretrained Wan2.1-1.3B and Wan2.1-14B checkpoints from the official release. The text encoder is the pretrained UMT5-XXL, and video tokenization uses Wan2.1's pretrained VAE.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric for pretraining experiments is **diffusion loss** — the flow-matching loss under the LogitNormal timestep sampling schedule — measured on a held-out validation set. For post-training evaluation, the paper reports **VBench scores** (a comprehensive video quality benchmark with multiple sub-metrics including quality and semantic consistency), **human preference judgments** via side-by-side comparisons on MovieGen prompts, and **wall-clock latency** (end-to-end generation time on H100/H200 GPUs). Attention score coverage (the fraction of total attention weight captured by the top-K cubes) is used as a diagnostic metric for understanding coarse-stage prediction quality.

- **Baselines.** The ablation experiments (Table 1a) compare VSA against five alternative attention mechanisms:
  1. **Spatial-Temporal** attention — alternates between spatial and temporal attention across layers (widely used in Open-Sora, OpenSora-Plan, LaVie, Latte).
  2. **Spatial-Full** attention — four spatial layers followed by one full-attention layer, repeated every five layers.
  3. **Compress KV** — pools only key and value tokens using 2×2×2 average pooling (8× attention FLOP reduction), with queries kept at full resolution.
  4. **Strided Window** attention — spatial layers attend within a temporal window (W_t = 2) and temporal layers attend within a spatial window (W_s = 8), inspired by Swin Transformer.
  5. **Full** attention — standard dense 3D attention (the upper-bound quality reference).
  
  For post-training evaluation (Section 3.3), baselines include the **original Wan2.1 model** (no finetuning), **full-attention finetuning** on the same synthetic data, and **SVG (Sparse VideoGen)** — a training-free attention sparsification method — at comparable sparsity levels (fp=0.03, fl=0.025, s=0.1, yielding 82.5% sparsity vs. VSA's 91.2% sparsity). For kernel benchmarks, **FlashAttention-3** and **FlexAttention** with identical 64×64 block-sparse masks serve as baselines.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute in **FLOPs** (floating-point operations) using the standard approximation: total FLOPs = 6ND + 4 · D · S · A · H · 3.5 · L, where N is parameter count, D is token count, S is sequence length, A is attention heads, H is head dimension, and L is transformer layers. This accounts for both the 6ND term from linear layers (following Hoffmann et al., 2022) and the attention-specific term from the FlashAttention formulation. For sparse attention variants, the attention FLOPs component is adjusted according to each method's sparsity pattern. The "generation budget" in post-training experiments is measured by **wall-clock latency** on H100/H200 GPUs for end-to-end video generation.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The ablation experiments train under a fixed compute budget of 4.5 × 10²⁰ FLOPs, which the paper establishes as compute-optimal for the 120M model size by demonstrating that a 120M full-attention model outperforms a 60M model trained with 4 × 10²⁰ FLOPs, with diminishing returns beyond 4.5 × 10²⁰. Hyperparameters (batch size, learning rate) are tuned via grid search on the full-attention baseline and held fixed for all ablation variants to ensure fair comparison. The scaling experiments (Figure 2b) fit parallel lines to the loss-vs-FLOPs curves to estimate whether VSA's advantage is consistent across scales. Human evaluations (Wan-14B) use 200 randomly sampled MovieGen prompts with side-by-side comparisons, though the paper does not report confidence intervals or inter-annotator agreement.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Ablation Results: VSA Outperforms Fixed-Pattern Methods and Full Attention (Table 1)

The ablation experiments in Table 1 establish the core empirical case for VSA at the 120M scale under a fixed 4.5 × 10²⁰ FLOPs budget.

**Fixed-pattern methods lose their advantage with extended training (Table 1a).** At the compute-optimal budget (4.5 × 10²⁰ FLOPs, "Loss – Opt" column), four fixed-pattern methods (Spatial-Temporal, Spatial-Full, Strided Window, Compress KV) outperform full attention (loss 0.13574–0.13271 vs. 0.13877). However, when training is extended to 4 × 10²¹ FLOPs ("Loss – Over" column), full attention drops to 0.12703 while the fixed-pattern methods stall at 0.13034–0.12716. This is a critical finding: the FLOP savings from fixed sparsity buy more training steps at moderate budgets, but the restricted receptive field eventually becomes a bottleneck that full attention overcomes with sufficient compute. VSA (Exp 6) achieves **0.13162** at the compute-optimal budget and **0.12687** at the extended budget — strictly better than full attention at both scales.

**Data-dependent patterns outperform fixed patterns (Table 1b).** Comparing data-dependent selection (the "F" fine-stage variant, Exp 8, loss 0.13296) against fixed local window attention (the "L" variant, Exp 7, loss 0.13330) shows a consistent advantage for learned sparsity. When the coarse stage's gated output is included ("C & F", Exp 10, loss 0.13162 vs. "C & L", Exp 9, loss 0.13220), the gap widens further. This demonstrates that the learned cube selection does not merely replicate local-window heuristics — it captures data-dependent long-range dependencies that fixed patterns miss.

**Global context via the coarse stage is necessary; explicit locality injection is not (Table 1b).** Three variants injecting explicit local attention (Exp 11: separate local stage, loss 0.13194; Exp 12: local stage with cube exclusion, loss 0.13124; Exp 13: forced local cube inclusion in fine stage, loss 0.13192) all perform within ±0.0003 of the simpler "C & F" baseline (Exp 10, 0.13162). The paper interprets this as evidence that the coarse stage already learns to attend to local tokens when they are relevant, making dedicated local modules redundant. Experiment 12's marginally better loss (0.13124) is noted but not adopted due to added complexity.

**Smaller tile sizes improve quality but hurt throughput (Table 1c-d).** Reducing tile size from B=256 (loss 0.13375, 478 TFLOPS) to B=64 (loss 0.13162, 408 TFLOPS) to B=16 (loss 0.13155, 181 TFLOPS) reveals a clear quality-efficiency tradeoff. The jump from B=64 to B=16 yields only a 0.00007 loss improvement while reducing throughput by 2.26× (181 vs. 408 TFLOPS). The paper selects B=64 as the Pareto-optimal configuration. Experiment 16 confirms that mismatched granularity between stages (coarse at 64-token cubes, fine at 256-token tiles) degrades performance (loss 0.13328 vs. 0.13162), demonstrating that both stages benefit from finer granularity.

**Mean pooling is the best compression strategy (Table 1e).** Among three pooling methods for the coarse stage, mean pooling achieves loss 0.13162, max pooling achieves 0.13929, and 3D convolution causes training instability (loss 0.27787). The convolution variant's failure likely stems from the additional learnable parameters creating optimization challenges when combined with the already-complex attention mechanism.

#### Scaling Results: VSA Maintains a Better Pareto Frontier Across Model Sizes (Figure 2)

The scaling experiments extend the ablation findings to larger models and higher compute budgets, addressing whether VSA's advantages are limited to the 120M scale or hold more generally.

**VSA matches full attention's loss curve with 2.53× less total FLOPs (Figure 2a).** A 410M-parameter DiT trained with VSA (K=32, 87.5% sparsity) on 16K-token sequences achieves a loss curve that nearly overlaps with full attention, despite the full-attention model requiring 2.53× more total FLOPs per training step. This is the central scaling result: at equivalent loss, VSA uses substantially less compute over the entire training trajectory, not just at convergence.

**The Pareto frontier advantage holds from 60M to 1.4B parameters (Figure 2b).** Across models ranging from 60M to 1.4B parameters trained with up to 4 × 10²¹ FLOPs, VSA consistently achieves lower loss at equivalent total FLOPs. The parallel fitted curves indicate that the 2.53× FLOP reduction is maintained across scale — i.e., the advantage does not diminish with model size. To the authors' knowledge, this is the first demonstration that trainable sparse attention produces a strictly better scaling curve than full attention for video DiTs, establishing VSA not as a quality-compromising speedup but as a compute-efficiency improvement that holds under rigorous scaling-law evaluation.

**The optimal sparsity level K depends on both sequence length and training budget (Figures 2c-d).** At a fixed budget of 4.5 × 10²⁰ FLOPs, K=32 performs well across sequence lengths 8,192 to 24,675 but underperforms K=16 at 61,440 tokens (Figure 2c). This contradicts the intuition that longer sequences always benefit from more attention. However, when the training budget is increased to 1 × 10²¹ FLOPs at the 61,440 sequence length, K=32 eventually outperforms K=16 (Figure 2c, "More FLOPS" marker). The paper hypothesizes that optimal-K increases with available compute, converging to full attention (K = L/B) with infinite resources, but leaves precise prediction as an open problem. This finding is methodologically important: it demonstrates that sparsity level should not be treated as a fixed hyperparameter but as a compute-dependent variable — analogous to how the Chinchilla scaling laws revealed that optimal model size depends on the training budget.

#### Sparse Adaptation Results: VSA Preserves Quality Post-Training While Reducing Latency (Figure 3, Table 3a)

The sparse adaptation experiments test whether VSA can be applied to pretrained full-attention models without quality loss — a critical practical scenario since retraining large DiTs from scratch is expensive.

**Wan-1.3B with VSA achieves comparable VBench scores to full-attention finetuning (Table 3a).** Finetuning Wan-1.3B with VSA at 91.2% sparsity (K=32 out of 364 cubes) on synthetic data from Wan-14B achieves VBench scores of 83.60% (quality), 79.47% (semantic), and 82.77% (total), comparable to full-attention finetuning (84.07%, 81.85%, 83.63%) and slightly above the original Wan-1.3B without finetuning (83.71%, 77.98%, 82.56%). The paper notes that the improvement over the original model may partially stem from training on synthetic data from a larger model rather than from VSA itself, but the fair comparison is against full-attention finetuning on the same synthetic data, where VSA matches within ~1 percentage point.

**Attribution time drops 6×, end-to-end latency drops 1.7×.** With VSA, Wan-1.3B's attention time drops by 6×, reducing end-to-end generation latency from 31 seconds (full attention with torch.compile) to 18 seconds on an H100. This makes VSA "the first video DiT where attention accounts for only 20% of runtime during both training and inference" — a shift from attention-dominated to compute-balanced execution.

**VSA outperforms the training-free SVG baseline at higher sparsity (Figure 3b, top).** In human preference judgments, VSA at 91.2% sparsity is preferred over SVG at 82.5% sparsity, even though VSA is more sparse. This demonstrates the advantage of training with sparsity (which allows the model to adapt its parameters) over post-hoc sparsification (which forces the model to operate with connections it never learned to live without).

**Wan-14B with VSA preserves quality at 720p resolution (Figure 3b, middle).** Human evaluation on 200 randomly sampled MovieGen prompts comparing VSA-finetuned Wan-14B (90% sparsity) against the official full-attention Wan-14B shows no significant preference difference, indicating VSA scales to larger models and higher resolutions. The end-to-end generation time drops from 1274 seconds to 576 seconds on an H200 — a 2.2× speedup.

**Sparse-Distill: VSA is compatible with step distillation (Figure 3b, bottom).** In a preliminary study, replacing the student model's full attention with VSA (80% sparsity) in DMD2 distillation while keeping all teacher models, distillation losses, and hyperparameters unchanged yields a 3-step generator with 50.9× acceleration (denoising time reduction) and no quality drop in human evaluation. To the authors' knowledge, this is the first demonstration that sparse attention is compatible with diffusion distillation — a non-trivial finding because prior sparsity methods that exploit diffusion timestep redundancy may fail in the extremely low-step regime where timestep-specific sparsity patterns are no longer valid.

#### Kernel Performance Results: Near-Theoretical Speedup with High GPU Utilization (Figure 4)

**VSA achieves over 6× speedup over FlashAttention-3 at long sequence lengths (Figure 4b).** For a fixed 87.5% sparsity, VSA's fine-stage block-sparse kernel reaches a nearly 7× speedup over FA3 at sequence length 131K, approaching the theoretical 8× maximum (since 87.5% sparsity means only 12.5% of attention is computed). The kernel maintains approximately 85% of FA3's MFU (Model FLOPs Utilization), meaning the overhead from sparse block iteration and index processing costs only ~15% of the theoretical peak throughput.

**FlexAttention with identical sparsity achieves only 2× speedup (Figure 4b).** PyTorch's general-purpose programmable attention API, when configured with the same 64×64 block-sparse mask that VSA uses, delivers only a 2× speedup over FA3 versus VSA's 6×+. This gap highlights the importance of purpose-built block-sparse kernels — general APIs cannot exploit the structure of known-size, contiguous blocks to the same degree.

**Coarse stage overhead is ~14% of total attention time (Figure 4a).** The runtime breakdown of a single Wan-1.3B transformer block shows that VSA's coarse stage (coarse attention + top-K selection + index conversion) accounts for a small fraction of total attention time, even with the fine stage at 87.5% sparsity. This justifies the paper's decision not to further optimize the coarse stage kernel — the engineering cost of fusing top-K selection into the attention kernel would be high, and the potential speedup (from the 14% fraction) is limited.

**End-to-end transformer block speedup is 2–3× (Figure 4a).** When integrated into full transformer blocks for Wan-1.3B and HunyuanVideo, VSA reduces the attention component from dominating the runtime to a minority contributor. The "Others" component (linear layers, normalization, feed-forward networks) becomes the new bottleneck — a sign that attention is no longer the primary scaling constraint.

#### Attention Pattern Analysis: Learned Sparsity is Highly Dynamic and Heterogeneous (Figure 5)

**VSA's selected cubes are data-dependent, not stereotyped (Figure 5a-f).** Visualization of the block-sparse attention masks from the finetuned Wan-1.3B model shows that different attention heads exhibit markedly different selection patterns: some approximate sliding-tile local attention, others display global attention (attending to cubes distributed across the entire video volume), and a third group shows mixed local-global behavior. This heterogeneity validates the paper's central claim that effective sparse attention must be data-dependent — a fixed pattern cannot simultaneously capture the needs of heads that require local vs. global context.

**Coarse-stage prediction accuracy is high and varies systematically (Figure 5e).** The fraction of total attention weight captured within the top-32 selected cubes (compared to the full attention matrix) averages above 60% across most layers and timesteps, reaching 90% in favorable configurations. A random selection baseline (the red plane) captures only 8%. This confirms that the coarse stage, despite operating on 64× compressed representations, accurately identifies the cubes containing high-weight tokens. Prediction accuracy increases monotonically with timestep (later denoising steps have more concentrated attention) and exhibits a zigzag pattern across transformer layers — findings that suggest opportunities for adaptive K values per layer and timestep.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Sparsity pattern type (fixed vs. data-dependent):** Table 1b (Exp 7 vs. 8, Exp 9 vs. 10) isolates the effect of pattern type by comparing VSA's data-dependent cube selection against a fixed local window of size (3, 3, 3), both with and without the gated coarse output. Data-dependent patterns consistently achieve lower loss, and the gap widens when coarse-stage output is included (0.13220 vs. 0.13162). This ablation is central to the paper's claim that trainable sparsity outperforms fixed heuristics.

**Coarse-stage output contribution:** Table 1b (Exp 8 vs. 10) demonstrates that including the coarse stage's output O_c in the final attention result provides a measurable improvement (loss 0.13296 → 0.13162) beyond using the coarse stage only for block selection. This differentiates VSA from MoBA, which uses pooled attention solely for gating and discards the pooled output.

**Locality prior injection (three variants):** Table 1b (Exp 11, 12, 13) systematically tests whether adding explicit local attention — either as a separate stage or by forcing local cube inclusion — improves over the simple "C & F" design. All three variants perform within noise of the baseline, leading to the paper's conclusion that the coarse stage learns to attend locally when needed without explicit guidance. This is a non-obvious negative result: the intuition from CNNs and vision transformers that locality should be explicitly encoded does not hold when the model can learn its own sparsity pattern.

**Tile size B (quality-efficiency tradeoff):** Table 1c (Exp 14-18) sweeps B from 256 to 16, measuring both loss and kernel throughput. The systematic degradation from B=256 (loss 0.13375) to B=64 (loss 0.13162) to B=16 (loss 0.13155) quantifies the quality cost of coarser sparsity, while the throughput measurements in Table 1d quantify the speed cost of finer sparsity. Experiment 16 specifically tests mismatched granularity (coarse at B=64, fine at B=256) and shows that coarsening either stage degrades performance, meaning both stages benefit from high resolution.

**Pooling method for coarse stage:** Table 1e compares mean pooling, max pooling, and 3D convolution with stride equal to kernel size. Mean pooling is the clear winner; convolution is unstable (loss jumping to 0.27787). The paper does not deeply analyze why convolution fails, but the likely explanation — introducing additional learnable parameters into an already complex gradient path — suggests that simplicity in the coarse stage is an important design principle.

**Optimal K across sequence lengths and budgets:** Figures 2c-d demonstrate that the best K value shifts with both sequence length and training FLOPs. At 61,440 tokens and 4.5 × 10²⁰ FLOPs, K=16 beats K=32; at 1 × 10²¹ FLOPs, the ordering reverses. This is treated as a diagnostic finding rather than a specific recommendation — the paper does not provide a formula for computing optimal-K, but flags it as an open scaling-law question.

**Sparsity annealing schedule (implicit ablation):** The sparse adaptation procedure (Section 2.3, Appendix C.5) uses a progressive K-decay schedule (reduce Top-K by 4 every 50 steps after 50-step full-attention warmup) rather than immediate sparsity. While the paper does not report an explicit ablation comparing progressive vs. immediate sparsity, the careful schedule design and the qualitative observation that "the model exhibits noticeable artifacts when switching from full attention to VSA" (Figure 6) imply that progressive annealing is important for stability. The initial zero-initialization of the coarse gate G_c and removal of the fine gate G_f (G_f = 1) are also design choices that deserve acknowledgment as implicit ablations — the paper chose these initializations to ensure the model starts in a dense-attention-equivalent state and gradually transitions to sparsity.

**Sparsity level for distillation:** The sparse distillation experiment uses 80% sparsity rather than the 91.2% used in the finetuning experiments. The paper does not ablate sparsity levels for distillation, so the sensitivity of distillation quality to sparsity level remains unexplored. Similarly, the 50.9× speedup figure combines sparsity (attention speedup) with distillation (step reduction), and the contribution of each factor is not decomposed.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: VSA cuts total training FLOPs by 2.53× with no drop in diffusion loss.

**Evidence:** Figure 2a shows overlapping loss curves for a 410M VSA model (87.5% sparsity) and a full-attention model, with the 2.53× FLOP reduction calculated from the attention overhead accounting described in Section 3.2. Figure 2b extends this to four model sizes, showing parallel fitted loss-vs-FLOPs curves that maintain the gap.

**Assessment: Supported, with caveats about extrapolation.** The evidence is strong for the tested regime: 60M–1.4B parameters, Wan2.1 architecture, Vchitect-T2V-Dataverse dataset, 16K sequence length, flow-matching loss. The "no drop in diffusion loss" claim is supported by near-identical loss curves. However, the paper explicitly acknowledges that "comprehensive scaling studies at longer sequence lengths" are left to future work. The 2.53× figure is specific to the attention fraction in the tested configuration — at longer sequences (where attention dominates even more), the reduction would be larger; at shorter sequences, smaller. The claim does not account for the cost of the coarse stage (which the paper shows is small but nonzero), and it implicitly assumes the same number of training steps — under a FLOP-matched comparison, VSA could process more tokens or train more steps, which might actually improve the loss gap beyond the "no drop" claim. The parallel-curve fit in Figure 2b is qualitative rather than a rigorous scaling law — the paper does not estimate the power-law exponent or provide confidence intervals for the gap at extreme scales.

#### Claim 2: VSA retrofitted into Wan2.1-1.3B speeds up attention by 6× and reduces end-to-end generation from 31s to 18s.

**Evidence:** Figure 4a shows attention time dropping 6×; the 31s → 18s latency numbers are reported in Sections 1 and 3.3.

**Assessment: Supported with qualifications about sparsity level.** The 6× attention speedup and 1.7× end-to-end speedup are concrete, measurable numbers. However, they are achieved at 91.2% sparsity (K=32 out of 364 cubes). The paper does not report latencies at lower sparsity levels where quality might be higher. The 18s figure includes the coarse-stage overhead, which the paper acknowledges is modest (14%) but still contributes. The speedup is measured on H100 GPUs with the custom ThunderKittens kernel — using a different GPU or kernel implementation would change the absolute numbers, though the relative speedup over FlashAttention should be broadly consistent at similar sparsity levels.

#### Claim 3: VSA is the first trainable sparse attention that shows better scaling than full attention on DiTs.

**Evidence:** Figure 2b shows VSA's Pareto frontier above full attention. The paper's literature review acknowledges DSV as pioneering work but claims VSA is the first to demonstrate superior scaling under rigorous evaluation.

**Assessment: Supported, but the claim's precision depends on definitions.** "Better scaling" is demonstrated as lower loss at equivalent FLOPs across the tested range. However, the paper's comparison to DSV is architectural rather than empirical — it does not reproduce DSV and compare against it directly. The claim that VSA is the "first" depends on interpreting DSV's contributions as not meeting the "better scaling than full attention" standard; the paper makes this argument in Appendix E by noting DSV's multi-stage training complexity and separate predictor training, but this is a qualitative distinction. A direct empirical comparison would strengthen the claim. Additionally, the tested range (up to 4 × 10²¹ FLOPs) represents a meaningful but incomplete scaling picture — the most expensive video DiT training runs (e.g., MovieGen, Sora-scale models) operate at several orders of magnitude higher FLOPs, so extrapolation is required.

#### Claim 4: Trainable sparse attention can match full attention quality only when the sparsity pattern is learned from data rather than applied post-hoc.

**Evidence:** Table 1a shows fixed-pattern methods losing their advantage at extended training budgets; Table 1b shows data-dependent patterns outperforming fixed patterns; Figure 3b top shows VSA preferred over SVG in human evaluation.

**Assessment: Strongly supported, with the SVG comparison being the most direct evidence.** The SVG comparison is the most compelling because it directly tests the train-test mismatch hypothesis: same base model, similar sparsity levels, but VSA adapts its parameters during finetuning while SVG applies sparsity as a post-hoc mask. The human preference result favoring VSA even at higher sparsity is a clean demonstration of the value of training with sparsity. The Table 1a results complement this by showing that the advantage of learned sparsity grows with training budget — post-hoc methods fail precisely because they cannot adapt to what the model would learn given more compute.

#### Claim 5: Learned sparsity patterns are heterogeneous and data-dependent, not stereotyped.

**Evidence:** Figure 5 visualizes attention patterns from the finetuned Wan-1.3B model, showing diverse selection strategies across heads and layers.

**Assessment: Supported as a qualitative insight, but limited in scope.** The visualizations are compelling and support the claim that fixed patterns cannot capture the full diversity of attention behavior. However, only a subset of heads/layers are shown, and the analysis is on a finetuned model rather than one trained from scratch with VSA. It would be informative to see whether from-scratch VSA models develop similar or different patterns. The paper also does not quantify the distribution of pattern types (what fraction of heads are local vs. global vs. mixed) or how this distribution evolves during training — such quantification would strengthen the claim beyond qualitative illustration.

#### Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:

- **Direct comparison with DSV:** As the most closely related prior work on trainable sparse attention for video DiTs, reproducing DSV (or at minimum, comparing against reported DSV numbers on the same dataset and architecture) would substantiate the claim that VSA is simpler while achieving comparable or better results.

- **Ablation of the gating mechanism:** The paper introduces learnable gates G_c and G_f but never ablates them against a fixed 0.5-0.5 sum or against using only one stage's output. This makes it difficult to assess whether the gating mechanism is actually learning useful layer-specific or feature-specific mixing ratios, or whether the improvement over a fixed combination would be marginal.

- **Longer sequence length scaling for from-scratch training:** The from-scratch pretraining experiments use 16K-token sequences, while the finetuning experiments use up to ~23K tokens for Wan-1.3B and ~77K for Wan-14B. The paper's own ablation (Figure 2c) shows that optimal sparsity varies with sequence length, so the 2.53× FLOP reduction at 16K may not hold at the longer sequences that video DiTs increasingly target. Training VSA from scratch at 30K–100K tokens would validate whether the scaling advantage persists.

- **Measuring the train-test mismatch directly:** The paper argues that post-hoc methods suffer from a train-test mismatch, but never quantifies this mismatch (e.g., by measuring attention pattern divergence between dense training and sparse inference, or by showing that specific tokens that were important during training become inaccessible at inference). A direct measurement would strengthen the conceptual argument.

- **Robustness to different video datasets:** All pretraining uses the Vchitect-T2V-Dataverse dataset. Demonstrating similar scaling behavior on other video datasets would address concerns about dataset-specific sparsity patterns.

- **Memory savings quantification:** The paper focuses on FLOPs and latency but does not report memory savings. Block-sparse attention can reduce peak memory usage by not materializing the full attention matrix, which would be practically significant for training at higher resolutions or with larger batch sizes.

#### Weaknesses that affect interpretation:

- **Small difficulty estimation cost is unaccounted for in the sparse adaptation regime.** The paper's sparse adaptation uses an annealing schedule that starts at full attention and gradually increases sparsity. The initial full-attention steps are expensive and not amortized in the reported speedup numbers. For a complete cost analysis, the total FLOPs of the adaptation process (including the initial dense steps) should be compared against training a dense model from scratch.

- **The 50.9× distillation speedup conflates two factors.** The Sparse-Distill result combines attention sparsity with step distillation, and the paper does not decompose how much of the speedup comes from each. A reader might mistakenly attribute the full 50.9× to VSA, whereas the majority likely comes from reducing denoising steps from 50 to 3.

- **No confidence intervals on human evaluations.** The human preference results (Figure 3b) are reported without confidence intervals, inter-annotator agreement metrics, or statistical significance tests. With 200 prompts, even a small preference margin might not be statistically reliable.

- **The parallel-curve fit in Figure 2b assumes a constant FLOP gap across scale.** This is an empirical observation, not a derived law. At very large model sizes where attention dominates an even greater fraction of total FLOPs, the gap might actually widen; at very small sizes where attention is negligible, it might shrink. The paper does not discuss these extrapolation considerations.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted for in Headline Efficiency Numbers

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire VSA framework rests on the coarse stage's ability to identify which cubes contain critical tokens. However, this identification itself costs compute — the coarse stage must compute a dense attention matrix over pooled cube representations, select top-K entries, and fuse these operations into a kernel that produces block indices. The paper explicitly quantifies this overhead in Section 2.4:

> "the memory overhead from materialization is negligible. FLOPS-wise, the coarse stage contributes less than 0.2% of total attention compute, and we show in §3.4 that its runtime accounts for only 14%, even when the fine stage is 87.5% sparse."

**The consequence.** The 0.2% FLOP figure and the 14% runtime fraction mean that the coarse stage imposes a floor on how much speedup VSA can achieve — the theoretical maximum speedup is bounded by `1 / (sparsity + coarse_overhead)` rather than `1 / sparsity`. At 87.5% sparsity, the theoretical speedup ceiling is `1 / (0.125 + 0.002) ≈ 7.9×` for FLOPs, but the actual ceiling is lower at shorter sequence lengths where the coarse stage represents a larger fraction of total work. The paper's kernel benchmarks (Figure 4b) show that VSA approaches ~7× speedup at 131K sequence length, but at 8,192 tokens — a common training resolution for smaller models — the coarse-stage overhead would consume a much larger fraction of the sparse attention's runtime, reducing the effective speedup. The paper does not report the coarse-to-fine runtime ratio as a function of sequence length, making it difficult for practitioners to predict speedup at their target resolution.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 (Appendix D) breaks down coarse-stage kernel runtime into QKT, softmax, top-K, and PV components, showing that top-K selection dominates. The profiling was done with `nsys` but at an unspecified sequence length, and the paper acknowledges the fusion of softmax and top-K provided "only modest improvements." Figure 4a shows the coarse stage as a visible fraction of attention time in the Wan-1.3B transformer block runtime breakdown, but this is at the specific sequence length of the Wan-1.3B latent (~23K tokens).

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly acknowledges that "coarse stage acceleration remains an important direction for future research" (Appendix D) but does not pursue it, arguing that the overhead "becomes negligible at longer sequence lengths — our primary target." This is a reasonable engineering decision but leaves a gap: the paper's headline training speedup numbers (2.53× total FLOP reduction) are measured at 16K sequence length for pretraining, where the coarse-stage FLOP fraction, while small, is not zero. The paper does not break down how much of the 2.53× would improve if the coarse stage were further optimized, nor does it provide a formula for estimating coarse-stage overhead as a function of sequence length so practitioners can compute expected speedups for their configurations.

---

### Optimal Sparsity Level Is Unknown A Priori and Depends Jointly on Compute Budget and Sequence Length

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's scaling experiments in Section 3.2 reveal that the optimal value of `K` (the number of cubes selected per query row, which determines the sparsity level) is not a fixed architectural constant — it shifts with both the total training FLOP budget and the sequence length. At sequence length 61,440 and a 4.5 × 10²⁰ FLOP budget, `K=16` outperforms `K=32`; when the budget is extended to 1 × 10²¹ FLOPs, the ordering reverses (Figure 2c). The paper does not provide a formula or heuristic for predicting optimal `K`, and explicitly states:

> "precisely predicting optimal K given budget, model size, and sequence length remains an open question. One promising direction is to explicitly model sparsity as an additional axis in the scaling law framework."

**The consequence.** A practitioner wanting to deploy VSA at a new scale — a different model size, sequence length, or training budget — has no principled way to choose `K` beyond running their own scaling sweep, which is expensive (the paper's scaling experiments required up to `4 × 10^21` FLOPs each). Choosing `K` too low leaves quality on the table (the model cannot attend to enough tokens to use the available compute effectively). Choosing `K` too high wastes FLOPs and increases inference latency without proportional quality gains. This is especially problematic because the paper shows that the penalty for choosing suboptimal `K` can be substantial: at 61,440 tokens and 4.5 × 10²⁰ FLOPs, `K=32` underperforms `K=16`, meaning the "default" `K=32` that works well at 16K–25K sequence lengths is not universally optimal.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 2c shows the loss-vs-K relationship at four sequence lengths (8,192 to 61,440) under a fixed 4.5 × 10²⁰ FLOP budget, plus one data point at 61,440 with a higher budget. Figure 2d shows similar patterns at other lengths. The evidence is systematic in demonstrating the contingency of optimal `K` but provides no predictive model — the paper diagnoses the problem without solving it.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this as an open problem (Section 3.2 and Section 5) and suggests extending scaling laws to include sparsity as a third axis alongside model size and total FLOPs as future work. In the short term, the paper defaults to `K=32` as a "consistently strong" value across the tested configurations, and this may serve as a reasonable heuristic for practitioners working at similar scales (60M–1.4B parameters, 8K–25K tokens, moderate training budgets). But for teams training at substantially different scales — e.g., 10B+ parameter models on 100K+ token sequences — the default may be far from optimal, and the cost of finding the right `K` through grid search could be prohibitive.

---

### Tile Size Tradeoff Is Hardware-Dependent and Not Fully Resolved

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's ablation in Table 1c-d establishes that smaller tile sizes `B` consistently improve model quality (lower diffusion loss) but reduce kernel throughput. The Pareto-optimal choice of `B=64` with `(4, 4, 4)` cubes is selected on the basis of experiments run on NVIDIA H200 GPUs (Section 3.1 and Appendix C.2). The paper does not test this tradeoff on other GPU architectures (A100, H100 for training, consumer GPUs for inference) or at other numerical precisions (FP16 vs. BF16 vs. FP8).

**The consequence.** GPU architectures differ substantially in their optimal tile sizes for matrix multiplication. NVIDIA Hopper (H100/H200) GPUs have tensor cores optimized for dimensions divisible by 16 and larger tiles than the previous Ampere (A100) generation, and the upcoming Blackwell architecture will shift optimal tile sizes again. A tile size of `B=64` that achieves 85% MFU on H200 may achieve significantly lower utilization on an A100 or a consumer-grade RTX 4090 — or worse, on inference-only hardware like edge devices. This means the quality-efficiency Pareto frontier the paper establishes is architecture-specific, and practitioners deploying on different hardware may find that `B=64` is no longer optimal — either because larger tiles are needed for acceptable throughput (sacrificing quality) or because smaller tiles become more viable (improving quality without throughput penalty).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1d reports TFLOPS for four tile size configurations (256×256 through 16×16) but does not specify which GPU these measurements were taken on beyond mentioning H200 in Appendix C.2. Figure 4b shows speedup curves on what is implied to be H100/H200 hardware. The paper provides no ablation of tile size across different GPU architectures or precisions. The ThunderKittens kernel implementation is specific to NVIDIA GPUs — there is no discussion of portability to AMD, Intel, or other accelerators.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The choice of `B=64` is presented as a general recommendation without qualification about hardware dependence. A more thorough analysis would sweep tile sizes on at least two GPU generations (e.g., A100 and H100) and report the quality-efficiency tradeoff curve for each, allowing practitioners to select the appropriate `B` for their deployment hardware. The paper's open-source code release may partially mitigate this by enabling practitioners to run their own tile-size benchmarks, but the pretraining cost required to determine quality at each tile size (each experiment in Table 1c required training a 120M model for 4.5 × 10²⁰ FLOPs) makes this expensive to replicate.

---

### Evaluation Limited to a Single Model Architecture, Dataset, and Task

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper — from the ablation studies through the scaling experiments to the sparse adaptation and distillation — use the Wan2.1 model architecture, the Vchitect-T2V-Dataverse dataset (for pretraining), synthetically generated videos from Wan-14B (for finetuning), and the flow-matching objective. The paper does not evaluate VSA on other video DiT architectures (HunyuanVideo, CogVideoX, MovieGen), other video datasets (WebVid, HowTo100M, Kinetics-derived datasets), or other generative modeling objectives (DDPM, score-based diffusion). Section 4 explicitly narrows the scope:

> "Our experiments are based on the Wan2.1 model architecture, a state-of-the-art open-source video DiT."

**The consequence.** The paper's core empirical claims — that VSA cuts training FLOPs by 2.53×, that learned sparsity outperforms fixed patterns, and that the scaling advantage holds across model sizes — are all demonstrated within a single architecture family and dataset. Video DiTs from different model families differ in ways that could affect sparsity behavior: attention head count and dimension, the presence of cross-attention vs. adaLN conditioning, video-text fusion mechanisms, and spatial-temporal patchification strategies. Similarly, different video datasets have different distributions of motion magnitudes, scene complexity, and temporal coherence — all of which influence which token relationships are critical. The paper's finding that optimal `K` varies with sequence length (Figure 2c) already suggests that sparsity behavior is sensitive to data characteristics, so the claim that VSA is "a key enabler for further scaling of video diffusion models" (abstract) is, strictly speaking, supported only for Wan2.1-style architectures trained on Vchitect-style data.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The kernel benchmarks in Figure 4a do include HunyuanVideo alongside Wan-1.3B, showing that VSA's block-sparse kernel can be applied to HunyuanVideo's attention operations and achieves speedup. However, this is a runtime evaluation only — there is no quality evaluation, no pretraining from scratch, and no sparse adaptation study for HunyuanVideo. The paper therefore demonstrates that the kernel *can run* on another architecture but not that VSA *works well* (in terms of generation quality) on that architecture.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim broader architectural generalization; it is transparent about using Wan2.1 throughout. The kernel-level HunyuanVideo benchmark provides weak evidence of partial portability. The authors likely view this as reasonable scope for a first paper establishing trainable sparse attention for video DiTs — demonstrating the concept thoroughly on one strong architecture before expanding. However, a practitioner using a non-Wan2.1 DiT cannot assume the 2.53× FLOP reduction will transfer without quality loss, and should plan for their own evaluation.

---

### Hard Problems (Long Sequences, Large Models, High Sparsity) May Require Substantial Hyperparameter Retuning

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's default configuration — `K=32`, `B=64` with `(4, 4, 4)` cubes, mean pooling, no explicit local stage — is established through ablations at the 120M scale on 16K-token sequences. These hyperparameters are then applied to the scaling experiments (up to 1.4B parameters, same 16K sequence length), the sparse adaptation (1.3B and 14B parameters, up to ~77K sequence length), and the sparse distillation. The paper assumes that the hyperparameter choices that work at 120M/16K remain near-optimal at 14B/77K.

**The consequence.** The paper's own findings undercut this assumption. Figure 2c shows that optimal `K` shifts with sequence length — and the shift direction is non-monotonic (at 61K tokens, `K=16` beats `K=32` at moderate budgets, then `K=32` overtakes `K=16` at higher budgets). The Wan-14B adaptation uses 77K-token sequences at 90% sparsity, but the `K` value corresponding to 90% sparsity is not reported, and there is no sweep over sparsity levels for the 14B model. If optimal `K` for Wan-14B differs from the value used, the reported quality preservation (Figure 3b, middle) might understate what VSA could achieve with tuned sparsity — or conversely, the reported latency might be achievable at higher quality with a better `K`. Similarly, the tile-size ablation was conducted only at 120M/16K — at 77K sequences, the quality-efficiency tradeoff between `B=64` and larger or smaller tiles may shift, but this is unexplored.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The Wan-14B sparse adaptation (Section 3.3) reports only final sparsity (90%) and latency numbers (1274s → 576s), with no ablation over `K` values or tile sizes at this scale. The sparse distillation uses 80% sparsity rather than the 91.2% used in Wan-1.3B finetuning — suggesting that optimal sparsity for distillation may differ from optimal sparsity for standard finetuning, but this difference is not systematically investigated or explained.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this as a limitation, though the acknowledgment that optimal-`K` prediction is an open problem (Section 5) implicitly recognizes the issue. Practitioners adapting VSA to significantly different scales should budget for hyperparameter sweeps over `K` and possibly `B`, rather than assuming the paper's defaults are transferable. The open-source code release partially mitigates this by enabling such sweeps, but the computational cost of pretraining-scale sweeps limits how thoroughly most practitioners can explore the space.

---

### Sparse Adaptation Requires Careful Annealing, Limiting Plug-and-Play Applicability

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 2.3 and Appendix C.5 describe the sparse adaptation procedure for converting a pretrained full-attention DiT to use VSA: initialize the coarse gate `G_c` to zero, remove the fine gate `G_f` (equivalent to `G_f = 1`), initialize sparsity at `K = L/B` (equivalent to full attention), warm up with full attention for 50 steps, then progressively reduce Top-K by 4 every 50 steps until reaching the target sparsity. The paper notes that "directly replacing full attention with VSA leads to unstable training" and that without the annealing schedule, "the model exhibits noticeable artifacts when switching from full attention to VSA" (Figure 6, Appendix A).

**The consequence.** Sparse adaptation is not a drop-in operation. It requires training — albeit finetuning rather than pretraining from scratch — with a carefully designed schedule that must be tuned based on the target sparsity level, model size, and possibly the data distribution. The paper used 4,000 steps for Wan-1.3B and Wan-14B finetuning, requiring 32 and 64 H200 GPUs respectively. A practitioner wanting to adapt a different pretrained DiT (e.g., a custom fine-tuned Wan2.1 variant, or a different architecture's checkpoint) would need to determine: (1) an appropriate number of warmup steps, (2) the Top-K decay rate, (3) the total number of adaptation steps, (4) the learning rate for the newly introduced gate parameters — all of which may interact with the model's pretraining quality and the target sparsity level. The paper's ablation experiments do not sweep these adaptation hyperparameters, so their sensitivity is unknown.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The qualitative examples in Figure 6 show artifacts at the start of sparse adaptation and their gradual disappearance. Table 3a shows that the 4,000-step schedule works for Wan-1.3B. The ReST<sup>EM</sup>-trained revision model failure in Appendix K of the related work discussion is not directly about VSA but highlights a broader pattern: training procedures that work in one setting can break when applied to a differently-initialized model with sparse attention, suggesting that adaptation hyperparameters may be brittle.

**Mitigation status.** The paper provides the specific schedule used (50-step warmup, reduce Top-K by 4 every 50 steps) so practitioners can replicate it. However, the paper does not ablate alternative schedules (faster decay, no warmup, different gate initializations) or provide guidance for how to adapt the schedule to different model sizes or sparsity targets. The sparsity schedule is described only in Appendix C.5, not in the main text, which may cause practitioners to underestimate its importance for successful adaptation. The code release provides the exact implementation, which is helpful but does not substitute for understanding the sensitivity of the adaptation process to its hyperparameters.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper represents a **paradigm shift** in how the video generation community should think about attention sparsity — moving it from a post-hoc inference optimization to a first-class architectural primitive that participates in training. The magnitude of this shift is comparable to how depthwise separable convolutions went from being viewed as approximations of standard convolutions to being recognized as the superior design choice for efficiency-oriented architectures. The paper's central empirical finding — that VSA produces a strictly better scaling curve than full attention across model sizes from 60M to 1.4B parameters (Figure 2b) — changes the default assumption. Before this work, the field's implicit consensus was that sparse attention inevitably degrades quality relative to full attention, and the engineering challenge was minimizing that degradation. After VSA, the burden of proof shifts: the question becomes **under what conditions does learned sparsity provide a net improvement rather than a net cost**, and the answer appears to be "under most conditions relevant to video DiT training."

The paper resolves a contradiction in the literature that has been quietly blocking progress. On one side, a series of papers (STA, Sparge Attention, SVG) demonstrated that post-hoc sparsity could accelerate inference with acceptable quality loss — suggesting attention sparsity was viable. On the other side, the best video DiTs (Wan2.1, HunyuanVideo, MovieGen, CogVideoX) continued to use full 3D attention despite its prohibitive cost — suggesting that the quality ceiling imposed by sparsity was too high for state-of-the-art models. The paper's Table 1a reconciles this: fixed-pattern methods outperform full attention at moderate training budgets (because saved FLOPs buy more training steps) but lose their advantage with extended training (because the restricted receptive field eventually becomes a bottleneck). VSA breaks through this ceiling by making the sparsity pattern learnable — it adapts to what each layer and head needs, continuing to improve with additional compute rather than saturating. This means the community's hesitation about sparse attention was justified for post-hoc methods, but does not apply to trainable sparsity.

The paper also reframes the research agenda around attention efficiency. Before VSA, the dominant approach to reducing attention cost in video DiTs was to substitute attention with cheaper alternatives — spatial-temporal factorization, compressed KV, or strided windows — each imposing a fixed inductive bias on what token relationships the model can capture. VSA demonstrates that **the right approach is not to restrict attention but to make it selective**: keep the full expressiveness of token-level attention, but apply it only where it matters, using a learned router to decide where that is. This reframes the research question from "what fixed pattern approximates full attention best?" to "how do we train models to identify critical token interactions efficiently?" — a question that opens up connections to the broader literature on learned routing (mixture-of-experts, dynamic sparsity in LLMs) and differentiable top-K operations.

A subtler shift concerns the role of **hardware-algorithm co-design**. The paper's meticulous attention to tile size as a joint quality-efficiency parameter (Table 1c-d, the `B=64` selection, the ThunderKittens kernel achieving 85% MFU of FA3) establishes that sparse attention for video DiTs cannot be designed purely as an algorithm and then mapped to hardware as an afterthought. The cube size `(4,4,4)`, the block-sparse compute layout, the fused softmax-topK-index kernel, and the decision not to further optimize the coarse stage are all driven by hardware considerations as much as by model quality. This co-design mindset — where the algorithm's grouping of tokens (cubes) maps 1-to-1 with the hardware's grouping of computation (threadblocks) — will likely become a template for future work on efficient attention mechanisms across modalities.

The shift in research prioritization is concrete. Before VSA, a team wanting to scale video DiTs had two options: accept the quadratic cost of full attention (expensive but safe) or adopt fixed-pattern sparsity (cheaper but with a known quality ceiling). This binary choice meant most teams chose full attention, making video DiT scaling an exercise in brute-force compute allocation. After VSA, the menu expands to include **trainable sparsity as a third option** — one that provides most of the cost savings of fixed-pattern methods with most of the quality of full attention, and in fact slightly better quality at equivalent FLOPs. For the field's largest video generation efforts, where compute budgets already stretch institutional resources, this third option is not merely convenient — it may be the difference between feasible and infeasible scaling to the next generation of resolution, duration, and model capacity.

The paper also introduces a **new diagnostic**: attention sparsity level `K` as a compute-dependent variable that should be jointly optimized with model size and training FLOPs (Section 3.2, Figures 2c-d). This parallels the Chinchilla scaling laws' reframing of data quantity from a fixed choice to a compute-dependent optimum, and suggests that future scaling-law work for video DiTs should include sparsity as a third axis alongside parameters and tokens. This is not a solved problem — the paper explicitly leaves optimal-`K` prediction as an open question — but establishing that it *is* a problem worth solving is itself a contribution that will shape subsequent scaling-law research.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Extending scaling laws to include sparsity level as a third axis.** The paper's Figure 2c-d demonstrates that the optimal `K` value shifts with both sequence length and total training FLOPs, but provides no predictive model — only the empirical observation that `K=32` works well in the tested regimes. A natural follow-up would systematically sweep `K` (and equivalently, sparsity level) across a wider range of model sizes, sequence lengths, and compute budgets, then fit a power-law relationship of the form `L_opt(N, D, S) = f(K, N, D)` where `N` is parameters, `D` is tokens, and `S` is sparsity. The specific experiment would pretrain models at 300M, 600M, and 1B parameters across sequence lengths from 8K to 64K, at sparsity levels from 75% to 95%, with budgets from 10^20 to 10^22 FLOPs, measuring whether the optimal sparsity follows a predictable functional form (e.g., decreasing with model size as larger models benefit from more attention, or increasing with compute budget as the paper hypothesizes). The Chinchilla scaling laws required a similar systematic sweep to establish the optimal parameter-to-data ratio; a "Chinchilla for sparse attention" would be the direct analog. This paper makes such a study newly tractable because it provides the first evidence that sparsity scaling laws exist — before VSA, there was no reason to believe sparse attention could be competitive enough with full attention to warrant scaling-law analysis.

**Direct comparison with DSV on a shared architecture and dataset.** The paper positions VSA against DSV in Appendix E but provides no empirical comparison. A strong follow-up would implement both methods within the same Wan2.1 120M or 410M architecture, train them on the same Vchitect-T2V-Dataverse dataset under identical FLOP budgets, and compare not only final loss but also wall-clock training time, implementation complexity (lines of code, number of training stages), and downstream generation quality. The key measurement would be: does DSV's more complex multi-stage predictor training yield better quality at equivalent sparsity, or does VSA's simpler end-to-end approach achieve parity? This comparison would validate (or challenge) VSA's claim to being "simpler while achieving comparable or better results." The experiment also serves as a stress test: if DSV outperforms VSA at high sparsity (>95%), it would indicate that VSA's reliance on mean-pooled cube representations has a resolution limit that dedicated low-rank predictors can surpass, refining our understanding of when hierarchical pooling suffices versus when learned attention predictors are necessary.

**Combining VSA with learned token pruning or merging for compound efficiency.** VSA reduces attention FLOPs by restricting which token pairs interact, but it still processes all `L` tokens in the fine stage — each token participates as a query, and each selected token participates as a key and value. A complementary line of work prunes or merges uninformative tokens entirely (e.g., removing background tokens in video frames, or merging tokens within homogeneous regions). A natural combination would use VSA's coarse stage not only to select which cubes to attend to, but also to predict which query tokens can be dropped or merged before the fine stage — extending the "critical token" concept from "which tokens should be attended to" to "which tokens are worth computing attention for at all." The experiment would add a token-pruning module that uses the coarse stage's cube-level attention weights as a signal: cubes that receive low attention weight from all query cubes could be pruned entirely, reducing `L` before the fine stage and yielding multiplicative FLOP savings (fewer tokens × sparser attention). The key measurement would be whether this compound savings exceeds the sum of each technique individually. This paper enables the experiment by providing a reliable critical-token predictor (the coarse stage) that can serve as the pruning signal — prior work on token pruning in video DiTs lacked such a lightweight, learned salience estimator.

**Stress-testing VSA at the limits of sparsity (>95%) and sequence length (>100K tokens).** The paper's main experiments operate at 87.5% sparsity (pretraining) and 90–91.2% sparsity (finetuning). The coarse stage's attention score coverage analysis (Figure 5e) shows that at `K=32` out of 364 cubes (~91.2% sparsity), the top-K selection captures ≥60% of total attention weight — meaning 40% of the attention mass is lost to sparsity and must be compensated for by the coarse stage's gated output. A natural stress test would push sparsity to 95%, 97.5%, and 99% — where the fine stage attends to only a handful of cubes — and measure at what point quality collapses. This would reveal whether VSA's hierarchical design has a fundamental sparsity ceiling (where even the gated coarse output cannot compensate for the missing fine-stage information) or whether quality degrades gracefully. The experiment should also push sequence length to 100K–200K tokens (roughly 10–20 seconds of 720p video) to test whether the coarse stage's `(4,4,4)` pooling remains sufficiently expressive at extreme compression ratios, or whether the cube size should scale with sequence length (e.g., `(8,8,8)` cubes for 200K tokens). This paper makes such stress-testing tractable because it provides the kernel infrastructure (ThunderKittens block-sparse kernel with 85% MFU) that can actually run these extreme configurations with wall-clock speedup — before VSA, even thinking about 200K-token DiT training was academic because full attention would be computationally infeasible.

**Learned sparsity for multi-modal DiTs with heterogeneous attention patterns.** The paper evaluates VSA on video-only self-attention, but state-of-the-art DiTs (Wan2.1, MovieGen, HunyuanVideo) also include cross-attention between video tokens and text embeddings, and some architectures (e.g., MovieGen) fuse video and audio tokens in a unified sequence. Cross-attention has fundamentally different sparsity patterns than self-attention: a video token may need to attend to only a small subset of text tokens (the words describing that region or moment), and audio tokens may attend primarily to temporally aligned video tokens. A natural extension would apply VSA to cross-attention layers by partitioning the text/audio sequence into cubes (or variable-length segments) and having the coarse stage learn which text/audio segments each video region needs. The experiment would measure whether the learned sparsity patterns in cross-attention differ qualitatively from those in self-attention (likely more concentrated, event-aligned), and whether the optimal sparsity level `K` differs between attention types. This paper enables the experiment by providing the hierarchical attention template (coarse pooled prediction + fine sparse attention + gated combination) that can be adapted to the cross-attention setting by changing the pooling strategy (text tokens may need different granularity than video tokens).

**Investigating whether learned sparsity provides implicit regularization against overfitting.** The paper's Figure 2a shows VSA nearly matching full attention's loss curve, and Figure 2b shows VSA achieving a better loss-vs-FLOPs Pareto frontier. But an underexplored question is whether VSA achieves better *generation quality* at equivalent loss, because the sparsity pattern may act as a regularizer — preventing the model from fitting spurious long-range correlations in the training data that do not generalize. The experiment would train VSA and full-attention models to equivalent validation loss (by giving full attention a larger FLOP budget), then evaluate generation quality through FVD, IS, or human preference studies. The hypothesis is that at matched loss, VSA's generated videos show fewer artifacts or better temporal coherence because the attention sparsity prevents the model from "cheating" by attending to irrelevant tokens. This paper makes the experiment possible by providing VSA as a training method that achieves near-identical loss — before VSA, sparse models had higher loss, making it impossible to isolate the regularization effect from the loss gap.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**High-resolution video generation for interactive creative tools.** A video editing platform (e.g., Runway, Pika, or an open-source equivalent) wants to offer real-time or near-real-time generation of 720p–1080p video clips based on text prompts or image-conditioned inputs. With full attention, a Wan2.1-1.3B model requires 31 seconds per video on an H100 — far too slow for interactive use where users expect feedback within seconds. By deploying VSA (reducing latency to 18 seconds, a 1.7× improvement), and further combining it with the Sparse-Distill approach (50.9× total acceleration for the 3-step generator), these tools can approach the ~5-second generation time reported in the paper for 5-second videos on a single H200. The specific benefit from the paper is not just the latency reduction but the **quality preservation**: Table 3a shows VSA finetuning matches full-attention VBench scores, and Figure 3b shows human evaluators cannot distinguish VSA-generated videos from full-attention baselines at 14B scale. This means the creative tool can offer faster generation without users noticing any quality degradation — a commercially critical property that post-hoc sparsity methods (which produce visible artifacts at high sparsity) cannot provide.

**Cost-efficient batch video generation for synthetic data pipelines.** A self-driving or robotics company generates millions of synthetic video clips from simulation for training perception models, using video DiTs to add visual variety to rendered scenes. The cost is dominated by GPU-hours for inference: renting H100 instances at scale. The paper's 6× attention speedup and 1.7× end-to-end speedup for Wan-1.3B, and the 2.2× speedup for Wan-14B (1274s → 576s), translate directly to a 40–55% reduction in cloud GPU costs per video. For a pipeline generating 10 million videos, this represents thousands of GPU-hours saved — enough to fund additional training runs or expand to higher resolutions. The specific benefit is that VSA finetuning can be applied to the company's fine-tuned DiT checkpoint using the sparse adaptation procedure (Section 2.3) without retraining from scratch: Appendix C.5 reports 4,000 steps on 32 H200 GPUs for Wan-1.3B, which is a small fraction of the original pretraining cost. This makes VSA adoption feasible even for teams with custom model weights, not just those using off-the-shelf checkpoints.

**Training larger video DiTs under fixed hardware budgets for academic and open-source teams.** A university lab or open-source collective wants to train a video DiT from scratch but is limited to, say, 64 H200 GPUs for a fixed duration. With full attention, the maximum model size and sequence length they can train are constrained by the quadratic attention cost — beyond a certain scale, attention dominates and further scaling becomes infeasible. The paper's 2.53× reduction in total training FLOPs means that under the same hardware budget, the lab can either train a model 2.53× larger (in FLOP-terms), train at 2.53× higher resolution or sequence length, or complete training 2.53× faster (freeing GPUs for other experiments). The specific evidence is Figure 2b, which shows that this FLOP reduction translates to a consistent loss improvement across the 60M–1.4B range — meaning the lab does not need to worry about whether VSA's advantages are limited to small scales. This application directly addresses the paper's stated goal of democratizing video generation: by lowering the compute barrier to competitive-quality models, VSA enables more teams to participate in video DiT research and development.

**On-device or edge deployment of distilled video generation models.** A mobile application wants to run a distilled few-step video generator directly on a device with a consumer GPU (e.g., an RTX 4090 in a workstation, or future mobile accelerators). The Sparse-Distill result (Figure 3b, bottom) demonstrates that VSA is compatible with distillation — the student model can be both sparse and few-step, achieving 50.9× total speedup over the teacher's denoising time. This compound acceleration is what makes on-device deployment plausible: the 3-step distilled generator with VSA can produce a 5-second video in approximately 5 seconds on an H200, and while a consumer GPU would be slower, the order-of-magnitude reduction from 50+ steps to 3 steps, combined with the 6× attention speedup from sparsity, brings generation times into a range where local execution becomes conceivable. The paper's contribution is demonstrating that these two acceleration techniques are **compatible** — prior sparsity methods that exploit diffusion timestep redundancy (e.g., different sparsity at early vs. late denoising steps) would break in the few-step regime where "early" and "late" steps collapse, but VSA's sparsity is timestep-agnostic (learned from the data distribution, not the denoising schedule), making it resilient to distillation.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not articulate a detailed tradeoff matrix against named alternatives, but its experimental design implies clear decision boundaries based on three factors: **training budget** (whether the model will be trained to near-convergence), **deployment stage** (training-time vs. inference-only speedup needed), and **sparsity target** (how much acceleration is required). Based on the paper's evidence:

- **Prefer VSA (trainable sparse attention) when:** the model will be trained from scratch or fine-tuned for non-trivial additional compute (Figure 2a shows VSA benefits grow with training budget); you need both training and inference cost reduction, not just inference (Section 1 explicitly targets both); you are targeting sparsity levels above ~87% where post-hoc methods show visible quality degradation (Figure 3b top shows VSA preferred over SVG at 91.2% sparsity); and you can afford the one-time cost of sparse adaptation finetuning for pretrained checkpoints (4,000 steps on 32–64 GPUs per Appendix C.5).

- **Prefer fixed-pattern methods (spatial-temporal, strided window) when:** the training budget is strictly limited to the compute-optimal regime where these methods outperform full attention (Table 1a, "Loss – Opt" column shows them competitive at 4.5 × 10²⁰ FLOPs); implementation simplicity is paramount (these methods require only mask manipulation, not custom kernels); and the model will not be trained beyond the crossover point where full attention pulls ahead (Table 1a, "Loss – Over" column). However, the paper's evidence suggests this window is narrow — VSA outperforms these methods at both budgets (Exp 6 vs. Exp 1-4 in both columns of Table 1a), making them primarily relevant as baselines rather than competitive alternatives once the cost of implementing VSA is amortized.

- **Prefer post-hoc sparsity (SVG, STA) when:** you have a frozen pretrained checkpoint that cannot be finetuned (e.g., a model with unavailable training data or prohibitive adaptation cost on the largest scales beyond what the paper tested); inference-only speedup is sufficient and training cost is already sunk; and the target sparsity is modest (<80%) where the paper acknowledges post-hoc methods' train-test mismatch is less severe. The SVG comparison (Figure 3b top) shows VSA clearly preferred at 91.2% sparsity, but the paper does not test VSA vs. SVG at, say, 50–70% sparsity where the quality gap may be smaller and the ease of post-hoc deployment (no finetuning) dominates.

An important boundary condition the paper does not fully resolve: at sparsity levels below ~75% (where most attention is still computed), the overhead of VSA's coarse stage (14% of attention time) may outweigh the benefits of learned sparsity, making simpler methods or even full attention preferable. The paper's experiments all operate at ≥87.5% sparsity, so the low-sparsity regime is unexplored — practitioners targeting modest speedups should run their own comparisons rather than assuming VSA's advantages extend below the tested sparsity range.
