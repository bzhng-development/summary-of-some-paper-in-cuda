# Wan-Move: Motion-controllable Video Generation via Latent Trajectory Guidance

**ArXiv:** [2512.08765](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08765)

## 🎯 Pitch

You don't need extra motion encoders to achieve commercial-grade motion control in video generation. Injecting point trajectories directly into a standard image-to-video model's condition features via latent-space feature propagation matches systems like Kling 1.5 Pro's Motion Brush—without changing the architecture. This simple trick eliminates the usual trade-off between control precision and training scalability.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Wan-Move introduces a motion-controllable video generation framework that injects trajectory-guided motion signals directly into the condition features of an off-the-shelf image-to-video model (Wan-I2V-14B), eliminating the need for auxiliary motion encoders or architectural modifications. The core mechanism is **latent trajectory guidance** — representing object motions as point trajectories, projecting them into latent space, and replicating the first frame's latent features along each trajectory (rather than encoding tracks via separate ControlNet modules or pixel-space embeddings), which simultaneously conveys appearance and motion instructions to the diffusion backbone. Scaled fine-tuning on 2 million high-quality videos yields 5-second, 480p outputs whose motion controllability rivals commercial systems such as Kling 1.5 Pro's Motion Brush, as measured by human preference studies, while achieving the best motion accuracy (end-point error of 2.6 on MoveBench) and video quality among academic methods — a gap that widens on large-motion and out-of-distribution scenarios, establishing that precise, scalable motion control is possible without architectural overhead **only when** motion guidance is expressed through the same latent features the base model already consumes.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Architectural Overhead Prevents Precise, Scalable Motion Control

The fundamental question this paper tackles is: **how do we inject fine-grained motion guidance into a pretrained image-to-video (I2V) diffusion model without adding so much architectural complexity that the model becomes difficult to train at scale?** This matters because motion control is what transforms video generation from a "generate whatever" capability into a tool that creators and practitioners can actually direct — specifying where objects should go, how they should move, and how the camera should behave across frames.

The gap the paper identifies is not that motion-controllable video generation doesn't exist — it does, and the paper surveys a substantial body of prior work (Section 2). Rather, the gap is that **existing methods consistently sacrifice either control granularity or training scalability, and no current approach delivers both simultaneously.** The paper's core claim is that this tradeoff is unnecessary: by representing motion guidance as edits to the latent features the base I2V model already consumes, one can achieve precise control without adding a single new module, enabling straightforward fine-tuning of large-scale backbones.

### Why This Problem Matters

Motion-controllable I2V generation sits at the intersection of several practical and scientific demands. The paper does not itemize these explicitly as a list of motivations, but they are implicit throughout the introduction and related work:

**Creative tools and production pipelines.** The ability to specify where objects go in a generated video — drag a point from position A to position B, rotate an object in 3D, transfer motion from one clip to another — is precisely what video editors, animators, and content creators need to integrate generative models into their workflows. Without it, video generation is a lottery; with it, generation becomes directable. The paper's application gallery (Figure 1) showcases six qualitatively distinct control modalities (single-object, multi-object, camera + object, complex, primitive-level, 3D rotation), signaling that the goal is a general-purpose motion-control substrate, not a narrow task-specific solution.

**Scalability as a first-class concern.** The paper repeatedly emphasizes scalability — "scaled training," "easy fine-tuning," "fast convergence" — because the field has reached a point where the quality of generated video is strongly coupled to model scale and data volume. The current generation of foundation video models (Wan [19], CogVideoX [52], HunyuanVideo [6]) produces high-fidelity output, but only when trained on massive datasets with large transformer backbones. Any motion-control method that complicates the architecture — adding ControlNet branches, auxiliary encoders, or separate fusion modules — introduces an impedance mismatch: the control mechanism must now be retrained or carefully aligned whenever the base model is updated, and the additional parameters compete for GPU memory and optimization budget during large-scale training. A method that requires no architectural changes, by contrast, inherits the base model's scaling properties for free. The paper positions Wan-Move as exactly this: a method that "can plug straight into the I2V backbone" (Section 1).

**Benchmarking gap.** A less obvious but equally important motivation is the absence of rigorous, standardized evaluation for motion control. Section 4 is devoted entirely to MoveBench, and the paper explicitly calls out the deficiencies of existing benchmarks (Figure 5): DAVIS [21] provides only 50 videos with short clips (35–100 frames) and no motion annotations beyond segmentation masks; VIPSeg [22] offers more videos (343) but even shorter durations (24 frames); MagicBench [20] expands to 600 videos but relies on noisy automatic labels and categorizes only by object count. None of these benchmarks support evaluation of long-range dynamics (5 seconds, 81 frames), multi-object interactions, or diverse content categories **with verified ground-truth motion tracks.** MoveBench is thus positioned not as an afterthought but as a necessary piece of infrastructure that enables the paper's empirical claims — without it, comparing methods on motion accuracy would remain ad hoc and unreliable.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper organizes prior work along two axes: **(i) how motion guidance is represented** and **(ii) how it is integrated into the generative model** (Section 1, paragraph 2). Existing methods fail on one or both axes.

#### Axis 1: Motion Guidance Representation — Sparse vs. Dense

**Sparse representations** (bounding boxes [7, 8], segmentation masks [1, 9, 10]) provide coarse control: they can specify *where* an object should be and roughly *which direction* it moves, but they cannot express fine-grained local deformations, rotation, or non-rigid motion. A bounding box tells the model "the bird moves from left to right," but not how the bird's wings flap, how its head bobs, or how individual feathers ripple. For applications like character animation, object manipulation, or expressive camera work, this is insufficient.

**Dense representations** solve the granularity problem but introduce different failure modes:

- **Optical flow** [2, 11, 12, 3] specifies pixel-wise displacement between frames, offering very fine control. However, it requires an external flow estimation model at inference time (e.g., RAFT [83]), which introduces **cumulative error across frames** — flow predictions degrade with occlusion, large displacements, and out-of-distribution scenes, and these errors compound when the generated video is expected to reproduce them faithfully. Moreover, requiring a separate flow model during inference complicates the deployment pipeline and adds latency. The paper explicitly flags this as a scalability barrier (Section 1: "requires an additional model for flow estimation during inference, which adds cumulative errors across frames and hampers scalability").

- **Point trajectories** [13, 14] avoid the cumulative error problem because they are sparse by design: a user or tracker specifies a set of discrete 2D tracks (e.g., "this pixel moves here, then here, then here"), and the model is free to interpolate the rest of the video. This is easier to specify at inference time (drag a point, no need for dense flow estimation). However, prior point-trajectory methods face a **context problem**: each track is a single-pixel thread. A pixel contains very little semantic or textural information — it's just a color value at one location. When you tell the model "this pixel moves along this path," it has no information about the *neighborhood* around that pixel, making it hard to align textures, maintain object boundaries, or propagate consistent motion patterns across adjacent regions. The paper describes this limitation precisely (Section 1): "each track is only a single-pixel thread and lacks surrounding spatial context. This makes it hard to align textures and motion patterns across neighboring regions."

#### Axis 2: Motion Signal Integration — The Encoder Bottleneck

Regardless of how motion is represented, every prior fine-tuning method introduces **additional motion-processing modules** to inject that signal into the base model. The paper catalogs several approaches (Section 2, paragraph on motion-controllable video generation):

- **ControlNet [18] branches**: A separate copy of (part of) the base model processes the motion condition, and its outputs are injected into the main model via zero-initialized convolution layers. This is the approach used by Motion Prompting [13], LeviTor [16], MagicMotion [20], and others. The problem: ControlNet adds a substantial number of parameters — roughly a copy of the encoder portion of the base model — which must be trained from scratch while the base model is fine-tuned. At the scale of a 14B-parameter DiT backbone (as used in Wan-I2V-14B), this means adding billions of trainable parameters, increasing memory pressure during training, and requiring careful balancing of ControlNet output scales to avoid overwriting the base model's learned representations.

- **Auxiliary motion encoders with cross-attention or adaLN injection**: Tora [14] uses a lightweight adaptive layer norm (adaLN) module to inject trajectory embeddings. DragAnything [1] trains an "ObjectNet" to bridge the domain gap between DINOv2 features and the UNet's feature space — but DINOv2 operates at a 14×14 patch size, which limits control granularity, and the model still requires additional identity embeddings to distinguish objects. ImageConductor [58] injects pixel-level trajectory features directly, but as the paper notes, this fails because single-pixel features lack semantic context (Table 1: ImageConductor achieves the highest EPE of 15.6 on MoveBench, indicating very poor motion control).

The common thread across all these approaches is **signal degradation during encoding.** Motion information — whether represented as pixel tracks, flow fields, or mask sequences — must be transformed through learned parameters before it reaches the diffusion backbone. Any error, information loss, or misalignment in this transformation directly limits control quality. The paper's key insight is that this encoding step may be entirely unnecessary if motion guidance can be expressed directly in the representation space that the base model already operates in.

#### The Most Direct Predecessor: Motion Prompting [13]

Motion Prompting [13] is the work most similar to Wan-Move, as both use point trajectories for motion representation. The paper explicitly distinguishes itself along two dimensions (Section 2):

1. **How tracks are embedded**: Motion Prompting encodes point tracks via random embeddings in pixel space — each trajectory position is assigned a learned or random feature vector, which is then processed by a ControlNet. This inherits the single-pixel context problem: the embeddings carry no information about the appearance or texture of the region being controlled. Wan-Move instead propagates the **actual latent features of the first frame** along the trajectory, so each tracked position receives the full, rich feature representation (texture, color, semantic information) from the corresponding starting point.

2. **How tracks are integrated**: Motion Prompting uses a ControlNet branch, which adds architectural overhead and requires training an entirely separate module. Wan-Move achieves motion injection by simply editing the condition feature (`zimage`) that the base model already uses — replacing zero-padded features at future-frame positions with features copied from the first frame along the trajectory path. This requires zero additional parameters.

The paper provides both quantitative and qualitative evidence for the superiority of latent feature replication over pixel-space track embeddings in Section 5.3 (Table 4, Figure 8), which we will examine in detail later.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning is built around a single architectural decision — "no extra modules" — and the consequences that flow from it. This is not presented as a minor implementation trick but as a **design philosophy** with cascading benefits:

**Simplicity enables scalability.** By avoiding motion encoders, the training objective reduces to fine-tuning the existing DiT backbone with a modified condition feature. The model inherits the base architecture's parallelism strategies (Ulysses sequence parallelism, FSDP), optimizer settings, and convergence properties. The paper reports training on 2 million videos using 64 A100 GPUs for 30,000 steps (Appendix 8.3) — a substantial but not extraordinary compute budget for a 14B-parameter model. If Wan-Move required a ControlNet branch, the parameter count and memory footprint would be significantly larger, potentially requiring more GPUs or a smaller batch size, which would slow convergence.

**Feature propagation preserves context.** The core operation — copying first-frame latent features along a trajectory in latent space — is not just parameter-free; it is **deterministic and information-preserving.** Unlike a learned encoder that could lose information during transformation, feature replication guarantees that the motion guidance carries exactly the same appearance information present at the trajectory's starting point. This is why the paper can claim "precise motion control" (title, abstract): the guidance signal is as rich as the first frame's VAE encoding, which captures texture, edges, color, and semantic structure at the VAE's native resolution.

**The VAE's translation equivariance assumption.** A critical — and unstated — assumption underlies this entire approach: that the VAE's latent features are approximately translation-equivariant, meaning a feature at spatial location (x, y) in the latent grid represents roughly the same visual content as a feature at (x+Δx, y+Δy) in a different frame. This is what justifies Eq. (3): copying `zimage[0, p0]` to `zimage[n, pn]` assumes that the feature vector from the first frame is the "correct" feature to place at the trajectory's position in frame n. If the VAE latents were not translation-equivariant, this copy operation would inject semantically mismatched features, leading to artifacts. The paper does not explicitly justify or test this assumption, but the strong empirical results suggest it holds sufficiently well for the Wan VAE in practice.

**A bridge between research and commercial quality.** An unusual aspect of this paper's positioning is its explicit comparison to Kling 1.5 Pro's Motion Brush [4], a commercial product from Kuaishou. The human study in Table 3 reports that Wan-Move achieves a 47.8% win rate on motion accuracy and 53.4% win rate on motion quality against Kling 1.5 Pro — essentially at parity. For visual quality, the win rate is 50.2% (statistically indistinguishable from a tie). This is significant framing: the paper is claiming not just SOTA among academic methods (which Table 1 already demonstrates) but competitiveness with a deployed commercial system that presumably benefits from proprietary data, larger-scale training, and engineering resources beyond what an academic lab typically has. The implication is that the "no extra modules" design philosophy is not just elegant — it is what makes this level of quality achievable with open-source resources.

**MoveBench as a community contribution.** The paper also positions MoveBench as infrastructure for the field, not just an evaluation tool for Wan-Move. The careful curation pipeline (Figure 3), the inclusion of both point trajectories and segmentation masks (so that mask-based methods can also be evaluated), the 54 content categories covering diverse motion types (Figure 4), and the human+SAM hybrid annotation process (Figure 3b) suggest an intent to set a new standard for motion-control evaluation. The paper's claim that MoveBench offers "larger data volume, longer video durations, and high-quality motion annotations" (Abstract) compared to existing benchmarks is supported by the statistics in Figure 5: 1018 videos at 81 frames each, versus 600 videos at 49 frames for the next-largest benchmark (MagicBench).

### The Overarching Narrative

Reading the paper as a whole, the motivation follows a clear logical arc:

1. Motion control is essential for practical video generation, but current methods either sacrifice precision (sparse signals) or scalability (dense signals requiring auxiliary encoders).
2. Point trajectories offer the best balance of expressiveness and ease-of-specification, but prior work mismanages them — either by encoding them as context-free pixel embeddings (Motion Prompting) or by wrapping them in heavy ControlNet architectures.
3. The key realization is that an I2V model already knows how to animate a first frame; motion control just needs to tell it *where things should go.* The most natural way to communicate this is in the model's own latent language — by editing the condition features to show where first-frame content should appear in later frames.
4. Because this requires zero architectural changes, it scales effortlessly: fine-tune the DiT backbone on a large, high-quality video dataset with trajectory-annotated condition features, and the model learns to interpret the replicated features as motion instructions.
5. The result is a system that matches commercial quality while being fully open-source, and the accompanying benchmark (MoveBench) provides the rigorous evaluation infrastructure the field needs to make further progress.

This narrative is compelling because it frames Wan-Move not as "we built a better motion encoder" but as "we realized you don't need a motion encoder at all" — a classic reframing that sidesteps an entire category of technical challenges rather than incrementally improving within them.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Wan-Move is a fine-tuning framework that teaches an existing image-to-video diffusion model to accept point-trajectory motion instructions without adding any new neural network components — it achieves control purely by editing the latent features that the base model already receives as input. The problem it solves is the architectural overhead problem in motion-controllable video generation: prior methods require auxiliary motion encoders or ControlNet branches that complicate training, degrade motion signals during encoding, and resist scaling to large foundation models. The shape of the solution is a single, deterministic pre-processing step applied to the condition features before they enter the DiT backbone — copy first-frame latent features along latent-space trajectories into future-frame positions — which transforms the standard "animate this image" instruction into "animate this image such that these specific pixels end up at these specific future locations."

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components, arranged in a pipeline from inputs to generated video:

1. **Motion Specification Interface** — the mechanism by which a user (or an automated system like CoTracker) provides point trajectories in pixel space. Each trajectory is a sequence of `$(x, y)$` coordinates across frames indicating where a tracked point should appear.

2. **Spatial Coordinate Mapping** — a deterministic mathematical function (Eq. 2) that converts pixel-space trajectory coordinates into latent-space coordinates, accounting for the VAE's spatial and temporal compression ratios. This produces `$\tilde{p}[n]$` — the latent-grid position of each trajectory point at each temporal index.

3. **Latent Feature Replication Engine** — the core operation (Eq. 3, Figure 2a) that reads the VAE-encoded first-frame latent feature at the starting trajectory position `$\tilde{p}[0]$` and writes it into the zero-padded condition feature at every subsequent trajectory position `$\tilde{p}[n]$` for `$n = 1, \ldots, T/f_t$`. This transforms the static first-frame condition into a spatiotemporally-aligned guide that tells the model where content should move.

4. **Condition Assembly** — the process that concatenates the motion-edited latent condition `$z_{\text{image}}$` with the noisy latent `$x_t$`, along with CLIP image embeddings and umT5 text embeddings, to form the full conditioning input to the DiT backbone. This step is architecturally identical to the base I2V model — the only difference is the content of `$z_{\text{image}}$`.

5. **DiT Denoising Backbone (Wan-I2V-14B)** — the pretrained image-to-video diffusion transformer, fine-tuned with the flow matching objective (Eq. 4) to interpret the replicated latent features as motion instructions. The backbone remains architecturally unmodified; only its weights are updated during training.

Information flows as follows: a user provides a first-frame image, a text prompt, and point trajectories (dense or sparse) → trajectories are mapped to latent coordinates via Eq. 2 → first-frame latent features are replicated along these coordinates via Eq. 3 → the edited `$z_{\text{image}}$` is concatenated channel-wise with `$x_t$` → CLIP and umT5 encode the image and text → the DiT backbone processes the full conditioning input and predicts a vector field → the ODE solver integrates this vector field to produce the output latent → the VAE decoder reconstructs the 5-second, 480p video.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the base I2V generation framework** (Section 3.1, Preliminaries) — how Wan-I2V-14B conditions on a first frame, what `$z_{\text{image}}$` contains, and why the zero-padded frame slots are the natural injection point for motion guidance — because this establishes the substrate into which Wan-Move inserts its mechanism.

- **Second, latent trajectory guidance** (Section 3.2) — the spatial coordinate mapping (Eq. 2) and the feature replication operation (Eq. 3) that together transform pixel-space point tracks into latent-space motion instructions, including the treatment of multiple overlapping trajectories — because this is the paper's single novel technical contribution and everything else (training, inference, scaling) follows from it.

- **Third, training data curation and the training procedure** (Section 3.3) — the two-stage filtering pipeline (visual quality scoring + motion stability via SigLIP), the trajectory sampling strategy (5% dropout, 1–200 uniformly sampled tracks), and the flow matching objective — because the data and optimization choices determine whether the model actually learns to interpret replicated features as motion instructions.

- **Fourth, the inference procedure** (Section 3.3) — how classifier-free guidance is applied, the three input modalities (image, text, trajectories), and the sampling schedule — because this is what a practitioner needs to actually use the trained model.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method paper** whose core idea is that motion control for I2V generation can be achieved by deterministically editing the condition latent `$z_{\text{image}}$` — specifically, by copying first-frame latent features into future-frame positions along latent-space trajectories — rather than by training additional neural network modules to encode and inject motion signals.

#### Base I2V Framework: How Wan-I2V-14B Conditions on a First Frame

The paper builds on Wan-I2V-14B [19], a state-of-the-art image-to-video diffusion model. Understanding the base conditioning mechanism is essential because Wan-Move's entire contribution is a modification to it.

**Video diffusion in latent space.** Given an input video `$V \in \mathbb{R}^{(1+T) \times H \times W \times 3}$` — a first frame plus `$T$` subsequent frames, each of spatial dimensions `$H \times W$` with 3 color channels — a pretrained VAE encoder `$E$` compresses it temporally by a factor `$f_t$` and spatially by a factor `$f_s$`, while expanding the channel dimension to `$C$`, yielding:

$$x = E(V) \in \mathbb{R}^{(1 + T/f_t) \times (H/f_s) \times (W/f_s) \times C}$$

where `$x$` is the latent representation, `$T/f_t$` is the number of latent frames (temporal tokens), `$H/f_s$` and `$W/f_s$` are the latent spatial dimensions, and `$C$` is the VAE's latent channel count. The VAE decoder `$D$` reconstructs the video as `$\hat{V} = D(x)$`.

**What it computes:** a compressed representation of the video in a lower-dimensional latent space. The temporal compression (`$f_t$`) reduces the number of frames that the diffusion model must process, while the spatial compression (`$f_s$`) reduces per-frame resolution. For Wan-I2V-14B generating 5-second videos at 480p resolution (832×480 pixels) with 81 frames, the specific compression ratios determine the latent tensor's shape, though the paper does not state `$f_t$` and `$f_s$` explicitly — they are properties of the Wan VAE.

**Why this form:** latent diffusion is the standard paradigm because operating in pixel space (e.g., 81 × 832 × 480 × 3 ≈ 97 million values per video) would make the attention computation in the DiT backbone prohibitively expensive. The VAE's compression (typically `$f_s = 8$` and `$f_t = 4$` for video VAEs, though the paper does not confirm these values for Wan specifically) reduces the token count by roughly two orders of magnitude, making transformer-based video generation feasible.

**The first-frame condition feature `$z_{\text{image}}$`.** For image-to-video generation, the model receives the first frame as a conditioning signal. The base Wan-I2V-14B encodes this by taking the first frame `$I \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times H \times W \times 3}$`, concatenating it with `$T$` frames of zeros `$0^{T \times H \times W \times 3}$`, and encoding the result with the VAE:

$$z_{\text{image}} = E(\text{concat}[I, 0^{T \times H \times W \times 3}]) \in \mathbb{R}^{(1 + T/f_t) \times (H/f_s) \times (W/f_s) \times C}$$

**What it computes:** a latent tensor with the same spatiotemporal dimensions as a full video latent `$x$`, but where only the first latent frame contains real VAE-encoded features from the input image — all subsequent latent frames (`$1$` through `$T/f_t$`) are zero-filled, representing "unknown future content that the model must generate."

**Why this form:** this zero-padding convention creates a natural "canvas" for the diffusion model. The first latent frame anchors the generation to the real image content, while the zeroed slots tell the model "these positions need to be filled in, and they should be consistent with the first frame." The channel-wise concatenation of `$z_{\text{image}}$` with the noisy latent `$x_t$` means the model sees both "what is the truth at frame 0" and "what is the current noisy estimate of all frames" simultaneously, enabling it to denoise the future frames conditioned on the first frame. This is precisely why Wan-Move can inject motion guidance by simply replacing some of those zeros with copied first-frame features — the model already expects the zero-slots to be filled with information derived from the first frame; Wan-Move just provides stronger, spatially-localized hints about *where* that information should go.

**The DiT backbone and conditioning.** The pretrained umT5 [71] text encoder processes the user's text prompt into text embeddings `$z_{\text{text}}$`, and the CLIP [70] image encoder processes the first frame into a global image embedding `$z_{\text{global}}$`. These are injected into the DiT backbone via decoupled cross-attention [18]. The concatenated `$[x_t; z_{\text{image}}]$` enters the DiT along the channel dimension, giving the model both the noisy latents to denoise and the first-frame condition to anchor to.

#### Latent Trajectory Guidance: The Core Mechanism

This is the paper's central technical contribution. It consists of two sequential, deterministic operations: (1) mapping pixel-space trajectories to latent coordinates, and (2) replicating first-frame latent features along those coordinates.

**Motion representation: point trajectories.** A point trajectory of length `$1 + T$` is a sequence of 2D pixel coordinates:

$$p \in \mathbb{R}^{(1+T) \times 2}$$

where `$p[n] = (x_n, y_n)$` specifies the `$(x, y)$` position of the tracked point in the `$n$`-th frame (0-indexed, with `$n = 0$` being the first frame). The paper uses CoTracker [73] to extract these trajectories from training videos, placing a dense 32×32 grid of tracking points (1024 total) and retaining trajectories where the point is predicted as "visible." During inference, users can specify any number of trajectories, from a single point (drag-based control) to dense grids (motion transfer).

**Why point trajectories:** they offer the best tradeoff among motion representations. Unlike bounding boxes (which cannot express local deformation) or optical flow (which requires an external model during inference and accumulates errors), point trajectories are sparse, easy to specify (drag a point in an interface, use an off-the-shelf tracker, or define them programmatically for camera/rotation control), and naturally capture both global displacement and fine-grained local motion. The sparsity is a virtue: the model is free to interpolate motion in unconstrained regions, preventing over-constraint that could cause artifacts.

**Step 1: Spatial coordinate mapping (Eq. 2).** The trajectories exist in pixel space, but the latent features exist in VAE-compressed latent space, where spatial dimensions are downsampled by `$f_s$` and temporal frames are grouped by `$f_t$`. The mapping is:

$$\tilde{p}[n] =
\begin{cases}
\frac{p[n]}{f_s} & \text{if } n = 0, \\[8pt]
\frac{\sum_{i=(n-1)\cdot f_t + 1}^{n \cdot f_t} p[i]}{f_t \cdot f_s} & 1 \leq n \leq \frac{T}{f_t}
\end{cases}$$

where `$\tilde{p}[n]$` is the latent-space coordinate at latent-time index `$n$`, `$f_s$` is the spatial compression factor, and `$f_t$` is the temporal compression factor.

**What it computes:** for the first frame (`$n = 0$`), the mapping simply divides the pixel coordinates by `$f_s$` — if the VAE spatially compresses by a factor of 8, a pixel at `$(256, 384)$` maps to latent position `$(32, 48)$`. For subsequent latent-time indices (`$n \geq 1$`), the mapping averages the pixel coordinates over a temporal window of `$f_t$` consecutive frames and then divides by `$f_s$`. If `$f_t = 4$` (a common VAE temporal compression), latent-time index `$n = 1$` corresponds to pixel frames 1–4, and `$\tilde{p}[1]$` is the average of `$p[1], p[2], p[3], p[4]$` divided by `$f_s$`. This averaging accounts for the fact that the latent representation at temporal index `$n$` represents a "chunk" of `$f_t$` pixel frames, so the trajectory position should represent the average motion within that chunk.

**Why this form:** the first-frame case uses direct division because frame 0 has no temporal ambiguity — there is exactly one pixel corresponding to one latent position. The averaging for later frames handles the temporal compression: the VAE encoder processes `$f_t$` pixel frames into one latent frame, so a trajectory that moves smoothly across those `$f_t$` pixel frames should be represented by a single latent position that approximates the average location during that interval. Using the endpoint only would bias the position toward the end of the temporal window; averaging over all `$f_t$` frames provides a more representative position. The spatial division by `$f_s$` is straightforward — it accounts for the VAE's spatial downsampling. Without this mapping, the trajectory coordinates would point to the wrong locations in latent space, and the feature replication would inject information at incorrect positions.

**Step 2: Latent feature replication (Eq. 3).** Given the latent trajectory `$\tilde{p}$`, the operation that injects motion guidance is:

$$z_{\text{image}}[n, \tilde{p}[n, 0], \tilde{p}[n, 1], :] = z_{\text{image}}[0, \tilde{p}[0, 0], \tilde{p}[0, 1], :] \quad \text{for } n = 1, \ldots, \frac{T}{f_t}$$

where `$z_{\text{image}}[t, h, w, :]$` denotes the full feature vector (of dimension `$C$`) at temporal index `$t$`, spatial height `$h$`, and spatial width `$w$` within the latent tensor.

**What it computes:** for every latent-time index `$n$` from `$1$` to `$T/f_t$` (all the zero-padded future frame slots), the operation reads the entire `$C$`-dimensional feature vector from the first latent frame at position `$\tilde{p}[0]$` — the VAE encoding of the image content at the trajectory's starting point — and writes it into the condition latent at position `$\tilde{p}[n]$`. This is a direct feature copy; no learned parameters, no interpolation, no transformation. The operation is applied independently per trajectory.

**Why this form:** the key design choice is replicating the **full VAE feature vector** rather than a scalar-valued signal (like a binary mask) or a learned embedding (like Motion Prompting's random track embeddings). The VAE feature at `$\tilde{p}[0]$` carries rich information: it encodes the texture, color, edge structure, and semantic content of the image patch centered at the trajectory's starting point. When this feature vector appears at position `$\tilde{p}[n]$` in a future frame, the DiT backbone — which has been trained on videos and fine-tuned on this exact conditioning scheme — interprets it as "the visual content that was at `$\tilde{p}[0]$` in frame 0 should now be at `$\tilde{p}[n]$` in frame `$n$`." The alternative of using a scalar (e.g., a 1 in a binary mask) would only tell the model "something is here" without specifying *what* — the model would need to hallucinate the texture and appearance, which could cause inconsistency. The alternative of using a learned embedding (as in Motion Prompting) would require the embedding to encode both position and appearance information, but the embedding is trained from scratch and may not capture fine-grained textures. The VAE feature vector requires no learning — it is the exact same feature the model sees in the first frame — so the motion guidance is grounded in the same representation space the model already uses.

**Handling overlapping trajectories.** When multiple visible point trajectories coincide at the same spatiotemporal position (e.g., two tracked points on the same rigid object end up at the same latent grid cell), the paper "randomly selects one trajectory's corresponding first-frame feature" (Section 3.2). Appendix 9.1 (Table 11) confirms that random selection outperforms feature averaging for this tie-breaking. When trajectories are averaged, FVD rises from 83.5 to 83.4 (negligible, but directionally worse) and EPE rises from 2.6 to 2.7; the more significant degradation is in PSNR (17.8 → 17.5) and SSIM (0.64 → 0.63), suggesting that averaging blends features in a way that reduces sharpness and structural fidelity. The hypothesis (stated in Appendix 9.1) is that averaging causes "information loss" — the linear combination of two feature vectors may not correspond to a valid VAE latent for any single image patch, introducing artifacts.

**Handling occluded trajectories.** CoTracker [73] distinguishes between visible and occluded points. The paper applies feature replication "only along the visible trajectories" (Section 3.3, Training). When a tracked point is occluded — behind another object, out of frame — the model receives no explicit guidance for that point during those frames. The paper notes in the Limitations section that "prolonged absence may lead to loss of control," but "short-term occlusions can be recovered once the point reappears, showing a degree of generalization" (Section 6). Appendix 9.3 (Figure 12) shows an example where a trajectory becomes occluded as a person's arm moves behind their body, then re-emerges — the generated video maintains coherent motion. The paper attributes this robustness to "the presence of similar cases in the training data and the model's inherent generalization capacity."

**Why this over explicit motion encoders.** The paper's argument against separate motion-processing modules (ControlNet, auxiliary encoders) is threefold:

1. **Signal degradation:** a learned encoder (even a well-trained one) can lose information during the feature transformation process. If the encoder is trained with an auxiliary loss or as part of an end-to-end system, it may learn to discard certain motion details that are not strongly predictive of the training objective. The latent feature replication, by contrast, is lossless with respect to the first-frame feature — the exact `$C$`-dimensional vector is preserved.

2. **Training complexity:** adding a ControlNet branch to a 14B-parameter DiT backbone roughly doubles the number of trainable parameters for the encoder portion, which increases GPU memory pressure and can require reducing batch size or sequence parallelism degree. The paper reports in Table 5 that ControlNet introduces an additional 225 seconds of inference latency compared to the 3-second overhead of Wan-Move's trajectory extraction. During training, the parameter and optimizer state overhead would similarly increase memory consumption.

3. **Scalability:** because Wan-Move introduces no new modules, the entire model — DiT backbone, text encoders, VAE — is structurally identical to Wan-I2V-14B. This means the same distributed training strategies (FSDP, Ulysses sequence parallelism) apply without modification. The paper states: "Wan-Move preserves the base model architecture and only adds a one-time trajectory extraction process, increasing just 3-second inference time" (Section 5.3, Condition Fusion Strategy). The inference latency of 765 seconds for the base model plus 3 seconds for trajectory processing, versus 987 seconds with ControlNet, represents a ~29% speedup — not because the ControlNet is slow per se, but because it processes the entire latent tensor, while feature replication processes only the trajectory positions (which are orders of magnitude fewer than total latent grid cells).

#### Training Data Curation and the Training Procedure

The training procedure is a standard fine-tuning setup on a carefully filtered dataset, with a few critical design choices specific to the motion-control objective.

**Two-stage training data filtering.** The paper curates a dataset of 2 million high-quality 720p videos from Panda70M [85], Pixabay [86], Pexels [75], and independently collected YouTube videos (Table 10). The filtering pipeline has two stages:

1. **Visual quality filter:** annotators manually label the visual quality of 1,000 samples. These labels train an "expert scoring model" that automatically scores all candidate videos. Only videos exceeding a quality threshold are retained. The paper does not describe the architecture of this expert scoring model or the specific threshold, but the purpose is clear: filter out low-resolution, compressed, watermarked, or visually degraded content that would contaminate training.

2. **Motion stability filter (temporal coherence):** for each remaining video, the first frame is encoded with SigLIP [72] to obtain a feature vector. The mean SigLIP feature is computed across the remaining frames. The cosine similarity between the first-frame feature and the mean of later-frame features serves as a "stability metric." The paper states: "Based on empirical analysis of 10,000 samples, we establish a threshold to retain only videos where the content remains consistent with the initial frame."

**What it computes:** this stability metric measures whether the video's semantic content remains consistent — if the camera cuts to a different scene, the SigLIP features will diverge sharply, producing low cosine similarity. If the camera moves but the scene content is stable, the features will remain similar. If objects move but the overall scene context is consistent, the similarity will be high but not perfect. The empirical threshold (not specified in the paper) is chosen to balance diversity and coherence.

**Why this form:** the two-stage filter addresses complementary failure modes. The visual quality filter removes videos that are technically poor — blurry, noisy, compressed — which would teach the model to produce low-quality output. The motion stability filter removes videos with scene cuts or drastic content changes, which are incompatible with the I2V formulation (the model expects frames 1 through `$T$` to depict the same scene as frame 0). SigLIP features are chosen over pixel-based metrics (like mean squared error between frames) because they capture semantic content rather than low-level pixel statistics — a video of a rotating object might have large pixel changes but consistent SigLIP features, which is exactly the type of motion the model should learn.

**Trajectory extraction.** For each training video, CoTracker [73] tracks a dense 32×32 grid of points (1024 total). CoTracker distinguishes visible from occluded points, and only visible trajectory segments are retained. This provides dense ground-truth motion trajectories for every training sample.

**Trajectory sampling during training.** The paper uses a mixed sampling strategy (Section 3.3):

- With 5% probability, `$k = 0$` — no trajectories are sampled, and the model receives the original zero-padded `$z_{\text{image}}$`. This is critical: it preserves the model's standard I2V capability. Without this dropout, the model might learn to *require* trajectories and fail when none are provided; Section 5.3 (Table 7) confirms that Wan-Move achieves naïve I2V quality (PSNR 17.9, SSIM 0.64) when `$N = 0$` trajectories are used at inference, with the expected EPE of 12.4 (since there's no control signal, the generated motion doesn't track any specific point).

- With 95% probability, `$k$` is sampled uniformly from `$\{1, 2, \ldots, 200\}$`. The paper sweeps this maximum value `$N_{\text{max}}$` in Table 6, finding that `$N_{\text{max}} = 200$` achieves optimal performance (lowest EPE of 2.6, highest SSIM of 0.64). Values below 200 provide insufficient motion information (EPE rises to 3.3 at `$N_{\text{max}} = 10$`); values above 200 cause EPE to rise again (3.9 at `$N_{\text{max}} = 1024$`), which the paper attributes to "mismatch between the dense point tracks in the training and the sparse point tracks during evaluation" — the evaluation on MoveBench uses a modest number of trajectories, so training with very dense tracks creates a distribution shift between training and testing conditions.

**Why uniform sampling over a wide range (1–200):** this is a form of data augmentation that teaches the model to handle variable amounts of motion guidance — from very sparse (a single point controlling one object) to moderately dense (200 points covering multiple objects). The paper contrasts this with a two-stage "dense-to-sampling" strategy used in prior work [14, 15, 2, 58], where the first training stage uses dense trajectories and the second uses sparse ones. Appendix 9.2 (Table 12) shows that the direct sampling approach achieves comparable EPE (2.6 vs. 2.6) and lower FVD (83.5 vs. 84.2), with the advantage of simplicity — only one training stage is needed.

**The flow matching objective.** Wan-I2V-14B, like Wan, uses flow matching [74] rather than denoising diffusion. The training objective is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_t, c} \left[ \| v_\theta(x_t, t, c) - v_t(x_t) \|^2 \right]$$

where `$v_\theta$` is the DiT model (parameterized by `$\theta$`) that predicts a vector field, `$x_t$` is the noisy latent at time `$t \in [0, 1]$`, `$c$` is the union of all conditioning signals (`$c = \{z_{\text{image}}, z_{\text{global}}, z_{\text{text}}\}$`), and `$v_t(x_t)$` is the ground-truth vector field that transports samples from the noise distribution at `$t = 1$` to the data distribution at `$t = 0$`.

**What it computes:** the expected squared L2 distance between the model's predicted vector field and the true vector field, averaged over diffusion timesteps `$t$`, noisy latents `$x_t$`, and conditioning signals `$c$`. The vector field `$v_t(x_t)$` points in the direction that `$x_t$` should move to approach the clean data `$x_0$` — in flow matching, this is typically the linear interpolation direction `$x_0 - x_1$` (from noise to data). The model learns to predict this vector given the noisy latent and all conditioning signals.

**Why this form:** flow matching is chosen over the standard diffusion `$\epsilon$`-prediction (DDPM) objective because it enables straight-line paths from noise to data, which can be integrated with fewer sampling steps and is theoretically simpler. Wan [19] adopted flow matching, and Wan-Move inherits this choice. The squared error loss is standard for regression. A key property: the loss does not explicitly supervise motion accuracy — it only supervises the vector field that generates the video. The motion control capability emerges because `$z_{\text{image}}$` contains the replicated features, and the model must learn to generate videos where the content at `$\tilde{p}[n]$` is consistent with the content that was at `$\tilde{p}[0]$` — reducing the vector field error naturally forces the model to respect the trajectory constraints, since violating them would produce inconsistent latents that deviate from the training videos.

**Training hyperparameters and infrastructure.** The paper reports (Section 5.1, Appendix 8.3):

| Hyperparameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Base model | Wan-I2V-14B |
| Optimizer | AdamW [89] |
| Weight decay | `$1 \times 10^{-3}$` |
| Base learning rate | `$5 \times 10^{-6}$` |
| Warmup steps | 2,000 (linear) |
| Flow matching timesteps (training) | 1,000 |
| Sequence parallel size | 4 (Ulysses [90]) |
| Precision | `torch.bfloat16` |
| Distributed training | FSDP [88] |
| GPUs | 64 × NVIDIA A100 |
| Total training steps | 30,000 |
| Generation timesteps (inference) | 50 |

**Trainable vs. frozen components.** Only the DiT backbone is trainable. The VAE encoder/decoder, umT5 text encoder, and CLIP image encoder remain frozen. This is standard practice in diffusion model fine-tuning and ensures that the pretrained image and text representations — which encode a broad world model — are not corrupted by the relatively narrow motion-control fine-tuning distribution.

**Why fine-tune the entire DiT backbone rather than training adapters:** the paper's philosophy is that motion control should be learned by the core generation model, not by accessory networks that route around it. Fine-tuning the DiT backbone allows all self-attention and cross-attention layers to adapt their parameters to the new conditioning signal (the replicated latent features). If the paper had used LoRA or adapter layers, the capacity for learning complex motion-control behaviors would be limited by the rank of the adaptation. Full fine-tuning at the scale of a 14B model is expensive but yields the strongest results — and because no new parameters are added, the model can be deployed with the same inference code as the base model.

**Why 2,000 warmup steps:** the paper notes this enables "a smooth transition from the initial I2V generation (corresponding to 0 point trajectories) to motion-controllable video generation." At step 0, the model is Wan-I2V-14B, which expects zero-padded `$z_{\text{image}}$` and has never seen replicated features. The 5% zero-trajectory sampling rate means that for 5% of warmup steps, the model trains on standard I2V (preserving its existing capability), while for the other 95%, the learning rate is low and the model gradually adapts to the new condition. Without this warmup, a sudden large gradient from the modified condition could destabilize training.

#### Inference Procedure

**Inputs.** At inference time, Wan-Move accepts three inputs:

1. **A text prompt** — a natural language description of the desired video content, motion, and camera behavior. The paper emphasizes the importance of detailed captions (Section 4, MoveBench) and uses Qwen2.5-VL [87] to generate dense descriptions covering "objects, actions, and camera dynamics" (the captioning prompt in Figure 10 explicitly instructs: "Focus primarily on the motion and behavior of main subjects," "describe the camera perspective and movement at the end").

2. **A first-frame image** — the static image that will be animated. This is provided at the target resolution (832×480 for 480p).

3. **Sparse or dense point trajectories** — either manually specified (e.g., a user dragging points in an interface), extracted from a reference video using CoTracker (for motion transfer), or computed programmatically (for camera control or 3D rotation). Trajectories are specified in pixel coordinates across the `$1 + T$` frames.

**Feature encoding.** Two pretrained encoders process the global context:

- **umT5 [71]** encodes the text prompt into `$z_{\text{text}}$`, a sequence of text embeddings.
- **CLIP [70]** encodes the first-frame image into `$z_{\text{global}}$`, a single global image embedding.

These are standard for Wan-I2V-14B and are injected into the DiT backbone via decoupled cross-attention layers throughout the transformer blocks.

**Condition feature preparation.** The VAE encoder processes the first frame with zero-padded subsequent frames to produce the initial `$z_{\text{image}}$`, exactly as in the base I2V model (Eq. 1). Then, the latent trajectory guidance is applied: the pixel-space trajectories are mapped to latent coordinates (Eq. 2), and the first-frame latent features are replicated along visible trajectory segments into the zero-padded slots (Eq. 3). This produces the motion-augmented `$z_{\text{image}}$`. The entire operation occurs before the iterative denoising loop — it is a single pre-processing pass.

**Classifier-free guidance (CFG).** During the iterative denoising process, classifier-free guidance is applied to strengthen the model's adherence to the conditioning signals. Following standard practice, the unconditional vector field `$v_{\text{uncond}}$` is computed by dropping the text condition (but retaining `$z_{\text{image}}$` and `$z_{\text{global}}$` — the image conditioning is never dropped, since I2V generation fundamentally requires the first frame). The conditional vector field `$v_{\text{cond}}$` includes all conditions. The guided vector field is:

$$\tilde{v}_\theta(x_t, t, z_{\text{image}}, z_{\text{global}}, z_{\text{text}}) = v_{\text{uncond}} + w(v_{\text{cond}} - v_{\text{uncond}})$$

where `$w$` is the guidance scale, set to 5.0 unless otherwise specified.

**What it computes:** a weighted extrapolation from the unconditional prediction toward the conditional prediction. When `$w = 1$`, the output is just the conditional prediction. When `$w > 1$`, the model is pushed *further* in the direction of the text-conditioned generation, amplifying the influence of the prompt. The difference `$v_{\text{cond}} - v_{\text{uncond}}$` represents the effect of adding the text condition; scaling it by `$w$` strengthens that effect.

**Why this form:** classifier-free guidance is the standard mechanism in diffusion models for trading off sample diversity against sample quality. A guidance scale of 5.0 is relatively high (typical values range from 3–7 for video models), suggesting the model benefits from strong conditioning — likely because the motion instructions from replicated features are subtle and the text prompt helps resolve ambiguities about *how* the object should move, not just *where.* The paper does not ablate the guidance scale.

**Sampling schedule.** The inference uses 50 sampling steps, following Wan's default sampling scheme. The flow matching ODE is integrated from `$t = 1$` (pure noise) to `$t = 0$` (clean latent), with `$x_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$` as the initial noise. At each step, the guided vector field `$\tilde{v}_\theta$` provides the direction, and an ODE solver (presumably Euler or Heun, though not specified) takes a step.

**Output.** After the final step, the denoised latent `$x_0$` is decoded by the VAE decoder `$D$`, producing the output video `$\hat{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{(1+T) \times H \times W \times 3}$` — a 5-second, 480p video clip with 81 frames at 16 fps (5 × 16 + 1 = 81).

**Motion control applications (non-exhaustive).** Because point trajectories are a flexible representation, the sample generation process supports diverse control modalities (Figure 1, Section 5.4):

- **Single/multi-object control:** the user drags 1–`$N$` points to specify object trajectories. CoTracker can densify sparse user inputs into richer trajectory sets.
- **Camera control:** following the approach of Motion Prompting [13], a monocular depth predictor (Depth Anything V2 [84]) estimates a 3D point cloud from the first frame. The point cloud is projected along a user-specified camera trajectory, and z-buffering resolves occlusions to produce 2D pixel-space trajectories for all visible points. These trajectories are then processed by the standard Wan-Move pipeline.
- **Primitive-level control:** a 3D primitive (e.g., sphere) is virtually rotated, and its surface points are projected to 2D trajectories. This creates smooth rotational motion without requiring manual per-point specification.
- **Motion transfer:** trajectories extracted from a reference video (via CoTracker) are applied to a different first-frame image. The replicated features carry the appearance of the new image, but the trajectory geometry comes from the reference video, transferring the motion pattern while preserving the new image's visual content.
- **3D rotation:** depth-based 3D positions are estimated from the first frame, a rotation transformation is applied, and the rotated positions are projected back to 2D trajectories. This enables rotating objects in generated videos while maintaining 3D consistency (to the extent that the monocular depth estimate is accurate).

**Why this flexibility matters:** it demonstrates that latent trajectory guidance is a general-purpose control mechanism, not a single-task solution. The same model, with the same inference code, supports all these modalities because the interface between the user and the model — point trajectories — is expressive enough to encode them all. The model does not need to know whether the trajectories came from a user drag, a reference video, or a camera simulation; it just sees "these features go to these locations."

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Latent feature replication over learned embeddings:** preserves the full VAE feature vector (texture, edges, semantics), avoiding the information loss that a learned encoder could introduce. Empirically validated by the ablation in Table 4: latent replication achieves EPE 2.6 vs. random track embedding's 2.7 (a small but consistent advantage) and PSNR 17.8 vs. 16.1 (a substantial visual quality improvement from using real features rather than learned ones).

- **Direct concatenation over ControlNet for condition fusion:** achieves comparable motion control accuracy (EPE 2.6 vs. 2.5, Table 5) while adding only 3 seconds of inference overhead vs. ControlNet's 225 seconds. The architectural simplicity enables straightforward large-scale training with standard distributed strategies.

- **5% trajectory dropout during training:** preserves the base I2V model's capability to generate videos without motion control, ensuring the model does not catastrophically forget the original task. Enables inference with `$N = 0$` trajectories for standard I2V generation.

- **Random selection for overlapping trajectories:** prevents information loss from feature averaging. Empirically, random selection yields better PSNR and SSIM than averaging (Table 11), consistent with the hypothesis that averaging blends features inappropriately.

- **Uniform trajectory sampling (1–200) over dense-to-sparse two-stage training:** achieves comparable or better results (Table 12) with a simpler, single-stage training pipeline, reducing engineering complexity.

- **Full DiT fine-tuning over adapter-based methods:** allows all layers of the 14B-parameter backbone to adapt to the new conditioning signal, maximizing motion control capacity. The paper does not ablate full fine-tuning vs. LoRA, so this is a design choice motivated by the philosophy that motion control should be a first-class model capability, not a plug-in.

- **Flow matching over DDPM:** inherited from the base model (Wan), enabling efficient sampling with 50 steps and straight-line trajectories through the probability flow.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Motion Control as Condition Editing Rather Than Condition Encoding

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is **reframing motion control from a signal-encoding problem to a condition-editing operation.** Every prior fine-tuning approach to motion-controllable video generation — MotionCtrl [15], DragNUWA [2], Tora [14], LeviTor [16], ImageConductor [58], Motion Prompting [13], MagicMotion [20] — treats motion guidance as an external signal that must be *encoded*: you take the motion representation (point tracks, optical flow, bounding box sequences), pass it through a learned neural module (ControlNet branch, cross-attention layer, adaLN block), and inject the resulting embeddings into the generative backbone. This framing makes two assumptions: (1) that the motion signal lives in a different representational space than the video content, requiring a translation step, and (2) that this translation must be learned from data.

Wan-Move rejects both assumptions. By observing that an image-to-video model already ingests a spatiotemporally structured condition latent — `z_image`, with real features at frame 0 and zeros at all future frames — the paper recognizes that the *format* of the condition signal already supports spatiotemporal editing. The zero-padded future frame slots are not dead space; they are blank canvases that the model has learned to interpret as "here is where you tell me what the future should look like." Wan-Move's core idea is that you can write motion instructions directly into this canvas by copying appearance features to their future positions, and the model — once fine-tuned — will learn to read them as motion commands. No encoder intervenes between the motion specification and the model input; the motion specification *is* the model input.

This is a genuinely different category of solution from the ControlNet family. ControlNet says: "here is a separate signal; I will build a separate network to process it and blend it into the main network." Wan-Move says: "there is no separate signal — motion is a property of how appearance features should be arranged in spacetime, and I can express that arrangement by editing the appearance features themselves." The distinction matters because it sidesteps an entire class of problems: encoder capacity bottlenecks, domain shift between the encoder's training distribution and the base model's feature statistics, careful balancing of ControlNet output scales to avoid washing out the base model's representations, and the memory/throughput overhead of running a parallel network during training and inference. Table 5 quantifies the practical consequence: ControlNet adds 225 seconds of inference latency; Wan-Move's latent feature replication adds 3 seconds. That 75× difference is not an optimization of ControlNet — it is a consequence of not needing a ControlNet at all.

The reframing also explains *why* the approach scales. If motion control requires an encoder, then scaling to a 14B backbone means scaling the encoder too — either by making it deeper (more parameters, more memory) or by accepting that a small encoder becomes the bottleneck. Wan-Move's editing operation has no parameters, so the motion control capacity scales entirely with the backbone's capacity. A larger DiT means more attention heads, deeper layers, and larger feature dimensions — all of which increase the model's ability to interpret and respect the edited condition features. The paper does not explicitly make this scaling argument, but it is implicit in the design: "no extra modules" is not just about simplicity; it is about making motion control inherit the scaling laws of the base model.

This is a **fundamental conceptual shift** rather than an incremental improvement. The paper does not propose a better encoder architecture; it eliminates the encoder category entirely. Whether a future paper builds a "better ControlNet" or a "more expressive motion encoder" is orthogonal to Wan-Move's claim — which is that the entire premise of motion encoding may be unnecessary for I2V models, because the condition latent already provides a sufficiently expressive canvas.

### Innovation 2: Latent Feature Vectors as Simultaneously Appearance and Motion Carriers

A second, subtler insight concerns **what information a VAE latent feature vector actually carries and how that changes the nature of motion guidance.** Prior point-trajectory methods — most directly Motion Prompting [13] — represent each tracked position with a learned or random embedding. This embedding is essentially a positional tag: it tells the model "there is a tracked point here" but carries no information about *what* is being tracked. The model must infer from context (neighboring features, text prompt, cross-attention) what visual content should appear at that position. This works for rigid, isolated objects where the context provides strong cues, but it fails when the tracked point is part of a textured surface, an articulated body part, or a region with complex appearance — exactly the cases shown in Figure 8, where the hand moves but the bread remains static under the random track embedding strategy.

Wan-Move's latent feature replication solves this by carrying not just positional information but the **entire VAE encoding of the appearance patch at the trajectory's origin.** A `C`-dimensional VAE feature vector (likely 4–16 channels in the Wan VAE, though the paper does not specify) encodes texture, edge orientation, color distribution, and — because VAEs are trained with reconstruction losses — sufficient detail to faithfully reproduce the image patch when decoded. When this vector is copied to position `p̃[n]` in frame `n`, the model receives a rich instruction: "the visual content that was here in frame 0 should be here in frame `n`." The instruction is self-contained; the model does not need to retrieve appearance information from elsewhere, because the feature vector *is* the appearance information.

This is why Table 4 shows a PSNR jump from 16.1 (random track embedding) to 17.8 (latent feature replication) — a 1.7 dB improvement in reconstruction fidelity, which is substantial in image quality terms. The random track embedding achieves reasonable motion accuracy (EPE 2.7 vs. 2.6 for latent replication) because even a positional tag can guide coarse displacement, but it cannot preserve fine texture alignment, leading to blurrier, less faithful output. Figure 8 visualizes this: the latent replication preserves the texture of the bread as it moves, while the random embedding produces smeared, inconsistent texture.

This insight has implications beyond point-trajectory control. It suggests that for any conditioning signal where the goal is to specify *where existing appearance elements should appear in the future*, the most effective representation is the appearance element itself — not an abstract reference to it. Optical flow methods [2, 11, 3] implicitly do this by warping pixel values, but at the cost of requiring flow estimation and accumulating errors. Wan-Move's approach achieves a similar effect — specifying spatial correspondence through feature transport — without computing dense correspondences at inference time. The sparsity of point trajectories keeps the specification lightweight (only tracked points need replication), while the richness of the replicated features provides dense-appearance guidance at those points.

This is an **incremental refinement shifted to a different representational regime** — moving from "learned positional embeddings in pixel space" to "copied appearance features in latent space" — but the practical consequences are substantial enough to justify elevation to a key insight. It changes the motion guidance from a sparse, context-free signal to a sparse-but-appearance-rich signal, and the empirical gains in visual quality (PSNR, SSIM, FVD) across all tables confirm that this distinction matters for practical video generation quality.

### Innovation 3: Trajectory Density Generalization as an Emergent Property of Sampling-Based Training

A finding that emerges from the ablation studies rather than being explicitly claimed as a contribution is that Wan-Move's training strategy — uniformly sampling 1–200 trajectories per video, rather than the dense-to-sparse two-stage pipeline common in prior work [14, 15, 2, 58] — produces a model that **generalizes to trajectory counts far beyond its training distribution.** Table 7 shows that at inference time, providing 1024 trajectories (the maximum extractable by CoTracker from a 32×32 grid) yields the best overall performance: EPE drops to 1.1, FVD to 45.2, PSNR rises to 21.9 — all substantially better than the training-time maximum of 200 trajectories (EPE 2.6, FVD 83.5, PSNR 17.8).

This is not guaranteed by the training procedure. The model was trained with at most 200 trajectories; at inference, it receives 5× that many and performs better, not worse. The paper attributes this to "strong generalization capability," but the underlying mechanism is worth examining: the model learns to interpret replicated features as motion instructions *per individual trajectory*, not as a holistic "motion field." Each trajectory independently copies a feature vector from `p̃[0]` to `p̃[n]`; the DiT backbone processes all these replicated features simultaneously through self-attention, which allows it to reconcile potentially conflicting or redundant signals. When more trajectories are provided, the model receives more constraints — a denser sampling of the motion field — and these constraints are individually interpretable because the feature replication operation is identical regardless of how many trajectories there are.

This is significant because it eliminates the need for staged training. Prior work uses a dense-to-sparse pipeline because dense tracks (e.g., optical flow from every pixel) provide strong supervision but are unavailable or expensive at inference time; the second sparse-training stage teaches the model to interpolate. Wan-Move's finding that single-stage random sampling works equally well (Table 12) and generalizes upward to higher densities suggests that the model does not need explicit dense supervision to learn motion smoothness — the self-attention mechanism, combined with the shared VAE feature space, naturally interpolates between sparse constraints in a way that respects visual continuity.

This is a **negative result with positive practical implications** — the simpler training strategy works, and works better than expected — but it is more than an ablation win. It reveals something about the interaction between trajectory-based conditioning and transformer architectures: when each trajectory carries its own rich appearance context (via replicated VAE features), self-attention can perform implicit interpolation without needing explicit dense supervision. This insight could inform future conditioning strategies beyond motion control (e.g., sparse keypoint animation, partial-view reconstruction) where dense ground-truth annotations are expensive to obtain.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All quantitative experiments use two primary evaluation sets: (1) **MoveBench**, the paper's newly introduced benchmark of 1,018 videos at 480×832 resolution spanning 5 seconds (81 frames), curated from Pexels [75] across 54 content categories with human+SAM-verified motion annotations including point trajectories and segmentation masks, and (2) **DAVIS** [21], a public video object segmentation dataset with 50 videos (35–100 frames), used as an additional reference point. The authors explicitly motivate MoveBench by contrasting it with existing benchmarks in Figure 5: DAVIS provides only 50 short clips, VIPSeg [22] offers 343 videos but only 24 frames each, and MagicBench [20] includes 600 videos but relies on noisy automatic labels with coarse categorization. MoveBench's combination of larger volume, longer duration, diverse content, and hybrid-verified annotations is presented as filling a critical evaluation infrastructure gap.

- **Base model(s).** The primary experiments use **Wan-I2V-14B** [19], a state-of-the-art open-source image-to-video diffusion transformer with 14 billion parameters. For fairness comparisons controlling for backbone and data scale, the paper also trains two variants using **CogVideoX1.5-5B-I2V** [52]: Wan-Move-Cog-23K (trained on 23K samples, matching MagicMotion's data scale) and Wan-Move-Cog-630K (trained on 630K samples, matching Tora's data scale). The choice of Wan-I2V-14B as the primary backbone is motivated by it being "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 5.1) — meaning it sits in a useful performance regime where motion control can provide meaningful gains — and because its open-source availability enables full fine-tuning and reproduction.

- **Metrics.** The paper evaluates along two axes: **video quality** and **motion accuracy**. For video quality, the standard suite is used: **FID** [80] (Fréchet Inception Distance, measuring distributional similarity between generated and real video features), **FVD** [81] (Fréchet Video Distance, extending FID to spatiotemporal features), **PSNR** (peak signal-to-noise ratio, measuring per-frame reconstruction fidelity), and **SSIM** [82] (structural similarity index, measuring perceptual frame quality). For motion accuracy, the primary metric is **EPE** (end-point error), defined as the L2 distance between ground-truth point tracks and tracks estimated from generated videos using CoTracker [73], following the convention established by Motion Prompting [13]. All evaluations are performed at 480p resolution. The paper does not report confidence intervals, statistical significance tests, or variance across multiple runs for any metric — all tables present single scalar values, which limits the ability to assess whether observed differences exceed experimental noise.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five methods spanning the space of motion-control approaches:
  - **ImageConductor** [58]: injects pixel-level trajectory features directly, representing the simplest (and weakest) point-track baseline.
  - **LeviTor** [16]: uses 3D trajectory representation with ControlNet-based fusion.
  - **Tora** [14]: trajectory-oriented diffusion transformer using lightweight adaLN injection rather than ControlNet.
  - **MagicMotion** [20]: dense-to-sparse trajectory guidance with ControlNet, also accepting sparse masks and bounding boxes as input.
  - **Kling 1.5 Pro** (Motion Brush) [4]: a commercial system from Kuaishou, included only in the human study (Table 3) and qualitative comparisons (Figure 6), not in quantitative metrics (since its internal representations are inaccessible for computing FID/FVD/EPE).
  The selection covers both academic SOTA methods (LeviTor, Tora, MagicMotion) and the relevant commercial frontier (Kling 1.5 Pro), though notably absent is a direct comparison to Motion Prompting [13] — the most architecturally similar prior work — in the main tables. Motion Prompting is discussed qualitatively in Section 2 and ablated against in the guidance strategy study (Table 4, "Random track embedding" adapted from Motion Prompting's approach), but no head-to-head quantitative comparison on MoveBench is reported.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not define a unified compute budget metric (comparable to "generations" in the LLM test-time compute paper). Instead, comparisons are made at the fixed output specification all methods share: 5-second, 480p videos at 81 frames. Inference latency is reported in Table 5 as a secondary efficiency metric (765s base model + 3s trajectory processing for Wan-Move vs. 987s for ControlNet-based approaches). Training compute is reported in Appendix 8.3: 30,000 steps on 64 NVIDIA A100 GPUs using FSDP with sequence parallel size 4 — a substantial but not prohibitive budget for a 14B model. The paper does not explicitly compare total training FLOPs against baselines, which matters because methods with auxiliary modules (ControlNet branches) would consume more GPU memory per step and potentially require smaller batch sizes, changing the effective sample efficiency.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not apply cross-validation, statistical testing, or multiple random seeds to any quantitative evaluation. The human study (Table 3) uses 2AFC (two-alternative forced choice) with 50 samples per method evaluated by 20 participants, but no inter-annotator agreement metrics (e.g., Cohen's kappa, Fleiss' kappa) are reported. The ablation studies (Tables 4–9) report single-run results without error bars. For a paper that introduces a new benchmark (MoveBench) and makes strong claims about commercial-grade quality, the absence of any statistical rigor is a meaningful limitation — we cannot determine whether a 0.1 difference in EPE or a 2-point difference in FVD is statistically significant or within run-to-run variance.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Single-Object Motion Control on MoveBench and DAVIS

The headline result from Table 1 is that **Wan-Move achieves the best performance across all five metrics on both MoveBench and DAVIS**, with particularly large margins in video quality metrics. On MoveBench:

- **FID**: Wan-Move achieves 12.2, compared to the next-best of 17.5 (MagicMotion) — a 5.3-point advantage indicating substantially better distributional match to real video features.
- **FVD**: Wan-Move achieves 83.5, vs. 96.7 (MagicMotion) and 98.8 (LeviTor) — FVD captures spatiotemporal coherence, so the 13–15 point gap suggests Wan-Move generates more temporally consistent motion.
- **PSNR**: Wan-Move reaches 17.8, vs. the 15.7–14.9 range for other methods — a ~2 dB advantage in per-frame reconstruction quality.
- **SSIM**: Wan-Move achieves 0.64, compared to 0.49–0.56 for baselines — nearly a 0.1 absolute improvement in structural similarity, which is substantial.
- **EPE**: Wan-Move records 2.6, vs. the 3.2–3.4 range for LeviTor, Tora, and MagicMotion. The gap (~0.6–0.8 pixels in L2 distance) indicates noticeably better point trajectory adherence. ImageConductor's EPE of 15.6 confirms that naive pixel-level trajectory injection fails dramatically.

On DAVIS, the pattern replicates consistently: Wan-Move attains FVD 94.3 (vs. 113.4–129.2 for baselines), PSNR 16.5 (vs. 12.8–13.7), SSIM 0.61 (vs. 0.49–0.53), and EPE 2.5 (vs. 3.5–3.7). The DAVIS results are notable because DAVIS was not part of the training distribution — the training data came from Panda70M, Pixabay, Pexels, and YouTube (Table 10), none of which overlap with the DAVIS benchmark. Strong DAVIS performance therefore provides evidence of generalization beyond the training data domain, though DAVIS's small size (50 videos) limits the strength of this conclusion.

**What Table 1 does NOT show:** the performance gap between Wan-Move and the best baselines is larger in video quality metrics (FID, FVD, PSNR, SSIM) than in motion accuracy (EPE). The EPE improvement over MagicMotion is 2.6 vs. 3.2 — a 19% relative reduction. The FVD improvement over MagicMotion is 83.5 vs. 96.7 — a 14% relative reduction. The PSNR improvement is 17.8 vs. 14.9 — a 19% relative increase. All are meaningful, but the paper's central claim is about *motion control*, not video quality. The fact that the largest gains appear in video quality rather than pure motion accuracy suggests that latent feature replication may benefit overall generation fidelity through mechanisms beyond trajectory adherence — for instance, the replicated features might serve as stronger spatial anchors that improve the model's ability to maintain consistent textures and object boundaries across frames, even in non-tracked regions.

**How does this compare to the "rivals commercial quality" claim?** The quantitative metrics cannot directly validate the claim that Wan-Move "rivals Kling 1.5 Pro's Motion Brush" because Kling's internal representations are inaccessible. The human study in Table 3 is the sole empirical support for this claim: Wan-Move achieves win rates of 47.8% (motion accuracy), 53.4% (motion quality), and 50.2% (visual quality) against Kling 1.5 Pro. These are essentially at parity — a 50% win rate would be a perfect tie. However, the human study uses only 50 samples, evaluated by 20 participants. With 50 samples, the standard error on a proportion near 0.5 is approximately `sqrt(0.5 × 0.5 / 50) ≈ 0.07`, meaning the 95% confidence interval for the true win rate spans roughly 34–66%. So while the point estimate suggests parity, the sample size is too small to distinguish "Wan-Move is exactly as good as Kling" from "Wan-Move is modestly worse" or "Wan-Move is modestly better." The claim of commercial-grade quality is directionally supported but not rigorously established.

Additionally, Kling 1.5 Pro is evaluated only qualitatively (Figure 6) and in the human study — it is not included in Table 1 or any quantitative metric table. The paper cannot claim quantitative superiority over the commercial baseline on FVD/PSNR/EPE because those numbers are not computable for a closed system.

#### Multi-Object Motion Control

Table 2 evaluates the more challenging setting of multi-object motion control, using the 192 videos in MoveBench that contain annotations for multiple moving objects. The baselines are reduced to ImageConductor and Tora (LeviTor and MagicMotion are not evaluated on this subset, for reasons the paper does not explain — possibly because they do not natively support multi-trajectory input, or because their authors did not provide multi-object evaluation results).

The results show that **Wan-Move substantially outperforms both baselines, with larger relative margins than in the single-object setting:**

- **FVD**: Wan-Move achieves 226.3, vs. 350.0 (Tora) and 764.5 (ImageConductor). The 124-point gap over Tora is proportionally larger than the 17-point gap in the full MoveBench evaluation (Table 1), suggesting that Wan-Move's advantages become more pronounced as scene complexity increases.
- **EPE**: Wan-Move achieves 2.2, vs. 3.5 (Tora) and 9.8 (ImageConductor). The motion accuracy gap widens — from 0.7 EPE advantage over Tora in single-object (2.6 vs. 3.3) to 1.3 EPE advantage in multi-object (2.2 vs. 3.5) — indicating that Wan-Move's trajectory guidance scales better to multiple simultaneous constraints.

**Why might multi-object be a harder test for other methods but not for Wan-Move?** Methods that use learned motion embeddings (Tora, ImageConductor) must disentangle multiple overlapping trajectory signals during encoding. When two trajectories cross or coincide, a learned encoder may blend them, producing ambiguous guidance. Wan-Move's approach of randomly selecting one trajectory's feature at overlapping positions (Table 11) provides a deterministic, spatially-local resolution that does not require the model to disambiguate blended signals — only one feature occupies each latent grid position, and self-attention can resolve any resulting conflicts using surrounding context. The paper does not explicitly analyze this mechanism, but the empirical pattern is consistent with this interpretation.

#### Sparse Signal Control (Bounding Boxes)

Figure 7 and the associated text (Section 5.2) report a qualitative comparison with MagicMotion [20] using sparse motion signals — specifically, bounding boxes derived from MoveBench's segmentation mask annotations. This is noteworthy because Wan-Move is fundamentally a point-trajectory method (dense, fine-grained signals), yet it can accept bounding boxes as input. The paper does not describe how bounding boxes are converted to point trajectories for Wan-Move (presumably by sampling points within the box and interpolating trajectories between keyframes, or by using CoTracker to densify the box annotations).

The qualitative results in Figure 7 show that MagicMotion produces visible motion control failures (marked with red dots) where objects deviate from the specified bounding box trajectories, while Wan-Move maintains tighter adherence. However, no quantitative metrics are reported for this comparison — no EPE, FVD, or SSIM values are provided for the box-input setting. This limits the strength of any claim about Wan-Move's superiority for sparse-signal control. The paper's primary strength is clearly in dense point-trajectory control; the box-input capability is demonstrated as a flexibility feature rather than a core advantage.

#### Human Study

Table 3 presents the 2AFC human evaluation comparing Wan-Move against four methods (LeviTor, Tora, MagicMotion, and Kling 1.5 Pro) on three criteria: motion accuracy, motion quality, and visual quality. 50 samples per method, 20 participants.

**Against academic methods**, Wan-Move's win rates are overwhelmingly positive:
- vs. LeviTor: 98.2% (motion accuracy), 98.0% (motion quality), 98.8% (visual quality)
- vs. Tora: 96.2% (motion accuracy), 93.8% (motion quality), 98.4% (visual quality)
- vs. MagicMotion: 89.4% (motion accuracy), 96.4% (motion quality), 98.2% (visual quality)

The lowest win rate (89.4% against MagicMotion on motion accuracy) still represents a decisive preference. These results are consistent with the quantitative metrics in Table 1 — Wan-Move is consistently preferred by human evaluators across all dimensions.

**Against Kling 1.5 Pro**, the results are near-parity: 47.8%, 53.4%, and 50.2%. However, several methodological concerns apply:
- **Sample size**: 50 samples per method pair is small. A difference of 2–3 percentage points from 50% is within the noise floor.
- **Sample selection**: the paper does not describe how the 50 samples were selected. If they were cherry-picked from MoveBench (e.g., choosing videos where Wan-Move performs well), the win rates could be inflated. A random or stratified sample from MoveBench would be more defensible.
- **Task framing**: the evaluation criteria ("motion accuracy," "motion quality," "visual quality") are defined but not operationalized in the paper — we don't know what instructions were given to participants or whether they were calibrated with examples.
- **Evaluator expertise**: the 20 participants are not characterized (e.g., are they researchers, crowd workers, artists?). Evaluator expertise can substantially affect aesthetic and motion quality judgments.
- **Kling 1.5 Pro configuration**: we don't know whether Kling was used optimally — what prompt format, what Motion Brush parameters, whether multiple attempts were made and the best selected. If Kling was used in a default or suboptimal configuration, the comparison is unfair to the commercial baseline.

Despite these limitations, the near-50% win rates against Kling 1.5 Pro are directionally impressive. Even with generous confidence intervals, it is unlikely that the true win rate is substantially below 40% or above 60%, which means Wan-Move is at least in the same quality tier as a commercial system. For an open-source research model, this is noteworthy.

#### Large-Motion and Out-of-Distribution Motion Scenarios

Table 9 evaluates robustness on two challenging subsets of MoveBench: **large-motion videos** (top 20% by optical flow magnitude, computed as the average of the top 2% largest optical flow values extracted by RAFT [83]) and **out-of-distribution motion videos** (50 manually curated cases including "complex foreground–background interactions, objects moving out of frame, and rare camera motions").

On both subsets, Wan-Move maintains its advantage over Tora and MagicMotion, and crucially, the **performance gap widens compared to the full MoveBench results:**

- **Large-motion subset**: Wan-Move achieves FVD 86.6 vs. 119.3 (MagicMotion) — a 32.7-point gap, compared to 13.2 on the full benchmark (83.5 vs. 96.7). EPE: 3.0 vs. 4.1 — a 1.1-point gap, compared to 0.6 on the full benchmark (2.6 vs. 3.2).
- **OOD subset**: Wan-Move achieves FVD 86.0 vs. 115.7 (MagicMotion) — a 29.7-point gap. EPE: 2.8 vs. 3.9 — a 1.1-point gap.

Additionally, Wan-Move's performance on these challenging subsets degrades only modestly from its full-benchmark results (FVD: 86.6 on large-motion vs. 83.5 overall; EPE: 3.0 vs. 2.6). By contrast, MagicMotion degrades more sharply (FVD: 119.3 on large-motion vs. 96.7 overall). This pattern — widening gaps under distribution shift — is consistent with the interpretation that Wan-Move's latent feature replication provides more robust guidance than learned motion encoders when the motion patterns deviate from the training distribution.

**Why might this be?** A learned motion encoder (like MagicMotion's ControlNet branch) learns a mapping from motion specification → conditioning features. If the test-time motion is outside the training distribution (unusually large displacements, complex interactions, rare camera paths), the encoder's mapping may produce features that are unfamiliar to the DiT backbone, causing artifacts or loss of control. Wan-Move's latent feature replication does not learn any such mapping — it deterministically copies VAE features. The features at the trajectory destination are always "in-distribution" for the DiT backbone because they are literal copies of features from the same video, just at different spatial positions. The only thing the DiT backbone needs to learn is *how to interpret copied features as motion instructions*, which appears to generalize more robustly than learning an encoder mapping.

**Caveat on OOD subset construction**: the 50 OOD cases are manually curated, making them potentially subject to curator bias. The paper does not describe the curation criteria in enough detail to assess whether the OOD subset systematically favors Wan-Move (e.g., by selecting motion types where point trajectories are naturally informative but bounding boxes or optical flow would be ambiguous). The large-motion subset, by contrast, is automatically selected via optical flow magnitude, making it more objective — and the results on this subset are similarly strong.

#### Backbone and Data Scale Fairness (Table 8)

Table 8 addresses a potential confound in the main comparisons: Wan-Move uses a 14B backbone (Wan-I2V-14B) trained on 2M videos, while Tora and MagicMotion use a 5B backbone (CogVideoX-5B) trained on 630K and 23K videos respectively. To isolate the method from the backbone and data advantage, the paper trains two Wan-Move variants using the same CogVideoX-5B backbone and comparable data scales:

- **Wan-Move-Cog-23K** (23K videos, matching MagicMotion's data scale): achieves FVD 92.3 vs. MagicMotion's 96.7, and EPE 2.8 vs. 3.2.
- **Wan-Move-Cog-630K** (630K videos, matching Tora's data scale): achieves FVD 87.3 vs. Tora's 100.4, and EPE 2.8 vs. 3.3.

Crucially, Wan-Move-Cog-630K with a 5B backbone achieves EPE 2.8 — which is close to the full Wan-Move's 2.6 with a 14B backbone — suggesting that the method's motion accuracy is not heavily dependent on backbone scale. The video quality metrics (FVD, PSNR, SSIM) do improve with the larger backbone (FVD: 87.3 → 83.5; PSNR: 17.2 → 17.8), which is expected since a larger DiT generates higher-fidelity frames regardless of the control mechanism.

**What this establishes**: Wan-Move's advantage over Tora and MagicMotion is not solely attributable to using a larger backbone or more training data. Under matched conditions, Wan-Move still outperforms both methods. This is the strongest evidence in the paper that latent feature replication — the method — rather than the scale — the compute budget — drives the improvements.

**What it does not establish**: whether Wan-Move's advantage persists at even larger scales (e.g., if Tora or MagicMotion were trained on a 14B backbone with 2M videos). The paper does not train baseline methods at the full Wan-Move scale, so we cannot determine whether the architectural advantage (no motion encoder) compounds with scale or whether learned motion encoders catch up given sufficient capacity and data.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

Each ablation addresses a specific design choice in Wan-Move. The results are reported in Tables 4–12 and Appendix Tables 11–12.

- **Trajectory guidance strategy (Table 4, Figure 8):** Comparing three methods of injecting motion guidance into latent condition features. **Pixel replication** (copy-pasting pixel values along trajectories, then VAE-encoding the result) achieves EPE 3.7 and PSNR 15.3 — the worst performance among the three. **Random track embedding** (adapting Motion Prompting's [13] approach by assigning randomly initialized latent embeddings to trajectory positions) achieves EPE 2.7 and PSNR 16.1 — improved motion accuracy but still subpar visual quality. **Latent feature replication** (Wan-Move's method) achieves EPE 2.6 and PSNR 17.8 — the best on both axes. Figure 8 visualizes the qualitative difference: pixel replication produces severe artifacts where features are misaligned; random track embeddings cause stiff, textureless motion around tracked points (the bread does not move naturally with the hand); latent replication preserves natural texture and coherent motion. This ablation validates that using real VAE features (not learned or pixel values) is the active ingredient in Wan-Move's approach.

- **Condition fusion strategy (Table 5):** Comparing **ControlNet** (a separate network branch processing motion conditions, following [18]) against **direct concatenation** (Wan-Move's approach). ControlNet achieves EPE 2.5 (marginally better than Wan-Move's 2.6) and FVD 84.6 (comparable to 83.5). However, **inference latency** differs dramatically: ControlNet adds 225 seconds to the base model's 765 seconds (total 987s), while Wan-Move adds only 3 seconds (total 768s). The inference time numbers (765s base, 987s with ControlNet, 768s with Wan-Move) are not normalized by hardware configuration or batch size, making absolute comparisons difficult, but the relative magnitude of the overhead (225s vs. 3s) is the key takeaway. This is a strong practical argument for Wan-Move's approach: equivalent motion quality at a fraction of the inference cost.

- **Maximum number of training trajectories (Table 6):** Sweeping the maximum `N` in the uniform sampling range `{1, ..., N}` during training. Optimal performance occurs at `N = 200` (EPE 2.6, SSIM 0.64). At `N = 10` (too few trajectories), EPE rises to 3.3, indicating insufficient motion supervision. At `N = 1024` (very dense trajectories), EPE rises to 3.9, which the paper attributes to "mismatch between the dense point tracks in the training and the sparse point tracks during evaluation." This is a classic distribution-shift effect: training with 1024 tracks teaches the model to expect very dense constraints, but MoveBench evaluation uses sparser tracks; the model becomes reliant on dense guidance and performs poorly when it is absent. The non-monotonic relationship between training trajectory density and evaluation performance is a useful practical finding — more is not always better.

- **Number of trajectories at inference (Table 7):** Sweeping the number of point trajectories provided at inference time. As trajectories increase from 0 to 1024, EPE drops monotonically from 12.4 (no control) to 1.1 (dense control). FVD improves from 87.9 to 45.2. PSNR improves from 17.9 to 21.9. Notably, the model generalizes to 1024 trajectories at inference despite being trained with a maximum of 200. The paper does not explain *why* this generalization works, but we can hypothesize: each trajectory independently copies a VAE feature vector; the DiT's self-attention can process arbitrarily many such constraints as long as they fit within the sequence length; and the feature vectors themselves are in-distribution (they are real VAE features from the first frame). This generalization is a significant practical advantage — users can provide dense CoTracker trajectories for maximum control without needing the model to have seen equally dense trajectories during training.

- **Feature replication under trajectory overlap (Table 11, Appendix 9.1):** When multiple trajectories coincide at the same latent position, comparing **feature averaging** vs. **random selection**. Random selection achieves EPE 2.6 vs. averaging's 2.7, and PSNR 17.8 vs. 17.5. The PSNR degradation from averaging suggests that the linear combination of two distinct VAE feature vectors produces a feature that does not correspond to any natural image patch, introducing reconstruction artifacts. Random selection avoids this by ensuring the feature at each position is a valid VAE encoding of some real image content.

- **Dense-to-sampling vs. direct sampling training (Table 12, Appendix 9.2):** Comparing the two-stage "dense-to-sampling" strategy used in prior work [14, 15, 2, 58] against Wan-Move's single-stage uniform sampling. Direct sampling achieves EPE 2.6 (identical to dense-to-sampling) and FVD 83.5 (vs. 84.2 for dense-to-sampling, a slight improvement). This is framed as a simplification: Wan-Move achieves comparable or better results with a simpler training pipeline. However, the dense-to-sampling baseline is reimplemented by the authors (not reported from the original papers), so we cannot rule out that a more carefully tuned two-stage pipeline would outperform.

- **Model performance under trajectory disappearance (Appendix 9.3, Figure 12):** A qualitative robustness check showing that Wan-Move maintains motion coherence when trajectories temporarily become occluded. A trajectory tracking an arm becomes invisible as it passes behind the body, then re-emerges — the generated video shows natural continuation rather than jerky re-acquisition. The paper attributes this to similar cases in training data and model generalization. No quantitative metric is reported for this scenario, so the claim is illustrative rather than rigorously established.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim: Wan-Move achieves precise, fine-grained motion control ("motion controllability rivals Kling 1.5 Pro's Motion Brush")

**What the experiments show**: On MoveBench, Wan-Move achieves the lowest EPE among all academic methods (2.6 vs. 3.2–3.4 for competitors; Table 1), and this advantage grows on multi-object (Table 2), large-motion, and OOD subsets (Table 9). The human study (Table 3) shows win rates of ~50% against Kling 1.5 Pro, with academic baselines heavily disfavored (89–98% win rates for Wan-Move). Qualitative comparisons (Figures 6, 14) show visibly better trajectory adherence and fewer motion artifacts.

**What is not tested**: The EPE metric measures point trajectory accuracy only at the tracked points. It does not measure whether *untracked* regions move naturally, whether object boundaries remain crisp during motion, or whether physical constraints (rigidity, occlusion ordering, contact dynamics) are respected. A model could achieve low EPE by perfectly matching the tracked points while producing unnatural distortions elsewhere. The paper's SSIM and PSNR metrics partially address this (they measure full-frame quality), but they are global averages that may mask local artifacts. A more targeted metric — e.g., tracking error on randomly sampled points that were *not* part of the control signal — would test whether the model genuinely learns coherent motion or merely overfits to the provided trajectories.

**The Kling comparison**: The human study sample (50 samples, 20 participants) is too small to distinguish a true 50% win rate from a 40% or 60% win rate with confidence. Additionally, the Kling comparison is purely qualitative — no quantitative metrics are computed because Kling is a closed system. The claim of "commercial-grade" quality is therefore directionally supported but not rigorously established. A stronger design would include a larger-scale human study (200+ samples, 50+ evaluators), a third-party evaluation service, or a quantitative proxy for Kling performance (e.g., using CoTracker to extract and compare trajectories from Kling-generated videos, though this raises its own validity questions).

**The comparison to Motion Prompting [13]**: The most architecturally similar prior work — which also uses point trajectories — is not included in the main quantitative comparisons. The paper adapts Motion Prompting's "random track embedding" approach for the ablation in Table 4, but this is a reimplementation of one component, not a head-to-head comparison of the full systems. Since Motion Prompting [13] is the most direct predecessor, its absence from Table 1 is a notable gap. It is possible that Motion Prompting's full pipeline (which includes ControlNet and a specific trajectory encoding scheme) would outperform the simplified random embedding ablation — the paper does not test this.

#### Claim: "No extra modules" enables scalable fine-tuning and fast convergence ("adding no new modules and allowing the easy fine-tuning of base models at scale")

**What the experiments show**: Table 5 demonstrates that Wan-Move adds 3 seconds of inference overhead vs. 225 seconds for ControlNet. Table 8 shows that Wan-Move's method outperforms baselines even when controlling for backbone size and data scale (CogVideoX-5B, matched data volumes). The training configuration (Appendix 8.3) confirms that standard distributed training strategies (FSDP, Ulysses sequence parallelism) are used without modification, consistent with the "no architectural changes" claim.

**What is not tested**: The paper does not report training throughput (samples/second), GPU memory consumption, or convergence speed (steps to reach a given EPE) relative to baselines. The claim of "easy scalable fine-tuning" is supported by the architectural argument (no new parameters, standard parallelism) but not by direct throughput comparisons. A ControlNet-based method might require more GPU memory per step (due to the additional parameters), forcing a smaller batch size and potentially more total steps — but the paper does not measure this. Additionally, the paper does not demonstrate that Wan-Move *requires* fewer training steps to converge than a ControlNet-based approach — the 30,000-step training budget is reported, but comparable convergence data for baselines is not provided. The "fast convergence" claim (from Section 1, paragraph on contributions) is not directly tested.

#### Claim: Latent feature replication outperforms pixel-space track embeddings and other guidance strategies

**What the experiments show**: Table 4 clearly demonstrates that latent feature replication (EPE 2.6) outperforms pixel replication (EPE 3.7) and random track embedding (EPE 2.7). The PSNR gap (17.8 vs. 16.1 vs. 15.3) is even more pronounced than the EPE gap, indicating that the advantage is primarily in visual quality rather than pure motion accuracy. Figure 8 provides qualitative corroboration.

**What is not tested**: The paper does not ablate *what properties* of latent VAE features drive the improvement. Is it the channel dimensionality (VAE features have C channels vs. scalar pixel values)? The semantic content (VAE features encode high-level structure)? The spatial context (VAE features have a receptive field covering a neighborhood of pixels)? Understanding which properties matter would inform whether the approach generalizes to other VAE architectures (with different channel counts, compression ratios, or training objectives). The paper also does not test an intermediate approach — e.g., replicating a local neighborhood of pixels (not just a single pixel, as in pixel replication) to see whether spatial context alone drives the improvement, or whether the VAE's learned feature space is necessary.

Additionally, the "random track embedding" ablation does not exactly replicate Motion Prompting [13] — it adapts the idea of using learned embeddings for trajectory positions but in latent space rather than pixel space. The original Motion Prompting uses a ControlNet to process these embeddings and inject them into the base model; the ablation in Table 4 appears to use a simpler injection mechanism. This means the ablation establishes that *learned embeddings alone* (without the full Motion Prompting pipeline) are insufficient, but it does not fully isolate the contribution of Wan-Move's feature replication from the contribution of using the VAE's latent space.

#### Claim: Wan-Move generalizes to diverse control modalities (single-object, multi-object, camera, transfer, 3D rotation)

**What the experiments show**: Figure 1 and Figures 14–18 demonstrate qualitatively that Wan-Move can handle these modalities. The paper describes the pipeline for each modality (Section 5.4, Appendix 10): camera control uses Depth Anything V2 + trajectory projection; motion transfer uses CoTracker on reference video + Wan-Move on new image; 3D rotation uses depth estimation + 3D transform + 2D projection. No quantitative metrics are reported for any of these modalities individually.

**What is not tested**: Are all modalities equally well-controlled? Intuitively, motion transfer might be harder than drag-based control because the transferred trajectories come from a different video and may not align well with the new image's content. Camera control depends on the quality of the monocular depth estimate — if Depth Anything V2 produces inaccurate depth, the projected trajectories will be physically inconsistent, and the model might produce distorted output. The paper does not quantitatively evaluate failure rates or quality degradation for different modalities. A table reporting per-modality EPE/FVD/PSNR on MoveBench subsets (or on task-specific test sets) would substantially strengthen this claim.

#### Methodological weaknesses that apply across claims

1. **No error bars or significance testing**: Every quantitative table reports single scalar values without confidence intervals, standard deviations, or p-values. With a 1,018-video benchmark, random variation across videos could easily produce 0.1–0.3 EPE differences or 1–3 point FVD differences. Without variance estimates, we cannot determine whether Wan-Move's advantage over MagicMotion (EPE 2.6 vs. 3.2) is robust or within noise. This is the most significant methodological weakness.

2. **Single model family for the main results**: All primary results use Wan-I2V-14B as the backbone, with CogVideoX-5B variants used only for fairness comparisons in Table 8. The claim that latent trajectory guidance is generally effective (not specific to Wan's architecture) is supported by the CogVideoX results, but only two backbones are tested. A broader evaluation across, e.g., Stable Video Diffusion [68], AnimateDiff, or other I2V architectures would strengthen the generality claim.

3. **Benefit over baselines is larger for video quality than motion accuracy**: Across Tables 1, 2, 8, and 9, the relative advantage in FID/FVD/PSNR/SSIM is proportionally larger than the advantage in EPE. This raises a question: is Wan-Move primarily a *better video generator* (which happens to have motion control), or is it genuinely *better at motion control*? The two are correlated — better video quality may result from better motion consistency — but the paper frames its contribution as motion control, not video quality improvement. A stronger case for the motion-control claim would require an evaluation that isolates motion fidelity from overall visual quality, such as motion-only perceptual metrics or a human study that asks evaluators to judge "does the motion follow the specification?" while ignoring visual artifacts.

4. **MoveBench curation may favor Wan-Move**: MoveBench is curated from Pexels using CoTracker for trajectory extraction. Since Wan-Move is trained with CoTracker-extracted trajectories (from Panda70M, Pixabay, Pexels, YouTube), the evaluation distribution (CoTracker trajectories on Pexels videos) is similar to the training distribution. A benchmark using a different tracker (e.g., TAPIR, PIPs) or a different video source might produce different relative rankings. The paper does not evaluate on a benchmark with human-annotated trajectories (which would be expensive but more independent of the training pipeline).

5. **Missing ablation: trajectory temporal density**: The paper ablates the number of trajectories (Tables 6, 7), but not the number of frames over which trajectories are specified. All experiments use 81-frame trajectories (full 5-second duration). What if only keyframes are specified (e.g., every 5th frame) and the model must interpolate? This is a practical use case (users might not want to specify every frame) and would test whether the model truly learns continuous motion or merely memorizes frame-by-frame correspondences.

6. **No evaluation of failure cases at scale**: Appendix 9.4 (Figure 13) identifies three failure modes qualitatively, but no quantitative analysis of failure rates is provided. What fraction of MoveBench videos exhibit each failure mode? Does Wan-Move fail more or less often than baselines on each failure mode? A systematic failure analysis would help practitioners understand when to use Wan-Move and when to use alternative approaches.

Despite these weaknesses, the experimental evidence collectively supports the paper's central thesis: **directly editing the condition latent with replicated VAE features is an effective mechanism for motion-controllable I2V generation, and it achieves this without the architectural overhead that prior methods require.** The improvements over baselines are consistent across metrics (FID, FVD, PSNR, SSIM, EPE), datasets (MoveBench, DAVIS), difficulty levels (single-object, multi-object, large-motion, OOD), and controlled conditions (matched backbone and data scale in Table 8). The gains are not uniformly large — the EPE improvement over the best baseline is 0.6, while the FVD improvement is 13 points — but they are directionally consistent and appear across all comparisons. The Kling-parity claim is the weakest link, supported by an underpowered human study, but the academic-comparison claims are well-supported with the caveat of missing statistical rigor.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 Hard Problems Require Capabilities the Base Model Simply Does Not Possess

**The assumption or constraint.** Wan-Move fundamentally operates by guiding where existing first-frame appearance features should move — it does not synthesize new visual content that was never present in the input image. This means the method can only control motion for scene elements that are already visible and whose appearance can be propagated forward. The paper acknowledges this constraint implicitly in Section 6 (Limitations): "Wan-Move uses point trajectories to guide motion, which can be unreliable when tracks are missing due to occlusion," and more explicitly in Appendix 9.4 (Figure 13), which identifies three failure modes including "violating rigorous physical laws" and "visual artifacts under complex and crowded environment." What the paper does not explicitly state — but what the method's design implies — is that Wan-Move cannot generate content that was absent from the first frame. If an object needs to rotate to reveal a previously hidden side, or if a character needs to open their mouth to reveal teeth that were occluded in the first frame, the replicated features will have no information about that hidden content. The model must either hallucinate it (producing artifacts) or fail to generate plausible motion.

**The consequence.** Wan-Move is bounded by the first frame's visible content. This is not a minor edge case — it constrains the class of motions the model can produce. Any motion that involves disocclusion (revealing previously hidden surfaces), object articulation revealing new visual elements, or large out-of-plane rotations will produce degraded quality because the replicated VAE feature at the trajectory origin does not contain the information needed for the future appearance. The paper's own failure case analysis (Appendix 9.4, Figure 13c) shows "motion outputs that violate rigorous physical laws" as a failure mode, which is consistent with the model attempting to generate content beyond what the first frame provides.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not quantitatively measure disocclusion-related failures. The qualitative failure analysis in Appendix 9.4 is illustrative rather than systematic — three examples are shown, but no statistics on failure rates by motion type are provided. The EPE metric used throughout the paper measures trajectory adherence at tracked points, which may remain accurate even as untracked disoccluded regions degrade. A more targeted evaluation — e.g., tracking randomly sampled points on surfaces that become visible only after rotation — would reveal this limitation, but it is not performed.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not propose any mechanism for synthesizing disoccluded content or handling appearance changes beyond what can be propagated from the first frame. This is a fundamental bound on the method's applicability that no amount of additional training data can solve — it is a consequence of representing motion guidance as first-frame feature replication rather than as a learned generative prior over how appearance changes under motion.

---

### 6.2 Difficulty Estimation Is Implicitly Assumed, Not Solved

**The assumption or constraint.** Wan-Move assumes that the point trajectories provided at inference time are accurate and sufficient for the desired motion. But in practice, a user must *specify* those trajectories — either by manually dragging points, by extracting them from a reference video with CoTracker, or by programmatically generating them (for camera control or 3D rotation). Each specification method has failure modes that the paper does not characterize:

- **Manual specification**: A user dragging a single point can only specify translation, not rotation, scaling, or deformation of the tracked object. The paper's "single-object control" examples (Figure 1, row 1) show rigid translation, but many real-world motions (a person turning, a flower blooming, a flag waving) cannot be captured by a single trajectory. The model might produce plausible results for simple translations but fail silently for complex motions where the user's sparse input under-constrains the problem.

- **CoTracker extraction**: CoTracker [73] is used both for training data generation (Section 3.3) and for inference-time trajectory densification (motion transfer, camera control). CoTracker itself has failure modes — it can lose tracks under occlusion, drift on textureless regions, or produce physically inconsistent trajectories when multiple objects interact. The paper acknowledges that "erroneous tracking points identified by CoTracker may compound these failure modes" (Appendix 9.4), but does not measure how often this occurs or how sensitive Wan-Move is to trajectory noise.

- **Programmatic generation for camera control**: The camera control pipeline (Section 5.4) estimates a monocular depth map (Depth Anything V2 [84]), projects it to 3D, applies a camera transformation, and projects back to 2D trajectories. Every step introduces error: monocular depth estimation is inherently ambiguous, the point cloud is sparse (only as dense as the trajectories), and z-buffering for occlusion is approximate. If the depth estimate is wrong, the generated trajectories will not correspond to physically correct motion, and Wan-Move will faithfully reproduce physically incorrect motion — because it has no mechanism to reject or correct implausible trajectories.

**The consequence.** Wan-Move's output quality is bounded by the quality of its input trajectories, but the paper provides no guidance on how to assess trajectory quality before generation, how many trajectories are sufficient for a given motion complexity, or what failure modes to expect from each trajectory specification method. A practitioner deploying Wan-Move would need to develop their own heuristics for trajectory specification — and would likely encounter failures that are attributable not to Wan-Move's generation but to the upstream trajectory pipeline, leading to a debugging challenge where the failure source (specification vs. generation) is unclear.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 7 shows that increasing the number of trajectories from 1 to 1024 monotonically improves all metrics — EPE drops from 2.6 to 1.1, FVD drops from 83.5 to 45.2, PSNR rises from 17.8 to 21.9. This demonstrates that trajectory density strongly affects output quality, but it does not characterize the *quality* of trajectories — only their quantity. A study that adds controlled noise to ground-truth trajectories and measures EPE degradation would reveal sensitivity to trajectory accuracy, but this is not performed. The OOD evaluation (Table 9) partially addresses the robustness question by testing on unusual motions, but the trajectories for those motions are still derived from ground-truth CoTracker extractions — the trajectories themselves are not OOD, only the motion patterns are.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper states that "short-term occlusions can be recovered once the point reappears" (Section 6, Limitations), but this refers to trajectory *disappearance* (the model generalizing over gaps), not trajectory *inaccuracy* (the model receiving incorrect positions). There is no mechanism to validate, refine, or reject input trajectories. This is a practical deployment barrier — the method assumes high-quality trajectories as input but provides no tools for producing or verifying them.

---

### 6.3 Persistent Trajectory Occlusion Causes Loss of Control With No Recovery Mechanism

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper applies feature replication "only along the visible trajectories" (Section 3.3, Training), meaning that when a tracked point becomes occluded — behind another object, outside the frame boundary, or invisible due to lighting — the model receives no explicit guidance for that point during the occluded frames. The paper notes in Section 6: "prolonged absence may lead to loss of control." This is not a theoretical concern — in any video involving object interactions, articulated bodies, or camera motion around a scene, some tracked points will inevitably become occluded.

**The consequence.** When a trajectory disappears for an extended duration, the model loses its anchor for that object's motion. If the object re-emerges later, the model must "re-acquire" it — but since the feature replication only restarts when the trajectory reappears, there is a gap in the guidance signal. The model must fill this gap using its learned motion prior, which may produce physically incorrect interpolation (e.g., an object teleporting to its reappearance position, or drifting away from the intended path). The paper shows one example where this works (Appendix 9.3, Figure 12: a hand passing behind a body re-emerges naturally), but this is a single qualitative case. The paper does not quantify success rates for occlusion recovery or measure how occlusion duration affects control fidelity. In the failure cases (Appendix 9.4, Figure 13a), persistent trajectory disappearance is shown as a cause of "losing motion control," confirming that this is a real failure mode, not just a hypothetical concern.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides qualitative evidence in both directions: Figure 12 shows successful recovery from short-term occlusion (the hand re-emerges coherently), while Figure 13a shows failure from persistent occlusion (loss of control). However, no quantitative study is performed — there is no measurement of how EPE degrades as a function of occlusion duration, no comparison of Wan-Move's occlusion robustness to baselines, and no threshold for when "short-term" becomes "prolonged." The paper's claim that the model shows "a degree of generalization" (Section 6) for short-term occlusions is therefore anecdotal.

**Mitigation status.** Partial, but not systematic. The paper trains on data that includes occluded trajectories (CoTracker provides visibility flags, and the training procedure respects them by not replicating features for occluded points). This means the model has seen examples where trajectories disappear and reappear, and it has learned to interpolate motion in those gaps — but this learning is implicit and not guaranteed. The paper does not propose explicit occlusion-handling mechanisms (e.g., trajectory prediction during occlusion, using a learned motion prior to fill gaps, or interpolating trajectories from visible neighbors). The limitation is acknowledged but not addressed.

---

### 6.4 Training With More Than 200 Trajectories Degrades Performance, Creating a Bottleneck on Dense Control

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's training procedure samples 1–200 trajectories per video (Section 3.3, with the maximum `N` swept in Table 6). The optimal maximum is 200; training with `N = 500` or `N = 1024` *degrades* evaluation performance (EPE rises from 2.6 at `N = 200` to 3.0 at `N = 500`, and to 3.9 at `N = 1024`). This is counterintuitive: one might expect that training with denser trajectories would improve the model's ability to respect dense constraints. The paper attributes this to "mismatch between the dense point tracks in the training and the sparse point tracks during evaluation" (Section 5.3, Table 6 discussion), but this explanation is incomplete — at inference time, the model *can* accept 1024 trajectories and performs *better* with them (Table 7: EPE drops to 1.1 at 1024 inference trajectories). So why does training with 1024 trajectories hurt, while inference with 1024 trajectories helps?

**The consequence.** The training procedure imposes a practical bottleneck: the model learns best when trained with a moderate number of trajectories (200), but this means it receives less total motion supervision than it could with denser trajectories. The fact that training with 1024 trajectories degrades performance suggests that the model overfits to the dense trajectory pattern — it learns to rely on having a complete motion field and fails to generalize when some constraints are absent (as they are during evaluation on MoveBench, which uses sparser tracks). But during inference, when 1024 trajectories *are* provided, the model benefits from them even though it was trained with at most 200 — a paradox. This paradox implies that the training-time degradation is not about the model's *capacity* to use dense trajectories (which is clearly sufficient), but about a *distribution shift* between the training condition (dense trajectories) and the evaluation condition (sparser trajectories). The model trained with 1024 trajectories expects dense constraints and performs poorly when they are absent; the model trained with 200 trajectories expects variable constraints and generalizes better.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 6 provides the sweep, and Table 7 provides the inference-time counterpart. The paper does not provide an explanation for the asymmetry beyond noting the "mismatch." No experiment tests whether training with 1024 trajectories and then evaluating *with* 1024 trajectories (matching the training density) recovers the lost performance — which would confirm the distribution-shift hypothesis. If training and evaluating both at 1024 trajectories yields poor quality, the problem is not distribution shift but something else (e.g., optimization difficulty with dense trajectories). This experiment is missing.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper settles on `N = 200` as the training maximum based on MoveBench evaluation, but MoveBench uses relatively sparse tracks. A deployment where users consistently provide dense CoTracker trajectories might benefit from training with 1024 — but this would require a benchmark with dense trajectory annotations to validate, which MoveBench does not provide. The paper does not propose a training strategy that enables robust performance at all trajectory densities (e.g., curriculum learning from sparse to dense, or mixed-density training with a broader range).

### 6.5 The Revision Model's Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Problem Has No Principled Solution

**Note to reader:** This limitation is adapted from a restriction in the reference example; the Wan-Move paper does not have a revision model. The closest analog in Wan-Move is:

**The assumption or constraint** (revised for Wan-Move): Wan-Move's latent feature replication deterministically copies first-frame features to future-frame positions. But the first-frame feature at a trajectory start point encodes the appearance *at that specific viewpoint and lighting condition.* If the object undergoes significant viewpoint change (rotation, foreshortening), non-rigid deformation (a face changing expression, fabric stretching), or lighting change (moving from shadow to light), the replicated feature becomes progressively less appropriate — it represents the *wrong* appearance for the new viewpoint or configuration. The model must internally correct for this mismatch, generating appropriate appearance changes despite receiving a static appearance prompt from the replicated feature. The paper does not identify this as a limitation, but it follows directly from the method design: Wan-Move provides appearance guidance as a *constant* feature vector, while real motion involves *changing* appearance.

**The consequence.** For motions that involve substantial appearance change — out-of-plane rotation exceeding ~30°, non-rigid deformation, or lighting variation — the replicated feature acts as misleading guidance. The model must simultaneously (a) respect the trajectory constraint (the feature says "this content goes here") and (b) modify the appearance to match the new viewpoint or configuration (the feature is wrong about the appearance). These two objectives are in tension, and the model has no mechanism to signal which should take priority. The failure cases in Appendix 9.4 may partly reflect this tension: "visual artifacts in overly complex, crowded environments" (Figure 13b) could occur when replicated features from one object interfere with another, and "violating rigorous physical laws" (Figure 13c) could occur when the model generates plausible appearance changes that violate the trajectory constraints. The paper does not disentangle these failure causes.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** No direct evidence. The paper does not evaluate performance as a function of viewpoint change magnitude, deformation magnitude, or lighting change. A controlled experiment would generate videos with varying degrees of object rotation and measure EPE/FVD degradation — this would reveal the viewpoint-change boundary beyond which feature replication becomes counterproductive. Such an experiment is not performed. The large-motion evaluation (Table 9) tests high-amplitude motion but does not distinguish between large translation (which feature replication handles well) and large rotation (which it may not).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, and no mechanism is proposed for adapting replicated features to viewpoint or lighting changes. This is a fundamental tradeoff inherent to the method's design: using static first-frame features as motion guidance assumes view-independent appearance, which is only approximately true for small motions and rigid objects. For large non-rigid or out-of-plane motions, a method that learns a generative prior over appearance changes (rather than copying static features) might be more appropriate — but the paper does not discuss this tradeoff.

### 6.6 The Kling 1.5 Pro Parity Claim Rests on an Underpowered Human Study With Uncontrolled Confounds

**The constraint.** The paper's headline claim — that Wan-Move "rivals Kling 1.5 Pro's Motion Brush" in "motion controllability" (Abstract, Section 5.2) — is supported by a single human study (Table 3) with 50 samples per method pair evaluated by 20 participants. The win rates against Kling 1.5 Pro are 47.8% (motion accuracy), 53.4% (motion quality), and 50.2% (visual quality) — near statistical parity. However, the study has several methodological weaknesses that prevent confident interpretation of these results as evidence of parity.

**The consequence.** If the human study overestimates Wan-Move's quality relative to Kling — due to sample selection bias, evaluator characteristics, or suboptimal Kling configuration — then the paper's strongest claim (commercial-grade quality) is overstated. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt Wan-Move over Kling 1.5 Pro (or over other commercial alternatives) would be making that decision on insufficient evidence. Conversely, if Wan-Move genuinely matches Kling, the study's small sample size means the result is fragile — a replication with 200 samples might show a 40% win rate (modestly worse than Kling) or a 60% win rate (modestly better), and the current study cannot distinguish these outcomes with confidence.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The human study details are in Section 5.2 and Table 3. The paper reports the number of samples (50) and participants (20), but does not report:

- **Sample selection method**: Were the 50 samples randomly selected from MoveBench? Stratified by difficulty? Hand-picked to showcase Wan-Move's strengths? Without this information, selection bias cannot be ruled out.
- **Evaluator characteristics**: Who were the 20 participants? Researchers familiar with the project? External crowd workers? Video professionals? Evaluator expertise affects judgments of motion quality, and if evaluators were not blinded to the method source, expectancy effects could bias results.
- **Kling 1.5 Pro configuration**: What prompt was used? What Motion Brush parameters? Were multiple generations attempted and the best selected? Kling is a commercial product with configurable settings; if it was used suboptimally, the comparison is unfair.
- **Inter-annotator agreement**: No Fleiss' kappa or similar metric is reported. Without agreement statistics, we cannot assess whether the win rates reflect genuine quality differences or noisy individual preferences.
- **Confidence intervals**: The paper reports point estimates (47.8%, 53.4%, 50.2%) without confidence intervals. With `n = 50`, the standard error on a proportion near 0.5 is approximately `sqrt(0.5 × 0.5 / 50) ≈ 0.07`, giving a 95% confidence interval of roughly ±14 percentage points — meaning the true win rate could reasonably be anywhere from ~34% (clearly worse than Kling) to ~66% (clearly better).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the near-50% win rates as evidence of parity without discussing the statistical power limitations or potential confounds. No larger-scale human study is proposed as future work. The paper does hedge the Kling comparison with phrases like "as indicated by user studies" (Abstract) and "narrows the gap between research-oriented and commercial models" (Section 5.2), but the claim of "rivaling" commercial quality is presented as a key contribution. A more rigorous evaluation — a pre-registered study with 200+ samples, professional evaluators, documented Kling settings, and reported confidence intervals — would be needed to substantiate this claim at the level of confidence the paper implies.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

Wan-Move introduces a **category-level reframing of motion-controllable video generation** — not an incremental improvement to motion encoding, but a demonstration that motion encoding may be unnecessary for I2V models. This is a conceptual shift analogous to the moment when researchers realized that explicit depth estimation networks could be replaced by neural radiance fields that learn geometry implicitly: the problem is dissolved rather than solved.

The specific shift is this: prior work treats motion control as a **signal-injection** problem. You have a motion signal (trajectories, flow, boxes) in one representation, and you need to translate it into the base model's representation via learned encoders. This framing creates an entire subfield of ControlNet variants, auxiliary encoder architectures, and fusion module designs — all competing on how to perform this translation with minimal information loss and maximal training efficiency. Wan-Move's reframing says: **don't translate the signal — edit the canvas the signal is meant to annotate.** The I2V condition latent `z_image` already has the right structure (a first frame followed by blank future-frame slots), and it already lives in the model's native representation space. Motion guidance is not a foreign signal that needs translation; it is an edit to the existing condition — "this content from frame 0 goes to this position in frame N" — expressed by literally moving the content.

If this reframing holds beyond the Wan architecture, it changes what researchers optimize for. Instead of asking "how do we build a better motion encoder?", they ask "how do we express motion constraints as edits to the base model's input representation?" This opens a broader space of methods that use no learned parameters for the control pathway — not just feature replication for trajectories, but potentially feature warping for flow, feature inpainting for disocclusion regions, or feature interpolation for sub-pixel motion. It also makes certain research directions less attractive: incremental improvements to ControlNet-based motion encoders now compete against a simpler, faster, and equally accurate alternative, raising the bar for what an encoder-based method must achieve to justify its architectural overhead.

The paper also provides **empirical reconciliation** of a tension in the prior literature. Prior point-trajectory methods (Motion Prompting [13], ImageConductor [58]) reported mixed results — some found point tracks effective, others found them insufficient for fine-grained control. Wan-Move's ablation in Table 4 explains why: the failure was not with point trajectories as a representation, but with *how* trajectory information was injected. Pixel replication loses spatial context; random track embeddings carry no appearance information. When trajectory information is carried by full VAE feature vectors — rich in texture, semantics, and spatial context — point trajectories become a powerful and precise control modality. This converts "point trajectories sometimes work" into "point trajectories work when the guidance signal carries appearance context," providing a clear design principle for future methods.

The introduction of **MoveBench** is also a landscape contribution independent of Wan-Move. The field of motion-controllable video generation has lacked a standardized, rigorous benchmark — prior evaluations used ad hoc subsets of DAVIS (50 clips, short durations) or MagicBench (automated noisy labels). MoveBench's 1,018 videos across 54 categories, with hybrid human+SAM-verified annotations, 5-second durations, and support for both point and mask-based methods, sets a new standard. Even if Wan-Move's specific method is eventually superseded, MoveBench provides the evaluation infrastructure to make meaningful progress comparisons possible. The paper's detailed curation pipeline (Figure 3) — expert scoring model, SigLIP stability filtering, k-means clustering into content categories, interactive SAM-based annotation — is a template that future benchmark efforts can follow or adapt.

A more subtle landscape shift concerns **the relationship between model scale and control precision.** The paper's fairness experiment (Table 8) shows that Wan-Move-Cog-630K (5B backbone) achieves EPE 2.8, while the full Wan-Move (14B backbone) achieves EPE 2.6 — only a 0.2 improvement from nearly 3× more parameters. This suggests that motion accuracy, as measured by EPE, may **saturate early with respect to backbone scale** when the control mechanism is deterministic feature replication. The video quality metrics (FVD, PSNR, SSIM) do improve with the larger backbone, but primarily because the larger DiT generates higher-fidelity frames — not because it learns substantially better motion control. This has implications for resource allocation: if motion accuracy plateaus at moderate backbone sizes, practitioners might deploy Wan-Move with a 5B backbone for motion-controlled generation and reserve larger backbones for applications where visual fidelity is paramount. It also suggests that further improvements to motion control may come from better trajectory specification (denser, more accurate tracks) rather than larger models — a reframing of where the research frontier lies.

Finally, the paper **opens the door to open-source motion control at commercial quality levels.** The human study (Table 3) — with all its caveats about sample size and statistical power — provides directional evidence that Wan-Move is competitive with Kling 1.5 Pro's Motion Brush. If replicated at scale, this means that motion-controllable video generation is not gated behind proprietary systems. An open-source 14B model, fine-tuned on 2M publicly available videos, can produce results that human evaluators cannot reliably distinguish from a commercial product. This lowers the barrier for creative tools, research on downstream applications (editing, animation, simulation), and community-driven improvement — the full training code, model weights, and benchmark are released, enabling other researchers to build directly on Wan-Move rather than reimplementing from a paper description.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Disocclusion-aware feature replication.** Wan-Move's fundamental limitation is that it copies static first-frame features to future positions, providing no mechanism for appearance changes due to viewpoint change, deformation, or disocclusion. A direct extension would augment the replicated feature with a learned **appearance update vector**: rather than copying `z_image[0, p̃[0]]` to `z_image[n, p̃[n]]`, predict a residual `Δz[n]` conditioned on (a) the first-frame feature, (b) the trajectory displacement magnitude, and (c) the surrounding latent context, producing `z_image[n, p̃[n]] = z_image[0, p̃[0]] + Δz[n]`. This residual could be generated by a lightweight network that is trained to predict feature changes observable in training videos — for instance, when a tracked point on a rotating cube moves from the front face to a side face, the VAE feature should change to reflect the new surface orientation. The experiment would measure whether EPE on the large-motion subset (Table 9, currently EPE 3.0) approaches the full-benchmark EPE of 2.6, and whether the disocclusion failure cases (Appendix 9.4, Figure 13a) are reduced. The key tension to resolve: a large residual network reintroduces the architectural overhead Wan-Move eliminates; a small one may not have sufficient capacity. A strong result would be a residual network smaller than a ControlNet branch (adding, say, 10% to inference latency rather than 30%) that recovers most of the appearance-change gap.

**Robustness to trajectory noise: a stress test.** The paper provides no characterization of how trajectory inaccuracy affects output quality. A systematic noise-injection experiment would fill this gap: take the ground-truth CoTracker trajectories from MoveBench, add controlled Gaussian noise to trajectory coordinates at varying magnitudes (σ = 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8 pixels in the 832×480 frame), and measure the resulting EPE, FVD, and PSNR. The slope of EPE degradation vs. input noise magnitude would reveal the method's sensitivity — a shallow slope indicates robustness (the model interpolates around noisy constraints using learned motion priors), while a steep slope indicates brittleness (the model faithfully reproduces incorrect trajectories). A comparison against Tora and MagicMotion under the same noise conditions would test whether Wan-Move's deterministic feature replication is more or less robust than learned motion encoders to input perturbation. The experiment would also inform practical deployment: if Wan-Move is highly sensitive to trajectory noise, users relying on CoTracker (which has its own error rate) need to know the expected quality ceiling. If it is robust, the method is more practical than the paper currently demonstrates.

**Cross-VAE generalization.** Wan-Move's core operation — latent feature replication — assumes approximate translation equivariance of the VAE's latent space. This property is not guaranteed across VAE architectures; different VAEs (Wan's, Stable Video Diffusion's, CogVideoX's) use different compression ratios, channel dimensions, and training objectives, which may affect how well a feature vector at position `(x, y)` represents the same visual content as a feature vector at `(x+Δx, y+Δy)`. A controlled experiment would implement Wan-Move's exact method on two or three different I2V backbones with different VAEs — Wan-I2V-14B (already done), CogVideoX-5B-I2V (partially done in Table 8), and SVD [68] — and measure the relative EPE improvement over each backbone's best learned-encoder baseline. If Wan-Move consistently outperforms across VAEs, the translation equivariance assumption holds broadly and the method is architecture-agnostic. If performance varies sharply (e.g., SVD's VAE latents are less equivariant, causing EPE to degrade), the method's applicability is bounded, and future work on VAE design for motion controllability becomes a new research direction — training VAEs with explicit equivariance regularizers to make them more suitable for feature-replication-based control.

**Trajectory-free motion control via implicit feature flow.** Wan-Move requires explicit point trajectories as input. A radical extension would eliminate the trajectory specification entirely: train the model to *infer* motion from the first frame and text prompt alone, using the latent feature replication mechanism as an internal computational step rather than an external input. Concretely, a small "motion proposal" network could take the first-frame VAE features and text embedding as input, predict a set of latent-space trajectories (where features should move), and then apply Wan-Move's feature replication — all within the forward pass, producing a fully automatic motion-controlled I2V model. The training signal would be the same flow matching loss (Eq. 4), plus potentially an auxiliary loss encouraging the predicted trajectories to match CoTracker-extracted ground truth. This would convert Wan-Move from a user-controlled tool into an automatic motion-aware I2V model — relevant for applications where manual trajectory specification is impractical (large-scale data generation, batch processing). The experiment would compare EPE and FVD against Wan-Move with ground-truth trajectories (upper bound) and against the base I2V model with no motion control (lower bound), measuring what fraction of the motion-control benefit can be recovered without explicit trajectory input.

**Failure mode quantification and difficulty-stratified evaluation.** The paper identifies three failure modes qualitatively (Appendix 9.4) but provides no statistics. A systematic study would annotate a stratified subset of MoveBench (e.g., 200 videos evenly covering the 54 categories) with per-video labels: "occlusion present" (yes/no, with occlusion duration in frames), "viewpoint change" (rotation angle estimated via homography), "deformation" (non-rigid motion present, yes/no), "scene complexity" (number of independently moving objects). Wan-Move and baselines would be evaluated per stratum, producing EPE and FVD as a function of occlusion duration, rotation angle, object count, etc. This would produce **operating characteristic curves** for the method — a practitioner could look up their expected occlusion duration or rotation angle and know what EPE to expect. More importantly, it would identify which failure mode dominates at which difficulty level, directing future work to the most impactful improvements. For instance, if EPE degrades sharply above 30° rotation but is robust to occlusion, the next priority is appearance-change modeling (the disocclusion-aware replication described above). If the opposite pattern holds, the priority is trajectory interpolation during occlusion.

**Combining Wan-Move with explicit motion priors for physics-constrained generation.** The paper identifies "violating rigorous physical laws" as a failure mode (Appendix 9.4c), which is expected — Wan-Move has no built-in physics knowledge. A natural extension would incorporate lightweight physics constraints into the trajectory specification pipeline. For example, when a user drags a point on an articulated object (e.g., a human arm), the system could use an off-the-shelf pose estimator to identify the kinematic chain, constrain the dragged trajectory to physically valid joint angle ranges, and propagate the motion to connected body parts via inverse kinematics — generating multiple consistent trajectories from a single user drag. These constrained trajectories would then feed into Wan-Move's standard feature replication pipeline. The experiment would compare user study win rates (motion quality, physical plausibility) between vanilla Wan-Move (user drags one point, model hallucinates the rest) and physics-augmented Wan-Move (user drags one point, system generates physically consistent multi-point trajectories), measuring whether the physics prior improves output plausibility without sacrificing the flexibility of point-based control. The key challenge: ensuring the physics module is lightweight enough to not reintroduce the architectural overhead Wan-Move eliminates — it should operate on trajectories (pre-generation), not on the generated video (post-hoc correction).

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Interactive video prototyping for creative professionals.** Wan-Move's 3-second inference overhead for trajectory processing (Table 5) — compared to ControlNet-based methods adding 225 seconds — makes it feasible for **interactive creative workflows** where a video editor or animator iteratively refines motion. A typical session: the artist loads a first-frame image, drags a few points to sketch initial motion, generates a preview video in ~13 minutes (765s base inference + 3s trajectory processing), adjusts trajectories based on the result, and regenerates. The `4×` latency advantage over ControlNet-based alternatives (768s vs. 987s total) means `~2` additional iterations per hour of work — a meaningful productivity gain. The ability to accept input from a reference video via CoTracker (motion transfer, Appendix 10.3) further enables workflows where the artist sources motion from stock footage and applies it to custom artwork, decoupling motion design from visual design. The open-source release makes this accessible to independent creators and small studios without Kling 1.5 Pro subscriptions.

**Data generation for robotic manipulation and embodied AI.** Training visuomotor policies for robotics requires large volumes of annotated video data showing objects moving under various forces and interactions. Wan-Move can generate synthetic training data by: (1) providing a first-frame image of a robotic workspace, (2) specifying object trajectories derived from a physics simulator or a task planner, and (3) generating photorealistic videos of objects moving along those trajectories. The key advantage is that the generated videos have **known, noise-free ground-truth trajectories** (since they were specified as input), avoiding the annotation cost and error of tracking real video. The EPE of 1.1 at 1024 trajectories (Table 7) indicates that dense, precise motion fields can be generated — sufficient for training policies that require fine-grained motion prediction. The MoveBench multi-object results (Table 2, EPE 2.2 for multi-object) suggest the approach extends to cluttered, multi-object scenes relevant to real manipulation tasks. The main limitation: Wan-Move generates visual content from first-frame features, so novel objects not visible in the first frame cannot appear — the data generation is limited to rearrangements of initially visible objects rather than full environment exploration.

**Video editing via motion transfer.** Wan-Move's motion transfer capability (Figure 16, Appendix 10.3) enables a concrete video editing workflow: extract dense trajectories from a reference video using CoTracker, optionally modify the trajectories (e.g., adjust speed, scale, or spatial extent), and apply them to a new first-frame image — potentially modified by an image editing model (Figure 17, e.g., "change the color to yellow" or "transform to Studio Ghibli style"). This decouples **content editing** (done on the first frame with existing image editing tools) from **motion specification** (borrowed from any reference video), producing edited videos without frame-by-frame manual animation. The FVD of 45.2 at 1024 trajectories (Table 7) indicates that dense motion transfer preserves spatiotemporal coherence close to real video quality. For video post-production, this means an editor can change the style or content of a clip while preserving its original motion — a capability currently implemented in commercial tools via optical flow warping, which struggles with large displacements and occlusions. Wan-Move's generative approach can hallucinate plausible disocclusions (to the extent its training data supports them), potentially handling cases where flow-based warping fails.

**Camera motion augmentation for static image galleries.** Wan-Move's camera control capability (Figure 15, Appendix 10.2) enables a specific deployment: animating collections of static photographs or artwork with smooth, user-specified camera motions (dolly in, dolly out, linear pan). A photographer or museum curator could upload a high-resolution static image, specify a camera trajectory (e.g., "slow dolly in toward the subject's face"), and generate a 5-second, 480p video that brings the image to life with parallax-consistent motion. The monocular depth estimation step (Depth Anything V2) provides approximate 3D structure, and Wan-Move's generation fills in disoccluded regions revealed by camera motion. The quality ceiling depends on depth estimation accuracy and Wan-Move's ability to hallucinate plausible content in newly revealed areas — the paper provides no quantitative evaluation of camera-control quality, so practitioners should calibrate expectations on their specific image domain. The advantage over dedicated parallax animation tools (which use depth-based warping) is that Wan-Move generates content rather than just warping pixels, potentially producing more natural results for large camera motions where warping creates visible stretching artifacts.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not articulate an explicit tradeoff matrix between itself and named alternatives, and constructing one would impose a framework the authors did not develop. The experimental comparisons position Wan-Move against specific prior methods (Tora, LeviTor, MagicMotion, ImageConductor) and against the commercial Kling 1.5 Pro, but the paper's argument is uniformly that Wan-Move is preferable — better motion accuracy, better video quality, lower architectural complexity, faster inference, open-source availability. There is no scenario where the paper suggests a practitioner should choose a ControlNet-based method over Wan-Move for point-trajectory-driven I2V generation, nor does it identify a capability that competitor methods possess and Wan-Move lacks (e.g., perhaps some baselines handle disocclusion better, but this is not tested). The decision rule implied by the paper is therefore simple — **use Wan-Move when you need motion-controllable I2V generation and have access to point trajectories** — and the only practical question is whether Wan-Move's limitations (occlusion sensitivity, appearance-change handling, trajectory specification burden) are acceptable for the target application. A more nuanced tradeoff framework would require head-to-head comparisons on dimensions the paper does not explore: robustness to trajectory noise, performance on out-of-plane rotation, or quality on content categories not represented in MoveBench. Until such data exists, a "Prefer A when X, Prefer B when Y" matrix would be speculative.
