Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (host of The Peterman Post, sharing a decade of personal goal-setting lessons):

**The core ritual: writing and retrospection every six months**

Ryan builds his goal-setting around a written reflection every six months—yearly isn’t frequent enough because “life priorities can often change a lot in a year.” The process starts with a retrospective on the previous half: what went well and, more importantly, what didn’t. The real value comes from “considering what didn’t go well and thinking about how to do better the next time.” From there he breaks life into a handful of categories—career, relationships, health, hobbies—and for each asks what it would take to be completely satisfied. That north-star exercise becomes the basis for the specific goals he sets. Writing it all out isn’t just record-keeping; “writing is thinking”—it’s the mechanism that structures and clarifies his thoughts, and the document becomes a reference he can revisit throughout the half.

**What they don’t tell you about SMART goals**

The standard SMART-goals advice is sound, Ryan says, but putting it into practice surfaced three counterintuitive lessons. First, **don’t set too many goals**—three to five is the sweet spot. Life is too complex to capture in a handful of bullets, so the temptation is to list everything, but that dilutes focus and adds overhead. Since your top goals are “exponentially more important than others,” cutting down the list is high-leverage (he keeps a small parking lot for items that don’t make the cut).

Second, **output metrics beat input metrics**. An output metric measures the result (e.g., how much weight you can lift); an input metric measures the activity (e.g., how many times you hit the gym per week). Output metrics are more motivating and better aligned with outcomes, so he uses them whenever possible.

Third, **achieving the goal isn’t what matters**—making your best effort in the right direction is. He internalized this at Meta, where teams routinely set **P50 goals** (a 50% chance of success), intentionally ambitious enough that missing them is expected. He now sets similar goals and doesn’t sweat misses as long as he’s made good progress.

**What worked: social skin in the game**

One of Ryan’s most powerful discoveries is sharing goals with friends. The mechanism is simple: you won’t want to let a friend down, and they’ll check in on you naturally. He settled on a three-branch incentive structure: if both people achieve the goal, they celebrate together; if only one achieves it, the other pays the achiever; if both fail, both put money toward something (a charity or shared expense, for example). “The important part is you set up incentives so that you’re both rooting for each other and have skin in the game.” Money is just an example—the core is mutual accountability with stakes.

**What worked: habits as the engine**

For goals that demand consistency, Ryan leans on habits rather than willpower. The strongest he ever was came from the habit of working out immediately upon waking. His most successful Mandarin-learning phase coincided with a standing weekly lesson. “Habits are a powerful tool. For many goals, consistency is key and habits are the best way to achieve that consistency.”

He also journals progress every two weeks—just an honest check of which goals are on track and which aren’t. One half-year he skipped this ritual and simply forgot about several goals entirely. The biweekly pulse-check also lets him pace himself and make adjustments mid-cycle, rather than discover derailment only at the end.

**What didn’t work: measurement overhead and self-rewards**

As an engineer, Ryan’s instinct was to measure everything—but measurement isn’t free. “It adds a lot of overhead and is sometimes impossible.” When the tracking system grew too complex, it became self-defeating, making the whole process less effective rather than more.

He also tried promising himself a reward for hitting a goal, assuming it would be motivating. In practice, he “never thought about it while working towards my goals.” He enjoyed the reward when it came, but it exerted zero pull during the actual work—a dead end he’s since abandoned.

**Closing principles**

Ryan’s strongest universal recommendation is the writing itself: “The process of writing where you want to be and why will give you a lot of clarity on your life’s direction.” The rest is personal—”knowing yourself is an important part of coming up with a system that works for you”—picking the tactics that match your psychology, and being willing to drop the ones that don’t, even if they sound good on paper.

---

Ryan’s **writing is thinking** conviction is the bedrock beneath every other tactic: the retrospective, the category scan, the biweekly journal pulse-check, even the shared-goal incentive structure all depend on first getting thoughts out of his head and onto the page. His **output metrics over input metrics** rule and his **P50 goals** posture both reflect the same underlying philosophy—optimize for direction and effort, not for a perfect scorecard, because what matters is that you’re “going in the right direction with your best effort.” Together they form a system designed to produce clarity and sustained momentum rather than rigid checkboxes, built through a decade of trial and error he’s still refining every six months.
