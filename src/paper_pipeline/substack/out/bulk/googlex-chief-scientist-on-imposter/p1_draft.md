Here are the takeaways from this Carey Nachenberg interview (rose from first intern to Symantec Fellow / VP-equivalent IC engineer, later Chief Scientist at a GoogleX moonshot, despite a lifelong battle with imposter syndrome):

**Growth from intern to Fellow at Symantec**

Carey started in 1992 as the very first intern at Peter Norton Group (later acquired by Symantec). He literally worked in a QA lab run by a former knife salesman because “back then, they weren't professionally trained software people.” After being acquired, Symantec was a Wild West with no real engineering culture—agile didn’t exist, unit testing didn’t exist, code was tossed over the wall to QA. But this chaos gave Carey an extraordinary gift: from his first day as a junior engineer, no one ever assigned him work. He was told, “Carey, you already know the technology… Go figure out what to do and do it.” That license to self-direct lasted his entire 21-year tenure.

Promotions through staff and senior staff were handled inside divisions, but for distinguished engineer and fellow, a company-wide committee of technologists led by the CTO reviewed candidates quarterly. Carey himself was promoted to Fellow not through that formal process, but because the Veritas acquisition introduced the Fellow title and Symantec needed to level-set; he was nominated without his knowledge. His portfolio of impactful projects was so far above the bar that it happened automatically. The key insight behind that portfolio: he didn’t just work on technically hard problems—he worked on things with **big business impact**, finding gaps where the company needed something and no one else was stepping up. He estimates six or seven times in his career he’d disappear for two to five months, prototype something entirely new, and then spend the other 80% of his time incrementally improving and tech-transferring those systems into shipping products.

**Developing “project taste”**

His canonical example of project taste: in the early days, the team fought polymorphic viruses by hand-writing assembly signatures for each variant—a process that took six months per virus, only to have three new variants drop the next day. Carey saw a gap. He realized you couldn’t solve this with a regex; you needed a fundamentally different detection architecture. He made it his master’s thesis, built it, and transferred it into Norton Antivirus. That pattern—look for an unsolved business need, build a prototype, land it—repeated across his career. When Ryan asks how he trained that project taste, Carey ties it back to a later observation at GoogleX: “A lot of people were really smart, but it wasn't clear that they were picking projects that would land.” Intelligence alone doesn’t give you that. He defines the skill as constantly asking: what are the outcomes the business or the customer actually needs, and what’s the simplest way to deliver them?

**The Stuxnet detour**

Carey didn’t analyze Stuxnet himself (his later career shifted from manual reverse-engineering to detection algorithms), but he tells the story to illustrate sophistication: multi-platform (Windows plus microcontroller firmware for centrifuges), six zero-day vulnerabilities to propagate, stealth mechanisms that hid its presence even as logic was downloaded and re-uploaded from controllers. “It was just amazing.” He says it’s a safe bet the Israelis and American government wrote it, noting that coding styles or “not watermarks, but…” implicate both.

**Why C beat assembly**

As a junior engineer, Carey pushed to rewrite the assembly-language antivirus engines in C for maintainability—and ended up making them *five times faster*. The speedup didn’t come from the compiler (which weren’t great yet); it came from replacing linear searches over 60,000 signatures with hash tables and binary search. “The people back then didn't know algorithms. They didn't understand what a big O was.”

**Imposter syndrome as a career anchor**

Carey stayed at Symantec for 21 years because he was terrified he wasn’t good enough for Google or Meta. At Symantec he was known, respected, and safe. “I complained all the time. I wasn't happy.” Toward the end, senior-level BS—strategic meetings full of posturing, debating people who “don’t necessarily know that much but they have an opinion”—was draining. But the fear that somewhere else the standards would be higher and he’d fail the interview kept him anchored. The only thing that broke the cycle was an external pull: a former Symantec COO who had moved to GoogleX reached out and said, “Why don’t you talk to the team and see if there’s a good fit?” Carey went in assuming he’d fail, but did it anyway. Throughout the conversation, imposter syndrome recurs as a theme—at GoogleX, when considering risky architectural projects, and even after leaving Lyft. It never fully left; he just learned to act despite it.

**What matters more than intelligence**

At GoogleX, Carey encountered people of dazzling intellect—including one engineer he pegged as “clearly over 200 IQ” who was still an L4. Why? Poor communication skills, projects interesting only to him with no business impact, lack of collaboration. “Just because you have intelligence doesn’t mean you’re gonna be successful.” The lesson he offers instead: focus on **outcomes**. It’s easy for engineers to optimize for their own interests—10% faster, cooler tech—but if you don’t project yourself into the company’s or customer’s shoes and identify *their* outcomes and metrics, people won’t care about your work. He acknowledges that career growth at both Symantec and Google was generally meritocratic, looking at impact, complexity, patent portfolios, and communication skills. You need a baseline of intelligence, but beyond that, the rounding out of skills matters far more.

**The GoogleX / Chronicle chapter**

Carey joined a stealth cybersecurity project inside X called Project Lantern. The team spent six months just debating what to build, then converged on a big-data security product, eventually named Backstory and spun out as Chronicle. The insight: security teams were drowning in logs—firewall, DHCP, web proxy, email, client telemetry—but existing tools ingested a fraction of the data and queries took 30 minutes. “We have Google. Planet-sized compute and storage. How could we totally turn this around?” Backstory ingested petabytes per month, indexed everything, and delivered Google-search-speed queries. You could plug in a malware hash or IP address and instantly see every impacted device, related artifacts, first and last infiltration times. Carey built part of the storage architecture; his code, he believes, is still running six or seven years later.

Chronicle’s spin-out gave the team freedom from Google’s notoriously lengthy performance-review culture. “As soon as we spun out, we had one-page performance reviews… in a Google slide.” He left partly because the post-spin-out environment stopped feeling like the trusting Symantec culture he’d known. There were juicy architectural problems he could have tackled—migrating off expensive Spanner onto different file formats—but he didn’t feel leadership would have his back if a multi-month bet didn’t land. At senior levels, “if you go off and try to do something and then people are like, what have you been doing the last couple months?… I didn't feel the confidence.” He’d also been talking to Waymo people and had caught the self-driving bug.

**The Lyft autonomous-vehicle pivot**

Despite having zero robotics experience, Carey got into Lyft through a former UCLA student who set up a coffee with the president. (He’d previously been rejected by Waymo—a hazard of being very senior without domain expertise: “you’re very expensive. You might not have the skills they need.”) His proudest project: co-designing a new architecture to move Lyft’s self-driving stack away from hand-coded, hand-parametrized robotics—the classic Carnegie Mellon approach where lane-change and avoidance algorithms were separate state machines, and a hundred parameters all interacted painfully. He saw that as a dead end. Together with the head roboticists (“who were old school, by the way”), he architected an **end-to-end neural-network-based driver with a safety guardrail layer**—a thin, hard-coded safety system whose only job was to slam the brakes if the network tried to run a red light. The hard part wasn’t coding; it was influencing domain experts who didn’t want to change and had no reason to trust a cybersecurity guy. He succeeded by collaborating and letting them co-own the idea.

**Getting credit (and why he didn’t)**

The architecture work at Lyft became a painful lesson. Since Carey didn’t want to alienate his collaborators, he let them present the work upward while he stayed quiet. At review time, leadership believed their manager had initiated the project. “I have PTSD from that experience.” His advice for others: take contemporaneous notes on what you did, the metrics, and the value—because you’ll forget. In promo packets, be specific and granular about your own contributions; don’t claim credit for everything else, or committees will spot the embellishment and you’ll lose credibility. And you do have to **toot your own horn**—not with embellishment, but by clearly documenting your slice of the work.

**Becoming a UCLA lecturer without a PhD**

Carey had taught programming at a for-profit school called Learning Tree in the early ’90s and loved it. At Symantec, a colleague who was a part-time UCLA lecturer brought him an application; Carey filled it out thinking “UCLA would never hire me. I don’t have a PhD.” Months of silence, then a frantic call two weeks before fall quarter 2001: “our lecturer bailed, can you still teach?” He had two weeks to plan a curriculum. Twenty-five years later, he’s still at it.

**How to give great lectures (and speak well)**

His design principle flows directly from his imposter syndrome: “Whatever intelligence I have… helps me write better lectures because unless I could understand something myself, being, I think, sort of slow, other people can’t understand it either.” He targets the 30th to 50th percentile, not the top 5%. He spends days bouncing concepts off ChatGPT o3 and Gemini to simplify them to the essence. He also designs for joy—colorful, funny, slightly unpredictable slides—so students want to come to class. On public speaking: it’s pure practice. “My speaking deteriorates over time when I'm not actively using it.” At GoogleX, a talk he gave on Stuxnet led people to tell him “you’re one of the smartest people I’ve ever met”—he laughs because they had no idea, but notes that “people will think you’re intelligent and they’ll give you more credit… based on your ability to communicate.”

**How AI is changing his students**

Last fall, Carey allowed LLMs for autocomplete and simple sub-tasks on projects; he now considers that a mistake. Students were auto-completing far more than intended, and exam scores showed a big delta from project scores. MIT research he cites suggests “synaptic connections are down” when students lean on LLMs instead of working through problems. Going forward, he’ll allow LLMs for learning and clarifying concepts but ban them from projects. Cheating detection is partly automated: consistent error messages, identical idioms, and similar variable names across submissions light up the cheat-checking software.

On the bigger question—should anyone still get a CS degree?—he splits the world in two. In a near-term world where LLMs are powerful but not AGI, software engineering remains vital because someone must supervise, verify, and correct what the models produce. In an AGI world where arbitrarily complex code can be written perfectly by a model, the job transforms. The most valuable skill becomes **understanding customer outcomes at a granular level**—what jobs they’re trying to get done, what pain points they actually measure, what they don’t care about—and then translating those requirements with extreme clarity to an LLM. He essentially describes a hyper-competent product manager, but adds that in his lifetime he’s met “very few competent product managers.” Most operate on intuition and feature envy rather than a repeatable process of customer-outcome discovery (he cites methodologies like Jobs to Be Done and Outcome-Driven Innovation). His toothbrush-drool metric illustrates the difference: a real customer pain point is small, specific, and measurable, not a generic feature request.

**Career regrets**

He should have left Symantec earlier. His rule: stay as long as you’re learning new skills and feel empowered to try uncomfortable things with a safety net for occasional failure. There’s genuine value in staying five or even ten years if you’re growing—institutional knowledge and reputation compound. But “when you get to that point where you’re not growing anymore and you’re stagnant… it’s time to leave.” He also warns that when you do leave a place where you’re well-known, you start over entirely: “at Google, they’re like, what have you done for us? We don’t care that you did those things.” On the golden-handcuffs question, he won’t say leave if the money matters for your life stability—but if you’re optimizing for enjoyment and hard problems, staying too long in comfort “can be toxic.”

**Finding work you enjoy**

His own passion for cybersecurity was completely random (third internship, “oh, viruses”). His advice: try breadth early—multiple internships, different domains—because you might find a passion in a field you’d never expect. “Don’t try to wait and find the perfect job. Find a job where you have a good manager, there’s some hard problems to solve, and you don’t know anything about it.” That can become a 25-year dream.

**Advice to his younger self**

Don’t let fear of failure hold you back—you can probably do more than you think. Focus on the outcomes of whoever will use your work; optimize for their metrics, not for perfection. When presenting to senior leaders, speak to *their* needs (revenue, speed, headcount savings), not your technology’s internals. Find a good manager who trusts you and gives you rope—that can make or break your life. And learn to collaborate without just telling people they’re wrong; understand their point of view first.
