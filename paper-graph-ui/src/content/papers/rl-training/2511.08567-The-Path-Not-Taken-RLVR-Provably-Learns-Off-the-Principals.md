# The Path Not Taken: RLVR Provably Learns Off the Principals

**ArXiv:** [2511.08567](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08567)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper studies why Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) produces sparse parameter updates that are paradoxically effective, analyzing publicly released checkpoints from models like DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B and Qwen3-8B across diverse RL variants (GRPO, DAPO, Reinforcement++).

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies why Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) produces sparse parameter updates that are paradoxically effective, analyzing publicly released checkpoints from models like DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B and Qwen3-8B across diverse RL variants (GRPO, DAPO, Reinforcement++). The authors propose a **Three-Gate Theory** — Gate I (KL Anchor) constrains each on-policy step via an implicit KL leash (the ratio clipping in DAPO), Gate II (Model Geometry) steers updates off principal directions into low-curvature, spectrum-preserving subspaces (avoiding the top singular vectors that SFT targets), and Gate III (Precision) hides sub-threshold updates in non-preferred regions, making the optimization bias appear as sparsity under bfloat16 storage. The paper demonstrates that RLVR achieves gains through minimal spectral drift and off-principal update alignment, preserving the pretrained weight spectrum far more faithfully than SFT, and shows that SFT-era parameter-efficient methods targeting principal directions (PiSSA, principal-only sparse masks) are misaligned with RLVR's dynamics, often degrading performance or causing training collapse, establishing that RLVR operates in a fundamentally distinct, geometry-driven optimization regime where directly repurposing SFT heuristics is counterproductive.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don't Understand *How* RLVR Works at the Parameter Level

The fundamental question this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **when RLVR improves a language model's reasoning abilities, what is actually happening to the model's parameters?** This matters because the post-training pipeline for modern Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) like OpenAI-o3 and DeepSeek-R1 has settled into a standard two-stage recipe: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) followed by large-scale RLVR using automatically verifiable rewards (e.g., pass/fail on math problems, execution success on code). The RL stage is where the most dramatic capability jumps occur — models learn to produce long chains of reasoning, self-correct, and solve problems far beyond their SFT-only counterparts.

Yet, despite the enormous computational investment in RL (the paper notes that xAI, 2025, devotes substantial resources to it), and despite the striking behavioral transformations it produces, **the mechanism by which RL shapes model internals remains poorly understood**. This is not merely an academic curiosity. Without a mechanistic understanding, practitioners are forced to treat RLVR as a black box: they throw compute at it, hope for improvements, and borrow design heuristics (like parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods) from the SFT era without knowing whether those heuristics are appropriate for RL's optimization dynamics.

The gap is particularly acute because, as the paper establishes in Section 2, RL and SFT exhibit **fundamentally different parameter update patterns**. SFT produces dense updates — most parameters change visibly. RL produces apparently sparse updates — only a small fraction of parameters show visible changes under standard bfloat16 storage. This counterintuitive finding (first observed by Mukherjee et al., 2025) creates a paradox: **a high-cost, high-gain process that relies on surprisingly minimal weight modification**. Understanding this paradox requires peering inside the black box of RL optimization dynamics, which is precisely what this paper sets out to do.

### Why This Problem Matters: Practical and Theoretical Stakes

The paper's motivation operates on two levels — practical deployment and theoretical understanding — and the stakes are high on both.

**Practical stakes: PEFT method selection is currently blind.** In production LLM deployments, full-parameter fine-tuning is often prohibitively expensive in terms of memory and communication bandwidth. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) and sparse fine-tuning (where only a subset of weights is updated) are therefore essential. The existing PEFT literature, however, is almost entirely developed and validated in the SFT regime. If RLVR operates in a fundamentally different optimization regime — as this paper argues — then PEFT methods designed around SFT's geometry (e.g., PiSSA, which explicitly targets principal singular directions; Meng et al., 2024a) may be **misaligned with RL's dynamics** and perform poorly or even destabilize training. The paper's case studies in Section 5 provide direct evidence of this: principal-targeted sparse masks degrade RL training dynamics, and principal-aligned LoRA variants (PiSSA) offer no gain over standard LoRA while collapsing at higher learning rates. Without the mechanistic understanding this paper provides, practitioners would continue to blindly carry over SFT-era PEFT methods into RLVR pipelines, leaving performance on the table or, worse, causing training instability.

**Theoretical stakes: resolving the sparsity paradox.** The observation that RL produces sparse updates (Mukherjee et al., 2025) raises a deep theoretical question: if only a small fraction of weights change visibly, how can the model's behavior change so dramatically? One might hypothesize that the sparse updates target particularly important parameters. The paper shows the opposite: RL updates **avoid** the principal weights (the high-magnitude, high-influence parameters identified by SVD reconstruction; Liu et al., 2025c) and instead concentrate in off-principal, low-magnitude regions. This is counterintuitive — why would RL avoid the most influential parameters? The paper's Three-Gate Theory provides a mechanistic answer: the KL leash from ratio clipping (Gate I) constrains the per-step update magnitude, the pretrained model's geometry (Gate II) steers these constrained updates toward low-curvature, spectrum-preserving subspaces (off the principal directions), and finite precision (Gate III) masks the micro-updates that do occur in non-preferred regions. This theoretical framework transforms the sparsity paradox from a puzzling observation into a predictable consequence of identifiable forces.

**A third stake: bridging mechanism and behavior.** Beyond PEFT and sparsity, the paper addresses a growing need to connect parameter-space dynamics to the behavioral effects of RLVR. Concurrent work (Wu et al., 2025; Shenfeld et al., 2025) has characterized RLVR's effects at the policy and distributional level — showing, for example, that the final RL policy remains KL-proximal to the base model, that RL forgets less than SFT, and that RL generalizes better out-of-distribution. But these accounts operate at the level of outputs, not weights. The paper explicitly positions itself as providing the **first parameter-space account** that links RL optimization dynamics to weight evolution, complementing these policy-level analyses. This is essential because weight-space understanding is what ultimately enables the design of better optimizers, better PEFT methods, and better training recipes — you cannot optimize what you cannot characterize.

### Where Prior Work Falls Short

The paper identifies several specific limitations in existing research that motivate its investigation.

**Limitation 1: Sparsity was observed but not explained.** Mukherjee et al. (2025) first documented that RL fine-tunes only a small fraction of network parameters, but this work was purely descriptive. It did not characterize *which* parameters change, *why* they change, whether the pattern is consistent across runs and models, or whether it generalizes beyond the specific checkpoints studied. Crucially, Mukherjee et al. used a fixed absolute-tolerance threshold ($10^{-5}$) to detect parameter changes — a method the current paper shows is unreliable under bfloat16 precision (Appendix E.1), since bfloat16's representable spacing scales with magnitude (numbers around 1024 have an ULP of 8, while numbers around $10^{-6}$ have an ULP of $7.45 \times 10^{-9}$). This means prior sparsity measurements may be significantly miscalibrated. The current paper develops a bfloat16-aware probe (Definition 2.1) that correctly identifies storage-level changes and finds that while RL is indeed sparser than SFT, the absolute levels on recent checkpoints are lower than earlier reports suggested (Table 1), underscoring the need for careful measurement.

**Limitation 2: SFT-vs-RL comparisons focused on outputs, not internals.** A growing body of work has established performance hierarchies between SFT and RL: RL-tuned models generalize better out-of-distribution (Han et al., 2025; Chu et al., 2025), transfer more effectively to new tasks (Huan et al., 2025), and can outperform offline SFT even with the same expert data (Ross et al., 2011). However, these studies operate at the behavioral level — they measure accuracy, generalization, and transfer — without investigating how the two methods differentially affect the model's internal structure. The paper cites these works approvingly but positions its contribution as investigating a **different dimension**: not whether RL outperforms SFT, but how each method reshapes the parameter space, and why the differences matter for algorithm design.

**Limitation 3: The PEFT literature is SFT-centric.** Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods — LoRA and its variants (DoRA, PiSSA, LoRA+), sparse fine-tuning, adapter layers — have been extensively studied, benchmarked, and theoretically analyzed almost exclusively in the context of supervised fine-tuning. The implicit assumption is that the optimization dynamics of SFT and RL are similar enough that methods effective for one will transfer to the other. A recent report (Schulman & Lab, 2025) reinforced this assumption by finding that low-rank LoRA, even rank-1, can match full-parameter RL performance, and speculated that principal-targeted variants like PiSSA should yield further gains. The current paper directly challenges this assumption: its geometry-based account predicts that principal-targeted methods are misaligned with RLVR's off-principal bias, and its experiments confirm this prediction (Figures 10 and 11). The SFT-centric PEFT literature, therefore, is not just incomplete — it may be actively misleading when applied to RL.

**Limitation 4: No mechanistic account of RL optimization dynamics existed.** Prior to this work, there was no theory explaining why RL updates would be sparse, structured, or geometry-dependent. The policy-gradient literature provides convergence guarantees and regret bounds, but these operate at the level of expected reward, not parameter geometry. The neural network optimization literature provides analyses of gradient descent dynamics (e.g., the spectral bias toward low-frequency functions, the role of curvature in determining effective step size), but these are typically developed for training from random initialization, not for fine-tuning pretrained models with RL objectives. The paper identifies a gap: **no existing framework explains how the specific components of RLVR — the KL constraint (explicit or implicit from ratio clipping), the pretrained model's curvature structure, and the precision of storage — interact to produce the observed parameter-level patterns.**

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself at the intersection of three research threads and explicitly differentiates its contribution from each:

**Relative to sparsity observation work (Mukherjee et al., 2025):** The paper accepts the empirical finding that RL produces sparse updates but argues that sparsity is a **surface artifact** of a deeper mechanism — specifically, "a persistent, model-conditioned optimization bias" (Section 2). The paper moves beyond quantification to ask *where* updates land, *why* they land there, and *what* this implies. It shows that the observed sparsity is not an intrinsic property of the RL gradient (since optimizer states are in float32 and micro-updates do occur) but rather a visibility artifact arising from the interaction of a geometry-driven optimization bias with bfloat16 precision limits. By increasing the learning rate to push sub-ULP updates above the representable threshold, the paper demonstrates that apparent sparsity largely disappears — directly challenging the interpretation that sparsity stems from genuinely zero gradients.

**Relative to policy-level RL analysis (Wu et al., 2025; Shenfeld et al., 2025):** The paper explicitly acknowledges these concurrent works, which characterize RLVR's effects at the policy and distributional level — showing that the final policy remains KL-proximal to the base model (the "invisible leash" of Wu et al., 2025) and that RL forgets less than SFT (the "RL's razor" of Shenfeld et al., 2025). The paper cites these approvingly and uses similar variational arguments in its Gate I derivation (Proposition 3.1, inspired by the variational approach of Shenfeld et al., 2025). However, it positions its contribution as **complementary and orthogonal**: while these works explain *what* the policy does (stays close to the base, forgets less), this paper explains *how* the parameters evolve to achieve that (off-principal updates, spectrum preservation, geometry-dependent routing). In the authors' words, "Our focus [...] is to understand how this leash affects the weight change dynamics" (Section 3.1, Take-away 1).

**Relative to PEFT for RL (Schulman & Lab, 2025):** The paper engages directly with a recent influential report finding that low-rank LoRA matches full-parameter RL performance. Rather than contradicting this finding, the paper **explains** it through its geometric framework: since RL's effective updates lie off the principal directions and induce only small spectral changes, low-rank adapters can naturally approximate these off-principal updates while freezing the base weights regularizes against principal-direction moves. However, the paper **disagrees** with the report's speculation that principal-targeted variants (PiSSA) should yield further gains, predicting instead that such methods will be misaligned and potentially unstable. The experiments in Section 5.2 directly test and confirm this prediction, establishing that the geometry-based theory has predictive power beyond merely explaining existing observations.

**The paper's core framing: geometry as the missing piece.** Throughout, the paper frames its contribution as providing a **geometric account** of RLVR optimization — an account centered on the pretrained model's spectral structure (singular values, principal subspaces, curvature directions) and how it interacts with the RL objective's constraints. This is a deliberate departure from both the output-focused behavioral analyses and the sparsity-as-magnitude-counting approach of prior work. The paper's central metaphor — "RL learns off the principals" — captures this framing concisely: RLVR achieves its gains not by modifying the dominant directions in weight space (the "principals"), but by operating in the low-curvature, spectrum-preserving subspaces that avoid dramatic changes to the model's core representations. The Three-Gate Theory formalizes why this happens (KL constraint limits the step size, geometry determines where a small step goes, precision makes the bias visible) and the experiments validate that the signature is detectable, consistent, and causal (geometry disruption abolishes the effect; Section 4.3).

**Why "off-principal" matters beyond RLVR.** The paper hints at broader implications. If RL consistently avoids principal directions while SFT targets them, then the distinction is not just about two training algorithms — it reflects two fundamentally different paradigms for updating neural networks. SFT follows an externally provided target (the teacher's output) and must adjust the model's strongest representational pathways (principal directions) to align with that target. RL, by contrast, works within the model's existing representational structure, reweighting its own distribution toward higher-reward outcomes without pulling toward an external reference. The geometry account thus connects to deeper questions about the nature of learning: supervised imitation versus autonomous discovery, and how each is reflected in the parameter space. The paper does not delve deeply into these philosophical implications, but they provide intellectual motivation for why understanding RLVR's geometric signature matters beyond practical PEFT design.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This is primarily a **mechanistic analysis paper** — it does not propose a new training algorithm, but instead develops a theory for *why* RLVR produces sparse, structured parameter updates and then validates that theory through targeted experiments. The central idea is that RLVR's apparent update sparsity is a surface artifact of a deeper, geometry-driven optimization bias: a KL leash constrains each per-step update (Gate I), the pretrained model's curvature structure steers those constrained updates off the principal directions into low-curvature subspaces (Gate II), and bfloat16 precision hides micro-updates in non-preferred regions, making the bias visible as sparsity (Gate III). The paper builds a framework for characterizing where RL updates land in parameter space, why they land there, and what this means for the design of parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods for RL.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The analytical framework has four major components:

1. **A bfloat16-aware probe for parameter change detection** — a numerically robust definition of what counts as an "unchanged weight" under bfloat16 storage, replacing the unreliable fixed-absolute-tolerance methods used in prior work. This probe serves as the measurement instrument for all subsequent analysis.

2. **The Three-Gate Theory** — a mechanistic model with three sequential "gates" that collectively explain how RLVR's raw gradient updates are constrained, steered, and filtered into the observed sparse, off-principal update pattern. Gate I (KL Anchor) bounds the per-step policy divergence, Gate II (Model Geometry) determines which parameter directions receive the bounded update, and Gate III (Precision) makes the resulting bias visible as sparsity under bfloat16.

3. **Spectral diagnostics for weight-space characterization** — a suite of theory-guided metrics (principal subspace rotation via Wedin's sin–Θ theorem, normalized spectral drift, overlap with principal-weight masks) that quantify how RLVR and SFT differentially reshape the pretrained model's parameter geometry.

4. **Controlled intervention and ablation experiments** — targeted tests that isolate causal mechanisms: geometry-scrambling via orthogonal rotations and head permutations (to test Gate II), sparse masks constructed from pretrained geometry (to test whether off-principal updates are sufficient), and LoRA-variant comparisons (to test whether SFT-era PEFT heuristics transfer to RL).

Information flows as follows: a pretrained model checkpoint enters the analysis → the bfloat16-aware probe produces a change mask (which parameters changed and which did not) → the spectral diagnostics characterize the geometry of the change (spectrum preservation, principal subspace rotation, overlap with principal weights) → the Three-Gate Theory explains the observed pattern → controlled interventions validate the causal claims → implications for PEFT design are drawn and tested.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the bfloat16-aware probe and update characterization (Section 3.4.1), because all subsequent analysis depends on correctly identifying which parameters actually changed and where they are located. This is the measurement foundation — if the probe itself is unreliable, none of the downstream conclusions hold.

- **Second**, the Three-Gate Theory (Sections 3.4.2–3.4.4), which is the paper's core theoretical contribution. Each gate is presented with its mathematical formalization, its physical interpretation, and its testable predictions. Gate I (KL Anchor) explains why updates are bounded, Gate II (Model Geometry) explains why bounded updates go off the principals, and Gate III (Precision) explains why the bias appears as sparsity.

- **Third**, the spectral diagnostic suite (Section 3.4.5), which operationalizes the theory's predictions into concrete, measurable quantities — principal subspace rotation angles, normalized spectral drift, and principal-weight overlap ratios. These are the instruments used in Section 4 to validate the theory against real checkpoints.

- **Fourth**, the intervention and ablation designs (Section 3.4.6), which specify the causal tests: how geometry is deliberately disrupted to test Gate II, how sparse masks are constructed to probe which weight subsets are necessary for RLVR's optimization trajectory, and how LoRA variant comparisons are set up to test PEFT alignment predictions.

#### 3.4.1 The Bfloat16-Aware Probe: Correctly Detecting Which Parameters Changed

The analysis of RLVR's parameter updates hinges on a fundamental measurement question: **when comparing a fine-tuned checkpoint against its base model, which weights can we say genuinely changed?** This is not a trivial question under bfloat16 precision, and the paper demonstrates that the naive method used in prior work is unreliable. The development of a bfloat16-aware probe is therefore the critical first step — without it, all downstream claims about sparsity, update locality, and optimization bias rest on shaky measurement.

##### Why a fixed absolute tolerance fails

The bfloat16 number format has a distinctive property that makes fixed-absolute-tolerance change detection unreliable: **the spacing between representable numbers scales with the magnitude of the number**. Specifically, bfloat16 uses 7 mantissa bits and 8 exponent bits. In any binade (range of numbers sharing the same exponent), the unit in the last place (ULP) — the smallest representable difference between two distinct bfloat16 values — is:

$$
\text{ULP}_{\text{bf16}}(x) = 2^{e-7} \quad \text{where} \quad 2^e \leq |x| < 2^{e+1}
$$

This means that for numbers around $10^3$ (the typical magnitude of many weight matrix entries), the ULP is $2^{10-7} = 8$ — two values can differ by up to 4 in either direction and still round to the same bfloat16 code. For numbers around $10^{-6}$, the ULP is $2^{-27} \approx 7.45 \times 10^{-9}$ — tiny differences are representable. A fixed absolute tolerance of $10^{-5}$ as used in Mukherjee et al. (2025) would therefore **incorrectly flag a change** for large-magnitude weights (where a $10^{-3}$ difference is invisible in storage) and **incorrectly miss a change** for small-magnitude weights (where a $10^{-9}$ difference *is* visible in storage). The paper provides concrete examples in Appendix E.1: weights at 1024.001 and 1024.002 differ by $10^{-3} > 10^{-5}$ and would be wrongly classified as changed, yet both round to the same bfloat16 code; weights at $10^{-6}$ and $2 \times 10^{-6}$ differ by $10^{-6} \leq 10^{-5}$ and would be wrongly classified as unchanged, yet they are separated by approximately 134 ULPs and quantize to different bfloat16 codes.

##### The corrected definition: a magnitude-relative threshold

The paper's solution is to make the tolerance **proportional to the magnitude** of the weights being compared, using the observation that the minimal relative change required to produce a distinct bfloat16 code is bounded by the ULP-to-magnitude ratio. For any normalized bfloat16 value $|x| \in [2^e, 2^{e+1})$, the relative ULP satisfies:

$$
\frac{\text{ULP}_{\text{bf16}}(x)}{|x|} \in (2^{-8}, 2^{-7}] = (0.390625\%, 0.78125\%]
$$

This means a change smaller than approximately $0.195\%$ of the magnitude (half the ULP) is guaranteed not to produce a different stored value. The paper formalizes this as an unchanged-weight condition:

**Definition 2.1 (Unchanged Weight in bfloat16).** Let $w_i, \hat{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}$ be scalars stored in bfloat16 (finite, nonzero). We say $w_i$ is **unchanged** with respect to $\hat{w}_i$ if and only if:

$$
|\hat{w}_i - w_i| \leq \eta \max(|w_i|, |\hat{w}_i|), \quad \eta = 10^{-3}.
$$

**What this computes:** given two weight values (from the base model and the fine-tuned model), this test checks whether their absolute difference is at most $0.1\%$ of the larger magnitude. If it is, the two values are guaranteed to have the same bfloat16 representation (they are bitwise identical in storage). The use of $\max(|w_i|, |\hat{w}_i|)$ rather than $|w_i|$ alone ensures symmetry — it does not matter which weight is the "original" since both stored representations are being compared.

**Why $\eta = 10^{-3}$:** the paper proves in Proposition E.3 that any threshold $\eta < 2^{-9} \approx 1.953 \times 10^{-3}$ guarantees soundness (the test passes only when the stored bfloat16 values are identical) and completeness (if the stored values are identical, the test passes). Since $10^{-3} < 2^{-9}$, the choice is safe and yields a bitwise-equality check that is robust to floating-point arithmetic in the comparison itself.

**Why this form over an absolute tolerance:** the magnitude-relative form respects the bfloat16 representation's physics — larger numbers require larger absolute changes to register because their ULP is larger. An absolute tolerance would systematically under-report changes on small-magnitude weights and over-report changes on large-magnitude weights. The relative form makes the test **calibrated** across the entire range of weight magnitudes, meaning a "changed" verdict has the same meaning (different stored bfloat16 code) regardless of the weight's scale.

##### The bfloat16-aware sparsity metric

Using the unchanged-weight condition, the paper defines the bfloat16 change count and update sparsity:

**Definition 2.2 (bf16-aware Update Sparsity).** Write $x \overset{\text{bf16}}{\approx}_\eta y$ for Definition 2.1. Define the bfloat16 change count:

$$
\|\theta_1 - \theta_0\|^{\text{bf16}}_{0,\eta} := |\{ i : \theta_1^i \not\overset{\text{bf16}}{\approx}_\eta \theta_0^i \}|
$$

and the corresponding sparsity:

$$
\text{sparsity}_{\text{bf16}}(\theta_0, \theta_1; \eta) := 1 - \frac{\|\theta_1 - \theta_0\|^{\text{bf16}}_{0,\eta}}{n}
$$

where $n$ is the total number of parameters, $\theta_0$ is the base model parameter vector, and $\theta_1$ is the fine-tuned parameter vector.

**What this computes:** the fraction of parameters whose stored bfloat16 value did not change between the base and fine-tuned models. A sparsity value of 0.92 means 92% of weights are bitwise identical to the base model in bfloat16 storage, while only 8% show any representable change. A value near 0 means almost every weight appears to have changed.

**What the probe reveals (Table 1):** applying this probe across 14 publicly released checkpoints spanning multiple RLVR variants (GRPO, DAPO, Reinforcement++), datasets (math, code, mixed), and model families (Qwen, DeepSeek, Llama) reveals that RL consistently produces substantially higher update sparsity than SFT. SFT sparsity ranges from 0.6% to 18.8% (almost every weight changes), while RL sparsity ranges from 36.3% to 91.7% (most weights appear unchanged). However, the absolute levels are lower than earlier reports (which had claimed even higher sparsity), underscoring the importance of the corrected probe.

**A critical interpretive note:** the paper emphasizes throughout that this probe measures **visible (stored) changes** — what is written to disk in bfloat16 format. The actual optimizer updates (computed in float32, as the verl framework keeps optimizer states and gradient reductions in float32) may be denser. The sparsity metric is therefore an **observable readout** of an underlying optimization process, not a direct measure of which gradients were zero. This distinction is central to the paper's argument that sparsity is a surface artifact of the optimization bias interacting with finite precision, not an intrinsic property of the RL gradient.

##### The temporal analysis setup

Beyond comparing final checkpoints, the paper tracks how the update pattern emerges over training. For a given model trained for $T$ steps, checkpoints are saved at multiple intervals (e.g., $t \in \{240, 720, 1200\}$). At each checkpoint, the bfloat16 change mask is computed relative to the base model. The evolution of the mask is characterized by two per-layer metrics:

- **Row-wise update ratio:** $\rho_{\ell,i}(t) = \frac{1}{n_\ell} \sum_j M_{\ell,ij}(t)$ — the fraction of weights in row $i$ of layer $\ell$ that have changed by step $t$, where $n_\ell$ is the number of columns in the weight matrix.
- **Column-wise update ratio:** $\kappa_{\ell,j}(t) = \frac{1}{m_\ell} \sum_i M_{\ell,ij}(t)$ — the fraction of weights in column $j$ that have changed, where $m_\ell$ is the number of rows.

These ratios capture the **spatial structure** of the update mask: a row-dominant pattern (as observed in Q/K/V projections) means certain rows consistently collect updates while others remain untouched; a column-dominant pattern (as observed in O projections) means certain columns are consistently targeted.

#### 3.4.2 Gate I: The KL Anchor — Why Updates Are Bounded at Each Step

The Three-Gate Theory's first gate explains why the per-step parameter update magnitude is constrained during RLVR training. The paper argues that this constraint arises from the mathematical structure of on-policy RL objectives, specifically the ratio clipping mechanism that bounds the divergence between the new and old policies.

##### The RLVR objective and its KL regularization roots

RLVR algorithms optimize a surrogate objective that encourages the policy to assign higher probability to token sequences that yield high (verifiable) rewards. The general form, encompassing PPO, GRPO, DAPO, and REINFORCE++, is:

$$
\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x), x \sim \mathcal{X}} \left[ R(x, y) - \beta \, \text{KL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x) \| \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)) \right]
$$

where $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is a fixed reference policy (typically the base or SFT model), $\beta \geq 0$ controls the strength of KL regularization, and $R(x,y)$ is a verifiable reward (e.g., +1 for correct answer, −1 for incorrect). When $\beta = 0$, the explicit KL term disappears — this is the regime used by DAPO, which the paper adopts as its primary experimental algorithm to eliminate the confounding effect of an explicit KL penalty on update dynamics.

The surrogate used for gradient updates operates on token-level importance ratios. For a token $y_{i,t}$ (the $t$-th token in the $i$-th sampled completion), the importance-weighted advantage is:

$$
w_{i,t} = \underbrace{\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{<t})}}_{\text{importance ratio}} \, \hat{A}_{i,t} \, I_{\text{clip}} \in \mathbb{R}
$$

where $\pi_{\text{old}}$ is the policy that generated the batch of rollouts, $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is an estimated advantage (normalized to have zero mean and unit variance in DAPO), and $I_{\text{clip}} \in \{0, 1\}$ implements the ratio clipping that restricts updates where the policy would change too much. The token-level loss (DAPO variant) is:

$$
J_{\text{RL}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}, \, y_{1:G} \sim \pi_{\text{old}}} \left[ \frac{1}{\sum_i |y_i|} \sum_{i=1}^G \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t} \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i}^{<t}) \right]
$$

where $G$ is the number of rollouts per prompt and $|y_i|$ is the number of tokens in completion $i$.

**What this computes:** for each token in each sampled completion, the system scales the log-probability under the current policy $\pi_\theta$ by the importance-weighted advantage $w_{i,t}$. When $w_{i,t} > 0$ (the token was part of a good trajectory relative to the batch average), the loss encourages $\pi_\theta$ to increase its probability on that token. When $w_{i,t} < 0$, the loss penalizes the token. The normalization by sequence length $\sum_i |y_i|$ prevents longer sequences from dominating the gradient, and the clipping via $I_{\text{clip}}$ prevents any single token from having an outsized influence.

**Why this form (the clipped surrogate):** without clipping, the importance ratio $\pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{old}}$ can become arbitrarily large when $\pi_\theta$ drifts far from $\pi_{\text{old}}$, leading to unstable gradient updates and policy collapse. The clipping mechanism (standard in PPO and inherited by DAPO) bounds the per-token contribution to the loss, implicitly constraining how much the policy can change in a single step. This clipping is the source of Gate I's KL leash, even when the explicit KL penalty ($\beta = 0$) is absent.

##### The implicit KL leash from ratio clipping

The paper formalizes this intuition with two propositions. The first shows that a one-step policy gradient update, when interpreted as a parametric M-projection (fitting a policy to an exponentially-tilted improvement oracle), satisfies a KL bound even without an explicit KL penalty term.

**Proposition 3.1 (One-step policy-KL leash).** Let $q(\cdot|x)$ be a full-support reference policy and let $\tilde{q}_\beta(\cdot|x) \propto q(\cdot|x) \exp(R/\beta)$ denote the soft-regularized improvement oracle — the policy that optimally trades off expected reward against KL divergence from $q$. Let $\theta_+$ be obtained by the M-projection of $\tilde{q}_\beta$ onto the parametric policy class:

$$
\theta_+ \in \arg\min_\theta D_{\text{KL}}(\tilde{q}_\beta \| \pi_\theta)
$$

Then, for a sufficiently small one-step update:

$$
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_+} \| \pi_\theta) \leq (1 + o(1)) \, D_{\text{KL}}(\tilde{q}_\beta \| \pi_\theta)
$$

where the $o(1)$ term vanishes as $D_{\text{KL}}(\tilde{q}_\beta \| \pi_\theta) \to 0$.

**What this states:** the KL divergence from the old policy $\pi_\theta$ to the new policy $\pi_{\theta_+}$ after one update is bounded by (approximately) the KL divergence from the old policy to the exponentially-tilted oracle $\tilde{q}_\beta$. Since the oracle tilts $q$ toward higher-reward outcomes by a factor $\exp(R/\beta)$, and $R$ is bounded (verifiable rewards are typically ±1 or similar), the oracle itself is not arbitrarily far from $\pi_\theta$ in KL terms. Consequently, the one-step policy change is constrained — the policy cannot jump to a distant configuration in a single update.

**The proof strategy (Appendix F.2.1):** the proof uses a local Pythagorean identity for the M-projection, expanding $D_{\text{KL}}(\tilde{q}_\beta \| \pi_\theta)$ around $\theta_+$ and relating the projection Hessian to the Fisher information via a bound that depends on the total variation distance between $\tilde{q}_\beta$ and $\pi_\theta$ (Lemma F.6: $\|H_{\tilde{q}}(\theta) - F(\theta)\|_{\text{op}} \leq 2L \, \text{TV}(\tilde{q}_\beta, \pi_\theta)$). When the initial KL divergence $D_{\text{KL}}(\tilde{q}_\beta \| \pi_\theta)$ is small, the Hessian and Fisher are close, and the one-step policy KL is approximately equal to that initial KL, satisfying the bound.

**The clipping-only case ($\beta = 0$).** Lemma F.9 addresses the case where the explicit KL penalty is absent (DAPO regime) and the leash comes solely from ratio clipping. If clipping enforces $r_t \in [1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$ on the batch (the importance ratio stays within $\varepsilon$ of 1), then:

$$
\hat{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_+} \| \pi_\theta) \leq \hat{\mathbb{E}}[T(x)] \cdot \max\{-\log(1 - \varepsilon), \log(1 + \varepsilon)\}
$$

where $T(x)$ is the sequence length. In the small-step regime where the advantage has zero mean, this tightens to $O(\varepsilon^2)$. The paper confirms empirically (Figure 15) that the token-wise KL divergence increases steadily but remains bounded during a DAPO run without an explicit KL penalty — the clipping alone is sufficient to constrain policy drift.

##### From policy KL to weight-norm bound

The policy-KL leash implies a bound on parameter changes, formalized in Proposition 3.2:

**Proposition 3.2 (Policy-KL leash ⇒ weight bound).** Assume $\log \pi_\theta$ is $C^3$ (three times continuously differentiable) and let $F(\theta)$ denote the Fisher information matrix. If a one-step update $\theta_+ = \theta + \Delta$ satisfies $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_+} \| \pi_\theta) \leq K$ and, on the update subspace, $F(\theta) \succeq \mu I$ (the Fisher is positive-definite with minimum eigenvalue $\mu$), then for $K$ sufficiently small:

$$
\|\Delta\|_{F(\theta)} := \sqrt{\Delta^\top F(\theta) \Delta} \leq \sqrt{2K} \, (1 + o(1))
$$

$$
\|\Delta\|_2 \leq \frac{\sqrt{2K}}{\sqrt{\mu}} \, (1 + o(1))
$$

Consequently, for any weight matrix block $W \subset \theta$:

$$
\|\Delta_W\|_F \leq \frac{\sqrt{2K}}{\sqrt{\mu}} \, (1 + o(1))
$$

**What this states:** the Euclidean norm of the full parameter update $\|\Delta\|_2$ is bounded by $\sqrt{2K/\mu}$, and the Frobenius norm of any individual weight block's update is bounded by the same quantity. The bound tightens as the per-step KL budget $K$ shrinks (more conservative updates) and as the minimum curvature $\mu$ grows (steeper directions resist movement). The Fisher-norm bound $\|\Delta\|_{F(\theta)}$ measures the update in a metric that accounts for each parameter's influence on the output distribution, weighting directions that change the policy more heavily.

**The proof strategy (Appendix F.2.2):** the proof begins with the quadratic expansion of the policy KL (Lemma F.5):

$$
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta+\Delta} \| \pi_\theta) = \frac{1}{2} \Delta^\top F(\theta) \Delta + \mathcal{R}(\Delta), \quad |\mathcal{R}(\Delta)| \leq C \|\Delta\|^3
$$

where $\mathcal{R}(\Delta)$ is the cubic remainder from Taylor expansion. Using the spectral lower bound $F(\theta) \succeq \mu I$ and the constraint $D_{\text{KL}} \leq K$, the proof bounds $\Delta^\top F(\theta) \Delta \leq 2K(1 + o(1))$, from which the Euclidean bound follows via $\|\Delta\|_2 \leq \sqrt{\Delta^\top F(\theta)\Delta / \mu}$.

**What this enables for Gate II:** Proposition 3.2 provides the input constraint for Gate II. The per-step update is limited in norm — it cannot be arbitrarily large in any direction. Gate II then determines **which directions** receive this limited update budget, given the pretrained model's geometric structure. The key insight is that a bounded step on a highly structured landscape will naturally flow toward low-curvature directions where a given norm budget produces a larger policy change, a phenomenon the paper formalizes through the spectral analysis of Gate II.

#### 3.4.3 Gate II: Model Geometry — Where the KL-Bounded Update Goes

Gate II is the theoretical core of the paper. It answers the question: **given a KL-constrained per-step update budget, which parameter directions receive the update, and why?** The answer lies in the spectral structure of pretrained weight matrices and how this structure interacts with the norm constraint from Gate I.

##### The layer-conditioned norm budget

Gate I provides a global KL leash, but the resulting norm bound applies per parameter block. For a specific linear layer with pretrained weight matrix $W_0$ and update $\Delta_W$, the paper derives a layer-conditioned budget using the Schur complement of the full Fisher information. Let the Fisher be partitioned as:

$$
F(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} F_{W,W} & F_{W,\neg W} \\ F_{\neg W, W} & F_{\neg W, \neg W} \end{bmatrix} \succeq 0
$$

where $W$ represents the parameters of the target layer and $\neg W$ represents all other parameters. The layer-conditioned curvature proxy is defined using the Schur complement:

$$
S_W := F_{W,W} - F_{W,\neg W} F_{\neg W, \neg W}^{-1} F_{\neg W, W} \succeq 0
$$

This $S_W$ captures the effective curvature felt by the layer's parameters after accounting for the coupling with other layers — it is the Hessian of the loss with respect to $W$, optimized over changes in all other parameters. The per-layer KL budget is:

$$
\delta_W := \frac{1}{2} \text{vec}(\Delta_W)^\top S_W \, \text{vec}(\Delta_W) \leq K
$$

where $\text{vec}(\Delta_W)$ flattens the matrix update into a vector. If $S_W \succeq \mu_W I$ on the update subspace (positive-definiteness with minimum eigenvalue $\mu_W$), then the Frobenius and spectral norms are bounded:

$$
\|\Delta_W\|_F \leq \sqrt{\frac{2\delta_W}{\mu_W}}, \quad \|\Delta_W\|_2 \leq \|\Delta_W\|_F \leq \sqrt{\frac{2\delta_W}{\mu_W}}
$$

**What this means:** a weight matrix's update is bounded in norm, and the tightness of the bound depends on $\mu_W$ — the minimum curvature in the layer's parameter space. Directions with high curvature (steep eigenvalues of $S_W$) resist movement because the same KL cost produces a larger penalty; directions with low curvature (flat eigenvalues) permit larger parameter movements for the same KL budget. This is the channel through which the pretrained geometry steers the update: the optimization naturally routes the limited norm budget toward low-curvature subspaces where it achieves greater policy change per unit of parameter movement.

##### Principal subspaces and the high-curvature proxy

Computing $S_W$ directly — requiring second derivatives of the log-policy with respect to millions of parameters — is computationally prohibitive, especially for large reasoning models generating long chains of thought. The paper therefore adopts a practical proxy for high-curvature directions: **principal weights**, defined as the entries with largest magnitude in the rank-$k$ SVD reconstruction of $W_0$.

**Definition (Principal Weights, adapted from Liu et al., 2025c).** For a weight matrix $W_0$, compute its truncated SVD: $W_0^{(k)} = U_k \Sigma_k V_k^\top$, where $U_k$ and $V_k$ contain the top-$k$ left and right singular vectors and $\Sigma_k = \text{diag}(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_k)$ contains the top-$k$ singular values. The score of each weight position $(i,j)$ is:

$$
s_{ij}^{(k)} = |W_0^{(k)}(i,j)|
$$

The principal mask $M_{\text{princ}}^{(k)}$ selects the top-$\alpha$ fraction of weights with the highest scores $s_{ij}^{(k)}$:

$$
M_{\text{princ}}^{(k)} = \text{Top}_\alpha(s_{ij}^{(k)})
$$

**Why this is a valid curvature proxy:** Liu et al. (2025c) demonstrated that perturbing these specific high-magnitude SVD-reconstruction weights causes sharp degradation in reasoning performance, while perturbing other weights of equal original magnitude (but lower SVD-reconstruction magnitude) has minimal effect. Through a Taylor expansion of the loss, this sensitivity is linked to high-curvature: weights whose perturbation causes large loss changes sit in directions of high second derivative. The proxy is approximate — the principal weights capture a subset of high-curvature directions but not necessarily all of them — but it is computationally efficient and empirically validated.

##### Three spectral consequences of the constrained update

Given that the per-layer update $\Delta_W$ is bounded in norm, the paper applies classical matrix perturbation theory (Stewart, 1998) to derive three predictions about how the weight matrix's spectrum changes under RLVR. These predictions collectively characterize the "off-principal" regime: updates that avoid the dominant singular subspaces and preserve the pretrained spectral structure.

**Consequence 1: Limited subspace rotation (Theorem 3.3).**

**Theorem 3.3 (Constrained subspace rotation with Wedin's sin–Θ theorem).** Let $\gamma_k := \sigma_k(W_0) - \sigma_{k+1}(W_0)$ be the singular value gap between the $k$-th and $(k+1)$-th singular values of the pretrained weight matrix. For any $k$ with $\gamma_k > 0$, the principal angles between the original and updated top-$k$ singular subspaces satisfy:

$$
\max\left( \|\sin\Theta(U_k(W_0), U_k(W_+))\|_2, \; \|\sin\Theta(V_k(W_0), V_k(W_+))\|_2 \right) \leq \frac{\|\Delta_W\|_2}{\gamma_k} \leq \frac{\sqrt{2\delta_W / \mu_W}}{\gamma_k}
$$

where $W_+ = W_0 + \Delta_W$, $U_k(\cdot)$ denotes the matrix of top-$k$ left singular vectors, and $V_k(\cdot)$ the top-$k$ right singular vectors. The sine of the principal angle between two subspace bases is computed as $\sin\Theta(U, \hat{U}) = \sqrt{I - (U^\top \hat{U})^2}$ (or its matrix equivalent for multiple angles); it is zero when the subspaces are identical and one when they are orthogonal.

**What this computes:** the rotation of the dominant $k$-dimensional singular subspaces caused by the weight update. For each singular subspace (left and right), the theorem bounds the sine of the maximal principal angle between the original and updated subspace bases. The bound is $\|\Delta_W\|_2$ divided by the singular value gap $\gamma_k$.

**What this predicts:** when the singular value gap $\gamma_k$ is large (clear separation between the $k$-th and $(k+1)$-th singular values), the subspace rotation is tightly bounded — the dominant directions barely move. The top principal components of the weight matrix are **stable** under the constrained update. This is a direct consequence of Wedin's sin–Θ theorem (Wedin, 1972), which states that the sensitivity of an invariant subspace to perturbation is inversely proportional to the gap separating its eigenvalues from the rest of the spectrum.

**Why this matters for RLVR:** the KL leash (Gate I) makes $\|\Delta_W\|_2$ small, and pretrained weight matrices typically have well-separated top singular values (the spectrum decays). The combination means the dominant representational directions — the principal components that capture the most variance in the layer's transformation — are preserved. RLVR does not rearrange the model's core computational structure; it works within it.

**Consequence 2: Singular value stability (Corollary 3.4).**

**Corollary 3.4 (Singular-value stability).** For each singular value index $k$:

$$
|\sigma_k(W_+) - \sigma_k(W_0)| \leq \|\Delta_W\|_2 \leq \sqrt{\frac{2\delta_W}{\mu_W}}
$$

and for the squared sum of singular value changes (Hoffman-Wielandt bound):

$$
\sum_i (\sigma_i(W_+) - \sigma_i(W_0))^2 \leq \|\Delta_W\|_F^2 \leq \frac{2\delta_W}{\mu_W}
$$

**What this computes:** the per-singular-value perturbation (Weyl's inequality: the absolute change in any singular value is bounded by the spectral norm of the update) and the total squared spectral drift (Hoffman-Wielandt: the Frobenius norm of the update bounds the $\ell_2$ distance between the singular value vectors).

**What this predicts:** the singular values themselves — the "energies" of the principal directions — change minimally. The top singular values, which dominate the Frobenius norm (since $\|W_0\|_F^2 = \sum_i \sigma_i^2$), remain nearly identical. This means RLVR preserves not just the directions but also the magnitudes of the dominant transformations.

**Consequence 3: Top-k energy preservation (Corollary 3.5).**

**Corollary 3.5 (Top-k energy and Ky Fan norms).** Let $\|\cdot\|_{(k)} := \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i(\cdot)$ be the Ky Fan $k$-norm (sum of the $k$ largest singular values). Then:

$$
|\|W_+\|_{(k)} - \|W_0\|_{(k)}| \leq \sum_{i=1}^k |\sigma_i(W_+) - \sigma_i(W_0)| \leq k \|\Delta_W\|_2 \leq k \sqrt{\frac{2\delta_W}{\mu_W}}
$$

**What this computes:** the cumulative energy in the top-$k$ singular components (Ky Fan norm) changes by at most $k$ times the spectral norm of the update.

**What this predicts:** the total energy carried by the dominant representational modes is preserved. Since the Ky Fan norm for small $k$ captures most of the weight matrix's action (by the low-rank nature of pretrained weights), this means the model's overall computational capacity in its principal pathways is largely unchanged — the gains from RLVR come from elsewhere.

**Take-away from Gate II:** under the KL leash's norm constraint, RLVR updates tend to preserve the pretrained weight spectrum — minimal subspace rotation, stable singular values, preserved top-$k$ energy. This naturally favors updates that avoid the principal directions (which would require overcoming the high curvature associated with large singular values and their gaps) and instead operate in the **off-principal subspace** — the low-curvature, low-magnitude directions where a given norm budget achieves greater policy change.

#### 3.4.4 Gate III: Precision — How the Bias Becomes Visible as Sparsity

Gate III addresses a subtle but crucial point: if optimizer states are stored in float32 and micro-updates occur in all directions, why does the stored bfloat16 checkpoint show sparse changes? The answer is that the optimization bias (Gate II) causes RLVR to route **larger** updates toward preferred (off-principal) regions and **smaller** updates toward non-preferred (principal) regions. Finite precision then acts as a threshold: small updates that fall below the bfloat16 representability limit are not recorded in storage, making the differential routing visible as sparsity.

**Corollary 3.6 (Magnitude-dependent realization threshold).** A stored weight $W_{ij}$ changes at a step if and only if:

$$
|\Delta W_{ij}| \gtrsim \frac{1}{2} \text{ULP}_{\text{bf16}}(W_{ij})
$$

where $\text{ULP}_{\text{bf16}}(W_{ij})$ is the unit in the last place — the minimum absolute difference that produces a distinct bfloat16 code at the magnitude of $W_{ij}$. Since $\text{ULP}_{\text{bf16}}(W_{ij}) \approx 2^{e-7}$ where $2^e \leq |W_{ij}| < 2^{e+1}$, the realization threshold is approximately $2^{e-8}$, which is $0.195\%$ to $0.391\%$ of the weight's magnitude.

**What this means in practice:** if RLVR consistently applies sub-threshold updates to principal-direction weights (because Gate II steers the limited norm budget elsewhere) and above-threshold updates to off-principal weights, then the stored checkpoint will show changes only in the off-principal regions — exactly the sparse, structured pattern the paper observes. The sparsity is not because gradients are zero for principal weights, but because the updates are systematically smaller there, falling below the bfloat16 representability floor.

**The learning rate scaling test.** The paper validates this interpretation by increasing the learning rate to scale otherwise sub-ULP updates above the representable threshold. As predicted: "the apparent update sparsity largely disappears." This directly challenges the interpretation that sparsity stems from zero gradients (Mukherjee et al., 2025) and supports the view that sparsity is a readout of the optimization bias interacting with precision limits.

**Clarification on precision's role.** The paper emphasizes that precision is "more an amplifier for visible sparsity, not the cause of optimization bias." The optimizer states (gradients, momentum, variance) are maintained in float32 by the verl framework, with `reduce_type` and `buffer_dtype` both set to float32. Therefore, the routing bias — which directions receive larger vs. smaller updates — is determined by the optimization dynamics (Gates I and II). Gate III simply determines **which of these differential updates are visible in the stored checkpoint**. Removing Gate III (using float32 storage) would reveal a denser update pattern — but the underlying **routing bias** (off-principal weights receiving systematically larger updates) would persist. The paper cites concurrent work (Shenfeld et al., 2025) confirming that sparsity mostly vanishes under float32 storage even though task performance does not improve, which is exactly the signature of a precision artifact layered on top of a genuine optimization bias.

#### 3.4.5 The Spectral Diagnostic Suite: Operationalizing the Theory's Predictions

To validate the Three-Gate Theory against real checkpoints, the paper defines a set of quantitative metrics that operationalize Gate II's predictions about spectrum preservation and off-principal routing. These metrics are applied to pairs of checkpoints (base vs. fine-tuned) in Section 4.

##### Metric 1: Principal subspace rotation

For a weight matrix $W_0$ and its fine-tuned counterpart $W_+$, compute the truncated SVD to obtain the top-$k$ left singular vectors $U_{0,k}$ and $U_{+,k}$ (and similarly for right vectors $V_{0,k}$ and $V_{+,k}$). The principal angles between the subspaces are:

$$
\cos\theta_i(U) := \sigma_i(U_{0,k}^\top U_{+,k}), \quad \cos\theta_i(V) := \sigma_i(V_{0,k}^\top V_{+,k})
$$

where $\sigma_i(\cdot)$ denotes the $i$-th singular value of the $k \times k$ cross-product matrix. A cosine near 1 for all $i$ means the subspaces are nearly aligned (minimal rotation); a small minimum cosine indicates substantial rotation of at least one principal direction.

**What this captures:** this directly tests Theorem 3.3's prediction. Under RLVR, the principal angles should remain small (cosine near 1) — the top singular subspaces barely rotate. Under SFT, the principal angles may be larger because SFT lacks the KL leash and geometry steering — it can aggressively modify the dominant directions to align with the supervised target.

**Operational details:** the paper computes these angles for varying $k$ (the number of top singular components considered) and plots curves showing $\cos\theta_i$ against $k$ for individual layers (exemplar readout) and $\max_i \theta_i$ across all layers (aggregate summary, Figure 4 and Appendix Figures 16-18).

##### Metric 2: Normalized spectral drift

Beyond subspace rotation, the paper quantifies how much the singular values themselves change:

$$
\text{NSS}(W) = \frac{\|\sigma(W_+) - \sigma(W_0)\|_2}{\|\sigma(W_0)\|_2}
$$

where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the vector of all singular values of the weight matrix. This is the relative $\ell_2$ change in the singular value spectrum.

**What this captures:** this tests Corollary 3.4's prediction. Under RLVR, NSS should be small — the singular value profile is nearly identical to the base model. Under SFT, NSS may be larger because SFT amplifies or suppresses principal components to fit the target distribution.

##### Metric 3: Overlap with principal weights

This metric moves from aggregate spectral analysis to per-weight characterization. For a given layer, define the bfloat16-aware update mask $M$ (positions where the stored weight changed, per Definition 2.2) and the principal mask $M_{\text{princ}}$ (top-$\alpha$ fraction of weights with highest magnitude in the rank-$k$ SVD reconstruction of $W_0$). The overlap ratio is:

$$
\text{Overlap}(M_{\text{princ}}, M) = \frac{|M_{\text{princ}} \cap M|}{|M|}
$$

**What this computes:** among the weights that RLVR actually changed (visible in bfloat16 storage), what fraction are principal weights? The random baseline is the density of $M_{\text{princ}}$ itself (i.e., $\alpha$): if updates were uniformly distributed, approximately an $\alpha$ fraction of changed weights would be principal. An overlap significantly below $\alpha$ indicates that RLVR **avoids** updating principal weights; an overlap significantly above $\alpha$ indicates that RLVR **preferentially** updates them.

**What this captures:** this directly tests the off-principal routing prediction. Gate II predicts that RLVR updates are steered away from principal (high-curvature) directions, so the overlap should be **sub-random**. SFT, by contrast, is expected to target principal directions (as shown by Liu et al., 2025c), so its overlap should be super-random or at least at the random baseline.

**Refinement with low-magnitude mask.** The paper further examines the **composition** of principal-weight overlap by intersecting with a low-magnitude mask $M_{\text{low}} = \text{Bottom}_\alpha(|W_0|)$ — the top-$\alpha$ fraction of weights with the **smallest** absolute values in the original (non-reconstructed) weight matrix. The refined metric is:

$$
\text{Overlap}(M_{\text{princ}} \cap M_{\text{low}}^c, M)
$$

This measures overlap with principal weights that are **not** also low-magnitude. The prediction: the residual overlap between RL updates and principal weights is largely attributable to weights that happen to be both principal (high in the SVD reconstruction) and low-magnitude (small in the raw matrix), and after excluding these, the overlap drops significantly. This is important because low-magnitude weights are predicted to be updatable (low resistance from Gate III's precision threshold), so their presence in the principal-weight overlap set would artificially inflate the apparent overlap if not controlled for.

##### Metric 4: Temporal stability of routing patterns

To test whether the optimization bias is temporally stable (emerging early and persisting) rather than a transient artifact, the paper tracks the row-wise update ratio $\rho_{\ell,i}(t)$ and column-wise update ratio $\kappa_{\ell,j}(t)$ (defined in Section 3.4.1) across multiple checkpoints during training. The claim is that the **relative profiles** (which rows/columns are peak vs. trough) remain aligned over time, even as the overall density grows — the routing pattern is established early and reinforced.

##### Metric 5: Cross-run consistency (Jaccard overlap)

For multiple independent RLVR runs from the same base model (with different datasets and algorithms), the paper measures the pairwise spatial agreement between their final update masks using the Jaccard coefficient:

$$
J(M^{(r)}, M^{(s)}) = \frac{|M^{(r)} \cap M^{(s)}|}{|M^{(r)} \cup M^{(s)}|}
$$

where $M^{(r)}$ and $M^{(s)}$ are the bfloat16 change masks for runs $r$ and $s$. The random baseline for independent Bernoulli masks with densities $p$ and $q$ (the fraction of changed weights in each run) is:

$$
\mathbb{E}[J] = \frac{pq}{p + q - pq}
$$

A Jaccard substantially above this baseline indicates that the runs are targeting overlapping — not independent — parameter subsets, consistent with a shared, model-conditioned optimization bias.

##### Metric 6: Consensus ratio visualization

Beyond scalar overlap, the paper visualizes the **spatial structure** of update consistency using the consensus ratio:

$$
C_{\ell,ij} = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^R M_{\ell,ij}^{(r)}
$$

where $R$ is the number of independent runs (five in the paper's primary analysis). $C_{\ell,ij}$ ranges from 0 (no run updated weight $(i,j)$ at layer $\ell$) to 1 (all runs updated it). Plotting $C_{\ell,ij}$ as a heatmap reveals whether the consistently-updated positions form structured patterns (stripes, bands) or random scatter.

#### 3.4.6 Intervention and Ablation Designs: Testing Causal Claims

The paper's theory makes specific causal predictions that are tested through controlled interventions.

##### Geometry-scrambling intervention (testing Gate II)

Gate II claims that the pretrained model's geometry is the cause of the update routing bias. To test this causally, the paper deliberately disrupts the geometry of specific layers while leaving others intact, then measures whether the update routing bias disappears in the disrupted layers but persists in the intact ones.

**Intervention 1: Loss-preserving orthogonal rotation of V/O projections.** For a transformer layer with grouped-query attention (GQA), let $D$ be the head dimension, $H_q$ the number of query heads, $H_{kv}$ the number of key/value heads, and $n_{\text{rep}} = H_q / H_{kv}$ the query-to-KV head ratio. The value projection $W_v \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times (H_{kv} D)}$ and output projection $W_o \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times (H_q D)}$ are modified by right-multiplication with block-orthogonal matrices built from a single $D \times D$ orthogonal matrix $R$ (drawn from the Haar distribution or a Hadamard matrix):

$$
R_{kv} = \text{diag}(\underbrace{R, \ldots, R}_{H_{kv}}) \in \mathbb{R}^{(H_{kv}D) \times (H_{kv}D)}
$$

$$
R_q = \text{diag}(\underbrace{R, \ldots, R}_{H_q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{(H_q D) \times (H_q D)}
$$

The modified weights are:

$$
W_v' = W_v R_{kv}, \quad W_o' = W_o R_q
$$

If a bias $b_v$ exists, it is reshaped per head and rotated: $b_v' = b_v R_{kv}$.

**Proposition D.1 (Exact invariance).** The layer's output is unchanged by this rotation. Let $\text{Ctx} = \text{Attn}(Q, K, V) \in \mathbb{R}^{\cdot \times (H_q D)}$. Under the modification:

$$
\text{out}' = \text{Attn}(Q, K, V R_{kv}) (W_o R_q)^\top = \text{Ctx} R_q R_q^\top W_o^\top = \text{Ctx} W_o^\top = \text{out}
$$

**What this does:** the rotation scrambles the coordinate system within each attention head's value subspace while preserving the overall function. The weight matrix's singular values are unchanged (orthogonal transformations preserve the spectrum), but the **directions** of the singular vectors are rotated arbitrarily. This disrupts the alignment between the model's geometry and the optimization landscape's curvature structure — effectively destroying the "compass" that Gate II claims steers RLVR updates.

**Intervention 2: Head permutation (lossless reindexing).** Let $P_{kv}$ be a permutation of the $H_{kv}$ KV heads and $P_q$ its grouped expansion to $H_q$ heads (each KV head is duplicated $n_{\text{rep}}$ times in the Q projection). The heads are relabeled by:

- Applying $P_{kv}$ to the columns of $W_k$ and $W_v$ (reordering which head processes which subspace)
- Applying $P_q$ to the columns of $W_q$
- Applying $P_q^{-1}$ (the inverse permutation) to the columns of $W_o$

**What this does:** this relabels which attention head index corresponds to which representational subspace, leaving the block function unchanged but scrambling the positional correspondence between parameter space coordinates and functional roles.

**Experimental protocol:** the paper modifies (i) layer 20 with both Rotation and Permutation, and (ii) layer 25 with Rotation only, in a Qwen3-4B-Base model. Two independent RLVR runs are performed: one without intervention (base run) and one with the geometry-scrambled model. The update overlap ratio $\text{Overlap}(M^{(\text{base})}, M^{(\text{perturbed})})$ is computed for each layer. By comparing the overlap in intervened layers against non-intervened layers, the experiment tests whether the routing bias is geometry-dependent.

**Prediction:** if Gate II is correct, the overlap in intervened layers should collapse to (or below) the random baseline, while overlap in untouched layers should remain high — the routing bias depends on the specific pretrained geometry, not on a model-agnostic optimization property.

##### Sparse fine-tuning probe (testing which weights matter for RLVR trajectory)

The paper uses sparse fine-tuning — freezing a subset of weights to their pretrained values while updating the rest — to probe which parameter subsets are **necessary** and **sufficient** to reproduce the full RLVR optimization trajectory. Rather than selecting weights based on any training signal (which would be circular), the masks are constructed **directly from the pretrained model** without any fine-tuning.

**Mask definitions (50% sparsity for head-to-head comparisons):**

1. **$M_{\text{princ}}$ (principal-only):** freeze all but the top-50% principal weights (highest magnitude in rank-$k$ SVD reconstruction of $W_0$). This mask tests whether updating only the high-curvature, principal directions can support RLVR learning.

2. **$M_{\text{princ}}^c$ (non-principal-only):** freeze the top-50% principal weights and update the remaining 50%. This is the complementary mask — it tests whether the off-principal subspace alone is sufficient.

3. **$M_{\text{low}}$ (low-magnitude-only):** freeze all but the lowest-50% magnitude weights (smallest absolute values in the raw $W_0$). This tests whether low-magnitude weights — predicted to have low resistance to micro-updates from Gate III — can support RLVR.

4. **$M_{\text{low}} \cup M_{\text{princ}}^c$ (the "safe" mask):** freeze only the weights that are both principal AND high-magnitude ($M_{\text{princ}} \cap M_{\text{low}}^c$). The updated subset includes all non-principal weights and all low-magnitude weights (even if principal). This mask covers approximately 74% of parameters (in the paper's experiments) and is predicted by theory to capture the subspace where RLVR's effective updates occur.

5. **Random control:** a random mask with the same layer-wise sparsity as $M_{\text{low}} \cup M_{\text{princ}}^c$, generated independently for each layer. This controls for the effect of simply updating any 74% of parameters — if the safe mask outperforms the random mask, it indicates that the specific geometric selection (non-principal, low-magnitude) is what matters, not just the update volume.

**Evaluation metric (following Shenfeld et al., 2025):** the paper tracks the **token-wise forward KL divergence** between the sparse-run policy and the dense-run reference policy throughout training:

$$
\text{KL}(\pi_{\text{sparse}} \| \pi_{\text{ref}})
$$

where $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the frozen base model (not the moving reference policy from the RL objective). This metric measures how quickly and how closely the sparse run's optimization trajectory follows the dense baseline's trajectory. If freezing certain weights blocks optimization progress, the KL divergence will increase more slowly (the sparse run lags behind the dense run). The paper also reports final task accuracy on MATH-500, AMC23, AIME24, and AIME25.

**Training configuration:** the sparse fine-tuning experiments use DAPO on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B with the DS+SR training corpus (47k DeepScaler problems plus high-difficulty SimpleRL problems), running for 320 steps (extended to 500 steps in Table 5). Masks are one-shot (computed once from the pretrained model and held fixed throughout training), applied only to linear layers, excluding embedding and head layers.

##### LoRA variant comparison (testing PEFT alignment predictions)

The paper's geometry-based account makes a specific, testable prediction about parameter-efficient fine-tuning for RL: principal-targeted LoRA variants (like PiSSA, Meng et al., 2024a) should perform no better than standard LoRA, and may collapse at higher learning rates, because they enforce updates in the principal directions that RLVR's dynamics tend to avoid.

**LoRA and PiSSA mechanics.** Standard LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) decomposes the weight update for a linear layer as:

$$
W = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A
$$

where $W_0$ is the frozen pretrained weight, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times r}$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{\text{in}}}$ are low-rank learnable matrices, $r$ is the rank, and $\alpha$ is a scaling factor (typically equal to $r$, so the effective scaling is 1). The initialization sets $A$ to random Gaussian and $B$ to zero, so the initial update is zero.

PiSSA (Principal Singular Values and Singular Vectors Adaptation) modifies the initialization to explicitly target the principal subspace:

$$
W = \underbrace{W_0 - U_r \Sigma_r V_r^\top}_{\text{residual (frozen)}} + \underbrace{B A}_{\text{adapter (trainable)}}
$$

where $U_r \Sigma_r V_r^\top$ is the rank-$r$ SVD of $W_0$. At initialization, $A = \Sigma_r^{1/2} V_r^\top$ and $B = U_r \Sigma_r^{1/2}$ (or similar), so the adapter initially reconstructs the principal singular components. The residual $W_0^{\text{res}} = W_0 - U_r \Sigma_r V_r^\top$ is frozen. This means PiSSA **explicitly routes the adapter's capacity toward the principal directions** — the top singular vectors of $W_0$.

**The prediction from the Three-Gate Theory:** Gate II predicts that RLVR naturally avoids principal directions and operates in off-principal subspaces. Standard LoRA, initialized with random (non-principal) $A$ and zero $B$, has no geometric bias in its initial search directions — it can discover off-principal updates. PiSSA, by contrast, **forces** the adapter to work in the principal subspace (since the residual is frozen and the adapter basis is exactly the top singular vectors). This creates a mismatch: the RL objective wants to move in off-principal directions, but PiSSA's parameterization only allows movement in principal directions. At low learning rates, the mismatch may simply produce no gain over LoRA (since the frozen residual blocks off-principal adaptation). At higher learning rates — which are typically needed for low-rank adapters to match full-parameter performance — the enforced principal-direction updates will push the policy toward high-curvature regions that the KL leash naturally avoids, leading to instability and potential training collapse.

**Experimental setup:** the paper runs DAPO training on two model-LLM pairs:

1. **DS-Qwen-1.5B** with the DeepMath-103K corpus (He et al., 2025), sweeping ranks $\{8, 32, 64\}$ and learning rates $\{1 \times 10^{-4}, 5 \times 10^{-5}, 1 \times 10^{-5}\}$ for 200 steps. Evaluation on AIME24 and AMC23 using pass@1 averaged over 16 samples.

2. **Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct** with a Math corpus, sweeping learning rates $\{1 \times 10^{-4}, 5 \times 10^{-5}, 1 \times 10^{-5}, 1 \times 10^{-6}\}$ with a fixed rank of 64 for 200 steps. Evaluation on MATH500 using pass@1 averaged over 4 samples.

For both settings, the training uses DAPO with global batch size 128 (mini-batch 32), four gradient updates per step, and 16 rollouts per prompt to reduce compute cost during the sweep. The learning rate $1 \times 10^{-6}$ in the Llama experiment serves as the full-fine-tuning baseline (since adapters typically require higher learning rates).

**What the comparison tests:** by varying both rank and learning rate, the experiment disentangles two effects: (i) whether PiSSA's principal initialization provides any benefit at low learning rates (where instability is not an issue), and (ii) whether PiSSA becomes unstable at the higher learning rates needed for adapters to match full-parameter performance, as predicted by the geometric mismatch hypothesis.

#### 3.4.7 Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Bfloat16-aware probe over fixed absolute tolerance:** mathematically rigorous in capturing storage-level changes; avoids false positives at large magnitudes and false negatives at small magnitudes that plague the prior method. The threshold $\eta = 10^{-3}$ is chosen to be safely below $2^{-9}$, guaranteeing bitwise equality detection (Proposition E.3).

- **DAPO without explicit KL penalty:** used as the primary RL algorithm to eliminate the confounding effect of an explicit KL term, isolating the intrinsic parameter update dynamics without additional regularization. The ratio clipping in DAPO still imposes an implicit KL leash (Lemma F.9, Figure 15), so the Gate I constraint remains active even without the explicit penalty.

- **Rank-$k$ SVD reconstruction for principal weights:** adopted from Liu et al. (2025c) as a computationally efficient proxy for high-curvature directions. Direct Hessian computation is infeasible for large reasoning models, and the perturbation-sensitivity results in Liu et al. (2025c) validate that these weights are genuinely high-influence.

- **Two-fold intervention design (rotation + permutation):** the rotation scrambles the directional alignment of the geometry while preserving the spectrum (orthogonal transformations don't change singular values), isolating the effect of direction-vs-magnitude. The permutation scrambles the positional correspondence without changing the function. Together, they provide strong causal evidence that geometry — not function or spectral magnitude — is the steering mechanism.

- **Pre-computed, one-shot sparse masks:** masks are derived purely from the pretrained model without any training signal, ensuring that the mask selection is not contaminated by knowledge of which weights RLVR "wants" to update. This makes the sparse fine-tuning a genuine probe of the pretrained geometry's predictive power.

- **KL divergence as optimization trajectory metric:** following Shenfeld et al. (2025), tracking the forward KL to the base model rather than just final accuracy provides a dynamic readout of whether optimization is progressing normally or is blocked by the frozen weights. Two sparse configurations might reach similar final accuracy along very different trajectories; KL divergence disambiguates them.

- **Learning rate sweep in LoRA comparison:** controlling for learning rate is essential because the geometric mismatch hypothesis predicts instability specifically at the elevated learning rates required for low-rank adapters to match full-parameter performance. Sweeping across a range prevents cherry-picking a single learning rate where PiSSA happens to perform well or poorly.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing RLVR Update Sparsity as a Precision-Amplified Optimization Bias, Not a Zero-Gradient Phenomenon

The paper's most intellectually distinctive move is to reject the straightforward interpretation of RLVR update sparsity — that only a small subset of parameters receive non-zero gradients — and replace it with a more nuanced account in which sparsity is the **visible readout** of a deeper geometric routing mechanism, amplified by the finite precision of bfloat16 storage. Prior to this work, the dominant assumption, crystallized by Mukherjee et al. (2025), was that RL fine-tunes only a small subnetwork because gradients are genuinely zero for most parameters. This assumption carried an implicit corollary: identifying which parameters change is equivalent to identifying which parameters *matter* for RL, and freezing the rest should preserve performance.

The paper dismantles this assumption through a measurement critique and a theoretical reframing. The measurement critique (Appendix E.1) shows that the fixed-absolute-tolerance probe used in prior work is unreliable under bfloat16 — it systematically over-reports changes on large-magnitude weights (where the ULP can be as large as 8) and under-reports changes on small-magnitude weights (where the ULP is ~7.5×10⁻⁹). The corrected, bfloat16-aware probe (Definition 2.1) uses a magnitude-relative threshold derived from the bfloat16 representation's physics, guaranteeing bitwise-equality detection regardless of weight magnitude. This alone is a methodological contribution: it provides practitioners with a principled tool for analyzing parameter changes under mixed-precision training, applicable well beyond RLVR.

But the deeper conceptual move is the reframing of sparsity itself. The paper shows that when the learning rate is increased — pushing sub-threshold micro-updates above the bfloat16 representability floor — the apparent sparsity largely disappears. Since optimizer states are maintained in float32 throughout training (verl's `reduce_type` and `buffer_dtype` are both float32), genuine gradient computation occurs at full precision; the sparsity observed in stored checkpoints is not a gradient-level phenomenon. This transforms the interpretation: sparsity is not evidence of *which parameters receive gradients*, but rather evidence of a **differential routing bias** — the optimization process consistently applies larger-magnitude updates to some parameter regions and smaller-magnitude updates to others, and bfloat16 precision acts as a threshold that renders only the larger updates visible in storage.

This reframing is significant beyond the specific RLVR context because it identifies a general class of precision-amplified artifacts: phenomena that exist as weak signals in full-precision optimization but become salient — and potentially misleading — under reduced-precision storage. The finding that sparsity "mostly vanishes under float32 storage" (citing concurrent work by Shenfeld et al., 2025) without improving task performance confirms that the artifact is a precision effect, not a genuine gradient property. The intellectual contribution is the **diagnostic framework**: sparsity should be analyzed as the convolution of an optimization bias with a precision filter, and disentangling the two requires careful measurement design (the bfloat16-aware probe) and controlled experiments (learning rate scaling, precision comparisons). This framework is portable to any setting where reduced-precision storage interacts with structured optimization — a contribution that outlasts the specific RLVR checkpoints analyzed in the paper.

The evidence anchoring this reframing includes Table 1 (consistent sparsity patterns across 14 diverse checkpoints), the learning rate scaling experiment (Section 2.3), and the precision analysis in Appendix E (Lemmas E.1–E.2, Proposition E.3). These collectively establish that the prior zero-gradient interpretation is empirically unsupported and that the precision-bias interaction account explains the data more parsimoniously.

---

### Innovation 2: The Three-Gate Theory as a Unified Mechanistic Account Connecting RL Objective Structure to Parameter Geometry

Where prior work characterized RLVR's effects at the behavioral or distributional level — showing that the final policy remains KL-proximal to the base model (Wu et al., 2025), that RL forgets less than SFT (Shenfeld et al., 2025), or that RL generalizes better out-of-distribution (Chu et al., 2025) — this paper provides the first mechanistic theory that traces these effects to **parameter-level dynamics**. The Three-Gate Theory is not merely a taxonomy of observations; it is a causal chain that explains *how* the mathematical structure of the RL objective (ratio clipping producing a KL leash), the geometric structure of the pretrained model (curvature eigenvalues steering where bounded updates go), and the representational structure of the storage format (bf16 precision filtering visibility) combine to produce the observed sparse, off-principal update pattern.

The intellectual distinctiveness lies in the **sequential, conditional logic** of the gates. Gate I (KL Anchor) establishes that updates are bounded — but bounding alone does not determine direction. Gate II (Model Geometry) determines that the bounded update will flow toward low-curvature, off-principal subspaces — but this steering alone does not explain why the pattern appears sparse rather than merely biased. Gate III (Precision) explains why the steering becomes visible as sparsity — but only because the steering (Gate II) creates a magnitude differential that the precision filter (Gate III) can amplify. Each gate is individually grounded in known principles (trust-region clipping for Gate I, spectral perturbation theory for Gate II, floating-point representation physics for Gate III), but the **integration** into a coherent account — and the demonstration that all three must operate together to produce the observed phenomenon — is novel.

This is a fundamental rather than incremental contribution because it provides **generative, testable predictions** rather than post-hoc descriptions. The theory predicts that disrupting pretrained geometry should abolish the routing bias (tested and confirmed in Section 4.3). It predicts that principal-targeted PEFT methods should be misaligned with RLVR dynamics (tested and confirmed in Section 5). It predicts that the routing bias should be invariant to datasets and RL variants but dependent on the base model (tested and confirmed via the cross-run stability analysis in Section 2.2 and the cross-model-family observations in Figure 13). A mere taxonomy of sparsity patterns could not generate these predictions — only a mechanistic account can.

The theory also provides a **unifying framework** that reconciles previously disconnected observations. The finding that RL policy stays KL-proximal to the base model (Wu et al., 2025) is a consequence of Gate I. The finding that RL preserves pretrained capabilities better than SFT (the "forgets less" result of Shenfeld et al., 2025) is a consequence of Gate II — by avoiding principal directions and preserving the spectrum, RL avoids overwriting the representations that encode general capabilities. The observation that sparsity differs across precision levels (Shenfeld et al., 2025) is a consequence of Gate III. The Three-Gate Theory thus subsumes these disparate empirical findings under a single mechanistic umbrella, which is exactly what good theory accomplishes.

The evidence anchoring this contribution spans the entire paper: Propositions 3.1 and 3.2 and Lemma F.9 (Gate I formalization), Theorem 3.3 and Corollaries 3.4–3.5 (Gate II formalization), Corollary 3.6 (Gate III formalization), and the spectral validation suite in Section 4 (Figures 4, 16, 17 — showing RL preserves spectrum while SFT distorts it). The geometry-scrambling experiment (Section 4.3, Figure 6) provides the strongest causal evidence: when geometry is disrupted, the routing bias collapses to random levels in the disrupted layers while remaining intact in untouched layers, establishing that geometry is not merely *correlated with* the bias but *causally responsible for* it.

### Innovation 3: Identifying the "Off-Principal" Regime as a Fundamental Distinction Between How RL and SFT Operate on Parameters

The paper's most practically consequential discovery is that RLVR and SFT occupy **fundamentally different regions** of parameter space, even when achieving comparable task performance. This is not an observation about output quality or generalization — it is a geometric claim about *which directions in weight space each method modifies*. The paper demonstrates this through a multi-pronged parameter-level characterization: RLVR preserves the pretrained spectral structure (minimal subspace rotation, stable singular values — Figure 4), while SFT distorts it; RLVR updates avoid principal weights (sub-random overlap — Figure 5), while SFT targets them (Meng et al., 2024a; Liu et al., 2025c); RLVR's update routing is causally dependent on pretrained geometry (Section 4.3), while SFT's dense updates are driven by the external teacher signal.

The reason this is an innovation rather than an incremental observation is that it reframes the RL-vs-SFT comparison from a performance competition to a **mechanism distinction**. Prior work (Chu et al., 2025; Han et al., 2025; Huan et al., 2025) established that RL outperforms SFT on various generalization and transfer metrics, but these comparisons treated the two methods as points on a continuum — better or worse at the same task, achieved through different objectives. This paper's geometric characterization shows they are not on the same continuum at all; they are **orthogonal modes of weight modification** that happen to both improve task performance. SFT modifies the principal representational pathways to align with an external target distribution. RLVR modifies the off-principal, low-curvature directions to reweight the model's own distribution toward higher-reward outcomes without disturbing the core representational structure. The metaphor in Figure 1 captures this vividly: SFT takes the "high-curvature route over the mountain" (modifying dominant directions to reach the target), while RLVR "takes a low-curvature detour around it" (working within the existing geometry to reach better outcomes).

The practical significance of this distinction is immediate and actionable: it means that techniques developed for SFT — particularly parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods that exploit SFT's principal-direction targeting — **cannot be assumed to transfer to RLVR**. The paper's case studies in Section 5 demonstrate this concretely: principal-only sparse masks (aligned with SFT's geometry) produce the weakest optimization trajectory under RLVR (Figure 9), and principal-targeted LoRA variants (PiSSA, designed for SFT) provide no gain over standard LoRA and often collapse at higher learning rates (Figures 10, 11). These are not merely negative results — they are **predictive validations** of the geometric account, showing that the theory can anticipate which SFT-era heuristics will fail under RL.

This innovation also connects to broader questions about the nature of post-training. If SFT and RL modify parameters in orthogonal subspaces, then the standard two-stage pipeline (SFT followed by RL) is not merely a sequential application of different objectives — it performs a kind of **representational decomposition**: SFT establishes the principal representational structure (or aligns it with a target distribution), and then RL refines within the residual, off-principal degrees of freedom. This suggests that the two stages are complementary rather than redundant, and that the optimal allocation of representational capacity between them (how much SFT, how much RL, in which layers) is a parameter-level optimization problem that current practice solves only by heuristic. The paper does not fully explore this decomposition concept, but the geometric characterization it provides lays the necessary groundwork for doing so.

The evidence anchoring this innovation includes the spectral comparisons in Figure 4 (RL preserves spectrum, SFT distorts it), the principal-weight overlap analysis in Figure 5 (RL shows sub-random overlap, dropping further when low-magnitude principal weights are excluded), and the sparse fine-tuning results in Table 4 and Figure 9 (updating only non-principal, low-magnitude weights closely tracks the dense RLVR trajectory). This evidence spans multiple model families (Qwen, DeepSeek, Llama), RL algorithms (GRPO, DAPO, Reinforcement++), and data domains (math, code, mixed), establishing that the off-principal signature is robust and general rather than an artifact of a specific training configuration.

### Innovation 4: Using Weight-Space Diagnostics — Not Just Behavioral Metrics — to Characterize and Predict Learning Dynamics

A less flashy but methodologically important contribution is the paper's development and validation of a **weight-space diagnostic toolkit** for analyzing post-training dynamics. The standard approach in the LLM post-training literature is to evaluate checkpoints on downstream benchmarks (accuracy, pass@k, win rates) and, if possible, probe internal representations through activation-level analyses (probing classifiers, attention pattern visualization, representational similarity). The paper shows that a complementary set of purely **parameter-level metrics** — principal subspace rotation (Wedin's sin–Θ), normalized spectral drift, principal-weight overlap ratios, update mask consensus — can reveal regularities that behavioral metrics miss and can predict which PEFT methods will succeed or fail.

What makes this an innovation rather than a routine application of linear algebra is the **theoretical grounding** of each metric in the Three-Gate Theory. The metrics are not arbitrary summaries — each one directly operationalizes a specific prediction from the mechanistic account:
- Principal subspace rotation tests Theorem 3.3's prediction that KL-constrained updates preserve top singular subspaces.
- Normalized spectral drift tests Corollary 3.4's prediction that singular values remain stable.
- Overlap with principal weights tests Gate II's prediction that updates avoid high-curvature directions.
- Cross-run Jaccard overlap tests the claim that the optimization bias is model-conditioned (invariant to data and algorithm variance).
- Consensus ratio visualization tests the claim that the bias is spatially structured (stripes and bands) rather than random.

This theory-metric coupling is what distinguishes the paper's diagnostics from prior parameter-level analyses. Mukherjee et al. (2025) measured update sparsity but had no theory to explain it. Liu et al. (2025c) identified principal weights as important for SFT but did not connect this to RL dynamics or to a broader account of how optimization objectives interact with weight geometry. The paper's diagnostic suite is **explanatory** rather than merely descriptive — it tells you not just *what* changed (sparsity, overlap, rotation), but *why* it changed that way (KL leash bounds the step, geometry steers it, precision makes it visible).

The significance of this contribution is that it provides a **template for white-box analysis** of any post-training method, not just RLVR. If a new RL algorithm is proposed, the diagnostic suite can characterize whether it operates on-principal or off-principal, whether it preserves or distorts the pretrained spectrum, and whether its update patterns are model-conditioned or algorithm-specific. If a new PEFT method is proposed, the diagnostics can predict whether it is geometrically aligned or misaligned with the training objective before expensive training runs are conducted. The paper's case studies (Section 5) demonstrate this predictive utility: the geometric analysis correctly anticipates that PiSSA will provide no gain and may collapse, and that principal-only sparse masks will underperform. This closes the loop from theory to diagnostics to prediction, establishing that the weight-space approach is not just a post-hoc characterization tool but a **prospective design instrument**.

The evidence for this innovation is distributed across the paper's experimental sections. The spectral diagnostics are validated in Section 4.1 (Figures 4, 16–18) across multiple model families and both RLVR and RLHF settings. The principal-weight overlap diagnostics are validated in Section 4.2 (Figure 5). The predictive power is validated in Section 5 (Figures 9–11, Tables 4–5), where geometry-based mask design outperforms random masks and principal-targeted LoRA variants underperform. The causal validation comes from Section 4.3 (Figure 6), where geometry disruption causes diagnostic signatures to collapse in the specific layers where geometry was scrambled.

---

### Innovation 5: Demonstrating That RLVR Signatures Generalize Beyond Math/Code to Agentic Tasks and RLHF, Implying a Common Optimization Regime

A subtle but important contribution is the paper's extension of the RLVR diagnostic analysis to settings beyond verifiable-reward math and code — specifically, to agentic tasks (multi-turn planning, tool use, web search) and to RLHF (preference optimization via DPO and SimPO). The finding, summarized in Section 4.4 and Figures 7–8 and 18, is that the same spectral signatures — minimal subspace rotation, preserved singular values, off-principal update routing — appear across all these settings. This is significant because it suggests the Three-Gate Theory may capture a **universal feature of KL-anchored RL post-training**, not merely an idiosyncratic property of RLVR with rule-based verifiers.

Why this is innovative rather than a simple generalization test: the theoretical mechanism proposed in Gates I and II does not depend on the *type* of reward — it depends only on the presence of a KL constraint (from ratio clipping or explicit penalty) and the structured geometry of a pretrained model. Verifiable rewards (math, code) and learned reward models (RLHF) and multi-turn task completion scores (agents) all induce policy gradient updates under some form of trust-region constraint. If the Three-Gate Theory is correct, the resulting parameter-level dynamics should be similar across all these settings, because the constraints (KL leash) and the landscape (pretrained geometry) are shared. The paper's agent and RLHF results are therefore a critical **stress test** of the theory's generality — and the theory passes.

This finding has implications for how the field thinks about post-training pipelines. Currently, verifiable-reward RL (RLVR), preference-based RL (RLHF), and agent training are often treated as distinct methodological subfields with different best practices. The paper's parameter-level evidence suggests they may share a common optimization regime, distinguished from SFT primarily by the presence of a KL anchor. If so, insights developed for one setting — such as geometry-aware PEFT design, principal-weight diagnostics, or difficulty-conditioned update allocation — may transfer to others. The paper does not fully develop this unification, but the empirical evidence in Section 4.4 provides the foundation for it.

The evidence for this innovation includes the spectral analyses of agent checkpoints in Figure 8 (layer spectra nearly identical to base models; minimal top-k subspace rotation), the update–principal misalignment visualizations in Figure 7 (showing striped off-principal routing in agent layers), and the RLHF spectral results in Figure 18 (same spectrum-preserving, minimal-rotation signature). The model suite in Table 3 spans 8 checkpoints across agent and RLHF settings, providing reasonable coverage for the generalization claim — though the paper does not analyze as many agent/RLHF checkpoints as math/code checkpoints, leaving room for further validation.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary analysis uses publicly released checkpoints from diverse post-training pipelines, as summarized in Table 1 (14 models) and Table 3 (8 additional agent and RLHF models). The paper's own training experiments (sparse fine-tuning, LoRA comparisons) use mathematics corpora: DAPO+MATH (DM — the DAPO-Math-17k set combined with the MATH dataset; Hendrycks et al., 2021), DS+SR (the 47k DeepScaler collection from Luo et al., 2025b combined with high-difficulty problems from SimpleRL; Zeng et al., 2025a), and DeepMath-103K (He et al., 2025). Evaluation benchmarks include AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, and MATH-500 (Lightman et al., 2023), with the specific split from Lightman et al. (2022) used for MATH.

- **Base model(s).** The primary analysis focuses on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (DS-Qwen-1.5B), which serves as a robust case study because it underwent over 3,000 RL steps on a diverse mixture of mathematics, coding, STEM, logic puzzles, and instruction-following tasks (Liu et al., 2025a). Additional analysis spans Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-30B-A3B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, and Mistral-Small-24B. For the paper's own training experiments, the base models are DS-Qwen-1.5B (for sparse fine-tuning and LoRA studies), Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen3-4B-Base, and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. The paper argues DS-Qwen-1.5B is representative because of its extensive RL training across diverse data domains.

- **Metrics.** The paper uses several complementary metrics:
    - **Update sparsity ($\text{sparsity}_{\text{bf16}}$):** the fraction of parameters whose stored bfloat16 value did not change between base and fine-tuned checkpoints (Definition 2.2). Values range from 0 (all weights show visible changes) to near 1 (few weights changed).
    - **Jaccard overlap:** $\frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}$ between update masks from different runs, measuring spatial consistency of where updates land. Compared against an independent Bernoulli baseline $pq/(p+q-pq)$ where $p$ and $q$ are the mask densities.
    - **Consensus ratio:** $C_{\ell,ij} = \frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^R M_{\ell,ij}^{(r)}$, the fraction of runs (out of $R$) that updated weight $(i,j)$ at layer $\ell$. Visualized as heatmaps to reveal spatial structure.
    - **Principal subspace rotation:** for top-$k$ singular subspaces, principal angles computed as $\cos\theta_i(U) = \sigma_i(U_{0,k}^\top U_{+,k})$, where $\sigma_i$ denotes singular values of the $k \times k$ cross-product matrix. Small angles (cosine near 1) indicate subspace preservation.
    - **Normalized spectral shift (NSS):** $\|\sigma(W_+) - \sigma(W_0)\|_2 / \|\sigma(W_0)\|_2$, the relative $\ell_2$ change in singular values.
    - **Overlap with principal weights:** $\frac{|M_{\text{princ}} \cap M|}{|M|}$, where $M_{\text{princ}}$ is the top-$\alpha$ fraction of weights with highest magnitude in the rank-$k$ SVD reconstruction of $W_0$, and $M$ is the bf16-aware update mask. Compared against the random baseline $\alpha$.
    - **KL divergence (for sparse fine-tuning):** token-wise forward KL between the sparse-run policy and the frozen base model, $\text{KL}(\pi_{\text{sparse}} \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$, tracking whether optimization progresses normally when certain weights are frozen.
    - **Task accuracy:** pass@1 (averaged over $k$ samples) on AIME24 (avg@64), AIME25 (avg@64), AMC23 (avg@32), MATH-500 (avg@2).

- **Baselines.** The paper establishes several baselines:
    - **SFT checkpoints:** compared against RL checkpoints on the same spectral metrics (sparsity in Table 1, spectral drift in Figures 4, 16–18, principal-weight overlap implicitly via Figure 5).
    - **Random mask baseline:** for Jaccard overlap, the expected value under independent Bernoulli masks (Table 2). For overlap with principal weights, the density $\alpha$ of the principal mask itself (Figure 5).
    - **Dense RLVR:** for sparse fine-tuning, the full-parameter training trajectory serves as the reference, with KL divergence tracking how closely each sparse mask tracks it (Figure 9).
    - **Standard LoRA:** serves as the baseline for PiSSA comparisons (Figures 10, 11), with full-parameter fine-tuning at a baseline learning rate ($1 \times 10^{-6}$) also shown for Llama (Figure 11).
    - **Random-M$_{\text{princ}}^c \cup M_{\text{low}}$:** a random mask with the same layer-wise sparsity as the safe mask, controlling for the effect of simply updating any 74% of parameters (Tables 4–5).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For the publicly released checkpoints, no compute accounting is performed — the analysis is purely post-hoc on already-trained models. For the paper's own training experiments (sparse fine-tuning on DS-Qwen-1.5B, LoRA/PiSSA comparisons on DS-Qwen-1.5B and Llama-3.2-3B), training is run for a fixed number of steps: 320 steps (extended to 500 for the extended sparse fine-tuning in Table 5) for DS-Qwen-1.5B with DAPO on DS+SR, and 200 steps for LoRA studies on DS-Qwen-1.5B (DeepMath-103K) and Llama-3.2-3B (Math corpus). All RL training uses DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) without explicit KL penalty (ratio clipping only), a global batch size of 256 (mini-batch 64) with 4 gradient updates per step for main experiments, and a reduced batch size of 128 (mini-batch 32) with 16 rollouts per prompt for LoRA sweeps to reduce compute cost. Training hardware is NVIDIA H200 GPUs: 8 GPUs for Qwen2.5-Math-7B, 16 for DS-Qwen-1.5B, and 32 for Qwen3-4B-Base. The actor optimizer is AdamW with constant learning rate $1 \times 10^{-6}$ ($\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$) for full fine-tuning; LoRA sweeps test learning rates $\{1 \times 10^{-4}, 5 \times 10^{-5}, 1 \times 10^{-5}\}$ (plus $1 \times 10^{-6}$ for the full-finetune baseline on Llama).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For the cross-run stability analysis (Section 2.2), five independent RLVR checkpoints from the same base model (DS-Qwen-1.5B), trained on different datasets and with different RL algorithms (GRPO, DAPO, Reinforcement++), are compared. The mean off-diagonal of the pairwise Jaccard matrix is reported (Table 2), with Jaccard matrices visualized in Figure 12. For the temporal analysis (Figures 3, 14), checkpoints at multiple training steps ($t \in \{240, 720, 1200\}$) are analyzed within a single run. For the geometry-scrambling experiment (Section 4.3, Figure 6), two independent runs (one without intervention, one with) are compared, with overlap ratios computed per layer. The sparse fine-tuning experiments use a single run per mask configuration, with final accuracy averaged over multiple evaluation samples (avg@64 for AIME, etc.) but no cross-validation across training seeds — the paper does not report error bars or confidence intervals for any of its main training results (Figures 9–11, Tables 4–5), which is a notable limitation.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Update Sparsity Across Models and Algorithms

**Headline result:** RLVR consistently produces substantially higher update sparsity than SFT, with RL sparsity ranging from 36.3% to 91.7% across 14 checkpoints, compared to 0.6%–18.8% for SFT (Table 1).

Table 1 reports $\text{sparsity}_{\text{bf16}}$ — the fraction of weights that remain bitwise identical in bfloat16 storage between base and fine-tuned checkpoints — for 14 publicly released models. The pattern is stark and consistent:
- **SFT checkpoints:** DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (SFT on mixed data) shows only 2.8% sparsity — meaning 97.2% of weights changed. Klear-Reasoner-8B-SFT (SFT on math+code) shows 0.6% sparsity. UniReason-Qwen3-14B-think-SFT shows 18.8% — the highest SFT sparsity observed, but still far below typical RL values.
- **RLVR checkpoints:** The sparsest RL checkpoint is UloRL-A3B (GRPO on math) at 91.7%. Others include GT-Qwen3-8B-Base at 79.9%, Polaris-4B-Preview at 79.3%, NV-ProRL at 38.4%, and DeepScaleR-1.5B-Preview at 53.8%. The variation across RL checkpoints (36.3%–91.7%) is larger than across SFT checkpoints, likely reflecting differences in training duration, learning rate, and data mixture.

**Interpretation from the paper:** the authors emphasize that "absolute levels on recent checkpoints are lower than earlier reports (Mukherjee et al., 2025), underscoring the need for bf16-aware probes and re-evaluation on current models." This is an important methodological point — the corrected probe reveals that the sparsity effect, while real, is less extreme than previously thought.

#### Cross-Run Stability and Consensus Analysis

**Headline result:** Update masks from independent RLVR runs on the same base model show high Jaccard overlap (0.552–0.597 across layers, well above the random baseline of ~0.37–0.47), and consensus ratio heatmaps reveal structured stripe-like routing patterns rather than random scatter (Table 2, Figure 2).

Table 2 reports the mean off-diagonal Jaccard overlap for the 13th transformer block of DS-Qwen-1.5B across five independent runs trained on different datasets and RL algorithms. For Q projections, the overlap is 0.580 (random baseline: 0.430); for V projections, 0.597 (baseline: 0.467); for O projections, 0.552 (baseline: 0.373); and for MLP projections, 0.575–0.585 (baselines: 0.437–0.453). All overlaps substantially exceed the independent Bernoulli expectation, confirming that "a shared footprint" exists across runs. Figure 12 visualizes the full pairwise Jaccard matrices, showing high off-diagonal values consistent with strong cross-run consistency.

Figure 2 visualizes the consensus ratio $C_{\ell,ij}$ — the fraction of the five runs that changed each weight — for the 13th layer's Q/K/V/O projections and MLP down projection. The key qualitative observation is structured, contiguous stripe patterns: "obvious row-wise stripes in Q/K/V projections and column-wise stripes in O projections." This spatial structure indicates that RLVR's updates are not randomly scattered but concentrated in specific, coherent regions of the weight matrices — certain entire rows (for Q/K/V) or columns (for O) consistently collect updates across all runs, while other rows/columns remain untouched.

**Temporal emergence (Figure 3):** The row-wise update ratio $\rho_{\ell,i}(t)$ and column-wise ratio $\kappa_{\ell,j}(t)$ at steps $t \in \{240, 720, 1200\}$ for the 13th attention block show that the relative profiles (peaks and troughs) persist across time, even as overall density grows. The routing bias "emerges early and is reinforced over training, indicating a temporally stable phenomenon rather than a transient artifact." The complementary column-wise (Q) and row-wise (O) update ratios shown in Figure 14 "show a much weaker bias," confirming that the structured pattern is specific and not an artifact of uniform row/column growth.

**Cross-model-family generalization (Figure 13):** The paper reports that "similar stripe-structured footprints" appear on Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-Small-24B models analyzed from the zero-RL checkpoints of Zeng et al. (2025b), suggesting the routing bias is "generic to RLVR" rather than specific to the Qwen family.

#### Spectral Geometry: RLVR Preserves, SFT Distorts

**Headline result:** Across Qwen3-8B, DS-Qwen-1.5B, and Qwen3-14B, RLVR checkpoints exhibit consistently small principal-subspace rotation and minimal spectral drift, with singular-value profiles nearly identical to the base model, while SFT induces substantially larger rotations and pronounced spectral distortion (Figures 4, 16, 17).

Figure 4 provides the central comparison for Qwen3-8B (Su et al., 2025), showing four diagnostic panels:
- **Single-layer principal angle curves (left):** for an exemplar layer, the top-$k$ principal angles (cosine values) under RL remain near 1.0 across all $k$, indicating near-zero rotation of the singular subspaces. Under SFT, the cosine values drop visibly for several $k$, indicating larger rotation — the subspaces are being reoriented.
- **Single-layer singular value curves (center-left):** the singular value profile under RL is nearly identical to the base model across all indices, while SFT shows clear deviations — some singular values increase, others decrease, indicating spectral reshaping.
- **All-layer normalized spectral drift (center-right):** plotted across all layers, RLVR shows consistently low NSS values, while SFT shows substantially higher and more variable drift across layers.
- **All-layer maximum principal angle (right):** the maximum rotation angle across top-$k$ subspaces is consistently small for RLVR across layers, and consistently larger for SFT.

Figures 16 (DS-Qwen-1.5B) and 17 (Qwen3-14B) replicate these patterns: "RLVR shows surprisingly stable top-k spectrum with minimal subspace rotation and top-k eigenvalue changes." Figure 8 extends the analysis to agent tasks and Figure 18 to RLHF settings, showing that the spectrum-preserving signature persists beyond math/code RLVR — "Across RLHF checkpoints, RL training preserves layer spectra and induces only minor rotation of the top-k subspaces, consistent with the RLVR regime."

**Quantitative specifics:** the paper does not report exact numerical values for NSS or maximum principal angle in the main text, relying instead on the visual separation between RL and SFT curves in the figures. This is a limitation for rigorous reproducibility — the reader must estimate values from the plots rather than cite precise measurements.

#### Principal-Weight Avoidance

**Headline result:** RLVR update masks show sub-random overlap with principal weights, and this effect is even more pronounced when excluding principal weights that are also low-magnitude (Figure 5).

Figure 5 visualizes the layer-wise overlap between the RL update mask and three target masks:
- **$M_{\text{princ}}$ (principal mask):** Overlap is consistently below the random baseline (dashed line at density $\alpha$), indicating RLVR actively avoids updating principal weights. The sub-random effect is present across all layers.
- **$M_{\text{low}}$ (low-magnitude mask):** Overlap is super-random across layers — RLVR preferentially updates low-magnitude weights, consistent with Gate III's prediction that these have lower resistance to micro-updates.
- **$M_{\text{princ}} \cap M_{\text{low}}^c$ (principal but NOT low-magnitude):** After excluding principal weights that are also low-magnitude (which Gate III predicts are easily updatable regardless), the overlap "drops significantly" below even the already-sub-random $M_{\text{princ}}$ overlap. The paper interprets this as evidence that the residual overlap between RL updates and principal weights "is highly accounted for by weights that are both principal (defined by the rank-$k$ approximation of $W_0$) and low-magnitude (original $W_0$)."

The paper does not specify the exact $\alpha$ (top fraction) used for these masks, nor the rank $k$ for the SVD reconstruction, in the main text — these details may be in appendices or omitted. This is a gap in reproducibility for this specific analysis.

#### Causal Validation: Geometry Disruption Abolishes the Routing Bias

**Headline result:** When specific layers' geometry is scrambled via function-preserving orthogonal rotations and head permutations, the update overlap between the intervened run and a baseline run collapses to random levels in the intervened layers, while remaining high in all untouched layers (Figure 6).

Figure 6 reports the overlap ratio $\text{Overlap}(M^{(\text{base})}, M^{(\text{perturbed})})$ for each layer of a Qwen3-4B-Base model. Layer 20 underwent both Rotation (V/O orthogonal transformation) and Permutation (head reindexing); layer 25 underwent Rotation only. The results show:
- **Intervened layers (20, 25):** Overlap drops to approximately the random baseline level (shown as a dashed line in Figure 6).
- **Untouched layers (all others):** Overlap remains high — approximately 50–60%, consistent with the cross-run stability numbers reported in Table 2.

This provides strong causal evidence for Gate II's central claim: the pretrained model's geometry is not merely correlated with the update routing bias but is **causally responsible** for it. When geometry is preserved (untouched layers), different runs target overlapping parameter regions; when geometry is scrambled (intervened layers), the routing bias collapses and updates become effectively random relative to the baseline run.

#### Sparse Fine-Tuning: Off-Principal Masks Track Dense RLVR, Principal Masks Lag

**Headline result:** A mask updating only non-principal and low-magnitude weights ($M_{\text{low}} \cup M_{\text{princ}}^c$, covering ~74% of parameters) closely reproduces the dense RLVR optimization trajectory (KL divergence curve and final accuracy), while a principal-only mask yields the worst trajectory and lowest accuracy (Figure 9, Tables 4–5).

Figure 9 shows the token-wise forward KL divergence $\text{KL}(\pi \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$ across 300 training steps for six configurations on DS-Qwen-1.5B:
- **Dense (full-parameter):** The reference trajectory — KL rises steadily to approximately 0.012–0.013 by step 300.
- **$M_{\text{low}} \cup M_{\text{princ}}^c$ (safe mask):** Nearly perfectly tracks the dense KL curve throughout training, reaching essentially the same KL level by step 300. This validates the theory: the union of non-principal and low-magnitude weights captures the subspace where RLVR's effective updates occur.
- **$M_{\text{princ}}$ (principal-only, 50% sparsity):** The KL curve rises much more slowly — by step 300, it reaches only approximately 0.007–0.008, about 55–65% of the dense trajectory's KL. This indicates blocked optimization progress: freezing the off-principal weights severely impedes RLVR's ability to reshape the policy.
- **$M_{\text{princ}}^c$ (non-principal-only, 50% sparsity):** Tracks the dense curve better than $M_{\text{princ}}$ but lags slightly — reaching approximately 0.009–0.010 by step 300. The 50% sparsity limits the update capacity compared to the safe mask's 74%.
- **$M_{\text{low}}$ (low-magnitude-only, ~58.6% sparsity):** Tracks reasonably well, consistent with low-magnitude weights capturing much of the updatable subspace, but lags behind the safe mask due to lower update capacity.
- **Random-$M_{\text{low}} \cup M_{\text{princ}}^c$ (random mask with same sparsity):** Tracks below the safe mask, indicating that the specific geometric selection — not just any 74% of parameters — is what matters.

Table 4 reports final task accuracy at 320 steps, averaged across MATH-500, AMC23, AIME24, and AIME25:
| Configuration | Average Accuracy | #Params |
|---|---|---|
| Dense | 57.44 | 100% |
| $M_{\text{princ}}$ | 53.82 | 50% |
| $M_{\text{princ}}^c$ | 55.40 | 50% |
| $M_{\text{low}}$ | 56.69 | 58.59% |
| $M_{\text{princ}}^c \cup M_{\text{low}}$ | 56.24 | 74.02% |
| Random-$M_{\text{princ}}^c \cup M_{\text{low}}$ | 55.34 | 74.02% |

The principal-only mask underperforms dense by 3.62 percentage points and underperforms its complement ($M_{\text{princ}}^c$) despite both using 50% of parameters — "this directly confirms that principal-targeted directions favored in SFT are ineffective for RL." The safe mask ($M_{\text{princ}}^c \cup M_{\text{low}}$) achieves 56.24%, within 1.2 points of dense, using only 74% of parameters. The random control (55.34%, same sparsity) underperforms the safe mask by 0.9 points, confirming the geometric selection adds value.

Table 5 extends training to 500 steps to test whether the gap closes with longer training:
| Configuration | Average Accuracy | #Params |
|---|---|---|
| Dense | 58.59 | 100% |
| $M_{\text{princ}}$ | 55.44 | 50% |
| $M_{\text{princ}}^c$ | 56.90 | 50% |
| $M_{\text{low}}$ | 57.77 | 58.59% |

The gap between dense (58.59%) and the safe mask (58.37%) narrows to 0.22 points — nearly identical. The principal-only mask remains 3.15 points behind dense, confirming that the deficit is not a transient effect that longer training resolves. The safe mask now outperforms the random mask by 1.48 points.

**Insight from the paper:** "Our results suggest a simple yet effective alternative: Freezing principal and large-magnitude weights while updating non-principal, low-magnitude ones closely reproduces dense RLVR behavior (KL trajectory and final accuracy) using roughly 70% the parameters."

#### LoRA vs. PiSSA: Principal-Targeted Adapters Provide No Gain and Can Collapse

**Headline result:** Across two model families (DS-Qwen-1.5B and Llama-3.2-3B), principal-targeted PiSSA provides no additional gains over standard LoRA, and at higher learning rates that force principal-direction updates, PiSSA often collapses early while LoRA remains stable (Figures 10, 11).

**DS-Qwen-1.5B results (Figure 10):** Training on DeepMath-103K for 200 steps with DAPO, sweeping ranks {8, 32, 64} and learning rates {$1 \times 10^{-4}, 5 \times 10^{-5}, 1 \times 10^{-5}$}, evaluated on AIME24 (top row, pass@1 avg@16) and AMC23 (bottom row, pass@1 avg@32):
- **Across all configurations:** PiSSA and LoRA curves largely overlap or LoRA slightly outperforms PiSSA. There is no visible rank or learning rate where PiSSA provides a clear, consistent advantage.
- **At the highest learning rate ($1 \times 10^{-4}$):** PiSSA exhibits unstable training on AIME24 at rank 64 — the accuracy curve drops sharply after approximately 75–100 steps, consistent with the predicted collapse from forcing principal-direction updates. LoRA at the same settings remains relatively stable, though it also shows some degradation at the highest learning rate.
- **At lower learning rates ($5 \times 10^{-5}, 1 \times 10^{-5}$):** Both methods produce similar, stable learning curves with no clear winner.

**Llama-3.2-3B results (Figure 11):** Training on a Math corpus for 200 steps, fixed rank 64, learning rates {$1 \times 10^{-4}, 5 \times 10^{-5}, 1 \times 10^{-5}$}, plus full-finetune at $1 \times 10^{-6}$, evaluated on MATH500 (pass@1 avg@4):
- **At $1 \times 10^{-4}$:** PiSSA collapses to approximately 0.3 accuracy after 50–75 steps and remains at that level. LoRA at the same learning rate is more stable, reaching approximately 0.45–0.48 accuracy by step 200, albeit with some oscillation.
- **At $5 \times 10^{-5}$:** LoRA reaches approximately 0.50 by step 200. PiSSA is more stable than at $1 \times 10^{-4}$ but shows a noticeable drop mid-training (around steps 75–125) before partially recovering to approximately 0.47–0.48.
- **At $1 \times 10^{-5}$:** Both methods perform similarly, reaching approximately 0.48–0.50 by step 200, close to the full-finetune baseline ($1 \times 10^{-6}$, reaching approximately 0.51–0.52).
- **Full-finetune at $1 \times 10^{-6}$:** As expected, the most stable training curve, serving as an upper reference.

**Interpretation:** The paper's geometric account explains this pattern: "Scaling the learning rate in PiSSA enforces updates along principal directions, higher-curvature and spectrum-distorting, precisely the directions RLVR tends to avoid. The result is brittle optimization and early collapse, whereas LoRA's off-principal updates remain better aligned with RLVR's geometry."

#### RLVR Signatures in Agentic and RLHF Settings

**Headline result:** The same spectral signatures — minimal subspace rotation, near-identical singular value profiles, and off-principal update routing — persist in agent-trained checkpoints (multi-turn planning, tool use, web search) and RLHF checkpoints (DPO, SimPO), extending the RLVR optimization regime beyond verifiable-reward math/code (Figures 7, 8, 18).

Figure 8 shows spectral diagnostics for four agent checkpoints (SkyRL-Agent-WebResearch-8B, VT-deepsearch-8B, VT-SWE-8B, agentflow-planner-7b), using the second block O-projection layer as an exemplar readout:
- **Singular value curves:** Nearly identical to their respective base models for all four checkpoints, confirming spectrum preservation.
- **Principal angle curves:** Cosine values remain near 1.0 across all $k$, indicating minimal rotation of top singular subspaces.

Figure 18 extends this to RLHF checkpoints (Llama-3-Instruct-8B-DPO and Llama-3-Instruct-8B-SimPO from Meng et al., 2024b): "Across RLHF checkpoints, RL training preserves layer spectra and induces only minor rotation of the top-k subspaces, consistent with the RLVR regime."

Dashed red boxes highlight stripe regions where RL updates concentrate outside principal-weight bands. The paper notes that these represent "robust off-principal routing in agent and tool-use settings" — the same qualitative pattern observed in math/code RLVR.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Learning rate scaling test for the precision-gate claim (Section 2.3):** The paper reports — without a dedicated figure in the main text, but described in prose — that "increasing the learning rate to scale otherwise sub-ULP updates above the representable threshold" causes the apparent update sparsity to "largely disappear." This directly tests Gate III's claim that sparsity is a precision-amplified artifact: if larger learning rates push micro-updates above the bfloat16 representability floor, sparsity should decrease. The paper states the test was performed but does not provide a quantitative sparsity-vs-learning-rate table, which is a minor weakness — the reader cannot assess the magnitude of the effect. The paper also cites concurrent work (Shenfeld et al., 2025) independently confirming that "sparsity mostly vanishes under float32 storage" without performance improvement, providing external validation.

**Extended training for sparse fine-tuning (Table 5 vs. Table 4):** Extending training from 320 to 500 steps reveals that:
- The safe mask ($M_{\text{princ}}^c \cup M_{\text{low}}$) narrows its gap to dense from 1.20 points (56.24 vs. 57.44 at 320 steps) to 0.22 points (58.37 vs. 58.59 at 500 steps), nearly matching dense performance.
- The principal-only mask gap actually widens from 3.62 points at 320 steps to 3.15 points at 500 steps (dense: 58.59, principal-only: 55.44) — the absolute gap remains large, confirming principal-direction updates are structurally ineffective for RL, not merely slower to converge.
- The random mask gap widens from 0.90 points (55.34 vs. 56.24) at 320 steps to 1.48 points (56.89 vs. 58.37) at 500 steps, indicating that the geometric selection becomes more important — not less — with extended training. This is a non-obvious finding: one might expect random masking to eventually catch up given sufficient training, but the opposite occurs.

**Rank and learning rate sweep for LoRA/PiSSA (Figures 10-11):** The ablation across three ranks {8, 32, 64} and three learning rates {$1 \times 10^{-4}, 5 \times 10^{-5}, 1 \times 10^{-5}$} tests several hypotheses simultaneously:
- **Does PiSSA help at any rank?** No — across all nine (rank, learning rate) combinations for DS-Qwen-1.5B, PiSSA provides no clear advantage over LoRA.
- **Is instability specific to high learning rates?** Yes — PiSSA's collapse is most pronounced at $1 \times 10^{-4}$, the highest learning rate tested. At lower learning rates, both methods are stable but PiSSA still provides no benefit.
- **Is instability specific to high ranks?** Partially — on DS-Qwen-1.5B, rank-64 PiSSA at $1 \times 10^{-4}$ shows the sharpest collapse, while rank-8 and rank-32 are somewhat more stable. However, the Llama experiment (Figure 11) uses a fixed rank of 64 and shows severe PiSSA collapse, confirming that learning rate is the primary driver, with rank as a secondary factor.
- **Does LoRA itself have stability limits?** Yes — even LoRA shows some oscillation at $1 \times 10^{-4}$ on both models, suggesting that very high learning rates challenge all low-rank adapters, but PiSSA is significantly more fragile.

**Two model families (DS-Qwen-1.5B and Llama-3.2-3B) for LoRA comparison (Figures 10 vs. 11):** The replication across different model families and training data (DeepMath-103K vs. a Math corpus) strengthens the claim that the principal-initialization penalty is not Qwen-specific. The qualitative results are consistent — PiSSA either provides no gain (low learning rates) or collapses (high learning rates) — though the Llama collapse at $5 \times 10^{-5}$ is more severe than the DS-Qwen-1.5B collapse at the same learning rate, suggesting some model-specific sensitivity.

**Multiple RL algorithms and datasets in cross-run stability analysis (Table 2, Figure 12):** The five independent DS-Qwen-1.5B runs use different RL variants (GRPO, DAPO, Reinforcement++) and different data mixtures (math, code, mixed). The high Jaccard overlaps across these diverse configurations provide strong evidence that the routing bias is "largely invariant to datasets and RL recipes" — it is the base model's geometry, not the specific training algorithm or data, that determines where updates land. This is a critical robustness check because it rules out the alternative hypothesis that the routing pattern is an artifact of a particular RL algorithm's gradient structure or a particular dataset's problem distribution.

**Multiple model families for routing bias (Figure 13):** The observation of "similar stripe-structured footprints" on Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-Small-24B extends the routing bias claim beyond the Qwen family. However, this evidence is presented only qualitatively in the appendix (Figure 13), without quantitative Jaccard or consensus metrics — making it suggestive rather than definitive. A quantitative cross-family comparison would have been stronger.

**Temporal stability analysis (Figures 3, 14):** Tracking the row-wise and column-wise update ratios at three time points ($t \in \{240, 720, 1200\}$) establishes that the routing bias is not a transient artifact of early training. The relative profiles (peak and trough positions) remain aligned while overall density grows, indicating "a temporally stable phenomenon." The lack of clear trend in column-wise Q and row-wise O ratios (Figure 14) confirms the bias is structured, not a uniform growth pattern.

**DAPO without explicit KL penalty (Figure 15):** To establish that Gate I's KL leash is active even without an explicit KL term, the paper tracks token-wise KL loss during a DAPO run without KL penalty. Figure 15 shows "a steadily increasing KL loss instead of being unconstrained" — the ratio clipping alone is sufficient to bound policy divergence. This validates that the KL anchor (Gate I) is not an artifact of an explicit regularization term but emerges from the trust-region clipping mechanism inherent to PPO-family algorithms.

**Last-step vs. alternative aggregation for PRM:** Not applicable — the paper does not train process reward models, as it analyzes publicly released checkpoints rather than training RL systems from scratch.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim: "RLVR exhibits a persistent, model-conditioned optimization bias in where updates land — highly consistent across runs and largely invariant to datasets and RL recipes."

**What the experiments demonstrate:** The cross-run stability analysis (Table 2, Figure 12) convincingly shows that five independent DS-Qwen-1.5B runs with different algorithms and datasets produce update masks with substantially above-chance Jaccard overlap (0.55–0.60 vs. random baselines of 0.37–0.47). The consensus ratio visualizations (Figure 2) reveal structured stripe patterns. The temporal analysis (Figure 3) shows the bias is stable over training. The cross-model-family observation (Figure 13) suggests generality.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate:** The "largely invariant to datasets and RL recipes" claim is supported by five runs from a single base model (DS-Qwen-1.5B) — a sample of one model family. The cross-family evidence (Figure 13) is qualitative only, without quantitative overlap metrics for Llama and Mistral. To fully support invariance, one would want: (a) quantitative Jaccard matrices for multiple base models, (b) runs that hold the dataset constant while varying the algorithm (and vice versa) to isolate which factor drives variance, and (c) statistical confidence intervals on the overlap estimates. The current evidence supports consistency *for DS-Qwen-1.5B* but generalizes only qualitatively to other families.

**Conditionality:** The temporal analysis (Figure 3) is based on a single training run (Appendix C.1: "training setting in Appendix C.1"). The claim of temporal stability would be stronger with multiple seeds showing the same peak/trough alignment — as it stands, we cannot distinguish a genuine stable pattern from a single-run trajectory that happens to preserve its shape.

---

#### Claim: "RLVR learns off the principals — it preserves spectral geometry, avoids principal weights, and the optimization bias vanishes when pretrained geometry is disrupted."

**What the experiments demonstrate:** The spectral comparisons (Figures 4, 16, 17) clearly show that RLVR checkpoints have much smaller subspace rotation and spectral drift than SFT checkpoints across three model families. The principal-weight overlap analysis (Figure 5) shows sub-random overlap, with the effect strengthening when low-magnitude principal weights are excluded. The geometry-scrambling experiment (Figure 6) provides strong causal evidence: overlap drops to random in disrupted layers while remaining high in untouched layers.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate:** Several aspects are underspecified:
- **The principal-weight overlap analysis (Figure 5)** does not report the $\alpha$ threshold for "top-$\alpha$ fraction" or the SVD rank $k$, making it impossible to reproduce the exact numbers or assess sensitivity to these hyperparameters. The random baseline itself depends on $\alpha$ — without knowing $\alpha$, the reader cannot independently verify the sub-random claim.
- **The geometry-scrambling experiment (Figure 6)** reports overlap ratios but does not report the absolute number of changed weights in intervened vs. untouched layers — if the rotation inadvertently changed the magnitude distribution of weights (even though singular values are preserved, individual entry magnitudes may shift), the bfloat16 threshold could interact differently with the scrambled layers, potentially confounding the overlap metric. The paper's Proposition D.1 proves function preservation but does not address whether the *distribution of individual weight magnitudes* is preserved under the rotation.
- **The claim "avoids principal weights"** is demonstrated on *stored* (bf16-visible) updates, not on the full float32 gradient trajectory. Since the paper itself argues that sparsity is a precision-amplified artifact, the principal-weight avoidance observed in bf16 masks may partly reflect Gate III amplification rather than genuine steering — principal weights tend to have larger magnitudes, meaning their bfloat16 ULP is larger, meaning the same-sized float32 update is *less* likely to be visible for principal weights than for low-magnitude weights, independent of any geometric steering. The paper acknowledges this interaction in the $M_{\text{princ}} \cap M_{\text{low}}$ analysis (Figure 5), but the point deserves more weight: principal-weight avoidance is at least partially a precision artifact, not purely a geometric steering effect. The geometry-scrambling experiment partially addresses this (since magnitudes are preserved under orthogonal transformations), but would be stronger if complemented by a float32 analysis showing that the *gradient magnitudes* themselves are smaller for principal directions, not just the *stored updates*.

---

#### Claim: "SFT-era principal-aligned PEFT methods are misaligned with RLVR's off-principal dynamics — principal-only sparse masks degrade performance, and PiSSA offers no gain over LoRA while collapsing at higher learning rates."

**What the experiments demonstrate:** The sparse fine-tuning results (Figure 9, Tables 4–5) convincingly show that principal-only masking produces the worst KL trajectory and lowest accuracy, while the off-principal-focused masks (especially $M_{\text{princ}}^c \cup M_{\text{low}}$) closely track dense RLVR. The LoRA-vs-PiSSA results (Figures 10–11) show PiSSA provides no benefit across ranks and learning rates and collapses at high learning rates.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate:** Several limitations:
- **The sparse fine-tuning uses one-shot, fixed masks** — the masks are computed once from the pretrained model and held constant throughout training. This is a probe of which weight subsets *can* support RLVR, not a practical training algorithm. The paper acknowledges this (Section 5.1: "While the masks used here are one-shot and fixed, combining this framework with dynamic mask refresh or adaptive scheduling is a promising next step"), but the distinction matters for the claim that "SFT-era sparse fine-tuning" is misaligned — SFT-era sparse fine-tuning often uses dynamic pruning, gradient-based selection, or learned masks, which may behave differently from the one-shot geometry-based masks tested here. The paper is critiquing a straw-man version of SFT sparse fine-tuning.
- **The LoRA experiments train for only 200 steps** — within the LoRA literature, 200 steps may be sufficient for convergence on small datasets, but the paper's own dense RLVR runs go to 300–500 steps. It is possible that PiSSA's disadvantage is a transient early-training effect that would diminish with longer training. The PiSSA collapse at high learning rates is clearly harmful regardless of duration, but the claim of "no gain" at lower learning rates could be duration-dependent.
- **The LoRA experiments use one training corpus per model** (DeepMath-103K for DS-Qwen-1.5B, a Math corpus for Llama) — the paper does not test whether PiSSA might provide gains on different data distributions (e.g., code, agents). The principal-initialization penalty may be specific to math reasoning or may depend on how well the pretrained principal directions align with the task.
- **No comparison to other LoRA variants:** The paper only tests PiSSA vs. standard LoRA. Other SFT-era LoRA variants — DoRA (weight-decomposed), LoRA+ (different learning rates for A and B), AdaLoRA (adaptive rank allocation) — are not evaluated. The claim that "SFT-era PEFT methods" are misaligned is therefore supported for exactly two methods (principal-only sparse masking and PiSSA), not for the broader PEFT landscape. A fairer characterization would be that *principal-aligned* methods are misaligned, which is a narrower and more defensible claim.
- **The sparse fine-tuning results show the safe mask outperforms the random mask by 0.90 points at 320 steps and 1.48 at 500 steps** — these are modest margins given the 74% parameter budget. The practical benefit over random masking is real but small, suggesting that while the geometric theory identifies the right subspace, much of the gain comes simply from having a sufficient update budget rather than from the precise geometric selection. The paper's narrative emphasizes the geometric alignment; the numbers suggest a more nuanced picture where volume (how many parameters are updated) and geometry (which specific parameters) both matter, with geometry contributing a small but consistent additional benefit.

---

#### Claim: "RLVR's optimization dynamics — minimal rotation, spectrum preservation, off-principal routing — persist beyond verifiable math/code to agents and RLHF."

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figures 7, 8, and 18 show spectral diagnostics and update-principal misalignment for a total of 8 checkpoints across agent and RLHF settings. The spectral signatures qualitatively match those observed for math/code RLVR — near-identical singular value profiles and minimal subspace rotation.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate:** The agent/RLHF analysis is substantially less rigorous than the main math/code analysis:
- **No quantitative metrics are reported.** For the agent checkpoints, the paper shows visual plots (Figures 7, 8) but does not report principal angle values, NSS numbers, or overlap ratios as it does (at least partially) for the math/code checkpoints. The reader must visually confirm that "spectra remain near-identical" — which is apparent from the plots but not quantified.
- **No cross-run stability analysis or consensus ratio visualization for agents/RLHF.** The key evidence for the "model-conditioned bias" claim — the Jaccard overlap across independent runs — is absent for these settings. We cannot assess whether the agent/RLHF update patterns are consistent across runs or merely happen to look off-principal in a single checkpoint.
- **The agent checkpoints are all from the Qwen family (Table 3).** There is no Llama or Mistral agent checkpoint analyzed, limiting the cross-family generalization evidence for agents specifically.
- **The RLHF checkpoints are from the Llama family but only two are analyzed (DPO and SimPO).** This is a thin sample for claiming a general RLHF regime.

The paper's claim that these signatures "persist beyond verifiable math/code" is **suggestive** rather than **demonstrated** — the qualitative evidence is consistent with the claim but lacks the quantitative rigor and cross-run replication that the main math/code analysis provides. This is perhaps the most honest characterization: the paper would need additional agent/RLHF checkpoints, quantitative metrics, and cross-run analyses to elevate this from a promising observation to a robust finding.

---

#### Missing Experiments That Would Have Strengthened the Paper

**Float32 analysis of update routing:** The paper's central argument — that sparsity is a precision-amplified artifact of an underlying routing bias — would be substantially strengthened by showing that the *gradient magnitudes* (computed in float32 optimizer states) exhibit the same off-principal bias, not just the *stored updates*. If float32 gradient norms are systematically smaller for principal weights and larger for off-principal weights, the precision-amplification interpretation is on solid ground. If not — if the float32 gradients are uniform in magnitude but the bfloat16 threshold simply hides updates to large-magnitude (principal) weights — then the "off-principal routing" is largely a precision artifact, not a genuine optimization bias. The paper's current evidence cannot fully distinguish these interpretations, though Gate II provides a theoretical rationale for genuine routing.

**Dynamic or learned sparse masks:** The paper critiques SFT-era sparse fine-tuning but only tests one-shot, geometry-based masks. Comparing against a dynamic pruning method (e.g., iterative magnitude pruning during training, or gradient-based mask adaptation) would test whether the geometry-informed static mask is genuinely superior or merely a reasonable first guess that any adaptive method would match or exceed.

**Longer LoRA/PiSSA training:** The 200-step training horizon is a limitation. Extending to 500 steps (as done for sparse fine-tuning in Table 5) would test whether PiSSA's disadvantage persists or whether standard LoRA and PiSSA converge to similar performance given sufficient training, at the lower, stable learning rates.

**More RLHF and agent checkpoints with quantitative metrics:** The paper's most expansive claim — that the Three-Gate Theory describes a universal RL post-training regime — requires quantitative validation across a broader sample. The current agent/RLHF evidence is more of an existence proof (the signature *can* appear in these settings) than a universality demonstration (it *consistently does*).

**Confidence intervals and multiple seeds for training experiments:** The sparse fine-tuning results (Tables 4–5) report single-run accuracies. The KL curves in Figure 9 appear to be from single runs (no error bands are shown). The LoRA/PiSSA curves in Figures 10–11 also lack visible error bands. Without multiple seeds, the reported accuracy differences (e.g., 56.24 vs. 55.34 for safe mask vs. random mask at 320 steps) could be within run-to-run variance. This is a significant limitation for the sparse fine-tuning ablation, where the claimed geometric advantage over random masking is modest (~1 percentage point at 320 steps, ~1.5 at 500 steps) and could plausibly be noise.

---

#### Summary Assessment

The experiments provide strong support for the paper's core mechanistic claims: RDVR updates exhibit structured, geometry-dependent routing patterns that avoid principal directions and preserve spectral structure, in stark contrast to SFT. The causal evidence from geometry disruption (Figure 6) is particularly compelling and distinguishes this work from merely correlational studies. The sparse fine-tuning and LoRA experiments demonstrate the practical implications — principal-aligned PEFT methods indeed underperform or collapse under RLVR, validating the theory's predictive power.

However, the evidence base has systematic limitations that constrain the scope of the conclusions. The cross-run consistency and routing bias analyses are anchored primarily on a single model family (DS-Qwen-1.5B), with cross-family evidence that is qualitative rather than quantitative. The precision-amplification mechanism (Gate III), while theoretically well-motivated, is not fully disentangled from genuine geometric steering in the principal-weight avoidance analysis — some fraction of the observed "avoidance" is almost certainly a bfloat16 threshold effect rather than differential gradient routing. The agent/RLHF generalization claims rest on a thin sample without quantitative metrics or cross-run replication. The sparse fine-tuning and LoRA results are limited by short training horizons, single seeds, and a narrow selection of PEFT methods (only two are tested).

The paper's contributions are therefore most solidly established for the core geometric characterization of math/code RLVR on Qwen-family models, with suggestive but not definitive extension to other model families, tasks, and PEFT methods. The Three-Gate Theory provides a coherent, predictive framework that unifies the observations, but the empirical validation — while strong in its causal tests (Figure 6) — is incomplete in its quantitative coverage (particularly for Gates I and III, whose evidence is more theoretical than experimental). This is understandable for a first paper opening a new line of investigation, but the reader should recognize which parts of the picture are painted in high resolution (Qwen-family spectral analysis, causal geometry disruption) and which remain sketched in broad strokes (cross-family quantitative analysis, agent/RLHF replication, precision-vs-steering disentanglement).

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost for Computing the Compute-Optimal Policy Is Unaccounted For

**The limitation.** The paper's headline $4\times$ efficiency gains over best-of-N are computed *after* the difficulty of each question is known. The procedure for obtaining this difficulty estimate consumes significant computation that is explicitly excluded from the reported budget. Specifically, the method generates 2048 samples per question and scores them with the PRM to bin questions into five difficulty quintiles. The paper acknowledges this in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity."

**The consequence.** In any realistic deployment, amortizing 2048 generations per prompt to estimate difficulty would dwarf the test-time compute budget being studied (which maxes out at 256–512 generations). The reported $4\times$ efficiency improvements therefore represent an **upper bound on achievable gains** that is only attainable if difficulty can be estimated at negligible cost — a condition the paper does not satisfy. For low-volume, high-stakes applications (where 2048 generations might be justifiable amortized over many subsequent queries with similar difficulty profiles), this limitation may be less severe. For per-query allocation decisions, where each prompt's difficulty must be estimated independently, it is fatal. The paper's proposal to train a difficulty predictor from question text is mentioned as future work but is not realized.

**Evidence in the paper.** The difficulty estimation procedure is described in Section 3.2, with the cost explicitly noted there. The $4\times$ claims appear in Section 5.3 (search: "16 generations matching 64") and Section 6 (revisions: "64 generations matching 256"). The cost of the 2048-sample estimation is never added to the x-axis of the scaling curves. This means, for example, that the compute-optimal search curve in Figure 4 at 16 generations should, in a fair accounting, be plotted at $16 + 2048 = 2064$ generations — a point far to the right of even the largest baseline budget shown.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly flags this as "a key avenue for future work" (Section 3.2) and suggests training a model to predict difficulty directly from the question text, or using the exploration-exploitation framing of multi-armed bandits to amortize difficulty estimation into the problem-solving process. Neither approach is implemented or evaluated. The predicted (non-oracle) difficulty bins partially address the *oracle* aspect of the limitation (showing that PRM scores can substitute for ground-truth correctness), but they do not address the *cost* aspect — the predicted bins still require 2048 generations per question.

---

### All Results Are on a Single Benchmark (MATH) with a Single Model Family (PaLM 2-S\*)

**The limitation.** The entire experimental analysis — search against PRMs, iterative revisions, compute-optimal policy selection, FLOPs-matched comparisons, and all ablations — is conducted on the MATH benchmark (500 test questions) using PaLM 2-S\* as the base model. The paper states in Section 4 that it "believes this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but this claim is not empirically verified by any cross-model or cross-benchmark experiments.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or benchmark-specific:

- **PRM quality and over-optimization behavior** depend on PaLM 2-S\*'s output distribution and error patterns. A model with different calibration properties, different reasoning strategies, or different typical mistakes might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves — and consequently different compute-optimal policies. For instance, if a different model's errors are more systematically identifiable by a PRM, beam search might not over-optimize on easy problems, altering the optimal strategy per bin.

- **The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples** depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities. The edit-distance-based pairing strategy for constructing revision training data (Section 6.1) assumes that structurally similar incorrect answers provide useful context for learning to correct mistakes. This assumption may not hold for models with different in-context learning behaviors.

- **MATH consists exclusively of competition-level symbolic math problems.** The finding that beam search helps on medium-difficulty problems but over-optimizes on easy ones (Figure 3, right) may be specific to tasks where correctness is deterministic and verifier training benefits from clear correctness signals. For tasks with ambiguous or multi-dimensional correctness criteria (open-ended generation, creative writing, dialogue), the verifier itself would be harder to train, and the difficulty-dependent dynamics could differ qualitatively.

- **The test set contains only 500 questions**, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each, then further split for two-fold cross-validation — meaning the compute-optimal policy is selected based on **~50 questions per fold per bin**. This is a small sample, and the paper does not report confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves. The specific strategies selected (e.g., beam search $M=4$ for bin 3 at 64 generations) may not be robust at this sample size.

**Evidence in the paper.** Section 4 describes the experimental setup as MATH-only with PaLM 2-S\*. The difficulty bin sizes and cross-validation protocol are described in Section 3.2. The paper does not include any experiments on alternative benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, HumanEval, MBPP, ARC) or alternative model families. The "belief" that PaLM 2-S\* is representative is stated as an assertion in Section 4 without supporting evidence.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation beyond the statement of belief about representativeness. Section 8 (conclusion) does not mention benchmark or model generalization as an avenue for future work, focusing instead on combining search with revisions, improving difficulty estimation, and developing self-improvement pipelines. A reader interested in applying these insights to a different model (e.g., a Llama or Mistral variant) or a different domain (e.g., code generation, scientific reasoning) would need to replicate the entire analysis from scratch — the paper provides a methodology but no evidence that the specific numerical findings (e.g., the $4\times$ factor, the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratios, the difficulty thresholds where beam search becomes beneficial) generalize.

---

### The $14\times$ Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained and Uses No Test-Time Compute

**The limitation.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023) rather than compute-optimal pretraining (Hoffmann et al., 2022), where both data and model size would be scaled. Additionally, the $14\times$ larger model is evaluated using only **greedy decoding** — no majority voting, no best-of-N sampling, no search, and no revision model. The paper acknowledges this explicitly in Section 7:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

**The consequence.** The comparison is systematically biased in favor of test-time compute for two independent reasons:

1. **Non-optimal pretraining baseline:** A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with $14\times$ more total FLOPs (scaling both parameters and data tokens proportionally) would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model, making the pretraining baseline weaker than it could be. The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining — for example, +27.8% relative improvement on easy questions at $R \ll 1$ for revisions (Figure 1, top-right bar chart) — may shrink or reverse against a properly compute-optimal larger model.

2. **No test-time compute for the larger model:** The $14\times$ larger model is evaluated with greedy decoding only. A fairer comparison would give the larger model at least *some* test-time compute budget (e.g., best-of-8, majority voting, or its own PRM search) to see whether the advantage of the smaller model with extensive test-time compute persists. Given that the paper's entire thesis is that test-time compute is under-invested, it is inconsistent to deny the larger model any test-time compute at all. It is possible — and the paper provides no evidence either way — that best-of-4 sampling with the larger model would outperform the smaller model's compute-optimal scaling at all difficulty levels, even in the $R \ll 1$ regime.

**Evidence in the paper.** Section 7 describes the FLOPs-matched setup, specifying $D_{\text{pretrain}}$ is held fixed while parameters are scaled. Figure 9 and Figure 1 (right bar charts) present the FLOPs-matched results. The larger model's performance is plotted as stars in Figure 9, and the text notes it uses greedy decoding (Section 7, description of Figure 9). The acknowledgment about compute-optimal pretraining is quoted above.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly flags the compute-optimal pretraining caveat and leaves it to future work. The absence of test-time compute for the larger model is *not* flagged as a limitation — the paper treats the comparison as "test-time compute with a small model vs. pretraining with a large model," framing it as a resource allocation question (spend FLOPs on training or on inference). However, a more realistic framing would be "how should a fixed total FLOPs budget be split between training and inference," which would permit the larger model to also benefit from some test-time compute. This more general question is not addressed. The practical consequence is that the paper's comparison answers a narrower question than the one most practitioners would ask: not "should I use test-time compute or train a bigger model?" but "if I can only use test-time compute with the small model and only greedy decoding with the large model, which wins?" The answer to the first question remains unknown.

---

### The Revision Model Suffers from a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate, and Revision Training Is Fragile

**The limitation.** The revision model, during sequential revision chains, converts approximately 38% of correct intermediate answers back to incorrect ones (Section 6.1, reported as a percentage of correct answers that get "revised" wrong). This is a direct consequence of the training data construction: the model is trained only on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect (followed by a correct target), so it has no learned behavior for the case where the current answer is already correct — it always tries to "revise," and sometimes this breaks a correct answer.

**The consequence.** The reversion problem means that **the last answer in a revision chain is not reliably the best answer**; the system must rely on verifier-based selection or majority voting across the entire chain to pick the best intermediate answer. This selection mechanism is an imperfect patch — it adds computational overhead (the verifier must score every step in the chain) and introduces its own failure modes (verifier errors can select an incorrect answer over a correct one). Moreover, the reversion rate means that simply lengthening the revision chain does not monotonically improve quality — after some optimal chain length, additional revisions become counterproductive because they are more likely to break a correct answer than to fix an incorrect one. The paper reports that revision model pass@1 plateaus around 24–25% by steps 15–20 and remains in the 23–25% range out to 64 steps (Figure 6, left), suggesting this saturation is already reached.

**Evidence in the paper.** The 38% reversion rate is stated in Section 6.1 without a dedicated figure or table — it appears as a prose statistic. The plateau in per-step pass@1 is shown in Figure 6 (left). The need for verifier-based or majority-based selection across the chain is described in Section 6.1 as mitigation. The ReST$^{EM}$ experiment in Appendix K (Figure 16) provides additional evidence of fragility: attempting to optimize the revision model with RL-style training caused performance to **degrade substantially** with sequential revisions, with fully sequential performance dropping to approximately 33.5% compared to roughly 38.5% at the optimal ratio. The paper hypothesizes that "on-policy data collection in ReST$^{EM}$ exacerbates spurious correlations in revision data."

**Mitigation status.** The paper mitigates the reversion problem with post-hoc selection (verifier-based best-of-N weighted across the chain, or majority voting), but this is a patching strategy, not a solution. A principled fix — training the model to recognize when no revision is needed (e.g., by including sequences where the current answer is correct and the target is to output it unchanged) — is not explored. The ReST$^{EM}$ failure (Appendix K) is reported but not deeply diagnosed, leaving the fragility of revision training as an open problem. For practitioners, this means that deploying revision models requires careful monitoring of chain quality and likely an additional verifier, increasing system complexity and compute cost.

---

### Verifier Over-Optimization Limits Scaling and the Paper Does Not Improve Verifier Robustness

**The limitation.** The paper identifies verifier over-optimization as the primary bottleneck preventing unbounded improvements from additional test-time compute — beam search degrades easy-problem performance at high budgets (Figure 3, right), lookahead search paradoxically performs worst overall despite being the strongest optimizer (Figure 3, left), and qualitative examples in Appendix M show search producing degenerate outputs (repetitive low-information steps, overly short 1–2 step solutions) that score highly under the PRM. However, the paper does not attempt to improve the verifier to overcome this bottleneck. The PRM is trained once via Monte Carlo rollouts and held fixed; no adversarial training, ensemble verification, or constrained search methods are tested.

**The consequence.** The compute-optimal policy **mitigates** over-optimization by routing easy problems away from aggressive search (using best-of-N instead of beam search) and routing hard problems away from any method (since the base model cannot produce correct solutions anyway). But it does not **solve** the underlying problem. On medium-difficulty problems where beam search is deployed, over-optimization still limits the scaling ceiling — the beam search curves in Figure 3 flatten and, in some configurations, decline well before the maximum budget of 256 generations is exhausted. This means the compute-optimal approach is **fundamentally bounded by verifier quality**: the maximum accuracy achievable with any amount of test-time compute is capped by the point at which additional search over-optimizes the available verifier signal. Improving the PRM would shift this ceiling upward and potentially alter the optimal strategy per difficulty bin — for instance, a more robust PRM might make beam search beneficial even on easy problems, changing the compute-optimal policy.

**Evidence in the paper.** The over-optimization evidence is concentrated in Section 5.3 (Figure 3 and associated discussion) and Appendix M (qualitative examples of degenerate beam search outputs). The paper explicitly identifies "over-optimization of the PRM" as the explanation for beam search's degradation at high budgets and lookahead search's underperformance. However, no experiment attempts to improve verifier robustness — the PRM architecture, training procedure, and aggregation method are all fixed after the initial development in Section 5.1 and Appendices D–E.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not directly address improving verifier robustness. Section 8 suggests "improving verifier robustness" as a direction for future work but provides no concrete proposals or preliminary experiments. For practitioners, this limitation means that the current results are **specific to the verifier quality achievable with one particular training recipe** (Monte Carlo rollouts from PaLM 2-S\*, soft labeling, last-step aggregation, best-of-N weighted selection). A better resourced team that can invest in more sophisticated verifier training (e.g., larger-scale rollout data, on-policy PRM updates during training, ensemble PRMs) may find that the difficulty thresholds shift and that different compute-optimal strategies emerge. The paper provides a framework for analyzing this — the difficulty-bin and compute-optimal policy methodology — but the specific optimal strategies it reports are contingent on its particular verifier.

### The Paper Never Combines PRM Search with Iterative Revisions, Leaving the Full Potential of Joint Optimization Unknown

**The limitation.** The paper studies two complementary test-time compute mechanisms — PRM-guided search (which modifies how outputs are selected) and iterative revisions (which modifies what the model generates) — but never combines them. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

This is a significant gap because the two mechanisms have complementary, difficulty-dependent strengths identified by the paper itself: revisions are most effective on easy problems (where the model needs local refinement of roughly correct answers), while PRM search is most effective on medium-hard problems (where the model needs to explore qualitatively different solution strategies). A combined system — using the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search, or using the PRM to guide which revision branches to pursue — could capture the benefits of both and potentially break through the performance ceilings that each method hits individually.

**The consequence.** The reported results represent a **lower bound** on what a fully integrated system could achieve. The compute-optimal policy in Section 5 (search) and Section 6 (revisions) selects between search algorithms and revision strategies independently — it never considers configurations where, say, a beam search tree uses revision-model-conditioned proposals at each step, or where a revision chain uses PRM scores to decide whether to continue revising or restart. This means the $4\times$ efficiency gain over best-of-N, while impressive, may be far from the true Pareto frontier of what test-time compute can achieve. The difficulty-dependent analysis (search helps medium problems, revisions help easy problems) strongly suggests that combining them would yield gains on both categories simultaneously, but this remains untested.

**Evidence in the paper.** The search experiments (Section 5) use the few-shot prompted base model as the proposal distribution, not the revision model. The revision experiments (Section 6) use verifier-based selection (best-of-N weighted or majority voting) on revision chains but never apply beam search or lookahead search to select among revision branches. The acknowledgment in Section 8 is the only mention of this gap. No ablation, pilot experiment, or even conceptual design for a combined system is provided.

**Mitigation status.** The paper flags this as a natural next step in Section 8 but does not pursue it. For practitioners, this limitation is both a caution and an opportunity: the current results should not be interpreted as the best that test-time compute can do, but rather as evidence that even uncombined mechanisms provide substantial gains. A team willing to engineer a combined system (revision-model beam search, PRM-guided revision branching) may achieve efficiency gains beyond the $4\times$ reported here, but the paper provides no guidance on architecture, hyperparameters, or expected interactions between the two mechanisms. The risk of negative interaction — e.g., the revision model's distribution shift confusing the PRM, or beam search's over-optimization being exacerbated by revision-conditioned proposals — is uncharacterized.
