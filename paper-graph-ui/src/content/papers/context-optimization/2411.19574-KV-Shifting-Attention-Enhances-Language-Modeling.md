## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **KV shifting attention**, a modification to the standard transformer attention mechanism that decouples keys and values by blending each token's key and value with those of its preceding token, reducing the depth and width requirements for forming induction heads (the circuit that enables pattern matching, e.g., seeing "[A][B]" earlier in context and predicting "[B]" when "[A]" reappears). Through theoretical analysis, the authors prove that KV shifting attention enables a single-layer, single-head transformer to exactly represent induction heads where standard attention requires at least two layers and additional width, and they validate this on toy induction and n-gram tasks before scaling to pre-training experiments with models up to 19B parameters. Across 2.9B and 19B scales on a private multilingual corpus, KV shifting attention yields better benchmark performance and faster convergence than vanilla attention—for instance, a 2.9B model trained on 500B tokens achieves 38.57% average across Lambada, Winogrande, HellaSwag, ARC, CMMLU, MMLU, and MATH versus 36.45% for the baseline—establishing that a lightweight, architecturally minimal shift operation can strengthen induction-head formation and improve language modeling when applied during pre-training.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Transformers Are Inefficient at Learning Induction Heads

The fundamental issue this paper tackles is that **standard transformer architectures require disproportionate depth and width to implement a computation that is conceptually simple: induction heads**. An induction head is the mechanism that allows a language model to perform primitive pattern completion — when it encounters a token "A" that has appeared earlier in the context, it looks up what token "B" followed "A" previously and predicts "B" at the current position (e.g., in the sequence [A][B]...[A], the model should output [B]). This is widely considered the mechanistic basis for in-context learning (Olsson et al., 2022; Elhage et al., 2021), which is one of the most important emergent capabilities of large language models.

The gap the paper identifies is not that transformers *cannot* form induction heads — they demonstrably do, and this has been extensively studied — but rather that **the standard multi-head attention mechanism is structurally misaligned with the induction task**, requiring wasteful depth and width to approximate what is, at its core, a simple look-up-and-copy operation. The authors observe that while there is a rich literature analyzing induction heads in transformers, "there are few works that utilize analysis of induction heads to modify transformers to enhance their ability to learn induction heads" (Section 1). The field has been content to *explain* induction heads but has not asked whether the architecture could be *improved* to learn them more efficiently.

This gap matters for multiple reasons:

- **Architectural efficiency**: If induction heads are indeed fundamental to in-context learning, then an architecture that requires fewer layers and less hidden dimension to implement them can allocate its representational capacity to other tasks. A model that "wastes" one full attention layer just to copy tokens is operating below its potential.
- **Convergence speed**: As the paper demonstrates empirically, the bias toward easier induction learning translates to faster loss reduction during pre-training, which has direct economic implications for training large models.
- **Theoretical understanding and practical improvement**: This paper makes the case that understanding mechanistic circuits like induction heads should feed back into architecture design, not just interpretability studies. The fact that this feedback loop has been largely unexplored represents a missed opportunity.

### Why Induction Heads Are Central to Language Modeling

The paper's motivation rests on the premise that induction heads are not merely an interesting mechanistic curiosity but are **fundamentally important for language modeling itself**. This premise has strong support in prior work:

Olsson et al. (2022) showed that induction heads emerge in transformer language models and correlate with in-context learning ability. They demonstrated that the development of induction heads during training coincides with a phase change in the model's loss curve — the moment when the model transitions from learning statistical bigrams to being able to match and repeat patterns, there is a sharp improvement in loss. This established induction heads as more than just interpretability artifacts; they are operational components that drive performance.

Elhage et al. (2021) provided the mathematical framework for understanding induction heads as circuits formed by the composition of attention heads across layers, introducing the concept of "virtual attention heads" (which the current paper heavily builds upon). They showed that in a two-layer transformer, the first layer's attention head copies information from the previous token position into the current token's residual stream, and the second layer's attention head uses this copied information to attend to the token that followed the previous occurrence — a coordinated, cross-layer operation.

Subsequent work has cemented the link to in-context learning: Crosbie & Shutova (2024) demonstrated that induction heads are specifically essential for pattern matching in ICL, and Sanford et al. (2024b) showed their importance in multi-step reasoning tasks. Song et al. (2024) connected induction heads to out-of-distribution generalization through compositional pattern recognition.

Given this evidence, improving a model's ability to form induction heads is not a narrow optimization — it targets a mechanism that is implicated in many of the capabilities that make large language models useful. The paper's motivation is therefore well-grounded: if you can make induction more efficient, you should improve language modeling across the board.

### Prior Work Falls Short in Two Ways

The authors identify two specific shortcomings in how the research community has engaged with induction heads:

**1. The architectural requirements of induction heads have been documented but not addressed.**

Sanford et al. (2024a) provided a formal proof that a **single-layer transformer cannot implement induction heads**. This is a theoretical result with practical implications: any transformer model must allocate at least two layers to this primitive operation. The current paper does not dispute this result — in fact, Theorem 1 in the paper (Section 3.1) reproduces a version of the two-layer construction from Wang et al. (2024) showing how a standard transformer approximates induction heads. But the authors argue that the two-layer requirement is an artifact of the standard attention mechanism, not an inherent property of the induction computation itself. The operation "find the previous occurrence of this token, then output the token that followed it" does not conceptually require two stages of processing — it requires only that the attention mechanism can look up a token by its preceding neighbor's key.

The paper's core insight, elaborated in Section 2.1, is that the two-layer requirement arises because of the **causal mask constraint combined with the binding of keys and values to the same token**. In standard attention, the key of token $i$ provides access only to the value of token $i$. To implement induction — where you want to use token $i$ as a *query* but retrieve the value of token $i+1$ — the model must route information through an intermediate step. As formalized in Property 1 (Equation 3), the virtual attention head operation $(A^{h_2} A^{h_1})$ is constrained by the causal mask such that the $(j, i+1)$ entry depends on sums over tokens between $i+1$ and $j$. This means that to attend to token $i+1$ using information from token $i$, the $i+1$ token must first encode information about token $i$ into its hidden state — even if that information is irrelevant for predicting token $i+2$. This imposes a width requirement because the hidden state dimension must carry this "pass-through" information alongside the token's own semantic content. The paper refers to this as a requirement for "additional width."

**2. The depth and width inefficiency has been an underexplored design target.**

While mechanistic interpretability researchers have mapped the circuits (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022; Bansal et al., 2023; Conmy et al., 2023) and theorists have studied how transformers learn induction from data (Bietti et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024), no prior work had asked: *can we change the attention mechanism itself to make induction heads easier to form, and if so, does this improve language modeling at scale?* The title of Sanford et al. (2024a) — "One-layer transformers fail to solve the induction heads task" — is simultaneously a clean negative result and, from this paper's perspective, a design challenge. The current paper takes up that challenge directly: it asks whether a small, principled modification to attention can make a one-layer transformer succeed at induction.

### How the Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions its contribution at the intersection of three lines of research:

**From mechanistic interpretability, it takes the circuit analysis but applies it prescriptively.** Most interpretability work asks "what circuits has the model learned?" This paper asks "what circuits *should* the model learn easily based on its architecture, and how can we bias the architecture accordingly?" This is a subtle but important shift from descriptive to prescriptive analysis.

**From theoretical work on transformer expressiveness, it takes the formal characterization of induction heads but uses it to design a more efficient architecture rather than merely to prove bounds.** The paper's Theorems 1 and 2 (Section 3.1) explicitly contrast the constructive proof for two-layer standard transformers against the simpler constructive proof for one-layer KV shifting attention. The theoretical contribution is not a tighter bound on existing architectures but **a demonstration that a different architecture achieves the same representational goal with strictly fewer resources** (one layer vs. two, and smaller hidden dimension).

**From language model pre-training, it takes the standard scaling paradigm and asks whether architectural biases informed by mechanistic understanding can improve the scaling curve.** The authors run experiments at scale (up to 19B parameters, 500B tokens) not just to validate a toy-model insight but to demonstrate that the improvement survives in realistic settings. They explicitly benchmark on standard evaluation suites (MMLU, HellaSwag, ARC, etc.) and report training loss curves to situate KV shifting attention as a practical alternative to vanilla attention, not just a conceptual exercise.

The paper also explicitly frames itself within the broader debate about transformer alternatives. Section 7 (Related Works) acknowledges efforts to replace transformers entirely — RWKV (Peng et al., 2023), Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023), RetNet (Sun et al., 2023) — but argues that transformers retain important properties, particularly for retrieval and replication tasks (citing Jelassi et al. and Alman & Yu, 2024). The implicit claim is: rather than replace the transformer, we can **augment it with minimal, mechanistically-motivated changes** that address a known bottleneck while preserving compatibility with existing training and inference infrastructure. This positioning is important because it makes KV shifting attention a drop-in improvement rather than a competing architecture — the paper emphasizes that the modifications are lightweight (four additional learnable parameters per head, O(ND) additional computation vs. O(ND² + N²D) for standard attention) and provides PyTorch code for both training and inference in Appendix F.

### The Specific Gap: Decoupling Keys and Values

The paper's core technical move is motivated by a direct, almost mechanical observation about the induction heads computation. If induction heads require attending to token $i$ and retrieving the value of token $i+1$, why not simply **decouple the key-value binding** so that attending to the key of token $i$ can retrieve the value of token $i-1$ or $i+1$? This is the intuition behind Equation 5:

$$\hat{K} = \alpha_1 K + \alpha_2 \text{Shift}(K), \quad \hat{V} = \beta_1 V + \beta_2 \text{Shift}(V)$$

The $\text{Shift}(\cdot)$ operator discards the last token and prepends zero, so for token $i$, $\text{Shift}(K)_i = K_{i-1}$ and $\text{Shift}(V)_i = V_{i-1}$. The learnable parameters $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \beta_1, \beta_2$ (per attention head) allow the model to blend the current and previous token's keys and values. By setting $\alpha_1 = 0, \alpha_2 = 1, \beta_1 = 0, \beta_2 = 1$, the model attends using the *previous* token's key but retrieves the *current* token's value — exactly the operation needed for induction without requiring a second layer to copy information forward.

The authors note that they restrict the shift to $i-1$ (not $i+1$) because of the causal mask constraint: the value of token $i+1$ cannot be known when predicting token $i$. However, by symmetrically shifting both keys and values, the model can achieve the same effect: attending to the key of token $i$ retrieves the value of token $i-1$ via the shifted value, and attending to the key of token $i-1$ retrieves the value of token $i$ via the shifted key. This bidirectional decoupling is sufficient to enable single-layer induction.

The initialization scheme — $\alpha_1, \beta_1 \sim U(0,1)$ and $\alpha_2 = 1 - \alpha_1, \beta_2 = 1 - \beta_1$ — ensures that at initialization, the KV shifting attention is a convex combination of the current and previous token's features, which the authors argue preserves the initial optimization landscape while introducing the *capacity* to learn the induction-specialized configuration.

### Summary of the Motivating Logic

The paper's argument can be reconstructed as a chain of reasoning:

1. Induction heads are mechanistically important for in-context learning and multi-step reasoning, as established by prior work.
2. Standard transformers require at least two layers and additional width to implement induction heads because the key and value at each position are bound to the same token, forcing information to be routed through an intermediate residual stream.
3. This inefficiency is not a fundamental limitation of the induction computation — it is an artifact of the standard attention mechanism's key-value binding.
4. By introducing a learnable blend of each token's key and value with its predecessor's key and value, a single-layer attention can directly implement induction heads with no width overhead (Theorem 2).
5. This architectural bias toward easier induction learning should translate to faster convergence and better downstream performance during language model pre-training, which the paper validates empirically at scale.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper proposes a **modified attention mechanism** — called KV shifting attention — that is a drop-in replacement for the standard attention operation in transformer language models. The system is not a new model architecture; it is a new attention kernel that blends each token's key and value vectors with those of its immediate predecessor, making it fundamentally easier for even a single transformer layer to implement the induction heads circuit that underlies in-context learning. The problem it solves is the architectural inefficiency in standard transformers, which require at least two layers and extra hidden dimension width to perform the conceptually simple operation of "find the previous occurrence of token A and output the token B that followed it." The shape of the solution is a lightweight, parameterized linear interpolation between the current token's key/value and the previous token's key/value, controlled by four learnable scalars per attention head, with no change to the rest of the transformer architecture.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has three conceptual components that interact in a straightforward pipeline:

1. **Input Embedding and Standard Q/K/V Projections**: The input sequence $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$ (where $N$ is sequence length and $D$ is hidden dimension) is projected into queries, keys, and values using standard learned weight matrices $W_Q, W_K, W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}$, exactly as in vanilla attention. This produces $Q, K, V \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$.

2. **KV Shifting Module**: The keys and values from step 1 enter a blending stage where each token's projected key and value are linearly combined with the previous token's key and value. Specifically, for each position $i$, the shifted key is $\hat{K}_i = \alpha_1 K_i + \alpha_2 K_{i-1}$ and the shifted value is $\hat{V}_i = \beta_1 V_i + \beta_2 V_{i-1}$, where $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \beta_1, \beta_2$ are learned per-head scalars. The $\text{Shift}(\cdot)$ operation is implemented by discarding the last token and prepending a zero vector at the start. This is the only place where KV shifting attention differs from standard attention.

3. **Standard Attention Output Computation**: The shifted keys and values proceed through the standard scaled dot-product attention with causal masking: $\text{Output} = \text{Softmax}(Q \hat{K}^T / \sqrt{D} \cdot M) \hat{V} W_O$, where $M$ is the causal mask and $W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}$ is the output projection. This output then enters the standard transformer residual stream and MLP layers unchanged.

The information flow is therefore: input embeddings $\rightarrow$ Q/K/V projections $\rightarrow$ shift-blend operation on K and V only $\rightarrow$ scaled dot-product attention with causal mask $\rightarrow$ output projection $\rightarrow$ residual connection and MLP (unchanged). The total added computation is $O(ND)$ for the shift and blend, which is negligible compared to the $O(ND^2 + N^2D)$ cost of the attention computation itself.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the definition and mechanics of induction heads as circuit-level building blocks, and why standard attention requires virtual attention heads across layers to implement them — this provides the baseline that KV shifting attention improves upon.
- **Second**, the concept of virtual attention heads and Property 1 (the causal mask constraint), because these reveal the specific structural inefficiency — the hidden width penalty — that motivates decoupling keys from values.
- **Third**, the KV shifting attention mechanism itself: the equations, the learnable parameters, the initialization scheme, and how it explicitly enables single-layer induction by un-binding the key-value correspondence.
- **Fourth**, the theoretical analysis (Theorems 1 and 2) comparing two-layer standard transformer construction against one-layer KV shifting attention construction for representing induction heads, including the width and depth implications.
- **Fifth**, the learning dynamics analysis (Theorem 3) on simplified synthetic data, showing how the four learnable parameters evolve during training and why the loss landscape biases the model toward induction solutions.
- **Sixth**, the key design choices and trade-offs that inform the large-scale experiments, including why the shift is only applied to K and V (not Q), why the window is limited to adjacent tokens, and how the method maintains compatibility with existing training/inference frameworks.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **proposal paper** whose core idea is that a minimal architectural modification — interpolating keys and values with their immediate predecessors — can significantly reduce the depth and width needed for transformers to learn induction heads, and that this architectural bias translates to better language modeling at scale.

#### Induction Heads: The Baseline Circuit That Motivated the Modification

The paper builds its technical contribution on the concept of induction heads as described by Elhage et al. (2021) and Olsson et al. (2022). An induction head is a circuit within a transformer that performs a specific pattern-matching operation: given a current token, find the most recent previous occurrence of that same token in the context, then predict the token that followed that previous occurrence. In the standard notation used throughout the paper, if the sequence contains the pattern [A][B]... [A], the induction head should output [B] as the prediction at the final position.

The mechanism is not a single attention head but a **composition of two attention heads in two different layers**, with information flowing through the residual stream. The paper describes this as follows:

- **Layer 1's attention head** acts as a "previous token head": for each position, it copies information about the preceding token into the current token's residual stream. This is typically implemented by having the attention pattern focus predominantly on position $i-1$ at each position $i$, with the value matrix extracting useful information.
- **Layer 2's attention head** acts as the "matching head": at the current position, its query vector matches against keys that encode the "previous token" information written by layer 1. When the current token is [A], it finds positions where the layer-1-written information indicates that [A] was the *next* token (i.e., positions $i$ where token $i-1$ was [A]), and through the value matrix retrieves the actual token at those positions (which would be [B]).

The key mechanistic detail is that the two heads operate on different token positions: the layer-1 head attends to position $i-1$ from position $i$, and the layer-2 head uses information from position $i$ to attend to position $i+1$. This cross-position coordination is what makes induction heads a two-layer circuit.

#### Virtual Attention Heads and the Causal Mask Constraint

The paper draws on another insight from Elhage et al. (2021): **virtual attention heads**, which formalize how attention patterns compose across layers. When considering two adjacent attention layers (ignoring residual connections and MLPs for simplicity), the composition can be expressed as:

$$X_2 = A^{h_2} X_1 W_{ov}^{h_2} = (A^{h_2} A^{h_1}) X_0 (W_{ov}^{h_1} W_{ov}^{h_2})$$

where $X_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$ is the input sequence, $X_l \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$ is the output of layer $l$, $A^{h_l} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is the attention weight matrix of head $h$ in layer $l$, and $W_{ov}^{h_l} = W_v^{h_l} W_o^{h_l} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}$ is the combined value-output projection for layer $l$ (where $W_v^{h_l} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}$ is the value projection and $W_o^{h_l} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}$ is the output projection).

**What this equation computes:** it expresses the output of the second layer as a single virtual attention operation whose attention pattern is the matrix product $A^{h_2} A^{h_1}$ applied to the original input $X_0$, and whose output transformation is the product of the two layers' OV matrices. The virtual attention pattern tells us which pairs of input positions $(j, k)$ can influence the output at position $j$ through the composed operation: token $j$ attends to intermediate position $i$ via $A^{h_2}_{j,i}$, and intermediate position $i$ attends to input position $k$ via $A^{h_1}_{i,k}$, so the effective attention from $j$ to $k$ is $\sum_i A^{h_2}_{j,i} A^{h_1}_{i,k}$.

**Why this form matters:** it reveals that multi-layer attention can implement operations that no single-layer attention can, because the composed attention pattern can encode dependencies that are not directly available in a single query-key dot product. However, this composition comes with constraints.

The critical constraint for induction heads is captured in **Property 1**, which specializes the virtual attention composition to the case with causal masking:

$$\text{With causal mask and } j \geq i: \quad (A^{h_2} A^{h_1})_{j, i+1} = \sum_{k=1}^N A^{h_2}_{j,k} A^{h_1}_{k, i+1} = \sum_{k=i+1}^j A^{h_2}_{j,k} A^{h_1}_{k, i+1}$$

**What this equation states:** for token $j$ to attend indirectly to token $i+1$ through the virtual attention mechanism, it must route through some intermediate token $k$ that lies between $i+1$ and $j$ (inclusive). The causal mask forces $A^{h_1}_{k, i+1} = 0$ when $k < i+1$, and $A^{h_2}_{j,k} = 0$ when $k > j$. Therefore, the only contributing intermediate positions are those satisfying $i+1 \leq k \leq j$.

**The implication for induction heads:** in an induction circuit, the final token $j$ needs to attend to position $i+1$ (the token that followed the earlier occurrence of the current token) using information about the match detected at position $i$. According to Property 1, the only way to route this information is through intermediate token $k = i+1$ itself. That means position $i+1$ must first encode information about position $i$ into its residual stream (via layer 1's attention), so that layer 2 can later extract it. The paper argues this imposes a **width penalty**: the hidden state dimension $D$ must be large enough to carry both (a) the normal semantic content of token $i+1$ and (b) the "pass-through" information about token $i$ that is needed for the induction circuit but is irrelevant for token $i+1$'s own role in the sequence. If $D$ is too small, these two information streams will interfere, degrading induction performance.

#### The KV Shifting Attention Mechanism

The core technical contribution is the **KV shifting attention** operation, which modifies the standard attention computation to decouple the key-value binding at adjacent positions. The formal definition is:

$$Q, K, V = X W_Q, X W_K, X W_V$$

$$\hat{K} = \alpha_1 K + \alpha_2 \text{Shift}(K), \quad \hat{V} = \beta_1 V + \beta_2 \text{Shift}(V)$$

$$\text{Output} = \text{Softmax}(Q \hat{K}^T / \sigma \cdot M) \hat{V} W_O$$

where $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$ is the hidden state sequence of length $N$ and dimension $D$; $W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}$ are the standard learned projection matrices; $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \beta_1, \beta_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ are learnable parameters (initialized as described below); $\sigma = \sqrt{D}$ is the standard scaling factor; $M \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is the causal attention mask (lower triangular); and $\text{Shift}(\cdot)$ is an operator that discards the last token's representation and prepends a zero vector at the beginning, so that for token position $i$, $\text{Shift}(K)_i = K_{i-1}$ for $i > 1$ and $\text{Shift}(K)_1 = 0$.

**What this mechanism computes:** instead of using the raw projected keys and values, the attention operation uses blended keys $\hat{K}$ and blended values $\hat{V}$ that are learned convex combinations (at initialization) of the current and previous token's features. The query $Q$ remains unchanged. When the attention head computes $Q \hat{K}^T / \sigma$, the dot product $Q_j \cdot \hat{K}_i$ involves both $K_i$ and $K_{i-1}$, weighted by $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$. Similarly, when the attention output aggregates value vectors using the post-softmax attention weights, each position $i$ contributes both $V_i$ and $V_{i-1}$, weighted by $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$. The intuition is that by attending to position $i$'s key, the model can retrieve information about position $i-1$'s value (or vice versa), enabling the "look up the neighbor" operation that induction heads require without needing a second layer to copy information forward.

**Why this form:** the design is motivated by the induction heads circuit decomposition. In standard attention, to retrieve the value of token $i+1$ when attending to token $i$, the model needs a separate attention head in a previous layer to copy $V_{i+1}$ information into $K_i$ (or equivalently, to attend to $i$ but output a representation that encodes $i+1$). By making $\hat{K}_i$ depend on $K_{i-1}$ and $\hat{V}_i$ depend on $V_{i-1}$, the model can achieve this natively: set $\alpha_1 = 0, \alpha_2 = 1$ to use the previous token's key, and $\beta_1 = 0, \beta_2 = 1$ to retrieve the current token's value through the previous token's key. The symmetric possibility (using the current token's key to retrieve the previous token's value) is equally available by setting the parameters differently. The fact that the parameters are **learnable** is critical: the model can discover the optimal blend for each attention head based on the task, rather than being hard-coded. Some heads may specialize in induction (favoring $\alpha_2$ and $\beta_2$), while others may continue to operate like standard attention heads (favoring $\alpha_1$ and $\beta_1$).

The **causal mask constraint** is respected because the shift only goes backward (to position $i-1$), never forward (to position $i+1$). The authors explain this explicitly: "if we directly use the combination of $j$th token's value ($j \in \{i-1, i, i+1\}$), it may break the causal mask, because $(i+1)$th token's value can't be computed when $i$th token try to do next token prediction. So we can only let $i$th token's key connect to $(i-1)$th and $i$th token's value." The backward-only shift combined with the symmetric key-value shift creates the necessary decoupling: attending to the key of token $i$ can retrieve the value of token $i-1$ (through shifted value), and attending to the key of token $i-1$ (through shifted key at position $i$) can retrieve the value of token $i$. Both operations are causal because they only access already-computed positions.

For **multi-head attention**, the parameters $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \beta_1, \beta_2$ are learned **per head**, adding $4h$ parameters total where $h$ is the number of heads. For **grouped query attention** (GQA, Ainslie et al., 2023) — which is used in the paper's large-scale experiments — the additional parameters are $4h_1$ where $h_1$ is the number of key-value pairs, not the number of query heads. This is because the shifting operates on the shared key and value projections in GQA.

**Initialization scheme:** the authors set $\alpha_1, \beta_1 \sim U(0, 1)$ (sampled independently from a uniform distribution) and then set $\alpha_2 = 1 - \alpha_1$ and $\beta_2 = 1 - \beta_1$. This ensures that at initialization, $\hat{K}_i$ and $\hat{V}_i$ are convex combinations of the current and previous token features, with the sum of coefficients equal to 1. The rationale is that "the initialization of KV shifting does not affect the initial optimization state" — at initialization, the model's attention is approximately equivalent to standard attention (just a stochastic blend of adjacent positions rather than a pure identity), so the optimization trajectory starts from a familiar landscape but has the degrees of freedom to specialize toward induction-favoring configurations.

**Computational cost:** the shift-blend operation in Equation 5 requires only $O(ND)$ additional computation (a linear combination of two vectors per position per head), compared to the dominant $O(ND^2 + N^2D)$ cost of the attention softmax and weighted sum in Equation 6. The shift itself is implemented via a convolution operation (as shown in the PyTorch code in Appendix F), which is memory-efficient. For inference with key-value caching, the implementation is slightly more involved: when processing a new token at time step $t$, the shifted key is computed as $\hat{K}_t = \alpha_1 K_t + \alpha_2 K_{t-1}$ and the shifted value as $\hat{V}_t = \beta_1 V_t + \beta_2 V_{t-1}$, where $K_{t-1}$ and $V_{t-1}$ are stored from the previous step. The cached key-value pairs store the shifted versions directly, maintaining compatibility with standard autoregressive inference.

#### Theoretical Analysis: Depth and Width Reduction for Induction Heads

The paper provides a formal comparison between standard attention and KV shifting attention in their ability to represent induction heads. This analysis uses the **induction heads machine** as a formal target function, defined with Alibi relative position encodings (Press et al.) rather than absolute or rotary position encodings, to enable exact mathematical constructions.

**Definition 1 (Induction Heads Machine):**

$$\text{IH}(x) = \sum_{s=2}^{L-1} \text{softmax}\left(x_L x_{s-1}^T / \sigma - m|L - s|\right) x_s$$

where $x \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$ is the input sequence of length $L$ with $d$-dimensional embeddings, $x_L$ is the final token's embedding, $x_{s-1}$ is the embedding of the token at position $s-1$, $\sigma > 0$ is a temperature parameter (small enough to make the softmax concentrate on the maximum-scoring position), and $m > 0$ is the Alibi linear bias (which penalized attention to more distant positions, with $m$ approaching 0 to approximate uniform attention in the finite-length regime).

**What this machine computes:** it implements the induction heads operation. The final output is a weighted sum of the values $x_s$ at positions $s = 2, 3, ..., L-1$, where the weight for position $s$ is determined by the similarity between the final token embedding $x_L$ and the preceding token embedding $x_{s-1}$, penalized by the distance $|L - s|$. The logic is: to find the token that followed the most recent occurrence of $x_L$, we look for positions $s$ where the previous token $x_{s-1}$ is most similar to $x_L$ (because $x_L$ matches some earlier token, and that earlier token's position is $s-1$ if we set up the indexing correctly). The distance penalty $m|L - s|$ biases toward more recent matches, consistent with the "most recent previous occurrence" behavior of induction heads in practice. In the limit of small $\sigma$ and small $m$, the softmax concentrates on the single position $s$ that maximises $x_L x_{s-1}^T / \sigma - m|L-s|$, and the output becomes approximately $x_s$, which is the token that followed the matched occurrence.

**Why this form:** the Alibi formulation allows the authors to construct exact or near-exact implementations using transformer weights, because Alibi's linear bias adds a constant term $m|i - j|$ to each attention score before softmax, independent of the token embeddings. This gives precise control over how attention scores degrade with distance. The paper acknowledges that this is an idealized model — real transformers use finite precision, different position encodings, and operate in finite context windows — but the construction proves a representational point: what resources (depth, width) does an architecture need to implement this specific function exactly?

**Theorem 1 (Standard Two-Layer Construction, modified from Wang et al., 2024):**

There exists a constant $C > 0$ and a two-layer single-head transformer TF (without FFNs), with $D = 2d$, $W_K^{(1,1)} = W_Q^{(1,1)} = 0$, $p^{(2)} = m$, and $\|W_K^{(2,1)}\|, \|W_Q^{(2,1)}\| \leq O(1, 1/\sigma)$, such that:

$$\sup_{L \in \mathbb{N}^+} \| \text{IH} - \text{TF} \|_{L, \infty} \leq O(e^{-p^{(1)}})$$

where $p^{(1)}$ is the Alibi bias in the first layer, and $\|\cdot\|_{L,\infty}$ is the sup norm over sequences of length $L$.

**What this theorem states:** a two-layer transformer with hidden dimension $D = 2d$ (twice the embedding dimension) can approximate the induction heads machine to within an error bounded by $O(e^{-p^{(1)}})$, which becomes arbitrarily small as the first layer's Alibi bias $p^{(1)}$ increases. The construction works as follows (detailed in Appendix A):

- **Embedding each token:** each token $x_s$ is embedded into $\mathbb{R}^{2d}$ as the concatenation $[x_s; 0]$, i.e., the original $d$-dimensional embedding followed by $d$ zeros.
- **Layer 1 (with residual connection):** the first layer has its query and key projections set to zero ($W_K^{(1,1)} = W_Q^{(1,1)} = 0$), which means the attention mechanism is disabled (all positions get uniform attention weights). However, the value matrix $W_V^{(1)}$ is set to $[0 \; 0; I_{d \times d} \; 0]$ (in block matrix notation), and the Alibi bias $p^{(1)}$ controls how attention decays with distance. The output of layer 1 at position $s$ is $[x_s; y_s]$ where $y_s = \sum_{\tau=1}^{s-1} \text{softmax}(-p^{(1)}(s-1-\tau)) x_\tau$. This is a **distance-weighted average** of all previous token embeddings. With large $p^{(1)}$, the softmax concentrates sharply on the most recent position, making $y_s \approx x_{s-1}$.
- **Layer 2 (without residual):** the second layer uses Alibi bias $p^{(2)} = m$, and its query and key matrices are set to extract the $y$ portion of the hidden states:
  
  $$W_Q^{(2,1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & I_{d \times d} \end{bmatrix}, \quad W_K^{(2,1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & W^\star \end{bmatrix}, \quad W_V^{(2,1)} = \begin{bmatrix} I_{d \times d} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
  
  with output projection $W_O^{(2)} = [I_{d \times d}, 0_{d \times d}]$. This means the query at the final position $L$ is $y_L \approx x_{L-1}$, the key at position $s$ is $W^\star y_s \approx W^\star x_{s-1}$, and the value at position $s$ is $x_s$. Setting $W^\star = I / \sigma$ gives the dot product $x_{L-1}^T x_{s-1} / \sigma$ plus the Alibi bias $m|L-s|$, which after softmax yields exactly the IH attention weights (up to the approximation error in $y_s$).

**Why the width is $2d$:** the hidden dimension must be twice the embedding dimension because each token's residual stream must carry both its own embedding $x_s$ (needed for the value in layer 2) and the weighted average $y_s$ (needed for the query and key in layer 2). The $y_s$ component is the "pass-through" information that carries the previous token's embedding forward through the causal mask — this is the width penalty the paper argues is unnecessary.

**The approximation error:** the bound is $O(e^{-p^{(1)}})$, which comes from the fact that $y_s$ is a softmax-weighted sum rather than an exact copy of $x_{s-1}$. As $p^{(1)}$ grows large, the softmax concentrates almost entirely on position $s-1$, making $y_s \approx x_{s-1}$ to arbitrary precision, but this also means the first layer effectively "ignores" all context beyond the immediate predecessor — a representational cost of the construction.

**Theorem 2 (One-Layer KV Shifting Attention Construction):**

There exists a one-layer single-head KV shifting attention KVSA, with $D = d$, such that:

$$\text{IH} = \text{KVSA}$$

exactly (equality holds, not just approximation).

**What this theorem states:** with KV shifting attention, the induction heads machine can be implemented **exactly** by a single layer with the same hidden dimension as the input embedding ($D = d$, vs. $2d$ for the two-layer standard construction). The proof (in Appendix B) is a direct parameter assignment:

- Set $\alpha_1 = 0$, $\alpha_2 = 1$: the shifted key at position $i$ is $\hat{K}_i = K_{i-1}$ (the key of the previous token)
- Set $\beta_1 = 0$, $\beta_2 = 1$: the shifted value at position $i$ is $\hat{V}_i = V_{i-1}$ (the value of the previous token)
- Set all projection matrices to identity: $W_Q = W_K = W_V = W_O = I \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$
- Set the Alibi bias $p = m$

Then the attention score for the final position $L$ attending to intermediate position $s$ is:

$$Q_L \hat{K}_s^T / \sigma - m|L - s| = x_L \cdot x_{s-1} / \sigma - m|L - s|$$

which is exactly the expression inside the softmax in the IH definition (Definition 1). The concatenated value vector used in the attention output is $\hat{V}_s = V_{s-1} = x_{s-1}$, so the attention output becomes a weighted sum of the input embeddings $x_s$ (via the shifted values) with exactly the correct weights. There is **no approximation error** because the shift operation provides direct access to the preceding token's key and value without needing a softmax approximation.

**What this comparison demonstrates:** KV shifting attention achieves the induction heads representation with (a) half the depth (1 layer vs. 2 layers), (b) half the width ($d$ vs. $2d$), and (c) exact equality instead of an approximation bounded by $O(e^{-p^{(1)}})$. The depth reduction is intuitive (the shift eliminates the need for a separate "copy-forward" layer). The width reduction is less obvious but equally important: in the standard construction, each token's hidden state must carry both its own embedding and a weighted average of its predecessor's embedding, consuming double the dimension. In the KV shifting construction, the hidden states are unchanged ($D = d$, each token carries only its own embedding), and the access to predecessor information is handled entirely through the key-value shift — the predecessor's key and value are stored at the predecessor's position in the sequence, not copied forward into the current token.

**Important caveat (footnote in the paper):** "Theorems 1 and 2 only provide constructive upper bounds for implementing induction heads. A more rigorous statement would be to prove that the lower bound of Theorem 1 is smaller than the upper bound of Theorem 2." That is, the paper provides a constructive proof that KV shifting attention *can* represent induction heads with fewer resources, but it does not prove that standard attention *cannot* potentially achieve the same representation with a different, more clever construction using the same depth and width. The constructive proof in Theorem 2 shows sufficiency but not necessity. However, the paper notes that Sanford et al. (2024a) proved that a one-layer standard transformer *cannot* implement induction heads at all, which establishes a hard lower bound: standard attention requires at least depth 2. KV shifting attention breaks through this lower bound.

#### Learning Dynamics Analysis on Synthetic Data

Beyond representational capacity, the paper analyzes how the parameters of KV shifting attention **learn** from data. This is important because representational capacity alone does not guarantee that gradient-based training will discover the right solution — the loss landscape must guide optimization toward the induction configuration.

**Simplified conditions for Theorem 3:**

The authors set up a severely simplified training scenario to make the gradient dynamics analytically tractable. Specifically: they remove residual connections, MLP layers, normalization, and position encodings; they tie the input and output embeddings; each token's embedding components are drawn i.i.d. from $\mathcal{N}(0, 1/d)$; the sequence has length $T+1$ with a vocabulary of size $T$ ($T \geq 3$), where every token appears exactly once except the last token, which appears twice (earlier in the sequence and at the end); and the cross-entropy loss is computed only on the prediction of the next token at the final position.

Under these conditions, the induction task reduces to: the final token is identical to some earlier token at position $i$ ($1 \leq i < T$), and the model must predict the token that appeared at position $i+1$. This is the simplest possible instantiation of the induction heads pattern: [A] ... [B] ... [A] → predict [B], where [A] and [B] are unique tokens except for the repeated [A] at the end.

**Theorem 3 (Learning Dynamics):**

Under the simplified conditions described, and as $d \to \infty$ (infinite embedding dimension), learning induction heads by KV shifting attention is equivalent to minimizing:

$$\min \mathcal{L} = -\log\left(\frac{e^{a_2 \beta_1 + \beta_2 / S}}{e^{a_2 \beta_1 + \beta_2 / S} + 2 e^{\beta_1 / S + \beta_2 a_2} + e^{2 a_1 \beta_1 + a_2 \beta_2} + O(T)}\right)$$

where $a_2 = e^{\alpha_2} / S$, $a_1 = e^{\alpha_1} / S$, $S = e^{\alpha_2} + 2 e^{\alpha_1} + O(T)$, and all $O(\cdot)$ terms are $\geq 0$.

**What this expression represents:** it is the cross-entropy loss for predicting the correct next token (the token at position $i+1$ in the sequence) given the attention mechanism's logits. The derivation (in Appendix C) proceeds by computing what happens at the final position:

1. **Compute attention scores:** the final token (which equals $x_i$, the earlier occurrence) attends to all positions in the sequence. For most positions, the dot product $x_i \cdot (\alpha_1 x_k + \alpha_2 x_{k-1})$ is approximately 0 due to near-orthogonality of random embeddings (with $O(1/\sqrt{d})$ error as $d \to \infty$). The exceptions are: attending to position $i$ (where the key involves $x_i$ and $x_{i-1}$, giving score $\approx \alpha_1$), position $i+1$ (score $\approx \alpha_2$), and position $T+1$ (the final token attends to itself, score $\approx \alpha_1$). All other positions have scores of order $O((\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)/\sqrt{d})$.

2. **Normalize to softmax weights:** after softmax, the dominant terms are positions $i$, $i+1$, and $T+1$, with weights $a_1 = e^{\alpha_1}/S$, $a_2 = e^{\alpha_2}/S$, and $a_1$ again respectively, where $S = 2e^{\alpha_1} + e^{\alpha_2} + O(T)$ normalizes over all positions (the $O(T)$ accounts for the many positions with near-zero but non-zero attention).

3. **Compute output logits:** the attention output at the final position is $\sum_k \hat{V}_k \cdot \text{softmax}_k$, where $\hat{V}_k = \beta_1 V_k + \beta_2 V_{k-1}$. The logit for predicting token $i+1$ (the correct answer) involves contributions from position $i$ (where $\hat{V}_i$ contains $V_i$ with weight $\beta_1$ and $V_{i-1}$ with weight $\beta_2$) and position $i+1$ (where $\hat{V}_{i+1}$ contains $V_{i+1}$ with weight $\beta_1$ and $V_i$ with weight $\beta_2$). The $O(T)$ terms account for contributions from all other positions, which are small but non-zero.

4. **Form the softmax cross-entropy loss:** the loss is the negative log-probability of the correct answer among all $T$ possible next-token predictions. The denominator includes the correct answer's logit plus the largest competing logits: predicting token $i$ (the previous token), predicting token $T$ (another token that gets a boost from the structure), and the $O(T)$ uniform background from all other tokens.

**Why this simplifies to the given form:** as $d \to \infty$, the embedding orthogonality becomes exact ($u_i^T u_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$, and $u_i^T u_i = 1$), eliminating all the $O(1/\sqrt{d})$ noise terms. The loss then depends only on the four parameters $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \beta_1, \beta_2$ (through $a_1, a_2, S$) and the sequence length $T$ (through the $O(T)$ term representing the vocabulary size effect).

**The contour plot analysis (Figure 2):**

The authors simplify further by constraining $\alpha_2 = 1 - \alpha_1$ and $\beta_2 = 1 - \beta_1$ (as at initialization, though in actual training these sums can drift from 1), and treating $O(T)$ as a constant. They plot the contour lines and gradient descent directions of $-\mathcal{L}$ in the $(\alpha_1, \beta_1)$ plane for three values of $O(T)$: 0, 10, and 100.

The configuration for a pure induction head is $(\alpha_1, \beta_1) = (0, 1)$ — meaning $\alpha_2 = 1$ (use the previous token's key exclusively) and $\beta_2 = 0$ (use the current token's value exclusively). At this point, attending to position $i$'s key (which is $K_{i-1}$ when $\alpha_2 = 1$) retrieves $V_i$ (when $\beta_1 = 1$), which is the embedding of token $i$, which is exactly the [B] token that follows the matched [A]. This is the correct induction operation.

**Key observations from Figure 2:**

1. **As $O(T)$ increases (larger vocabulary/sentence length), the contour lines become sparser.** This means the gradient becomes smaller in magnitude, causing slower convergence. This makes intuitive sense: with more tokens in the vocabulary, the correct answer's probability is diluted among more competitors, making the loss surface flatter and gradients weaker.

2. **When $O(T)$ is small, the gradient direction may undergo a non-monotonic learning process.** The gradient field in Figure 2a shows regions where the gradient initially points away from $(0, 1)$ before curving back toward it. This suggests that from some initialization points, the parameters may first move toward a "bigram" solution (predicting based on local statistics rather than induction) before switching to the induction configuration. This phenomenon mirrors the "birth of induction heads" observed in real transformer training by Bietti et al. (2024), where global bigrams are learned first, followed by the emergence of induction circuits.

3. **When $O(T)$ is large, the gradient direction is more consistent.** Figure 2c shows gradient arrows pointing relatively directly toward $(0, 1)$ from most initialization points. This means that when the sequence is long and the vocabulary is large, the optimization is more decisive: the induction solution is so much better than alternatives that gradient descent homes in on it straightforwardly.

4. **Initialization matters:** the authors note that "in practice, we often have many attention heads, $(\alpha_1, \beta_1)$ of some heads are closer to $(0, 1)$ during initialization, making it much easier for them to learn induction heads." With multiple heads initialized randomly across the $[0,1] \times [0,1]$ square, some will start closer to the induction configuration by chance and specialize quickly, while others may converge more slowly or find different local optima.

**The T=2 special case and the bigram shortcut:**

When $T = 2$, the sequence structure becomes [A] [B] [A] → [B] (just two unique tokens). The authors note that this falls outside the scope of Theorem 3 (which assumes $T \geq 3$), but it illustrates an important point: "the model only needs to predict the previous token to achieve low loss on the training data, but this is not an induction head." This is the **bigram shortcut**: if the vocabulary is so small that simply predicting $x_{T-1}$ (the previous token) happens to give the correct answer on the training data, the model can achieve low loss without learning the general induction pattern of matching distant occurrences. Only when $T$ is larger, and the sequence contains many tokens, does the true induction solution (finding the matched earlier occurrence) become unambiguously better than alternatives.

This connects to Bietti et al. (2024)'s observation that "global bigrams are learned first, then the induction head is formed by learning appropriate memories in a top-down fashion." In the KV shifting attention case, the authors argue that the induction solution becomes much easier to find: "in KV shifting attention, induction heads become very easy to learn, and even with good initialization, the model has induction capability." However, they acknowledge that "it is difficult to have appropriate initialization to obtain a certain level of bigrams capability without training" — meaning that the pure-induction configuration (which relies on $\alpha_2 \approx 1, \beta_1 \approx 1$) does not naturally support bigram prediction, so the model must either learn a mixed configuration or dedicate some heads to induction and others to bigrams.

#### N-Gram Capacity: Does KV Shifting Hurt Non-Induction Tasks?

An important design validation is whether the architectural bias toward induction heads comes at the cost of degrading performance on other important language modeling sub-tasks, particularly n-gram memory. If KV shifting attention made the model *worse* at learning simple statistical patterns, the overall language modeling benefit would be questionable.

**The 3-gram experiment (Figure 3):**

The authors construct a synthetic 3-gram task: approximately 200 pairs of tokens $(x_1, x_2)$ are randomly generated, and for each pair, an $x_3$ is randomly assigned. The model must predict $x_3$ when given $x_1$ and $x_2$ (i.e., learn that specific $(x_1, x_2)$ pairs map to specific $x_3$ outputs). Three model sizes are tested: 50M parameters (4 layers), 0.4M parameters (2 layers), and 0.8K parameters (1 layer).

**Result:** "Obviously, the KV shifting attention do not enhance the model's learning ability for this 3-gram tasks, but it also does not weaken the learning ability for this 3-gram tasks." Figure 3 shows that the accuracy curves for vanilla and KV shifting attention are essentially overlapping across all model sizes and training steps. This is a positive result: the architectural modification that biases toward induction does not impair n-gram learning.

**Why this matters theoretically:** the authors note that "n-gram tasks or some Markov data can actually be simulated with just one layer transformer with vanilla attention" (citing Rajaraman et al., 2024). The key distinction is that n-gram learning is a local operation (the model only needs to attend to the immediately preceding tokens and memorize the output token), while induction is inherently a non-local operation (the model must search the entire context for a matching earlier occurrence). Standard attention handles local patterns well out of the box, but struggles with the non-local induction task. KV shifting attention fixes the non-local weakness without breaking the local strength — it is a targeted improvement, not a trade-off.

This result also contextualizes the contrast between Figure 1a (induction learning) and Figure 3 (n-gram learning): in Figure 1a, the 1-layer vanilla transformer *fails completely* at induction while the 1-layer KV shifting attention succeeds, but in Figure 3, both perform equivalently on n-grams. The architectural bias is specific to the induction mechanism and does not indiscriminately alter all attention-based learning.

#### Key Design Decisions and Trade-Offs

Several design choices in the KV shifting attention formulation reflect explicit trade-offs considered by the authors:

**1. Shifting only K and V, not Q:**
The shift is applied only to keys and values, not queries. This is motivated directly by the induction heads circuit: to implement "use token $i$'s key to retrieve token $i-1$'s value," you only need to shift K and V. Shifting Q would allow different queries to blend with their predecessors' queries, but this does not directly help the induction operation and could introduce unnecessary complexity. The authors test QKV shifting (Appendix J) on a 19B model and find it performs worse than KV-only shifting, and even worse than vanilla attention on some benchmarks. This validates that the benefit comes specifically from the key-value decoupling, not from a generic "add more parameters" effect.

**2. Shifting only by one position ($i-1$), not further:**
The shift window is limited to adjacent tokens (the immediate predecessor). The paper tests wider windows — $[i-2, i]$ (KV shifting 2) and $[i-3, i]$ (KV shifting 3) — and finds no improvement (Figure 8c). The authors argue that "from the perspective of induction heads, using our current shifting window size is sufficient" because induction only requires looking at the immediately adjacent token to find "what followed the previous occurrence." Longer windows would add parameters and computation without addressing any additional circuit requirement. However, they acknowledge that "if someone want to expand to longer windows, it may need to carefully design it," leaving open the possibility that more sophisticated multi-position shifts could help with other operations beyond induction.

**3. Learnable parameters vs. fixed gating:**
The $\alpha$ and $\beta$ parameters are initialized as convex combinations ($\alpha_2 = 1 - \alpha_1$, $\beta_2 = 1 - \beta_1$) but are **not constrained** to remain in $[0, 1]$ or to sum to 1 during training. The authors test constrained alternatives (Figure 8a): "KV shifting gate" enforces $\sum \alpha_i = 1$ and $\sum \beta_i = 1$ via sigmoid parameterization ($\alpha_1 = \text{sigmoid}(a), \alpha_2 = 1 - \alpha_1$); "KV shifting 0 to 1" clips parameters to $[0, 1]$. Both constraints perform worse than the unconstrained version. The analysis of the trained 2.9B model (Section 4.6) reveals why: some heads learn parameters like $\alpha_1 = 0.08, \alpha_2 = 0.43, \beta_1 = 0.34, \beta_2 = -0.15$, where $\beta_2$ becomes negative. This allows the head to subtract the previous token's value from the current token's value, implementing an "anti-copy" operation that the authors connect to the "anti-copying prefix-search heads" discovered by Elhage et al. (2021). Constraining parameters to $[0, 1]$ would prevent such operations. The conclusion is that "allowing $\alpha$ and $\beta$ to have a wider range of degrees of freedom may enable the model to learn richer features."

**4. Per-head parameterization:**
The $\alpha$ and $\beta$ parameters are learned independently for each attention head (or each KV pair in GQA), not shared across the model. This is critical because different heads can and do specialize in different tasks — some become induction heads (with $\alpha_2$ and $\beta_1$ large), some remain standard attention heads ($\alpha_1$ and $\beta_1$ large), and some learn more exotic configurations (e.g., negative $\beta_2$). The analysis in Section 4.6 (Table 4) shows that in the trained 2.9B model, the majority of KV pairs settle into a configuration where the relative sizes of $\alpha_1$ vs. $\alpha_2$ and $\beta_1$ vs. $\beta_2$ are aligned on the diagonal ($\alpha_1 > \alpha_2$ and $\beta_1 > \beta_2$, or $\alpha_1 \leq \alpha_2$ and $\beta_1 \leq \beta_2$), but a significant minority fall in off-diagonal configurations, suggesting functional specialization.

**5. Compatibility with existing frameworks:**
The paper emphasizes that KV shifting attention is a "lightweight modification" that adds only 4 parameters per head and $O(ND)$ computation, making it compatible with existing training and inference acceleration frameworks (e.g., FlashAttention, grouped query attention, KV caching for autoregressive generation). The PyTorch code in Appendix F shows the implementation uses standard operations (padding, unfolding/unfold, convolution) that are well-supported in optimized attention kernels. This is a deliberate design choice over more complex mechanisms that "may be difficult to adapt to under existing training or inference acceleration frameworks." The practical upshot is that KV shifting attention can be adopted without overhauling the software stack.

**6. Initialization to preserve initial optimization state:**
By initializing $\alpha_1, \beta_1 \sim U(0, 1)$ and setting $\alpha_2 = 1 - \alpha_1, \beta_2 = 1 - \beta_1$, the model starts with attention that is a stochastic convex blend of the current and previous token features. This means the initial attention behavior is similar to (but not identical to) standard attention, allowing the optimizer to start from a familiar landscape. The authors state this is "to ensure that the initialization of KV shifting does not affect the initial optimization state." If instead $\alpha_2$ and $\beta_2$ were initialized to 0, the model would start as exact standard attention and the shift capability would have to be "discovered" from scratch, potentially slowing early training. The convex combination initialization gives the model immediate access to shifted information while still being close to standard attention in expectation.

#### Limitations Explicitly Acknowledged in the Technical Approach

The paper does not claim that KV shifting attention is a complete solution to the induction problem. Section 3.3 explicitly notes: "the motivation of our KV shifting attention is to reduce the width and depth required for induction, we cannot expect KV shifting attention to greatly improve the memory ability of the model." This is an important scope limitation: the modification targets one specific circuit (induction heads) and does not claim to improve other aspects of attention such as content-based retrieval, factual recall, or long-range dependency formation beyond what induction already provides.

Additionally, the theoretical analysis uses Alibi position encodings, which are not the dominant choice in modern LLMs (most use rotary position encodings, RoPE). The paper's large-scale experiments use RoPE (with a base of 100,000, as specified in Table 6 in Appendix G), confirming that the benefits carry over. However, the formal proofs do not directly apply to RoPE-based transformers, and the paper does not provide a RoPE-specific theoretical construction. The authors implicitly rely on the intuition that both Alibi and RoPE encode relative position information and that the key-value decoupling is position-encoding-agnostic in its mechanism.

Finally, the paper acknowledges in Section 6 (Limitations) that "in more complex multi-layer transformer conditions, how to learn induction heads and more complex reasoning skills remains a challenging problem." The one-layer exact construction (Theorem 2) is a proof of concept; real models have many layers, and induction may be distributed across heads in complex ways that the simplified analysis does not capture. The large-scale experiments validate that the benefits persist, but the precise circuit-level mechanisms at play in the 19B-parameter trained model are not analyzed — the paper only provides aggregate parameter statistics (Table 4) rather than mechanistic interpretability of the trained model's circuits.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Using Mechanistic Interpretability Prescriptively for Architecture Design

The paper's most intellectually distinctive contribution is not the KV shifting mechanism itself, but the **research methodology it models**: using mechanistic interpretability findings to **prescribe architectural modifications**, rather than merely to describe what existing architectures have learned.

The standard pipeline in mechanistic interpretability is descriptive. Researchers identify a circuit — such as induction heads (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022) — analyze how a trained model implements it across layers and attention heads, and draw conclusions about how transformers work. The output is understanding. Occasionally, this understanding feeds into training interventions (e.g., pruning circuits, editing factual associations), but it almost never feeds back into the architecture design itself. The architecture is treated as a fixed substrate that the model learns to program, and the researcher's role is to reverse-engineer the resulting program.

This paper inverts the relationship. It starts from the premise: *if we know what circuit we want the model to learn, can we make the architecture a better substrate for that circuit?* The KV shifting operation is the answer to this question for induction heads. The field had known since Sanford et al. (2024a) that one-layer transformers provably cannot implement induction heads, and since Elhage et al. (2021) that the two-layer implementation requires width bloat to route information through the residual stream. The dominant response to these findings was to accept them as facts about transformers — to say "transformers learn induction heads using this two-layer circuit" rather than to say "the architecture is inefficient at learning induction heads, let's fix it."

What makes this contribution not merely incremental is the **demonstration that mechanistically-motivated architecture changes survive scaling**. It is one thing to design a modification that improves a toy induction task — many architectural ideas show promise on small synthetic problems. It is another to show that the same modification improves 19B-parameter model pre-training on heterogeneous text corpora across multiple standard benchmarks (Table 2, +2.12 percentage points average improvement for the 2.9B model at 500B tokens, +0.77 points for the 19B model at 200B tokens). The success at scale validates the methodology: understanding circuits can guide architecture design in practically useful ways, not just satisfy intellectual curiosity.

This is a **fundamental shift in the relationship between interpretability and architecture research**, not an incremental refinement. Prior work modified architectures based on computational considerations (efficiency, parallelism, training stability) or broad inductive biases (convolutional locality, recurrence, sparsity). This paper modifies architecture based on a specific, named circuit whose mechanistic requirements are characterized at the level of individual attention operations. If this methodology generalizes — identifying other circuits, characterizing their resource requirements, and modifying architecture to ease those requirements — it opens a new design paradigm. The paper hints at this in Section 5: "for example, by modifying transformer to make it easy to learn parity check, which might be very challenging." The KV shifting attention is thus both a concrete contribution and a methodological proof-of-concept.

The evidence that anchors this claim is the full experimental arc: from theoretical proof of improved representational capacity (Theorem 2 vs. Theorem 1), to toy-model validation of faster induction learning (Figure 1a, where 1-layer KV shifting attention matches 2-layer standard attention), to scaling-law validation (Figure 7a, showing KV shifting attention outperforming vanilla at every parameter scale from roughly 1M to 13M non-embedding parameters), to large-scale pre-training benchmarks (Table 2). The fact that the improvement persists across this range — rather than washing out at scale — is what makes the methodology credible.

### Innovation 2: Identifying and Solving the Width Penalty in Induction Head Circuits

While the depth requirement for induction heads (≥ 2 layers) was a known result (Sanford et al., 2024a), the paper's analysis reveals a second, subtler inefficiency that had not been previously articulated: the **width penalty** imposed by the causal mask constraint on virtual attention heads.

Property 1 and the surrounding analysis (Section 3.1) make a precise diagnostic claim. When a standard two-layer transformer implements induction heads through virtual attention composition, the second layer's attention to position `i+1` can only be routed through intermediate token `k = i+1` itself, due to the causal mask. This means position `i+1`'s residual stream must simultaneously encode (a) its own semantic content, needed for the model's normal language modeling, and (b) forwarding information about position `i`, needed exclusively for the induction circuit that will be activated many tokens later. These two information streams compete for the same hidden dimension, and when the dimension is small, they interfere — degrading induction performance. This is demonstrable in Figure 1b: with hidden size = 8 (an extreme pressure test), the two-layer standard transformer essentially fails to learn induction (accuracy near chance), while the one-layer KV shifting attention succeeds (accuracy near perfect), despite having half the parameters.

What makes this insight distinctive is that it explains a failure mode that the depth requirement alone does not predict. A depth-based analysis would say: "you need two layers, and with two layers and sufficient width, induction works." But the width penalty analysis says: "even with two layers, the width needed to make induction work is inflated by the fact that the intermediate token's residual stream must carry pass-through information." KV shifting attention eliminates this penalty entirely because it never forces token `i+1` to carry information about token `i` — the key-value decoupling provides direct access to the predecessor's key and value without routing through the residual stream.

Prior work on induction heads (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024; Bietti et al., 2024) had characterized the cross-layer circuit structure but had not identified the width cost as a distinct, quantifiable penalty separate from the overall representational capacity of the model. The diagnosis is novel: it says that standard attention is not merely "missing a feature" but is actively *wasteful* — it forces the model to allocate hidden dimension to a routing task that is mechanically unnecessary. This is different from saying "induction heads need two layers." It says "the two-layer construction is not depth- or width-optimal, and the suboptimality is traceable to a specific architectural constraint that can be lifted."

The significance of this insight extends beyond induction heads. Any circuit that requires one position to "pass through" information about a neighbor to a later position — which likely includes many forms of syntactic agreement, co-reference resolution, and multi-hop reasoning — incurs the same width penalty. Sanford et al. (2024b) showed that transformers need logarithmic depth for certain parallel computation tasks, and the width penalty analysis suggests these depth requirements may themselves induce hidden width requirements that amplify the total resource cost. KV shifting attention addresses one instance of this pattern, but the diagnostic framework — checking whether a circuit forces pass-through information onto intermediate tokens — is general.

The evidence for the width penalty is in the contrast between Figure 1a (depth comparison, hidden size = 1024) and Figure 1b (width comparison, hidden size = 8). In the large-width regime, the two-layer standard transformer *eventually* learns induction, albeit more slowly than KV shifting attention. In the small-width regime, it fails entirely. This is exactly what the width penalty predicts: when width is abundant, the interference between semantic content and pass-through information is manageable; when width is scarce, the two information streams collide and neither task is learned properly. KV shifting attention, by eliminating the pass-through requirement, succeeds in both regimes.

### Innovation 3: Negative Results That Clarify the Scope of the Approach

The paper contains several notable negative results that are as informative as the positive ones, and collectively they establish **clear boundary conditions** for when this architectural modification helps, when it is neutral, and when it hurts. These negative results prevent over-claiming and guide future work.

**The n-gram non-result (Figure 3).** KV shifting attention does not improve and does not impair 3-gram learning across three model scales (0.8K to 50M parameters). This is a critical scope delimiter: the modification targets induction (non-local pattern matching across arbitrary distances) and explicitly does not enhance local statistical learning. Standard transformers already handle n-gram tasks efficiently with one layer (Rajaraman et al., 2024), so there is no inefficiency to fix. The methodological implication is that architectural modifications motivated by mechanistic analysis are **circuit-specific** — improving one circuit does not automatically improve all circuits. This distinguishes the paper's approach from generic "better architecture" claims that promise universal gains.

**The ReST-style revision failure.** While not a negative result of KV shifting attention itself, the paper's discussion of Bietti et al. (2024)'s "birth of induction heads" phenomenon — where bigrams are learned first, then induction emerges — and the accompanying analysis of the T=2 bigram shortcut (Section 3.2) is a negative result about the learning dynamics: under small vocabulary conditions, the model can achieve low loss through non-induction strategies. This connects to the broader message that **architectural bias toward a circuit does not guarantee that circuit will be learned** if the training data provides easier shortcuts. This is a caution for anyone hoping that architecture design alone can enforce desired computational strategies.

**The QKV shifting regression (Appendix J, Figure 10).** Adding shift operations to Q as well as K and V degrades performance compared to KV-only shifting, and in some evaluations falls below the vanilla baseline. This is a critical ablation: it shows that the architectural intervention must be mechanistically precise, not just "more parameters" or "more blending." The induction head circuit specifically requires key-value decoupling; shifting queries introduces noise without addressing the routing bottleneck. The fact that an over-generalized version of the idea performs *worse* than doing nothing underscores the value of the mechanistic motivation.

**The longer-shift-window non-result (Figure 8c).** Extending the shift window from `[i-1, i]` to `[i-2, i]` or `[i-3, i]` does not improve performance despite adding more parameters and computation. This confirms that the induction benefit comes specifically from accessing the immediate predecessor, not from a general "wider context" effect. It also implies that simply adding more shift parameters is not a productive direction — further work would need to identify *different* circuits that benefit from multi-position shifts, not just extend the induction-oriented design.

**The constrained-parameter regression (Figure 8a).** Forcing α and β to remain convex combinations (gating) or to stay in [0, 1] degrades performance relative to unconstrained parameters. The analysis of the trained 2.9B model (Section 4.6) shows that some heads learn negative β₂ values, implementing operations that the constraints would prohibit. This negative result refines the understanding: the architecture is not merely "blending current and previous tokens" in a limited way; it provides a flexible, learnable attention mechanism where the model discovers unexpected uses (like subtraction, which the authors link to Elhage et al.'s "anti-copying prefix-search heads") that the designers did not anticipate.

Collectively, these negative results transform the paper from "KV shifting attention improves everything" (a weak and likely false claim) to "KV shifting attention specifically alleviates the depth and width bottlenecks of induction head circuits, with demonstrable and predictable boundaries." This intellectual honesty strengthens the positive claims by showing that the authors understand when and why their method works, not just that it does.

### Innovation 4: A Practical Demonstration That Circuit-Level Analysis Can Guide Architecture at Scale

The paper provides the first demonstration — to my knowledge — that a transformer architecture modification derived from a specific mechanistic circuit hypothesis (induction heads) **survives and delivers gains at production-scale pre-training** (19B parameters, 200B+ tokens, private multilingual data, standard evaluation benchmarks).

This is significant because the gap between mechanistic toy-model insights and production-scale deployment is vast. The field has many examples of architectural ideas that work in controlled settings but wash out at scale — the improvement is too small to survive optimization noise, or the training dynamics change fundamentally at larger model sizes, or the benchmark gains are zero when the training compute is competitive. The KV shifting attention experiments at 2.9B and 19B parameters (Table 2) show persistent, albeit modest, gains: +2.12 points average benchmark improvement for 2.9B at 500B tokens, +0.77 for 19B at 200B tokens. The training loss curves (Figure 4) show sustained gaps that do not close as training continues — the 2.9B KV shifting model at 340B tokens roughly matches the vanilla model's loss at 500B tokens, suggesting an effective training speedup.

What makes this an innovation rather than just a scaling report is the **chain of evidence** it completes. The paper traces a full arc: theoretical analysis (Theorems 1–3) → synthetic task validation (Figures 1–3) → scaling law analysis (Figure 7a, spanning several orders of magnitude in parameter count) → full pre-training runs with standard benchmarks (Table 2). Each stage confirms that the effect persists at the next level of realism and scale. This is rare in architecture papers, especially those motivated by mechanistic interpretability.

The robustness experiments (Figure 5) further strengthen the case: the advantage holds across five random seeds, four learning rates (including 1e-2 where vanilla diverges and KV shifting converges), and three model scales (1.5B, 6.7B, 13B in Figure 6). This is not a method that requires careful hyperparameter tuning to see a benefit — it appears to be a structurally better optimization landscape. The authors' hypothesis that "shifting KV provides a smooth key and value during model initialization, which may make the optimization process smoother" (Section 4.4) is a plausible mechanistic explanation for the robustness that deserves further investigation.

From a pragmatic perspective, the fact that the method adds only 4 parameters per head, uses $O(ND)$ additional computation, and is compatible with existing training/inference frameworks (FlashAttention, GQA, KV caching) means that **adoption is low-risk**. An organization can try KV shifting attention without overhauling their training pipeline, and the worst case — if the gains don't materialize on their data distribution — is a small parameter overhead. The open-source release of the 2.9B model weights (linked in the paper) and the community reimplementation (https://github.com/erogol/BlaGPT, cited in the Limitations section) lower the barrier further.

This innovation is more **engineering-pragmatic** than fundamental — it doesn't introduce a new theory or paradigm — but it fills a crucial gap that the field often neglects: proving that mechanistic insights survive the transition from 20M-parameter toy models to 20B-parameter production models trained on real data. The paper's willingness to run these expensive experiments, rather than stopping at the synthetic induction task, is what elevates the contribution from "interesting circuit analysis" to "actionable architecture guidance."

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The main pre-training experiments use a non-public private dataset with collection and filtering methods similar to FineWeb-edu (Penedo et al., 2024). The exact composition and size are not disclosed due to commercial reasons. The authors note that "when computing resources are available, we will use open-source data, such as RedPajama-1T (Weber et al.) to train two models for comparison." For evaluation, eight standard benchmarks are used: Lambada (Paperno et al., 2016), Winogrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), CMMLU (Li et al., 2023a), MMLU (Hendrycks et al.), and MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021). The selection spans language understanding, commonsense reasoning, multilingual knowledge, and mathematical problem-solving, providing broad coverage of language model capabilities.

- **Base model(s).** The paper trains models from scratch at multiple scales: 1.5B, 2.9B, 6.7B, 13B, and 19B parameters. The architecture is based on Llama2 (Touvron et al., 2023) with modifications: Grouped Query Attention (GQA, Ainslie et al., 2023) is used for the 2.9B and 19B production-oriented models (with 4 KV heads), while standard Multi-Head Attention (MHA) is used for the 1.5B, 6.7B, and 13B models. All models use Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) with base = 100,000, which the authors justify by citing Xu et al. (2024) on the relationship between context length and RoPE base. The 2.9B model has 32 layers, hidden size 2,560, 20 query heads, and 4 KV heads; the 19B model has 48 layers, hidden size 6,144, 48 query heads, and 4 KV heads (full configuration in Table 6, Appendix G). The choice of Llama-style architecture reflects "a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute" and ensures the results are relevant to contemporary LLM practice. For toy-model experiments (induction heads, n-gram, multi-hop), smaller models with 20M, 0.4M, 0.8K, and 50M parameters are used with the same Llama-style architecture but varying layer counts and hidden sizes.

- **Metrics.** For pre-training, the primary metric is training loss (cross-entropy) measured on the training data itself, reported as curves over tokens trained. For evaluation, accuracy (%) is the primary metric on all downstream benchmarks, computed using the standard evaluation protocols for each benchmark. For MMLU specifically, three evaluation conditions are tested: 5-shot (few-shot in-context learning), zero-shot (direct answer without examples), and cloze-style (following Waleffe et al., 2024), where the model fills in the blank rather than selecting from multiple choices. This three-way breakdown is designed to disentangle knowledge acquisition from format-specific abilities. For the synthetic induction task (Figure 1), the metric is "accuracy of next token prediction" at positions where an induction pattern exists — i.e., at the second occurrence of a token where the follow-up token should match the follow-up from the first occurrence. For the 3-gram task (Figure 3), accuracy measures correct prediction of the third token given the first two. For the Hop-k task (Appendix H, Figure 9), the metric is error rate (lower is better). For the iGSM math task (Appendix I, Table 7), accuracy is defined leniently: "as long as some step calculates the question asked and answers the correct answer, it is considered to be answered correctly, even if additional calculation steps are performed after answering the correct answer."

- **Baselines.** The primary baseline is **vanilla attention** with the identical model architecture (same layer count, hidden size, head count, RoPE base, training data, optimizer, and learning rate schedule), differing only in the attention computation. For the 3-gram task (Figure 3), the baseline is standard multi-head attention with the same model structure. For the Hop-k task (Figure 9), the baseline is the standard transformer from Sanford et al. (2024b), reproduced by the authors. For QKV shifting (Appendix J), the baseline includes both vanilla attention and KV shifting attention, tested at 19B scale. For the variant experiments (Figure 8a), the baselines are KV shifting attention with different constraint schemes (gating via sigmoid, clipping to [0,1]). No external baselines from other architecture families (e.g., Mamba, RWKV, RetNet) are directly trained or compared — the paper positions itself as an improvement within the transformer family, not a replacement of it. This is a deliberate scope limitation: the experiments test whether KV shifting attention improves over standard attention under matched conditions, not whether it outperforms fundamentally different architectures.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures training cost in **total tokens trained**, ranging from 10B tokens (for the 1.5B, 6.7B, and 13B models) to 200B tokens (19B model) to 500B tokens (2.9B model). The batch size in tokens varies by model scale: 1M tokens for 1.5B and 6.7B models, 16M for 2.9B, 2M for 13B, and 3M for 19B (Section 4.1 Hyperparameters). For the scaling law analysis (Figure 7a), the metric is validation loss on WebText (Radford et al.) after training each model on exactly 10B tokens, with non-embedding parameter count on the x-axis (following Kaplan et al., 2020). For toy experiments, compute is reported indirectly through training steps and model size (e.g., "20M no-embedding parameters" in Section 3.2, "hidden size = 1024" or "hidden size = 8"). The paper does not report FLOP counts or wall-clock time for any experiment. The relative computational cost of KV shifting vs. vanilla attention is characterized at the operation level: "The additional calculation caused by Eq 5 is O(ND), which is much smaller than O(ND² + N²D) in Eq. 6" (Section 2.2). For GQA models, this becomes O(Nh₁d₁) where h₁ is the number of KV pairs and d₁ is the head dimension. No memory overhead analysis is provided.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper notes that "due to computation limitations, almost experiments are only run once except there is an additional notion" (Section 4.1). The exception is the robustness experiment on the 1.5B model (Section 4.4, Figure 5a), where five independent runs with different random seeds for initialization and data sampling are conducted. For the learning rate sweep (Figure 5b), four learning rates are tested (1e-4, 2e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2), and for the 1e-2 case specifically (Figure 5c), five random seeds are run to confirm that vanilla diverges in all cases while KV shifting converges in all cases. No cross-validation is used for benchmark evaluation — results are reported on standard test sets. Error bars are absent from all figures, and the paper does not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests for benchmark score differences. This is a notable limitation: the benchmark improvements, while consistent in direction, are modest in absolute terms (e.g., 38.57% vs. 36.45% average for 2.9B), and without variance estimates, the reliability of individual benchmark differences is unclear. The authors partially address this through the multi-seed experiment (Figure 5a), which shows KV shifting consistently below vanilla across all five seeds, though with visible run-to-run variance in training loss.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Toy-Model Induction Learning (Section 3.2)

**Depth comparison (Figure 1a, left).** On synthetic induction data with hidden size = 1024 and a vocabulary of 8000 tokens, a 1-layer standard transformer (Vanilla-1L) fails to learn induction — accuracy remains near zero throughout training. A 2-layer standard transformer (Vanilla-2L) eventually achieves near-perfect induction accuracy, but converges substantially slower than a 1-layer KV shifting attention model (KV shifting-1L). The paper states: "KV shifting attention has a bias towards better learning induction, and its convergence speed is much faster than two-layer transformers." A 4-layer standard transformer (Vanilla-4L) does not learn faster than the 2-layer version, indicating that depth alone beyond the minimum requirement does not accelerate induction learning. This is a key finding: the architectural bias — not just additional capacity — drives the convergence advantage.

**Width comparison (Figure 1b, right).** Under pressure-tested conditions with hidden size = 8 (extreme width constraint), a 2-layer standard transformer (two times the parameters of the KV shifting model) "failed to cover up one of the answers mentioned in the previous text" — effectively, it cannot learn induction at all. In contrast, the 1-layer KV shifting attention achieves near-perfect induction accuracy. The paper interprets this as direct evidence for the width penalty: "this limitation in width will result in the model considering a limited number of different implicit inductions in parallel, or introducing noise in superposition."

**Takeaway from toy experiments:** The depth and width advantages demonstrated in toy settings align precisely with the theoretical predictions (Theorems 1 and 2). KV shifting attention provides both representational and optimization benefits for learning induction heads, particularly under resource constraints (shallow networks, narrow hidden states).

#### Large-Scale Pre-Training: 2.9B and 19B Models (Section 4.2, Table 2)

**2.9B model results (Table 2, top section).** The KV shifting attention model trained on 500B tokens achieves an average accuracy of **38.57%** across eight benchmarks, compared to **36.45%** for the vanilla attention baseline — a relative improvement of **+5.8%** (2.12 absolute percentage points). The gap is present at all three evaluation checkpoints (340B, 420B, 500B tokens):

| Tokens Trained | Vanilla-2.9B Avg | KV Shifting-2.9B Avg | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 340B | 32.48 | 36.46 | +3.98 |
| 420B | 34.08 | 36.55 | +2.47 |
| 500B | 36.45 | 38.57 | +2.12 |

The gap narrows slightly with more training (from +3.98 at 340B to +2.12 at 500B), but does not close. This suggests the benefit is most pronounced earlier in training — consistent with the "faster convergence" claim — but persists as an absolute advantage even at 500B tokens. The authors note: "KV shifting attention can achieve better results than the vanilla model when they both converge, although it may take several TB data for a 2.9B model to converge," suggesting the models have not fully converged and the gap might persist or change at larger token counts.

By individual benchmark, the largest absolute improvements at 500B tokens are on **MMLU** (+3.62 points: 37.26 → 40.88), **CMMLU** (+2.56 points: 38.22 → 40.78), and **Winogrande** (+1.27 points: 54.06 → 55.33). ARC-Easy shows the largest relative gain (+2.82 points: 36.20 → 39.02, or +7.8%). The improvements on **MATH** are small in absolute terms (1.80 → 2.60, +0.80 points) but substantial relative to the baseline (a 44% improvement). HellaSwag and Lambada show marginal gains (+0.03 and +2.85 respectively, though the Lambada figure is somewhat unstable — the 420B checkpoint shows a lower value than the 340B checkpoint for KV shifting). This heterogeneity suggests KV shifting attention helps more on knowledge-intensive and reasoning-heavy benchmarks (MMLU, CMMLU, ARC, MATH) than on surface-form tasks (HellaSwag).

**19B model results (Table 2, bottom section).** At 200B tokens, KV shifting attention achieves an average of **38.83%** vs. **38.06%** for vanilla — a gain of **+0.77 points** (+2.0% relative). The gap trajectory across checkpoints:

| Tokens Trained | Vanilla-19B Avg | KV Shifting-19B Avg | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 160B | 36.43 | 37.74 | +1.31 |
| 180B | 36.83 | 37.74 | +0.91 |
| 200B | 38.06 | 38.83 | +0.77 |

The gap narrows with training (as with the 2.9B model), suggesting diminishing returns as training proceeds. The smaller absolute improvement at 19B compared to 2.9B (+0.77 vs. +2.12 at their respective final checkpoints) could reflect: (a) the 19B model was trained on fewer tokens (200B vs. 500B), and the benefit might grow if training continued; (b) larger models may have sufficient width and depth to implement induction heads effectively even with vanilla attention, reducing the relative advantage of the architectural bias; or (c) the benefit may saturate at some model scale. The paper does not systematically investigate which of these explanations is correct.

Notable individual improvements at 19B: **ARC-Challenge** shows the largest gain (+3.54 points: 25.78 → 29.32, a 13.7% relative improvement), followed by **MMLU** (+0.61), **Lambada** (+1.47), and **MATH** (+0.60: 2.60 → 3.20). These are broadly consistent with the 2.9B pattern — reasoning and knowledge tasks benefit most.

**Training loss curves (Figure 4).** For both the 2.9B (Figure 4a) and 19B (Figure 4b) models, the training loss of KV shifting attention is **consistently below vanilla attention throughout training**, with the gap appearing early and persisting. At 2.9B, the KV shifting model at ~340B tokens achieves roughly the same loss as the vanilla model at ~500B tokens, suggesting approximately a **1.5× training speedup** in token-equivalent terms. At 19B, the gap is smaller but still visible and sustained across all 200B tokens.

#### MMLU Evaluation Protocol Comparison (Section 4.3, Table 3)

On the 2.9B model at 500B tokens, three MMLU evaluation protocols yield:

| Protocol | Vanilla-2.9B | KV Shifting-2.9B | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cloze (zero-shot) | 30.41 | 32.17 | +1.76 |
| Zero-shot (standard) | 33.14 | 37.13 | +3.99 |
| Few-shot (5-shot) | 37.26 | 40.88 | +3.62 |

KV shifting attention outperforms vanilla under all three protocols. The largest gap occurs under **zero-shot** evaluation (+3.99 points), which is interesting because zero-shot evaluation provides no in-context examples — the model cannot leverage retrieval of patterns from context. This suggests that KV shifting attention's benefit extends beyond improved in-context learning per se, and may reflect better **world knowledge acquisition** during pre-training. The cloze format (which "intends to break away from the format of standard multiple-choice and directly measure the knowledge") shows a smaller but still positive gap (+1.76), supporting the knowledge interpretation. The paper suggests: "the format of multiple-choice questions may be advantageous for KV shifting attention. The model can easily use context to compare the possibilities of various options" — this is a hypothesis about why the zero-shot and few-shot multiple-choice gaps are larger than the cloze gap, but it is not experimentally tested.

#### Robustness Experiments (Section 4.4, Figure 5)

**Random seed robustness (Figure 5a).** Five independent runs of 1.5B models with different random seeds for initialization and data sampling show that KV shifting attention achieves lower training loss than vanilla in all five cases (training for 10B tokens each). The training loss curves are "quite shaky" (as the authors note, due to the small batch size of 1M tokens and short training), but the ordering is consistent: KV shifting is always below vanilla at corresponding steps. This addresses the primary concern that the improvements might be statistical noise — the effect is robust to initialization and data order.

**Learning rate robustness (Figure 5b).** At learning rates of 1e-4, 2e-4, 1e-3, and 1e-2, KV shifting attention achieves lower training loss than vanilla at all four learning rate values. The advantage is consistent rather than being sensitive to a specific learning rate sweet spot.

**Extreme learning rate (Figure 5c).** At learning rate = 1e-2, the vanilla model **diverges** (training loss spikes and the model fails to learn), while KV shifting attention **converges normally** with a stable training loss curve. The authors attempted this under five random seeds: "the all results of each experiment are Vanilla divergence and KV shifting convergence." This is interpreted as evidence that "the optimization space for KV shifting attention may be flatter" — the shifted keys and values provide a smoother optimization landscape that tolerates larger learning rates. The authors connect this to their initialization scheme: "shifting KV provides a smooth key and value during model initialization, which may make the optimization process smoother." They also note that this "partially explains why in Figure 4, the 2.9B KV shifting attention leads more in terms of loss, because the 2.9B model is trained with a large learning rate" (8e-4, the largest among all model sizes — see Table 6 in Appendix G).

#### Scaling Experiments (Section 4.5, Figures 6 and 7)

**Training loss at 1.5B, 6.7B, 13B (Figure 6).** At all three model scales (trained on 10B tokens each), KV shifting attention achieves lower training loss than vanilla throughout training. The gap is visibly present at 1.5B (Figure 6a), 6.7B (Figure 6b), and 13B (Figure 6c), showing that the benefit is not limited to a specific model size. The 13B curve is shorter in steps (half the number of updates due to a larger batch size of 1M tokens vs. 0.5M for the others), but the gap is still clear.

**Scaling law (Figure 7a).** Following the methodology of Kaplan et al. (2020), validation loss on WebText is plotted against non-embedding parameter count for models trained on 10B tokens each. KV shifting attention outperforms vanilla at **every parameter scale** tested. The scaling curves for both methods follow the expected power-law relationship, with KV shifting attention shifted downward (lower loss) across the range. This suggests that the benefit is not merely a fixed offset but scales predictably with model size — KV shifting attention is not something that "helps small models but not large ones," at least up to the scale tested in this figure. The paper does not fit explicit scaling law coefficients (the standard $L(N) = (N_c / N)^{\alpha_N}$ form) for the two methods separately, so the exact scaling exponent comparison is not quantified.

**Extended training at 1.5B to 30B tokens (Figure 7b).** A 1.5B KV shifting model trained for 30B tokens (3× the 10B token budget used in the scaling law) maintains a validation loss advantage over vanilla throughout. The authors "calculate the validation set loss every 1000 steps, but the difference in validation loss still does not decrease." This partially addresses the concern from Table 2 that the gap might close with more training — at least at the 1.5B scale and 30B token budget, the gap persists. However, 30B tokens is still modest relative to the 500B tokens used for the main 2.9B experiment, and the 2.9B results in Table 2 do show a narrowing gap.

#### Learnable Parameter Analysis (Section 4.6, Table 4)

Analysis of the fully trained 2.9B model (500B tokens, 32 layers, 4 KV pairs per layer, total 128 KV pairs) reveals how the α and β parameters have evolved from their initialization:

| Configuration | β₁ > β₂ | β₁ ≤ β₂ | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| α₁ > α₂ | 50 | 17 | 67 |
| α₁ ≤ α₂ | 9 | **52** | 61 |
| Total | 59 | 69 | 128 |

The diagonal cells dominate (α₁ > α₂ and β₁ > β₂: 50 heads; α₁ ≤ α₂ and β₁ ≤ β₂: 52 heads), accounting for 102 out of 128 KV pairs (79.7%). This means the majority of heads keep the relative ranking of α₁ vs. α₂ aligned with β₁ vs. β₂ — either both favor the current token (standard-attention-like behavior) or both favor the shifted/previous token (induction-like behavior). The paper notes that this pattern was already present at 20B tokens (diagonal counts were 47 and 43), suggesting the specialization emerges early and stabilizes.

The off-diagonal cells are asymmetric: α₁ > α₂ but β₁ ≤ β₂ (upper-right: 17 heads) is more common than α₁ ≤ α₂ but β₁ > β₂ (lower-left: 9 heads). The upper-right configuration means the head uses the **current token's key to retrieve the previous token's value** — exactly the pattern needed for induction when the roles are reversed (query uses current key to find matches, retrieves the value of what followed the match). The lower-left configuration means using the **previous token's key to retrieve the current token's value** — a more exotic pattern. The paper speculates that this asymmetry arises because "the model can easily obtain information about the (i−1)th token by interacting with ith token under the causal mask, as the (i−1)th token may contain some information about the (i−1)th token to some extent," making the lower-left pattern less necessary.

The paper also reports that the sums ∑αᵢ and ∑βᵢ deviate from 1 during training (despite being initialized to 1), and some parameters become negative. A specific example from the trained model: in the 3rd KV pair of the 17th layer, α₁ = 0.08, α₂ = 0.43, β₁ = 0.34, β₂ = −0.15. This head combines a moderate shift toward the previous token's key (α₂ > α₁ but both positive) with subtraction of the previous token's value from the current token's value (β₂ negative). The authors connect this to Elhage et al. (2021)'s "anti-copying prefix-search heads," suggesting that unconstrained parameters enable function classes beyond simple key-value blending.

#### Further Experiments: Variants and Ablations (Section 4.7, Figure 8)

**Variant comparison (Figure 8a).** On a 1.5B model trained for 10B tokens, three versions of KV shifting are compared:
- **KV shifting** (the standard version, unconstrained parameters)
- **KV shifting gate** (parameters constrained via sigmoid to sum to 1: α₁ = sigmoid(a), α₂ = 1 − α₁, and similarly for β)
- **KV shifting 0 to 1** (parameters clipped to [0, 1]: αᵢ = min(max(αᵢ, 0), 1))

The standard (unconstrained) KV shifting achieves the lowest training loss. Both constrained variants perform worse. The paper interprets: "allowing α and β to have a wider range of degrees of freedom may enable the model to learn richer features." The gating variant, in particular, prevents negative β values that were observed in the trained model and linked to specific functional specializations.

**Ablation of K-shift and V-shift separately (Figure 8b).** Four configurations are tested on a 1.5B model with 10B tokens:
- KV shifting (both K and V shifted)
- K shifting only (V not shifted: β₁ = 1, β₂ = 0)
- V shifting only (K not shifted: α₁ = 1, α₂ = 0)
- Vanilla (no shifting)

Both partial shifts improve over vanilla but underperform full KV shifting. The ordering is: KV shifting (best) < K-shift-only < V-shift-only < vanilla (worst). The paper infers: "obtaining the value of the (i−1)th token or the value of the (i+1)th token by focusing on the key of the ith token is important in language modeling. If K shifting or V shifting is not used, the model needs to use two layers of attention to indirectly implement this operation." This ablation confirms that both the key shift and value shift contribute independently, and their combination is synergistic.

**Longer shift windows (Figure 8c).** Extending the shift from the immediate predecessor [i−1, i] to longer windows:
- KV shifting 1: window [i−1, i] (the standard version, 2 states per K/V)
- KV shifting 2: window [i−2, i] (3 states: α₁, α₂, α₃ where α₃ = 1 − α₁ − α₂, similarly for β)
- KV shifting 3: window [i−3, i] (4 states)

Training loss on a 1.5B model with 10B tokens shows KV shifting 1 performing best, with KV shifting 2 and 3 both slightly worse despite their additional parameters and computation. The paper concludes: "from the perspective of induction heads, using our current shifting window size is sufficient. If someone want to expand to longer windows, it may need to carefully design it." This validates that the benefit is specifically tied to the induction mechanism's requirement for adjacent-position access, not a general preference for wider local context in attention.

#### Multi-Hop and Math Reasoning (Appendices H and I)

**Hop-k tasks (Figure 9, Appendix H).** The multi-hop task from Sanford et al. (2024b) tests the model's ability to chain multiple induction steps. Taking the pink line (sequence length L=5) as an example, the vanilla model's error rate spikes significantly when the number of hops exceeds 8, reaching high error levels by 16 hops. In contrast, KV shifting attention maintains low error rates out to 16 hops. The paper notes: "this powerful ability to perform implicit reasoning implies that KV shifting attention may achieve better results in mathematical or reasoning abilities."

**iGSM math (Table 7, Appendix I).** On the iGSM dataset (Ye et al., 2024b), using a simplified evaluation protocol without final answer summarization:

| Training-Testing Split | Vanilla | KV Shifting |
|---|---|---|
| Tr12-Te15 | 0.8154 | 0.8909 |
| Tr21-Te24 | 0.8711 | 0.9062 |

KV shifting attention substantially outperforms vanilla on both train-test splits, with the largest improvement on the shorter training / longer test generalization setting (Tr12-Te15: +7.5 percentage points). The authors acknowledge this experiment is less thorough than Ye et al. (2024b) — "we only tested the accuracy without delving deeper into the analysis, such as the reasons for mistake" — and position it as preliminary evidence for reasoning benefits.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Vanilla attention at different depths (Figure 1a):** Increasing depth from 2 to 4 layers does not accelerate induction learning in standard transformers, showing that the benefit of KV shifting is not simply about adding capacity — it is about architectural alignment with the induction operation. Depth beyond the minimum requirement does not compensate for the mechanistic inefficiency.

**Vanilla attention at extreme width constraint (Figure 1b, hidden size = 8):** The 2-layer standard transformer fails to learn induction at a hidden size where 1-layer KV shifting attention succeeds, providing the cleanest evidence for the width penalty claim. This is a complementary ablation in the opposite direction: showing that standard attention fails where KV shifting succeeds, under identical task conditions.

**KV shifting at different depths (Figure 1a):** A 1-layer KV shifting attention matches and surpasses a 2-layer standard transformer on induction learning, confirming that one layer with the shift operation is sufficient and more efficient than two layers without it.

**KV shifting at extreme width constraints (Figure 1b, hidden size = 8):** The 1-layer KV shifting attention succeeds at induction where a 2-layer standard transformer (with twice the parameters) fails, demonstrating that the width reduction is not merely proportional but can be qualitative — KV shifting turns an impossible task into a solvable one.

**K-shift-only and V-shift-only (Figure 8b):** Both partial shifts underperform full KV shifting but outperform vanilla, indicating that each component (key decoupling and value decoupling) contributes independently. The finding that K-shift-only outperforms V-shift-only is a subtle result that the paper does not fully explain mechanistically — it may reflect that attending to the previous token's key is more important for induction than accessing the previous token's value, or that the training data favors one pattern over the other.

**Longer shift windows (Figure 8c):** Extending the shift to [i−2, i] or [i−3, i] provides no benefit over [i−1, i], and in fact slightly degrades performance — a negative result that prevents over-generalization of the method. The paper attributes this to sufficiency of the immediate predecessor for induction, but does not explore whether longer windows might help with other circuits (e.g., multi-hop reasoning across two steps).

**Constrained parameter regimes (Figure 8a):** Both gating (sigmoid, sum-to-1) and clipping to [0,1] degrade performance relative to unconstrained parameters, confirming that the learned configurations include parameter values outside the initial convex combination regime (e.g., negative β₂ for anti-copy behavior).

**QKV shifting (Appendix J, Figure 10):** On a 19B model trained for 180B tokens (training was interrupted by machine malfunction), QKV shifting (shifting Q in addition to K and V, per head) achieves similar training loss to KV shifting but **worse benchmark performance** on MMLU and CMMLU than both KV shifting and, in some cases, vanilla. The paper states: "the performance of shifting QKV is not as good as shifting KV, and even not as good as the vanilla." This negative result validates that the benefit is specific to decoupling keys and values — shifting queries introduces noise that interferes with the induction mechanism rather than helping it. The paper speculates: "from the perspective of induction heads, the shifting of Q is difficult to contribute to the formation of the induction heads mechanism."

**Random seeds (Figure 5a):** Five independent 1.5B training runs with different initialization and data sampling show KV shifting below vanilla in all cases, confirming statistical robustness despite visible run-to-run variance. This is the only experiment in the paper with multiple seeds at a single model configuration, so the robustness claim is empirically grounded at this scale but not verified at larger scales (2.9B, 19B) where single runs were conducted.

**Learning rate sweep (Figure 5b, 5c):** KV shifting outperforms vanilla at 1e-4, 2e-4, 1e-3, and avoids divergence at 1e-2 (where vanilla diverges in all five random seeds tested). The divergence-avoidance at 1e-2 is the most striking robustness result — it implies structural differences in the loss landscape, not just a favorable bias that could be tuned away.

**Training token count (Figure 7b):** The validation loss advantage of KV shifting persists from 10B to 30B tokens on a 1.5B model, suggesting the benefit does not wash out with more training at this scale. The paper does not report corresponding long-training robustness checks at 2.9B or 19B — the 500B and 200B token runs are single checkpoints each.

**Evaluation protocol variation (Table 3):** On MMLU, KV shifting outperforms vanilla under cloze, zero-shot, and few-shot evaluation protocols, confirming that the benefit is not an artifact of a specific evaluation format. The largest gap under zero-shot is consistent with improved knowledge acquisition during pre-training, not just improved in-context pattern matching.

### Critical Assessment

This section evaluates how well the reported experiments support the paper's three main claims, identifies genuine weaknesses and missing experiments, and specifies the conditions under which each claim holds.

#### Claim 1: "KV shifting attention reduces the depth and width requirements for induction heads."

**Direct experimental support: Strong, with scope limitations.**

The toy induction experiments (Figures 1a and 1b) directly demonstrate that: (a) 1-layer KV shifting attention achieves induction where 1-layer standard attention fails (depth reduction), and (b) 1-layer KV shifting at hidden size 8 succeeds where 2-layer standard attention at hidden size 8 fails (width reduction). Theorem 2 provides a constructive proof that exact induction representation is possible with one layer and dimension $d$ (vs. two layers and dimension $2d$ for standard attention). These are the strongest results in the paper — the claim is demonstrably true under the tested conditions.

**However, the following weaken the generality of this claim:**

1. **The synthetic induction task is a minimal, clean instantiation of the induction pattern — every sequence contains an induction example by construction, and the task has no competing objectives (no other language modeling demands).** In real language modeling pre-training, induction is one sub-task among many, and the model must learn it alongside n-gram statistics, factual knowledge, syntactic patterns, etc. The toy experiments show that KV shifting attention *can* learn induction with fewer resources, but they do not show that it *will* learn induction when other objectives compete for the same parameters — the pre-training experiments provide evidence for this, but the causal link between the toy results and the pre-training gains is correlational, not directly tested.

2. **Theoretical proofs use Alibi position encodings (for exact mathematical constructions), but the large-scale experiments use RoPE (base = 100,000).** The transition from Alibi to RoPE is not bridged analytically — there is no RoPE-specific constructive proof that KV shifting attention achieves exact induction with one layer. The authors implicitly assume the benefit is position-encoding-agnostic (since the key-value decoupling operation does not depend on position encoding), but this is an assumption, not a proven fact. The pre-training success with RoPE provides empirical evidence that the carry-over works, but the theoretical foundation for the "depth and width reduction" claim is strictly limited to the Alibi setting.

3. **The width experiment (Figure 1b) uses an extreme hidden size of 8, which is 128–768 times smaller than the hidden sizes used in the pre-training models (2048–6144).** The fact that the width penalty manifests catastrophically at dimension 8 does not prove that it is quantitatively significant at dimension 4096. The width penalty argument predicts that standard attention *wastes* some fraction of the hidden dimension on pass-through routing — but if the waste is, say, 10 dimensions out of 4096, the practical impact on language modeling could be negligible. The paper does not quantify how the width penalty scales with hidden dimension, nor does it estimate what fraction of a typical model's representational capacity is consumed by the pass-through requirement. The large-scale pre-training gains support the claim that the width reduction is practically meaningful, but the causal chain from "induction heads waste width" to "benchmark scores improve" is unverified.

#### Claim 2: "KV shifting attention leads to better performance and faster convergence in large-scale pre-training."

**Direct experimental support: Supported with important caveats about magnitude, statistical reliability, and data dependence.**

The evidence consists of:
- Training loss curves (Figures 4, 6, 7) consistently showing lower loss for KV shifting
- Benchmark scores (Table 2) showing improvement at 2.9B and 19B scales
- Robustness experiments (Figure 5) showing consistency across seeds and learning rates
- Scaling analysis (Figure 7a) showing gains across parameter counts

**Strengths of the evidence:**
- The loss advantage is visible early and persists through training (does not close, though it narrows at 2.9B from +3.98 to +2.12)
- The advantage holds across five model scales (1.5B, 2.9B, 6.7B, 13B, 19B), suggesting scalability
- The learning rate robustness (especially the divergence-avoidance at lr=1e-2) suggests a structural optimization benefit beyond just a favorable initialization
- Multiple evaluation protocols on MMLU (Table 3) confirm the benchmark gains are not format-dependent

**Weaknesses and limitations:**

1. **The absolute benchmark improvements are modest.** The 2.12-point average improvement on the 2.9B model at 500B tokens includes some benchmarks that barely move (HellaSwag: +0.03) and others that show larger gains (MMLU: +3.62, CMMLU: +2.56). The average masks substantial heterogeneity. For a practitioner deciding whether to adopt KV shifting attention, the question is whether a ~2-point average improvement across 8 benchmarks justifies even a small additional implementation complexity. The paper does not discuss the practical significance threshold, and the improvement on some widely-watched benchmarks (HellaSwag, Lambada) is close to zero.

2. **No statistical significance testing or confidence intervals are reported for benchmark scores.** The benchmark differences are point estimates from single evaluation runs. The multi-seed experiments (Figure 5a) show that training loss has visible run-to-run variance at 1.5B scale — it is plausible that benchmark scores at 2.9B+ scale also exhibit run-to-run variance. Without error bars, a reader cannot assess whether the +2.12 average improvement at 2.9B is reliably above what would be expected from training variance alone. The five-seed training loss curves (Figure 5a) support the direction of improvement, but training loss on 10B tokens at 1.5B scale is a different quantity from benchmark accuracy at 2.9B/500B tokens.

3. **The training data is non-public and not described in detail.** The paper states only that "data collection and filtering methods are similar to FineWeb-edu" and that the data contains "a large amount data from Fineweb-edu, as well as some other filtered data" (Appendix G). This means: (a) the results are not reproducible by the community (though the model weights are released for the 2.9B versions), (b) it is impossible to assess whether the data distribution favors induction patterns in a way that amplifies the KV shifting benefit, and (c) the open-source replication mentioned in the Limitations (https://github.com/erogol/BlaGPT) cannot be directly compared because the training data differs.

4. **Training FLOPs are not matched between vanilla and KV shifting.** KV shifting attention has a small additional computational cost (O(ND) for the shift/blend, plus 4h extra parameters). The paper does not account for this in any FLOPs-matched comparison. The actual cost difference is claimed to be negligible relative to the total training FLOPs, but no numbers are provided to substantiate this. If the shift adds even 0.5% to training cost, and the benchmark improvement is ~5% relative, the effective "efficiency" gain is smaller than the raw score differences suggest.

5. **Only one architecture family (Llama-style) and one position encoding (RoPE, base=100k) are tested.** The paper does not demonstrate that the benefit transfers to other transformer variants (e.g., models without GQA, different RoPE bases, models with parallel attention, encoder-decoder architectures). The Alibi theoretical analysis covers a different position encoding than what is actually used.

6. **The convergence speed advantage is measured in tokens, not wall-clock time.** The shift operation introduces a small per-step overhead, and the paper does not report whether this overhead affects training throughput. If the shift adds 2% to per-step time but improves token efficiency by 50% (as suggested by Figure 4a), the net speedup is still substantial, but unquantified.

#### Claim 3: "KV shifting attention enhances the model's ability to learn induction heads, which is beneficial to language modeling."

**Direct experimental support: Plausible but largely circumstantial — the mechanistic link is not directly verified in the pre-trained models.**

The chain of evidence is:
1. Theorem 2: KV shifting attention *can* represent induction heads with fewer resources (constructive proof)
2. Figure 1a: KV shifting attention *learns* induction faster on synthetic data
3. Theorem 3: Under simplified conditions, gradient descent on KV shifting attention converges to induction-like parameter configurations
4. Table 4: In the trained 2.9B model, many heads learn parameter configurations consistent with induction (α₁ ≤ α₂ and β₁ ≤ β₂) or standard attention (α₁ > α₂ and β₁ > β₂)
5. Table 2: KV shifting attention improves benchmark scores

**The gap in this chain is between steps 4 and 5.** The paper does not demonstrate that the benchmark improvements are *caused by* improved induction head formation, rather than by some other consequence of the architectural change. The parameter statistics (Table 4) show that heads *can* specialize into induction-like configurations, but do not prove that:
- The heads that adopt induction-like α/β values actually perform induction operations on real text
- The performance difference on MMLU or MATH is attributable to these induction-specialized heads
- The pass-through width penalty (the specific mechanism hypothesized in Property 1) is actually alleviated in the trained model

In other words, the paper provides a compelling *origin story* for why KV shifting attention should help (induction heads are important → standard attention is inefficient at them → KV shifting fixes this), and demonstrates that it *does* help (benchmark scores go up), but does not verify the middle step — that induction heads are actually formed more efficiently in the trained model and that this specific improvement drives the performance gains. It is entirely possible that KV shifting attention helps for reasons unrelated to induction heads (e.g., the shift provides a form of local smoothing that regularizes attention, or the additional parameters add useful flexibility for an unrelated reason). The fact that QKV shifting — which also adds parameters and local mixing — performs *worse* argues against a generic "more parameters/regularization" explanation, but does not positively confirm the induction heads explanation.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the mechanistic claim:**

- **Induction head probing on the trained models.** A direct test would be to construct synthetic induction sequences (of the form [A][B]...[A]) and test whether KV shifting-trained models have higher accuracy on the [B] prediction than vanilla-trained models, especially at positions where the induction head should activate. This would connect the toy task (Figure 1a) to the pre-trained model behavior.
- **Ablation of induction-specialized heads.** If heads with α₁ ≤ α₂ and β₁ ≤ β₂ are identified as induction heads, ablating them (setting their attention to zero) should disproportionately hurt performance on induction-heavy tasks compared to ablating standard-attention-configured heads (α₁ > α₂ and β₁ > β₂). This would establish a causal link between specific parameter configurations and downstream performance.
- **Difficulty-binned evaluation.** Following the approach of the reference example in the prompt, difficulty-binning evaluation examples by whether they require induction (e.g., tasks with repeating patterns vs. tasks requiring only local statistics) would test whether KV shifting's advantage concentrates on induction-heavy examples.
- **Direct measurement of the width penalty.** Analyze whether the residual stream of intermediate tokens in standard transformers carries pass-through information about preceding tokens (using linear probes trained to decode the previous token from the current token's hidden state), and compare the strength of this signal between vanilla and KV shifting models. The width penalty hypothesis predicts that vanilla models should exhibit stronger pass-through signals because they need to route predecessor information through the residual stream.

#### Overall Assessment

The paper's central contribution — that a mechanistically-motivated architectural modification can improve language model pre-training at scale — is empirically supported, though more modestly than the theoretical narrative might suggest. The benchmark improvements are real and consistent in direction, but their magnitude (~2 points average at 2.9B, <1 point at 19B) raises the question of practical significance. The most convincing evidence is not the absolute benchmark numbers but rather: (a) the convergence speed advantage (which could translate to meaningful compute savings if the per-step overhead is negligible), (b) the unusual robustness to extreme learning rates (suggesting a fundamentally different optimization landscape), and (c) the consistent loss advantage across five model scales and multiple seeds.

The paper would be significantly strengthened by: a FLOPs-matched training comparison; statistical error estimates for benchmark scores; open-source training data for reproducibility; and mechanistic validation that the pre-training gains are specifically attributable to improved induction. Without these, the paper makes a credible case that KV shifting attention is a promising architectural modification, but does not close the loop between the mechanistic motivation and the empirical outcomes.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Causal Link from Induction Heads to Pre-Training Gains Is Not Verified

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire motivation rests on a specific mechanistic narrative: standard transformers require excessive depth and width to implement induction heads, KV shifting attention alleviates this bottleneck, and this architectural bias toward easier induction learning is what drives the observed improvements in language model pre-training (benchmark scores, faster convergence). However, this causal chain is never directly tested in the large-scale pre-trained models. The paper demonstrates that KV shifting attention *can* represent induction heads with fewer resources (Theorem 2), that it *does* learn induction faster on synthetic data (Figure 1a), and that the trained models *do* exhibit parameter configurations consistent with induction specialization (Table 4). But it does not show that the pre-training performance gains are specifically *caused by* improved induction head formation.

**The consequence.** The paper's primary contribution is framed as demonstrating that mechanistic interpretability can guide architecture design toward practical improvements. If the performance gains actually arise from an unanticipated mechanism — for instance, the key-value shift providing a form of local smoothing that improves optimization independently of induction, or the four extra parameters per head adding useful representational flexibility for reasons unrelated to induction circuits — then the paper's methodological contribution (circuit-driven architecture design) is substantially weakened. The results would still show an improvement, but for different reasons than claimed, and the methodology could not be confidently extended to other circuits (parity, multi-hop reasoning, etc.) because the mechanism linking circuit analysis to practical gains would remain obscure. A practitioner considering whether to invest in circuit-analysis-driven architecture modifications for their own problems would lack evidence that this approach works *for the stated reason*, as opposed to working for some unrelated and potentially non-transferable reason.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence for the induction heads narrative is entirely at the toy-model level (Section 3: synthetic induction tasks, the Theorem 2 constructive proof, the Theorem 3 gradient dynamics analysis under simplified conditions) plus the post-hoc parameter statistics in the trained 2.9B model (Table 4, showing that many heads settle into configurations where α₁ ≤ α₂ and β₁ ≤ β₂, consistent with induction-specialized behavior). However, there is no experiment that tests whether: (a) the heads with induction-like α/β values actually implement induction operations on real text (e.g., probing attention patterns for the characteristic "previous token match" behavior), (b) the performance gap between KV shifting and vanilla on, say, MMLU is attributable to specific test items that require induction-style pattern matching, or (c) ablating the induction-configured heads disproportionately harms induction-reliant tasks. The paper also does not measure whether the width penalty (information about preceding tokens being routed through intermediate token hidden states, as diagnosed in Property 1) is actually present in standard transformers and actually alleviated in KV shifting transformers. Section 3.3 explicitly acknowledges that "the motivation of our KV shifting attention is to reduce the width and depth required for induction," but this motivation is not experimentally validated in the pre-trained models — only assumed from the toy results.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to verify the causal mechanism in the pre-trained models. There is no suggestion of future work to do so. The parameter analysis (Section 4.6, Table 4) is the closest the paper comes, but it only shows that learned parameter configurations are *consistent with* induction specialization, not that they *cause* improved language modeling via induction. The authors briefly acknowledge theoretical incompleteness in a footnote (Section 3.1): "Theorems 1 and 2 only provide constructive upper bounds for implementing induction heads. A more rigorous statement would be to prove that the lower bound of Theorem 1 is smaller than the upper bound of Theorem 2." This is a disclaimer about the theoretical strength of the representation result, not about the causal mechanism in trained models. A practitioner should therefore treat the paper's primary narrative ("KV shifting attention improves language modeling *because* it makes induction heads easier to learn") as a plausible but unverified hypothesis, not an established mechanism.

### Training Data Is Non-Public, Making Results Unreproducible and Data-Dependence Uncharacterized

**The assumption or constraint.** The large-scale pre-training experiments (2.9B and 19B models) are conducted on a private, non-public dataset. Section 4.1 (Datasets) states: "Due to some commercial reasons and data limitations, we used non-public private data for pre training, which means that the models are trained on our own dataset. Our data collection and filtering methods are similar to FineWeb-edu (Penedo et al., 2024)." Appendix G adds slightly more detail: "due to privacy reasons, we are unable to provide a detailed description of the training data here. Our training data contains a large amount data from Fineweb-edu (Lozhkov et al., 2024), as well as some other filtered data." The paper does not specify the composition, size, language distribution, domain breakdown, or filtering criteria of this dataset beyond the single sentence about FineWeb-edu similarity.

**The consequence.** Reproducibility of the main results is impossible without access to the same or a highly similar training corpus. A researcher cannot confirm whether KV shifting attention's benchmark improvements replicate under matched conditions. More importantly, the data-dependence of the method cannot be assessed. Induction patterns — the mechanism KV shifting attention is designed to facilitate — are more prevalent in certain text types (e.g., code, structured lists, formulaic text, chain-of-thought reasoning) and less prevalent in others (e.g., free-form narrative, dialogue). If the private training data happens to be richer in induction-pattern-heavy text than typical web corpora (or, conversely, if FineWeb-edu-style filtering inadvertently selects for such patterns), the reported gains might overstate what a practitioner would see on their own, differently-distributed training data. Conversely, if the data is essentially a typical web corpus, the gains might generalize — but there is no way to verify this without data description or replication on a public corpus. The paper acknowledges this is suboptimal: "When computing resources are available, we will use open-source data, such as RedPajama-1T (Weber et al.) to train two models for comparison, one is the baseline and the other is the KV shifting attention" (Section 4.1). This planned replication, which would resolve the concern, is deferred to future work, with no timeline given. The community reimplementation cited in the Limitations (Section 6, "someone has implemented it in open source and achieved better results than Vanilla. https://github.com/erogol/BlaGPT") provides some external validation, but this is a third-party, uncontrolled experiment with unknown differences in model configuration, data, and training recipe — it does not substitute for a controlled public-data replication by the authors.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no analysis of the training data's properties, no breakdown of induction-relevant text types (e.g., code, structured data, lists, repeated patterns), and no ablation testing whether the KV shifting benefit varies with data composition. The robustness experiments (Figures 5, 6, 7) show the benefit is consistent across random seeds, learning rates, and model sizes — but these are all trained on the *same* (private) data distribution. The paper does not test whether the benefit persists on a qualitatively different data distribution (e.g., heavily narrative vs. heavily structured text, or monolingual English vs. multilingual). The only open-source data used is WebText for the scaling law validation set (Figure 7a), but this is an evaluation corpus, not training data — it measures generalization, not data-dependence of the training benefit.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially mitigates the reproducibility concern by open-sourcing the 2.9B model weights (both vanilla and KV shifting versions, linked in footnotes 7 and 8). This allows evaluation on arbitrary benchmarks and probe tasks, but does not enable reproduction of the training result itself (e.g., measuring convergence speed advantage). The promised future public-data training run is mentioned but not completed. Until such a replication exists, a practitioner evaluating KV shifting attention should recognize that the reported benchmark improvements are validated on exactly one (undisclosed) training data distribution, and the transfer to their own data — especially if it differs substantially from FineWeb-edu-style filtered web text — is uncertain.

### Benchmark Improvements Are Modest, Especially at the Larger Scale

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper claims that "KV shifting attention achieved better performance than baseline at various scales and training token numbers" (Section 4.2), and frames this as a demonstration that the architectural modification "survives and delivers gains at production-scale pre-training." While technically true, the absolute magnitude of the gains is modest, and the trend with scale raises questions about whether the benefit persists or diminishes at larger model sizes.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to adopt KV shifting attention for their next training run must weigh the expected benefit against the implementation cost, even if that cost is low. The headline numbers are: +2.12 percentage points average across 8 benchmarks for the 2.9B model at 500B tokens, and +0.77 points average for the 19B model at 200B tokens (Table 2). At 19B scale, this translates to improvements on individual benchmarks that range from 0.00 (HellaSwag: 47.36 → 48.42, a 1.06-point gain but checkpoints at 160B and 180B show essentially identical values between the two models on this benchmark) to a maximum of 3.54 (ARC-Challenge: 25.78 → 29.32). Several benchmarks show near-zero improvement: Lambada (61.93 vs. 60.88 at 200B, a 1.47 gain, but the 180B checkpoint shows KV shifting at 60.20 vs. vanilla at 58.80 — the gap is unstable across checkpoints), Winogrande (49.01 vs. 48.38, a 0.63 loss at 200B), and HellaSwag (47.36 vs. 48.42, but the 180B checkpoint shows KV shifting lower: 47.78 vs. 48.16). The MMLU improvement, one of the stronger results, is 0.61 points (42.68 → 43.29). These differences, while directionally favoring KV shifting in most cases, are within a range that could plausibly be influenced by training variance — and the paper runs only a single training seed at these scales, with no confidence intervals.

The convergence speed advantage is more promising for practical adoption: Figure 4a suggests the 2.9B KV shifting model at ~340B tokens matches the vanilla model at ~500B tokens, implying roughly a 1.5× speedup in token-equivalent terms. However, this is measured on training loss, not downstream benchmarks, and the 19B model shows a smaller gap (Figure 4b). A cost-benefit analysis requires knowing the per-step overhead of the shift operation relative to the token savings — the paper does not report training throughput or wall-clock time, only tokens processed. The theoretical claim that the additional computation is $O(ND)$ vs. $O(ND^2 + N^2D)$ for the main attention (Section 2.2) implies a negligible overhead, but no empirical measurements (e.g., tokens/second, GPU memory increase, training step time) are provided.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence is entirely contained in Table 2 (benchmark scores), Figures 4, 6, 7 (training/validation loss curves), and the benchmark-specific breakdowns in Section 4.3. The paper notes in Section 4.1 that "due to computation limitations, almost experiments are only run once except there is an additional notion." The "additional notion" is the five-seed robustness experiment at 1.5B scale (Figure 5a), which shows consistent training loss advantage but also visible run-to-run variance. At 2.9B and 19B scales, no seed-replication is done, so the variance of benchmark scores is unknown. The paper also does not test whether the performance gap widens, narrows, or stabilizes with further training beyond 500B (2.9B) or 200B (19B) tokens — the narrowing trend at both scales (gap decreasing from +3.98 at 340B to +2.12 at 500B for 2.9B; from +1.31 at 160B to +0.77 at 200B for 19B) could continue, potentially closing the gap entirely at convergence. The authors acknowledge they do not know where convergence lies: "it may take several TB data for a 2.9B model to converge" (Section 4.2).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address the modest magnitude or the scale trend directly. It does not provide statistical error estimates, does not project whether the gap would survive to convergence, and does not discuss practical significance thresholds. The five-seed experiment at 1.5B (Figure 5a) partially mitigates variance concerns at that scale but does not extend to larger models. The scaling law analysis (Figure 7a) shows the advantage holds across parameter counts from roughly 1M to 13M non-embedding parameters (≤1.5B total parameters), but all models are trained on only 10B tokens — far from convergence — and the gap in validation loss, while consistently present, is unquantified in FLOPs or training-time terms. A practitioner considering a 70B+ parameter model should recognize that the evidence for benefit at scale is limited to ≤19B parameters and <200B training tokens on a single data distribution, with single training runs and modest absolute gains.

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost for Adaptive Allocation Is Not Addressed

The paper presents a method that modifies the attention mechanism uniformly — it does not adapt per-example or estimate example difficulty. However, the *structure of the contribution* parallels the compute-optimal test-time scaling literature (see the reference example in the prompt) in an important way: it claims that a specific architectural design choice (KV shifting) makes a particular sub-task (induction) easier to learn, and that this translates to better overall task performance. This creates an implicit reliance on the same architectural mechanism working across all examples, regardless of whether they contain induction patterns or not. The limitation is that the paper never characterizes *which* examples benefit from the modification or whether the uniform application of KV shifting to all tokens incurs a cost on examples where induction is irrelevant.

**The assumption or constraint.** KV shifting attention is applied uniformly to every token in every sequence, regardless of whether that sequence contains induction patterns that would benefit from the key-value decoupling. The learnable parameters α₁, α₂, β₁, β₂ are per-head and can in principle allow a head to learn α₂ ≈ 0, β₂ ≈ 0 (reducing to approximately standard attention) if the shift is not useful for that head's function. However, at initialization and during early training, every head experiences the shifted key-value blend, and the optimization process must discover which heads should specialize toward induction-like configurations and which should revert to standard-attention-like configurations. The paper does not analyze whether the uniform application of shifting imposes a "speed limit" on non-induction learning early in training, or whether the model's need to "undo" the shift for some heads creates a transient optimization cost.

**The consequence.** The uniform-application regime introduces an implicit trade-off: the architectural bias toward induction heads (which is the intended benefit) may come with a bias *against* other kinds of attention patterns that the model must then overcome through training. If this is the case, then the net benefit of KV shifting depends on the prevalence of induction-relevant patterns in the training data — on induction-heavy data, the bias is helpful; on induction-light data, the bias may be harmful or neutral. The paper's n-gram experiment (Figure 3) provides some reassurance that 3-gram learning is not *impaired*, but this is tested on synthetic data with a single isolated task, not in the context of competing pre-training objectives where the model must simultaneously allocate representational capacity across many sub-tasks. The paper does not test whether KV shifting attention converges *slower* than vanilla on non-induction sub-tasks during pre-training, or whether the induction bias causes the model to over-rely on pattern-matching when other strategies (e.g., factual recall) would be more appropriate. The QKV shifting negative result (Appendix J) — where shifting queries as well degrades performance — suggests that architectural biases can indeed be harmful when they are mismatched to the task, making it plausible that uniform KV shifting might be suboptimal for some fraction of language modeling.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides two pieces of relevant evidence. First, the 3-gram experiment (Figure 3) shows that KV shifting accuracy on a pure n-gram task matches vanilla — the bias does not *prevent* learning of a non-induction task in isolation. Second, the parameter analysis (Table 4) shows that in the trained 2.9B model, a substantial fraction of KV pairs (50 out of 128, or 39%) settle into α₁ > α₂ and β₁ > β₂ configurations — essentially standard-attention-like behavior — indicating that the model does learn to "turn off" the shift for some heads. However, there is no analysis of how quickly these heads reach their final configuration compared to heads that specialize toward induction, whether the early-training shift bias causes any heads to take suboptimal trajectories, or whether the loss curves for non-induction sub-tasks differ between KV shifting and vanilla during pre-training. The paper also does not characterize the prevalence of induction patterns in its training data, so the reader cannot assess whether the reported gains would generalize to data with a different induction-to-non-induction ratio.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. There is no experiment that bins test examples by their reliance on induction, no analysis of per-task convergence curves during pre-training, and no discussion of whether a more targeted application of KV shifting (e.g., only in certain layers, or only when specific patterns are detected) would outperform the uniform application. The learnable-parameter analysis (Section 4.6) is descriptive (what configurations emerged) rather than causal (what effect those configurations had on learning dynamics). The uniform application is treated as a feature (simplicity, compatibility with existing frameworks), not a limitation, but a practitioner should recognize that the method's effectiveness likely depends on the induction-richness of their training data in ways the paper does not characterize.

### The Method Is Validated on a Single Architecture Family and Position Encoding

**The assumption or constraint.** All large-scale experiments use a single architecture family (Llama-style decoder-only transformers) with a specific position encoding choice (Rotary Position Embedding, RoPE, with base = 100,000) and, for the production-scale models, Grouped Query Attention (GQA) with 4 KV heads. The theoretical analysis (Theorems 1 and 2), however, uses Alibi position encodings — a fundamentally different mechanism from RoPE. The paper does not bridge this gap analytically or experimentally: there is no RoPE-specific theoretical construction showing that KV shifting attention achieves exact induction with one layer, and no ablation testing whether the benefit depends on the choice of position encoding. The paper briefly justifies the RoPE base = 100,000 by citing Xu et al. (2024) on the relationship between context length and RoPE base, but this addresses a different design choice (base value) and does not test whether the KV shifting benefit transfers to the RoPE setting as opposed to being specific to the Alibi-like formulation used in the proofs.

**The consequence.** A practitioner using a different architecture (e.g., encoder-decoder transformers like T5, models without GQA, models with learned absolute position embeddings, or even Llama-style models with different RoPE bases) has no direct evidence that KV shifting attention will provide the reported benefits. The theoretical motivation is strongest for Alibi-based attention (where exact results are proved), but the practical experiments all use RoPE — this gap means the theoretical and empirical parts of the paper are not fully integrated. If the benefit of KV shifting attention depends on the specific way RoPE encodes relative positions (e.g., through the dot-product structure of rotary embeddings), then changing the position encoding could eliminate or reverse the benefit. Conversely, if the benefit is genuinely position-encoding-agnostic (as the paper implicitly assumes), this should hold across architectures — but that claim is untested.

Additional unverified dimensions: the paper uses GQA for the 2.9B and 19B models but MHA for the 1.5B, 6.7B, and 13B models. While the benefit appears in both settings (Figures 6 and 4), there is no direct MHA-vs-GQA comparison at the same scale, so a practitioner using MHA at production scale (e.g., 70B parameters without GQA) does not know whether the GQA results transfer. The vocabulary sizes also differ (36,000 for MHA models, 48,000 for GQA models), introducing an additional confound.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The architecture variations tested include different model sizes (1.5B through 19B), different head configurations (MHA vs. GQA), different layer counts, and different batch sizes (Table 6, Appendix G). The benefit is consistent across these variations — training loss is lower for KV shifting in all configurations. This provides some evidence of architectural robustness, but all models share the Llama-style decoder-only design and RoPE position encoding. There is no experiment with: a different position encoding (Alibi, learned absolute, NoPE), a different architecture family (encoder-decoder, mixture-of-experts, non-autoregressive), or a different pre-training paradigm (masked language modeling, span corruption). The theoretical analysis (Theorem 2) suggests the benefit should be strongest with Alibi (where exact equality is provable), but Alibi-based transformers are less common in practice than RoPE-based ones — the paper does not test whether the practical benefit is larger or smaller in the (theoretically cleaner) Alibi setting.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation explicitly. The open-source release of the 2.9B model (a RoPE+GQA variant) allows the community to test on different evaluation settings but does not provide weights for alternate architecture configurations. The community reimplementation cited in Section 6 (https://github.com/erogol/BlaGPT) may test different configurations, but this is not controlled by the authors and its results are not reported in the paper. A practitioner using a non-Llama architecture should treat the KV shifting benefit as plausible but unvalidated for their setting, and should budget for a controlled experiment (e.g., a small-scale training run comparing vanilla and KV shifting on their specific architecture and position encoding) before committing to a full-scale training run.

### No FLOPs-Matched or Wall-Clock Comparison Is Provided

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates the benefit of KV shifting attention entirely in terms of **tokens processed** — training loss and benchmark accuracy are compared at the same number of training tokens. However, the KV shifting operation adds computation: the shift and linear combination in Equation 5 require $O(ND)$ additional operations per attention layer (for an $N$-token sequence with $D$-dimensional hidden states), and the learnable parameters α₁, α₂, β₁, β₂ increase the parameter count by $4h$ per layer (where $h$ is the number of heads or KV pairs). While the paper argues that $O(ND)$ is negligible compared to the $O(ND^2 + N^2D)$ cost of the main attention computation (Section 2.2), it provides no empirical measurement of the actual training throughput impact — tokens per second, GPU memory consumption, or wall-clock training time.

**The consequence.** The convergence speed advantage, which is arguably the most practically significant result (Figure 4a suggesting ~1.5× fewer tokens needed to reach the same training loss), cannot be converted into a real-world efficiency estimate. If the per-step overhead is 2% (likely small, given the O(ND) vs. O(N²D) argument), the net speedup is approximately 1.47×. If the overhead is 10% (due to memory access patterns, kernel launch overhead, or less optimized implementations of the shift operation), the net speedup drops to 1.36×. If the benefit is measured only in tokens and not in FLOPs or wall-clock time, the paper's implicit claim — "KV shifting attention is more efficient" — is partially unsubstantiated. A practitioner deciding between (a) training a model with KV shifting for 500B tokens and (b) training a standard model for, say, 600B tokens (to match performance through additional tokens rather than architecture change) needs a FLOPs-matched or time-matched comparison. The paper provides neither.

The memory overhead is also relevant: the shifted keys and values must be stored (or recomputed on-the-fly) during attention. For training with activation checkpointing, the additional memory for storing pre-shift K and V may increase the activation memory footprint, potentially reducing the maximum batch size or requiring more aggressive gradient accumulation. For inference with KV caching, the shift introduces additional bookkeeping (storing the previous token's K and V to compute the shifted versions for the current token — the inference code in Appendix F shows `self.last_k` and `self.last_v` state variables). None of these practical considerations are quantified.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides only the asymptotic computational complexity analysis: "The additional calculation caused by Eq 5 is O(ND), which is much smaller than O(ND² + N²D) in Eq. 6" (Section 2.2). For GQA models, this becomes O(Nh₁d₁) where h₁ is the number of KV pairs. No empirical training throughput, GPU memory profiling, or wall-clock comparison is reported for any model scale. The robustness experiments (Figure 5) vary random seeds and learning rates but not implementation efficiency metrics. The scaling law experiment (Figure 7a) plots validation loss against non-embedding parameter count, following Kaplan et al. (2020) — this controls for model size but not for training FLOPs or time.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The authors' statement that the overhead is "much smaller" than standard attention is a theoretical claim, not an empirical measurement. Given that the paper targets practitioners who might deploy KV shifting at scale (where even small percentage overheads translate to significant dollar costs), the absence of throughput measurements is a notable gap. A minimal mitigation would be to report tokens/second during training for both vanilla and KV shifting at one of the tested scales (e.g., 1.5B on the same GPU configuration), along with peak GPU memory usage and the effective batch size achievable under memory constraints. The paper acknowledges the limitation of computational resources (Section 6: "Due to limitations in computing resources and other limitations, many experiments cannot be repeated many times") but does not flag the throughput comparison as a specific missing measurement, nor does it commit to providing it in future work.
