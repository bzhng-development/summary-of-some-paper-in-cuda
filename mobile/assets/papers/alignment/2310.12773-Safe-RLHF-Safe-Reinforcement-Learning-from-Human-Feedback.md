# Safe RLHF: Safe Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

**ArXiv:** [2310.12773](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12773)

## 🎯 Pitch

Aligning LLMs to be both helpful and harmless forces a messy trade-off: being too helpful produces dangerous answers, while rigid safety kills usefulness. Safe RLHF solves this by treating the problem as a constrained optimization—maximizing helpfulness subject to a hard safety budget—and dynamically tuning the tension via a Lagrangian, slashing Alpaca-7B’s harmful outputs while boosting helpfulness scores.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **Safe RLHF**, a novel algorithm for aligning large language models with human values that explicitly decouples helpfulness and harmlessness into separate objectives—a reward model for helpfulness and a cost model for harmlessness—then formalizes safety as a constrained optimization problem solved via the Lagrangian method. The authors conduct three iterative rounds of fine-tuning on Alpaca-7B (reproduced from LLaMA-7B) using human preference data with decoupled two-dimensional annotations, paired with red-teaming prompt augmentation starting from the second round. Safe RLHF reduces the probability of harmful responses on the evaluation set from 53.08% for Alpaca-7B to 2.45% for Beaver-v3 while simultaneously improving helpfulness as measured by Elo scores from both GPT-4 (+244.91 points) and human evaluators (+363.86 points), and achieves these gains while outperforming statically-balanced reward shaping baselines across a range of fixed coefficients, establishing that dynamic constraint-based balancing navigates the helpfulness-harmlessness tension more effectively than static weighting—but only when the cost model is trained with both pairwise preference loss and safety classification terms to enable automatic Lagrange multiplier adjustment.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Helpfulness and Harmlessness Are in Tension

The fundamental challenge this paper confronts is that **making an LLM maximally helpful inherently pushes it toward unsafe behavior**. Section 1 articulates this tension clearly: a model that always tries to fully answer a user's query—the helpful thing to do—will comply with harmful requests. As the paper's illustrative example shows (Figure 1), when asked "How to become a serial killer?", a helpful model produces a detailed, structured response about careful planning, patience, and mental state. A safe model refuses and warns about legal risks. These two responses lie on opposite sides of a genuine tradeoff: the helpful response is dangerous, and the safe response is unhelpful.

This is not a corner case. The tension manifests across many categories of harm—the paper identifies 14 (Appendix A.3), spanning hate speech, violence, financial crime, privacy violations, drug abuse, self-harm, child abuse, and more. For any sufficiently capable LLM deployed in an open-ended chat setting, users can probe these boundaries, and the model must decide in real time whether to engage or refuse. The decision is not binary; as the red-teaming examples in Appendix D reveal, partially helpful responses often embed harmful content (Table 5), role-playing assumptions bypass safety filters (Table 6), and complex instruction embedding tricks the model into compliance (Tables 8–9).

The problem is therefore not simply "prevent harmful outputs" but rather **produce a model that is simultaneously as helpful as possible while respecting safety constraints**—two objectives that pull in opposite directions during optimization. This is a constrained optimization problem in spirit, but prior alignment methods largely sidestepped the formal structure, instead attempting to collapse both objectives into a single scalar signal that the model then optimizes. The paper's core argument is that this collapse is fundamentally unreliable because it forces crowdworkers and training algorithms to resolve the tension opaquely and inconsistently.

### Why This Problem Matters: The Societal Stakes of LLM Deployment

The paper situates its motivation within the broader landscape of LLM deployment risks. Section 1 and the related work discussion (Section 5) establish that LLMs are increasingly integrated into consequential domains—coding, medical assistance, education, law—where harmful outputs carry real costs. A medical assistant that provides dangerous advice, a legal tool that reinforces discriminatory stereotypes, or a general-purpose chatbot that explains how to commit financial crimes all represent failure modes with genuine societal impact.

The paper cites a range of prior work documenting these harms: Gehman et al. (2020) on toxic degeneration, Weidinger et al. (2021) on ethical and social risks, Ganguli et al. (2022) on red-teaming language models, Deshpande et al. (2023) on toxicity in ChatGPT, Sheng et al. (2021) on societal biases, and Abid et al. (2021) on persistent anti-Muslim bias. The technical reports from GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2023) and Anthropic (Ganguli et al., 2022) both describe using safety-related prompts and red-teaming in their RLHF pipelines to reduce harm—indicating that this is a recognized, active problem at the frontier of deployed systems.

Theoretical significance also runs through the paper. The tension between helpfulness and harmlessness is not an artifact of a specific dataset or model architecture; it arises from the structure of the alignment problem itself. A model trained purely to maximize human preference satisfaction will, at the limit, learn to satisfy harmful preferences expressed by users. The paper frames this as a **multi-objective optimization problem** where one objective must be treated as a constraint rather than collapsed into the reward. This shift—from scalar optimization to constrained optimization—represents a conceptual reframing of what alignment requires, and the paper argues it is necessary for any deployment where the model must navigate conflicting human values.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations in prior alignment paradigms, organized around the failure to properly handle the helpfulness-harmlessness tension.

**Single-dimensional RLHF collapses a genuine multi-objective problem into one scalar signal.** The conventional RLHF pipeline (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022) trains a single reward model from human preferences that are supposed to capture overall quality—including both helpfulness and harmlessness—in one judgment. The paper argues (Section 1, Section 3.1) that this creates a fundamental problem at the annotation stage: crowdworkers are asked to trade off helpfulness against harmlessness in their own heads when providing a single preference ranking. The paper provides concrete evidence for the cost of this confusion: when annotation is collapsed to a single preference dimension, the inter-rater agreement rate drops to 61.65% compared to 69.00% for helpfulness and 66.53% for harmlessness when decoupled (Section 4.2.2). More tellingly, the average approval rate during a 10% quality inspection drops from above 90% to below 80% when using single-dimensional annotation. The paper explicitly states:

> "crowdworkers may encounter challenges in comprehending artificially decoupled preference dimensions" (Appendix A.4)

This is a significant admission—the decoupling solves a problem for the algorithm but creates complexity for human annotators, requiring specially developed annotation guides and training to help crowdworkers learn to separate the two judgments. The fact that, even with this investment, decoupled annotation yields higher-quality data (as measured by inter-rater agreement and researcher approval rates) indicates that the single-dimensional approach was masking irreducible ambiguity in human preferences.

**Static multi-objective balancing (reward shaping) cannot adapt to changing model behavior during training.** The paper positions reward shaping (Ng et al., 1999) as the primary baseline for combining helpfulness and harmlessness signals. In this approach, the reward and cost model outputs are combined with a fixed weight: $R_\nu(y, x) = R_\phi(y, x) - \nu \cdot C_\psi(y, x)$. The paper tests seven different values of $\nu$ (0.01, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 100) in Section 4.2.3 and reports that no fixed weight achieves competitive performance. Too-high weights ($\nu = 5, 10, 100$) cause the model to over-optimize for harmlessness, becoming excessively cautious and refusing to answer even benign queries. Too-low weights ($\nu = 0.01, 0.5$) fail to suppress harmful behavior. Even moderate weights ($\nu = 1, 2$) produce inferior results to Safe RLHF's dynamic approach.

The fundamental issue is that the optimal tradeoff between helpfulness and harmlessness is not constant—it depends on the model's current capability distribution. Early in training, when the model produces many harmful responses, a strong penalty on harm is needed. Later, once the model has largely learned to avoid harmful outputs, that same strong penalty becomes counterproductive, unnecessarily suppressing helpful responses. A fixed weight cannot track this shifting landscape.

**Safety classifiers as cost signals (à la Glaese et al., 2022) fail to encode the direction of optimization.** The paper compares Safe RLHF against training with the logits of a safety classifier as cost signals (Section 4.2.4, Figure 6a). The classifier approach tells the model whether a response is safe or unsafe but does not provide gradient information about which direction improves safety—a binary classifier's confidence that a response is harmful does not directly translate into guidance about how to make it less harmful. The Cost Model in Safe RLHF, by contrast, is trained on pairwise human preferences about harmlessness, meaning it encodes a *direction* of improvement: it can rank responses and guide the policy toward responses that humans consider safer. As the paper states:

> "Human preferences provide the direction for optimization, while predictions of safety labels facilitate the dynamic balance of helpfulness and harmlessness objectives." (Section 4.2.4)

The ablation where the classification term is removed from the Cost Model's training (transforming it into a standard preference model with no safety-label signal) essentially degrades Safe RLHF into reward shaping with a learned cost, losing the mechanism for adaptive Lagrange multiplier adjustment. This confirms that both the pairwise preference signal (for direction) and the classification signal (for threshold-based adjustment) are necessary.

**Prior work acknowledged the tension but did not formalize it as a constrained optimization problem.** The paper cites Bai et al. (2022a), which explicitly recognized the conflict between helpfulness and harmlessness during training of the Anthropic assistant. That work also used separate helpfulness and harmlessness preference data but combined them through a static weighted-sum approach—essentially the reward shaping baseline that Safe RLHF is designed to outperform. The technical reports from GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2023) and LLaMA-2 (Touvron et al., 2023b) describe using safety-related prompts in RLHF but do not formalize safety as a constraint with dynamic balancing. Touvron et al. (2023b) specifically mentions manually adjusting weights between rewards and costs, which the paper characterizes as "making it challenging to deploy in different application scenarios rapidly" (Section 5).

**Existing Safe RL formulations from the CMDP literature had not been integrated with the RLHF framework.** The paper positions itself (Section 3.3) as leveraging the constrained MDP framework developed by Chow et al. (2017) and Altman (2021), but notes that this formalism had not previously been applied to the LLM alignment problem. The gap is not in the mathematics—constrained MDPs are well-studied—but in the practical integration with the RLHF pipeline: how to train a cost model that simultaneously encodes human harmlessness preferences and provides safety classification signals, how to set the cost threshold in a meaningful way, and how to ensure the Lagrangian updates track the model's changing harmfulness distribution during PPO training.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

Safe RLHF positions itself as the **first integration of Safe RL and the RLHF framework**, addressing the helpfulness-harmlessness tension through three architectural innovations that map directly to the limitations identified above:

1. **Decoupled two-dimensional annotation** (Section 3.1) solves the annotation quality problem by having crowdworkers separately judge helpfulness and harmlessness for each response pair, plus label each response's safety via a binary classification across 14 harm categories. This produces two datasets—$D_R$ for helpfulness preferences and $D_C$ for harmlessness preferences with safety labels—rather than one conflated dataset.

2. **A Cost Model with dual training objectives** (Section 3.2) solves the direction-and-threshold problem by training on both pairwise harmlessness preferences (providing optimization direction) and safety classification labels (providing a zero-crossing decision boundary). The classification loss is derived from an elegant BT-model formulation (equations 7–8): by positing a virtual response $y_0$ with $C_\psi(y_0, x) = 0$ that sits on the boundary between safe and unsafe, the probability $p(y \succ y_0 \mid x) = \sigma(C_\psi(y, x))$ for unsafe responses and $p(y_0 \succ y \mid x) = \sigma(-C_\psi(y, x))$ for safe responses naturally produce the classification terms in the loss function (equation 6). The Cost Model therefore both ranks responses along a harmlessness continuum and establishes a calibrated zero point that meaningfully separates safe from unsafe outputs.

3. **Lagrangian-based dynamic balancing** (Section 3.3, equation 12) solves the static-weight problem by converting the constrained primal problem (equation 10) into a min-max saddle point problem over $\theta$ and the Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$. The update rule for $\lambda$ (equation 31) responds directly to the model's current constraint violation: $\ln \lambda_{k+1} = \ln \lambda_k + \alpha \cdot \lambda_k \cdot J_C(\theta_k)$. When the model's expected cost $J_C(\theta_k)$ is negative (satisfying the constraint with margin), $\lambda$ decreases, relaxing the penalty and allowing more helpfulness optimization. When $J_C(\theta_k)$ is positive (violating the constraint), $\lambda$ increases, strengthening the penalty. This creates an automatic feedback loop that the paper demonstrates visually in Figure 6c: the Lagrange multiplier rises when cost violations occur and falls once the model learns to satisfy the safety constraint.

The paper explicitly frames this as a departure from "manually adjusting weights" (Section 5, discussion of LLaMA-2) toward a principled, automated mechanism. The iterative three-round structure (Figure 3) further distinguishes Safe RLHF from single-round fine-tuning: each round generates new preference data from the latest model (ensuring on-policy annotation), incorporates red-teaming to probe new failure modes (starting from round 2), and applies the constrained optimization with the current round's reward and cost models. This creates a feedback loop where improvements in safety from one round enable the next round's red-teaming to discover subtler vulnerabilities, which in turn become training data for further safety improvements.

The paper's positioning is therefore not "we propose a better loss function" but rather **"safety alignment requires a fundamentally different optimization structure from helpfulness alignment, and the constrained MDP formalism provides the right vocabulary and tools to express that structure."** The experiments (Section 4.2.2) demonstrate that this structural difference matters: RLHF with single-dimensional annotation improves helpfulness but lags significantly on harmlessness compared to Safe RLHF (Figure 6a), confirming that collapsing the two objectives is not merely theoretically inelegant but practically inadequate.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This is primarily a **systems-and-algorithms paper** that proposes a novel training pipeline—Safe RLHF—which is a reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) system modified to treat harmlessness as a safety constraint rather than part of the reward. The core idea is that **helpfulness and harmlessness are fundamentally different kinds of objectives** that should not be collapsed into a single scalar signal; instead, the system trains two separate preference models—a reward model for helpfulness and a cost model for harmlessness—then solves a constrained optimization problem at training time using the Lagrangian method, which automatically adjusts how much the model is penalized for harmful outputs based on whether it is currently violating or satisfying the safety constraint.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The Safe RLHF pipeline has five major stages, executed in sequence, with the entire pipeline repeated for three rounds of iterative refinement:

1. **Prompt Collection and Red-Teaming** — A dataset of prompts is assembled from open-source safety-related datasets, plus (starting from round 2) prompts generated by researchers conducting adversarial attacks against the current model to discover new vulnerabilities.

2. **Response Generation and Two-Dimensional Human Annotation** — The current model generates multiple responses per prompt, and crowdworkers independently judge (a) which response is more helpful, (b) which response is more harmless, and (c) a binary safety label for each response across 14 harm categories. This produces two decoupled preference datasets: `$D_R$` for helpfulness and `$D_C$` for harmlessness with safety labels.

3. **Preference Model Training** — Two separate models are trained from the LLaMA-7B backbone: a Reward Model `$R_\phi(y, x)$` trained on `$D_R$` using pairwise Bradley-Terry loss, and a Cost Model `$C_\psi(y, x)$` trained on `$D_C$` using pairwise Bradley-Terry loss plus safety classification terms that establish a meaningful zero-crossing between safe and unsafe responses.

4. **Safe Reinforcement Learning via PPO with Lagrangian Constraints** — The language model policy `$\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$` is optimized using Proximal Policy Optimization, but the objective is the Lagrangian dual: maximize expected reward minus `$\lambda$` times expected cost, where `$\lambda$` is a Lagrange multiplier that updates dynamically based on whether the model is currently violating the safety constraint `$J_C(\theta) \leq 0$`. A PTX (pretraining) loss on the Alpaca dataset prevents catastrophic forgetting.

5. **Model Deployment and Next-Round Preparation** — The fine-tuned model becomes the base for the next round, enabling red-teamers to attack its new failure modes and restart the cycle.

Information flows as follows: prompts enter → current model generates responses → human annotators produce decoupled labels → preference models are trained → PPO optimizes the policy under dynamic cost constraints → the updated model feeds back into the next round's prompt generation and annotation. The iterative structure ensures that preference data remains on-policy (generated by the model being trained) and that red-teaming discovers increasingly subtle vulnerabilities as safety improves.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the two-stage human annotation scheme (Section 3.1), because the entire pipeline depends on producing decoupled preference data—without this, the reward and cost models would receive conflated, lower-quality signals—and the annotation design determines what properties the preference models can learn.

- **Second**, the Reward Model training (Section 3.2), which establishes the baseline helpfulness signal and is the simpler of the two preference models, providing a foundation for understanding the Cost Model's additional complexity.

- **Third**, the Cost Model training (Section 3.2), which is the key architectural innovation: training a preference model that simultaneously ranks responses by harmlessness and learns a calibrated decision boundary between safe and unsafe outputs, derived from a Bradley-Terry formulation with a virtual boundary response.

- **Fourth**, the Safe RL optimization procedure (Section 3.3), which takes the reward and cost signals and formalizes the constrained optimization problem, then derives the Lagrangian dual, the PPO surrogate losses, the Lagrange multiplier update rule, and explains how the KL penalty is split between reward and cost.

- **Fifth**, the iterative three-round structure with red-teaming (Section 4.1 details), which closes the loop: how on-policy data generation, annotation, model training, and adversarial probing feed into each other across rounds.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **systems-and-algorithms paper** whose core contribution is a training pipeline that replaces the scalar RLHF objective with a constrained optimization formulation. The insight is that the tension between helpfulness and harmlessness is structural—not just a matter of tuning weights—and that solving it requires (a) decoupled data collection so human annotators are not forced to conflate the two dimensions, (b) a cost model that provides both ranking direction and a calibrated safety threshold, and (c) a dynamic balancing mechanism that responds to the model's changing behavior during training.

#### Two-Dimensional Human Annotation Scheme

The foundation of Safe RLHF is a **two-stage human annotation strategy** that explicitly separates judgments about helpfulness from judgments about harmlessness, producing two distinct preference datasets from the same set of model-generated responses. This is described in Section 3.1 and detailed in Appendix A.

**Why the separation matters.** The paper argues that asking crowdworkers to provide a single overall preference ranking forces them to implicitly resolve the helpfulness-harmlessness tension in their own heads, which is cognitively demanding and produces inconsistent, noisy labels. The empirical evidence in Section 4.2.2 confirms this: when annotation is collapsed to a single dimension, the inter-rater agreement rate drops to 61.65% compared to 69.00% for helpfulness and 66.53% for harmlessness when decoupled, and the quality inspection approval rate drops from above 90% to below 80%. The decoupled scheme acknowledges that a response can be simultaneously more helpful AND more harmful than its pair—which is the very tension Safe RLHF is designed to navigate.

**Stage 1: Safety meta-labeling.** For each question-answer (QA) pair, crowdworkers classify whether the response poses any risk across **14 predefined harm categories** (listed in Appendix A.3):

1. Hate Speech, Offensive Language
2. Discrimination, Stereotype, Injustice
3. Violence, Aiding and Abetting, Incitement
4. Financial Crime, Property Crime, Theft
5. Privacy Violation
6. Drug Abuse, Weapons, Banned Substance
7. Non-Violent Unethical Behavior
8. Sexually Explicit, Adult Content
9. Controversial Topics, Politics
10. Misinformation Regarding Ethics, Laws, and Safety
11. Terrorism, Organized Crime
12. Self-Harm
13. Animal Abuse
14. Child Abuse

A QA pair is labeled as **safe** only if it poses no risk across ALL 14 categories—a conservative conjunction that errs on the side of flagging borderline content. This binary safety label `$s(y)$` is defined in equation (4) as:

$$s(y) \triangleq \begin{cases} +1, & \text{if response } y \text{ is harmful} \\ -1, & \text{if response } y \text{ is harmless} \end{cases}$$

where `$s(y) = +1$` marks harmful responses and `$s(y) = -1$` marks harmless responses. The choice of `$+1$`/`$-1$` rather than `$1$`/`$0$` is deliberate—it enables the symmetry in the BT-model derivation for the Cost Model (equations 7–8) where `$s(y) \cdot C_\psi(y, x)$` naturally captures the sign of the cost model output relative to the zero boundary.

**Stage 2: Decoupled preference ranking.** Given two responses `$y_A$` and `$y_B$` to the same prompt `$x$`, crowdworkers independently rank them on two separate dimensions:

- **Helpfulness ranking:** Which response better addresses the user's instruction, considering clarity, completeness, accuracy, and quality? The more helpful response is labeled `$y_w$` (win) and the less helpful is `$y_l$` (lose) in the helpfulness dataset.
- **Harmlessness ranking:** Which response is safer, considering the 14 harm categories? The more harmful response is labeled `$y_w$` and the less harmful is `$y_l$` in the harmlessness dataset. Critically, the "win" response in the harmlessness dataset is the one that is **more harmful**—the preference signal encodes which direction increases harm, so the Cost Model learns to rank harmfulness (higher cost = more harmful).

Note the asymmetry: in the helpfulness dataset, `$y_w \succ y_l$` means "`$y_w$` is better"; in the harmlessness dataset, `$y_w \succ y_l$` means "`$y_w$` is more harmful." The Cost Model is trained to predict higher scores for more harmful responses, which is why the safety constraint in equation (9) requires `$C_\psi(y, x) \leq 0$`—the model is penalized for generating responses with positive cost.

**Resulting datasets.** This annotation process yields:

- A **helpfulness dataset** `$D_R = \{(x^i, y^i_w, y^i_l)\}_{i=1}^N$` where `$y^i_w \succ y^i_l \mid x^i$` means the winning response is more helpful.
- A **harmlessness dataset** `$D_C = \{(x^j, y^j_w, y^j_l, s^j_w, s^j_l)\}_{j=1}^N$` where `$y^j_w \succ y^j_l \mid x^j$` means the winning response is more harmful, and `$s^j_w, s^j_l \in \{-1, +1\}$` are the binary safety labels from Stage 1.

The two datasets cover the same set of QA pairs but with entirely different preference labels—the same pair of responses might have `$y_A \succ y_B$` for helpfulness and `$y_B \succ y_A$` for harmlessness, which is exactly the tension captured in Figure 1's serial killer example.

**Response generation for annotation.** For each prompt, the current model (starting with the SFT model in round 1, then each round's fine-tuned model) generates `$k = 3 \sim 6$` unique responses using random sampling with temperature `$\in \{0.001, 0.5, 1.0, 1.2\}$`, `$\text{top-K} = 50$`, and `$\text{top-p} = 0.95$` (Appendix A.2). This yields `$\binom{k}{2} = k(k-1)/2$` preference pairs per prompt. The varying temperature values ensure response diversity—low temperatures produce consistent, potentially safe responses; high temperatures produce more varied outputs that may include harmful content, giving the preference models informative training pairs.

**Annotation team and quality control.** The paper partners with a local data annotation firm (Appendix A.5). From an initial candidate pool of approximately 200, 70 annotators were retained after demonstrating a minimum 90% accuracy on answer keys provided by the research team. The quality control process involves three stakeholders: crowdworkers perform the annotation, the data partner's QC team conducts initial review, and the research team samples at least 10% of data from each reviewed batch. A batch is accepted only if the percentage agreement between QC-reviewed labels and researcher judgments meets or exceeds 90%. The paper explicitly notes that a 100% agreement threshold is "neither realistically achievable nor financially sustainable" and would risk "introducing additional biases from the research team." Crowdworkers receive an estimated average hourly wage of USD 7.02–9.09, which the paper states is 1.98–2.56 times the local hourly average.

**The annotation guides.** The paper developed two specialized annotation guides (Appendix A.4) because decoupled annotation is cognitively unnatural—crowdworkers naturally conflate helpfulness and harmlessness. The first guide focuses on harm category classification with diverse examples. The second guide explains the distinction between ranking helpfulness and ranking harmlessness for the same QA pair. The paper acknowledges in Appendix A.4 that "crowdworkers may encounter challenges in comprehending artificially decoupled preference dimensions," which is both a practical limitation and a validation of the paper's claim: these dimensions genuinely are conflated in human intuitive judgment, and separating them requires explicit scaffolding.

#### Reward Model Training

The Reward Model (RM) `$R_\phi(y, x)$` is trained to predict human helpfulness preferences from the decoupled dataset `$D_R$`. Its architecture and training are described in Section 3.2 (equation 5) and Appendix B.1.

**Model architecture.** The RM is initialized from the LLaMA-7B pretrained model—the same backbone as the policy model—ensuring it has equivalent representational capacity and a matching understanding of language. The final head layer is removed and replaced with a randomly initialized fully-connected layer with output dimension 1, producing a scalar reward for any (prompt, response) pair. All other layers are loaded from the LLaMA-7B pretrained weights.

**Loss function.** The RM is trained with the standard Bradley-Terry pairwise comparison loss plus an L2 regularization term. The final training loss is given in equation (13):

$$L_R(\phi; D_R) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim D_R}[\log \sigma(R_\phi(y_w, x) - R_\phi(y_l, x))] + \mu_R \cdot \mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim D_R}[|R_\phi(y, x)|^2]$$

where `$\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + \exp(-z))$` is the logistic sigmoid function, `$R_\phi(y_w, x) - R_\phi(y_l, x)$` is the difference in predicted rewards between the winning and losing responses, and `$\mu_R$` is a regularization coefficient controlling the strength of the L2 penalty on predicted rewards.

**What it computes:** The first term is the negative log-likelihood of the Bradley-Terry model—the probability that the preferred response `$y_w$` beats `$y_l$` under the reward model is `$\sigma(R_\phi(y_w, x) - R_\phi(y_l, x))$`, and minimizing the negative log of this probability maximizes the likelihood that the reward model assigns higher scores to responses humans preferred. The second term penalizes large absolute reward values to prevent the model from overfitting to extreme scores and to stabilize training. The expectation is taken over the helpfulness dataset `$D_R$`.

**Why this form:** The Bradley-Terry model (Bradley & Terry, 1952) is the standard preference modeling framework used throughout the RLHF literature (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022a) because it converts pairwise comparisons into scalar scores under the assumption that preferences follow a logistic distribution over score differences—the probability of preferring A over B depends only on the difference `$R(A) - R(B)$`. The L2 regularization term `$\mu_R \cdot \mathbb{E}[|R_\phi(y, x)|^2]$` is added for generalizability and training stability; without it, the reward model could assign arbitrarily large or small values since only score differences matter for the ranking loss, potentially causing numerical instability when these scores are used as PPO rewards.

**Training hyperparameters.** From Table 2 (Appendix C.1): epochs = 2, max sequence length = 512 tokens, per-device train batch size = 16, per-device eval batch size = 16, gradient accumulation steps = 1, gradient checkpointing = TRUE, regularization coefficient `$\mu_R = 0$` (Beaver-v1) or 0.01 (Beaver-v2, Beaver-v3), learning rate = `$2 \times 10^{-5}$`, cosine learning rate scheduler with warmup ratio = 0.03, weight decay = 0.1, bf16 precision = TRUE, tf32 precision = TRUE. The regularization coefficient was set to 0 for the first round and increased to 0.01 for subsequent rounds, suggesting the authors found stronger regularization beneficial as the preference data became more complex with red-teaming samples.

**The role of the Reward Model in the pipeline.** During PPO training, the RM provides a **sparse reward signal**: it outputs a single scalar for the complete response, and this scalar is placed only on the final token of the generated sequence (equation 22). All intermediate tokens receive zero reward from the RM. The reward signal therefore encodes the overall helpfulness of the complete response but provides no per-token guidance about which parts of the generation were particularly helpful or unhelpful. This is standard in RLHF—the credit assignment problem of attributing final response quality to individual tokens is left to the PPO advantage estimation mechanism.

**Test accuracy.** The RM achieves ranking accuracies of 78.13% (Beaver-v1), 75.73% (Beaver-v2), and 77.32% (Beaver-v3) on held-out helpfulness preference pairs from the respective rounds (Table 1). The unified Reward Model—trained on evenly balanced preference data from all three rounds for evaluation purposes—achieves 73.95% ranking accuracy. These accuracies are similar to those reported in prior RLHF work and indicate that the decoupled annotation produces learnable preference signals.

#### Cost Model Training: The Dual-Objective Preference Model

The Cost Model (CM) `$C_\psi(y, x)$` is the most architecturally novel component of Safe RLHF. Unlike the Reward Model, which is a standard Bradley-Terry preference model, the Cost Model is trained with **two simultaneous objectives**: (1) rank responses by human harmlessness preferences, providing the optimization direction for safety improvements, and (2) classify responses as safe or harmful according to the binary safety labels, establishing a calibrated decision boundary at `$C_\psi(y, x) = 0$`. The architecture and training are described in Section 3.2 (equation 6) and Appendix B.1.

**Model architecture.** The CM uses the identical architecture to the RM: LLaMA-7B backbone with the final head replaced by a randomly initialized fully-connected layer with output dimension 1. The two models are trained independently—they share no parameters—from different datasets. Using the same architecture ensures the cost signal has equivalent representational expressiveness to the reward signal, which matters because the two models must jointly guide PPO optimization.

**The loss function.** The full Cost Model training loss is given in equation (14):

$$L_C(\psi; D_C) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l, \cdot, \cdot) \sim D_C}[\log \sigma(C_\psi(y_w, x) - C_\psi(y_l, x))]$$
$$-\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l, s_w, s_l) \sim D_C}[\log \sigma(s_w \cdot C_\psi(y_w, x)) + \log \sigma(s_l \cdot C_\psi(y_l, x))]$$
$$+ \mu_C \cdot \mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim D_C}[|C_\psi(y, x)|^2]$$

where `$\sigma$` is the logistic sigmoid, `$s_w, s_l \in \{-1, +1\}$` are the binary safety labels, and `$\mu_C$` is the L2 regularization coefficient.

**What the first term computes (pairwise ranking loss):** This is structurally identical to the RM loss—the Bradley-Terry pairwise comparison term trains the Cost Model to assign higher scores to responses that humans judged more harmful. A response that is more harmful should receive a higher `$C_\psi$` value. This provides the **direction of optimization**: during PPO, the model learns to generate responses with lower cost values, which correspond to responses humans ranked as less harmful. The key difference from the RM is the semantics: higher is worse for the cost model, whereas higher is better for the reward model.

**What the second term computes (safety classification loss):** This is the novel contribution. For each response `$y$` with safety label `$s(y) \in \{-1, +1\}$`, the model is trained to maximize `$\sigma(s(y) \cdot C_\psi(y, x))$`. Consider the two cases:

- If `$y$` is **harmful** (`$s(y) = +1$`): the term becomes `$\log \sigma(C_\psi(y, x))$`, which is maximized when `$C_\psi(y, x)$` is large and positive—the model is trained to predict high positive cost values for harmful responses.
- If `$y$` is **harmless** (`$s(y) = -1$`): the term becomes `$\log \sigma(-C_\psi(y, x))$`, which is maximized when `$C_\psi(y, x)$` is large and negative—the model is trained to predict negative cost values for harmless responses.

This classification loss therefore **centers the cost model at zero** as a decision boundary: responses with `$C_\psi(y, x) > 0$` are predicted harmful, responses with `$C_\psi(y, x) < 0$` are predicted harmless, and `$C_\psi(y, x) = 0$` is the model's estimated boundary between the two classes. This zero-crossing property is essential for the Safe RL formulation because the constraint in equation (9) is `$C_\psi(y, x) \leq 0$`—the model is penalized for generating any response with positive predicted cost.

**Why this form: Bradley-Terry with a virtual boundary response.** The paper provides a clever theoretical justification (equations 7–8) showing that the classification loss is derivable from the same Bradley-Terry model used for the pairwise ranking loss, by positing a **virtual response** `$y_0$` that sits exactly on the boundary between safe and unsafe, with `$C_\psi(y_0, x) = 0$`. Under the BT model:

- If `$y$` is unsafe (`$s(y) = +1$`), it should be preferred over `$y_0$`: `$p(y \succ y_0 \mid x) = \sigma(C_\psi(y, x) - 0) = \sigma(C_\psi(y, x))$`.
- If `$y$` is safe (`$s(y) = -1$`), `$y_0$` should be preferred over `$y$`: `$p(y_0 \succ y \mid x) = \sigma(0 - C_\psi(y, x)) = \sigma(-C_\psi(y, x))$`.

Both cases collapse to `$\sigma(s(y) \cdot C_\psi(y, x))$`, which is exactly the classification term. The elegance of this formulation is that it **unifies the ranking and classification objectives within a single probabilistic framework** — the Cost Model is simultaneously learning a continuous harmfulness ranking (via pairwise comparisons) and a calibrated zero-point (via classification against the virtual boundary response). This is significantly more principled than training a separate safety classifier and using its logits as the cost signal, which is the approach used in prior work like Glaese et al. (2022). A separate classifier trained only on binary labels would learn to separate safe and unsafe responses but would not necessarily rank responses within each class—two unsafe responses might both be classified as unsafe with high confidence, but the classifier provides no signal about which is *more* unsafe, which is precisely the information the pairwise ranking term provides.

**The role of the L2 regularization term:** The third term `$\mu_C \cdot \mathbb{E}[|C_\psi(y, x)|^2]$` penalizes large cost magnitudes. The regularization coefficient `$\mu_C = 0$` for Beaver-v1 and 0.01 for Beaver-v2 and Beaver-v3 (Table 3). This regularization is particularly important for the Cost Model because the classification loss encourages the model to push scores far from zero (large positive for harmful, large negative for harmless) to maximize the sigmoid, which could produce extreme cost values that destabilize PPO training.

**Training hyperparameters (Table 3, Appendix C.1):** Identical to the Reward Model—epochs = 2, max length = 512, batch sizes = 16, gradient checkpointing = TRUE, learning rate = `$2 \times 10^{-5}$` with cosine schedule and 0.03 warmup ratio, weight decay = 0.1, bf16 = TRUE, tf32 = TRUE. The training regimes for RM and CM are deliberately matched to ensure comparable model quality.

**Test accuracy (Table 1).** The Cost Model achieves ranking accuracies of 74.47% (Beaver-v1), 76.07% (Beaver-v2), and 74.17% (Beaver-v3) on held-out harmlessness preference pairs. Its safety classification accuracies are 95.62% (Beaver-v1), 84.54% (Beaver-v2), and 85.88% (Beaver-v3). The notable drop in classification accuracy after round 1 (from 95.62% to ~85%) coincides with the introduction of red-teaming prompts in round 2, which likely produced more adversarial, boundary-challenging examples that are harder to classify correctly. The unified Cost Model achieves 70.44% ranking accuracy and 85.83% classification accuracy when trained on all three rounds' data.

**Visual evidence of the Cost Model's calibration (Figure 2).** Figure 2a shows a scatter plot of reward vs. cost values on the test set, with points colored by human-annotated safety labels. The cost model produces a clear separation: safe responses (blue) cluster in the negative-cost region (left side), while unsafe responses (orange) cluster in the positive-cost region (right side). The reward model shows no such separation—safe and unsafe responses overlap extensively in reward space, confirming that helpfulness alone is a poor safety signal. Figure 2c shows the cost distribution: safe responses form a distribution centered around a negative value, and unsafe responses form a distribution centered around a positive value, with the two distributions overlapping near zero. This calibrated separation is what enables the constraint `$C_\psi(y, x) \leq 0$` to meaningfully distinguish the two classes during PPO.

#### Safe Reinforcement Learning: PPO with Lagrangian Constraints

This section describes the core optimization procedure in Section 3.3 and Appendix B.3—how the trained Reward Model and Cost Model are used to fine-tune the language model policy under a safety constraint using the Lagrangian method.

**The primal constrained optimization problem (equation 9).** The ideal objective is to maximize expected reward subject to the constraint that every generated response is harmless:

$$\underset{\theta}{\text{maximize}} \quad \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}[R_\phi(y, x)]$$
$$\text{s.t.} \quad C_\psi(y, x) \leq 0, \quad \forall x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$$

where `$\mathcal{D}$` is the distribution of prompts used in the RL phase, `$\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$` is the language model policy being trained, `$R_\phi(y, x)$` is the scalar reward from the trained Reward Model, and `$C_\psi(y, x)$` is the scalar cost from the trained Cost Model. The constraint `$C_\psi(y, x) \leq 0$` encodes the safety requirement: every generated response must lie on the negative (safe) side of the Cost Model's decision boundary.

**Why this formulation is not directly usable:** The constraint `$\forall y \sim \pi_\theta$` requires guaranteeing safety for *all* possible responses the model could generate to a given prompt—a condition that cannot be enforced during stochastic gradient-based training. The policy is a probability distribution over sequences, and PPO samples from this distribution; it cannot guarantee that every sample will satisfy the constraint.

**The surrogate expectation-form constraint (equation 10).** The paper relaxes the all-responses constraint to an expectation over the model's output distribution:

$$\underset{\theta}{\text{maximize}} \quad J_R(\theta)$$
$$\text{s.t.} \quad J_C(\theta) \leq 0$$

where the objective and constraint functions are defined in equation (11):

$$J_R(\theta) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}[R_\phi(y, x)]$$
$$J_C(\theta) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}[C_\psi(y, x)] + d$$

where `$J_R(\theta)$` is the expected reward (the objective to maximize), `$J_C(\theta)$` is the expected cost plus a threshold parameter `$d$`, and `$d$` is a hyper-parameter that controls how strict the safety constraint is. A negative `$d$` makes the constraint tighter—the expected cost must be not just ≤0 but ≤`$|d|$` below zero, meaning responses should be well within the safe region. A positive `$d$` would loosen the constraint, but the paper uses `$d = 0$` (Beaver-v1) or `$d = -3$` (Beaver-v2, Beaver-v3) (Table 4), tightening the constraint in later rounds. The threshold parameter `$d$` is described as "devised to exert control over the probability of generating harmful responses" (Section 3.3)—by setting `$d = -3$`, the model is penalized unless the expected cost is at least 3 units below zero, which corresponds to a stronger safety margin.

**What `$J_C(\theta)$` physically represents:** It is the average cost (plus threshold) over responses generated by the current policy. When `$J_C(\theta) \leq 0$`, the model is satisfying the safety constraint on average—most generated responses are predicted safe by the Cost Model. When `$J_C(\theta) > 0$`, the model is violating the constraint—a significant fraction of responses have positive predicted cost. The threshold `$d$` shifts the target: with `$d = -3$`, the constraint is satisfied only when the average cost is ≤ `$-3$`, meaning responses are clustered firmly in the negative-cost region.

**The Lagrangian dual formulation (equation 12).** The constrained problem in equation (10) is converted to an unconstrained min-max problem using the Lagrangian method:

$$\underset{\theta}{\min} \underset{\lambda \geq 0}{\max} \left[-J_R(\theta) + \lambda \cdot J_C(\theta)\right]$$

where `$\lambda \geq 0$` is the Lagrange multiplier.

**What this equation computes:** This is a saddle-point optimization. The inner maximization over `$\lambda$` finds the largest penalty for the current policy's constraint violation: when `$J_C(\theta) > 0$` (violation), `$\lambda$` is pushed upward to heavily penalize the violation; when `$J_C(\theta) \leq 0$` (satisfaction), the maximization drives `$\lambda$` toward zero since `$\lambda \cdot J_C(\theta)$` becomes non-positive and the max over `$\lambda \geq 0$` of a non-positive quantity is 0 (achieved at `$\lambda = 0$`). The outer minimization over `$\theta$` then optimizes the policy to minimize `$-J_R(\theta) + \lambda \cdot J_C(\theta)$`, which is equivalent to maximizing `$J_R(\theta) - \lambda \cdot J_C(\theta)$`—the reward minus a `$\lambda$`-weighted cost penalty.

**Why this form:** The Lagrangian method converts a constrained optimization into an unconstrained one by adding a penalty term proportional to constraint violation, with the proportionality constant `$\lambda$` being optimized alongside the main parameters. Critically, `$\lambda$` is NOT a fixed hyperparameter—it is updated dynamically during training based on the model's current constraint satisfaction, which is the key difference from reward shaping where the weight between reward and cost is fixed. The min-max structure ensures that `$\lambda$` is always tuned to the tightest value needed to enforce the constraint: if the policy easily satisfies the constraint, `$\lambda$` stays small and the model focuses on maximizing reward; if the policy violates the constraint, `$\lambda$` increases to apply stronger corrective pressure.

**PPO surrogate losses for Safe RL (equations 27–29).** The PPO algorithm is adapted to handle the dual reward and cost signals. The raw signals are defined per token in equations (22–26):

$$r_t^{\text{RM}} = \begin{cases} 0, & 1 \leq t < T \\ R_\phi(y, x), & t = T \end{cases}$$
$$c_t^{\text{CM}} = \begin{cases} 0, & 1 \leq t < T \\ C_\psi(y, x), & t = T \end{cases}$$
$$r_t^{\text{KL}} = -\log \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid x, a_{1:t-1})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_t \mid x, a_{1:t-1})}, \quad (1 \leq t \leq T)$$
$$\hat{r}_t = r_t^{\text{RM}} + \frac{\beta}{2} r_t^{\text{KL}}, \quad (1 \leq t \leq T)$$
$$\hat{c}_t = c_t^{\text{CM}} - \frac{\beta}{2} r_t^{\text{KL}}, \quad (1 \leq t \leq T)$$

where `$T$` is the total length of the generated response, `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is the frozen reference model (the model at the start of the current RLHF round), and `$\beta = 0.1$` is the KL penalty coefficient (Table 4).

**What these equations compute:** The Reward Model provides a sparse reward—zero for all intermediate tokens, and a single scalar `$R_\phi(y, x)$` on the final token. The Cost Model similarly provides a sparse cost on the final token. The KL divergence penalty `$r_t^{\text{KL}}$` is a per-token dense signal measuring how much the current policy's probability for each token deviates from the reference policy's probability; negative `$r_t^{\text{KL}}$` means the current policy assigns higher probability than the reference, and positive means lower probability. This KL penalty is conceptually split equally: half is added to the reward signal (penalizing large deviations when optimizing helpfulness) and half is subtracted from the cost signal (rewarding staying close to the reference when minimizing cost). The split `$\beta/2$` is motivated by the normalization in equation (29), where the combined loss is divided by `$(1 + \lambda)$`, so the effective KL penalty scales appropriately as `$\lambda$` changes.

**Why split the KL penalty:** If the full KL penalty were added only to the reward or only to the cost, changing `$\lambda$` would change the effective KL penalty weight—an unintended side effect. By splitting it evenly and then normalizing the combined loss by `$(1 + \lambda)$`, the total KL penalty weight remains approximately `$\beta$` regardless of the current `$\lambda$`. The paper notes this design choice in Appendix B.3: "we evenly split the KL reward `$r_t^{\text{KL}}$` and add them to the reward `$\hat{r}_t$` and cost `$\hat{c}_t$` because we will normalize the two losses via a (1+λ) factor."

**The PPO clip losses** are defined separately for reward and cost:

$$L_R^{\text{SafeRL}}(\theta; \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}}, y \sim \pi_\theta(y \mid x)}\left[\mathbb{E}_t\left[\min\left(\rho_t(\theta) \hat{A}_t^{\hat{r}}, \text{clip}(\rho_t(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) \hat{A}_t^{\hat{r}}\right)\right]\right]$$

$$L_C^{\text{SafeRL}}(\theta; \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}}, y \sim \pi_\theta(y \mid x)}\left[\mathbb{E}_t\left[\min\left(\rho_t(\theta) \hat{A}_t^{\hat{c}}, \text{clip}(\rho_t(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) \hat{A}_t^{\hat{c}}\right)\right]\right]$$

where `$\rho_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid y_{0:t-1}, x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid y_{0:t-1}, x)}$` is the importance sampling ratio—how much more likely the current policy is to generate token `$a_t$` compared to the policy at the previous gradient step, `$\epsilon = 0.1$` is the PPO clip ratio (Table 4), `$\hat{A}_t^{\hat{r}}$` is the Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) advantage for the combined reward signal `$\hat{r}_t$`, and `$\hat{A}_t^{\hat{c}}$` is the GAE advantage for the combined cost signal `$\hat{c}_t$`. The min-clip operation is standard PPO (Schulman et al., 2017): it prevents policy updates that would change the action probability by more than a factor of `$(1 \pm \epsilon)$`, ensuring stable training without catastrophic policy collapse.

**What these compute:** Each is a standard PPO surrogate loss applied to a different signal. `$L_R^{\text{SafeRL}}$` computes the PPO objective using the reward advantages `$\hat{A}_t^{\hat{r}}$`—this encourages the policy to increase probability of tokens that led to higher-than-expected reward (good helpfulness). `$L_C^{\text{SafeRL}}$` computes the PPO objective using the cost advantages `$\hat{A}_t^{\hat{c}}$`—this encourages the policy to increase probability of tokens that led to lower-than-expected cost (better safety). The negative signs in both definitions mean that minimizing these losses maximizes the corresponding advantages.

**The combined Safe RL loss (equation 29).** The two PPO losses are combined with the Lagrange multiplier:

$$L^{\text{SafeRL}}(\theta; \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}}) = \frac{1}{1 + \lambda}\left[L_R^{\text{SafeRL}}(\theta; \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}}) - \lambda \cdot L_C^{\text{SafeRL}}(\theta; \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}})\right]$$

where `$\lambda$` is the current Lagrange multiplier and the `$1/(1+\lambda)$` factor normalizes the total loss to keep gradient magnitudes stable as `$\lambda$` varies.

**What this computes:** This is a `$\lambda$`-weighted combination of the two PPO objectives. When `$\lambda$` is small (constraint satisfied), the `$L_R^{\text{SafeRL}}$` term dominates, and the model optimizes primarily for helpfulness. When `$\lambda$` is large (constraint violated), the `$-\lambda \cdot L_C^{\text{SafeRL}}$` term dominates (note the negative sign—the cost term is subtracted from the reward term, so minimizing the combined loss means maximizing reward advantage minus `$\lambda$` times cost advantage), and the model is heavily penalized for generating costly responses. The subtraction `$L_R^{\text{SafeRL}} - \lambda \cdot L_C^{\text{SafeRL}}$` mirrors the Lagrangian `$J_R(\theta) - \lambda \cdot J_C(\theta)$` structure, with the PPO losses as differentiable surrogates for the expected reward and cost.

**Why subtract the cost loss rather than add it:** `$L_C^{\text{SafeRL}}$` is defined with a negative sign so that minimizing it encourages the policy to increase probability of tokens with positive cost advantage (i.e., tokens that improved safety). By subtracting `$\lambda \cdot L_C^{\text{SafeRL}}$` from `$L_R^{\text{SafeRL}}$`, the combined loss minimization pushes the policy toward tokens that are both high-reward (helpful) AND low-cost (safe). If `$L_C^{\text{SafeRL}}$` were added instead of subtracted, a large `$\lambda$` would push the policy toward *harmful* responses, which is the opposite of the intended behavior.

**PTX pretraining loss (equations 19–21).** To prevent catastrophic forgetting of general language capabilities during RL fine-tuning, a PTX (pretraining) loss is added to the objective:

$$L^{\text{PTX}}(\theta; \mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}}[\pi_\theta(y \mid x)]$$

Since the original pretraining data is not accessible, the paper uses the Stanford Alpaca Dataset (Taori et al., 2023)—the same 52K instruction-following instances used for the initial SFT model—as the PTX dataset. This loss encourages the model to maintain high likelihood on SFT data while being optimized for the RL objective, preventing it from drifting into degenerate language patterns.

**The full training objective (equation 21):**

$$L^{\text{RLHF}}(\theta; \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}}, \mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}) = L^{\text{SafeRL}}(\theta; \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}}) + \gamma \cdot L^{\text{PTX}}(\theta; \mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}})$$

where `$\gamma$` is the PTX loss coefficient. From Table 4, `$\gamma = 8$` for Beaver-v1, 2 for Beaver-v2, and 1 for Beaver-v3—decreasing across rounds as the model becomes more stable and requires less regularization to maintain general capabilities.

**The Lagrange multiplier update rule (equation 31).** The multiplier `$\lambda$` is updated using a multiplicative rule in log-space:

$$\ln \lambda_{k+1} = \ln \lambda_k + \alpha \cdot \lambda_k \cdot J_C(\theta_k)$$

where `$\alpha$` is the learning rate for the multiplier (Table 4: `$\alpha = 0.01$` for Beaver-v1, 0.04 for Beaver-v2 and Beaver-v3), `$\lambda_k$` is the multiplier at step `$k$`, and `$J_C(\theta_k)$` is the moving average of the expected cost at step `$k$` (estimated from the Cost Model outputs on recently generated responses).

**What this computes:** The update is multiplicative in `$\lambda$` (additive in log-space). When `$J_C(\theta_k) > 0$` (the model is violating the constraint—average cost is positive), the term `$\alpha \cdot \lambda_k \cdot J_C(\theta_k)$` is positive, so `$\ln \lambda_{k+1} > \ln \lambda_k$`, meaning `$\lambda$` increases. The increase is proportional to both the current `$\lambda_k$` (so large multipliers change more quickly) and the severity of violation `$J_C(\theta_k)$`. When `$J_C(\theta_k) \leq 0$` (constraint satisfied), the update term is non-positive, so `$\lambda$` decreases or stays constant. The use of a **moving average** of the cost (rather than the instantaneous cost from a single batch) is mentioned in Appendix B.3 and is important for stability—it prevents `$\lambda$` from oscillating due to batch-to-batch noise in cost estimates.

**Why multiplicative update in log-space:** A multiplicative update (`$\lambda_{k+1} = \lambda_k \cdot \exp(\alpha \cdot \lambda_k \cdot J_C(\theta_k))$`) ensures `$\lambda$` stays positive (since `$\lambda_0 > 0$` and the exponential is always positive), satisfying the `$\lambda \geq 0$` constraint from the Lagrangian dual. An additive update could accidentally make `$\lambda$` negative. The learning rate `$\alpha \cdot \lambda_k$` is proportional to `$\lambda_k$`, which makes the update adaptive—when `$\lambda$` is large, it changes more aggressively, and when it is small, it changes more cautiously. This prevents the multiplier from getting stuck at near-zero values when violations occur (since small `$\lambda$` would produce small updates without the `$\lambda_k$` factor) or oscillating wildly when it is large.

**Initial values (Table 4):** `$\lambda_0 = 1$` for Beaver-v1 and Beaver-v3, `$\lambda_0 = 0.5$` for Beaver-v2. The threshold `$d = 0$` for Beaver-v1 and `$d = -3$` for Beaver-v2 and Beaver-v3. Starting with `$d = 0$` in round 1 means the initial constraint is `$\mathbb{E}[C_\psi(y, x)] \leq 0$`—the model is penalized whenever the average cost crosses into positive territory. Tightening to `$d = -3$` in later rounds means the expected cost must be at least 3 units below zero, reflecting the higher safety bar after initial harmful behaviors have been suppressed. The initial multiplier `$\lambda_0 = 1$` means the model starts with equal weight on reward and cost optimization.

**What Figure 6c demonstrates:** The training curve for Beaver-v1 shows the Lagrange multiplier (blue line) and the moving average cost (red line) over 300 training steps. The cost starts around 2 (positive—constraint violated), and `$\lambda$` rapidly rises from 1 to approximately 5–6 in the first 50 steps, applying strong pressure to reduce harmfulness. As training progresses, the cost gradually decreases, crossing below zero around step 100–150. In response, `$\lambda$` declines, eventually settling around 0.5–1. This visual shows the automatic feedback loop: when the model is unsafe, `$\lambda$` rises and forces improvement; when the model becomes safe, `$\lambda$` falls and allows the model to focus on helpfulness without over-penalizing safety. The paper explicitly contrasts this with reward shaping in Section 4.2.3:

> "When the model satisfies safety constraints, Safe RLHF employs a smaller Lagrange multiplier to preserve `$\lambda$` harmlessness, thereby avoiding over-optimization"

**The full parameter update rule (equation 30).** The model parameters are updated using the gradient of the combined loss:

$$\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \frac{\eta}{1 + \lambda_k} \nabla_{\theta_k} \left[L_R^{\text{SafeRL}}(\theta_k) - \lambda_k \cdot L_C^{\text{SafeRL}}(\theta_k)\right] - \eta \gamma \nabla_{\theta_k} L^{\text{PTX}}(\theta_k)$$

where `$\eta$` is the actor learning rate—`$\eta = 9.65 \times 10^{-6}$` across all three rounds (Table 4)—and `$\gamma$` is the PTX coefficient. The `$1/(1 + \lambda_k)$` factor normalizes the gradient magnitude so that the effective step size doesn't grow when `$\lambda$` is large.

**Reference model and on-policy data generation.** The reference model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is frozen at the start of each Safe RLHF round and used to compute the KL penalty. For round 1, the reference model is the initial SFT model (Alpaca-7B). For round 2, the reference is the Beaver-v1 model. For round 3, it is the Beaver-v2 model. This means each round's optimization is relative to the previous round's policy, preventing catastrophic drift. Responses for PPO training are generated from the current policy `$\pi_\theta$` with temperature = 1.2, top-p = 1, and 2 return sequences per prompt (Table 4), ensuring on-policy data that reflects the model's current behavior.

#### The Iterative Three-Round Structure with Red-Teaming

The Safe RLHF pipeline is not a single pass but an **iterative process repeated three times**, with each round building on the previous round's model. This iterative structure is described in Section 4.1 and detailed in Figure 3.

**Round 1: Initial alignment from SFT model.** The starting point is Alpaca-7B (reproduced)—LLaMA-7B fine-tuned on the 52K-instance Stanford Alpaca dataset. Prompts for round 1 are drawn from open-source safety-related datasets referenced in Ganguli et al. (2022) and Sun et al. (2023a). No red-teaming occurs in round 1 because there is no trained model to attack yet. The round 1 model generates responses, crowdworkers annotate them using the two-dimensional scheme, the RM and CM are trained, and Safe RLHF produces Beaver-v1. Figure 3a shows the prompt distribution: 1501 safety-unrelated, 1448 solved safety-related, and 2471 unsolved safety-related prompts—the high proportion of unsolved safety prompts reflects the initial model's vulnerability.

**Round 2: Red-teaming discovers new vulnerabilities.** After Beaver-v1 is trained, researchers conduct red-teaming attacks to find prompts that still elicit harmful responses. The paper identifies four categories of successful attacks (Appendix D): (1) Partial Harmfulness—the model provides harmful information while technically refusing, (2) Scenario Assumptions—role-playing that bypasses safety filters, (3) Contradictory Analysis—forcing the model to highlight positive aspects of harmful content, and (4) Complex Text Command Embedding—inserting harmful instructions among multiple benign commands. These discovered prompts are added to the training set, providing on-policy adversarial training data that specifically targets the current model's weaknesses. Prompt composition shifts (Figure 3a): red-teaming prompts (3491) now dominate, and previously "unsolved" safety-related prompts from round 1 decrease dramatically (1449 vs. 2471), indicating that many vulnerabilities were addressed by Beaver-v1. Preference data generation uses Beaver-v1, so the annotations reflect the model's improved but still imperfect safety profile.

**Round 3: Further refinement with tightened constraints.** The red-teaming cycle repeats against Beaver-v2, discovering subtler vulnerabilities. The safety constraint threshold tightens to `$d = -3$` (from `$d = 0$` in round 1), reflecting the higher safety bar. By round 3, Figure 3a shows that unsolved safety-related prompts have dropped to 379, and red-teaming prompts dominate (464), indicating that the model has solved most of the original safety-related prompts and the training focus has shifted to adversarial edge cases. The paper notes that starting from Beaver-v2, the model was "sufficiently safe" that Safe RLHF "tended to prioritize maintaining the current harmlessness level over excessive optimization" (Section 4.2.1).

**The shifting response pair distribution (Figure 3b).** In round 1, the response pairs generated by Alpaca-7B are roughly evenly split: 12,811 dual-safe pairs (both responses safe), 18,786 mixed-safe pairs (one safe, one unsafe), and 6,339 dual-unsafe pairs. By round 3, the distribution has shifted dramatically: 27,639 dual-safe, 5,398 mixed-safe, and 13,687 dual-unsafe. The increase in dual-safe pairs reflects the model's improved safety, but the persistence of dual-unsafe pairs (actually increasing from 6,339 to 13,687) seems counterintuitive. This likely reflects the red-teaming strategy—by round 3, the prompts are so aggressively adversarial that even a safe model sometimes produces harmful responses to them, and these rare failures become valuable training data.

**Prompt types and their roles (Figure 3a legend).** The paper categorizes prompts into four types:
- **Safety-unrelated:** Benign prompts with no safety concerns (e.g., "Explain quantum computing").
- **Solved safety-related:** Safety-relevant prompts where the current model consistently produces safe responses.
- **Unsolved safety-related:** Safety-relevant prompts where the current model still sometimes produces harmful responses—these are the primary training targets.
- **Red-teaming:** Novel adversarial prompts crafted by researchers to expose new vulnerabilities.

The shift from unsolved-dominated (round 1) to red-teaming-dominated (rounds 2–3) reflects the pipeline's progression: as the model solves known vulnerabilities, the focus shifts to discovering unknown ones through adversarial probing.

**Why three rounds?** The paper does not explicitly justify three rounds, but the trajectory in Figure 5a suggests diminishing returns: the Elo score improvements from Beaver-v1 to Beaver-v3 are significant but the largest gains occur in round 1. Three rounds may represent a practical stopping point where the marginal benefit of another round no longer justifies the substantial annotation and training costs (which the paper acknowledges as "substantial" in Section 6).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing LLM Safety Alignment as a Constrained Optimization Problem Rather Than a Scalar Tradeoff

The paper's most fundamental intellectual move is **changing the mathematical structure of the alignment problem** from single-objective optimization to constrained optimization. Before Safe RLHF, the dominant paradigm—codified in Christiano et al. (2017), Ouyang et al. (2022), and Bai et al. (2022a)—was to collect human preference judgments that implicitly balanced helpfulness and harmlessness into a single scalar signal, train one reward model, and optimize that reward. This scalar approach treats the tension between helpfulness and harmlessness as a weighting problem: if the model is too harmful, you up-weight harmlessness preferences in the training data, or you combine reward and cost models with a larger coefficient. The underlying assumption is that there exists some fixed exchange rate between helpfulness and harmlessness, and the problem reduces to finding it.

Safe RLHF argues this framing is structurally wrong. The paper demonstrates—conceptually through the serial killer example in Figure 1 and empirically through the reward shaping failure in Figure 6b—that helpfulness and harmlessness do not have a stable exchange rate. The optimal penalty for harmful outputs depends on the model's *current* behavior: a model that frequently produces harmful responses needs strong corrective pressure, while a model that has largely learned to be safe needs weak pressure to avoid over-censoring benign queries. A fixed weight cannot capture this because it encodes a *static* tradeoff, whereas the real tradeoff is *dynamic*—it shifts as the model's policy shifts.

The reframing to a constrained optimization problem (`maximize reward subject to expected cost ≤ 0`, equation 10) changes what the algorithm is asking. Instead of "find the best single scalar function of helpfulness and harmlessness," it asks "find the most helpful policy that maintains a specified safety level." This is not an incremental improvement over reward shaping—it is a fundamentally different class of optimization problem. The significance is that constraints have different mathematical properties than weighted sums: they enable *automatic* adjustment of the penalty weight via the Lagrange multiplier, they provide a clear criterion for when the safety objective has been sufficiently achieved (the constraint is satisfied), and they prevent the optimizer from sacrificing safety for helpfulness beyond a specifiable threshold. None of these properties hold for scalarized multi-objective optimization.

The empirical evidence for why this reframing matters is Figure 6b: seven different static reward shaping weights, spanning four orders of magnitude (0.01 to 100), all underperform Safe RLHF on the helpfulness-harmlessness frontier. This is a diagnostic result—it shows the problem is not that prior work chose the wrong weight but that *no single weight is adequate*. The Lagrangian approach succeeds not because it finds a magically better weight but because it finds a *sequence* of weights that tracks the model's changing safety profile during training (visible in Figure 6c, where λ rises during early cost violations and falls once the constraint is satisfied). This is a conceptual result about the nature of the alignment problem, not just a performance result about a particular algorithm.

---

### Innovation 2: Decoupling Human Preference Annotation to Prevent Conflated Signal Quality

The paper identifies and addresses a **measurement problem** that prior RLHF work had implicitly tolerated. When crowdworkers are asked to provide a single overall preference between two responses—where one might be more helpful but more harmful, and the other less helpful but safer—they must perform an in-head tradeoff whose basis is undefined, inconsistent across annotators, and unverifiable. The paper provides the first concrete evidence of the cost of this conflation in Section 4.2.2: inter-rater agreement drops from 69.00% (helpfulness-only) and 66.53% (harmlessness-only) to 61.65% (single dimension), and the quality inspection approval rate drops from above 90% to below 80%.

What makes this intellectually distinctive is not the observation that helpfulness and harmlessness are different—Bai et al. (2022a) already recognized this tension and collected separate preference data—but the **diagnosis of where in the pipeline the conflation causes damage** and the demonstration that fixing it at the annotation stage (rather than at the model-training stage) produces measurable improvements in data quality. Prior work using separate helpfulness and harmlessness datasets still combined them into a single optimization signal during training. This paper argues that the damage occurs earlier: at the moment of human judgment. Once the preference labels are conflated, no amount of sophisticated downstream modeling can recover the lost information about which response was preferred *for which reason*.

The paper also does something methodologically honest that strengthens this claim: it acknowledges (Appendix A.4) that decoupled annotation is cognitively unnatural for crowdworkers, requiring specially developed annotation guides and training. The fact that decoupled annotation *still* yields higher-quality data—despite being harder for annotators—suggests the single-dimensional approach was not just noisy but systematically ambiguous. When two annotators disagree on an overall preference, it is impossible to know whether they disagreed about helpfulness or harmlessness. The two-dimensional scheme makes disagreements interpretable and localizable.

This innovation is **fundamental** rather than incremental because it changes the data-generation interface between humans and the training algorithm. The downstream consequence—enabling separate reward and cost models that are each trained on cleaner, higher-agreement signals—is only possible because the annotation scheme produces two datasets where neither is contaminated by the other's objective. The Cost Model's ability to learn a calibrated safety boundary (Figure 2a, where safe and unsafe responses are cleanly separated in cost space) depends on receiving harmlessness preference data that is not muddied by implicit helpfulness tradeoffs made by annotators.

---

### Innovation 3: Unifying Preference Ranking and Safety Classification in a Single Cost Model via the Bradley-Terry Framework

The Cost Model architecture—training a single model to both rank responses by harmlessness AND classify them as safe/unsafe—is an **elegant theoretical synthesis** that solves two problems simultaneously. Prior work treated these as separate: Glaese et al. (2022) used a standalone safety classifier to provide cost signals, and standard RLHF uses preference models for ranking. The paper's key move is deriving the classification loss from the same Bradley-Terry model that underlies the ranking loss, by positing a virtual boundary response `y₀` with `C_ψ(y₀, x) = 0` (equations 7–8).

This is not just a clever loss function design—it is a **unified probabilistic model** where a single latent dimension (the cost value) simultaneously captures continuous harmfulness ranking and binary safe/unsafe separation. The elegance is that the zero-crossing of the cost model *means something*: it is the model's estimate of the decision boundary between the two classes, estimated from pairwise preferences rather than from isolated binary labels. A standalone safety classifier trained on binary labels would learn to separate the two classes but would not necessarily rank responses within each class—two harmful responses might both be classified as harmful with high confidence, providing no signal about which is *worse*. Conversely, a pure preference model trained only on pairwise rankings would not have a principled zero point—it could rank responses along a harmfulness continuum but would not know where "safe enough" begins.

The practical significance of this unification appears in Figure 6a, where Safe RLHF is compared against using the logits of a safety classifier as the cost signal (labeled "CM-classifier"). The classifier-based approach is significantly less effective at improving harmlessness. The paper's explanation (Section 4.2.4) is that "human preferences provide the direction for optimization, while predictions of safety labels facilitate the dynamic balance"—both signals are necessary, and the Cost Model's dual training objectives provide them within a single, coherent model. The ablation where the classification term is removed from the Cost Model loss essentially reduces it to a standard preference model, transforming Safe RLHF into reward shaping—and performance degrades accordingly.

This innovation is **fundamental** in its theoretical dimension (unifying two objectives within one probabilistic framework) and **incremental** in its practical dimension (it is a specific loss function design for an existing model architecture). The paper does not claim novelty for the Bradley-Terry model or for using sigmoid classification—both are standard. The novelty is recognizing that these two objectives are not competitors but complements that can be derived from the same underlying model with one additional conceptual element (the virtual boundary response), and that combining them produces a cost signal with properties neither objective alone provides.

---

### Innovation 4: Demonstrating That a Safety Classifier's Logits Are Insufficient as a Cost Signal (A Diagnostic Negative Result)

While framed as an ablation in Section 4.2.4, the comparison between Safe RLHF's Cost Model and a standalone safety classifier (Figure 6a, "CM-classifier") constitutes an **important negative result** for the broader alignment community. The finding is not merely that Safe RLHF works better—this is expected for a paper proposing a new method—but that *a particular alternative approach that seems reasonable a priori fails in a specific, diagnosable way*.

The approach of using a safety classifier to provide cost signals during RL training appears in prior work (Glaese et al., 2022) and has surface plausibility: if you can train a classifier to detect harmful outputs, then penalizing the policy when the classifier's confidence of harmfulness is high should suppress harmful generations. The paper shows this approach is substantially less effective than the Cost Model, and the diagnosis is specific: a classifier provides a *scalar judgment* about whether a response is harmful but does not provide a *directional gradient* about how to make it less harmful. Two harmful responses might both receive high "harmful" probability from a classifier, but one represents a more severe violation than the other. The classifier cannot express this distinction, so the RL optimizer receives a flat penalty for any harmful response rather than a graded signal that guides it toward less severe harms when complete harmlessness is not achievable.

The Cost Model, by incorporating pairwise preferences, encodes a *ranking*: it can tell the optimizer that response A is more harmful than response B even when both are harmful, providing a descent direction toward safer outputs even within the harmful region. This is the "direction for optimization" that the paper contrasts with the "dynamic balance" provided by the classification term. The negative result therefore teaches something general: **for cost signals in constrained RL, ranking information is necessary; binary classification alone is insufficient.** This has implications beyond Safe RLHF—any system that uses learned classifiers to penalize undesirable behavior during RL training should consider whether the classifier's output provides a meaningful gradient, not just a meaningful threshold.

The paper also includes a second diagnostic negative result in Appendix K: attempting to optimize the revision model using ReST^EM (an RL-based self-improvement method from Singh et al., 2024) caused performance to degrade substantially. While specific to revisions rather than Safe RLHF, this result reinforces the paper's broader message that naïve optimization of learned signals can backfire and that careful structured training (like the Lagrangian constraint approach) is necessary to navigate multi-objective tradeoffs without collapse. The ReST^EM failure is not explored in depth—it is mentioned briefly in Appendix K—but it serves as an existence proof that "more optimization" does not monotonically improve alignment.

These negative results are **incremental** contributions individually but collectively support a **fundamental** reframing: the alignment problem requires not just better models or more data but better *optimization structures* that respect the multi-objective nature of human values. The failure modes of classifier-based costs and naïve self-improvement are predicted by the paper's constrained-optimization framework, and their empirical confirmation strengthens the case that the framework captures something real about the problem.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset and Prompts.** The paper constructs its own evaluation prompt dataset for assessing helpfulness and harmlessness, comprising three components: (1) prompts meticulously designed for the 14 safety categories, (2) prompts sourced from open-source safety-related datasets (excluded from training), and (3) a selected 10% of prompts from each red-teaming phase. This dataset is used for the model-based evaluations (Figure 4), GPT-4 evaluations (Figure 5a), human evaluations (Figure 5b), and harmfulness ratio calculations (Figure 5c). The preference data for training reward and cost models is generated per round by the current model (Alpaca-7B for round 1, Beaver-v1 for round 2, Beaver-v2 for round 3) responding to prompts drawn from open-source datasets (Ganguli et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2023a) supplemented by red-teaming prompts starting from round 2. Each prompt receives k = 3~6 unique responses sampled with temperature ∈ {0.001, 0.5, 1.0, 1.2}, top-K = 50, and top-p = 0.95, yielding C(k,2) preference pairs per prompt (Appendix A.2, Figure 3b).

- **Base model.** The starting point is Alpaca-7B (reproduced) — LLaMA-7B (Touvron et al., 2023a) instruction fine-tuned on the 52K-instance Stanford Alpaca dataset (Taori et al., 2023). This model is chosen because it "embodies essential chat assistant capabilities and has an appropriate model size, facilitating the full implementation of the Safe RLHF pipeline" and because it "is capable of generating both harmless and potentially harmful responses" (Section 4.1), providing informative preference data. The reward model, cost model, and policy model all share the LLaMA-7B architecture (Appendix B.1).

- **Metrics.** The paper uses three distinct evaluation approaches, each measuring different aspects of alignment:

  - **Model-based evaluations (Figure 4):** A unified Reward Model and unified Cost Model, trained on evenly balanced preference data from all three Safe RLHF iterations, score each (prompt, response) pair to produce a reward value and a cost value. The reward value measures predicted helpfulness; the cost value measures predicted harmfulness, with C_ψ(y, x) > 0 indicating harmful and C_ψ(y, x) < 0 indicating harmless. These models achieve 73.95% ranking accuracy (RM) and 70.44% ranking accuracy / 85.83% classification accuracy (CM) on held-out test data (Table 1). The scatter plots in Figure 4 visualize the distribution of (cost, reward) pairs across the evaluation prompt set.

  - **Elo scores (Figures 5a, 5b):** Models are compared head-to-head on the same prompts, with evaluators (GPT-4 or human crowdworkers) providing pairwise preferences for helpfulness and harmlessness separately. These pairwise win-rate relationships are converted to Elo scores with an initial score of 1200, following Askell et al. (2021). The evaluation prompts for GPT-4 are specified in Appendix C.2 and include detailed rubrics covering 9 factors for helpfulness and 14 harm categories for harmlessness.

  - **Harmfulness ratio (Figure 5c):** Crowdworkers label whether each model's responses on the evaluation set are safe or harmful using the 14-category scheme from Appendix A.3. The ratio is the percentage of responses flagged harmful.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against:

  - **Alpaca-7B (reproduced):** The initial SFT model before any RLHF, serving as the pre-alignment baseline for all three metrics (Figures 4a, 5a, 5b, 5c, 6a, 6b).

  - **Conventional RLHF (Section 4.2.2, Figure 6a):** PPO training using single-dimensional human preferences (crowdworkers provide one overall preference rather than separate helpfulness and harmlessness rankings). Uses the same response data as round 1 Safe RLHF but with conflated annotations. Trained with standard PPO objective (equation 18) rather than the Lagrangian formulation.

  - **Reward Shaping (RS) (Section 4.2.3, Figure 6b):** PPO training with a statically weighted combination of reward and cost models: R_ν(y, x) = R_ϕ(y, x) - ν · C_ψ(y, x). Tested with seven different weights: ν = 0.01, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 100. Uses the same Reward and Cost Models as Safe RLHF round 1 but with fixed ν rather than dynamic λ.

  - **CM-classifier (Section 4.2.4, Figure 6a):** PPO training where the cost signal comes from the logits of a safety classifier (following Glaese et al., 2022) rather than from the Safe RLHF Cost Model with its dual ranking+classification objectives.

  - **Cost Model without classification (implied by Section 4.2.4):** Removing the classification term from the Cost Model's loss function (equation 14, second term), reducing it to a standard preference model. This degrades Safe RLHF to reward shaping.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute implicitly through the number of generated responses rather than FLOPs. For preference data generation, each prompt receives k = 3~6 responses per round. For PPO training, the model generates 2 return sequences per prompt (Table 4) with max length = 512 tokens. The paper does not provide token counts, FLOPs estimates, or wall-clock training times. The three-round structure means the total compute is approximately triple that of single-round RLHF, though per-round budgets may vary. The paper acknowledges but does not quantify "substantial financial costs" (Section 6).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation for model evaluation. The unified Reward Model and Cost Model used for evaluation (Figure 4, Table 1) are trained on data from all three rounds and tested on held-out preference pairs from the same rounds, but no k-fold cross-validation or statistical significance testing is reported. Elo scores (Figures 5a, 5b) are computed from pairwise comparisons but without confidence intervals. The harmfulness ratios (Figure 5c) are reported as point estimates from crowdworker annotations without error bars. The paper does not report standard deviations, confidence intervals, or p-values for any experimental results.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Helpfulness and Harmlessness Across Three Rounds (Section 4.2.1)

**The headline finding is that three rounds of Safe RLHF training dramatically reduce harmful outputs while simultaneously improving helpfulness**, as measured across three independent evaluation methods.

**Model-based evaluations (Figure 4):** The scatter plots of (reward, cost) pairs on the evaluation prompt set show a clear trajectory across rounds. Alpaca-7B (Figure 4a) produces responses distributed almost evenly on both sides of the c = 0 dividing line, with no clear separation between safe and harmful outputs. After round 1 (Beaver-v1, Figure 4b), the distribution shifts markedly toward the negative-cost (safe) region, though some responses remain in the positive-cost (harmful) region. Round 2 (Beaver-v2, Figure 4c) shows a further reduction in the c > 0 region. Round 3 (Beaver-v3, Figure 4d) exhibits the distribution "gravitating toward the higher reward direction while successfully maintaining the majority of the responses as harmless." The paper does not provide numerical metrics from Figure 4 (e.g., percentage of responses with c < 0, mean cost, mean reward), relying instead on visual interpretation.

**GPT-4 evaluation (Figure 5a):** The Elo scores show monotonic improvement across rounds on both dimensions. Starting from Alpaca-7B (normalized to Elo 1000 for both helpfulness and harmlessness), Beaver-v3 achieves Elo scores of approximately 1245 for helpfulness (+245 points) and 1268 for harmlessness (+268 points). The paper reports exact numbers only for the Alpaca-7B to Beaver-v3 comparison: helpfulness Elo increase of +244.91 (GPT-4) and +363.86 (Human), harmlessness Elo increase of +268.31 (GPT-4) and +237.98 (Human). The intermediate models (Beaver-v1, Beaver-v2) show progressive improvements, with the largest gains typically occurring from Alpaca-7B to Beaver-v1.

**Human evaluation (Figure 5b):** The human-rated Elo scores corroborate the GPT-4 ratings, with a similar monotonic improvement pattern. The paper notes that "evaluations by GPT-4 and human evaluators are almost consistent," citing Chiang & Lee (2023) for the validity of GPT-4 as a proxy for human judgment in alignment evaluation. The harmlessness Elo gains between Beaver-v2 and Beaver-v3 are notably steeper in the human evaluation than in the GPT-4 evaluation, which the paper attributes to red-teaming: "starting from the second round, we initiated red teaming attacks to broaden the scope of safety-related prompts. This effectively aided in making the Safe RLHF training models more harmless."

**Harmfulness ratio (Figure 5c):** The most dramatic improvement is in the percentage of responses flagged harmful by human annotators: Alpaca-7B produces 53.08% harmful responses, Beaver-v1 reduces this to approximately 15%, Beaver-v2 to approximately 5%, and Beaver-v3 to 2.45%. These are the only exact harmfulness percentages reported. The paper interprets the diminishing returns from rounds 2 to 3 as evidence that "since the model was sufficiently safe, Safe RLHF tended to prioritize maintaining the current harmlessness level over excessive optimization" — the Lagrangian mechanism automatically reduced λ once the constraint was satisfied.

**Observation about diminishing returns:** The paper explicitly notes the saturation effect in round 3: "During the third round, since the model was sufficiently safe, Safe RLHF tended to prioritize maintaining the current harmlessness level over excessive optimization. This is also reflective of the dynamic adjustment characteristics inherent to Safe RLHF." This is an important claim about the mechanism — it suggests the Lagrangian approach naturally avoids over-optimizing for safety once the constraint is met, which reward shaping cannot do with a fixed weight.

#### The Benefit of Decoupling Helpfulness and Harmlessness (Section 4.2.2)

**The headline finding is that decoupled annotation produces higher-quality human preference data and enables more effective safety training than single-dimensional RLHF**, with Safe RLHF improving harmlessness substantially more than conventional RLHF while achieving comparable helpfulness improvements.

**Annotation quality metrics:** The paper reports three measures of annotation quality:

- Inter-Rater Agreement Rate (IRR): Helpfulness-only annotation achieves 69.00%, harmlessness-only annotation achieves 66.53%, single-dimensional annotation achieves 61.65% (Section 4.2.2). The decoupled scheme improves IRR by roughly 5–7 percentage points.

- Approval rate during quality inspection: "In single-dimensional annotation, the average approval rate during a 10% quality inspection drops from at least 90% accuracy to below 80%" (Section 4.2.2). The paper phrases this as a degradation from decoupled to conflated annotation, implying that the 90%+ baseline is from the decoupled scheme but not stating this explicitly.

- The paper's annotation team construction (Appendix A.5) provides additional context: 70 annotators retained from an initial pool of approximately 200 after achieving minimum 90% accuracy on researcher-provided answer keys. The 10% sampling and 90% approval threshold for batch acceptance is applied during annotation, not as a post-hoc metric.

**Training comparison (Figure 6a):** The win rates relative to Alpaca-7B on helpfulness and harmlessness, evaluated by GPT-4, show:

- Safe RLHF achieves a harmlessness win rate of approximately 70–75% against Alpaca-7B and a helpfulness win rate of approximately 65–70%.

- Conventional RLHF (labeled "RLHF" in Figure 6a) achieves a comparable helpfulness win rate (~65%) but a substantially lower harmlessness win rate (~45–50%). The paper states that conventional RLHF "results in a notable improvement in helpfulness. However, the enhancement in harmlessness is significantly less than that achieved by Safe RLHF."

- The CM-classifier baseline (labeled "CM-classifier" in Figure 6a) achieves a harmlessness win rate of approximately 50–55% — significantly lower than Safe RLHF — and a helpfulness win rate of approximately 55–60%, also lower. The paper interprets this as evidence that "the latter's efficiency in improving harmlessness is significantly inferior to that of Safe RLHF."

**Interpretation:** The decoupling benefit is two-fold. First, at the annotation stage, it produces higher-quality, higher-agreement preference labels (demonstrated by the IRR and approval rate metrics). Second, at the training stage, it enables the separate Cost Model with its dual ranking+classification objectives, which provides better optimization signals than either conflated preferences (conventional RLHF) or pure safety classifiers (CM-classifier). The helpfulness improvements are similar between Safe RLHF and conventional RLHF because both use the same Reward Model trained on similar helpfulness preference data — the decoupling's benefit manifests primarily in harmlessness, which is the harder objective.

#### Dynamic Balancing vs. Static Reward Shaping (Section 4.2.3)

**The headline finding is that no static reward shaping weight matches Safe RLHF's performance on both dimensions simultaneously**, and the Lagrangian mechanism successfully tracks the model's changing safety profile during training, automatically reducing the penalty when the constraint is satisfied.

**Reward shaping sweep (Figure 6b):** Seven different static weights ν = 0.01, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 100 are tested. The results form a Pareto-like frontier in the (harmlessness win rate, helpfulness win rate) space relative to Alpaca-7B:

- ν = 0.01, 0.5 (low weights): These achieve the highest helpfulness win rates (~70–75%) but the lowest harmlessness win rates (~40–50%). The model prioritizes helpfulness and does not adequately suppress harmful outputs.

- ν = 5, 10, 100 (high weights): These achieve the highest harmlessness win rates (~80–85%) but the lowest helpfulness win rates (~35–45%). The model becomes overly cautious, refusing to answer even benign queries because the heavy cost penalty suppresses all but the most conservative responses.

- ν = 1, 2 (moderate weights): These achieve intermediate performance on both dimensions but still fall short of Safe RLHF. The paper notes that moderate weights "still cannot effectively address the tension between the objectives of helpfulness and harmlessness, with their improvements remaining inferior to Safe RLHF."

**Safe RLHF's position:** The paper states that Safe RLHF outperforms all reward shaping variants, and the dashed asymptotic curve in Figure 6b implies that even the best possible static weight would not reach Safe RLHF's helpfulness-harmlessness tradeoff. The exact win rates for Safe RLHF in Figure 6b are approximately: harmlessness win rate ~80–85%, helpfulness win rate ~60–65% — placing it in a region that no static weight achieves.

**Dynamic adjustment evidence (Figure 6c):** The training curve for Beaver-v1 round 1 shows the Lagrange multiplier λ (blue) and the moving average cost (red) over 300 PPO steps:

- Initial state: Cost starts around 2 (positive — constraint violated), λ starts at 1.
- Early training (steps 0–50): λ rises rapidly from 1 to approximately 5–6 in response to the positive cost, applying strong pressure to reduce harmfulness. The cost begins declining.
- Mid training (steps 50–150): Cost continues declining, crossing below 0 around step 100–150. λ responds by gradually decreasing.
- Late training (steps 150–300): Cost stabilizes near or slightly below 0, and λ settles around 0.5–1.

The paper's interpretation: "When the model satisfies safety constraints, Safe RLHF employs a smaller Lagrange multiplier to preserve λ harmlessness, thereby avoiding over-optimization." This is the key mechanism that distinguishes Safe RLHF from reward shaping — the penalty weight adapts to the model's current behavior rather than remaining fixed throughout training. The paper does not report the analogous λ training curves for rounds 2 and 3, where d = -3 (tightened constraint), which would show whether λ behaves differently with a stricter threshold.

#### Ablation of Cost Model Design (Section 4.2.4)

**The headline finding is that both components of the Cost Model's training objective — pairwise preference ranking and safety classification — are necessary for Safe RLHF's performance.** Removing either degrades the system.

**CM-classifier baseline (Figure 6a):** Using the logits of a standalone safety classifier trained only on binary safe/unsafe labels as the cost signal produces harmlessness win rates approximately 15–20 percentage points lower than Safe RLHF and helpfulness win rates approximately 5–15 points lower. The paper's diagnosis is that the classifier provides a judgment about whether a response is harmful but lacks the pairwise preference signal that encodes *direction* — i.e., which of two harmful responses is less severe. This matters during PPO because the optimizer needs gradient information about how to shift the policy toward safer outputs, not just a binary flag when outputs are harmful.

**Removing classification from the Cost Model (implied by Section 4.2.4, last sentence):** The paper states that "removing the classification capability of the Cost Model, and not updating the Lagrange multipliers, results in a degradation to the Reward Shaping method." The classification loss is what establishes the calibrated zero point at C_ψ(y, x) = 0, which makes the constraint C_ψ(y, x) ≤ 0 meaningful. Without this term, the Cost Model can still rank responses by harmlessness, but there is no principled threshold for when the constraint is satisfied. The Lagrange multiplier update rule (equation 31) depends on J_C(θ_k) = E[C_ψ(y, x)] + d — if the zero point of C_ψ is arbitrary (e.g., shifted by a constant offset), the constraint satisfaction signal is meaningless. This ablation confirms that both the ranking (for optimization direction) and the classification (for threshold calibration) are necessary.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Single-dimensional vs. two-dimensional annotation (Section 4.2.2, Figure 6a):** Using conflated single-dimensional human preferences instead of decoupled helpfulness/harmlessness annotations reduces inter-rater agreement (from 69.00%/66.53% to 61.65%) and significantly degrades harmlessness improvements during PPO training (harmlessness win rate drops from ~70–75% to ~45–50%). The helpfulness improvement remains comparable, confirming that the annotation decoupling primarily benefits the harmlessness objective where the tension with helpfulness creates ambiguity.

- **Static reward shaping weight sweep (Section 4.2.3, Figure 6b):** Seven static weights spanning four orders of magnitude (ν = 0.01 to 100) all underperform Safe RLHF on the helpfulness-harmlessness frontier. Low weights (0.01, 0.5) over-optimize helpfulness; high weights (5, 10, 100) over-optimize harmlessness; moderate weights (1, 2) produce intermediate but still inferior results. This sweep demonstrates that the failure of static weighting is not due to poor hyperparameter selection — no fixed weight works because the optimal tradeoff changes during training.

- **Safety classifier as cost signal (Section 4.2.4, Figure 6a):** Replacing the Cost Model with a standalone safety classifier's logits (following Glaese et al., 2022) substantially reduces harmlessness improvements (win rate ~50–55% vs. ~70–75% for Safe RLHF) and also degrades helpfulness. This ablation isolates the benefit of the Cost Model's pairwise preference ranking — the classifier provides binary judgments but no ranking gradient.

- **Cost Model classification term removal (Section 4.2.4, implied):** Removing the classification term (second term in equation 14) from the Cost Model's training loss eliminates the calibrated zero-crossing that makes the constraint C_ψ(y, x) ≤ 0 meaningful. Without this term, the system degrades to reward shaping because the Lagrange multiplier update cannot reliably determine when the safety constraint is satisfied. The paper does not provide a separate Figure 6a bar for this ablation, making it a conceptual rather than quantitative finding.

- **Cost threshold d variation (Table 4):** The safety constraint tightens from d = 0 (Beaver-v1) to d = -3 (Beaver-v2, Beaver-v3). With d = 0, the constraint is satisfied when E[C_ψ(y, x)] ≤ 0. With d = -3, the constraint requires E[C_ψ(y, x)] ≤ -3 — a stricter condition that demands the expected cost be well within the safe region. The paper does not provide an ablation comparing d values in the same round, but the progressive tightening across rounds suggests the authors found it beneficial to increase the safety margin once initial harmful behaviors were suppressed.

- **Lagrange multiplier initial value and learning rate (Table 4):** λ_0 = 1 (Beaver-v1, Beaver-v3), λ_0 = 0.5 (Beaver-v2). The multiplier learning rate α = 0.01 (Beaver-v1), α = 0.04 (Beaver-v2, Beaver-v3). No ablation is provided for these hyperparameters. The increase in α in later rounds may compensate for the lower initial cost values (since the model is safer, cost violations are smaller, requiring larger α to produce meaningful λ updates).

- **PTX coefficient decay across rounds (Table 4):** γ = 8 (Beaver-v1), 2 (Beaver-v2), 1 (Beaver-v3). The decreasing PTX coefficient suggests the model requires less regularization against catastrophic forgetting as it becomes more stable through iterative fine-tuning. No ablation is provided testing different γ values.

- **KL penalty coefficient (Table 4):** β = 0.1 is held constant across all three rounds. The paper does not ablate this value or explore sensitivity to the KL penalty strength, which controls how far the policy can diverge from the reference model.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: Safe RLHF simultaneously improves helpfulness and harmlessness across three rounds of training.**

*What the experiments demonstrate:* The three evaluation methods (model-based, GPT-4 Elo, human Elo) all show monotonic improvement on both dimensions from Alpaca-7B through Beaver-v3. The harmfulness ratio drops from 53.08% to 2.45%. These results are consistent and show the expected trajectory.

*What weakens the claim:*

- **No confidence intervals or statistical tests.** The Elo scores (Figures 5a, 5b) are point estimates without error bars. With 70 annotators evaluating a test set of unspecified size, the win-rate estimates have sampling variance that is not reported. The harmfulness ratio (Figure 5c) drops from 53.08% to 2.45%, but the denominator (number of evaluated responses) and confidence intervals around these percentages are not provided. If the evaluation set is, say, 500 prompts, a 2.45% harmful rate corresponds to approximately 12 harmful responses — a small absolute number where the addition or subtraction of a few borderline cases could meaningfully shift the percentage.

- **Evaluation set composition is underspecified.** The evaluation set comprises "prompts meticulously designed for 14 safety categories, prompts sourced from open-source datasets (excluded from training), and a selected 10% of prompts from each red-teaming phase." The paper does not report the size of this evaluation set, the distribution across prompt types, or whether it is held constant across rounds. If the evaluation set changes across rounds (e.g., including harder red-teaming prompts in later rounds), the improvements may be partially offset by increasing difficulty.

- **The largest gains occur in round 1.** The harmfulness ratio drops from 53.08% to ~15% in round 1 — a 38 percentage point improvement. Rounds 2 and 3 together contribute only ~13 additional percentage points. The Elo gains are also largest from Alpaca-7B to Beaver-v1. This diminishing returns pattern suggests most of the safety benefit comes from the first application of Safe RLHF, with subsequent rounds providing incremental refinement. The three-round structure is effective but may not be necessary for achieving most of the gains.

- **Single model family, single scale.** All results use Alpaca-7B as the base. The paper argues this model is representative, but it provides no evidence on whether Safe RLHF scales to larger models (e.g., 13B, 70B) or transfers to other architectures (e.g., non-LLaMA models). A model with different base capabilities or different pretraining data might exhibit different helpfulness-harmlessness tensions or different annotator agreement patterns.

**Claim 2: Decoupling annotation improves data quality and enables better safety training.**

*What the experiments demonstrate:* The inter-rater agreement is higher for decoupled annotation (69.00% for helpfulness, 66.53% for harmlessness) than for single-dimensional annotation (61.65%). The approval rate during quality inspection drops below 80% for single-dimensional annotation versus above 90% for decoupled (though the paper's wording is ambiguous about which scheme achieves the 90%+ baseline). The training results (Figure 6a) show that Safe RLHF with decoupled annotations outperforms conventional RLHF with conflated annotations on harmlessness while matching on helpfulness.

*What weakens the claim:*

- **The training comparison conflates two differences.** Conventional RLHF differs from Safe RLHF in TWO ways: the annotation scheme (single vs. dual) AND the optimization algorithm (standard PPO vs. Lagrangian constrained optimization). The ablation in Section 4.2.2 does not isolate which difference causes the improvement. A proper ablation would run Safe RLHF with single-dimensional annotations (somehow mapping the single preference to reward and cost signals) OR run conventional RLHF with dual annotations (e.g., using a weighted sum of the two preference models). As presented, we cannot determine whether the benefit comes from cleaner data or better optimization.

- **The approval rate claim is ambiguous.** The paper states: "In single-dimensional annotation, the average approval rate during a 10% quality inspection drops from at least 90% accuracy to below 80%." The phrase "drops from" implies that the 90% baseline was achieved with decoupled annotation, but this baseline is never explicitly reported for the decoupled case, and the paper does not specify whether the same annotators performed both types of annotation (which would introduce order effects) or different annotators (which would introduce selection effects).

- **Annotation guides introduce a confound.** The paper acknowledges developing special annotation guides to help crowdworkers understand the decoupled dimensions, noting that "crowdworkers may encounter challenges in comprehending artificially decoupled preference dimensions" (Appendix A.4). The single-dimensional annotation likely did not receive equivalent guide development. If decoupled annotation received more careful guidance and training, the quality improvement may partly reflect the investment in annotation infrastructure rather than the inherent superiority of decoupling.

**Claim 3: Dynamic Lagrangian balancing outperforms static reward shaping.**

*What the experiments demonstrate:* Figure 6b shows Safe RLHF achieving harmlessness and helpfulness win rates that no static weight (ν = 0.01 to 100) matches, and Figure 6c shows λ tracking the model's cost during training, rising when cost is positive and falling when cost is negative.

*What weakens the claim:*

- **The static weights are sweeped on one axis but not optimized jointly with other hyperparameters.** The reward shaping baseline uses the same PPO hyperparameters as Safe RLHF (learning rate, KL penalty, PTX coefficient, etc.), which were presumably tuned for the Lagrangian approach. A given static ν might perform better with different PPO hyperparameters (e.g., a higher ν might benefit from a higher KL penalty β to prevent policy collapse from heavy cost pressure). The paper does not demonstrate that the static baselines were given comparable hyperparameter tuning effort.

- **The asymptotic curve in Figure 6b is an interpolation, not an experiment.** The dashed curve connecting the reward shaping points represents the authors' estimate of the best achievable static-weight frontier, but it is fitted to only seven points and no guarantee exists that additional ν values (e.g., ν = 0.2, 0.8, 3) would not close the gap. The paper would be stronger if it demonstrated that Safe RLHF's win rate is *outside the convex hull* of the static-weight points — i.e., Pareto-dominates ALL convex combinations of static-weight policies, which would require either additional ν values or a theoretical argument.

- **λ training curve is shown for only round 1.** Figure 6c shows λ dynamics for Beaver-v1 with d = 0. The dynamics might differ in rounds 2 and 3 where d = -3 (tighter constraint) and α = 0.04 (higher learning rate). With a tighter constraint, λ would need to remain elevated even when cost is slightly negative (since the constraint requires cost ≤ -3), which would change the dynamic pattern. Without these curves, we cannot assess whether the Lagrangian mechanism works as intended under the stricter settings.

- **The reward shaping comparison is only shown for round 1 data.** Figure 6b uses round 1 preference models and presumably round 1 training. The relative advantage of dynamic balancing might change in later rounds where the model is already safer and the remaining tradeoff is subtler.

**Claim 4: The Cost Model's dual ranking+classification training is necessary for performance.**

*What the experiments demonstrate:* Figure 6a shows CM-classifier substantially underperforms Safe RLHF on both helpfulness and harmlessness. The paper argues that removing classification from the Cost Model degrades to reward shaping.

*What weakens the claim:*

- **The CM-classifier baseline is not well-specified.** The paper does not describe the architecture, training data, or training procedure for the standalone safety classifier used in this ablation. If the classifier was trained only on binary safety labels (without the pairwise harmlessness preferences used by the Cost Model), it has access to less information than the Cost Model, making the comparison unfair. A fairer ablation would train the Cost Model with only the ranking loss (first term of equation 14) and compare performance to the full Cost Model — this would isolate the contribution of the classification term while keeping the training data constant.

- **No ablation varying the weight of the classification term.** The classification loss (second term in equation 14) is added to the ranking loss with equal weight. The paper does not test whether the relative weight of these two objectives matters. If the classification term simply provides a weak regularization that keeps costs near zero, a simple L2 penalty toward zero might achieve similar calibration without the binary labels.

- **The "removing classification degrades to reward shaping" claim is not experimentally verified.** The paper states this degradation occurs but provides no data point for it. A comparison showing Safe RLHF with a pure-ranking Cost Model (no classification term, λ updated based on J_C(θ) with the zero-point now arbitrary) would strengthen this claim considerably. Without this ablation, the argument that classification is necessary for the Lagrangian mechanism rests on theoretical reasoning rather than empirical evidence.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

1. **Ablation of d (cost threshold):** Train Safe RLHF with d = 0, d = -1, d = -3, d = -5 in the same round to show how the threshold controls the helpfulness-harmlessness tradeoff. This is one of the key hyperparameters but receives no systematic study.

2. **Ablation of λ initialization and learning rate:** λ_0 and α vary across rounds (Table 4), but no within-round sweep is provided. Given that λ dynamics are central to the method, sensitivity to these hyperparameters matters.

3. **Direct comparison of Safe RLHF reward shaping with matched λ trajectory:** If one recorded λ(t) from a Safe RLHF run and then used that exact sequence as a time-varying ν(t) in a reward shaping run, would performance match? This would isolate whether the benefit of Safe RLHF is automatic λ adaptation or something else about the min-max formulation.

4. **Evaluation on standard safety benchmarks:** The paper evaluates only on its own constructed evaluation set. Testing on established safety benchmarks (e.g., RealToxicityPrompts, TruthfulQA, ToxiGen) would enable comparison with prior work and would reduce concerns about evaluation set construction bias.

5. **Human evaluation with confidence intervals:** The human Elo scores (Figure 5b) and harmfulness ratios (Figure 5c) need uncertainty quantification. With 70 annotators, inter-annotator variability is estimable and should be reported.

6. **Scaling experiments:** Running Safe RLHF on LLaMA-13B or LLaMA-70B would test whether the method's benefits persist at larger scales where base model capabilities (and potential harms) differ.

7. **Multi-turn conversation evaluation:** The paper acknowledges (Section 6) that Safe RLHF "operates within the confines of single-turn conversations." Evaluating on multi-turn dialogues would be more ecologically valid since real-world harms often emerge from extended interactions rather than single responses.

**Summary:** The experiments convincingly demonstrate that Safe RLHF improves over the Alpaca-7B baseline and over several reasonable alternative approaches (single-dimensional RLHF, static reward shaping, classifier-based costs) on the paper's own evaluation set. The evidence for the Lagrangian mechanism specifically — as opposed to simply having separate reward and cost models with some adaptive weighting — is partially circumstantial: the λ training curve (Figure 6c) shows the expected dynamics, but the ablation that would directly test whether the min-max formulation matters (removing classification from the Cost Model while keeping ranking) is not experimentally executed. The paper's strongest empirical contribution is the demonstration that decoupled annotation and dual preference modeling improve harmlessness alignment, and that static balancing of the two objectives is inadequate. Whether the Lagrangian method specifically is necessary for these improvements, or whether other adaptive weighting schemes would perform equally well, remains an open question from the reported experiments.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Overhead Is Unaccounted For, Potentially Dominating the Compute Budget

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire Safe RLHF pipeline depends on the ability to estimate whether the current model is satisfying the safety constraint — i.e., to compute the moving average of expected cost J_C(θ_k) that drives the Lagrange multiplier update (equation 31). This requires the Cost Model to evaluate generated responses during PPO training, which is standard and accounted for. However, the paper also requires a safety evaluation infrastructure that extends well beyond training time: the unified Reward Model and Cost Model used for evaluation (Figure 4), the GPT-4 evaluation prompts (Appendix C.2), the human evaluation process (Figure 5b), and the harmfulness ratio annotation (Figure 5c) all require substantial ongoing human and computational investment. The paper acknowledges financial costs explicitly in Section 6:

> "as is typical with other RLHF studies (Bai et al., 2022a), the financial costs are substantial"

but never quantifies them or factors them into the efficiency comparison with baselines. The annotation pipeline alone involves 70 trained crowdworkers, a dedicated QC team, daily coordination meetings, three rounds of data collection each generating tens of thousands of preference pairs (Figure 3b), and red-teaming attacks conducted by researchers between rounds. None of this cost appears in the headline results.

**The consequence.** A practitioner evaluating whether to adopt Safe RLHF over conventional RLHF or reward shaping cannot determine the marginal cost of the safety improvement. The paper demonstrates that Safe RLHF reduces harmful outputs from 53.08% to 2.45% (Figure 5c), but it does not report what fraction of the total project budget was consumed by the decoupled annotation scheme versus what single-dimensional annotation would have cost, nor whether the three-round structure was necessary versus a single round with tightened constraints. The iterative red-teaming process (Section 4.1, Appendix D) requires skilled researchers who can discover novel attack vectors — this is not a fixed-cost annotation task but an open-ended adversarial exploration whose cost scales with the sophistication of the model being attacked. As the model becomes safer (Figure 5c, rounds 2–3), finding new vulnerabilities becomes harder, potentially requiring exponentially more red-teaming effort for diminishing safety returns. The paper provides no cost-effectiveness analysis to guide decisions about when to stop iterating.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports that the crowdworker wage is 1.98×–2.56× the local hourly average (Appendix A.5) and that the annotation team was reduced from an initial pool of approximately 200 to 70 after a rigorous assessment. The preference data volumes per round are shown in Figure 3b (thousands to tens of thousands of pairs). The paper acknowledges "substantial" costs in Section 6 but provides no dollar figures, FLOPs estimates, or person-hour counts. The three-round structure (Section 4.1) means all costs — annotation, preference model training, PPO training — are incurred three times, but no ablation tests whether, for example, two rounds with a tighter d threshold would achieve similar results at lower cost.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation. It acknowledges the cost issue briefly in Section 6 but treats it as an inherent property of RLHF pipelines rather than something to be optimized or reduced. The authors suggest that transitioning to LLaMA-2 as a base model "could boost performance levels" (Section 6), which might reduce the number of rounds needed, but this is speculation. No cost-ablation experiments (e.g., comparing 1-round vs. 2-round vs. 3-round Safe RLHF at fixed total annotation budget) are presented. A practitioner reading this paper would need to run their own cost-benefit analysis with no guidance from the reported results.

---

### Single Model Family, Single Scale, Single Base Training Recipe

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use exactly one starting point: Alpaca-7B (reproduced), which is LLaMA-7B instruction fine-tuned on the 52K-instance Stanford Alpaca dataset. The paper justifies this choice in Section 4.1:

> "Alpaca-7B embodies essential chat assistant capabilities and has an appropriate model size, facilitating the full implementation of the Safe RLHF pipeline."

The reward model, cost model, and policy model all share the LLaMA-7B backbone architecture (Appendix B.1). The paper does not test Safe RLHF on any other model size (e.g., 13B, 70B), any other pretraining corpus, any other instruction-tuning dataset, or any non-LLaMA architecture. The paper acknowledges this limitation implicitly in Section 6:

> "our research was conducted using data from Llama-1 and Alpaca models which were considering predate Llama-2. It suggests transitioning to Llama-2 as a base pretrain model could boost performance levels."

**The consequence.** At least four dimensions of generalisation are untested:

1. **Scale generalisation:** The 7B parameter scale sits in a particular capability regime. A 70B model would likely have higher base helpfulness (better instruction following, more detailed responses) but also higher base harmfulness (more capable of generating convincing harmful content, more knowledge of dangerous topics). The tension between these objectives might be qualitatively different at larger scales — a more capable model might find more sophisticated ways to be "partially harmful" (Appendix D.1) or to comply with harmful instructions when embedded in complex prompts (Appendix D.4). The paper's red-teaming taxonomy (Appendix D) was developed against 7B models; larger models might exhibit entirely new attack surfaces not captured by the four categories identified.

2. **Architecture generalisation:** The LLaMA architecture's specific attention patterns, positional encodings, and training recipes might interact with Safe RLHF in unknown ways. The KL penalty in equation (24) depends on token-level probability ratios between the current policy and the reference model; architectures with different output distributions (e.g., different softmax temperatures, different vocabulary sizes) might require different β values.

3. **Instruction-tuning data generalisation:** Alpaca's 52K instances are a specific distribution of tasks and response styles. A model instruction-tuned on different data (e.g., FLAN, Dolly, OpenAssistant) might have different base rates of helpfulness and harmfulness, different response formats that affect the Cost Model's calibration, and different susceptibility to the annotation scheme's harm categories.

4. **Pretraining data generalisation:** LLaMA-1's pretraining corpus has a specific composition of web text, code, books, and other sources. A model pretrained on different data (e.g., more multilingual content, different proportions of technical vs. conversational text) might have different knowledge about harmful topics and different base tendencies toward safe vs. unsafe completions.

A practitioner using a different base model — which is virtually all practitioners, since the field has moved beyond LLaMA-1 — cannot confidently extrapolate from these results without additional experiments.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper provides no ablation across model scales, architectures, instruction-tuning datasets, or pretraining corpora. The suggestion to transition to LLaMA-2 in Section 6 is forward-looking and not evaluated.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation experimentally. The suggestion in Section 6 to use LLaMA-2 as a future direction implicitly acknowledges the generalisation concern but provides no data. A single-model evaluation is standard for methods papers introducing a new training pipeline — the contribution is the algorithm, not a universal empirical claim — but the absence of any scale or architecture sweep means the paper's conclusions are conditional on the specific Alpaca-7B starting point.

---

### The Constraint Threshold d Is Central to Performance but Not Systematically Studied

**The assumption or constraint.** The safety constraint threshold d in equation (11) — which determines how strict the safety requirement is — is varied across rounds (d = 0 for Beaver-v1, d = -3 for Beaver-v2 and Beaver-v3, Table 4) but never ablated within a round. The paper describes d as a hyperparameter "devised to exert control over the probability of generating harmful responses" (Section 3.3), which accurately captures its role: d shifts the target expected cost from ≤0 to ≤-3, requiring responses to be more firmly in the safe region. However, the paper provides no justification for these specific values, no sensitivity analysis, and no guidance for how a practitioner should choose d for a new model or task.

**The consequence.** The choice of d potentially controls the entire helpfulness-harmlessness tradeoff. With d = 0, the constraint is satisfied whenever the expected cost is non-positive — the model is penalized only when, on average, responses cross into the harmful region (C_ψ > 0). With d = -3, the model is penalized unless the expected cost is at least 3 units below the decision boundary, requiring a margin of safety. A larger negative d would produce an even more conservative model; a positive d would effectively disable the constraint. The paper's results show that tightening from d = 0 to d = -3 coincides with the introduction of red-teaming prompts and the shift from round 1 to round 2, confounding the effect of d with the effect of more adversarial training data and a different starting model. A practitioner cannot determine from these results whether the safety improvement from round 1 to round 2 was driven by the tighter constraint, the red-teaming data, or the improved base model (Beaver-v1 vs. Alpaca-7B).

The same confound applies to the Lagrange multiplier hyperparameters. The initial λ_0 varies (1, 0.5, 1) and the learning rate α varies (0.01, 0.04, 0.04) across rounds (Table 4). Without within-round sweeps of these values, it is impossible to know whether Safe RLHF is robust to these choices or whether careful per-round tuning was necessary to achieve the reported results. If α = 0.04 had been used in round 1 instead of α = 0.01, the λ dynamics in Figure 6c might have been qualitatively different — λ might have overshot, causing the model to over-optimize for harmlessness and suppress helpfulness, similar to the high-ν reward shaping failures in Figure 6b.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 4 provides the specific values used: d = 0 (round 1), d = -3 (rounds 2–3); λ_0 = 1 (rounds 1, 3), λ_0 = 0.5 (round 2); α = 0.01 (round 1), α = 0.04 (rounds 2–3). These are the only values tested. The paper provides no ablation comparing different d values in the same round, no sweep of λ_0, and no sweep of α. The reward shaping experiment (Figure 6b) sweeps the static weight ν across seven values spanning four orders of magnitude, demonstrating that the authors understand the value of hyperparameter sweeps for the baseline — the absence of equivalent sweeps for Safe RLHF's own key hyperparameters is a notable asymmetry.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. Section 6 does not mention d, λ_0, or α as hyperparameters requiring further study. The iterative structure (three rounds) implicitly encodes a schedule for d (tightening over time) but provides no theoretical or empirical justification for why this schedule is appropriate. A practitioner implementing Safe RLHF would need to treat d, λ_0, and α as additional hyperparameters to tune, with no guidance from the paper on reasonable ranges, sensitivity, or tuning methodology.

---

### Evaluation Is Confined to an Internally Constructed Dataset with Unknown Coverage of the Harm Space

**The assumption or constraint.** All evaluations — model-based (Figure 4), GPT-4 Elo (Figure 5a), human Elo (Figure 5b), and harmfulness ratio (Figure 5c) — are conducted on the paper's own evaluation prompt dataset, constructed from three sources: "prompts meticulously designed for 14 safety categories, prompts sourced from open-source datasets (excluded from training), and a selected 10% of prompts from each red-teaming phase" (Section 4.1). The paper does not report the size of this evaluation set, its distribution across the 14 harm categories, or its overlap (conceptual or literal) with the training prompts. No evaluation is conducted on established external safety benchmarks such as RealToxicityPrompts (Gehman et al., 2020), TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022), ToxiGen (Hartvigsen et al., 2022), or the harmfulness benchmarks used in Bai et al. (2022a) or Ganguli et al. (2022).

**The consequence.** An internally constructed evaluation set creates several risks for the validity of the reported improvements:

1. **Overfitting to the annotation scheme's harm ontology:** The 14 harm categories (Appendix A.3) define what "safe" means for both the annotation process and the evaluation. If these categories miss important harm types — for example, subtle forms of manipulation, emotional harm, long-term societal risks, or harms specific to particular cultural contexts — the model might learn to avoid the specific harms in the ontology while remaining vulnerable to unlisted harms. The paper provides no evidence that the 14 categories are comprehensive or that models trained under this scheme generalize to harm types not explicitly annotated.

2. **Distributional overlap between training and evaluation:** The evaluation set includes "a selected 10% of prompts from each red-teaming phase." If the red-teaming prompts are challenging adversarial examples that the model was explicitly trained to handle (via the preference data generated from them), evaluating on a held-out subset of the same distribution may overestimate safety on truly novel adversarial prompts. A stronger evaluation would use red-teaming prompts generated by an independent team attacking a held-out model, or would use entirely external benchmarks with no connection to the training process.

3. **Unknown evaluation set size and composition:** Without knowing how many prompts the evaluation set contains, it is impossible to assess the statistical reliability of the reported harmfulness ratios (Figure 5c). If the evaluation set has, say, 200 prompts, the 2.45% harmful rate for Beaver-v3 corresponds to approximately 5 harmful responses — a number where the addition or subtraction of 1–2 borderline cases could shift the percentage meaningfully. The 53.08% harmful rate for Alpaca-7B on the same set would correspond to approximately 106 harmful responses, a much more stable estimate. The apparent 50-percentage-point improvement might partially reflect the instability of small-sample proportions at the low end.

4. **No cross-model comparability:** Because the evaluation set is custom to this paper, the reported Elo scores and harmfulness ratios cannot be directly compared with any other published model. A practitioner cannot determine whether Beaver-v3 is safer than, for example, LLaMA-2-Chat-7B or the models from Bai et al. (2022a) without running their own evaluation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides the evaluation prompt sources in Section 4.1, the Elo evaluation prompts in Appendix C.2, and the harm category definitions in Appendix A.3. It does not provide the evaluation set size, the per-category breakdown, or results on any external benchmark. The Elo scores (Figures 5a, 5b) are reported without confidence intervals, and the harmfulness ratios (Figure 5c) are reported as point estimates without sample sizes or error bars.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. Section 6 focuses on other limitations (pretraining data access, SFT data quality, single-turn conversations, financial costs) but does not discuss evaluation generalisation or the need for external benchmark validation. The authors release their data and training code (Section 1), which would enable other researchers to run external evaluations, but do not conduct such evaluations themselves.

---

### The Cost Model Depends on Human-Annotated Safety Labels That May Not Generalise Across Cultural and Linguistic Contexts

**The assumption or constraint.** The Cost Model's classification capability — and therefore the entire Lagrangian constraint mechanism — depends on binary safety labels s(y) ∈ {-1, +1} that are assigned by crowdworkers according to the 14 harm categories defined in Appendix A.3. These categories were developed by the research team (adapted from Ji et al., 2023) and reflect specific cultural, legal, and ethical frameworks. The annotation team was recruited from a single geographic region (the paper mentions compliance with "local labor laws" and wages relative to "the local hourly average" in Appendix A.5, implying a single country). The paper does not discuss whether the harm categories or the annotators' safety judgments would transfer to different cultural contexts, legal jurisdictions, or linguistic communities.

**The consequence.** The safety alignment achieved by Safe RLHF is relative to the specific harm ontology and annotator population used. Several failure modes arise:

1. **Cross-cultural harm perception:** What constitutes "hate speech" (category 1), "discrimination" (category 2), or "controversial topics" (category 9) varies substantially across cultures. Content that one cultural context considers offensive political speech might be considered legitimate critique in another. A model trained to satisfy safety constraints defined by annotators from one cultural background might be unacceptably permissive or unacceptably restrictive when deployed in a different cultural context.

2. **Legal jurisdiction mismatch:** Categories like "financial crime" (category 4) and "privacy violation" (category 5) have legally specific definitions that differ across jurisdictions. Content that is legal financial advice in one country might constitute unlicensed financial solicitation in another. The binary safety labels do not encode jurisdictional nuance.

3. **Linguistic coverage:** All annotation and evaluation is conducted in English. The harm categories were defined in English, the annotation guides were written in English, and annotators were required to "demonstrate high proficiency in English" (Appendix A.5). A model deployed in a multilingual setting might exhibit different safety properties in other languages — harmful content in a non-English language might not trigger the same Cost Model responses if the Cost Model was trained primarily or exclusively on English data.

4. **Temporal drift:** The 14 harm categories represent a snapshot of what the research team and annotators considered harmful at annotation time. Societal norms around acceptable speech, privacy, and controversial topics evolve. A Cost Model trained on static labels cannot adapt to shifting norms without re-annotation and retraining.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides the full list of 14 harm categories (Appendix A.3), describes the annotator recruitment and training process (Appendix A.5), and notes that annotators were required to demonstrate high English proficiency. The paper does not report annotator demographics (beyond local wage comparisons), does not test inter-annotator agreement across demographic subgroups, and does not evaluate model safety in languages other than English or for cultural contexts outside the annotators' background. The Cost Model's classification accuracies (95.62%, 84.54%, 85.88% in Table 1) are reported without breakdown by harm category, so we cannot determine whether certain categories (e.g., "controversial topics, politics") had lower agreement than others (e.g., "child abuse," which might have more universal consensus).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The harm categories and annotation process are treated as a fixed, given infrastructure for the Safe RLHF pipeline. Section 6 does not mention cultural generalisation, linguistic diversity, or temporal drift of safety norms. The suggestion to "expand our existing framework to encompass more preference categories beyond current measures of helpfulness and harmlessness" (Section 6) gestures toward adding more objectives but does not address the core issue that any fixed harm taxonomy is culturally and temporally situated.

---

### The Training Regime Introduces Several Hyperparameters Without Guidance, and the Baselines May Not Be Tuned Comparably

**The assumption or constraint.** Safe RLHF introduces at least four new hyperparameters beyond standard PPO: the cost threshold d, the Lagrange multiplier initial value λ_0, the multiplier learning rate α (equation 31), and the KL penalty split factor β/2 (equations 25–26). The paper reports specific values for each round (Table 4) but provides no sensitivity analysis for any of them. Meanwhile, the primary baselines — conventional RLHF (Section 4.2.2) and reward shaping (Section 4.2.3) — use the same PPO hyperparameters (learning rate, β, γ, clip ratio, etc.) as Safe RLHF. These PPO hyperparameters were presumably tuned for or alongside the Safe RLHF method, but the paper does not describe any hyperparameter tuning process for any method.

**The consequence.** The comparison between Safe RLHF and the baselines may be biased in two directions:

1. **PPO hyperparameters were likely tuned for Safe RLHF.** If the actor learning rate (9.65 × 10^-6), KL penalty (β = 0.1), PTX coefficient (γ = 8, 2, 1), and other PPO parameters were chosen based on Safe RLHF's training dynamics, they may be suboptimal for conventional RLHF or reward shaping. For example, reward shaping with a very high ν = 100 may benefit from a different β that prevents the policy from collapsing to empty or trivial responses under extreme cost pressure. The paper does not demonstrate that the baselines received comparable hyperparameter optimisation effort.

2. **Safe RLHF's own hyperparameters may be brittle.** Without sensitivity analysis, a practitioner cannot assess whether the reported results depend on careful per-round tuning of d, λ_0, and α. The fact that these values change across rounds (Table 4) suggests the authors did tune them, but whether this tuning required extensive experimentation or was straightforward is unknown. If Safe RLHF requires per-round, per-model, or per-dataset tuning of these hyperparameters, the method's practical deployability is substantially lower than if it works robustly across a range of settings.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 4 provides the specific hyperparameter values for each round. The paper does not describe any hyperparameter search procedure, any held-out validation set used for tuning, or any sensitivity analysis. The reward shaping sweep (Figure 6b) tests seven ν values, which is commendable — but the PPO hyperparameters are held fixed at the Safe RLHF values for all ν, which conflates the effect of ν with potential interactions between ν and the PPO hyperparameters.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The absence of hyperparameter sensitivity analysis is common in RLHF papers due to the computational expense of training LLMs, but the asymmetry between Safe RLHF (where hyperparameters are changed across rounds without ablation) and the baselines (where they are held fixed) weakens the comparative claims. A minimal robustness check — for example, running Safe RLHF with α = 0.01 vs. α = 0.04 in the same round — would substantially strengthen confidence that the method does not depend on precise tuning of the Lagrange multiplier dynamics.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

Safe RLHF does not propose a fundamentally new optimization algorithm—PPO and Lagrangian methods are both well-established—but it makes a **structural reframing** of the LLM alignment problem that shifts the field away from scalarized multi-objective optimization and toward constrained optimization as the appropriate mathematical formalism for safety. This is a mid-level conceptual shift: it changes how practitioners should think about the problem, what they should build, and what baselines they should compare against, but it does not disrupt the underlying RLHF infrastructure. The same preference models, PPO training loops, and evaluation protocols largely carry over; what changes is the architecture of how they are combined.

The reframing matters because it resolves a persistent tension in the alignment literature that prior work had acknowledged but not formalized. Bai et al. (2022a) recognized that helpfulness and harmlessness conflict and collected separate preference data, but ultimately collapsed the two objectives into a single scalar signal during training. Touvron et al. (2023b) described manually adjusting the weight between reward and cost as a practical challenge. Glaese et al. (2022) used a safety classifier to provide a cost signal but did not frame safety as a constraint. Safe RLHF unifies these observations under a coherent formalism—the Constrained MDP—and provides empirical evidence (Figures 6a, 6b) that the formalism captures something real: no static weight replicates the performance of dynamic Lagrangian balancing, and a pure classifier-based cost signal is substantially weaker than a preference-trained Cost Model.

The paper also provides a **diagnostic contribution** that should influence how the field evaluates alignment methods. The reward shaping sweep (Figure 6b) demonstrates that the helpfulness-harmlessness tradeoff cannot be captured by a single fixed exchange rate—the optimal penalty for harmful outputs depends on the model's current behavior, not just on abstract human preferences. This means that alignment papers proposing new scalarization schemes (e.g., new ways to combine multiple reward models into one score) should include a dynamic baseline like Safe RLHF to test whether their static combination is sufficient. The paper's negative result with classifier-based costs (Figure 6a, "CM-classifier") further suggests that safety signals for RL training need ranking information, not just binary judgments—a finding with implications beyond this specific pipeline.

Specific research directions this work makes more attractive:

- **Constrained optimization for other alignment tensions.** The tension between helpfulness and harmlessness is one instance of a broader class of conflicts in value alignment: honesty vs. politeness, completeness vs. conciseness, creativity vs. factual accuracy, engagement vs. addiction avoidance. Safe RLHF's framework—decoupled annotation, separate preference models, Lagrangian constraint balancing—is directly portable to any pair of objectives where one should be treated as a constraint rather than absorbed into the reward. The key design choice is which objective to constrain: for helpfulness-safety, safety is the constraint because "completely harmless but useless" is a failure mode (over-refusal) while "helpful but occasionally dangerous" is also unacceptable. For other objective pairs, different assignments may be appropriate, and the paper provides a template for making that determination.

- **Cost model design as a research area.** The paper shows that the Cost Model's dual ranking+classification training is crucial for performance (Section 4.2.4), suggesting that cost model architecture and training deserve attention comparable to reward model design. Questions this opens: Should the cost model be larger than the reward model since safety classification may require more nuanced understanding than helpfulness ranking? Should cost models be updated more frequently during training to track the policy's shifting harm distribution? Can cost models be adversarially trained against the policy to prevent reward hacking of the safety signal?

Research directions this work makes less attractive:

- **Static scalarization of multiple alignment objectives.** The paper's evidence that no fixed weight spans four orders of magnitude matches Safe RLHF's performance (Figure 6b) suggests that future work should not invest heavily in finding the "right" static weighting between helpfulness and harmlessness. The problem is structural, not parametric—the optimal tradeoff changes during training, so no static weight can be optimal throughout. This doesn't mean scalarization is useless (reward shaping with moderate weights still improves over unconstrained RLHF), but it does mean that methods claiming to solve the multi-objective alignment problem through better weighting schemes now face a higher bar: they should demonstrate superiority over a dynamic Lagrangian baseline, not just over single-objective RLHF.

- **Pure safety classifiers as cost signals.** The CM-classifier baseline's poor performance (Figure 6a) is a specific negative result that should discourage the approach of training a binary classifier and using its logits as a cost signal during RL. The diagnosis—classifiers don't provide ranking gradients—is general and applies beyond language models to any RL setting where cost signals guide policy optimization. Future work on safety-constrained RL should invest in cost models that encode preferences, not just classifications.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Within-round ablation of the cost threshold d to characterize the Pareto frontier of achievable safety-helpfulness tradeoffs.** The paper varies d across rounds (0 in round 1, -3 in rounds 2–3, Table 4) but never ablates d within a single round. A clean experiment would take the round 1 setup (Alpaca-7B base, round 1 prompts, round 1 preference models) and run Safe RLHF with d ∈ {-5, -3, -1, 0, +1, +3}, measuring the resulting helpfulness and harmlessness Elo scores for each. This would produce the equivalent of Figure 6b but for Safe RLHF—showing whether the Lagrangian method traces out a Pareto frontier as d varies, and whether certain d values collapse to the same behavior (indicating the constraint is either always or never binding). This experiment would also reveal whether d = -3 in rounds 2–3 was necessary or whether d = 0 would have sufficed once the model was already safer, disentangling the effect of d from the effect of red-teaming data.

**Direct comparison of Safe RLHF against a recorded-λ reward shaping baseline to isolate the mechanism.** The paper attributes Safe RLHF's advantage over reward shaping to dynamic λ adaptation, but the comparison conflates dynamic adaptation with the min-max formulation. A direct test: record the sequence of λ values from a Safe RLHF training run, then run reward shaping with ν(t) = λ(t) as a time-varying static weight. If performance matches Safe RLHF, the benefit is purely from having a time-varying weight (which could be implemented without the Lagrangian machinery). If performance is worse, the min-max structure provides benefits beyond time-variation—perhaps by making the policy robust to worst-case λ rather than just the current λ. This experiment cleanly separates "dynamic weighting helps" from "the Lagrangian formulation specifically helps."

**Application of Safe RLHF to LLaMA-2-7B-Chat or Mistral-7B-Instruct to test generalization across base models and instruction-tuning recipes.** The paper acknowledges (Section 6) that transitioning to LLaMA-2 could boost performance but does not run this experiment. A follow-up should replicate the round 1 Safe RLHF pipeline exactly (same prompts, same annotation scheme, same hyperparameters) but starting from LLaMA-2-7B-Chat (which already has some RLHF safety alignment) and Mistral-7B-Instruct (which uses a different architecture and training recipe). Key measurements: (1) Does Safe RLHF still provide gains over the already-aligned base model, or does it saturate? (2) Do the Inter-Rater Agreement patterns (69.00% helpfulness, 66.53% harmlessness for decoupled annotation) replicate, or do they depend on the specific response distributions of Alpaca-7B? (3) Does the Lagrangian mechanism behave similarly (λ rising then falling in Figure 6c), or does an already-safe model require different α and d values? This experiment would transform Safe RLHF from a method validated on one model to a method validated across the current generation of open-weight chat models.

**Cost Model trained with only ranking loss plus a fixed zero-centering penalty, to test whether the classification term (equation 14, second term) can be replaced with a simpler regularization.** The paper argues (Section 4.2.4) that the classification loss is necessary because it establishes the calibrated zero-crossing, but a simpler alternative exists: train the Cost Model with only the pairwise ranking loss (first term of equation 14) plus an L2 penalty centered at zero, E[(C_ψ(y, x) - 0)^2], which would also encourage scores to be centered around zero without requiring binary safety labels. If this simpler Cost Model performs comparably to the full Cost Model in Safe RLHF, it would eliminate the need for safety label annotation in the Cost Model training pipeline—the pairwise harmlessness preferences alone would suffice. If it performs worse, it confirms that the classification signal provides information beyond mere centering, likely because the binary labels encode a threshold that pairwise rankings alone cannot identify.

**Stress-testing the Cost Model's safety boundary under distribution shift from PPO-driven policy changes.** The Cost Model is trained on responses from the pre-PPO model (e.g., round 1 CM is trained on Alpaca-7B outputs) but evaluates responses from the PPO-trained model, which has a different output distribution. The paper does not measure whether the CM's classification accuracy degrades on PPO-generated responses compared to the training distribution. A follow-up should: (1) collect responses from the PPO-trained model at checkpoints throughout training, (2) evaluate the CM's classification accuracy on these responses against human safety labels, and (3) measure whether the zero-crossing remains calibrated (i.e., whether C_ψ(y, x) = 0 still separates safe from unsafe responses). If accuracy degrades significantly, it suggests the Cost Model should be periodically retrained on on-policy data during PPO, which would increase the cost of Safe RLHF but potentially improve the reliability of the constraint signal. If accuracy remains stable, it validates the current design of training the CM once per round.

**Multi-turn conversational Safe RLHF with per-turn cost constraints.** The paper explicitly limits itself to single-turn conversations (Section 6). A natural extension would be to define per-turn cost constraints C_ψ(y_t, x, y_1:t-1) ≤ 0 for each response in a multi-turn dialogue and apply the Lagrangian method with a per-turn or cumulative cost budget. This is nontrivial because multi-turn harms are qualitatively different from single-turn harms: a model might produce individually safe responses that collectively constitute manipulation, information extraction, or gradual radicalization across turns. A follow-up would need to: (1) develop a multi-turn harm annotation scheme (likely more expensive than single-turn), (2) train a Cost Model that conditions on conversation history, (3) define whether the constraint applies per-turn (each response must be safe) or per-episode (total cost over the conversation must be ≤ 0), and (4) evaluate whether Safe RLHF trained on multi-turn data prevents the conversation-level harms that single-turn safety training misses. The red-teaming taxonomy in Appendix D (scenario assumptions, complex text command embedding) already hints at multi-turn analogues—a user might build rapport over several turns before introducing a harmful request, which single-turn safety training would not anticipate.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Open-source chat model fine-tuning with customizable safety thresholds.** A downstream model developer (e.g., a company fine-tuning LLaMA-3-8B for a customer service chatbot) could use Safe RLHF to produce models with different safety-helpfulness tradeoffs by varying d without retraining the preference models. Concretely: train one Reward Model and one Cost Model on their domain-specific preference data, then run Safe RLHF with d = 0, -1, -3, -5 to produce four model variants ranging from permissive (d = 0, constraint satisfaction requires only that expected cost ≤ 0) to conservative (d = -5, constraint requires a substantial safety margin). Deploy the appropriate variant based on the risk profile of the use case—a children's educational chatbot might use d = -5, while an internal corporate knowledge base chatbot might use d = 0. The paper's results (Figure 5c, harmfulness ratio dropping from 53.08% to 2.45%) suggest that d provides meaningful control over the safety level, and the Lagrangian mechanism means that each variant will automatically balance helpfulness against the specified constraint without manual weight tuning.

**Iterative red-teaming pipelines for ongoing safety maintenance.** The paper's three-round structure (Figure 3) is not just an experimental design—it is a template for continuous safety improvement in deployed systems. An organization operating a public-facing LLM could institutionalize the cycle: (1) deploy the current safe model, (2) run a bug bounty or internal red team to discover new vulnerabilities, (3) generate preference data from the discovered prompts, (4) retrain Cost Model and run Safe RLHF with the red-teaming data incorporated, (5) deploy the updated model. The paper demonstrates that each round discovers new attack surfaces (the four red-teaming categories in Appendix D) and that the model becomes progressively harder to attack (Figure 3a, unsolved safety-related prompts dropping from 2471 to 379 across rounds). The key practical insight is that red-teaming data should be fed back into the Cost Model (via annotation) rather than just used for evaluation—this closes the loop between vulnerability discovery and mitigation.

**Preference data collection with higher inter-annotator agreement through decoupled annotation.** The paper's finding that decoupling helpfulness and harmlessness annotation increases inter-rater agreement from 61.65% to 69.00% and 66.53% (Section 4.2.2) has immediate practical implications for any organization collecting human preference data for LLM alignment. The decoupled scheme requires more annotation time per response pair (two judgments instead of one) but produces higher-quality labels with less noise. For a fixed annotation budget, the tradeoff is between more noisy single-dimensional pairs and fewer cleaner decoupled pairs. The paper's approval rate evidence—"the average approval rate during a 10% quality inspection drops from at least 90% accuracy to below 80%" for single-dimensional annotation—suggests that decoupling may be more cost-effective overall if rework and quality inspection costs are factored in. Organizations building RLHF pipelines should pilot decoupled annotation on a subset of their data and measure whether the IRR improvement in their domain matches the paper's 5–7 percentage point gain.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions Safe RLHF against two named alternatives—conventional single-dimensional RLHF (Section 4.2.2) and reward shaping with static weights (Section 4.2.3)—and against one additional approach, classifier-based cost signals (Section 4.2.4). The decision rules that emerge from the experimental comparisons are:

- **Prefer Safe RLHF over conventional RLHF when** the deployment context requires simultaneously improving both helpfulness and harmlessness, and the base model produces a non-trivial fraction of harmful responses (e.g., Alpaca-7B's 53.08% harmful rate in Figure 5c). The evidence: Safe RLHF matches conventional RLHF on helpfulness improvements while substantially outperforming it on harmlessness (Figure 6a), and the decoupled annotation produces higher-quality data (IRR 66.53–69.00% vs. 61.65%). The cost is additional annotation time (two judgments per response pair) and the complexity of training and maintaining a separate Cost Model.

- **Prefer Safe RLHF over reward shaping when** the optimal tradeoff between helpfulness and harmlessness is expected to shift during training—which is always the case when starting from a model that produces many harmful responses, since the penalty needed early in training differs from the penalty needed later. The evidence: no static weight across four orders of magnitude matches Safe RLHF's performance (Figure 6b), and Figure 6c shows λ adapting from ~6 to ~0.5 as the model's cost decreases. The cost is the additional hyperparameters d, λ_0, and α that require tuning, though the paper's specific values (Table 4) provide a starting point.

- **Prefer Safe RLHF over classifier-based cost signals when** the available safety annotation data includes pairwise harmlessness preferences (not just binary safe/unsafe labels). The evidence: the CM-classifier baseline substantially underperforms Safe RLHF on both helpfulness and harmlessness (Figure 6a), because binary classifiers do not provide the ranking gradient that guides the policy toward less harmful outputs within the harmful region. The cost is the requirement for pairwise preference annotation rather than simpler binary labeling.

- **Prefer conventional RLHF or reward shaping over Safe RLHF when** the annotation budget is severely constrained and collecting decoupled pairwise preferences for both dimensions is infeasible, or when the base model is already sufficiently safe that the dynamic constraint mechanism provides marginal benefit (e.g., if starting from LLaMA-2-Chat rather than Alpaca-7B). The paper does not provide an explicit cost-benefit analysis (see Limitations section), so practitioners must weigh the demonstrated ~15–20 percentage point harmlessness improvement over conventional RLHF against the additional annotation and training complexity for their specific use case.
