Here are the takeaways from this Ethan Evans interview (went from being fired twice for poor soft skills to VP at Amazon, leading 800+ engineers):

**Early career and the startup gold rush**

He grew up wanting to be an engineer at Apple, even writing them a physical letter as a kid; they replied that at the time they only hired from five universities (Stanford, Caltech, Berkeley, Carnegie Mellon, MIT). He got into Carnegie Mellon, did a master's at Purdue, then entered a string of startups all built around high-speed networking—physical-layer networking, internet search (acquired by Lycos), network management for telcos, internet advertising and audio streaming (video wasn't viable yet), a brief detour into entertainment lighting, and then Amazon, which recruited him to build Prime Video based on his music-streaming work.

His reason for doing startups wasn't strategic: his college roommate joined one early, got stock options before hardly anyone knew what they were, and made his first million by 25 or 26. "It was the startup dream. I joined a little later. I only made enough money to put a down payment on a house." He followed friends; he didn't start thinking intentionally about career shape until later.

**Getting fired twice (and the blunt correlation. "If I were that valuable, they'd have kept me around")**

His first performance review ever flagged the problem: his manager wrote, "I think sometimes Ethan's solution to problems would be, let's knock their heads together." He was combative, a self-described "loose cannon" who was "pointed at them." At one startup, he fought with a product VP who was correctly predicting their timeline was impossible; Evans couldn't handle the disagreement because it clashed with his religion of startup enthusiasm. When downsizing came, he told the CEO he had another offer and could "bounce," saving someone else's job—and, he later realized, the CEO was probably glad to shed the contention.

At the next startup, leading engineering during the .com bust, he clashed with the sales VP who was over-promising to close a deal. Evans argued "we can't do that," not grasping that it was either sign the contract or go out of business. He was laid off—"technically a layoff, but I was the only person laid off in the layoff. I'd call that being fired." It was a hundred percent about removing conflict.

After the second firing, with the post-9/11 economy making job hunting brutal, an interviewer told him: "Everything you say sounds fine, but I just really struggle to believe that if you were that valuable, these companies, even though they were struggling, wouldn't have found a way to keep you around." That cut deep. "He was right. If I were that valuable, they'd have kept me around." He finally asked: who's the common element? "It's me." The gap wasn't technical skills or ability to drive projects. It was playing well with others. He dove into the psychology of work—motivation, asking questions, drawing people in—and realized his default as an engineer was to make statements ("the answer is this"), not to ask. Once he treated soft skills as a learnable puzzle, he was hooked.

**The two mechanisms behind his firings—and how not to get "office-politicked"**

He identifies two self-inflicted dynamics. First, when you're loud and abrasive, people who disagree stop telling you—they go behind your back instead. He calls this being **strategically annoying without crossing into shutdown territory**: push hard enough to have an opinion and fight for it, but not so hard that people stop talking *to* you and start talking *about* you. Second, he built zero alliances. When person A disliked him, persons B, C, and D didn't have enough relationship with him to tip him off; they just nodded along. "I literally dug my own grave." The lesson: being right is not enough. Relationships and how others *feel* about the solution often outweigh technical correctness—"people will pick a worse solution that feels more comfortable. That happened all the time."

**Joining Amazon via Barnes & Noble competitive research**

His window into Amazon came earlier, while at Lycos. Lycos partnered with Barnes & Noble, which was losing badly to Amazon online, and his job was to figure out why. He talked to Barnes & Noble people, saw how far ahead Amazon was, and was left thinking the physical-world competitor was hopeless. When an Amazon recruiter later reached out, he could speak from that lens—he'd already studied them as a competitor and been impressed. Amazon had IPO'd by then and had about 10,000 people, which to someone used to "firms of dozens or hundreds" felt massive and stable.

**The director promotion: an outsized event, a high-risk bet, and a politely implied ultimatum**

He describes two promo archetypes: the **lifetime achievement award** (grinding away until the process eventually approves you) versus the **outsized event**. His director promotion came from an outsized event—the TiVo partnership for Amazon Video. His management disagreed with doing the project, but they'd told him to show he could lead. So he told his SVP: "Unless you order me not to do this, I'm gonna do it." The SVP essentially replied, "Great, hang yourself." The project succeeded, and the promo packet's key line became: "without Ethan, we wouldn't have had TiVo."

The other lever was his departing VP. Evans approached him with carefully chosen words—a technique he'd honed since the firings: "My career is very important to me, and I need to know if it's as important to Amazon, because if it's not as important to Amazon as it is to me, I have to think about that." He never said "promote me or I quit." He implied it in a way nobody could accuse him of threatening. It crystallized a philosophy: **you can find polite and civil ways to raise any topic**. That boss wouldn't have acted without the squeeze. He was "up or out."

Ryan notes how subtle that phrasing is, and Evans broadens the point: as a leader, when you have two roughly equal candidates but only one promo slot, the person agitating and implying they might leave tends to get it—not because the manager is evil, but because they're doing triage. "I'm going to move up this person and keep them, even though I'm a little bit being held hostage, or move up this person and lose the other one." He's willing to say what most leaders won't: this is the reality.

**The VP promotion: a 2.5-year, stakeholder-aligned process and the magic loop**

By the time he targeted VP, he truly understood how Amazon worked. He had a stable VP for two and a half to three years and good timing (his VP was reorged six months after the promo—a small delay would have meant rebuilding trust with a new boss). He lined up stakeholders deliberately.

The engine was what he calls the **magic loop**: "Working with your leader to figure out what they need you to do and then form a partnership that amounts to, I'll do everything you need done. You do one critical thing—make sure I'm rewarded for it." His VP eventually came to him and said, "I'm going to try and get your VP promotion through this cycle. I'm going to do everything I can. I can't promise you anything." After two and a half years, the VP was ready to put his own credibility on the line.

He pushes back on the belief that managers withhold promotions. "Speaking as vice president, I want all the directors I can have. The better my team is, the easier my job is. I want high performers. I want you to be good enough. But I can't do that for you. People say, why didn't you promote me? And I'm like, you did not promote yourself."

**The promotion failure that cost a star engineer a year**

Early at Amazon, he made a good-faith deal with a young engineer: help ship the first version of the product, and Evans would get him promoted from SDE1 to SDE2. Evans had come from startups where you just went to your boss and said "promote this person" and it happened. He didn't know Amazon had twice-a-year cycles with documents and deadlines. The project launch was messy, the cycle came and went, and the engineer asked, "Where's my promotion?" Evans had to admit he'd missed the window. The engineer left for another big tech company, effectively losing a year.

His framing: it was his ignorance as a new-to-Amazon manager, but the lesson for the individual contributor is **know the process yourself**, because *you* are the pig in the ham-and-eggs breakfast—you're committed, the manager is only involved. "Ask: by September 15th are you going to have a document done? And if they say, why does September 15th matter? That's a warning sign." Careers are 20–30 years; a lost year isn't fatal. "He moved on and was successful. I've moved on and been successful. You can make that change."

**The Twitch integration: giving up 800 people to practice influence without authority**

After becoming a VP running Amazon's Appstore with a team of 800, he purposefully sought the role of integration liaison for the $970M Twitch acquisition. It was a deliberate soft-skills challenge: he had a goal (help Twitch integrate and move toward profitability) but zero authority. He couldn't give orders to CEO Emmett Shear—who was newly wealthy, 15–16 years younger, running a business for Gen Z, and Evans was in his late forties. "How am I going to influence him when I'm neither his demo nor his boss?"

The biggest gap he found: Twitch was a classic VC-funded company bleeding cash, and shear and some of his team literally didn't see that as a problem. "Now that Amazon's bought us, you guys have lots of money. Why do we need to worry about profitability?" Meanwhile, Andy Jassy wanted profitability soon. Evans was trapped between a team that thought the money tap was infinite and a management chain that wanted results. The second gap he watched shear navigate painfully: not all early employees scale. shear had to face replacing or leveling friends, some of whom quit angry.

**Jeff Bezos vs. Andy Jassy: founder conviction vs. paid-leader partnership**

Having spent roughly 50 meetings with each, Evans' biggest differentiator is that Bezos is the founder and therefore can treat the company as his toy. He recounts a budget discussion where the CFO tried to pump the brakes on spending: "Jeff, we only have so much money in the bank." Bezos replied, "Tom, how much is that? Because I might want to spend it." The dynamic was: "Thank you, your job is to count the pennies and my job is to allocate them. Don't confuse your role and my role." Bezos metaphorically could "take the whole company to Vegas and bet it on black."

Jassy, by contrast, is a more classic business leader. "It's not his toy. He is the paid leader. No matter how much I respect Andy, that's hard to get over." For Evans personally, Bezos felt emotionally behind him and enthusiastic—"okay, I'm on board. Let's do it. We'll do it together." Jassy felt more like, "okay, it's your plan. You're on the hook now. Let me know when it's done." He acknowledges other executives have had different experiences, but for his personality, the founder energy was more inspiring.

**VP performance reviews when the business is failing (but hitting its goals)**

"The year I'm going to hit every goal and it's going to be meaningless" was his premonition about the Appstore—a business Amazon shut down externally a decade later. Prime Gaming, which he helped launch, likewise hit interim targets but never approached Bezos' original vision of competing with Tencent. "You can get a good review for ticking all the boxes and hitting all these interim goals and not really be on track for doing anything significant." Big companies confuse goal-hitting with real value creation.

There's a tension: for your review and pay, you want goals you know you can nail; for the good of the company, you want goals you have no idea how to accomplish. He fought to be measured on things he could deliver, even though the real aspiration—compete with Google Play as a sideloaded store on Android—was arguably impossible. "It turns out maybe they were impossible."

**Stack ranking, unregretted attrition, and the PIP trap**

Amazon runs an explicit "unregretted attrition" target, typically 5–7% per year. A decade ago they experimentally removed it. The result: "Managers immediately stopped having hard conversations." Without the gun to their head, it was easier to focus on good performers and let the problem person linger—especially if that person was well-liked, brought donuts, organized parties. Performance-managing them out meant looking like the bad guy. So Amazon reinstated the target and has kept it ever since.

On PIPs: "I think most PIPs are a combination of dishonest and psychologically unrealistic." Once a manager decides to put you on the list, they've already mentally moved to "we have to get rid of this person." Cognitive bias becomes self-fulfilling. He's coached half a dozen people on PIPs at Amazon; all were fired. He's tracked others he *didn't* coach; they were fired too. His advice now is blunt: "Spend that time on the PIP looking for your next job, because you're going to be fired." Even if a manager starts with honest intentions, the HR business partner and the manager's own boss are constantly asking "when are we going to exit them? I need them on my quota." Taking someone *off* a PIP means putting someone else *on*, and the lowest performer is already on it.

**The manager holds the gun (and gets to shoot first)**

His controversial statement: "A manager can fire anyone they want, and HR is not there to protect you." The nuance is, they can't do it *to everyone* without getting caught via turnover patterns, but for any single employee, the system is stacked. It's partly about who strikes first. By the time an employee goes to HR claiming unfairness, the manager has already put them on the list and told a narrative. HR hears "my boss hates me and it's unfair" every day from legitimately bad performers, so a genuine case sounds identical.

More insidiously, identical performance data can be spun either way. The same 700 pull requests in a quarter can be narrated as "Ryan uplevels the whole team through insightful reviews" or "Ryan doesn't contribute much—look how little he's actually written himself, just nitpicking and churning." Nothing about software engineering performance is so objective that a clever manager can't reframe it. "They get to make the preemptive strike. They get to tell the story about why that person's a problem before that person ever knows it's happening."

His practical takeaway: if you have a vindictive boss, don't get in a knife fight when they're holding a gun. "Figure out what can allow you to make friends with them or find a different manager. Don't think that somehow HR is going to come investigate and rescue you. That just isn't what they do."

**Advice to his younger self: ride the escalator, build the relationships**

The thing he'd keep: "always prefer high growth." His career ladder was actually an escalator—Amazon grew 100-fold in headcount and ~80x in revenue while he was there. "The escalator went up and I rode it. I also climbed." The thing he'd change: wake up far sooner to the fact that jobs are with other humans. Be an expert, be right, but "build the skills to have the relationships, make the friends, get to know lots of people." You don't need to be an extrovert—LinkedIn and similar tools let you build reputation from your keyboard. Amazon called *him*; he didn't cold-apply. "Build that reputation." And take more risks: "If I had my career to do over again, I'd probably take a lot more risk. I could have gone further."
