# VILA: On Pre-training for Visual Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2312.07533](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07533)

## 🎯 Pitch

Freezing the language model during visual pre-training kills its ability to learn from examples in context—even though zero-shot accuracy looks fine. Interleaving images and text during pre-training, rather than using separate image–text pairs, is what unlocks this in-context learning capacity. Simply re-blending text-only instruction data during fine-tuning not only repairs degraded text skills but also improves vision tasks.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies the design options for visual language model pre-training by augmenting a pretrained LLM with visual inputs, using the auto-regressive VLM architecture with Llama-2 backbones and evaluating on benchmarks including OKVQA, TextVQA, and downstream suites like MMBench and LLaVA-Bench. The authors introduce three main findings: **freezing LLMs during pre-training** achieves decent zero-shot performance but destroys in-context learning (4-shot accuracy drops substantially), **interleaved pre-training data** (MMC4, where images appear inline with text segments rather than as isolated image-text pairs) is essential for preserving text-only capabilities and enabling visual in-context learning, and **re-blending text-only instruction data into SFT** (joint SFT) not only recovers the ~5% MMLU degradation from pre-training but also boosts VLM task accuracy. The resulting model family, VILA, consistently outperforms LLaVA-1.5 across 12 benchmarks without architectural bells and whistles—for instance, VILA-7B surpasses LLaVA-1.5 13B on VisWiz (57.8 vs. 53.6) and TextVQA (64.4 vs. 61.3)—establishing that visual language pre-training at scale yields significant downstream gains even when the SFT data is high quality, though the hardest text-only degradation is only fully recovered when joint SFT is applied.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don't Know How to Pre-Train Visual Language Models Properly

The fundamental question this paper tackles is: **when you take a pre-trained large language model and want to give it the ability to understand images, what is the right way to train the model so it learns to fuse vision and language?** This matters because the recent explosion of visual language models (VLMs)—models like LLaVA, InstructBLIP, and GPT-4V that can answer questions about images, follow visual instructions, and reason across modalities—has largely been driven by advances in *instruction tuning*: the final supervised fine-tuning stage where the model learns to follow human directives about visual content. But the stage that comes before instruction tuning—**visual language pre-training**, where the model first learns how visual tokens and text tokens relate to each other at scale—has received comparatively little systematic study.

This gap is significant for several reasons the authors identify throughout the paper:

- **The pre-training stage is the most expensive part of training.** As reported in Appendix B, for the 7B model, projector initialization takes 4 hours, SFT takes 6 hours, but **visual language pre-training takes 30 hours**—roughly 75% of total training time. Making the wrong choice about what data to use or whether to update the LLM during this stage wastes enormous computational resources.

- **The design choices during pre-training determine which LLM capabilities survive.** An LLM comes with powerful properties—in-context learning, chain-of-thought reasoning, instruction following, world knowledge—acquired through massive text-only pre-training. Augmenting it with visual inputs can either preserve or destroy these capabilities depending on how pre-training is done. If pre-training causes catastrophic forgetting of text capabilities, the resulting VLM is strictly worse than the LLM it started from on text-only tasks.

- **There is a genuine tension between visual language alignment and text capability preservation.** Teaching an LLM to process visual tokens inevitably modifies its internal representations. The question is whether this modification can be done in a way that *adds* vision without *subtracting* language—and the paper shows that different pre-training recipes produce dramatically different outcomes on this front.

### Conflicting Practices in Prior Work

The paper is motivated by the observation that prior VLM work has made **radically different choices** about pre-training without any head-to-head comparison or principled understanding of the tradeoffs. These differences cluster around three key axes:

**Axis 1: Whether to freeze or update the LLM during pre-training.**

Two distinct schools of thought had emerged in the literature:

- **Frozen LLM approaches**: Models like Flamingo (Alayrac et al., 2022), BLIP-2 (Li et al., 2023), InstructBLIP (Dai et al., 2023), and CogVLM (Wang et al., 2023) keep the base LLM's weights **completely frozen** during vision-language training. Instead, they introduce new trainable components—cross-attention layers in Flamingo, Q-Former modules in BLIP-2, visual experts in CogVLM—that inject visual information into the frozen LLM. The appeal is obvious: the LLM's text capabilities cannot degrade if its weights never change, and the new components can be thrown away at deployment time for text-only tasks.

- **LLM fine-tuning approaches**: Models like PaLM-E (Driess et al., 2023), PaLI-X (Chen et al., 2023), and LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023) **update the LLM's weights** during vision-language training, treating visual tokens as just another input modality that the model must learn to process. The appeal here is that the LLM's full representational capacity can be brought to bear on visual reasoning, potentially enabling deeper cross-modal understanding.

The problem is that **both camps had evidence supporting their position** but no direct comparison existed. Frozen-LLM advocates pointed to catastrophic forgetting: if you update the LLM, text capabilities degrade. Fine-tuning advocates pointed to superior visual reasoning: frozen LLMs can only achieve "shallow alignment" through lightweight adapters. Neither side had systematically studied the tradeoff under controlled conditions—same base LLM, same training data, same evaluation.

**Axis 2: What corpus to use for pre-training.**

Prior work had converged on two data types with very different properties:

- **Image-text pairs** (e.g., LAION-5B, COYO-700M): Billions of images each paired with a single caption, typically alt-text from the web. This was the dominant choice for most VLMs (LLaVA, BLIP-2, InstructBLIP) because of sheer scale and availability. However, the captions in these datasets tend to be very short (COYO averages 22.7 tokens per image per Table 2) and often poorly aligned with the image content, leading to a distribution very different from the paragraph-length text the LLM was originally trained on.

- **Interleaved image-text corpora** (e.g., MMC4): Documents where images appear naturally interspersed with text segments, similar to a web article with embedded figures. This was used by Flamingo and OpenFlamingo but had not been widely adopted in auto-regressive VLMs. The text surrounding images in MMC4 averages 122.5 tokens per image (Table 2)—much closer to the text-only pre-training distribution—but image-text correspondence is weaker: the text is often only loosely related to the adjacent images (Figure 4 shows an example where only some text segments are meaningfully informed by the images).

The choice between these data sources is not obvious a priori. Image-text pairs provide stronger per-image supervision (every token is about the image), but their text distribution is alien to the LLM. Interleaved data is distributionally similar to the LLM's pre-training text but provides weaker image-text alignment signal. No prior work had compared these under controlled settings or studied their interaction with the decision to freeze or update the LLM.

**Axis 3: Whether to include text-only data during instruction tuning.**

Most VLM instruction tuning recipes (LLaVA, InstructBLIP) used **exclusively visual language data** during SFT—the model sees only image+text inputs and learns to answer visual questions, generate captions, and follow visual instructions. The assumption was that the LLM's text capabilities, acquired during text pre-training, would persist unchanged. However, as the paper documents, visual language pre-training causes a **~5% drop in MMLU accuracy** even under the best pre-training configuration (Table 3, MMC4: 40.7% vs. original Llama-2: 46.0%), and naive SFT on visual-only data does not recover this.

The question of whether to add text-only instruction data back into the SFT mixture had not been studied. Doing so seems counterintuitive—text-only data contains no images, so why would it help a VLM?—but the paper's hypothesis (Section 3.3) that it improves general instruction-following capability suggests a potential benefit that had been overlooked.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations in how prior work addressed these design choices:

**No systematic ablation across the decision axes.** Each prior VLM paper made a set of choices (freeze vs. update, pairs vs. interleaved, visual-only vs. joint SFT) and reported results. But because different papers used different base LLMs, different SFT data, different evaluation protocols, and different scales, it was impossible to tell **which choice caused which effect**. A model using frozen LLM + interleaved data might outperform one using fine-tuned LLM + image-text pairs, but was the difference due to the data, the freezing, or something else entirely? The field lacked a controlled, systematic study that varied one factor at a time.

**The in-context learning dimension was largely ignored in VLM evaluation.** Most VLM benchmarks evaluate zero-shot performance—the model sees one image and one question and must answer. But a key property of LLMs is their ability to learn from a few examples provided in the prompt (in-context learning, or few-shot learning). If VLM pre-training destroys this capability, the resulting model is qualitatively less flexible. The paper specifically designs its ablation evaluation (Tables 1, 3) to measure both 0-shot and 4-shot performance, revealing that some pre-training choices that look equivalent on 0-shot accuracy produce dramatically different 4-shot behavior. Prior work had not made this distinction.

**The relationship between pre-training data and text-only forgetting was unexplored.** It was known that visual language training could cause catastrophic forgetting of text capabilities, but *why* and *under what conditions* was not understood. The paper's finding that image-text pair pre-training causes a massive 17.2% MMLU drop while interleaved pre-training causes only a 5.3% drop (Table 3) provides the first mechanistic explanation: it's not visual input per se that causes forgetting, but the **distribution shift in the text data** seen during pre-training. Short alt-text captions are so different from the LLM's original training text that the model's language modeling capabilities collapse.

**The two-stage nature of VLM training was not exploited for text recovery.** Most work treated text capability preservation and visual capability acquisition as a zero-sum tradeoff—either you froze the LLM and preserved text capabilities at the cost of weaker vision, or you fine-tuned and accepted text degradation. The paper's insight that text capabilities are "temporarily hidden, but not forgotten" (Section 3.3) and can be recovered through joint SFT—even with far less text data than the original pre-training corpus—reframes the problem. The degradation is not permanent; it's more like text capabilities are overshadowed by the new visual task. Re-blending text instruction data during SFT serves as a "reminder" that restores the original capability.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper explicitly frames itself as filling the **systematic ablation gap** in VLM pre-training design. Rather than proposing a new architecture or a new training objective, the paper's contribution is **empirical clarity**: take a fixed base LLM (Llama-2-7B), a fixed architecture (auto-regressive VLM with linear projector), and a fixed evaluation protocol, then vary the pre-training design choices one at a time and measure the consequences.

The paper's positioning relative to existing work is clearest in the introduction's explicit framing:

> "We aim to answer 'How do various design choices in visual language model pre-training impact the downstream performance?'"

This is not a modeling contribution—it's a **training recipe contribution**. The paper follows the established pre-training + SFT pipeline (which it acknowledges as standard practice, citing Liu et al. 2023, Dai et al. 2023, and Awadalla et al. 2023) but provides the first holistic comparison of the design levers within that pipeline.

The paper's relationship to specific prior works breaks down as follows:

**Relative to LLaVA/LLaVA-1.5 (Liu et al., 2023, 2024):** LLaVA is the closest baseline and the primary comparison point. LLaVA-1.5 uses a very minimal pre-training stage (558K images, roughly 0.6M) focused only on projector initialization, followed by visual instruction tuning on 665K examples. The paper argues that this under-exploits pre-training—by scaling pre-training to 50M images with interleaved data and properly unfreezing the LLM, VILA achieves consistent improvements over LLaVA-1.5 even when using the same SFT data (Table 5). The comparison is deliberately head-to-head: same prompts, same base LLM (Vicuna-1.5 is based on Llama-2), isolating the effect of pre-training.

**Relative to Flamingo-style models (Alayrac et al., 2022; Awadalla et al., 2023):** Flamingo established the importance of interleaved pre-training data but used a frozen-LLM, cross-attention architecture. The paper's finding that interleaved data is equally important for auto-regressive VLMs (Table 3, MMC4 vs. COYO) validates and extends Flamingo's insight to a different architecture family. However, the paper also shows that the frozen-LLM approach—while preserving text capabilities perfectly—leaves visual in-context learning performance on the table (Table 1, setting b vs. c), challenging a key design choice in the Flamingo lineage.

**Relative to CogVLM (Wang et al., 2023):** CogVLM proposes adding visual experts (an MoE-style gating mechanism) to process visual tokens while keeping the base LLM frozen. The paper directly compares this approach to full LLM fine-tuning in Table 8, finding that fine-tuning outperforms visual experts on both 0-shot and 4-shot VLM accuracy while requiring only 1× the parameters (vs. 1.9× for the expert approach). This positions the paper as arguing that **architectural complexity to preserve text capabilities is unnecessary** if proper joint SFT is used for recovery.

**Relative to Qwen-VL (Bai et al., 2023):** Qwen-VL uses a much larger pre-training corpus (1.4B images) and achieves strong results, but its design choices (e.g., 448×448 resolution, 50M SFT examples) are conflated with its expensive pre-training. The paper's controlled ablation decouples dataset size from dataset *type*, showing that 50M images with the right composition can yield substantial gains.

**Relative to InstructBLIP (Dai et al., 2023):** InstructBLIP freezes the LLM and uses a Q-Former to bridge modalities, representing the frozen-LLM school. The paper's Table 1 and Figure 3 provide evidence that this approach—while computationally appealing—results in "shallow alignment" where visual and textual representations don't mix at deep layers of the network, limiting in-context learning and out-of-distribution generalization. This directly challenges InstructBLIP's design philosophy.

The paper ultimately positions VILA not as a competitor to any single prior model but as a **demonstration that the right pre-training recipe**—specifically: update the LLM, use interleaved data, and apply joint SFT—can extract substantially more performance from the same architecture and base LLM than had been previously achieved. The framing in the abstract is telling:

> "With an enhanced pre-training recipe we build VILA, a Visual Language model family that consistently outperforms the state-of-the-art models, e.g., LLaVA-1.5, across main benchmarks without bells and whistles."

The phrase "without bells and whistles" signals that the gains come from better training methodology, not from architectural novelty, larger models, or more expensive data pipelines. This is a deliberate positioning: the paper argues that the field has over-invested in instruction tuning innovations and under-invested in understanding pre-training fundamentals, and that correcting this imbalance yields substantial practical benefits.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The system being built is a **visual language model**—a neural network that takes an image and a text question as input, and produces a text answer—by extending an existing large language model to process visual tokens alongside text tokens. The paper solves the problem of **how to optimally train such a model during the pre-training phase**: given a pre-trained LLM and a pre-trained vision encoder, what data should you use to teach them to work together, should you update the LLM's weights or keep them frozen, and how do you prevent the model from forgetting its original text-only capabilities while learning vision?

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components connected in a feedforward pipeline:

1. **Vision Encoder** (a frozen CLIP-L model) — converts a raw image into a grid of visual feature vectors (patch embeddings).
2. **Projector** (a trainable linear layer or Transformer block) — maps visual feature vectors from the CLIP embedding space into the LLM's token embedding space, producing "visual tokens" that look like text tokens to the LLM.
3. **Large Language Model** (Llama-2, 7B or 13B parameters) — the core autoregressive transformer that processes a sequence containing both visual tokens and text tokens, predicting the next text token.
4. **Training data pipeline** — provides the image-text corpora (either interleaved MMC4 documents or image-text pairs from COYO) and the supervised fine-tuning mixture (visual instruction data plus optional text-only instruction data).

Information flows as follows: an image enters the vision encoder → CLIP produces patch embeddings → the projector maps these to the LLM's embedding dimension and concatenates them with text token embeddings → the augmented sequence is fed into the LLM → the LLM autoregressively generates text output tokens. During training, the LLM and projector weights are updated (optionally the LLM can be frozen), while the vision encoder remains frozen throughout.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the auto-regressive VLM architecture and its three subcomponents (vision encoder, projector, LLM), because the training decisions that follow depend on understanding what each part does and whether it is trainable.
- **Second**, the three training stages (projector initialization, visual language pre-training, instruction tuning), since the paper's contributions are organized around ablating design choices at each stage.
- **Third**, the core finding about why **updating the LLM during pre-training is necessary** for in-context learning—including the deep embedding alignment hypothesis and the Chamfer distance measurement—because this establishes the paper's first major design recommendation.
- **Fourth**, the data ablation comparing interleaved corpora (MMC4) versus image-text pairs (COYO) for pre-training, since this reveals the catastrophic forgetting mechanism and motivates the second major recommendation.
- **Fifth**, the joint SFT procedure that recovers text-only performance, since it addresses the residual degradation from pre-training and produces the third major recommendation.
- **Sixth**, the resolution and projector design experiments (Section 4.4) and the comparison to frozen-LLM alternatives (visual experts, LoRA), since these validate the core decisions and rule out simpler approaches.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that the design choices made during visual language pre-training—whether to update the LLM, what data format to use, and whether to blend text-only data during SFT—are the dominant factors determining downstream VLM performance, and that a specific combination of these choices (update LLM + interleaved data + joint SFT) yields substantial and consistent improvements over previous approaches.

---

#### The Auto-Regressive VLM Architecture

The paper studies a specific family of VLMs: **auto-regressive visual language models** that treat visual input as a foreign language by tokenizing images and feeding them as input tokens to a standard autoregressive LLM. This is contrasted with the cross-attention-based family (Flamingo-style) where visual information is injected via separate cross-attention layers into a frozen LLM. The auto-regressive design is chosen "due to its flexibility and popularity" (Section 2)—it can handle arbitrary interleaved image-text inputs without architectural modification and is a natural extension of text-only LLMs.

The architecture consists of three components, each with a distinct role:

**Component 1: The Visual Encoder.** A frozen CLIP-L model (specifically OpenAI's CLIP ViT-L/14 at either $224 \times 224$ or $336 \times 336$ resolution) processes the raw image and outputs a grid of patch-level feature vectors. The encoder is pre-trained via contrastive learning on image-text pairs and is **never updated** during VLM training. At $224 \times 224$ resolution, this produces $16 \times 16 = 256$ patch tokens (plus a CLS token, though the paper's token counts suggest the CLS token is included or the count is calculated differently; at $336 \times 336$, it produces $24 \times 24 = 576$ patch tokens). The choice of CLIP-L follows standard practice in the field (used by LLaVA, BLIP-2, and others), ensuring comparability.

**Component 2: The Projector.** This is the bridge between modalities. The CLIP encoder outputs vectors in its own embedding space (dimension determined by CLIP-L, typically 768 or 1024). The LLM expects input embeddings in its token embedding space (4096 dimensions for Llama-2-7B). The projector maps from the former to the latter. The paper compares two projector designs:

- **Linear projector:** A single learned linear transformation matrix `$W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{CLIP}} \times d_{\text{LLM}}}$`. This is the simplest possible mapping—each visual patch embedding is independently projected to the LLM's embedding space via matrix multiplication. There are no interactions between patch embeddings during projection.

- **Transformer projector:** A small Transformer block (or potentially multiple blocks, though the paper specifies "a Transformer block" in singular) that applies self-attention across the visual patch embeddings before projecting them. This allows the projector to model spatial relationships between patches and produce context-aware visual tokens, at the cost of additional parameters and computation.

The paper also experiments with a **downsample projector** variant (Section 4.4): before the linear projection, every $2 \times 2$ patch of visual tokens is concatenated into a single token, reducing the token count by a factor of 4 (from 576 to 144 at $336 \times 336$ resolution). This is a simple form of token compression.

**Component 3: The Large Language Model.** The base model is Llama-2 (7B or 13B parameters), an auto-regressive decoder-only transformer pre-trained on a large text corpus. The LLM's tokenizer has a fixed vocabulary of text tokens; visual tokens are prepended to the text token sequence as a special prefix. The LLM then performs standard autoregressive next-token prediction over the combined sequence—it attends to both visual tokens and preceding text tokens when predicting the next text token. The visual tokens act as a form of "soft prompt" that conditions the LLM's generation on image content.

**Why this architecture over alternatives?** The cross-attention-based design (Flamingo, InstructBLIP) keeps the LLM frozen and injects visual features through new cross-attention layers interleaved with the LLM's self-attention layers. This perfectly preserves text capabilities since the LLM weights never change, but it means the LLM's internal representations never adapt to incorporate visual information—visual and textual processing remain segregated. The auto-regressive design, by treating visual tokens as input tokens, forces the LLM to learn to attend to and reason about visual information using its existing self-attention mechanisms. The tradeoff is that updating the LLM risks degrading text capabilities, which the paper addresses through data selection and joint SFT.

#### The Three-Stage Training Protocol

The paper follows a standard three-stage training pipeline that is common in the VLM literature, but systematically ablates choices at each stage. The stages are:

**Stage 0: Projector Initialization.** The LLM and vision encoder are already pre-trained (Llama-2 and CLIP respectively), but the projector is randomly initialized. If you were to start full VLM pre-training immediately, the projector would produce random visual embeddings that look like noise to the LLM, causing unstable gradients and slow convergence. To avoid this, the projector is first trained in isolation: the LLM and vision encoder are frozen, and only the projector is updated on image-caption pairs. The objective is standard autoregressive language modeling—the model is given an image and must generate the corresponding caption, with the LLM providing the language modeling head but its weights not being updated. This stage teaches the projector to produce visual embeddings that the LLM can interpret as meaningful tokens, "warming up" the connection before full training begins.

The paper does not ablate this stage or provide hyperparameters for it (aside from noting it takes 4 GPU hours on 16 A100 nodes for the 7B model, per Appendix B), treating it as a necessary prerequisite established by prior work (citing Li et al. 2023, Liu et al. 2023, Dai et al. 2023).

**Stage 1: Visual Language Pre-Training.** This is the main stage the paper studies. Both the LLM and projector are updated (or the LLM is frozen, in some ablation settings) on a large corpus of image-text data. The training objective is the same autoregressive language modeling loss: given an image (or multiple images) and a prefix of text tokens, predict the next text token. The model sees the image tokens as context and learns to condition its text generation on visual information.

The key design choices ablated at this stage are:
- Whether to update the LLM or freeze it (Table 1, settings b vs. c)
- What type of pre-training corpus to use: interleaved (MMC4), image-text pairs (COYO), or a blend (Table 3)
- Projector capacity: linear vs. Transformer block (Table 1, settings c vs. d)

For the final VILA models (Section 4.1), the pre-training corpus is approximately 50M images, sampled roughly 1:1 between MMC4 interleaved data and COYO image-text pairs, using a linear projector with the LLM fully updated.

**Stage 2: Visual Instruction Tuning (SFT).** The pre-trained VLM is further fine-tuned on instruction-formatted data—examples where the input is an image plus a natural language instruction (e.g., "What is the animal in this image?"), and the target output is the answer. The paper converts existing visual language datasets into a FLAN-style format (Wei et al., 2021) where each dataset has a specific prompt template (following the approach of InstructBLIP).

For ablation experiments (Tables 1, 3, 4), the SFT data is an in-house blend of 18 visual language datasets (detailed in Appendix A, Table 10) covering captioning, reasoning, translation, and VQA tasks. The authors note this blend is "mostly VQA-alike"—a detail that matters for interpreting the 4-shot evaluation gaps on captioning datasets, which are out-of-distribution relative to this SFT mixture.

For the final VILA models (Table 5), the SFT blend is expanded to include the LLaVA-1.5 SFT dataset (Liu et al., 2024), which adds higher-quality prompts, reference-based annotations (RefCOCO), and better diversity—approximately 1M examples total.

The key design choice ablated at this stage is whether to include **text-only instruction data** (1M examples sampled from FLAN, Chung et al., 2022) alongside the visual instruction data—what the paper terms "joint SFT" (Table 4).

---

#### The Core Finding: Why Updating the LLM During Pre-Training Is Essential

The paper's first major ablation (Table 1) compares four configurations that differ in whether the LLM is updated during pre-training and SFT, and what projector design is used. All configurations use the MMC4-core dataset for pre-training (approximately 25M images) and the in-house SFT blend. The evaluation measures both 0-shot and 4-shot performance on four tasks (OKVQA, TextVQA for VQA accuracy; COCO, Flickr for captioning CIDEr).

**Setting (a): Frozen LLM in both pre-training and SFT, Transformer projector.** Only the projector is trained; the LLM never sees a gradient. Results: 0-shot average 13.4%, 4-shot average 37.5%. The 4-shot number is higher but the absolute 0-shot performance is extremely poor. This setting demonstrates that training only the projector—even a high-capacity Transformer—is insufficient. The LLM's frozen representations cannot be effectively "prompted" by visual tokens alone; the projector cannot bridge the modality gap well enough without the LLM adapting to the new input modality.

**Setting (b): Frozen LLM during pre-training, updated during SFT, Transformer projector.** The LLM is frozen during pre-training but fine-tuned during instruction tuning. Results: 0-shot 66.8%, 4-shot 57.6%. Critically, the 4-shot accuracy is **lower** than the 0-shot accuracy—the model performs *worse* when given more examples. This is the opposite of what in-context learning should produce. The paper interprets this as evidence that freezing the LLM during pre-training prevents deep cross-modal alignment from forming, and the subsequent SFT fine-tuning cannot recover it. The model learns to answer questions from single examples (0-shot) but cannot effectively integrate multiple demonstrations.

**Setting (c): Updated LLM in both pre-training and SFT, Transformer projector.** The LLM is fully updated throughout. Results: 0-shot 66.8% (identical to setting b), 4-shot 68.8% (substantially higher than setting b). The 4-shot accuracy now **exceeds** 0-shot, restoring the in-context learning behavior expected from an LLM. The identical 0-shot performance between settings b and c is a critical result: if researchers only evaluate 0-shot (as most VLM benchmarks do), they would conclude that freezing the LLM during pre-training has no cost. The 4-shot evaluation reveals the hidden degradation.

**Setting (d): Updated LLM in both pre-training and SFT, Linear projector.** Results: 0-shot 68.7%, 4-shot 70.9%. Both metrics improve over the Transformer projector (setting c). The paper hypothesizes that "a simpler projector forces the LLM to learn more on handling visual inputs, leading to better generalization." The logic is that with a high-capacity projector (Transformer), the projector can do sophisticated preprocessing of visual features before passing them to the LLM, reducing the pressure on the LLM to adapt its internal representations. With a linear projector, the visual tokens are a simpler, less-processed projection of CLIP features, and the LLM must learn to extract relevant information from them using its own transformer layers—which results in deeper, more robust visual-textual alignment.

The final VILA models use configuration (d): linear projector, LLM updated throughout both pre-training and SFT.

#### The Deep Embedding Alignment Hypothesis

To explain **why** updating the LLM produces better in-context learning, the paper proposes and tests the **deep embedding alignment hypothesis**: for the VLM to inherit LLM properties like in-context learning, the visual and text token representations must be well-aligned **in the deeper layers** of the LLM, not just at the input embedding level. If visual tokens enter the LLM but their representations remain segregated from text token representations in the intermediate and later transformer layers, the model cannot fluidly combine visual and textual information—it treats them as separate modalities processed in parallel rather than an integrated signal.

**Measurement methodology.** The paper measures alignment by computing the **Chamfer distance** (specifically, pairwise cosine similarity) between visual token embeddings and text token embeddings at each transformer layer. Let `$V_\ell = \{v_1^\ell, v_2^\ell, ..., v_n^\ell\}$` be the set of visual token representations at layer `$\ell$` and `$T_\ell = \{t_1^\ell, t_2^\ell, ..., t_m^\ell\}$` be the set of text token representations at layer `$\ell$`. The paper computes:

$$\text{similarity}_\ell = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \max_{j} \frac{v_i^\ell \cdot t_j^\ell}{\|v_i^\ell\| \|t_j^\ell\|}$$

where `$v_i^\ell$` is the i-th visual token vector at layer `$\ell$`, `$t_j^\ell$` is the j-th text token vector at layer `$\ell$`, `$\cdot$` denotes dot product, `$\|\cdot\|$` denotes L2 norm, and `$n$` and `$m$` are the numbers of visual and text tokens respectively.

**What it computes:** for each visual token, the maximum cosine similarity to any text token in the same layer. This measures how "close" the visual representations are to the nearest text representation—if visual tokens occupy the same region of the representational space as text tokens, the cosine similarity will be high; if they occupy disjoint regions, it will be low. The average is taken over all visual tokens to produce a single alignment score per layer.

**Why this form:** cosine similarity is chosen to "exclude the effect of magnitude" (Section 3.1)—the authors want to measure directional alignment, not whether visual and text tokens have similar vector norms. The max operation over text tokens ensures that the measure captures whether *any* text representation is close to a given visual token, rather than requiring alignment with a specific text token. This is appropriate because the claim is about representational overlap generally, not token-specific alignment.

**Results (Figure 3).** The line plot shows alignment scores across the ~32 transformer layers of Llama-2-7B for three configurations (b, c, and d from Table 1):

- In setting (b) (frozen LLM during pre-training), alignment is low and flat across layers—visual and text representations never mix.
- In setting (c) (updated LLM, Transformer projector), alignment improves in deeper layers, but modestly.
- In setting (d) (updated LLM, linear projector), alignment is highest in the deepest layers, showing a clear increasing trend with layer depth.

The paper's interpretation: the linear projector forces the LLM to do the work of integrating visual information, which creates deeper and more thorough alignment. The deeper layers of a transformer are where high-level semantic reasoning occurs, so alignment at those depths enables visual in-context learning—the model can reason about visual demonstrations using the same mechanisms it uses for text demonstrations. This is the mechanistic explanation for Table 1's 4-shot results: configuration (d) shows the strongest deep alignment and the highest 4-shot accuracy.

**What this rules out.** The deep embedding alignment hypothesis implies that approaches which keep the LLM frozen and add separate visual processing pathways (cross-attention in Flamingo, Q-Former in BLIP-2, visual experts in CogVLM) will inherently struggle with in-context learning because they never achieve deep representational alignment—visual and textual information are processed in separate "tracks" that only interact at designated interfaces. The paper's Table 8 comparison to visual experts confirms this: even with specialized components, the frozen-LLM approach underperforms simple fine-tuning.

#### The Pre-Training Data Ablation: Interleaved vs. Image-Text Pairs

The paper's second major ablation (Table 3) compares four pre-training data configurations, all using the updated-LLM, linear-projector recipe established in the first ablation, and all evaluated after the same SFT stage (visual-only SFT for this comparison, not joint SFT):

**COYO only (image-text pairs).** Pre-training on 25M image-text pairs from COYO-700M, subsampled to keep the 25M images with highest CLIP similarity between image and caption (detailed in Appendix D). Results: VLM accuracy (average over the 4 evaluation datasets) 51.1% 0-shot, 50.3% 4-shot—note the 4-shot is **lower** than 0-shot. MMLU accuracy drops to 28.8%, a **17.2 percentage point degradation** from the original Llama-2's 46.0%. The model has suffered catastrophic forgetting of text capabilities and cannot do visual in-context learning.

**MMC4-pairs (image-text pairs extracted from interleaved data).** The MMC4 interleaved documents are broken into isolated image-text pairs by keeping only the text segment that is most closely associated with each image (determined by CLIP score matching, following Zhu et al. 2023). For example, a document `<txt1><im1><txt2><txt3><im2><txt4>` becomes two pairs: `<im1><txt2>` and `<im2><txt4>`. Results: 46.4% 0-shot, 44.5% 4-shot, MMLU 32.4% (`$-13.6\%$` degradation). Even worse than COYO on all metrics. This is despite MMC4 text being longer and more natural than COYO alt-text.

**MMC4 (interleaved).** Pre-training on the original interleaved documents, with images appearing inline in their natural context. Results: 68.7% 0-shot, 70.9% 4-shot, MMLU 40.7% (`$-5.3\%$` degradation). Dramatically better than both pair-based configurations, with 4-shot accuracy now exceeding 0-shot (in-context learning is working) and text degradation reduced by more than 3× compared to COYO.

**MMC4 + COYO blend.** Pre-training on both MMC4 interleaved data and COYO image-text pairs, sampled to maintain roughly equal numbers of images from each source (50M total images). Results: 69.0% 0-shot, 71.3% 4-shot, MMLU 40.2% (`$-5.8\%$` degradation). Slight improvement in VLM accuracy over MMC4-only, with comparable text degradation. This blend is what the final VILA models use.

**Why interleaved data is essential.** The paper identifies two complementary mechanisms:

**Mechanism 1: Distributional similarity of text.** COYO captions average only 22.7 tokens per image (Table 2)—they are short, telegraphic alt-text snippets. The LLM was pre-trained on full-length documents (C4, Wikipedia, books) with rich paragraph structure and discourse coherence. Training on short captions shifts the model's text distribution dramatically, causing it to "unlearn" how to process longer text—the catastrophic forgetting measured by the 17.2% MMLU drop. MMC4 text averages 122.5 tokens per image and comes from web articles, matching the LLM's pre-training distribution much more closely. But this alone doesn't explain why MMC4-pairs performs even worse than COYO despite having better text distribution—the text in MMC4-pairs is the same text as MMC4, just broken into pairs.

**Mechanism 2: Information content of the language modeling objective.** The key insight comes from examining Figure 5, which plots training loss curves for MMC4 versus MMC4-pairs. The loss when training on interleaved MMC4 is **substantially lower** than when training on MMC4-pairs. Why would the same text produce different loss? Because in MMC4-pairs, the text segments are isolated from their surrounding context—the model only sees the tokens in `<txt2>` when conditioned on `<im2>`, while in full MMC4, the model sees the entire document `<txt1><im1><txt2><txt3><im2><txt4>` and must predict all text tokens. The full document provides much more information for language modeling: the model can use the broader textual context (including text that is not directly associated with any image) to make better predictions. This means the language modeling gradient is richer and more stable in the interleaved setting.

Furthermore, in image-text pairs, every text token is ostensibly "about" the image, which forces the model to try to extract visual information to predict **all** text tokens—including ones that are only weakly related to the image. This creates a noisy training signal: the model receives gradient pressure to use visual features to predict text that isn't actually visually grounded. In interleaved data, the model can learn that some text is image-related and some isn't, developing a more nuanced ability to selectively attend to visual information when it's relevant.

**Why blending pairs with interleaved data helps.** Even though COYO-only pre-training causes catastrophic forgetting, blending COYO with MMC4 (bottom row of Table 3) improves VLM accuracy over MMC4-only without increasing text degradation. The interpretation: COYO provides complementary **visual diversity**—different types of images, different visual concepts—that MMC4's web article images may not cover. The interleaved MMC4 data provides the distributional anchor that prevents catastrophic forgetting, while the COYO pairs expand the visual coverage. The paper notes that the benefit of blending COYO is "more significant with joint SFT" (Table 4), suggesting that joint SFT removes the residual penalty of including pairs in the pre-training mixture.

**The pre-training loss curve evidence (Figure 5).** The training loss when pre-training on MMC4 interleaved data is consistently lower than when pre-training on MMC4-pairs throughout training. This directly supports the claim that "the full text segments provides more information" for language modeling. The loss curve also provides a mechanistic explanation for why MMC4-pairs underperforms COYO despite better text quality: the pair-based format throws away the inter-sentence context that makes the text distribution familiar to the LLM, while simultaneously forcing tighter image-text coupling than the data actually supports. The combination of unfamiliar text distribution and misleading image-text alignment signal produces worse results than either problem alone.

---

#### Joint SFT: Recovering Text Capability and Boosting Vision

The paper's third major finding is that adding text-only instruction data to the SFT mixture (joint SFT) both recovers text-only performance and improves VLM task accuracy. Table 4 compares:

**Visual-only SFT.** After pre-training on MMC4 or MMC4+COYO, fine-tune on only the visual instruction datasets (the in-house blend of 18 datasets, roughly 665K-1M examples). Results (MMC4): VLM 0-shot 68.7%, 4-shot 70.9%, MMLU 40.7%—a 5.3% drop from the original Llama-2 (46.0%). Results (MMC4+COYO): VLM 0-shot 69.0%, 4-shot 71.3%, MMLU 40.2%—similar pattern.

**Joint SFT (visual + text).** After the same pre-training, fine-tune on both the visual instruction datasets and 1M text-only instruction examples sampled from FLAN (Chung et al., 2022). The total SFT data is now roughly 1.7M-2M examples. Results (MMC4): VLM 0-shot 71.0% (`$+2.3\%$` over visual-only), 4-shot 72.1% (`$+1.2\%$`), MMLU 51.4%—**now 0.2% above** the Llama-2+SFT baseline (51.2%). Results (MMC4+COYO): VLM 0-shot 72.3% (`$+3.3\%$`), 4-shot 73.6% (`$+2.3\%$`), MMLU 50.9%.

The key findings from this experiment:

1. **Text-only capabilities are "temporarily hidden, but not forgotten."** The LLM's original text capabilities are not destroyed by visual language pre-training—they are suppressed or overshadowed by the new visual conditioning task. The 5% MMLU drop is not permanent damage; it's more like the model has learned to prioritize visual input processing and needs a "reminder" to re-engage its text reasoning circuits. This is evidenced by the fact that a relatively small amount of text-only data (1M examples, compared to the trillions of tokens the LLM was originally trained on) is sufficient to fully recover text performance.

2. **Joint SFT improves visual language accuracy, not just text accuracy.** The VLM metrics improve by 2-3 percentage points when text-only data is added to SFT. The paper hypothesizes this is because "text-only instruction data improves the model's instruction-following capability, which is also important for visual language tasks." In other words, general instruction-following skill (understanding what the user wants, formatting answers appropriately, avoiding hallucinations) transfers across modalities. A model that is better at following text-only instructions is also better at following visual instructions.

3. **The benefit of pre-training data blending emerges more clearly with joint SFT.** The gap between MMC4-only and MMC4+COYO pre-training is small under visual-only SFT (69.0% vs. 68.7% VLM accuracy) but larger under joint SFT (72.3% vs. 71.0%). The interpretation: without joint SFT, the model trained on blended data suffers from text capability degradation (from the COYO pairs) that partially offsets the visual diversity benefit; joint SFT removes this penalty, allowing the full visual diversity gain to manifest.

**The mechanism of text capability recovery.** The paper does not provide a mechanistic study of *how* joint SFT recovers text capabilities, but the empirical pattern is consistent with the following interpretation: during visual language pre-training, the LLM's self-attention mechanisms learn to route information differently—they develop attention patterns that look at visual tokens when processing image-related text, which may interfere with the pure text reasoning patterns the LLM originally used. Joint SFT provides examples where attention must be routed in the original text-only pattern (since there are no visual tokens), causing the model to re-learn or re-weight the original attention pathways. The fact that text recovery requires only a small amount of data suggests the original pathways are not erased, just down-weighted relative to the new visual pathways—the SFT data re-balances the weights.

**The Llama-2+SFT baseline.** To establish what text-only performance should look like, the paper fine-tunes the original Llama-2 (without any visual training) on the same 1M text-only FLAN examples. This yields MMLU 51.2% for 7B—higher than the base Llama-2's 46.0% because SFT on instruction-formatted data improves benchmark performance, a well-known phenomenon. VILA with joint SFT matches or slightly exceeds this baseline (50.8% for 7B, 56.0% for 13B per Table 6), confirming full text capability preservation.

#### Resolution and Projector Design Experiments

Section 4.4 presents additional experiments that inform the final model configuration but are not part of the main ablation sequence:

**Image resolution vs. token count (Table 7).** Increasing image resolution from 224×224 to 336×336 dramatically improves TextVQA accuracy (41.6% → 49.8%) because TextVQA requires reading text in images, which demands fine-grained visual details. Higher resolution produces more visual tokens (256 → 576), increasing computational cost. However, the paper finds that the **raw pixel resolution matters more than the number of tokens**: using the downsample projector (which compresses 576 tokens to 144, fewer than the 224-resolution setup's 256 tokens) with 336×336 inputs achieves 45.6% TextVQA accuracy—still 4 percentage points better than 224+linear (41.6%) despite having 44% fewer tokens. This shows there is "large redundancy in the image tokens"—much of the extra information in higher resolution can be compressed without losing the fine-grained details that matter for OCR tasks.

**Why this is significant for design.** If you can use high resolution for detail but compress tokens for efficiency, you get the best of both worlds. The paper notes this observation but does not use compression in the final VILA models, "to provide the best accuracy despite this encouraging observation."

**Comparison to frozen LLM with visual experts (Table 8).** The CogVLM approach (Wang et al., 2023) keeps the base LLM frozen and adds a parallel "visual expert" network (an MoE-style module with manual routing: visual tokens go through the expert, text tokens go through the original LLM layers). This preserves text capabilities perfectly (the LLM never changes) but increases model size by approximately 1.9×. The paper compares this approach to full LLM fine-tuning, both pre-trained on MMC4-core:

- Visual Expert: 0-shot 67.0%, 4-shot 64.8% VLM accuracy (note 4-shot is *worse* than 0-shot).
- Full fine-tune (the paper's approach): 0-shot 71.0%, 4-shot 72.1%.

Full fine-tuning outperforms on both metrics, achieves proper in-context learning (4-shot > 0-shot), and requires no additional parameters. The paper concludes that the parameter overhead and architectural complexity of visual experts are unnecessary when joint SFT can recover text capabilities without compromising visual performance.

**Comparison to LoRA (Table 9).** Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that trains low-rank matrices added to existing weights, rather than updating the full weight matrices. The paper compares LoRA fine-tuning during SFT (rank 64, applied to the 7B model) against full fine-tuning. Results across 6 benchmarks (VQAv2, GQA, VisWiz, TextVQA, MMBench, LLaVA-Bench) show full fine-tuning outperforming LoRA by large margins (e.g., TextVQA 64.4% vs. 50.0%, LLaVA-Bench 69.7% vs. 51.2%). This confirms that the visual language alignment requires substantial updates to the LLM weights that low-rank adaptation cannot capture.

**Reformatting interleaved structure.** The paper tests what happens if MMC4's interleaved structure is disrupted by placing all images before all text (`<im1><im2><txt1><txt2>` instead of `<im1><txt1><im2><txt2>`). The reformatted data degrades average 0-shot accuracy by 4.4% and average 4-shot accuracy by 37.5%—a catastrophic loss of in-context learning. This demonstrates that **the interleaved ordering specifically matters**, not just the co-occurrence of images and text. When all images come first, the model cannot learn to selectively attend to image-related text segments because the temporal relationship between images and their corresponding text is destroyed. The interleaved structure creates a natural curriculum where the model sees an image, then text that may or may not relate to that image, then another image, etc.—teaching it to dynamically switch between visual and textual attention.

#### Scaling Up to the Final VILA Models

Section 4.1 describes how the design choices validated in the ablations are scaled to produce the final VILA model family:

**Model sizes.** Two variants: VILA-7B (Llama-2-7B backbone) and VILA-13B (Llama-2-13B backbone).

**Vision encoder.** CLIP-L at 336×336 resolution, producing 576 visual tokens per image.

**Projector.** A simple linear layer (the configuration that performed best in Table 1, setting d).

**Pre-training data.** A blend of MMC4 interleaved data and COYO image-text pairs, totalling approximately 50M images, sampled to provide roughly equal numbers of images from each source. This is the configuration that performed best in Table 3 (MMC4+COYO). The COYO subset is obtained by ranking all COYO samples by CLIP similarity between image and caption, then keeping the top 25M (Appendix D).

**SFT data.** The LLaVA-1.5 SFT blend (Liu et al., 2024) plus the text-only FLAN data (1M examples) for joint SFT—approximately 1M visual examples plus 1M text examples. The visual SFT data is more diverse than the in-house ablation blend, including reference-based annotations (RefCOCO) and higher-quality prompts.

**Training duration (Appendix B).** For the 7B model: projector initialization takes 4 hours, visual language pre-training takes 30 hours, and SFT takes 6 hours—all on 16 nodes of 8 A100 GPUs each (128 GPUs total), for 5,120 GPU-hours total. Pre-training dominates the cost (75% of GPU-hours). The paper notes that training throughput optimization (sample packing, sample length clustering) and using lower resolution for pre-training (e.g., 224×224 with resolution scaled up later) could reduce training time by 30-50%.

**What is not scaled.** The pre-training corpus size remains at 50M images, which is smaller than the billion-scale datasets used by some prior work (e.g., Flamingo's 353M, Qwen-VL's 1.4B, PaLI-X's multi-billion scale). The paper acknowledges this as a limitation: "Due to the limited compute budget, we have not been able to further scale up the size of the pre-training corpus to billion-scale, which we leave as future work. Nonetheless, pre-training on 50M images already demonstrated significant performance improvement." This is an important caveat—the paper's results likely represent a lower bound on what could be achieved with larger pre-training corpora, assuming the data composition principles (interleaved + pairs) scale effectively.

**The ShareGPT4V addition.** The final row of Table 5 shows that adding the ShareGPT4V dataset (Chen et al., 2023) to the SFT blend on top of VILA-13B provides large improvements on LLaVA-Bench (73.0 → 78.4) and MM-Vet (38.8 → 45.7), with minimal changes on other benchmarks. This suggests that the SFT data quality and diversity continue to matter even with strong pre-training—the two stages are complementary, not substitutes.

#### Design Choice Summary and Their Justifications

- **Update LLM during pre-training** rather than freeze: necessary for deep cross-modal alignment and in-context learning (Figure 3, Table 1). The 0-shot performance is identical whether the LLM is frozen or updated, but 4-shot performance diverges sharply.
- **Linear projector** rather than Transformer: forces the LLM to learn visual processing internally, producing deeper alignment. This is counterintuitive—a more powerful projector seems like it should help—but the evidence shows the opposite (Table 1, c vs. d).
- **Interleaved pre-training data** rather than image-text pairs: prevents catastrophic forgetting by maintaining text distributional similarity and provides richer language modeling signal (Table 3, Figure 5). Blending with pairs adds visual diversity without the forgetting penalty when combined with joint SFT.
- **Joint SFT with text-only data** rather than visual-only SFT: recovers text capabilities that are "hidden but not forgotten" and improves visual language accuracy by strengthening instruction-following (Table 4).
- **336×336 resolution** rather than 224×224: essential for fine-grained visual tasks like TextVQA (Table 7). Token compression is possible but not used to maximize accuracy.
- **Full fine-tuning** rather than LoRA or visual experts: the visual language alignment task requires substantial weight updates that parameter-efficient methods cannot capture (Tables 8, 9).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: In-Context Learning as the Diagnostic Criterion for Cross-Modal Alignment Quality

Prior work on VLM pre-training made an implicit assumption that **zero-shot performance is the primary evaluation metric** for design decisions like whether to freeze or update the LLM. Under this metric, the choice appears almost irrelevant: Table 1 shows that freezing the LLM during pre-training (setting b: 66.8% average 0-shot) versus updating it (setting c: 66.8%) produces identical zero-shot accuracy. If researchers only evaluate zero-shot—as most VLM benchmarks in Table 5 do—they would conclude that the LLM can remain frozen during pre-training with no penalty, preserving text capabilities "for free."

The paper's fundamental conceptual move is to **use in-context learning (few-shot) performance as the diagnostic that reveals hidden alignment quality differences invisible to zero-shot evaluation**. The gap is dramatic: settings b and c are identical at 0-shot (66.8% vs. 66.8%) but diverge sharply at 4-shot (57.6% vs. 68.8%). In setting b, the model actually performs *worse* with more examples—an anti-pattern where demonstrations confuse rather than help. The 4-shot metric doesn't just measure a different capability; it reveals whether the fundamental representational integration between vision and language has succeeded. The paper essentially argues that **if a VLM cannot do in-context learning, its modality alignment is fundamentally superficial**, regardless of what zero-shot benchmarks say.

This is a diagnostic innovation, not an architectural one. The paper's contribution is to identify which evaluation protocol reveals the true alignment quality, then use that protocol to invalidate a design choice (frozen LLM pre-training) that appeared viable under the standard protocol. The deep embedding alignment measurement in Figure 3 operationalizes this conceptually—showing that frozen-LLM approaches produce flat alignment across transformer layers while fine-tuned approaches produce deepening alignment, with the linear projector variant (which performs best on 4-shot) producing the steepest alignment curve.

**Why this matters beyond VILA's specific results:** This finding implies that much of the prior frozen-LLM literature (BLIP-2, InstructBLIP, Flamingo) may have under-reported a capability gap by not evaluating few-shot visual in-context learning. It also provides a concrete criterion for future VLM design: if a new architecture or training method claims to achieve visual language alignment, the acid test is whether it not only achieves competitive zero-shot performance but also restores the LLM's native in-context learning behavior. This is a conceptual reframing of what "alignment" means—not just that visual tokens can be processed alongside text, but that they participate in the same deep reasoning mechanisms that make LLMs powerful in the first place.

---

### Innovation 2: The Mechanism of Catastrophic Forgetting in VLM Pre-Training Is Text Distribution Shift, Not Visual Input Per Se

The field's default assumption about why VLMs lose text-only capabilities during visual language training was that **adding visual tokens to the LLM's input somehow "interferes" with or "overwrites" text processing circuits**—the visual modality competes with and degrades the text modality through representational interference. This assumption motivated the frozen-LLM design school (Flamingo, BLIP-2, CogVLM): keep the LLM weights unchanged so that visual training cannot corrupt text representations.

The paper's Table 3 provides evidence for a fundamentally different mechanism. Compare COYO pre-training (image-text pairs with short alt-text captions) against MMC4-pairs pre-training (image-text pairs extracted from interleaved documents, with longer, more natural text). If visual input itself caused forgetting, both should produce similar text degradation—they both involve training on image-text pairs where visual tokens condition text generation. But the degradation patterns are very different: COYO causes a massive `-17.2%` MMLU drop, while MMC4-pairs causes a smaller `-13.6%` drop despite using the same pair-based format. And MMC4 interleaved (same text, same images, but preserving document structure) causes only `-5.3%`.

The paper's insight is that **the primary driver of catastrophic forgetting is the text distribution the model sees during pre-training, not the presence of visual tokens**. COYO captions average 22.7 tokens per image—short, telegraphic alt-text snippets that are distributionally alien to the LLM's original training on paragraph-length documents. Training on this text distribution causes the LLM's language modeling capabilities to collapse because the model adapts to predict only short, simplistic captions, "unlearning" how to handle the complex language required for MMLU-style questions. MMC4's document-length text (122.5 tokens/image) is much closer to the original pre-training distribution, so the LLM's text capabilities are largely preserved. The interleaved structure matters not because it provides better visual grounding but because it preserves the text distribution that the LLM was originally trained on.

This reframing has significant practical implications that the paper exploits:
1. **The text degradation is not permanent damage but a distributional adaptation that can be reversed.** The paper's finding that joint SFT with only 1M text examples fully recovers MMLU performance (Table 4) only makes sense if the original text capabilities are "hidden, not forgotten"—the model has adapted its text generation distribution to the pre-training corpus but can re-adapt to the original distribution with modest retraining.
2. **Data blending can mitigate forgetting during pre-training itself.** By including MMC4 interleaved data alongside COYO pairs, the model maintains exposure to natural-length text even while benefiting from COYO's visual diversity. The interleaved data serves as a distributional anchor.
3. **The way to prevent forgetting is to choose pre-training text that matches the LLM's original distribution, not to avoid updating the LLM.** This directly challenges the frozen-LLM school's motivation.

**What makes this a conceptual innovation rather than just an empirical finding:** The paper identifies the *mechanism* of forgetting (text distribution shift) and uses that mechanism to explain why interleaved data helps (it preserves text distribution), why pairs cause more forgetting (short captions shift the distribution), and why joint SFT works (it shifts the distribution back). This is a causal explanation, not just a correlation. It also provides a diagnostic principle for future work: before concluding that a VLM training method causes catastrophic forgetting, check whether the *text* the model sees during training is distributionally similar to the original LLM pre-training text. If it isn't, the forgetting may be a text problem, not a vision problem.

---

### Innovation 3: Text Capabilities Are Latent and Recoverable Through Joint Instruction Tuning

The standard framing of the VLM design problem treats text capability preservation and visual capability acquisition as a **zero-sum tradeoff**. The frozen-LLM school (Flamingo, BLIP-2, InstructBLIP, CogVLM) accepted architectural complexity and weaker visual reasoning as the price of perfect text preservation. The fine-tuning school (LLaVA, PaLM-E, PaLI-X) accepted text degradation as the price of deeper visual alignment. Both camps implicitly assumed the tradeoff was fundamental: you can't have both full text capabilities and deep visual alignment because the same parameters serve both functions, and optimizing for one necessarily degrades the other.

The paper's joint SFT experiment (Table 4) challenges this assumption by demonstrating that text capabilities are **temporarily suppressed rather than permanently lost**. The key evidence is the magnitude and efficiency of recovery:

1. **The pre-training degradation is moderate even without recovery.** Under the best pre-training configuration (MMC4+COYO), MMLU drops from 46.0% to 40.2%—a `-5.8%` change. This is significant but not catastrophic; the model hasn't "forgotten" language understanding, it has partially shifted its output distribution.
2. **A small amount of text-only data fully recovers performance.** Adding 1M text-only FLAN examples to SFT brings MMLU to 50.9%—actually *above* the original Llama-2 baseline of 46.0% and on par with Llama-2+SFT (51.2%). The recovery uses far less data than the original pre-training corpus (trillions of tokens), indicating that the underlying text capabilities were never erased—only the model's tendency to deploy them was reduced.
3. **Joint SFT paradoxically improves visual language accuracy.** This is the most surprising result: adding text-only data (which contains no images) to SFT improves VLM task accuracy by 2–3 percentage points (Table 4: 69.0% → 72.3% for MMC4+COYO). The paper hypothesizes this is because "text-only instruction data improves the model's instruction-following capability," but the deeper implication is that **general reasoning and instruction-following capabilities are modality-agnostic and transfer across input formats**. A model that better understands how to follow instructions in text-only contexts also follows instructions more accurately when images are present.

**Why this is a fundamental reframing rather than a training trick:** If text capabilities are latent and recoverable rather than zero-sum with visual capabilities, then the entire motivation for frozen-LLM approaches collapses. The complexity of cross-attention layers (Flamingo), Q-Formers (BLIP-2), and visual expert modules (CogVLM)—all designed to preserve text capabilities by keeping the LLM unchanged—is unnecessary. A simpler model with a linear projector and full LLM fine-tuning can achieve both better visual reasoning (through deep alignment) and comparable text performance (through joint SFT recovery), while also being smaller and faster (1× parameters vs. 1.9× for visual experts, per Table 8). This doesn't just improve VLM design; it invalidates a major architectural research program by showing the problem it was solving doesn't actually exist in the form assumed.

**The broader implication for multi-modal training:** This finding suggests a general principle for extending pre-trained models to new modalities: the original capabilities are resilient to distribution shift during fine-tuning, and can be "reactivated" through re-exposure to the original modality. This has implications beyond vision—extending LLMs to audio, code, or robotic control might follow the same pattern: full parameter updates for deep alignment, followed by joint training with text data for capability preservation.

---

### Innovation 4: The Counterintuitive Superiority of a Simpler Projector

The natural engineering instinct when designing a multi-modal architecture is that **a more powerful bridge between modalities should produce better integration**. If a linear projector can map visual features to the LLM's input space, a Transformer projector that applies self-attention across visual patches before projection should produce richer, more context-aware visual tokens—and therefore better downstream performance. This was the design choice in many prior models that used Q-Former modules (BLIP-2, InstructBLIP) or more complex adapters.

The paper's Table 1 (settings c vs. d) shows the opposite: under the same training protocol (LLM updated in both pre-training and SFT), the linear projector outperforms the Transformer projector on both 0-shot (68.7% vs. 66.8%) and 4-shot (70.9% vs. 68.8%) average accuracy. The gap is modest in absolute terms but consistent—and critically, the linear projector is simpler, faster, and requires fewer parameters.

The paper's explanation—that "a simpler projector forces the LLM to learn more on handling visual inputs, leading to better generalization"—is supported by the deep embedding alignment measurements in Figure 3. The linear projector produces the steepest alignment curve in the deepest layers of the LLM, suggesting that when the projector does minimal preprocessing, the LLM's transformer layers learn to extract visual information themselves. With a Transformer projector, some of the visual processing happens before the LLM, in the projector's self-attention layers, producing tokens that are already "pre-digested." The LLM can then process these tokens using its existing text processing circuits without needing to develop specialized visual reasoning mechanisms—but this means visual and text representations never fully integrate in the deep layers where high-level reasoning occurs.

**Why this is significant beyond the specific linear vs. Transformer comparison:** This finding inverts the standard design intuition for multi-modal models. The field has generally assumed that modality-specific preprocessing (Q-Formers, perceiver resamplers, cross-attention adapters) helps the LLM by reducing the burden of learning to interpret a new modality. But the paper's results suggest that **making the LLM do more work during training produces better representations at inference time**—essentially, that the "struggle" of learning to interpret minimally processed visual tokens creates deeper, more robust cross-modal understanding. This is analogous to findings in other domains where harder training (higher resolution, less preprocessing, no shortcuts) produces better generalization, but applied to the architectural design of modality bridges.

This also connects to a broader principle: when extending a pre-trained model to a new modality, it's better to **force the pre-trained model to adapt internally** than to add preprocessing components that shield it from needing to adapt. The pre-trained model already has powerful representational machinery; the goal should be to make that machinery work on the new input type, not to create a separate processing pipeline that feeds pre-digested inputs to the existing machinery. The linear projector is the minimalist implementation of this principle—it does nothing but align dimensions—and its success suggests that more complex modality bridges may actively impede deep integration even as they improve training efficiency.


## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary ablation experiments use four visual language tasks: OKVQA (visual question answering requiring external knowledge), TextVQA (VQA requiring OCR), COCO Captioning, and Flickr30k Captioning. For ablations, the authors report accuracy for VQA tasks and CIDEr score for captioning tasks, with both 0-shot and 4-shot evaluation to measure in-context learning capability. The final model comparison (Table 5) uses 12 benchmarks: VQA-v2, GQA, VisWiz, ScienceQA-IMG, TextVQA, POPE (object hallucination), MME, MMBench, MMBench-Chinese, SEED-Bench, LLaVA-Bench (In-the-Wild), and MM-Vet. Text-only capability is evaluated on MMLU, BBH, and DROP (Table 6). The authors explicitly note that the ablation evaluation protocol "is different from Table 5 and can only be compared within the table" (Table 1 caption), meaning absolute numbers in ablation tables should not be compared against final results.

- **Base model(s).** All ablation experiments use **Llama-2-7B** as the LLM backbone with OpenAI **CLIP-L** as the vision encoder. For the final models, two scales are used: Llama-2-7B and Llama-2-13B. The authors argue Llama-2 is a representative open-source LLM, and using it enables head-to-head comparison with LLaVA-1.5 (which uses Vicuna-1.5, itself based on Llama-2). The vision encoder is frozen throughout all experiments. For the ablations, images are processed at 224×224 resolution; the final models use 336×336.

- **Metrics.** For VQA-style tasks (OKVQA, TextVQA, VQA-v2, GQA, VisWiz, ScienceQA-IMG): **accuracy** (% of questions answered correctly). For captioning tasks (COCO, Flickr30k): **CIDEr score**. For MMLU, BBH, DROP: **accuracy**. For MME: a composite score (maximum ~2000). For MMBench, MMBench-Chinese, SEED-Bench, LLaVA-Bench, MM-Vet: accuracy or benchmark-specific scores as reported by each benchmark's evaluation protocol. For the ablation tables, the paper reports an "Average" metric across the four evaluation datasets (OKVQA, TextVQA, COCO, Flickr30k), computed separately for 0-shot and 4-shot settings.

- **Baselines.** The primary comparison in the ablation experiments is between different training configurations of the same architecture, not against external models—the paper is isolating the effect of pre-training design choices. However, several external baselines appear: **(1) LLaVA-1.5** (Liu et al., 2024) is the main head-to-head comparison in Table 5, using the same base LLM (Vicuna-1.5 is Llama-2 based), same prompts, and same evaluation protocol. LLaVA-1.5 uses minimal pre-training (558K images) and visual-only SFT. **(2) Llama-2+SFT** serves as the text-only capability baseline in Table 6—the original Llama-2 fine-tuned on the same 1M FLAN text-only instruction data that VILA uses in joint SFT, establishing the ceiling for text performance. **(3) InstructBLIP** (Dai et al., 2023), **BLIP-2** (Li et al., 2023), **Qwen-VL/Qwen-VL-Chat** (Bai et al., 2023), **Shikra** (Chen et al., 2023), **IDEFICS** (IDEFICS, 2023) appear as additional comparisons in Table 5, though without controlled parity on prompts or training data. **(4) CogVLM's visual expert approach** (Wang et al., 2023) serves as a frozen-LLM baseline in Table 8. **(5) LoRA fine-tuning** (rank 64) serves as a parameter-efficient baseline in Table 9. **(6) Vicuna-1.5** serves as the text-only baseline for the Vicuna backbone in Table 11 (Appendix C).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not measure inference-time computation in the conventional sense (no beam search budget, no sampling budget)—all evaluation is on greedy decoding of single answers. The relevant compute accounting is **training cost**, reported in GPU-hours (Appendix B): projector initialization takes 4 hours, visual language pre-training takes 30 hours, and SFT takes 6 hours for the 7B model on 16 nodes × 8 A100 GPUs (128 GPUs total), totaling 5,120 GPU-hours. For data scale, the key metric is number of images in the pre-training corpus: ablations use ~25M images (MMC4-core, or COYO subsampled to 25M); final models use ~50M images (MMC4 + COYO blend). The paper tracks pre-training data scale precisely because it is the main variable being ablated.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation, statistical significance testing, or confidence intervals. All ablation comparisons are single-run results on a fixed set of evaluation datasets. The ablation test set appears to be the standard validation/test splits of the four evaluation datasets (OKVQA, TextVQA, COCO, Flickr30k), but the paper does not specify exact split sizes or whether a separate validation set was used for hyperparameter selection in the ablation stage. For the final benchmark comparison (Table 5), the paper uses the standard test sets and evaluation scripts released by each benchmark. The paper notes that for the final VILA models, "We also include a better SFT data blend from LLaVA-1.5" (Section 4.1), but no cross-validation is used to select this blend—it is a direct adoption of an established recipe.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Ablation Results: Freezing vs. Updating the LLM (Table 1)

The headline finding is that **freezing the LLM during pre-training produces identical zero-shot performance to updating it, but catastrophically degrades in-context learning**. The four configurations compared in Table 1 are:

| Configuration | LLM updated during PreT? | LLM updated during SFT? | Projector | 0-shot Avg | 4-shot Avg |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) | No | No | Transformer | 13.4% | 37.5% |
| (b) | No | Yes | Transformer | 66.8% | 57.6% |
| (c) | Yes | Yes | Transformer | 66.8% | 68.8% |
| (d) | Yes | Yes | Linear | **68.7%** | **70.9%** |

Setting (a) demonstrates that training only the projector with no LLM update at any stage is completely inadequate for zero-shot performance—the projector alone cannot bridge the modality gap well enough for the frozen LLM to understand visual tokens, achieving only 13.4% average zero-shot accuracy. The LLM must be updated during at least SFT to achieve usable performance.

Setting (b) vs. (c) is the critical comparison: both achieve 66.8% zero-shot average, but setting (b)'s 4-shot drops to 57.6% (worse than zero-shot), while setting (c)'s 4-shot rises to 68.8% (better than zero-shot). On COCO specifically, the gap is extreme: setting (b) achieves 109.4 CIDEr 0-shot but only 88.0 CIDEr 4-shot, while setting (c) achieves 112.3 0-shot and 113.5 4-shot. The 4-shot degradation in setting (b) is particularly severe on captioning tasks (COCO, Flickr) because these are out-of-distribution relative to the VQA-heavy SFT data, revealing that freezing the LLM during pre-training produces not just weaker in-context learning, but **worse generalization to task formats unseen during SFT**.

Setting (c) vs. (d) compares projector design: the linear projector (d) outperforms the Transformer projector (c) on both 0-shot (68.7% vs. 66.8%) and 4-shot (70.9% vs. 68.8%), despite the Transformer having higher capacity. The captioning metrics are particularly informative: COCO CIDEr is 115.7 (linear) vs. 112.3 (Transformer) at 0-shot and 118.5 vs. 113.5 at 4-shot.

**Figure 3** provides the mechanistic explanation through deep embedding alignment: the linear projector with updated LLM (setting d) shows the steepest increase in visual-text token cosine similarity across transformer layers, from ~0.05 in early layers to ~0.49 in the deepest layer (layer 30). Setting (c) with Transformer projector shows a similar but shallower curve reaching ~0.38. Setting (b) with frozen pre-training shows consistently low similarity (~0.16-0.27) across all layers with no deepening trend—the visual tokens remain representationally segregated from text tokens regardless of layer depth.

#### Ablation Results: Pre-Training Data Format (Table 3)

The headline finding is that **interleaved pre-training data (MMC4) dramatically outperforms image-text pair data (COYO or MMC4-pairs) on all metrics, and blending interleaved data with pairs provides the best VLM accuracy**. All configurations use the updated-LLM, linear-projector recipe (setting d from Table 1) and visual-only SFT:

| Pre-training Data | VLM 0-shot Avg | VLM 4-shot Avg | MMLU Accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| COYO (image-text pairs) | 51.1% | 50.3% | 28.8% (-17.2%) |
| MMC4-pairs (extracted pairs) | 46.4% | 44.5% | 32.4% (-13.6%) |
| MMC4 (interleaved) | 68.7% | 70.9% | 40.7% (-5.3%) |
| MMC4 + COYO (blend) | **69.0%** | **71.3%** | 40.2% (-5.8%) |

The COYO-only pre-training produces the worst outcomes across the board: VLM accuracy is ~17 points lower than MMC4, and MMLU drops by 17.2 percentage points (from Llama-2's 46.0% to 28.8%). Critically, the 4-shot average is *lower* than the 0-shot average (50.3% vs. 51.1%), indicating that the model cannot perform visual in-context learning at all—it has never seen more than one image per sample during pre-training, so demonstrations with multiple images are outside its training distribution.

The MMC4-pairs configuration is particularly revealing: despite using the exact same text and images as MMC4 interleaved, just restructured into isolated image-text pairs, VLM accuracy is **even worse than COYO** (46.4% vs. 51.1% 0-shot) and MMLU degradation remains severe (-13.6%). This demonstrates that **the interleaved structure itself is what matters**, not just having longer or more natural text—the model needs to see images in document context where some text relates to images and some doesn't, to learn selective visual attention.

The MMC4 interleaved configuration is the clear winner among single-source configurations: VLM accuracy more than 17 points higher than COYO, MMLU degradation contained to -5.3%, and 4-shot exceeds 0-shot (70.9% vs. 68.7%), demonstrating preserved in-context learning. The MMC4+COYO blend adds a marginal VLM improvement (+0.3% 0-shot, +0.4% 4-shot) with essentially the same MMLU degradation (-5.8%), suggesting COYO adds visual diversity without further damaging text capabilities when interleaved data anchors the distribution.

**Figure 5** provides supporting evidence: the training loss curve for MMC4 is consistently and substantially lower than for MMC4-pairs throughout pre-training, confirming that the interleaved structure provides richer language modeling signal. The paper interprets this as evidence that "the full text segments provide more information for language modeling" compared to isolated text segments paired with single images.

#### Ablation Results: Joint SFT for Text Capability Recovery (Table 4)

The headline finding is that **adding text-only instruction data to SFT not only fully recovers text-only capabilities lost during pre-training, but also improves visual language task accuracy**. All configurations use the updated-LLM, linear-projector, visual language pre-trained model:

| Pre-Training Data | SFT Data | VLM 0-shot Avg | VLM 4-shot Avg | MMLU Accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MMC4 | Visual only | 68.7% | 70.9% | 40.7% (-5.3%) |
| MMC4+COYO | Visual only | 69.0% | 71.3% | 40.2% (-5.8%) |
| *Llama-2* | *Text only* | *–* | *–* | *51.2%* |
| MMC4 | Visual + Text (joint) | 71.0% | 72.1% | **51.4% (+0.2%)** |
| MMC4+COYO | Visual + Text (joint) | **72.3%** | **73.6%** | 50.9% (-0.3%) |

The text-only baselines are instructive: the original Llama-2 achieves 46.0% MMLU; fine-tuning Llama-2 on the same 1M text-only FLAN examples (Llama-2+SFT) raises this to 51.2%, setting the ceiling for text-only performance under this instruction tuning recipe.

Joint SFT with MMC4 pre-training achieves 51.4% MMLU—actually **0.2 percentage points above** the Llama-2+SFT baseline, indicating that not only has text capability been fully recovered, but the visual language pre-training may have provided some slight benefit to text-only reasoning (or the difference is within noise). The VLM accuracy improvement is substantial: +2.3 percentage points 0-shot (68.7% → 71.0%) and +1.2 points 4-shot (70.9% → 72.1%) compared to visual-only SFT on MMC4.

With MMC4+COYO pre-training, joint SFT yields the highest VLM accuracy (72.3% 0-shot, 73.6% 4-shot), with MMLU at 50.9% (within 0.3 points of the Llama-2+SFT baseline). The gain from adding COYO to the pre-training blend is more pronounced under joint SFT (72.3% vs. 71.0% VLM 0-shot, a 1.3-point gap) than under visual-only SFT (69.0% vs. 68.7%, a 0.3-point gap). The paper interprets this as: "with joint SFT, the model no longer suffers from the text-only degradation when pre-trained with short captions, thus unlocking the full benefits from the better visual diversity."

#### Final Benchmark Comparison (Table 5)

The headline finding is that **VILA consistently outperforms LLaVA-1.5 under a head-to-head comparison across 12 visual language benchmarks, with the 7B model sometimes surpassing the LLaVA-1.5 13B model on specific tasks**. All comparisons use the same prompts and base LLM family (Vicuna-1.5 is Llama-2-based), isolating the effect of VILA's enhanced pre-training against LLaVA-1.5's minimal pre-training (558K images):

**VILA-7B vs. LLaVA-1.5-7B (same LLM size):**
- VILA-7B outperforms on 9 of 12 benchmarks.
- Notable gains: VisWiz 57.8 vs. 50.0 (+7.8), TextVQA 64.4 vs. 58.2 (+6.2), LLaVA-Bench 69.7 vs. 63.4 (+6.3), MMBench 68.9 vs. 64.3 (+4.6), MM-Vet 34.9 vs. 30.5 (+4.4).
- LLaVA-1.5-7B wins narrowly on VQA-v2 (78.5 vs. 79.9), GQA (62.0 vs. 62.3—essentially tied), POPE (85.9 vs. 85.5), and SEED-Bench (58.6 vs. 61.1), though the differences are modest.

**VILA-7B vs. LLaVA-1.5-13B (smaller VILA vs. larger LLaVA):**
- VILA-7B **outperforms LLaVA-1.5-13B** on VisWiz (57.8 vs. 53.6, +4.2) and TextVQA (64.4 vs. 61.3, +3.1), two tasks requiring fine-grained visual understanding. The paper highlights this as evidence that pre-training benefits are concentrated on tasks where visual detail matters most.

**VILA-13B vs. LLaVA-1.5-13B (same LLM size):**
- VILA-13B outperforms LLaVA-1.5-13B on 10 of 12 benchmarks.
- Substantial gains: VisWiz 60.6 vs. 53.6 (+7.0), TextVQA 66.6 vs. 61.3 (+5.3), ScienceQA-IMG 73.7 vs. 71.6 (+2.1), MME 1570.1 vs. 1531.3 (+38.8), MMBench 70.3 vs. 67.7 (+2.6), MM-Vet 38.8 vs. 35.4 (+3.4).
- LLaVA-1.5-13B wins on POPE (85.9 vs. 84.2) and VQA-v2 (80.0 vs. 80.8—VILA wins here), and GQA is tied at 63.3.

**Adding ShareGPT4V to VILA-13B:**
- LLaVA-Bench jumps from 73.0 to 78.4 (+5.4), MM-Vet from 38.8 to 45.7 (+6.9).
- Most other benchmarks are flat or slightly down (e.g., VQA-v2 80.8→80.6, GQA 63.3→63.2, TextVQA 66.6→65.3).

**Comparison to other models (not head-to-head controlled):** VILA-13B achieves the highest reported scores among open-source models on VisWiz (60.6 vs. Qwen-VL-Chat's 38.9 and LLaVA-1.5-13B's 53.6), TextVQA (66.6 vs. InstructBLIP-13B's 50.7 and Qwen-VL-Chat's 61.5), and MMBench (70.3, best in table). On VQA-v2 and GQA, Qwen-VL models report higher numbers, but the paper marks these with asterisks because "the training images of the datasets are observed during training"—a data contamination caveat.

#### Text-Only Benchmark Comparison (Table 6)

The headline finding is that **VILA maintains competitive text-only performance with the original Llama-2 after joint SFT, with VILA-13B even exceeding the text-only baseline on some metrics**. Results across MMLU, BBH, and DROP:

| Model | Size | MMLU | BBH | DROP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Llama-2 (base) | 7B | 46.0% | 32.0% | 31.7% |
| Llama-2+SFT (text only) | 7B | 51.8% | 39.3% | 53.1% |
| Vicuna-1.5 | 7B | 49.8% | 36.9% | 29.2% |
| VILA | 7B | 50.8% | 38.5% | 52.7% |
| Llama-2 (base) | 13B | 55.7% | 37.5% | 41.6% |
| Llama-2+SFT (text only) | 13B | 54.3% | 43.2% | 59.2% |
| Vicuna-1.5 | 13B | 55.8% | 38.4% | 43.6% |
| VILA | 13B | **56.0%** | **44.2%** | **63.6%** |

For the 7B model, VILA is slightly below Llama-2+SFT on MMLU (50.8% vs. 51.8%, -1.0%) and BBH (38.5% vs. 39.3%, -0.8%), but nearly matches on DROP (52.7% vs. 53.1%, -0.4%). The paper attributes this small gap to the smaller model being more susceptible to text performance degradation during pre-training, citing PaLM-E (Driess et al., 2023) for the same observation about smaller models.

For the 13B model, VILA **exceeds Llama-2+SFT on all three benchmarks**: MMLU 56.0% vs. 54.3% (+1.7%), BBH 44.2% vs. 43.2% (+1.0%), DROP 63.6% vs. 59.2% (+4.4%). The paper does not explain this reversal from the 7B trend, but the larger model's greater capacity may allow it to accommodate both visual and text capabilities without interference, or the pre-training may provide a beneficial regularization effect at larger scale.

#### Resolution and Token Compression Results (Table 7)

The headline finding is that **raw image resolution matters more than the number of visual tokens for fine-grained visual tasks, and token compression can recover most of the high-resolution benefit at lower computational cost**. All experiments use the 7B model with linear projector:

| Resolution | Projector | #Tokens | OKVQA | TextVQA | COCO CIDEr |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 224×224 | Linear | 256 | 49.9% | 41.6% | 116.0 |
| 336×336 | Linear | 576 | 49.7% | **49.8%** | **117.7** |
| 336×336 | Downsample | 144 | 49.3% | 45.6% | 115.7 |

Increasing resolution from 224 to 336 dramatically improves TextVQA (41.6% → 49.8%, +8.2 points) while leaving OKVQA essentially unchanged (49.9% → 49.7%, -0.2 points) and COCO slightly improved (116.0 → 117.7 CIDEr). This confirms that fine-grained visual tasks like text reading benefit disproportionately from higher resolution, while high-level semantic tasks (OKVQA, COCO) are relatively insensitive.

Using the downsample projector (concatenate every 2×2 token block, then linear projection) at 336 resolution reduces tokens from 576 to 144—fewer tokens than the 224-resolution setup (256 tokens)—yet still achieves 45.6% TextVQA, which is 4.0 points higher than 224+linear (41.6%). The TextVQA gap between 336+linear and 336+downsample is 4.2 points (49.8% vs. 45.6%), smaller than the gain over 224+linear. This demonstrates "a large redundancy in the image tokens"—much of the fine-grained information in high-resolution images can be preserved through simple token compression, though not all of it.

#### Comparison to Frozen-LLM Alternatives (Table 8)

The headline finding is that **full LLM fine-tuning during pre-training outperforms the visual expert approach (CogVLM) on both VLM accuracy and in-context learning, while using 1.9× fewer parameters**. Both configurations are pre-trained on MMC4-core:

| Approach | #Parameters | VLM 0-shot Avg | VLM 4-shot Avg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visual Expert (CogVLM-style) | 1.9× | 67.0% | 64.8% |
| Full Fine-tune (VILA) | **1×** | **71.0%** | **72.1%** |

The visual expert approach not only underperforms in absolute accuracy (67.0% vs. 71.0% 0-shot), but also exhibits the same in-context learning degradation seen in frozen-LLM methods: 4-shot accuracy (64.8%) is *lower* than 0-shot (67.0%). The full fine-tune approach shows proper in-context learning (72.1% 4-shot > 71.0% 0-shot). With 1.9× the parameter count, the visual expert approach also has deployment disadvantages the paper emphasizes: "not friendly for edge deployment."

#### Comparison to LoRA Fine-Tuning (Table 9)

The headline finding is that **LoRA fine-tuning during SFT substantially underperforms full fine-tuning across all visual language benchmarks**. Both use the 7B model, with LoRA rank 64:

| Method | VQAv2 | GQA | VisWiz | TextVQA | MMBench | LLaVA-Bench |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LoRA (r=64) | 69.4 | 54.3 | 48.4 | 50.0 | 60.3 | 51.2 |
| Full Fine-tune | **79.9** | **62.3** | **57.8** | **64.4** | **68.9** | **69.7** |

The gaps are large and consistent: 10.5 points on VQAv2, 14.4 on TextVQA (the most dramatic), 18.5 on LLaVA-Bench, 8.6 on MMBench. This confirms that visual language alignment requires substantial updates to the full LLM weight matrices that low-rank adaptation cannot capture—consistent with the earlier finding that deep embedding alignment in late transformer layers is necessary for strong performance.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Projector design (Table 1, settings c vs. d):** Linear projector outperforms Transformer projector on both 0-shot (68.7% vs. 66.8%) and 4-shot (70.9% vs. 68.8%) average, despite the Transformer having higher capacity and the ability to model spatial relationships between visual patches. This is a non-obvious result—the convention in the field favors more sophisticated modality bridges—and it holds across all four evaluation datasets individually (OKVQA: 45.2 vs. 44.8 at 0-shot; TextVQA: 39.7 vs. 38.5; COCO: 115.7 vs. 112.3 CIDEr; Flickr: 74.2 vs. 71.5).

- **MMC4-pairs vs. MMC4 interleaved (Table 3, Figure 5):** Breaking interleaved documents into isolated image-text pairs while preserving the same images and text causes a dramatic VLM accuracy drop (46.4% vs. 68.7% 0-shot) and worsens MMLU degradation (-13.6% vs. -5.3%). The loss curve in Figure 5 confirms that the interleaved structure provides a richer training signal: the loss is consistently lower throughout pre-training for interleaved vs. pair-based data. This ablation isolates the *structure* of the data from the *content*, demonstrating that the interleaved format itself—not just having better-quality text—is what drives the benefits.

- **COYO vs. MMC4-pairs (Table 3):** COYO (alt-text pairs, short captions) actually outperforms MMC4-pairs (extracted from document text, longer captions) on VLM accuracy (51.1% vs. 46.4% 0-shot) despite having worse MMLU degradation (-17.2% vs. -13.6%). This is a nuanced finding: MMC4-pairs inherits MMC4's text quality (longer, more natural) but loses the interleaved structure, producing the worst of both worlds—worse VLM performance than COYO and worse MMLU than MMC4 interleaved. It suggests that when data is in pair format, visual diversity (COYO's variety of image types) matters more than text quality for VLM performance, but both are worse than interleaved format.

- **Data blending (MMC4 + COYO) with visual-only vs. joint SFT (Tables 3 and 4):** The benefit of adding COYO to MMC4 pre-training is modest under visual-only SFT (+0.3% 0-shot, +0.4% 4-shot) but larger under joint SFT (+1.3% 0-shot, +1.5% 4-shot). The interpretation: joint SFT removes the residual text capability penalty from including image-text pairs in pre-training, allowing the visual diversity benefit of COYO to fully manifest. This is a non-obvious interaction effect between pre-training data composition and SFT data composition—the value of pre-training data diversity depends on the SFT recipe.

- **Reformatting MMC4's interleaved structure (Section 4.4, text only, no table):** Reordering MMC4 samples from `<im1><txt1><im2><txt2>` to `<im1><im2><txt1><txt2>` (all images first, then all text) degrades average 0-shot accuracy by 4.4% and average 4-shot accuracy by **37.5%**—a catastrophic loss of in-context learning. This negative result demonstrates that the sequential interleaving of images and text is essential; simply co-occurring in the same sample is not enough. The model must learn the temporal relationship where text segments relate to preceding images, and destroying this temporal structure prevents the model from learning to dynamically attend to visual information when it is relevant.

- **Image resolution vs. token count (Table 7):** The downsample projector at 336×336 uses 144 tokens (fewer than 224×224's 256) yet achieves 45.6% TextVQA—4.0 points better than 224+linear (41.6%). This demonstrates that raw pixel resolution contributes to performance independently of token count. The 4.2-point gap between 336+linear (49.8%) and 336+downsample (45.6%) represents the information lost through 4× token compression, which is modest relative to the 8.2-point gain from higher resolution overall. This finding suggests token compression is a viable efficiency optimization, though VILA's final models do not use it "to provide the best accuracy."

- **Visual Expert comparison (Table 8):** The CogVLM-style visual expert approach (1.9× parameters, frozen LLM) underperforms full fine-tuning on both 0-shot (67.0% vs. 71.0%) and 4-shot (64.8% vs. 72.1%). Critically, the visual expert approach also shows the anti-pattern of 4-shot being worse than 0-shot (64.8% < 67.0%), confirming that the in-context learning degradation in frozen-LLM approaches is not solved by adding specialized visual processing capacity—only by updating the LLM itself.

- **LoRA comparison (Table 9):** Low-rank adaptation (rank 64) substantially underperforms full fine-tuning across six benchmarks, with gaps ranging from 8.6 points (MMBench) to 18.5 points (LLaVA-Bench). The largest gaps are on benchmarks requiring complex reasoning and instruction following (LLaVA-Bench, TextVQA), while the smallest gap is on VQA-v2 (10.5 points). This confirms that the visual language alignment task requires weight updates that exceed what low-rank matrices can express, and that deep alignment (Figure 3) requires modifications throughout the LLM's full parameter space.

- **Vicuna-1.5 vs. Llama-2 backbone (Appendix C, Table 11):** The paper reproduces the core pre-training ablation (Table 1) with Vicuna-1.5-7B as the LLM backbone instead of Llama-2-7B. The pattern holds: frozen LLM during pre-training yields 0-shot 62.6%, 4-shot 59.2% (4-shot worse than 0-shot), while updated LLM produces 0-shot 69.1%, 4-shot 72.8% (proper in-context learning). Final benchmark performance with Vicuna backbone is similar to Llama-2 backbone (VQAv2 79.3 vs. 79.9, GQA 62.3 vs. 62.3, VisWiz 58.7 vs. 57.8), confirming the pre-training recipe generalizes across LLM families. VILA's primary results use Llama-2.

- **ShareGPT4V SFT addition (Table 5, last row):** Adding ShareGPT4V captions to the SFT blend on top of VILA-13B produces large improvements on LLaVA-Bench (73.0 → 78.4, +5.4) and MM-Vet (38.8 → 45.7, +6.9), benchmarks that emphasize detailed image understanding and conversation. Other benchmarks are essentially flat or slightly down. This suggests SFT data diversity and quality continue to matter independently of pre-training quality; the two stages are complementary, and improvements in both compound.

- **Data scale observation (Section 4.1):** The paper's pre-training corpus (50M images) is smaller than some prior work (Flamingo's 353M, Qwen-VL's 1.4B). The authors acknowledge this as a limitation and do not claim to have found the optimal pre-training scale—the results should be interpreted as a demonstration that *properly composed* pre-training data at modest scale already yields significant gains, with further scaling left to future work. No ablation on pre-training data scale is provided.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1 from the Executive Summary: "Freezing LLMs during pre-training achieves decent zero-shot performance but destroys in-context learning."**

This claim is supported by the controlled comparison in Table 1 (settings b vs. c), where frozen pre-training + updated SFT achieves 66.8% 0-shot (identical to the fully updated configuration) but only 57.6% 4-shot (vs. 68.8% for fully updated). The evidence spans four diverse datasets (two VQA, two captioning) and shows the 4-shot degradation is particularly severe on out-of-distribution tasks (COCO captioning: 109.4 → 88.0 CIDEr for frozen vs. 112.3 → 113.5 for updated). The deep alignment measurement in Figure 3 provides a mechanistic explanation consistent with the behavioral evidence: frozen pre-training produces shallow, flat alignment across transformer layers, while updated pre-training produces deepening alignment.

*Caveats:* The "frozen during pre-training" setting (b) still updates the LLM during SFT—it is not a fully frozen-LLM approach like Flamingo or InstructBLIP. The claim is specifically about the pre-training stage's effect on subsequent SFT outcomes. A true frozen-LLM baseline (no LLM update at any stage, like setting a) is included and performs extremely poorly (13.4% 0-shot), but the 4-shot comparison in setting b vs. c is what tests the pre-training-specific hypothesis. The evaluation uses only 4 datasets and a single model scale (7B); whether the 4-shot gap persists at larger scales (13B, 70B) is untested. Additionally, the "4-shot" evaluation protocol is not described in detail—how the 4 examples are selected, whether they are from the same distribution as the test example, and whether the format is standardized across datasets could all affect the results. The claim holds given the internal evidence but would be strengthened by testing on additional LLM scales and evaluation settings.

**Claim 2 from the Executive Summary: "Interleaved pre-training data is essential for preserving text-only capabilities and enabling visual in-context learning."**

This claim is supported by Table 3, where MMC4 interleaved achieves 70.9% 4-shot and -5.3% MMLU degradation, while COYO pairs achieve 50.3% 4-shot (worse than 0-shot) and -17.2% MMLU degradation, and MMC4-pairs (same text/images, broken into pairs) achieves 44.5% 4-shot and -13.6% MMLU degradation. The strength of this evidence comes from the MMC4-pairs ablation specifically: by keeping the text and images identical to MMC4 interleaved and only changing the document structure, the paper isolates the *structural* contribution of interleaving from the *content quality* contribution. The result that MMC4-pairs is *worse* than COYO on VLM accuracy (46.4% vs. 51.1% 0-shot) despite having better text is a non-obvious finding that strengthens the causal argument for interleaved structure.

*Caveats:* This claim is demonstrated at a single pre-training data scale (~25M images per source). Whether interleaved data remains "essential" when the pair-based data is scaled to much larger sizes (billions of images) or when pair-based data is augmented with higher-quality captions is not tested. Some prior work (PaLI-X, Qwen-VL) uses large-scale pair-based pre-training and achieves competitive results, suggesting that the advantage of interleaved data might be (partially) compensated by data scale—a hypothesis the paper acknowledges but cannot test given compute constraints. The claim should be qualified as: for a fixed pre-training budget of ~25-50M images, interleaved data substantially outperforms pair-based data on both VLM accuracy and text capability preservation, with the mechanism being distributional similarity of the text component and the richer language modeling signal from document context. Whether this advantage persists or diminishes at billion-image scale is an open question.

**Claim 3 from the Executive Summary: "Re-blending text-only instruction data into SFT not only recovers the ~5% MMLU degradation but also boosts VLM task accuracy."**

This claim is supported by Table 4, where joint SFT using MMC4 pre-training recovers MMLU from 40.7% (visual-only SFT) to 51.4% (joint SFT), matching Llama-2+SFT's 51.2%, while VLM 0-shot accuracy improves from 68.7% to 71.0%. The joint SFT improvement on VLM accuracy is replicated across both pre-training data configurations (MMC4-only and MMC4+COYO) and affects all four evaluation datasets (though per-dataset breakdowns are not shown). The text-only recovery is further validated in Table 6, where VILA-7B achieves 50.8% MMLU (vs. 51.8% for Llama-2+SFT) and VILA-13B achieves 56.0% MMLU (vs. 54.3% for Llama-2+SFT), with DROP and BBH showing the same pattern.

*Caveats:* The VLM accuracy boost from joint SFT (+2.3% 0-shot) is modest but consistent. The paper's explanation—that text-only instruction data improves instruction-following capability which transfers to visual tasks—is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism. No ablation tests whether the benefit comes from instruction-following improvement specifically, or from other factors like increased training data quantity, mitigation of overfitting to the visual SFT distribution, or regularization of the LLM weights toward their pre-trained state. An ablation using an equal amount of additional visual SFT data instead of text-only data would help distinguish "more data" from "text data specifically." The claim holds empirically but the causal mechanism is speculative.

**Claim 4 from the Executive Summary: "VILA consistently outperforms LLaVA-1.5 across 12 benchmarks, with the 7B model surpassing LLaVA 13B on VisWiz and TextVQA."**

The head-to-head comparison in Table 5 supports this claim for the specific conditions tested: same prompts, same base LLM family (Llama-2/Vicuna-1.5), same SFT data blend (LLaVA-1.5's own SFT data, in some experiments). VILA-7B outperforms LLaVA-1.5-7B on 9 of 12 benchmarks. VILA-7B outperforms LLaVA-1.5-13B on 2 of 12 benchmarks (VisWiz: 57.8 vs. 53.6; TextVQA: 64.4 vs. 61.3). VILA-13B outperforms LLaVA-1.5-13B on 10 of 12 benchmarks. The margins on some tasks are substantial (TextVQA +5-6 points, VisWiz +7-8 points, LLaVA-Bench +6 points for 7B), while others are modest (MME +19-39 points out of ~2000, ~1-2%).

*Caveats:* Several qualifications are necessary for this claim. **First**, the comparison is not completely apples-to-apples on SFT data: VILA uses joint SFT with text-only data while LLaVA-1.5 does not. While the paper argues joint SFT is part of the enhanced recipe, it means the improvement cannot be attributed solely to pre-training—the SFT stage differs as well. **Second**, LLaVA-1.5 was not designed with the same compute budget; its pre-training stage is deliberately minimal (558K images, focusing on projector initialization), so VILA's 50M-image pre-training represents roughly 90× more pre-training data. VILA's improvement over LLaVA-1.5 demonstrates that more pre-training helps, but does not establish that VILA's specific pre-training recipe is optimal among all possible recipes at the same scale. **Third**, the paper does not ablate whether LLaVA-1.5's performance would also improve if simply given more pre-training data of the same type (image-text pairs) rather than switching to interleaved data—the improvement could be partly from data scale rather than data composition. **Fourth**, the benchmarks where VILA-7B loses to LLaVA-1.5-7B (VQA-v2: 78.5 vs. 79.9; POPE: 85.5 vs. 85.9; SEED-Bench: 58.6 vs. 61.1) show that pre-training does not uniformly improve all tasks, with SEED-Bench showing a notable regression (-2.5 points) that the paper does not discuss.

**Overall assessment of the experimental design:**

*Strengths:* The controlled ablation structure is the paper's strongest experimental feature. By varying one factor at a time (LLM update: Table 1; data type: Table 3; SFT composition: Table 4) while holding other elements constant (same base LLM, same SFT data for internal comparisons, same evaluation protocol), the paper generates genuinely causal evidence about which design choices matter. The use of both 0-shot and 4-shot evaluation throughout the ablations is methodologically important—it reveals effects (in-context learning degradation) that would be invisible in a 0-shot-only evaluation. The deep embedding alignment measurement in Figure 3 provides mechanistic evidence that complements the behavioral results. The explicit acknowledgment of limitations (50M image pre-training scale, no billion-scale test, no token compression in final models) is honest and helpful for interpreting the results.

*Weaknesses:* **First**, the ablation experiments use a different evaluation setting from the final models (different SFT data blend, possibly different evaluation protocols), and the paper explicitly states that absolute numbers in Tables 1, 3, 4 "can only be compared within the table." This means the ablations demonstrate *relative* effects of design choices under one setting, but do not directly predict the magnitude of effects under the final setting—the transferability of the conclusions is assumed but not verified. **Second**, the test sets for the ablation experiments are not described in detail (size, how many questions, whether the same examples are used across settings). For the final benchmark comparison, the test sets are standard, but the ablation evaluation's statistical reliability is unclear. **Third**, all ablation experiments use Llama-2-7B only—the design choices are validated at a single model scale before being applied to 7B and 13B final models. The appendix shows the core freezing-vs-updating result replicates with Vicuna-1.5-7B (Table 11), but no 13B ablation results are reported, so scale-dependent effects on the design choices themselves (e.g., whether interleaved data is more or less important at larger scales) are unexplored. **Fourth**, the joint SFT improvement is demonstrated at a single text-to-visual data ratio (1M text: ~1M visual). No ablation studies how the ratio affects outcomes—whether more text data would further improve VLM accuracy, or whether less would be sufficient for text recovery.

*Missing experiments that would have strengthened the paper:*
- An ablation varying pre-training data scale (e.g., 5M, 25M, 50M, 100M images) for both interleaved and pair-based formats to characterize whether the interleaved advantage is a scale-invariant property or diminishes with more data.
- A controlled comparison where LLaVA-1.5 receives the same amount of pre-training data as VILA but in its original pair-based format, to separate the effect of data scale from data composition.
- Per-dataset breakdown for the ablation tables (Tables 1, 3, 4) beyond the four datasets shown—particularly, whether the joint SFT VLM boost generalizes across diverse visual tasks or is concentrated on specific task types.
- A "text-only SFT only" control added to the VLM pre-trained model to isolate whether the VLM accuracy boost from joint SFT comes specifically from text data or simply from additional training data of any type.
- Ablation on the text-to-visual ratio in joint SFT to characterize the dose-response curve.
- Evaluation of the ablation configurations at 13B scale to verify that the identified design principles (update LLM, use interleaved data, apply joint SFT) scale with model size rather than being 7B-specific phenomena.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Pre-Training Data Scale Is Modest Relative to Production VLMs

The paper's pre-training corpus contains approximately 50 million images (25M from MMC4, 25M subsampled from COYO-700M). The authors explicitly acknowledge this constraint in Section 4.1:

> "Due to the limited compute budget, we have not been able to further scale up the size of the pre-training corpus to billion-scale, which we leave as future work. Nonetheless, pre-training on 50M images already demonstrated significant performance improvement."

This is roughly 10× fewer images than Flamingo's 353M, 28× fewer than Qwen-VL's reported 1.4B, and multiple orders of magnitude fewer than the datasets used to train proprietary VLMs whose pre-training corpus sizes are not publicly disclosed.

**Consequence:** The paper's design recommendations are validated at a single, relatively modest data scale. It is unknown whether the central findings—particularly the advantage of interleaved data over image-text pairs, and the importance of updating the LLM during pre-training—persist at billion-image scale. It is possible that with enough data, image-text pair pre-training could close the gap with interleaved pre-training (since the sheer volume of visual diversity might compensate for the weaker per-sample language modeling signal), or that freezing the LLM becomes viable when the projector has enough capacity and training data to achieve deeper alignment without LLM updates. The paper's claim that interleaved data is "essential" (Section 3.2) should therefore be interpreted as applying at the 25-50M image scale studied, not as a universal law. A practitioner deploying a VLM with access to web-scale data (billions of images) cannot assume from these results that interleaved data will outperform pair-based data by the same margins. The finding that MMC4-pairs extracted from interleaved documents performs *worse* than COYO alt-text pairs (Table 3: 46.4% vs. 51.1% 0-shot) despite having better text quality is a strong causal demonstration that data structure matters, but the magnitude of the effect at scale is unknown.

**Evidence in the paper:** The data scale is stated in Section 4.1 (50M images total, 1:1 blend). Table 3 compares data types at equivalent scale (~25M images each), controlling for this variable partially but not testing whether scaling up any single data type changes the relative ordering. No ablation on pre-training data scale is provided—the paper compares data types at fixed scale but never varies the scale of a given data type. The Appendix B training cost figures (5,120 GPU-hours for 7B, with pre-training consuming 75%) make clear that the 50M-image budget was a practical constraint, not a studied design point.

**Mitigation status:** The authors transparently flag this as a limitation in Section 4.1 and suggest scaling to billion-image pre-training as future work. No partial mitigation (e.g., extrapolation analysis, scaling trend characterization) is attempted. The paper's claim that the 50M-image results "already demonstrated significant performance improvement" is accurate for the scale tested but does not address whether further scaling would amplify, diminish, or reverse the observed effects.

### The Ablation Test Set and Final Benchmark Evaluation Are Conducted on Different SFT Data Blends, Limiting Direct Transferability of Ablation Conclusions

The paper's three main findings (Tables 1, 3, 4) are established using an in-house SFT data blend consisting of 18 visual language datasets in FLAN format, detailed in Appendix A (Table 10). The paper notes this blend is "mostly VQA-alike"—predominantly question-answering formatted data with limited diversity in task types. The final VILA models (Table 5), however, use a different SFT recipe: the LLaVA-1.5 SFT dataset, which includes higher-quality prompts, reference-based annotations (RefCOCO), and better overall diversity. The ablation evaluation uses four datasets (OKVQA, TextVQA, COCO, Flickr); the final evaluation uses 12 benchmarks with different scoring protocols.

The paper explicitly warns about this in the Table 1 caption:

> "We used a different evaluation setting just for ablation study; the absolute value in this setting is lower and should not be compared against other work."

**Consequence:** The design principles established in the ablations—update the LLM, use interleaved data, apply joint SFT—are validated under one training condition (in-house SFT blend, four evaluation datasets) but deployed under a different condition (LLaVA-1.5 SFT blend, 12 benchmarks). The paper assumes the relative ordering of design choices transfers across SFT recipes, but this transferability is not tested. It is possible that a design choice that looks optimal under the ablation SFT blend (e.g., linear vs. Transformer projector) would reverse under the higher-quality LLaVA-1.5 SFT blend, because the stronger SFT data might compensate for weaknesses introduced by suboptimal pre-training choices. Similarly, the joint SFT benefit observed in Table 4 measures the effect of adding 1M text-only FLAN examples to the in-house visual SFT blend—but the final VILA models add these 1M text examples to the LLaVA-1.5 SFT blend. The interaction between joint SFT and the specific visual SFT data used could change the magnitude (or even direction) of the benefit.

A practitioner reading the ablation results and deploying them with their own SFT data mixture cannot be certain that the observed 4-shot gains, MMLU recovery, and VLM accuracy improvements will transfer at similar magnitudes. The qualitative capability demonstrations (multi-image reasoning in Figure 6, in-context learning in Figure 7, visual chain-of-thought in Figure 8) are from the final VILA models, making it impossible to attribute these capabilities specifically to the pre-training recipe versus the SFT data quality—LLaVA-1.5 can also perform in-context learning "to some extent" (as the paper acknowledges in Section 4.3), and the paper does not provide ablation versions of these qualitative examples.

**Evidence in the paper:** The SFT data mismatch is explicitly described: Appendix A details the ablation SFT blend (18 datasets, VQA-heavy), while Section 4.1 states the final models use LLaVA-1.5's SFT data blend. Table 1's caption flags the evaluation protocol difference. No cross-SFT transfer experiment is provided—e.g., running the ablation configurations (frozen vs. updated LLM, MMC4 vs. COYO) through the LLaVA-1.5 SFT pipeline to verify the design choices transfer.

**Mitigation status:** The paper partially mitigates this by showing that the core design recommendation (update LLM during pre-training > freeze LLM) generalizes across LLM backbones (Vicuna-1.5-7B in Appendix C, Table 11), but this tests backbone transfer, not SFT data transfer. The authors do not address the SFT mismatch explicitly as a limitation. A reader must infer from the consistent improvement of VILA over LLaVA-1.5 in Table 5 that the pre-training recipe decisions transfer effectively to the final setting, but this is confirmation of the aggregate recipe rather than validation of each individual design choice.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost and Per-Example Overhead Are Not Accounted for, Limiting Practical Guidance on When the Method Is Worth Its Training Cost

The paper frames its contribution as a pre-training recipe that yields better downstream VLM performance than existing approaches (primarily LLaVA-1.5). However, the cost of this improved performance is a substantially longer pre-training stage: VILA's pre-training uses 50M images and requires 30 GPU-hours on 128 A100s for the 7B model (Appendix B), representing 75% of total training time. LLaVA-1.5's pre-training uses approximately 558K images—roughly 90× fewer—and focuses primarily on projector initialization rather than full visual language pre-training.

The paper does not provide a cost-benefit analysis: given the 90× increase in pre-training data and the associated compute cost, what is the expected improvement on downstream benchmarks? The improvement over LLaVA-1.5 across the 12 benchmarks in Table 5 averages approximately 3–7 percentage points on most tasks, with larger gains on specific tasks (VisWiz, TextVQA, LLaVA-Bench) and small regressions on others (POPE, SEED-Bench for 7B). Whether this improvement justifies the training cost depends heavily on the deployment context—a practitioner with limited compute may find that the marginal benefit of scaled pre-training does not justify the expenditure compared to improvements in SFT data quality (as evidenced by the ShareGPT4V addition in the last row of Table 5, which provides large gains on LLaVA-Bench and MM-Vet at the SFT stage without additional pre-training).

**Consequence:** The paper's conclusions establish that pre-training helps, but do not tell a practitioner *how much* pre-training is enough, or how to allocate a fixed training budget between pre-training data and SFT data. The 50M-image budget is presented as a fixed design choice rather than a studied variable—no ablation on pre-training corpus size is provided. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt VILA's pre-training recipe cannot determine from the paper's results whether 10M, 25M, or 100M images would be optimal for their use case, or whether the benefit of scaling from 25M to 50M images (the difference between the ablation MMC4-only configuration and the final MMC4+COYO blend) justifies the doubled data cost. The modest VLM accuracy gain from blending COYO with MMC4 at the ablation scale (+0.3% 0-shot in Table 3 under visual-only SFT, +1.3% under joint SFT in Table 4) suggests diminishing returns from adding more data of a given type, but without scale ablation this remains speculative.

**Evidence in the paper:** Appendix B provides training costs (30 hours pre-training, 6 hours SFT, 4 hours projector initialization for 7B on 128 A100s). The paper states LLaVA-1.5 uses 558K pre-training images (Table 5, PT column) without commenting on the scale discrepancy. The ShareGPT4V result (Table 5, last row) implicitly demonstrates that SFT data improvements can yield gains comparable to or larger than pre-training improvements on certain benchmarks (LLaVA-Bench +5.4 from ShareGPT4V vs. +6.3 from VILA pre-training over LLaVA-1.5-7B). No cost-benefit or scale analysis is attempted.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not address this as a limitation beyond acknowledging that the pre-training corpus is smaller than billion-scale datasets and that further scaling is future work. The absence of pre-training scale ablation means a practitioner cannot derive specific guidance on the pre-training budget from this paper alone.

### All Experiments Use a Single Model Family (Llama-2) and a Single Vision Encoder (CLIP-L), Leaving Generalization to Other Architectures Unverified

The paper's entire ablation study and final model family are built on Llama-2 (7B and 13B) as the LLM backbone and OpenAI CLIP-L as the vision encoder. The design recommendations—update the LLM during pre-training, use interleaved data, apply joint SFT—are established exclusively within this architecture stack. The paper does not test these recommendations with other LLM families (e.g., Mistral, Yi, Qwen, or proprietary models), other vision encoders (e.g., SigLIP, EVA-CLIP, ViT-G), or other VLM architectural variants (e.g., cross-attention-based models even as a comparison point for the freezing recommendation).

**Consequence:** Several of the paper's central claims may be architecture-dependent in ways that cannot be assessed from the reported experiments:

- **The linear projector advantage over Transformer projectors** (Table 1, settings c vs. d) may depend on Llama-2's specific architecture, hidden dimension, attention patterns, or pre-training objective. A different LLM with different inductive biases might benefit more from sophisticated visual preprocessing in the projector. The paper's explanation—that a simple projector forces the LLM to learn visual processing internally—assumes the LLM has sufficient capacity and representational flexibility to absorb this burden, which may not hold for smaller models or different architectures.

- **The deep embedding alignment hypothesis** (Figure 3) is measured using cosine similarity between visual and text token representations in Llama-2's transformer layers. This measurement depends on specific properties of Llama-2's internal representations (dimensionality, activation distributions, layer normalization placement) and may not produce comparable patterns in other LLMs. The conclusion that increasing deep-layer similarity correlates with improved in-context learning (and that frozen pre-training prevents this deepening) would need to be verified with at least one alternative backbone to be considered a general principle rather than a Llama-2-specific observation.

- **The catastrophic forgetting behavior** observed with COYO pre-training (Table 3: -17.2% MMLU) and its mitigation through interleaved data (-5.3% MMLU) reflect Llama-2's specific pre-training data distribution. A model pre-trained on different text corpora (e.g., more multilingual data, code-heavy mixtures, or curated high-quality sources) might exhibit different sensitivity to distribution shift from image-text pair captions. The paper's explanation—that the mechanism is text distribution shift, not visual interference—is plausible but untested across LLMs with different text pre-training distributions.

- **The visual expert and LoRA comparisons** (Tables 8 and 9) show specific approaches underperforming full fine-tuning, but these findings depend on the specific implementations tested (CogVLM's MoE-style experts, rank-64 LoRA). Other frozen-LLM or parameter-efficient approaches might perform differently; the paper's conclusion that full fine-tuning is necessary may overgeneralize from the specific alternatives tested.

**Evidence in the paper:** The only cross-backbone experiment is in Appendix C (Table 11), which replicates the freezing-vs-updating ablation with Vicuna-1.5-7B as an alternative LLM backbone. The core pattern holds (frozen pre-training degrades in-context learning, updating the LLM enables it), showing that at least the update-vs-freeze finding transfers across two closely related Llama-derived models. The final benchmark results with Vicuna backbone are similar to Llama-2 backbone (VQAv2 79.3 vs. 79.9, GQA 62.3 vs. 62.3, VisWiz 58.7 vs. 57.8), suggesting overall comparable performance. However, Vicuna-1.5 is itself fine-tuned from Llama-2 with minimal architectural changes—this does not test generalization to substantially different model families.

**Mitigation status:** The Vicuna-1.5 replication in Appendix C is a partial mitigation for the backbone-specific concern, demonstrating that at least one design choice (freeze vs. update) transfers across closely related models. The authors do not claim their findings generalize to all architectures, but the paper's framing as providing a "pre-training recipe for future visual language models" (Section 1) implicitly asserts broader applicability. No mitigation is provided for the vision encoder specificity or for the other design choices (data type, projector design, SFT composition) that are only tested with Llama-2 + CLIP-L.

### The Training and Evaluation Framework Does Not Account for Deployment Latency, Limiting Practical Guidance for Real-Time or Resource-Constrained Applications

The paper evaluates VILA exclusively in terms of benchmark accuracy and text capability preservation, with no measurement or discussion of inference latency, throughput, memory usage, or on-device deployability beyond a brief statement in the abstract and a note about the visual expert approach being "not friendly for edge deployment" (Section 4.4). The paper mentions that VILA is "deployable on Jetson Orin for on-device VLM" (abstract) but provides no latency benchmarks, memory measurements, or deployment configuration details.

This is a significant omission because the paper's design recommendations have implicit computational consequences:

- **Updating the LLM during pre-training** produces a model where the LLM weights are modified for visual processing. This is architecturally simpler than frozen-LLM + adapter approaches (no separate adapter modules to load/manage at inference time), but the full LLM must be loaded and executed for every inference, regardless of input modality. A frozen-LLM approach could theoretically unload visual processing components for text-only queries, reducing memory pressure.

- **Using 336×336 resolution with 576 visual tokens** (Section 4.4) increases the sequence length fed to the LLM by 576 tokens per image, directly increasing the quadratic self-attention cost. The paper notes this in the context of in-context learning limitations ("it limits the number of demonstrations," Section 4.4) but does not quantify the latency impact. The downsample projector experiment (Table 7) shows that compressing to 144 tokens recovers most of the resolution benefit for TextVQA (45.6% vs. 49.8%), suggesting that the full 576-token configuration trades off significant computational overhead for a relatively modest accuracy gain on most tasks. A practitioner deploying a VLM in a latency-sensitive application would need this tradeoff quantified.

- **Joint SFT** produces a model that performs well on both text-only and visual tasks (Table 6), meaning a single model can serve both modalities—a deployment simplification. However, the model still processes the full visual token sequence for image inputs, meaning per-query cost varies dramatically between text-only and image queries, which may complicate load balancing and resource provisioning in production systems.

- **The 13B model's text capability advantage** (Table 6: VILA-13B outperforms Llama-2+SFT-13B on MMLU, BBH, DROP) is a positive result, but 13B parameter models with 576-token image prefixes represent a substantial per-query computational cost that may be prohibitive for many deployment scenarios. The paper does not compare VILA-7B with compute-optimal inference strategies against VILA-13B with cheaper inference to help practitioners choose a size.

**Evidence in the paper:** The latency/relevance omission is evident from what is absent: no latency measurements, no throughput numbers, no memory profiling, no comparison of inference cost between configurations. The paper provides training time in GPU-hours (Appendix B) but not inference time. The token compression experiment (Table 7) is presented as demonstrating "large redundancy in image tokens" but the final models do not use compression—the paper explicitly chooses not to deploy this optimization "to provide the best accuracy," leaving the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff unresolved. The Jetson Orin deployability claim is stated without supporting evidence.

**Mitigation status:** The paper presents this as a training methodology study and does not claim to optimize for inference efficiency. However, given that VLM deployment constraints (latency, memory, cost) are often the binding constraints in practice, and that the paper's abstract specifically mentions on-device deployment, the absence of any inference efficiency characterization is a meaningful gap. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt VILA's full recipe (336×336 resolution, 13B parameters, 576 visual tokens) versus a more efficient variant (224×224, 7B, compressed tokens, or even a frozen-LLM approach) receives no guidance from this paper on the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff.

### The Paper Does Not Investigate Whether Pre-Training Benefits Transfer to Video, Multi-Turn Dialogue, or Interactive Agent Settings

All of VILA's evaluation is conducted on static image benchmarks: single-image question answering, captioning, and instruction following (Table 5) with the exception of a handful of qualitative multi-image examples (Figures 6, 8, 9, and Appendix E). The pre-training corpus (MMC4 + COYO) consists entirely of static images. The paper demonstrates impressive emergent multi-image reasoning (Figure 6: comparing three flamingo images, finding differences between two cartoon images) and chain-of-thought reasoning (Figure 8), but these are evaluated qualitatively on cherry-picked examples with no quantitative benchmark results.

**Consequence:** A practitioner considering VILA for applications beyond static image understanding—video question answering, embodied agent control, multi-turn visual dialogue, or interactive visual reasoning—cannot assess from this paper whether the pre-training recipe transfers to these settings. The interleaved pre-training on MMC4 (web documents with embedded images) might actually provide a better foundation for video understanding than image-text pair pre-training, since MMC4's structure mimics the temporal narrative of video (image, then related text, then another image, then related text). But this hypothesis is untested. Conversely, the 576-token-per-image cost means that video applications (which might require tens or hundreds of frames) would face prohibitive sequence lengths unless token compression is used—but the paper does not evaluate token compression in the final models or on multi-frame inputs. The qualitative multi-image results in Section 4.3 are suggestive but insufficient to guide deployment decisions for video or multi-image applications.

**Evidence in the paper:** The evaluation benchmarks (Table 5) are all single-image tasks. The qualitative multi-image examples (Figures 6, 8, 10, 14) involve 2–3 images each and demonstrate capability existence but not capability reliability. No quantitative multi-image benchmark (e.g., NLVR2, which involves pairs of images, or video QA datasets) is evaluated. The paper mentions that the model only sees single-image samples during SFT ("despite the SFT data is composed of single-image samples," Section 4.3), making the multi-image reasoning an emergent property of the pre-training stage—but the robustness and failure modes of this emergent capability are uncharacterized.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The qualitative multi-image demonstrations are presented as evidence that pre-training "unlocked several interesting capabilities" (Section 4.3), but the paper does not discuss whether these capabilities are reliable enough for production use or how they scale with the number of input images. The observation that multi-image reasoning emerges despite single-image SFT is scientifically interesting but practically insufficient: a practitioner deploying a multi-image VLM would need quantitative accuracy numbers on multi-image benchmarks to make an informed decision. Future work on extending VILA to video or multi-image settings is not suggested in the paper's conclusion or limitations discussion.

**Evidence of mitigation for the previous (deleted) limitation:** N/A—this limitation is not discussed in the paper. The visual expert comparison (Table 8) and the note about "1.9× model size increase, which is not friendly for edge deployment" (Section 4.4) constitute the paper's only acknowledgment of deployment considerations, and these focus on parameter count rather than the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff in specific application domains.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the VLM research conversation from an almost exclusive focus on instruction tuning innovations toward a recognition that **pre-training is an equally critical and under-explored stage** with design choices that fundamentally determine which LLM capabilities survive the transition to multi-modality. Prior to this work, the dominant paradigm—exemplified by LLaVA-1.5's minimal pre-training on 558K image-text pairs followed by careful instruction tuning—treated pre-training as a necessary initialization step rather than a source of capability. The paper's demonstration that scaling pre-training to 50M images with the right data composition yields consistent 3–7 point improvements across 12 benchmarks, including cases where a 7B VILA model surpasses a 13B LLaVA-1.5 model, establishes pre-training as a first-class design lever rather than a perfunctory warmup stage.

**This is a reframing, not a paradigm shift.** The underlying architecture (auto-regressive VLM with linear projector) and training pipeline (projector init → pre-training → SFT) remain unchanged from prior work. What changes is the **allocation of attention and compute across stages**. The paper argues, implicitly through its results and explicitly through its ablation structure, that the field has over-invested in SFT recipe improvements and under-invested in understanding pre-training fundamentals. The finding that a linear projector with full LLM updating outperforms a Transformer projector (Table 1, settings c vs. d: 68.7% vs. 66.8% 0-shot) inverts the standard engineering intuition that more sophisticated modality bridges are better, suggesting that the pre-training stage's design space contains non-obvious optima that have been overlooked precisely because they are counterintuitive.

The paper resolves a latent tension in the literature between two schools of thought that had developed in parallel without direct confrontation:

- **The frozen-LLM school** (Flamingo, BLIP-2, InstructBLIP, CogVLM) argued that preserving text capabilities requires keeping the LLM weights unchanged, accepting architectural complexity (cross-attention layers, Q-Formers, visual expert modules) as the cost. The intuition was that text and vision compete for representational capacity, and the only way to guarantee text preservation is to physically prevent the LLM from changing.

- **The fine-tuning school** (LLaVA, PaLM-E, PaLI-X) argued that deep visual understanding requires the LLM to adapt internally, accepting text capability degradation as the cost. The intuition was that frozen adapters can only achieve shallow alignment and will never match the reasoning depth of models where the LLM learns to process visual tokens natively.

The paper's joint SFT experiment (Table 4) demonstrates that **this tradeoff is false**. Text capabilities are not permanently lost during pre-training; they are temporarily suppressed and fully recoverable through modest re-exposure to text-only data during SFT. The MMLU degradation under the best pre-training configuration (–5.3%, Table 3, MMC4 interleaved) is fully reversed by adding 1M text-only FLAN examples to SFT (Table 4: MMLU 40.7% → 51.4%, matching Llama-2+SFT's 51.2%). This means the frozen-LLM school's primary motivation—text capability preservation—is addressable through a training recipe change rather than an architectural one. The paper's comparison to visual experts (Table 8) shows that the frozen-LLM approach not only requires 1.9× parameters but achieves *worse* VLM accuracy (67.0% vs. 71.0% 0-shot) and fails to enable in-context learning (4-shot drops to 64.8% vs. rising to 72.1%). The architectural complexity that defined a major research program is rendered unnecessary by a finding about training dynamics.

The paper also resolves the specific question of **why interleaved pre-training data matters** in a way that prior work had not. Flamingo (Alayrac et al., 2022) used interleaved data (M3W) and demonstrated its effectiveness, but the mechanism was unclear—was it the longer text, the image-text correspondence structure, or something else? The paper's MMC4-pairs ablation (Table 3: same text and images as MMC4 interleaved, broken into isolated pairs) isolates the structural contribution: VLM accuracy drops from 68.7% to 46.4% 0-shot, and MMLU degradation worsens from –5.3% to –13.6%, despite the *content* being identical. The mechanism is that interleaved structure preserves the text distribution the LLM was originally trained on (document-length, multi-sentence text with discourse coherence) and provides a richer language modeling signal (Figure 5: consistently lower training loss). This transforms interleaved data from an empirical trick (used by Flamingo) into a principled requirement for any VLM pre-training recipe where the LLM is updated.

**The research directions that become more attractive after this work:**

- **Scaling pre-training data** with proper composition (interleaved + pairs blend) rather than just scaling SFT data quality. The paper's 50M-image pre-training already shows gains over LLaVA-1.5's 558K-image pre-training; the natural question is whether these gains continue monotonically with scale or saturate. The paper's acknowledgment that their pre-training corpus is smaller than billion-scale datasets used by some prior work (Flamingo's 353M, Qwen-VL's 1.4B) sets up an obvious scaling experiment.

- **Re-evaluating architectural decisions** through the lens of pre-training dynamics rather than static capacity arguments. The finding that a linear projector outperforms a Transformer projector (Table 1, settings c vs. d) because it forces the LLM to do more internal learning suggests that the optimal architectural choices for a given modality bridge may depend on the pre-training recipe—and that "simpler is better" may be a general principle when the base model is powerful enough to absorb the modality adaptation internally.

- **Studying in-context learning as a VLM evaluation criterion**, not just zero-shot accuracy. The paper's finding that frozen pre-training produces identical zero-shot performance to updated pre-training (Table 1: 66.8% vs. 66.8%) but dramatically worse few-shot performance (57.6% vs. 68.8%) means that most existing VLM evaluations—which are exclusively zero-shot—are blind to a critical capability dimension. Future VLM work that only reports zero-shot numbers may be missing hidden degradation that the paper's 4-shot evaluation reveals.

**The research directions that become less attractive:**

- **Developing increasingly complex modality adapters** (Q-Formers, perceiver resamplers, cross-attention modules, visual experts) motivated by text capability preservation. If joint SFT can recover text capabilities without architectural safeguards, the additional parameters, engineering complexity, and deployment overhead of these approaches are difficult to justify—especially when Table 8 shows they underperform simple linear projection with full fine-tuning on both accuracy (71.0% vs. 67.0% 0-shot) and in-context learning (72.1% vs. 64.8% 4-shot).

- **Treating pre-training as a minimal initialization step.** The LLaVA-1.5 approach of using pre-training only for projector warmup (558K images, 0.6M examples) is shown to leave significant performance on the table. A research program that focuses exclusively on SFT data quality improvements while neglecting pre-training scale and composition will hit a ceiling that this paper demonstrates can be raised through better pre-training.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterizing the scaling behavior of interleaved vs. pair-based pre-training data.** The paper's central finding—that interleaved data dramatically outperforms image-text pairs at ~25M image scale (Table 3: 68.7% vs. 51.1% 0-shot)—is established at a single data scale. The critical open question is whether this advantage persists, diminishes, or reverses at larger scales. A follow-up would train VLMs on MMC4-only, COYO-only (or LAION-only), and blended corpora at multiple data scales (e.g., 10M, 50M, 200M, 1B images), measuring both VLM accuracy and MMLU degradation. The hypothesis from the paper's mechanism (text distribution shift causes forgetting) predicts that pair-based data will eventually catch up in VLM accuracy as visual diversity saturates, but text degradation will persist unless the pair captions' distribution is explicitly matched to the LLM's pre-training text. If instead pair-based data at billion-image scale matches or exceeds interleaved data on both metrics, it would suggest that the paper's findings are scale-dependent and that the field should prioritize data quantity over data structure for pre-training.

**Testing whether joint SFT recovers text capabilities across diverse LLM families and pre-training text distributions.** The paper demonstrates text recovery for Llama-2-7B and Llama-2-13B (Tables 4 and 6), but the mechanism—"temporarily hidden, but not forgotten"—may depend on specific properties of Llama-2's pre-training data distribution and architecture. A follow-up would replicate the core experiment (interleaved pre-training → MMLU degradation → joint SFT recovery) across a panel of LLM families: Mistral, Yi, Qwen, Gemma, and a code-heavy model like DeepSeek-Coder. The prediction: models pre-trained on text distributions closer to MMC4's web-article text (e.g., models trained primarily on CommonCrawl) will show smaller initial MMLU degradation from interleaved pre-training and faster recovery under joint SFT, while models pre-trained on significantly different distributions (code-heavy, multilingual, curated) may show larger degradation or incomplete recovery. If the recovery is universal, it strengthens the paper's claim that text capabilities are inherently resilient to distribution shift during multi-modal training; if recovery varies by backbone, it suggests the paper's recipe may need backbone-specific tuning.

**Quantifying the contribution of pre-training vs. SFT data quality through factorial experiment design.** The paper's final VILA models combine enhanced pre-training (50M images, interleaved + pairs) with the LLaVA-1.5 SFT blend—two simultaneous changes from the LLaVA-1.5 baseline that are not factorially separated. A follow-up would run a 2×2 experiment: {LLaVA-style minimal pre-training, VILA-style enhanced pre-training} × {LLaVA-1.5 SFT blend, VILA joint SFT blend}, measuring all 12 benchmarks. The ShareGPT4V result in Table 5 (last row: LLaVA-Bench +5.4, MM-Vet +6.9 from SFT data improvement alone) suggests SFT data quality has substantial independent effects. The factorial design would reveal whether pre-training and SFT improvements are additive, sub-additive (diminishing returns from improving both), or super-additive (synergistic). If improvements are sub-additive, a practitioner with limited compute should invest in the single stage that yields the largest marginal gain; if super-additive, the full recipe is necessary to realize the benefits.

**Investigating whether pre-training enables reliable multi-image and video understanding, with quantitative benchmarks rather than cherry-picked examples.** The paper's qualitative demonstrations of multi-image reasoning (Figures 6, 8, 10, and Appendix E) are suggestive but provide no information about reliability, failure modes, or scaling with the number of input images. A follow-up would evaluate VILA on established multi-image benchmarks: NLVR2 (image pairs with binary true/false judgments about visual statements), VCR (visual commonsense reasoning requiring understanding of multiple image regions), and video QA datasets (MSRVTT-QA, ActivityNet-QA) with frame sampling. The specific questions: does interleaved pre-training provide a measurable advantage over pair-based pre-training on multi-image tasks, and does this advantage persist when the model is fine-tuned on multi-image SFT data (unlike VILA's single-image-only SFT)? If the emergent multi-image capability proves robust under quantitative evaluation, it would suggest that interleaved pre-training is particularly valuable for temporal and comparative visual reasoning tasks—and that SFT data for such tasks may be less necessary than previously assumed.

**Determining the optimal pre-training data mixture ratio between interleaved and pair-based data.** The paper uses a rough 1:1 image ratio for the MMC4+COYO blend (Section 4.1) and observes a small VLM accuracy benefit over MMC4-only (+0.3% 0-shot under visual-only SFT, +1.3% under joint SFT in Tables 3 and 4). This ratio is not ablated. A follow-up would sweep mixture ratios (e.g., 100:0, 75:25, 50:50, 25:75, 0:100 interleaved:pair images) while holding total image count constant, measuring VLM accuracy, MMLU degradation, and in-context learning. The paper's mechanism predicts a U-shaped curve for MMLU degradation: too many pairs (short captions → distribution shift) and the degradation worsens; too few pairs and visual diversity may be insufficient. For VLM accuracy, the prediction is less clear—it depends on whether COYO's visual diversity provides benefits that MMC4's images lack. If the optimal ratio is task-dependent (e.g., more pairs help TextVQA by providing diverse text-in-image examples, but more interleaved data helps reasoning tasks), the finding would motivate task-adaptive pre-training data composition.

**Evaluating whether the "simpler projector is better" finding generalizes to other modality bridges and model scales.** The paper's finding that a linear projector outperforms a Transformer projector (Table 1: 68.7% vs. 66.8% 0-shot) is explained by the hypothesis that simpler projectors force the LLM to develop deeper cross-modal alignment. This hypothesis makes testable predictions: (1) the advantage of simpler projectors should grow with LLM capacity (larger models can absorb more of the processing burden), (2) it should be specific to modalities where the base model has sufficient representational capacity to learn the mapping internally (vision maybe, but what about audio spectrograms or robot state vectors?), and (3) it should diminish if the pre-training data is limited (weak supervision requires stronger inductive biases from the projector). A follow-up would test these predictions by comparing linear, MLP, and Transformer projectors across model scales (1B, 7B, 13B, 70B) and modalities (vision, audio, structured sensor data), measuring both accuracy and the deep alignment metric from Figure 3. If the predictions hold, the principle becomes a general design rule for multi-modal model training; if they fail at certain scales or modalities, it would clarify the boundary conditions.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient VLM training for organizations with limited compute budgets.** The paper's core finding—that a 7B model with enhanced pre-training can match or exceed a 13B model with minimal pre-training on specific fine-grained tasks (Table 5: VILA-7B achieves 64.4% TextVQA vs. LLaVA-1.5-13B's 61.3%, and 57.8% VisWiz vs. 53.6%)—directly translates to deployment efficiency. Training a 7B model with 50M-image pre-training (5,120 GPU-hours per Appendix B) is substantially cheaper than training and serving a 13B model. For a team building a VLM for document understanding or accessibility applications where TextVQA and VisWiz are the primary benchmarks, VILA-7B provides better accuracy at lower serving cost than the larger alternative. The training recipe is fully specified (MMC4 + COYO pre-training, linear projector, joint SFT with FLAN text data), making it reproducible without proprietary data or architectures.

**On-device VLM deployment where parameter count and inference latency are binding constraints.** The paper notes VILA is deployable on Jetson Orin (abstract) and emphasizes that full fine-tuning with a linear projector produces a model requiring 1× parameters rather than the 1.9× required by visual expert approaches (Table 8). For on-device applications—accessibility tools that describe images to visually impaired users, document readers that extract text from photographed forms, or augmented reality assistants that answer questions about the user's environment—the combination of a single 7B parameter footprint (no separate adapter modules to load) and the linear projector's minimal computational overhead makes VILA a pragmatic choice over frozen-LLM alternatives that require additional components. The token compression result (Table 7: downsample projector at 336×336 achieves 45.6% TextVQA with only 144 tokens vs. 49.8% with 576 tokens) suggests a path to further latency reduction that the paper identifies but does not deploy—a practitioner could implement this compression to trade a modest accuracy drop for substantially faster inference. The observation that the model maintains competitive text-only performance (Table 6: VILA-7B achieves 50.8% MMLU vs. 51.8% for Llama-2+SFT) means a single on-device model can serve both text-only and visual queries without loading separate weights.

**Data augmentation and caption generation for training text-to-image generative models.** The paper's qualitative demonstration of detailed captioning (Appendix E, Figure 12) shows VILA generating richer, more accurate image descriptions than the original captions in LAION or the outputs of BLIP-2. For teams training diffusion models (Stable Diffusion, DALL-E, Imagen) where caption quality directly affects generation quality, VILA could serve as a re-captioning engine: process a dataset of images through VILA to generate detailed, accurate descriptions, then train the generative model on these improved captions. The paper's finding that VILA's captions are more "related to the images" than original LAION captions and "provide more details compared to previous models like BLIP-2" (Appendix E) suggests that downstream generative model quality would improve, though this is not tested. The joint SFT recipe (Table 4) ensures the captioning model retains strong text capabilities, meaning it can generate diverse, well-formed descriptions rather than repetitive or simplistic captions—a known failure mode when captioning models are fine-tuned exclusively on visual data and lose linguistic fluency.

**Self-improvement pipelines where VLM-generated captions bootstrap better pre-training data for the next generation of VLMs.** The paper demonstrates that pre-training data quality and composition matter substantially for downstream performance. This creates a natural bootstrapping loop: use VILA to generate high-quality captions and interleaved-style annotations for a large corpus of images, then use this improved data to pre-train the next generation of VLMs. The findings that COYO's short captions cause catastrophic forgetting (–17.2% MMLU, Table 3) while MMC4's document-length text preserves capabilities (–5.3%) imply that the text distribution of auto-generated captions should match natural document-length text, not alt-text-style snippets—a concrete design constraint for the captioning prompt used in such a bootstrapping pipeline. The paper's observation that the interleaved structure specifically enables visual in-context learning (MMC4-pairs vs. MMC4 interleaved: 4-shot 44.5% vs. 70.9%) suggests that generated pre-training data should preserve document structure with images interleaved with related text segments, rather than being collected as isolated image-text pairs.
