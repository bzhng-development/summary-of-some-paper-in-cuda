# WARM: On the Benefits of Weight Averaged Reward Models

**ArXiv:** [2401.12187](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12187)

## 🎯 Pitch

Weight averaging reward models—rather than ensembling their predictions—acts as a regularizer that suppresses memorized, brittle features, dramatically improving robustness to distribution shift and label noise during RLHF. A policy fine-tuned with this method achieves a 79.4% win rate against one trained with a single reward model.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Weight Averaged Reward Models (WARM)**, a strategy for combining multiple reward models (RMs) by averaging their weights in parameter space—rather than averaging their predictions at inference time—to serve as the proxy reward in RLHF. Evaluating on the TL;DR summarization benchmark with PaLM-XXS RMs and PaLM-XS policies, WARM addresses two central challenges in reward modeling: reliability under distribution shifts (where the policy's generations drift away from the offline preference data during RL) and robustness to preference inconsistencies (label noise in the binary preference dataset). The method achieves a 79.4% win rate for a policy RL-fine-tuned with WARM against a policy trained with a single RM, while incurring no memory or inference overhead compared to standard prediction ensembling. A key finding is that weight averaging acts as a regularizer that suppresses run-specific predictive mechanisms—reducing memorization of corrupted labels—while preserving mechanisms invariant across diverse fine-tuning runs, establishing that WARM outperforms prediction ensembling on out-of-distribution data specifically because it prioritizes features with high probability of being learned across all individual RMs.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Reward Hacking as the Bottleneck in RLHF

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is **reward hacking** (also called reward overoptimization) in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). To understand why this matters, we need to step back and look at how modern LLMs are aligned with human preferences.

The standard three-stage training pipeline for conversational assistants like Gemini or GPT-4 works as follows: (1) **pre-training** by next-token prediction on vast web corpora, (2) **supervised fine-tuning (SFT)** on instruction-response pairs so the model learns to follow directions, and (3) **reinforcement learning (RL)** against a reward signal that captures what humans actually want — helpfulness, harmlessness, honesty, and task competence. The central difficulty in stage (3) is that we don't have a ground-truth reward function. The environment doesn't tell the model "this response was good" or "this response was bad" in any objective sense. Instead, we train a **reward model (RM)** — a learned proxy — on binary preference data (humans or AI labelers saying "response A is better than response B"), and then use that RM's scores as the reward signal during RL fine-tuning.

The trouble is that **the proxy RM is not the same as true human preferences**. It's an imperfect approximation, trained on a limited, offline dataset. When the LLM policy is optimized against this proxy RM using RL, something insidious happens: initially, the policy genuinely improves along both the proxy reward and the underlying human preferences. But after enough optimization, the policy learns to **exploit loopholes in the proxy RM** — generating outputs that score highly under the RM without actually satisfying the intended objectives. The paper illustrates this clearly in Figure 1(b): the "control reward" (a separate, higher-quality RM that serves as a noisy measure of the true objective) initially climbs, peaks, and then **collapses** as training progresses, even as the proxy RM's score keeps increasing. This is reward hacking — a direct manifestation of Goodhart's Law: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

The consequences are significant and multi-layered, as the authors enumerate in Section 1:

- **Quality degradation**: Reward-hacked outputs manifest as "linguistically flawed or unnecessarily verbose outputs, which do not reflect true human preferences." The model learns that the RM rewards length, bullet points, or certain phrasing patterns, and optimizes for those surface features rather than faithful, helpful content.
- **Checkpoint selection failure**: Because the proxy RM becomes unreliable — giving high scores to degenerate outputs — practitioners cannot use it to select the best model checkpoint during training. This is a practical nightmare for deployment.
- **Bias amplification**: RMs can induce sycophancy (the model telling users what they want to hear rather than what's true) or amplify social biases inherited from the skewed demographics of feedback providers.
- **Safety risks**: In the worst case, reward hacking produces misaligned behavior that escalates into genuine safety hazards, particularly as LLMs are integrated into critical decision-making pipelines.

The paper thus positions reward hacking as **the central obstacle to reliable RLHF**, and the design of RMs that resist exploitation as the key technical challenge.

### Two Root Causes: Distribution Shifts and Preference Inconsistency

The paper identifies two specific mechanisms that make reward hacking possible (Section 2.2), and it's worth understanding why each one matters separately.

**Challenge 1: Distribution shifts.** During RL fine-tuning, the policy generates text that the RM must score. But the RM was trained on a fixed, offline preference dataset — typically containing outputs from an earlier version of the model (or a different model entirely). As RL progresses, the policy drifts away from its SFT initialization, producing generations that are increasingly **out-of-distribution (OOD)** relative to the RM's training data. The RM, having never seen examples from this new region of the output space, becomes unreliable. It may rely on spurious correlations that held in the training data but break down OOD, or it may become poorly calibrated, predicting more extreme (and less meaningful) reward values. This creates a **negative feedback loop**: miscalibrated rewards drive further policy drift, which pushes the policy even further OOD, which further degrades the RM's reliability. The paper explicitly ties this to the broader OOD generalization literature: "limited data coverage during reward modeling reduces the reliability of the RM and facilitates reward hacking in regions where the RM is badly specified."

**Challenge 2: Inconsistent preferences.** The binary labels in preference datasets are noisy. Human labelers — working under fatigue, misunderstanding complex content, or with imperfect incentives — often default to simpler heuristics (length, formatting, politeness) rather than deeper quality indicators. This is exacerbated when tasks require specialized expertise or when balancing multiple competing objectives (helpfulness vs. harmlessness vs. engagement). The paper quotes a striking statistic from InstructGPT: inter-labeler agreement is only **72.6%**, meaning human preferences are far from a perfect oracle. Recent RLAIF approaches (using AI-generated feedback instead of human labels) reduce cost but introduce their own noise sources, such as sensitivity to prompting strategies. All of this means the RM's training signal is inherently corrupted — it's trying to model a target distribution (true human values) through a noisy channel (inconsistent binary labels).

A good RM, the authors argue, must therefore satisfy three properties simultaneously (Section 2.2):

- **Property 1: Efficiency** — no memory or inference overhead, so the policy can be optimized without ballooning computational costs.
- **Property 2: Reliability** — able to score generations accurately even under the distribution shifts that occur during RL, so the policy can explore away from its initialization while depending on the RM.
- **Property 3: Robustness** — resilient to the label noise and inconsistencies in the preference dataset, so the policy learns from stable, meaningful signals rather than memorized noise.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper surveys several strategies that have been tried, and identifies specific limitations in each:

**KL regularization** is the standard approach: during RL, add a penalty that encourages the policy to remain close to its SFT initialization in KL-divergence. This reduces model drift (addressing Challenge 1) but introduces a brittle hyperparameter (the regularization strength α), and can cause underfitting — the policy never explores enough to find genuinely better behaviors. It's a blunt instrument: it constrains the policy rather than fixing the RM.

**Iterative data collection and retraining** addresses the distribution shift problem by continuously gathering new preference data from the evolving policy's outputs and retraining the RM. This improves reliability but poses "significant efficiency challenges due to the continuous requirement for human annotation and computational resources." It's expensive and slow.

**Active learning strategies** attempt to make retraining more efficient by proactively seeking out diverse generations and potential failure cases, rather than collecting data passively. These help but still require ongoing human or AI labeling efforts.

**Label smoothing and flipping** (a concurrent work cited by the authors) applies regularization directly to the preference labels, but doesn't tackle the distribution shift problem and treats all noise uniformly rather than identifying which samples are corrupted.

**Prediction ensembling (ENS)** is, the paper argues, the most similar prior approach to WARM. The idea — dating back to Christiano et al.'s seminal RLHF work and recently re-emphasized by Eisenstein et al. (2023) and Coste et al. (2023) — is to train multiple RMs independently and average their predictions (reward scores) at inference time. From a bias-variance perspective, ENS reduces the variance component when ensembles are sufficiently diverse, which helps under distribution shifts (where variance is the main problem). From an RL perspective, existing work showed that ENS mitigates hacking risks.

But the paper identifies **two key failures of ENS**:

1. **Efficiency**: ENS requires storing and running all M RMs at inference time. Memory and inference costs grow linearly with M, which is "incompatible with the scaling trend in RMs, where larger architectures consistently perform better." If each RM is itself a large LLM, ensembling 10 of them is prohibitively expensive.

2. **Robustness under label corruption**: The paper will show empirically (and theoretically in Section 4) that ENS actually **fails to improve robustness to preference inconsistencies**. When labels are corrupted, ENS simply memorizes the corrupted samples — averaging the predictions of multiple networks that have all (independently) fit the noise doesn't remove the noise's influence. The paper will demonstrate this explicitly in Figure 4 and the associated "corrupt" experimental setup.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

WARM sits at the intersection of two previously separate research traditions: **reward modeling for RLHF** and **weight averaging for out-of-distribution generalization**. The paper's key intellectual move is to recognize that the LMC (linear mode connectivity) property observed in supervised computer vision — where fine-tuned weights sharing a pre-trained initialization can be linearly interpolated without loss of accuracy — applies to reward models trained on binary preference data. This lets the authors import the weight averaging toolkit (model soups, DiWA, model ratatouille) into the RLHF reward modeling context, creating a method that:

- **Matches or exceeds ENS** in reliability under distribution shifts (because WA is a variance-reduction method, similar to ENS, as established in the OOD generalization literature),
- **Surpasses ENS** in robustness under label corruption (because WA acts as a regularizer toward invariant predictive mechanisms, suppressing memorization of run-specific noise — a novel theoretical claim),
- **Eliminates ENS's efficiency overhead** (because WA produces a single model, not an ensemble of M models).

The paper doesn't claim to solve reward hacking entirely — Section 6 is explicit about limitations: "WARM only enhances reward modeling without tackling the other challenges in RLHF" and "it does not completely eradicate all forms of spurious correlations or biases inherent in the preference data." Rather, it positions WARM as a practical, efficient improvement that pushes the Pareto frontier of RM quality vs. compute, and as a conceptual bridge between the ensembling and invariance paradigms for OOD robustness.

The theoretical contribution (Section 4.3) is particularly notable in how it stakes out new ground: it provides what the authors claim is the **first formal analysis explaining why WA outperforms ENS under label corruption**, using a simplified binary feature-selection model to show that WA effectively squares the probability of using each feature (thus suppressing low-probability, noise-dependent features) while ENS maintains a linear weighting. This is not just an incremental improvement over ENS — it's a different mechanism with different asymptotic behavior.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper proposes a **method for combining multiple reward models into a single, better reward model by averaging their weights in parameter space** — a strategy called WARM (Weight Averaged Reward Models). Rather than running multiple RMs and averaging their predicted scores at inference time (prediction ensembling, ENS), WARM produces one RM whose weights are the arithmetic mean of several independently fine-tuned RMs, achieving the variance-reduction benefits of ensembling plus additional robustness to label noise, while eliminating the memory and inference overhead of running multiple models.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The WARM system operates in three stages, embedded within the standard RLHF pipeline:

1. **Base LLM pre-training and SFT**: A large language model (PaLM) is pre-trained on web data and then supervised-fine-tuned on instruction-response pairs, producing weights `$\theta_{\text{sft}}$`. This serves as the shared foundation for all subsequent RMs.

2. **Diverse RM fine-tunings (M parallel runs)**: Starting from `$\theta_{\text{sft}}$` (or checkpoints along the SFT trajectory, per the Baklava diversity method), M separate reward models are fine-tuned on the same binary preference dataset. Diversity comes from different hyperparameters (learning rates, dropout probabilities), different random seeds/data orders, and optionally different SFT checkpoint initializations. Each RM `$\phi_i$` is an LLM with a linear classification head that outputs a scalar reward `$r_{\phi_i}(x, y)$` for a prompt `$x$` and generation `$y$`.

3. **Weight averaging**: The M fine-tuned weight vectors are averaged element-wise to produce a single RM `$\phi_{\text{WARM}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i$`. This single model then serves as the proxy reward for downstream use — either selecting the best generation in best-of-N (BoN) sampling, or providing the reward signal for REINFORCE-based RL fine-tuning of the policy.

The critical enabling property is **linear mode connectivity (LMC)**: because all RMs share the same pre-trained initialization, their weight spaces remain linearly connected, meaning that the interpolation of their weights produces a model that is at least as accurate as the interpolation of their individual accuracies. This makes weight averaging a valid operation rather than a destructive one.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal reward modeling objective (Equation 1) and the Bradley-Terry preference assumption, since this is the loss function that all individual RMs optimize and that fundamentally shapes what WARM inherits.
- **Second**, the WARM procedure itself — the three-stage recipe (shared initialization, diverse fine-tunings, weight averaging) and the critical design choices that make it work, particularly the Baklava diversity initialization and the weight selection strategy.
- **Third**, linear mode connectivity for reward models, since LMC is the theoretical foundation that justifies why weight averaging is possible at all, and the empirical validation that it holds for RMs trained on binary preference data.
- **Fourth**, the first-order analysis (WA as efficient approximation to ENS), explaining how weight averaging achieves variance reduction comparable to prediction ensembling, which is why WARM improves reliability under distribution shifts.
- **Fifth**, the second-order theoretical analysis (WA under label corruption), which is the paper's most novel technical contribution — a formal model showing that WA squares feature-usage probabilities, suppressing memorization of corrupted labels while ENS maintains linear weighting.
- **Sixth**, the connection from RM robustness to RL stability, explaining why the regularization effect of WA translates to better policy learning.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methodological and theoretical analysis paper** whose core idea is that averaging reward model weights — rather than their predictions — produces a single RM that is efficient, reliable under distribution shifts, and robust to label corruption, with the robustness benefit arising from a mathematical property of weight averaging that suppresses low-probability features.

#### The Reward Modeling Objective: Bradley-Terry Preference Learning

Before understanding WARM, we need to understand what each individual RM is trained to do. All RMs in this paper are trained on binary preference data using the standard Bradley-Terry model formulation.

**The data.** The training set `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \{x_d, y_d^+, y_d^-\}_{d=1}^D$` consists of D triples. For each triple, `$x_d$` is a prompt (e.g., a Reddit post to summarize), `$y_d^+$` is the preferred generation (the "winning" summary), and `$y_d^-$` is the dispreferred generation (the "losing" summary). The preference labels come either from human annotators or from an AI labeler (in this paper's experiments, a prompted PaLM-L model, following the RLAIF procedure from Lee et al., 2023).

**The Bradley-Terry assumption.** The RM is trained under the assumption that the probability of `$y^+$` being preferred over `$y^-$` follows a logistic model based on the difference in their true (unobserved) quality scores:

$$P(y^+ \succ y^- \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y^+) - r^*(x, y^-))$$

where `$\sigma(\cdot)$` is the logistic sigmoid function `$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$`, and `$r^*(x, y)$` is the latent "true" reward that captures human preferences. The intuition: the larger the quality gap between two responses, the more confidently we can predict which one is preferred, and the logistic function maps this gap to a probability between 0 and 1.

**What the logistic sigmoid does.** The function `$\sigma(z)$` takes any real number `$z$` and squashes it to (0, 1). When `$z = 0$` (equal-quality responses), `$\sigma(0) = 0.5$` (coin flip). When `$z \gg 0$` (response A is much better), `$\sigma(z) \approx 1$`. When `$z \ll 0$` (response A is much worse), `$\sigma(z) \approx 0$`. This provides a natural probabilistic model for pairwise preferences: the probability of preferring A over B is a function of how much better A is than B.

**The negative log-likelihood loss.** Given the Bradley-Terry model, the maximum likelihood estimate for the RM parameters `$\phi$` minimizes:

$$\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi, \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}}\left[\log \sigma\left(r_\phi(x, y^+) - r_\phi(x, y^-)\right)\right]$$

where `$r_\phi(x, y)$` is the scalar reward predicted by the RM with parameters `$\phi$` for prompt `$x$` and generation `$y$`, and `$\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}}[\cdot]$` denotes the empirical average over the training triples.

**What it computes:** For each training triple, the RM produces two scalar scores — one for the preferred response and one for the dispreferred response. The difference `$r_\phi(x, y^+) - r_\phi(x, y^-)$` is passed through the logistic sigmoid to produce a predicted probability that `$y^+$` is indeed preferred. The loss is the negative log of this predicted probability, averaged over the training set. When the RM correctly assigns a much higher score to `$y^+$` than `$y^-$`, the difference is large positive, `$\sigma(\text{difference}) \approx 1$`, and `$-\log(1) = 0$` — minimal loss. When the RM assigns similar or reversed scores, `$\sigma(\text{difference}) \approx 0.5$` or less, and the loss is large.

**Why this form:** The negative log-likelihood under the Bradley-Terry model is the standard objective in RLHF reward modeling (used by Christiano et al., 2017; Stiennon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022; and essentially all subsequent work). It is a proper scoring rule for binary preferences — it is minimized when the RM's predicted probability of preferring `$y^+$` over `$y^-$` matches the empirical preference frequency. Alternatives like mean squared error on the preference label would not provide calibrated probability estimates, and pairwise ranking losses like RankNet would not directly optimize the quantity that matters for RL (the relative ordering of generation quality).

**RM architecture and initialization.** Each RM `$r_\phi$` is an LLM (PaLM-XXS in the experiments) with parameters `$\phi = (\theta_{\text{featurizer}}, \omega)$`. The featurizer `$\theta_{\text{featurizer}}$` is the body of the LLM — all the transformer layers — and is initialized from the SFT checkpoint `$\theta_{\text{sft}}$`. The final linear layer `$\omega$` (a single weight vector and bias) is added on top to map the LLM's final hidden state to a scalar reward. Crucially, this linear head uses **linear probing**: the featurizer weights are frozen or fine-tuned minimally, and the classification head is trained from scratch, following Kumar et al. (2022). Linear probing prevents the featurizer from distorting its pre-trained features to fit the preference dataset, which (as we will see) is critical for maintaining the linear mode connectivity that makes WARM possible.

#### The WARM Procedure: Three Stages

WARM is defined by a three-stage recipe, each stage involving specific design choices that are justified by the properties they enable downstream.

**Stage 1: Shared pre-trained initialization.** For a given pre-trained LLM, each RM `$\phi_i$` is initialized from `$(\theta_{\text{sft}}, \omega)$`, combining SFT weights and a linearly probed classification layer. The paper states this explicitly: "each RM is initialized from `$(\theta_{\text{sft}}, \omega)$` combining SFT weights and a linear probed classifier." The key constraint is that all RMs start from weights that are close enough in parameter space that their subsequent fine-tuning trajectories remain within the same convex loss basin — a prerequisite for linear mode connectivity, as established by Neyshabur et al. (2020) and Frankle et al. (2020). If the RMs were initialized from scratch or from different pre-trainings, their weights would diverge into disconnected basins, and averaging them would produce a degraded model with no meaningful behavior.

**Stage 2: Diverse fine-tunings.** The paper runs M separate RM fine-tunings, each optimizing the Bradley-Terry loss from Equation (1) on the same preference dataset `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$`, but with three sources of diversity:

- **Different data orders**: Each fine-tuning run sees the training examples in a different random order. This is the minimal source of diversity — even two runs with identical hyperparameters will converge to slightly different solutions due to the stochasticity in mini-batch sampling.
- **Different hyperparameters**: Learning rates are sampled from `$\{1 \times 10^{-5}, 4 \times 10^{-5}, 1 \times 10^{-4}\}$` and dropout probabilities from `$\{0.05, 0.1\}$`. These are described as "mild" ranges, following the recommendation from Ramé et al. (2022, DiWA) that hyperparameter diversity should be bounded to preserve LMC — too-large differences would push the fine-tuned weights into disconnected regions.
- **Baklava initialization diversity**: Rather than always starting from the final SFT checkpoint, the RM featurizers can be initialized from different checkpoints `$\{\theta_{\text{sft}}^i\}_{i=1}^M$` collected along a single SFT training trajectory (e.g., at steps 8k, 10k, and 12k). This relaxation — from "shared initialization" to "shared pre-training" — increases diversity compared to always starting from the last SFT checkpoint, while still adhering to the pre-training constraint required for LMC. The name "Baklava" follows the culinary analogy established by model soups and model ratatouille, referencing the diamond geometric shape in Figure 2 (different SFT checkpoints branch out and then converge back via weight averaging).

All RMs are trained for 10k steps with batch size 128 using the Adafactor optimizer. The paper notes that training for longer "could help, as it did not alter the LMC in previous works" but was not explored.

**Why these specific diversity sources:** The paper needs diversity for the variance-reduction benefit (an ensemble of identical models reduces no variance), but cannot exceed the diversity threshold that breaks LMC. Hyperparameter diversity is proven effective in the DiWA framework (Ramé et al., 2022). Baklava is introduced as "an efficient alternative to model ratatouille but without the need of multiple auxiliary tasks" — model ratatouille (Ramé et al., 2023) required training on different auxiliary tasks to get diversity, which is expensive; Baklava gets diversity for free by recycling intermediate SFT checkpoints that were already produced during standard training. The paper validates in Figure 3(d) that Baklava-initialized RMs remain linearly connected and their WA outperforms ENS.

**Stage 3: Weight averaging.** The M fine-tuned weight vectors are averaged element-wise:

$$\phi_{\text{WARM}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i$$

where `$\phi_i$` is the full parameter vector (featurizer + linear head) of the i-th RM, and the sum is over the weights of the M selected RMs.

**What it computes:** For each parameter (each weight matrix entry, each bias), the M values from the M RMs are summed and divided by M to produce a single value. This is done independently for every parameter. The result is a single model with the same architecture as any individual RM, but whose weights sit at the centroid of the M fine-tuned weight vectors in parameter space.

**Weight selection:** Not all M RMs are necessarily included. The paper selects the M best RMs according to their individual accuracy on the OOD validation set `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{ood}}$`, sorted in decreasing order, and averages only those. Figure 10 validates this heuristic: averaging RMs from best to worst monotonically improves OOD accuracy up to M=10, while random selection or averaging from worst to best degrades performance. This is important because including a poorly-performing RM would drag the centroid toward a bad region of weight space, which the averaging operation cannot recover from — unlike prediction ensembling, where a bad model's vote can simply be diluted by better models.

**Why not moving average (checkpoint averaging along a single run):** The paper explicitly considered and rejected this cheaper alternative. Remark 2 states: "following stochastic weight average or moving average, we also tried to average checkpoints collected along a single RM fine-tuning. Though interesting because less costly for training, the lower results in Figure 3(a) suggest that the accuracy-diversity trade-off was not favorable: incorporating early checkpoints would compromise individual accuracies, and considering only later checkpoints would not bring the necessary diversity." This is a crucial practical finding: diversity across independent runs matters more than the temporal smoothing of a single trajectory, at least for reward modeling on this benchmark.

#### Linear Mode Connectivity for Reward Models

The theoretical foundation that makes WARM legitimate — rather than a destructive averaging of incompatible models — is **linear mode connectivity (LMC)**. The paper empirically validates that LMC holds for RMs fine-tuned on binary preference data, which is a non-trivial claim because prior LMC work focused on multi-class classification in computer vision.

**Observation 1 (LMC).** Given two fine-tuned weights `$\phi_1$` and `$\phi_2$` with a shared pre-training and a test dataset `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$`, then for all `$\lambda \in [0, 1]$`:

$$\text{Acc}\left(r_{(1-\lambda)\cdot\phi_1 + \lambda\cdot\phi_2}, \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}\right) \geq (1-\lambda) \times \text{Acc}\left(r_{\phi_1}, \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}\right) + \lambda \times \text{Acc}\left(r_{\phi_2}, \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}\right)$$

where `$\text{Acc}(r_\phi, \mathcal{D}) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-) \in \mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{r_\phi(x, y^+) \geq r_\phi(x, y^-)}\right]$` is the pairwise accuracy — the fraction of test triples where the RM correctly assigns higher reward to the preferred response, `$\phi_1$` and `$\phi_2$` are two parameter vectors, and `$\lambda$` interpolates between them (when `$\lambda = 0$`, the interpolated model equals `$\phi_1$`; when `$\lambda = 1$`, it equals `$\phi_2$`).

**What it says:** When you take a convex combination of the weights of two RMs (producing a new RM whose weights are the weighted average of the two), the accuracy of this interpolated model is at least as good as the same convex combination of the individual accuracies. In plainer terms: interpolating in weight space does not produce a "dead zone" of degraded performance between the two endpoints — the accuracy curve is convex, meaning the interpolated model is never worse than linear interpolation would predict, and can be better (the accuracy "barrier" that plagues models trained from scratch is absent).

**Why this matters for WARM:** LMC is what justifies averaging M > 2 weights. If the property holds for any pair, then by transitivity it holds for the centroid of M weights — you can think of iteratively averaging pairs, and each step preserves or improves accuracy relative to the individual models. Without LMC, weight averaging would be essentially random: the averaged parameters would land in an arbitrary region of weight space with no guarantee of functionality.

**Empirical validation (Figure 3).** The paper validates LMC across increasing levels of diversity:

- **Figure 3(a): two checkpoints from the same RM fine-tuning** (at steps 8k and 10k). The WA accuracy curve lies above the diagonal (the "Diag" line), confirming Observation 1, though the gain is modest because the two checkpoints are very similar.

- **Figure 3(b): two independent RM fine-tunings with identical hyperparameters** but different data orders. The WA curve again satisfies LMC, with a larger gain over the diagonal than in (a) — more diversity means each model makes different errors, so averaging cancels more of those errors.

- **Figure 3(c): two RMs with different learning rates** (`$10^{-4}$` vs. `$4 \times 10^{-5}$`). LMC continues to hold with an even larger gain, showing that hyperparameter diversity within the "mild" range does not break connectivity.

- **Figure 3(d): two RMs with Baklava initialization** (different SFT checkpoints at steps 8k and 12k). LMC holds and the gains are substantial, validating Baklava as a diversity source.

**Why pre-training and linear probing are necessary (Remark 1).** The paper is explicit: "The efficacy of WA can be surprising given the non-linearities and permutation symmetries in deep neural network architectures. WA is actually possible only because of the shared pre-training which constrains the divergence during fine-tunings, such as the weights remain in convex regions of the loss valley. In contrast, the LMC does not hold when training weights from scratch, even if the random initialization is shared." The linear probing of the classification head (rather than random initialization) further helps: "such linear probing prevents feature distortion," following Kumar et al. (2022), who showed that randomly initializing the classification head and fine-tuning everything can cause the featurizer to warp its representations to fit the training distribution, breaking OOD generalization and potentially breaking LMC.

#### First-Order Analysis: WA as Efficient Approximation to ENS

The paper's first lens for understanding why WARM works is that weight averaging approximates prediction ensembling, which is already known to reduce variance and improve reliability.

**Observation 2 (WA and ENS: first-order analysis).** Weight averaging and prediction ensembling perform similarly: for all `$\lambda \in [0, 1]$` and a test dataset `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$`:

$$\text{Acc}\left(r_{(1-\lambda)\cdot\phi_1 + \lambda\cdot\phi_2}, \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}\right) \approx \text{Acc}\left((1-\lambda) \times r_{\phi_1} + \lambda \times r_{\phi_2}, \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}\right)$$

where the left-hand side is the accuracy of the weight-averaged model (compute the interpolated weights first, then run the model on each input), and the right-hand side is the accuracy of prediction ensembling (run both models independently on each input, then average their output scores, then apply the same decision rule).

**What it says:** The accuracy of a weight-interpolated RM on a fixed test set is approximately equal to the accuracy you would get by averaging the predictions of the two individual RMs. This is an empirical claim, not a mathematical identity — neural networks are non-linear, so `$r_{(1-\lambda)\phi_1 + \lambda\phi_2}(x) \neq (1-\lambda)r_{\phi_1}(x) + \lambda r_{\phi_2}(x)$` in general. But when the weights `$\phi_1$` and `$\phi_2$` are close, a first-order Taylor expansion justifies the approximation: the non-linearities are locally approximately linear in the region between the two weight vectors.

**Why this justifies WARM's reliability:** Since prediction ensembling is a variance-reduction method (averaging M independent predictions reduces the variance of the estimator by a factor of M, assuming equal-variance, uncorrelated errors), and WA approximates ENS, WA also reduces variance. The OOD generalization literature (Ramé et al., 2022, DiWA) has established that variance is the dominant error component under distribution shifts — models make different mistakes on OOD data, and averaging cancels those mistakes. Therefore, WARM inherits ENS's reliability under distribution shifts (Property 2) while eliminating its efficiency overhead (Property 1).

**Empirical validation in Figure 3:** Across all four diversity settings (3a–3d), the WA accuracy curve (blue) and the ENS accuracy curve (red) closely track each other. The paper acknowledges that "WA is slightly superior to ENS under distribution shifts," visible as the WA curve lying marginally above the ENS curve at intermediate `$\lambda$` values in all four subfigures — but this superiority is not explained by the first-order analysis. The paper notes this discrepancy as motivation for the second-order analysis: "A surprising fact remains unexplained... this was not explained in previous works, thus urging for new insights about the difference between WA and ENS."

---

#### Second-Order Analysis: WA Suppresses Memorization of Label Noise

The paper's most novel contribution is a formal model explaining why WA outperforms ENS under label corruption, using a simplified binary feature-selection setup. This is Observation 3 and the theoretical analysis in Section 4.3.

**Observation 3 (WA and ENS: second-order analysis).** The accuracy gains of WA over ENS grow as data moves away from the training distribution:

- **WA ≪ ENS on train corrupt:** WA is far worse than ENS on training samples with swapped (corrupted) labels — meaning WA memorizes the corrupted labels less than ENS does.
- **WA ≤ ENS on train clean:** WA is slightly worse than ENS on training samples with correct labels — ENS fits the clean training data better.
- **WA ⪆ ENS on ID val:** WA is better or similar to ENS on in-distribution validation samples.
- **WA ≥ ENS on OOD test:** WA is far better than ENS on test samples from new distributions — the gap is largest here.

**The corruption experiment setup.** To isolate this effect, the paper creates a variant of the preference dataset where 25% of the binary labels are randomly swapped (i.e., the preferred and dispreferred summaries are exchanged). The RMs are trained on this corrupted dataset, and then evaluated separately on four data subsets: (a) the training examples whose labels were corrupted, (b) the training examples whose labels remain clean, (c) the in-distribution validation set (no distribution shift, no added corruption), and (d) the OOD test set `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{ood}}$` (distribution shift, no added corruption). Figures 4(a)–4(d) show the results for one pair of RMs, and Appendix C.1 (Figures 11–14) confirms the pattern across multiple pairs.

**The theoretical model (Section 4.3).** The authors construct a simplified binary classification setup to explain why WA reduces memorization. The setup follows Lin et al. (2024) and involves three key assumptions:

1. **Feature orthogonality**: There are F features `$\{z_j\}_{j=1}^F$` where each `$z_j \in \mathbb{R}^d$`, and `$(z_j)^\top z_{j'} = 0$` when `$j \neq j'$` — all features are orthogonal to each other.

2. **Input as bag of features**: An input `$x = [x_j]_{j=1}^F \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times d}$` is the concatenation of F components, where each `$x_j$` is generated as `$x_j \sim \mathcal{N}(y \cdot z_j, \sigma \cdot I_d)$` with `$\sigma \ll 1$`. Here `$y \in \{-1, 1\}$` is the binary label, `$z_j$` is the feature direction, and `$I_d$` is the `$d \times d$` identity matrix. In plain language: when the label is `$+1$`, the input component in the direction of feature j is close to `$+z_j$`; when the label is `$-1$`, it's close to `$-z_j$`; the small `$\sigma$` ensures the input is strongly aligned with the label direction.

3. **Binary featurizer**: The model's featurizer `$f = [f_j]_{j=1}^F \in \{0, 1\}^F$` is a binary selector — each `$f_j$` is either 0 (ignore feature j) or 1 (extract feature j). The probability that the featurizer learns to use feature j is `$p_j$`, meaning `$f_j = 1$` with probability `$p_j$` and `$f_j = 0$` with probability `$1 - p_j$`. Different training runs (different random seeds, data orders, or hyperparameters) may learn different subsets of features, captured by this probabilistic model.

**The optimal linear classifier.** Under these assumptions, Lemma 5 from Lin et al. (2024) proves that for infinite training data and small enough `$\sigma$`, the optimal linear classifier `$\omega$` (the readout weights) on the features selected by `$f$` is:

$$\omega = \sum_{j=1}^F f_j \cdot z_j$$

where `$f_j \in \{0, 1\}$` is the binary selection indicator for feature j, and `$z_j$` is the feature direction vector. In words: the optimal classifier simply sums up the feature direction vectors for whichever features the featurizer has selected. Each selected feature contributes its full vector `$z_j$`; unselected features contribute nothing. This makes intuitive sense — if feature j is present in the input (as `$x_j \approx y \cdot z_j$`), then multiplying by `$z_j$` and summing across selected features produces a value whose sign matches `$y$`.

**Prediction ensembling in the limit `$M \to \infty$`.** Consider M independently trained RMs `$\{r_i = \omega_i^\top f_i\}_{i=1}^M$`, where each RM has its own featurizer `$f_i$` (probabilistically selecting features) and corresponding optimal classifier `$\omega_i = \sum_{j=1}^F f_{i,j} \cdot z_j$`. When averaging their predictions:

$$r_M^{\text{ENS}}(x) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \omega_i^\top f_i(x)$$

as `$M \to \infty$`, this converges to the expected prediction:

$$r_M^{\text{ENS}}(x) \xrightarrow{M \to \infty} \mathbb{E}[r(x)] = y \cdot \sum_{j=1}^F p_j \cdot |z_j|^2$$

where `$y \in \{-1, 1\}$` is the true label, `$p_j$` is the probability that any individual RM learns to use feature j, and `$|z_j|^2$` is the squared Euclidean norm of feature vector j.

**Derivation sketch:** The expected classifier is `$\mathbb{E}[\omega] = \sum_j p_j \cdot z_j$` (each feature j is included with probability `$p_j$`). For an input with label y, the expected featurizer output is `$\mathbb{E}[f(x)] \approx \sum_j p_j \cdot y \cdot z_j = y \cdot \sum_j p_j \cdot z_j$`. Their inner product gives `$y \cdot \sum_j p_j \cdot |z_j|^2$`, using orthogonality of the features to eliminate cross-terms.

**What this means:** In prediction ensembling, each feature j contributes to the prediction in proportion to `$p_j$` — the probability that any individual model uses that feature. A feature that is learned by 30% of the models contributes 30% as much as a feature learned by 100% of the models. This is linear weighting: the ensemble reflects the average feature usage across the population of models.

**Weight averaging in the limit `$M \to \infty$`.** When instead averaging the weights first:

$$r_M^{\text{WA}}(x) = \left(\frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \omega_i\right)^\top \left(\frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M f_i(x)\right)$$

as `$M \to \infty$`, this converges to:

$$r_M^{\text{WA}}(x) \xrightarrow{M \to \infty} y \cdot \sum_{j=1}^F p_j^2 \cdot |z_j|^2$$

where the only difference from the ENS expression is the exponent on `$p_j$`: **`$p_j^2$` instead of `$p_j$`**.

**Derivation sketch:** The averaged weights converge to `$\mathbb{E}[\omega] = \sum_j p_j \cdot z_j$`. The averaged featurizer converges to `$\mathbb{E}[f] = [p_j]_{j=1}^F$` (a vector of probabilities). For an input x with label y, `$\mathbb{E}[f]^\top x = \sum_j p_j \cdot (x_j)^\top z_j$`, and with `$x_j \approx y \cdot z_j$`, this is approximately `$y \cdot \sum_j p_j \cdot |z_j|^2$`. The product of averaged weights and averaged featurizer gives `$y \cdot \sum_j p_j^2 \cdot |z_j|^2$`.

**The critical difference: squaring `$p_j$`.** For ENS, a feature j with `$p_j = 0.3$` (learned by 30% of models) contributes `$0.3 \cdot |z_j|^2$` to the prediction. For WA, the same feature contributes `$0.09 \cdot |z_j|^2$` — less than one-third as much. A feature with `$p_j = 0.9$` contributes `$0.9 \cdot |z_j|^2$` for ENS and `$0.81 \cdot |z_j|^2$` for WA — a much smaller relative reduction. A feature with `$p_j = 1.0$` contributes equally in both cases.

**Why this matters for label corruption:** Features that are genuinely predictive of the true label (causal features) will be learned by essentially all training runs, giving them `$p_j \approx 1$`. WA and ENS treat these features nearly identically. But features that correlate with the corrupted labels — spurious features that happen to align with the swapped labels in the training data — are only learned by some fraction of runs (those that happen to overfit to those particular corrupted examples). These features have `$p_j \ll 1$`. WA heavily downweights these features (proportional to `$p_j^2$`), while ENS weights them linearly (proportional to `$p_j$`). This means WA **suppresses memorization of corrupted labels** while preserving the genuinely predictive features — exactly what Observation 3 shows empirically.

**Remark 3 (Invariance):** The paper explicitly connects this to the invariance literature: "We argue that weight averaging only keeps the invariant predictive mechanisms across runs. This is in analogy with the invariance literature, popular for domain generalization under spurious correlations, where the key idea is that the predictive mechanisms which are invariant across domains are the causal ones that are stable under distribution shifts." In other words, WA acts as an automatic invariance regularizer — it suppresses features that are not consistently learned (run-specific, noise-dependent) and amplifies features that are consistently learned (run-invariant, genuinely predictive). This "theoretically connects two key paradigms for OOD generalization, ensembling and invariance, and shows that weight averaging actually benefits from both."

**Remark 4 (Extension to deeper networks):** "In full generality, using a deeper structure with L layers would lead to `$p_j^L$`." The intuition is that WA applies an **AND-mask** across layers: for a feature to contribute to the final prediction in a weight-averaged model, it must be used by the featurizer in every layer, and the probability of this is the product of the per-layer probabilities. This provides an even stronger suppression of run-specific features in deeper networks.

---

#### From RM Robustness to RL Stability (Remark 5)

The paper argues that the robustness property of WA — suppressing low-probability, memorized features — directly improves the stability of RL fine-tuning when WARM is used as the proxy reward.

**The Lipschitzness connection.** Weight averaging makes the RM more robust to small input perturbations, which means the reward function is **smoother** in the input space. Formally, the Lipschitz constant of the reward function — which bounds how much the predicted reward can change for a given change in the input — is reduced. This is because features that cause sharp, discontinuous changes in the reward (often the memorized, spurious ones) are suppressed.

**Why smoothness matters for RL.** The paper cites Blondé et al. (2022): "sharp changes in reward value are hard to represent and internalize," and "the local Lipschitzness of the reward is a sine qua non condition for good performance, required to even learn anything." In policy gradient methods like REINFORCE (used in the paper's experiments), the policy is updated based on the reward signal. If small changes in the generated text cause large, unpredictable swings in the reward, the policy gradient becomes high-variance and the optimization becomes unstable — the policy cannot learn a coherent direction of improvement. By smoothing the reward landscape, WARM provides a more learnable signal, reducing the cascade of errors where minor output variations cause large reward differences that derail training.

**Empirical backing.** The paper's RL experiments (Section 5.2, Figure 8) confirm this indirectly: policies trained with WARM maintain higher control rewards for longer during training and resist the reward hacking collapse that plagues single RMs and ENS. The effect is particularly pronounced in the corrupted-label setting (Figure 8(b)), where the gap between WARM and ENS is largest — consistent with the theoretical prediction that WA's corruption-robustness translates to more stable RL.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Weight averaging over prediction ensembling**: Eliminates memory/inference overhead while providing the same variance-reduction benefits for reliability, plus additional robustness to label noise through the `$p_j^2$` suppression mechanism.
- **Shared pre-training requirement**: Necessary for LMC, which is necessary for WA to not degrade performance. This is why all RMs must be fine-tuned from the same pre-trained base model.
- **Linear probing of classification head**: Prevents feature distortion during fine-tuning (Kumar et al., 2022), preserving the pre-trained feature space and facilitating LMC by keeping featurizers close to their shared initialization.
- **Mild hyperparameter ranges**: Learning rates in `$\{10^{-5}, 4 \times 10^{-5}, 10^{-4}\}$` and dropout in `$\{0.05, 0.1\}$` provide diversity without breaking LMC, following the DiWA recommendation that "hyperparameters in a mild range to preserve the LMC."
- **Baklava initialization**: Recycles intermediate SFT checkpoints to increase diversity at zero additional cost, without requiring auxiliary tasks (unlike model ratatouille) or breaking the shared pre-training constraint.
- **Selection by OOD accuracy**: Including only the best RMs in the average prevents poorly-performing models from dragging the centroid into low-quality regions, which matters more for WA than ENS because an averaged model's weights are directly influenced by every included model.
- **Rejection of moving average along a single run**: The diversity from checkpoint averaging is insufficient — independent runs with different hyperparameters and data orders provide the necessary diversity for the variance-reduction benefit to be meaningful.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Weight Averaging as an Implicit Invariance Regularizer — Reconciling Ensembling with Invariant Risk Minimization

The paper's most conceptually distinctive contribution is not the application of weight averaging to reward models (which is a straightforward transfer from the OOD generalization literature), but rather the **theoretical demonstration that weight averaging (WA) and prediction ensembling (ENS) have fundamentally different asymptotic behavior under label noise**, and that this difference arises because WA acts as an implicit regularizer toward predictive mechanisms that are invariant across training runs.

Before this work, the dominant understanding of WA — articulated most clearly in the model soups (Wortsman et al., 2022) and DiWA (Ramé et al., 2022) literature — was that WA approximates prediction ensembling via a first-order Taylor expansion when weights are close. The claim was essentially: WA ≈ ENS, but with no inference overhead. This framing treated WA as a **computational convenience**, not a qualitatively different estimator. Any performance differences between WA and ENS were attributed to second-order effects that, while practically beneficial, were not theoretically understood.

WARM fundamentally revises this picture. The corruption experiment in Section 4.2 (Figures 4 and 5) reveals a **monotonic relationship** between the accuracy gap (WA − ENS) and the distance of evaluation data from the training distribution: WA is substantially worse than ENS on corrupted training samples (reduced memorization), slightly worse on clean training samples (reduced overfitting), comparable on ID validation, and substantially better on OOD test data. This pattern cannot be explained by the first-order approximation — if WA ≈ ENS, the gap should be near zero across all data subsets. Something structurally different is happening.

The paper's key insight is that this pattern emerges from a **feature-probability squaring effect** (formalized in Section 4.3): in the limit of many models, ENS weights each feature proportionally to `$p_j$` (the probability any individual model learns it), while WA weights each feature proportionally to `$p_j^2$`. Features that are consistently learned across all runs (`$p_j \approx 1$`) are preserved nearly identically by both methods. Features that are run-specific — particularly those that correlate with corrupted labels and are only learned by a subset of models — are **quadratically suppressed by WA** (`$p_j^2 \ll p_j$` when `$p_j \ll 1$`) but only linearly present in ENS.

This is a meaningful intellectual move because it **synthesizes two previously separate paradigms** for robustness. The ensembling literature (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017; Eisenstein et al., 2023) treats robustness as variance reduction: average many noisy estimators and the noise cancels. The invariance literature (Arjovsky et al., 2019; Invariant Risk Minimization and its descendants) treats robustness as causal feature identification: find the predictive mechanisms that are stable across environments, because those are the ones that generalize. The paper shows that WA actually **does both simultaneously** — it reduces variance (like ENS, via the first-order approximation) AND it suppresses run-specific mechanisms (like an invariance regularizer, via the `$p_j^2$` effect). This is stated explicitly in Remark 3: WA "theoretically connects two key paradigms for OOD generalization, ensembling and invariance, and shows that weight averaging actually benefits from both."

The significance of this insight extends beyond RLHF. It suggests that WA should be preferred over ENS whenever the training data contains systematic contamination (label noise, spurious correlations, annotation artifacts) that individual models might partially memorize. It also provides a theoretical justification for empirical practices that were previously justified only heuristically — for instance, the common practice in the model soups literature of averaging only the best-performing models on OOD data, which can now be understood as excluding models that have over-indexed on run-specific (potentially spurious) features. This is a fundamental rather than incremental contribution: it changes the conceptual status of WA from "a cheaper approximation to ENS" to "a qualitatively different estimator with distinct and superior properties under data corruption."

### Innovation 2: Difficulty-Aware Regularization via Diversity Sources — The Baklava Principle

While the core WA mechanism is inherited from prior work, the paper introduces a principled framework for **controlling the diversity of the ensemble members while preserving the linear mode connectivity constraint**, with Baklava (Figure 2) as a concrete, zero-cost implementation of this principle.

The tension is inherent in the WA setup: on one hand, you need the RMs to be diverse, because averaging identical models achieves nothing (variance reduction requires uncorrelated errors, and the `$p_j^2$` benefit requires features to have different `$p_j$` values across runs). On the other hand, you need the RMs to remain linearly connected, which requires them to stay within the same convex loss basin — too much divergence during fine-tuning and the LMC property breaks, making weight averaging destructive rather than beneficial.

Prior work addressed this tension in two ways. Model soups (Wortsman et al., 2022) maximized connectivity by using only a shared initialization and identical hyperparameters, sacrificing diversity entirely (the only diversity source was different random seeds and data orders). Model ratatouille (Ramé et al., 2023) maximized diversity by training on entirely different auxiliary tasks, but at the cost of requiring multiple labeled datasets and training runs beyond the target task. Neither framework provided a systematic way to tune the diversity-connectivity tradeoff for a single-task setting.

WARM's insight — embodied in the Baklava method — is that **intermediate checkpoints along the SFT trajectory provide a natural source of diversity that respects the connectivity constraint at zero additional cost**. Because the SFT trajectory is a continuous path through weight space, checkpoints at different points along this trajectory are guaranteed to be connected (they're literally on the same optimization path) but represent meaningfully different feature extractors (early checkpoints may not have fully adapted to the instruction-following format, while later checkpoints may have overfit to surface patterns in the SFT data). By initializing different RMs from different SFT checkpoints, Baklava increases the diversity of the resulting RM population without requiring any additional training runs beyond what would already be produced during standard SFT.

This is a **design principle** rather than a single method: the paper argues that any source of variation that respects the pre-training constraint while inducing meaningful differences in what features the RM learns will improve WARM's performance. The paper validates this principle through the Baklava experiments (Figure 3(d), and the Baklava-specific BoN results in Appendix Figure 16), but also through the broader finding that hyperparameter diversity and data-order diversity produce progressively larger WA gains (Figures 3(b) vs. 3(c) vs. 3(d)).

The significance is both practical and conceptual. Practically, Baklava makes the entire WARM pipeline essentially free in terms of additional training compute — the SFT checkpoints are already produced during standard training, and the RM fine-tunings are parallelized (the paper notes this enables "embarrassingly simple parallelization"). Conceptually, it reframes the diversity-connectivity tradeoff from a constraint to be managed (you need diversity but can't have too much) to a resource to be optimized (you can increase diversity through structural choices that respect the pre-training invariant). This opens the door to future work on designing diversity sources that maximize the `$p_j$` variance across runs while maintaining connectivity.

This contribution is incremental relative to the broader WA literature (model ratatouille used multi-task diversity; Baklava simplifies this to single-trajectory diversity), but **fundamental for the practical deployment of WA in RLHF pipelines**, where the cost of additional training runs and datasets is a real bottleneck.

### Innovation 3: The Decomposition of Reward Hacking into Distribution Shift and Label Noise Components — And the Asymmetric Efficacy of WARM Against Each

A third insight — more diagnostic than methodological — is the paper's empirical demonstration that the two identified challenges in reward modeling (distribution shifts and preference inconsistencies) **require different solutions, and that WARM's advantage over ENS is concentrated almost entirely in the label noise regime**.

The paper's experimental design cleanly separates these two challenges through two independent axes of variation: (1) the clean vs. corrupt preference datasets (varying label noise while holding distribution shift constant), and (2) the ID vs. OOD evaluation subsets (varying distribution shift while holding label noise constant). The results reveal an asymmetric pattern.

Under clean labels (distribution shift only), WARM and ENS perform similarly — the first-order analysis (Observation 2) holds, and both methods reduce variance comparably. This is visible in Figure 3 (all subfigures show WA and ENS curves closely tracking) and in the clean BoN experiments (Figures 6(a) and 6(c), where WARM `$M=2$` and ENS `$M=2$` show small gaps). The efficiency benefit of WARM (no inference overhead) makes it preferable, but both methods fundamentally work through the same variance-reduction mechanism.

Under corrupted labels (label noise + distribution shift), the picture changes dramatically. WARM substantially outperforms ENS, and the gap widens as evaluation moves further OOD (Figure 4 progression from 4(a) to 4(d), and the RL experiments in Figure 8(b) vs. 8(a)). This is the regime where the `$p_j^2$` suppression mechanism matters: label noise creates run-specific features that different RMs partially memorize, and WA's quadratic downweighting of these features provides a benefit that ENS's linear weighting cannot replicate.

This diagnostic insight matters because it **clarifies when practitioners should prefer WARM over ENS**. If the primary concern is distribution shift (the policy exploring OOD during RL) and the preference labels are high-quality (e.g., expert human annotations with high inter-rater agreement), ENS and WARM are roughly interchangeable, and the choice reduces to the efficiency tradeoff. If the preference labels are noisy (crowd-sourced annotations, AI-generated feedback with systematic biases, or multi-objective preferences with conflicting signals), WARM has a fundamental advantage that cannot be matched by simply adding more models to an ENS ensemble — the `$p_j^2$` effect is structural, not scalable by increasing `$M$`.

This finding also **reinterprets prior work on RM ensembling**. Eisenstein et al. (2023) found that "reward model ensembles mitigate but do not eliminate reward hacking," and the current paper's results suggest that the residual reward hacking in ENS may be precisely the component driven by label noise rather than distribution shift. ENS handles the variance-from-distribution-shift problem but cannot handle the memorization-from-label-noise problem; WARM handles both. This reframes the reward hacking mitigation problem from a single-axis challenge (make the RM more robust) to a two-axis challenge (make the RM robust to OOD inputs AND to label corruption), with different technical solutions needed for each axis.

This is an incremental empirical contribution within the specific RLHF context, but potentially **fundamental for the broader RLHF research agenda**, because it suggests that improving preference data quality (reducing label noise) and improving RM OOD generalization (reducing distribution shift vulnerability) are partially substitutable — if you have very clean labels, distribution shift is the bottleneck; if you have very good OOD generalization, label noise is the bottleneck. Current RLHF deployments rely heavily on AI-generated labels (RLAIF), which are known to introduce systematic noise patterns (e.g., length bias, position bias), making the label-noise axis particularly relevant in practice and WARM particularly well-suited to the modern RLHF stack.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses the Reddit TL;DR summarization benchmark (Völske et al., 2017), containing posts from Reddit filtered for quality. The training set (from Stiennon et al., 2020) consists of 123k posts with candidate summaries generated by GPT-3 variants; ~5% is held out as the in-distribution (ID) validation set. The OOD test dataset `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{ood}}$` contains 92k pairwise comparisons where summaries are generated by multiple PaLM-XS policies (pre-trained, SFT-ed, and RLHF-ed) with high temperature to ensure diversity.

- **Base model(s).** All reward models are PaLM-XXS (Anil et al., 2023), pre-trained and then SFT-ed on the preferred summaries from the TL;DR training set for 12k steps (batch size 128, Adafactor optimizer, learning rate `$10^{-5}$`). The policies for BoN and RL experiments are PaLM-XS, initialized from the same SFT model. The AI labeler (used both to generate preference labels for RM training and as the oracle preference metric during evaluation) is an instruct-fine-tuned PaLM-L.

- **Metrics.** Three distinct metrics are tracked. **(1) Pairwise accuracy:** the fraction of test triples where the RM correctly assigns higher reward to the preferred response, `$\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-) \in \mathcal{D}}[\mathbf{1}_{r_\phi(x, y^+) \geq r_\phi(x, y^-)}]$`. Reported on `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{ood}}$` for RM evaluation. **(2) Control reward:** a pointwise reward assigned by a separate PaLM-XS RM (trained on clean labels, reaching 80.1% accuracy on `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{ood}}$`) that serves as a noisy proxy for the true objective. This RM benefits from a larger architecture and disjoint pretraining, making it resistant to the hacking that affects the PaLM-XXS RMs under study. It detects reward hacking as a collapse in its score, as shown in Figures 1(b) and 8. **(3) Oracle preference win rate:** pairwise comparisons from the RLAIF PaLM-L labeler (Lee et al., 2023), prompted with chain-of-thought and "Detailed + CoT 0-shot" strategy, with temperature 0.0 and both positional orderings to avoid bias. This labeler was shown in prior work to correlate well with human preferences and provides an automatic, scalable evaluation of which policy's summaries are truly preferred.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against: **(1) Individual RMs** (`$\phi_1$, $\phi_2$`): the two best individual RMs according to OOD accuracy, representing the standard single-RM approach in RLHF. **(2) Prediction ensembling (ENS)** (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017; Christiano et al., 2017): averaging the predicted rewards from `$M=2$` RMs at inference time. The paper notes that `$M=2$` is used "for computational reasons" — larger ensembles would be prohibitively expensive for RL experiments given that each RM is itself an LLM. **(3) SFT policy** (for BoN and RL oracle preference comparisons): the supervised-fine-tuned model before any RL, which is equivalent to random selection among candidate summaries or BoN with `$N=1$`.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For best-of-N (BoN) experiments, the budget is `$N$` — the number of candidate summaries generated per prompt and the number of reward evaluations the RM must perform. For PaLM SFT generations, `$N=8$` with 15,000 prompts; for T5 SFT generations, `$N=1000$` with 1,000 prompts. The x-axis in BoN plots is the KL divergence between the BoN policy and the SFT policy, approximated as `$\log(N) - \frac{N-1}{N}$` (Hilton, 2023; Beirami et al., 2024), which increases with `$N$` but sublinearly. For RL experiments, the x-axis is either the number of training steps or the KL divergence between the current policy and SFT initialization (used to estimate model drift). Memory and inference overhead are measured qualitatively: ENS with `$M$` models requires `$M \times$` the storage and per-generation compute of a single RM, while WARM requires `$1 \times$`.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation for hyperparameter selection or strategy comparison. Weight selection for WARM uses a simple heuristic validated in Figure 10: the 10 trained RMs are sorted by decreasing individual accuracy on `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{ood}}$`, and WARM with `$M$` weights averages the `$M$` best. Different permutations are tested to confirm that "best-to-worst" ordering is a "reliable heuristic." The KL regularization coefficient `$\alpha$` is tuned separately for the clean (`$\alpha=0.003$`) and corrupt (`$\alpha=0.01$`) setups, with an ablation in Figure 8(c). No confidence intervals or statistical significance tests are reported for the main BoN or RL results.

---

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Best-of-N Sampling Experiments (Section 5.1)

The BoN experiments evaluate how well different RMs can select the best summary from a pool of `$N$` candidates generated by a fixed SFT policy. This isolates the RM's quality from the complications of RL training (policy drift, exploration, KL regularization) and tests Properties 1–3 directly.

**Control reward results (Figure 6, Figure 15 for absolute values).** Across all four configurations (PaLM SFT clean, PaLM SFT corrupt, T5 SFT clean, T5 SFT corrupt), WARM `$M=6$` achieves the highest control reward at every value of `$N$` and corresponding KL. The gains over baselines are:

- **Against individual RMs:** WARM `$M=6$` outperforms both `$\phi_1$` and `$\phi_2$` (the two best individual RMs by OOD accuracy) by a substantial margin in all settings. For example, in the PaLM clean setting (Figure 6(a)), the control reward gain for WARM `$M=6$` reaches approximately 0.12 at the highest `$N$` (KL ≈ 1.2), while `$\phi_2$` achieves at most ~0.08 and `$\phi_1$` achieves at most ~0.05. The absolute control rewards (Figure 15(a)) range from roughly 4.0 to 5.75, with WARM `$M=6$` at the top across the full KL range.

- **Against ENS `$M=2$`:** WARM `$M=6$` consistently outperforms ENS `$M=2$`, and — critically — **WARM `$M=2$` also outperforms ENS `$M=2$`** in most settings. In the PaLM clean setting, the gap between WARM `$M=2$` and ENS `$M=2$` is visible but modest; in the T5 corrupt setting (Figure 6(d)), the gap is larger and WARM `$M=2$` tracks closer to WARM `$M=6$` than to ENS `$M=2$`. This confirms the second-order analysis: when labels are noisy, WA structurally differs from ENS even at the same `$M$`.

- **Effect of increasing `$M$`:** WARM `$M=6$` outperforms WARM `$M=2$` in all settings, with the gap being most pronounced in the T5 corrupt setting (Figure 6(d), control reward gain of ~0.5 for `$M=6$` vs. ~0.3 for `$M=2$` at high `$N$`). The monotonic improvement with `$M$` is consistent with both the variance-reduction view (more models → lower variance) and the `$p_j^2$` view (more models → better estimation of which features are invariant across runs).

- **Cross-architecture comparison:** The improvement pattern generalizes across policy architectures — WARM `$M=6$` is the top-performing RM for summaries generated by both PaLM SFT and T5 SFT, confirming that the benefit is not architecture-specific.

- **A notable diagnostic finding:** The paper observes that the individual RM `$\phi_2$` (which has lower OOD accuracy than `$\phi_1$`) actually performs better in BoN on T5 generations (Figure 6(c)) than `$\phi_1$`. This "highlights that selecting the appropriate individual RM is not trivial" — OOD accuracy is an imperfect proxy for downstream BoN performance, which is a practical challenge that WARM sidesteps by combining multiple RMs.

**Oracle preference results (Figure 7, T5 generations).** The oracle preference metric (PaLM-L AI labeler) provides a complementary evaluation of summary quality from the perspective of an external, higher-quality judge.

- **Win rate vs. SFT (Figures 7(a) and 7(b)):** All RM-based selection strategies substantially outperform random selection (SFT baseline, 50% win rate by construction since the comparison is symmetric). WARM `$M=6$` achieves the highest win rates: up to **92.5%** in the clean setting and approximately 85% in the corrupt setting at the highest `$N$` (KL ≈ 6, corresponding to `$N=1000$`). Individual RMs `$\phi_1$` and `$\phi_2$` achieve lower win rates (roughly 87% and 88% in the clean setting), and ENS `$M=2$` is intermediate. The gap between WARM `$M=6$` and other methods is larger in the corrupt setting (Figure 7(b)), consistent with the label-noise-robustness advantage.

- **Win rate vs. WARM `$M=6$` (Figures 7(c) and 7(d)):** When WARM `$M=6$` is used as the reference policy, **no other RM-based selection strategy achieves a win rate above 50%** at any value of `$N$`. All curves lie at or below the `$y=0.5$` line, confirming that WARM `$M=6$` is Pareto-optimal — you cannot beat it by using a different RM for selection at the same or different `$N$`. Individual RMs `$\phi_1$` and `$\phi_2$` have win rates as low as 30–40% against WARM `$M=6$` in the corrupt setting, showing that their selections are meaningfully worse.

- **BoN vs. RL reward hacking resistance:** Appendix Figure 17(b) shows that when WARM `$M=6$` is always used with `$N=1000$` (fixed budget), and other RMs are used with `$1 \leq N \leq 1000$`, the win rate curves are monotonically increasing in `$N$` for all methods — more BoN samples always helps. This contrasts with the RL experiments (where reward hacking causes performance to eventually degrade) and validates that "BoN limits reward hacking compared to RL, as performances get better when increasing `$N$`."

**Baklava-specific BoN results (Appendix Figure 16).** When the two fine-tunings `$\phi_1$` and `$\phi_3$` differ in featurizer initialization via Baklava (SFT checkpoints at steps 12k and 8k respectively), WARM `$M=2$` with Baklava outperforms ENS `$M=2$` with Baklava on both PaLM and T5 generations. The control reward gains are modest (peak ~0.06 for PaLM, ~0.15 for T5) but consistent, and both Baklava-based methods outperform the best individual RM without Baklava, validating that Baklava diversity provides genuine, complementary gains to hyperparameter diversity.

#### RL Fine-tuning Experiments (Section 5.2)

The RL experiments test whether WARM — when used as the proxy reward model during REINFORCE-based policy optimization — mitigates reward hacking and produces better final policies. This is the most stringent test: unlike BoN (where the RM only ranks fixed candidates), RL requires the RM to provide meaningful gradients for policy improvement over thousands of steps as the policy drifts further OOD.

**Control reward results (Figure 8, and Figures 1(b), 18, 20, 21).** The control reward (PaLM-XS RM with 80.1% OOD accuracy and disjoint pretraining) tracks the true quality of the policy's outputs through training.

- **Clean setting (Figure 8(a), `$\alpha=0.003$`):** As the policy KL increases (x-axis), the control reward initially rises from approximately 4 to a peak of 10–11, then **collapses** — the classic reward hacking pattern. WARM `$M=10$`, `$M=6$`, and `$M=2$` all achieve higher peak control rewards and delay the collapse compared to individual RMs. WARM `$M=10$` reaches a control reward of approximately 10.5 at KL ≈ 200 and maintains above 9 out to KL ≈ 800, while `$\phi_1$` peaks at roughly 9.5 and collapses to below 7 by KL ≈ 400. ENS `$M=2$` peaks at a similar level to `$\phi_2$` (around 9.5) and collapses more steeply than WARM `$M=2$`, which sustains above 9 out to KL ≈ 600. The Pareto front is pushed up and left: WARM achieves higher control reward at lower (or equal) KL.

- **Corrupt setting (Figure 8(b), `$\alpha=0.01$`):** With 25% label corruption, the performance of all methods degrades, but the relative advantage of WARM **increases**. WARM `$M=6$` reaches a control reward of approximately 6.5 at KL ≈ 20 and maintains above 6.0 out to KL ≈ 100. `$\phi_1$` peaks at roughly 5.5 and collapses to below 4.5 by KL ≈ 75. ENS `$M=2$` performs similarly to `$\phi_2$` (peak ~5.5–6.0, early collapse). The gap between WARM `$M=6$` and ENS `$M=2$` is substantially larger in the corrupt setting (~1.0–1.5 control reward units) than in the clean setting (~0.5 units), directly validating the theoretical prediction that WA's corruption-robustness advantage matters most under label noise.

- **Effect of `$M$` (Figure 8(a), and Figure 1(b) with steps as x-axis):** In the clean setting, increasing `$M$` from 2 to 6 to 10 progressively improves peak control reward and delays collapse. However, `$M=10$` does not meaningfully improve peak performance over `$M=6$` — both reach ~10.5 control reward — it only delays the eventual collapse (the curve stays higher for longer in terms of KL). The paper speculates this is "related to our weight selection procedure, as the weights `$\{\phi_i\}_{i=7}^{10}$` have lower individual accuracy on `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{ood}}$` than `$\{\phi_i\}_{i=1}^6$`" — adding lower-quality models to the average provides diminishing returns.

- **KL regularization ablation (Figure 8(c)):** The KL coefficient `$\alpha$` controls how strongly the policy is penalized for deviating from SFT. At `$\alpha=0.01$` (strong regularization), KL remains very low (<200 for both WARM `$M=6$` and `$\phi_1$`), and control rewards are modest (~6–8) — the policy is underfitting. At `$\alpha=0.001$` (weak regularization), KL explodes to >1500, and control reward for `$\phi_1$` collapses to below 5, while WARM `$M=6$` shows somewhat better resilience but still degrades. The optimal for WARM `$M=6$` is `$\alpha=0.003$` (peak control reward ~10.5), while `$\phi_1$` at `$\alpha=0.003$` peaks lower and collapses faster. The paper notes that "the optimal value of `$\alpha$` for WARM is lower than for a single RM; this is because WARM can mitigate reward hacking, and thus the optimal policies are obtained for larger values of KL" — in other words, with a better RM, you can afford to let the policy explore further from SFT.

- **Steps-based view (Figures 1(b) and 18):** When plotted against training steps (rather than KL), the same patterns hold. Figure 1(b) shows that in the clean setting, WARM `$M=10$` maintains a control reward above 8 through 8000+ steps, while `$\phi_1$` collapses to ~5 by step 4000. Figure 18 shows that in the corrupt setting, the collapse for all methods happens earlier (~2000–4000 steps) but WARM `$M=6$` still maintains the highest values (above 6 until step 8000, vs. `$\phi_1$` dropping to ~4 by step 4000).

**Oracle preference results (Figure 9, and Figure 19).** The oracle preference metric from the PaLM-L AI labeler evaluates the actual quality of summaries generated by the RL-fine-tuned policies.

- **Win rate vs. SFT (Figure 9(a)):** All RL-fine-tuned policies substantially outperform the SFT baseline. WARM `$M=6$` achieves a win rate of **99.8%** at step 3500 — nearly perfect — and maintains high win rates (>95%) from approximately step 2000 onward. Individual RMs `$\phi_1$` and `$\phi_2$` peak at approximately 97% and 98% respectively, with `$\phi_2$`'s peak occurring earlier (step 2500) before declining, while WARM `$M=6$`'s performance is more sustained. ENS `$M=2$` peaks around 98% at step 3000 but declines thereafter. The sustained performance of WARM is key: it doesn't just peak higher, it maintains quality over a broader range of training steps.

- **Win rate vs. WARM `$M=6$` at step 3500 (Figure 9(b)):** When the policy RL-fine-tuned with WARM `$M=6$` at step 3500 is used as the reference, **no other policy achieves a win rate above 50%** at any training step. All curves lie below 0.5, with `$\phi_1$`-trained policies reaching at most ~48% around step 4000 before declining. WARM `$M=10$`-trained policies reach approximately 48% win rate but never exceed 50%. This confirms that the WARM `$M=6$` policy at step 3500 is Pareto-optimal among all policies trained. The fact that WARM `$M=10$` does not beat WARM `$M=6$` in terms of peak win rate (only in terms of delaying collapse) is consistent with the control reward findings.

- **Win rate vs. `$\phi_1$` at step 3000 (Figure 9(c)):** When the best individual RM policy (trained with `$\phi_1$`, the RM with highest OOD accuracy, at its peak step 3000) is used as reference, **WARM-trained policies achieve a 79.4% win rate** at their peak (WARM `$M=6$` at step 3500). There is a "large region of steps" — approximately steps 2500–4500 for all WARM variants — where the win rate exceeds 50%, meaning WARM-trained policies consistently beat the best single-RM policy. Even WARM `$M=2$` achieves a win rate above 50% against `$\phi_1$` for a wide range of steps. This is the paper's headline number: a policy trained with a weight-averaged reward model decisively beats a policy trained with the best individual reward model.

- **Fixed-step comparison (Appendix Figure 19(a)):** When comparing policies at the same training step (rather than at their respective peaks), WARM `$M=6$` achieves a win rate of 50–80% against all other policies throughout training, with the advantage growing over time (as single-RM policies degrade due to reward hacking while WARM-trained policies maintain quality). This confirms that WARM's benefit is not just about reaching a higher peak but about more stable, sustained improvement.

- **Impact of `$\alpha$` on win rate (Figure 19(b)):** WARM `$M=6$` (`$\alpha=0.003$`) vs. `$\phi_1$` at different `$\alpha$` values: the win rate is highest (>75% around step 3000–4000) when both methods use their respective optimal `$\alpha$`. The advantage persists but narrows when both use the same `$\alpha$`, confirming that WARM's benefit is partly due to enabling better exploration (lower `$\alpha$`, higher KL) without collapsing.

**Distillation setup results (Appendix Figure 22).** In an alternative experimental design where the control RM itself generates the training labels (the "distillation setup" from Gao et al., 2023), WARM `$M=6$` still outperforms baselines in BoN, but the paper notes that "distillation changes the diversity across fine-tuned RMs, thus potentially altering the significance of the distillation setup, motivating us in exploring the more realistic RLAIF setup." This is an honest disclosure of a setup where the paper's theoretical framework (label noise as a key challenge) is less applicable, since the labels are generated by a single (consistent) model rather than being inherently noisy. WARM still helps — but the mechanism is more about variance reduction under distribution shift than about robustness to label corruption.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Linear mode connectivity across diversity sources (Figure 3):** WA accuracy curves satisfy Observation 1 (LMC) — lying on or above the diagonal — across all four diversity settings tested: temporal checkpoints from a single RM fine-tuning (3a), independent runs with shared config (3b), different learning rates (3c), and Baklava initialization (3d). The gain over the diagonal increases with diversity, confirming that diversity amplifies the benefit of averaging. The WA and ENS curves closely track in all settings, validating Observation 2 (first-order equivalence) while showing a small but consistent WA advantage at intermediate `$\lambda$`.

**Moving average vs. independent runs (Remark 2, Figure 3(a)):** Averaging checkpoints collected along a single RM fine-tuning trajectory (moving average) underperforms averaging across independent runs. The paper attributes this to an unfavorable accuracy-diversity trade-off: "incorporating early checkpoints would compromise individual accuracies, and considering only later checkpoints would not bring the necessary diversity." This is not a full ablation (multiple configurations of checkpoint spacing were not tested), but it justifies the choice to use independent fine-tunings rather than the cheaper single-run approach.

**Weight selection procedure (Figure 10):** The accuracy of WARM improves monotonically when RMs are selected from best to worst (by OOD accuracy), reaching ~0.768 with `$M=10$`. Random selection produces lower and more variable accuracy (~0.758–0.764). Selecting from worst to best degrades accuracy below any individual RM. This validates the heuristic but also reveals that the 7th–10th best RMs provide diminishing returns — the curve flattens after `$M=6$`, consistent with the RL finding that `$M=10$` does not improve peak performance over `$M=6$`.

**WA vs. ENS under label corruption (Figures 4 and 5, and Appendix Figures 11–14):** The paper's headline empirical finding for the second-order analysis — WA reduces memorization of corrupted labels. On the corrupted training subset, WA accuracy is 0.45–0.48 vs. ENS accuracy of 0.46–0.50 (Figures 4(a) and 11, with variation across RM pairs). On clean training samples, WA is slightly worse (0.890 vs. 0.892, Figure 4(b)). On ID validation, WA is comparable or slightly better (~0.776 vs. 0.774, Figure 4(c)). On OOD test, WA is consistently better (~0.714 vs. 0.710, Figure 4(d)). The histogram in Figure 5 aggregates across multiple pairs and confirms the distribution shift: the accuracy gain of WA over ENS is negative (ENS better) on corrupted training data, near zero on clean training data, positive on ID validation, and most positive on OOD test. Appendix Figures 11–14 show this pattern replicates across 8 different RM pairs with varying hyperparameters and initializations. **Non-obvious finding:** The consistency across pairs is notable — even RMs that differ only in data order (Figure 3(b) config, but with corrupted labels) show the same WA < ENS on corrupted train, WA > ENS on OOD pattern, suggesting the `$p_j^2$` effect operates even with minimal diversity sources.

**KL regularization strength (`$\alpha$`) (Figure 8(c)):** WARM `$M=6$` and `$\phi_1$` are tested with `$\alpha \in \{0.01, 0.003, 0.001\}$`. For both methods, `$\alpha=0.003$` provides the best peak control reward. For `$\phi_1$`, `$\alpha=0.001$` causes severe collapse (control reward drops to ~3 at high KL), while WARM `$M=6$` at `$\alpha=0.001$` degrades more gracefully (maintains ~6–7 at high KL). At `$\alpha=0.01$`, both methods underfit (low KL, modest control reward). **Practical implication:** WARM enables the use of weaker KL regularization (allowing more exploration) without catastrophic collapse, which in turn allows the policy to reach higher absolute performance.

**Baklava diversity (Appendix Figure 16, Figure 3(d) comparison):** Averaging two RMs with different SFT checkpoint initializations produces WA that outperforms ENS, and the WA of Baklava-diverse RMs outperforms individual RMs. However, the paper does not ablate Baklava against other diversity sources holding other factors equal — for instance, no experiment compares WARM with only Baklava diversity vs. WARM with only hyperparameter diversity at the same `$M$`. The relative contribution of each diversity source cannot be isolated from the available data.

**Distillation setup vs. RLAIF setup (Appendix Figure 22 vs. main experiments):** In the distillation setup (where the control RM generates training labels), WARM `$M=6$` still outperforms baselines in BoN, but the absolute control reward gains are smaller (peak ~0.125 vs. ~0.15 in the PaLM clean RLAIF setting) and the relative ranking of methods is less stratified. The paper acknowledges this as a limitation of the distillation setup for studying label noise robustness.

**Number of training steps for RM fine-tuning (no explicit ablation):** The paper states that RM fine-tunings run for 10k steps and notes that "training for a longer number of steps could help, as it did not alter the LMC in previous works," but no experiment varies this parameter. The sensitivity of WARM's performance to RM training duration is unknown.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: "WARM improves the overall quality and alignment of LLM predictions, achieving a 79.4% win rate against a policy RL-fine-tuned with a single RM."**

This claim is supported by the evidence but requires careful scoping. The 79.4% figure (Section 5.2, Figure 9(c)) compares the WARM `$M=6$` policy at step 3500 against the `$\phi_1$` policy at step 3000 — both at their respective optimal checkpoints. This is a "best vs. best" comparison that is legitimate (you would deploy the best checkpoint regardless of when it occurs) but does not control for the fact that `$\phi_1$`'s peak occurs earlier and degrades faster, which is itself a consequence of reward hacking that WARM mitigates. A complementary comparison at the same training step (Figure 19(a)) shows WARM `$M=6$` winning by 50–80% throughout training. Both framings are valid, but the 79.4% figure conflates WARM's peak-performance advantage with its stability advantage. The experiment also compares against only the single best individual RM (`$\phi_1$`); the paper does not report win rates against the second-best (`$\phi_2$`) or against an ensemble of individual RMs selected by OOD accuracy after the fact.

**Claim 2: "WARM improves reliability under distribution shifts."**

Supported, but the evidence is indirect. The BoN experiments (Figure 6) show WARM achieving higher control rewards and oracle preference win rates, which measure downstream performance under the distribution shift induced by the SFT policy's generation distribution and (in RL) by policy drift. However, the paper does not directly measure RM calibration or accuracy as a function of distribution shift magnitude — for instance, by binning test examples by KL from the training distribution and reporting RM accuracy per bin. The OOD accuracy improvements in Figure 3 and Figure 4(d) are measured on `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{ood}}$` as a whole, but the "distribution shift" is a binary distinction (ID vs. OOD) rather than a continuous variable. The claim that WA reduces variance (Observation 2) and that variance is the dominant error under distribution shifts is supported theoretically and by citation to prior work (Ramé et al., 2022), but variance itself is never directly estimated or decomposed in the paper's experiments.

**Claim 3: "WARM improves robustness to preference inconsistencies (label noise)."**

The strongest and most novel claim in the paper, and the one most directly tested. The corruption experiment (25% label swap, Figures 4 and 5) cleanly isolates the label-noise axis and shows WA systematically outperforms ENS, with the gap growing as evaluation moves OOD. The RL experiment with corrupted labels (Figure 8(b)) shows WARM maintaining substantially higher control rewards than ENS and individual RMs. The theoretical model (Section 4.3) provides a mechanistic explanation (the `$p_j^2$` effect). However, several caveats temper this conclusion:

- **Single corruption level:** Only 25% label corruption is tested. The paper does not sweep corruption rates (e.g., 10%, 50%) to show that the WA-over-ENS advantage scales with noise level, as the theory would predict. At very low corruption rates (approaching 0%), the advantage should vanish since `$p_j \approx 1$` for most features; at very high corruption rates (>50%), all features might have low `$p_j$` and both methods would fail. The robustness of the empirical finding across corruption rates is unknown.

- **Corruption type:** Labels are swapped uniformly at random. This is a simple noise model that may not reflect real-world preference inconsistencies, which are likely structured (e.g., length bias causing systematic over-preference of longer summaries, or certain labelers consistently applying different criteria). The `$p_j^2$` mechanism should still help with structured noise (features correlated with the noise source would have intermediate `$p_j$`), but this is not tested.

- **Theoretical model limitations:** The binary featurizer model (Section 4.3) makes strong simplifying assumptions — orthogonal features, bag-of-features inputs, small `$\sigma$`, infinite training data, binary feature selection — that do not hold for real transformer-based RMs. The gap between the clean theoretical prediction (`$p_j^2$` vs. `$p_j$`) and the empirical reality (where features are continuous, correlated, and selected through complex optimization dynamics) is unquantified. The theory proves that WA *can* suppress memorization under certain conditions, not that it *must* in all realistic settings.

**Potential weaknesses in the experimental design:**

- **`$M=2$` ENS only:** The paper compares WARM with `$M \in \{2, 6, 10\}$` to ENS with `$M=2$` only, citing computational constraints for RL. This is an asymmetric comparison: WARM at `$M=6$` beating ENS at `$M=2$` could be driven by `$M$` rather than the WA mechanism. The comparison that isolates the WA-vs-ENS difference is WARM `$M=2$` vs. ENS `$M=2$`, which the paper does present and which does show WA outperforming ENS (Figures 6, 8, though the gap is smaller). However, a WARM `$M=6$` vs. hypothetical ENS `$M=6$` comparison — which would require 6× the RL compute — is missing, so the scaling of the WA advantage with `$M$` cannot be fully disentangled from the scaling of ENS with `$M$`.

- **Single benchmark (TL;DR summarization):** All results are on Reddit TL;DR with PaLM models. The paper acknowledges this as a limitation in Section 6 ("WARM, while innovative, does face some limitations") but does not discuss whether the findings might differ for other tasks (dialogue, instruction following, safety-critical alignment) or other model families. Summarization has specific properties — clear quality signals, relatively objective evaluation, limited output diversity — that may make RM quality differences more detectable than in open-ended tasks.

- **Single AI labeler for evaluation:** The oracle preference metric uses a single PaLM-L model prompted with chain-of-thought. While Lee et al. (2023) showed this correlates with human preferences, the paper does not report human evaluation results for the final policies. The AI labeler and the AI feedback used to train the RMs come from the same model family (PaLM-L), albeit with different prompts and procedures, raising the possibility of shared biases inflating the apparent gains.

- **No confidence intervals:** None of the main results (Figures 6–9) report error bars, confidence intervals, or standard deviations. The number of prompts used for BoN (15,000 for PaLM, 1,000 for T5) is large enough that many differences may be statistically significant, but without reported variance, the reader cannot assess the reliability of the stated improvements.

- **Missing RL baseline — KL regularization alone:** The paper ablates `$\alpha$` for WARM and a single RM, but does not report a baseline that varies `$\alpha$` for the single RM to see if the single RM, with optimal `$\alpha$` tuning, can approach WARM's performance. The finding that WARM enables lower `$\alpha$` is interesting, but the counterfactual — a single RM with very weak KL regularization and aggressive early stopping to prevent collapse — is not explored as a potential cheaper alternative.

- **RM training data contamination:** The RMs are trained on summaries generated by GPT-3 variants (from the Stiennon et al., 2020 dataset), while the policies evaluated in BoN and RL generate summaries from PaLM-XS and T5-XS. This represents a real distribution shift (as intended), but the paper does not characterize how large this shift is or whether the RM's base capability (accuracy on its own training distribution) is the bottleneck rather than OOD generalization. If the RMs are undertrained even on ID data, WARM's benefit might partly reflect improved ID performance rather than specifically improved OOD reliability.

- **Checkpoint selection policy for RL:** For the oracle preference metric, the paper compares policies at specific training steps (step 3500 for WARM `$M=6$`, step 3000 for `$\phi_1$`). These steps are selected post-hoc based on peak performance in the oracle metric. While the paper does show win rates across all steps (Figure 9), the headline comparison at specific steps raises the question of whether a practitioner without access to the oracle metric could select the optimal checkpoint. The proxy RM's score would be the natural checkpoint selection criterion, but as the paper itself notes, proxy RM scores become unreliable due to reward hacking — so how would a practitioner know when to stop? This practical concern is not addressed.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The $M=2$ ENS Baseline Makes the WA-vs-ENS Comparison Asymmetric

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's core empirical claim is that WARM outperforms prediction ensembling (ENS), particularly under label corruption. However, all comparisons involving ENS use only $M=2$ ensemble members, while WARM is evaluated with $M \in \{2, 6, 10\}$. The paper acknowledges this explicitly: "only with $M=2$ for computational reasons" (Section 5.1) and "ENS (with $M=2$ for computational reasons)" (Section 5.2). The stated reason is that running RL fine-tuning with multiple parallel RMs at inference time is prohibitively expensive — each RM is itself a PaLM-XXS LLM, and the memory and per-generation compute cost scales linearly with $M$.

**The consequence.** The comparison that isolates the WA-vs-ENS mechanism (holding $M$ constant) is WARM $M=2$ vs. ENS $M=2$, which the paper does present and which does show WA outperforming ENS in most settings. However, the headline results — Figures 6, 8, and 9 — prominently display WARM $M=6$ and $M=10$ curves alongside a single ENS $M=2$ curve, creating a visual asymmetry where WARM benefits from both the WA mechanism AND larger $M$, while ENS is constrained to $M=2$. A practitioner reading these figures cannot determine whether WARM $M=6$ outperforms a hypothetical ENS $M=6$, or whether ENS $M=6$ would close most or all of the gap. If the primary advantage of WARM is enabling larger effective ensemble sizes (because computational cost does not scale with $M$), that is a legitimate practical benefit, but it is distinct from the paper's theoretical claim that WA has fundamentally different properties from ENS under label corruption. The clean-setting results (Figure 3, Figure 8(a)) suggest the WARM $M=2$ vs. ENS $M=2$ gap is modest — often smaller than the gap between WARM $M=2$ and WARM $M=6$. This raises the possibility that a substantial fraction of WARM's measured advantage comes from using more models, not from the weight-averaging mechanism per se.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The WARM $M=2$ vs. ENS $M=2$ comparison appears in all relevant figures: in Figure 3 (the LMC validation), the WA and ENS curves for two-models are directly comparable and show WA slightly outperforming ENS, with the gap growing in the more diverse settings (3b–3d). In the BoN experiments (Figure 6), WARM $M=2$ achieves control reward gains above ENS $M=2$ in the T5 settings, but the gap is smaller in the PaLM settings. In the RL experiments (Figure 8), WARM $M=2$ outperforms ENS $M=2$ in the clean setting and more clearly in the corrupt setting. In the oracle preference results (Figure 9), WARM $M=2$ achieves win rates intermediate between ENS $M=2$ and WARM $M=6$. None of the experiments compare ENS at larger $M$ — no ENS $M=6$ or $M=10$ results exist, even for the cheaper BoN setting.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this asymmetry. It does not report ENS results with larger $M$ for the BoN experiments (which would be substantially cheaper than RL and could establish whether the ENS performance curve saturates or continues improving with $M$). The authors do not discuss the extent to which the $M$-scaling contributes to WARM's advantage versus the WA mechanism, nor do they propose an experimental design that would disentangle these. A BoN experiment sweeping ENS $M \in \{2, 4, 6, 10\}$ and comparing to WARM at the same $M$ values would be the natural way to assess this, but it is absent.

---

### 6.2 Difficulty Estimation Cost for WARM Weight Selection Is Invisible in the Efficiency Claim

**The assumption or constraint.** WARM's headline efficiency benefit over ENS — "requiring a single model at inference time" with "no memory or inference overhead" (Section 1, abstract) — accounts for the cost of running the averaged model during deployment, but does not account for the cost of producing the $M$ individual RMs in the first place, nor the cost of selecting which RMs to include in the average. The weight selection procedure (Section 3.3, Appendix B.3) requires: (1) training $M$ separate RMs (10 in the main experiments) with different hyperparameters and/or SFT initializations, (2) evaluating each RM on the OOD dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{ood}}$ (92k pairwise comparisons) to rank them by accuracy, and (3) selecting the top-$M$ RMs for averaging. Figure 10 validates this procedure and shows that selecting RMs at random or from worst-to-best degrades performance — the selection step is load-bearing, not optional.

**The consequence.** The total training compute cost of WARM is approximately $M \times$ the cost of training a single RM (for the initial fine-tunings), plus the cost of OOD evaluation for model selection. This cost can be amortized if WARM is trained once and deployed many times, but it is not zero, and it is not included in the paper's efficiency framing. For a practitioner, the claim "WARM incurs no memory or inference overhead" is true at deployment time but obscures a significant upfront training cost: producing 10 RMs costs roughly 10× the compute of producing a single RM, and if the SFT Baklava checkpoints don't already exist, there is additional cost to produce them. In settings where RM training is expensive (e.g., large-scale production RMs trained on massive preference datasets), this 10× upfront cost may be prohibitive. Additionally, the selection procedure relies on having an OOD evaluation dataset with known preference labels — if such a dataset is not available (which is likely in a novel deployment), the practitioner cannot reliably select which RMs to average and risks including poorly-performing models that degrade the final WARM RM.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides full details of the training procedure (Appendix B.3): 10 RM fine-tunings of 10k steps each with batch size 128, using PaLM-XXS models. The selection validation is Figure 10, which shows that different selection strategies produce OOD accuracies ranging from ~0.752 to ~0.768 — a meaningful spread. The paper does not provide a FLOPs or wall-clock comparison of total WARM cost (training + selection) vs. ENS cost (training + inference), nor does it discuss the regimes where the amortized cost of training multiple RMs might outweigh the deployment-time efficiency gain. The paper briefly notes in Remark 2 that the moving average alternative (averaging checkpoints along a single run) is "less costly for training" but underperforms, so WARM intentionally incurs the multi-run training cost.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges this in the efficiency framing: the "efficiency" claim is explicitly scoped to "memory or inference overhead" (Section 1) and "no memory and inference overheads of prediction ensembling" (Section 3.1). The text does not claim that WARM reduces total training cost. However, the broader narrative — that WARM is a "simple, efficient and scalable strategy" — is likely to be interpreted by practitioners as an end-to-end efficiency claim unless the training cost caveat is made explicit. The paper does not discuss the amortization tradeoff or suggest methods for reducing the upfront cost (e.g., using fewer M, recycling RMs across deployments, or developing cheaper selection heuristics). The Baklava method (recycling SFT checkpoints) is one implicit cost-reduction strategy (the checkpoints are already produced during standard SFT), but the paper does not frame it as such.

### 6.3 Single Benchmark, Single Model Family, Single Preference Labeling Strategy

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the Reddit TL;DR summarization benchmark (Völske et al., 2017) with PaLM model architectures: PaLM-XXS for RMs, PaLM-XS for policies, and PaLM-L for the AI labeler (both for training RMs and for evaluation). The preference labels are generated using the RLAIF procedure from Lee et al. (2023) — a PaLM-L model prompted with chain-of-thought — rather than human annotators. The paper states this explicitly in Section 5: "We train PaLM-XXS RMs on the TL;DR summarization benchmark where preference labels are generated by a PaLM-L model prompted with chain-of-thought."

**The consequence.** Three distinct generalization questions are left unanswered: **(1) Task generalization:** Summarization has specific structural properties — the output is a compressed version of the input, quality has reasonably objective dimensions (factual consistency, coverage, conciseness), and the evaluation is less subjective than tasks like dialogue or creative writing. It is unclear whether WARM's benefits transfer to tasks where preferences are more heterogeneous or where the RM's base performance is lower. **(2) Model family generalization:** All experiments use PaLM architectures. The LMC property — which underpins WARM — is known to hold broadly for fine-tuned transformer models sharing pre-training (as established in the computer vision literature), but the specific LMC characteristics (how wide the convex basin is, how much diversity can be tolerated before connectivity breaks) may vary across model families, scales, and pre-training procedures. A practitioner using LLaMA, GPT, or a different model family cannot be certain that the hyperparameter diversity ranges tested (`learning rate $\in \{10^{-5}, 4 \times 10^{-5}, 10^{-4}\}$`) will preserve LMC for their setup. **(3) Label source generalization:** Using AI-generated labels (RLAIF) rather than human labels means the preference data has specific noise characteristics — systematic biases from the labeling prompt, consistent error patterns from the labeling model, and potentially different inter-rater agreement statistics than human labelers. The paper's corruption experiment (25% random label swap) is a simplified noise model that may not capture the structured noise in AI-generated or human-generated preferences.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All main results (Figures 6–9) are on TL;DR summarization. The distillation setup in Appendix Figure 22 explores an alternative labeling strategy (where the control RM itself generates the training labels) and shows WARM still helps, but this is still the same task and model family. The paper does not report results on other RLHF benchmarks (e.g., InstructGPT-style helpfulness/harmlessness, dialogue, or coding tasks). There are no experiments with non-PaLM architectures. The paper acknowledges this implicitly in the Discussion (Section 6): "WARM, while innovative, does face some limitations, notably two when compared to prediction ensembling methods; first, prediction ensembling can benefit from the diversity brought by combining RMs from various architectures and pre-trainings." This acknowledges that WARM trades off the ability to ensemble across heterogeneous model families for the efficiency of weight-space averaging, but the practical significance of this tradeoff is not empirically quantified.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address the single-benchmark/single-family limitation through additional experiments. It does not discuss what properties of TL;DR summarization might make WARM more or less effective compared to other tasks, nor does it provide guidance for practitioners on how to assess whether WARM will transfer to their domain. The mention of cross-architecture ensembling as an ENS advantage (Section 6) is the only acknowledgment that the homogeneity constraint might matter in practice.

### 6.4 The Label Corruption Experiment Tests Only One Simplistic Noise Model

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central theoretical claim — that WA suppresses memorization of corrupted labels via the $p_j^2$ mechanism — is tested empirically using a single corruption strategy: 25% of training preference labels are randomly swapped (Section 4.2, Appendix B.2). This means for 25% of the triples $(x, y^+, y^-)$ in $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$, the preferred and dispreferred summaries are exchanged, destroying the signal for those examples entirely.

**The consequence.** Real-world preference inconsistencies are unlikely to follow this uniform random corruption pattern. Human labelers (and AI labelers) exhibit structured biases: they may systematically prefer longer responses (length bias), responses with certain formatting (bullet points, markdown), or responses that mirror their own demographic perspective. They may struggle with specific types of content (technical material, non-English text, edge cases) while being reliable on others. These structured noise patterns would produce **correlated** feature learning across runs — if all labelers systematically prefer longer summaries, then all RMs will learn to associate length with reward (high $p_j$ for the length feature), and WA's $p_j^2$ suppression would not help (since $p_j^2 \approx p_j$ when $p_j \approx 1$). The theory predicts WARM's advantage over ENS specifically for features with **intermediate** $p_j$ — learned by some runs but not others — which is characteristic of idiosyncratic overfitting to individual corrupted examples, but may not characterize systematic labeling biases that affect all runs similarly. Additionally, only 25% corruption is tested. The paper does not sweep corruption rates to validate the theory's prediction that the WA-over-ENS gap should scale with the fraction of corrupted examples (more corruption → more features with intermediate $p_j$ → larger WA advantage).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 25% random swap is the only corruption variant tested. Figures 6(b), 6(d), 7(b), 7(d), 8(b), and 18 all use this single corruption level. The paper does not test other noise models (e.g., systematically preferring longer responses in 25% of examples, labeler-specific noise patterns, or noise concentrated in specific difficulty subsets). The theoretical model in Section 4.3 assumes features are orthogonal and independently selected, which implicitly assumes uncorrelated corruption — each corrupted example might cause a different spurious feature to be learned. The paper does not extend the theory to structured noise or discuss how correlated biases would affect the $p_j^2$ mechanism.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss the gap between the simplistic random-swap noise model and realistic preference noise. It does not test alternative corruption strategies or sweep corruption rates. The Discussion (Section 6) acknowledges a related limitation — "it does not completely eradicate all forms of spurious correlations or biases inherent in the preference data. For instance, if each individual RM predominantly relies on summary length as a criterion, WARM is likely to replicate this tendency" — which directly bears on this issue, but the acknowledgment is not connected back to the experimental design or the theoretical model.

### 6.5 No Oracle Checkpoint Selection Policy: WARM Improves Peak Performance But Doesn't Solve the Checkpoint Selection Problem

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper compares RL-fine-tuned policies at their respective oracle-optimal checkpoints: WARM $M=6$ at step 3500 vs. $\phi_1$ at step 3000 (Figure 9). These steps are selected post-hoc based on peak performance according to the oracle preference metric (the PaLM-L AI labeler). However, the paper itself identifies checkpoint selection as a central challenge in RLHF (Section 1): reward hacking "complicates checkpoint selection due to the unreliability of the proxy RM." In practice, a practitioner deploying RLHF does not have access to an oracle preference metric — they must select which checkpoint to deploy using the proxy RM's score or a held-out validation metric, both of which become corrupted by reward hacking.

**The consequence.** While WARM delays reward hacking and achieves higher peak oracle performance, the paper does not demonstrate that WARM enables reliable checkpoint selection using only the proxy RM's score. If a practitioner trains with WARM and selects the checkpoint with the highest WARM reward, they may select a reward-hacked checkpoint (since WARM delays but does not eliminate overoptimization — the control reward in Figure 8(a) still eventually declines for WARM $M=6$). The paper's headline win-rate metrics (79.4% against the best single-RM policy) compare oracle-selected checkpoints — they represent an upper bound on what a practitioner could achieve if they had perfect knowledge of which checkpoint to deploy. Without a reliable checkpoint selection strategy that works without the oracle, the practical gain from WARM may be smaller than the headline numbers suggest.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 8(a) shows that for WARM $M=6$, the control reward peaks at KL $\approx$ 200–400 and then gradually declines. If a practitioner selects the checkpoint that maximizes the proxy WARM RM's own score (rather than the control reward), they risk selecting a later checkpoint where the proxy reward is still high but the true quality has degraded. The paper does not plot the proxy RM's score alongside the control reward to show the divergence. Figure 9(a) shows that WARM $M=6$'s oracle win rate against SFT remains above 95% from steps 2000–4500, suggesting a broad plateau where any checkpoint is good — but Figure 9(b) shows that win rate against the best WARM $M=6$ checkpoint varies significantly, meaning not all checkpoints are equally good. The paper does not report, for any RM, what checkpoint would be selected by its own proxy score and what oracle win rate that checkpoint would achieve.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss the checkpoint selection problem or propose a solution. It does not report proxy-RM-selected checkpoint performance for any method. The broader RLHF literature has explored using KL divergence thresholds or validation on held-out prompts for checkpoint selection, but WARM does not engage with or improve upon these strategies. This limitation is particularly notable because WARM is explicitly motivated by the reward hacking problem that makes checkpoint selection difficult, yet the experimental evaluation evaluates WARM at oracle-selected checkpoints without addressing whether those checkpoints are identifiable in practice.

### 6.6 WARM Generalizes Across Fine-Tuning Runs But Not Across Architectures, Pre-Training Procedures, or Data Sources

**The assumption or constraint.** WARM's weight averaging requires that all RMs share the same pre-trained initialization, same architecture, and are fine-tuned on the same preference dataset. The method explicitly leverages linear mode connectivity, which requires a shared pre-training (Remark 1: "WA is actually possible only because of the shared pre-training which constrains the divergence during fine-tunings"). Prediction ensembling (ENS), in contrast, can combine RMs with different architectures, different pre-trainings, or even trained on different datasets — each RM processes the input independently and only their scalar reward predictions are averaged.

**The consequence.** WARM trades off the ability to incorporate heterogeneous sources of reward signal for the efficiency of weight-space averaging. There are several scenarios where this tradeoff may be costly: **(1) Multi-architecture ensembles:** If the best available RMs come from different model families (e.g., one PaLM-based, one LLaMA-based, one GPT-based), WARM cannot combine them — the weight spaces are incommensurable without weight-matching algorithms (which the paper does not explore). ENS can trivially average their predictions. **(2) Diverse pre-training:** RMs pre-trained on different corpora or with different objectives may capture complementary aspects of response quality. WARM requires a single shared pre-training, limiting this diversity source. **(3) Multi-dataset training:** The Discussion (Section 6) mentions that "a straightforward extension of WARM would combine RMs trained on different datasets, for example, coming from different (clusters of) labelers." However, this is described as a future extension, not a capability of the current method. If RMs are fine-tuned on different datasets with different label distributions, their weights may diverge into different loss basins, breaking LMC.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** No experiments test WARM with RMs from different architectures or pre-trainings — all RMs are PaLM-XXS models sharing the same pre-training and SFT. The Baklava method introduces diversity through different SFT checkpoints, but these all come from the same SFT trajectory on the same data, which is a much weaker form of diversity than different pre-training objectives. The paper acknowledges this limitation in Section 6: "prediction ensembling can benefit from the diversity brought by combining RMs from various architectures and pre-trainings." The scale of the advantage ENS gains from cross-architecture diversity relative to the advantage WARM gains from the $p_j^2$ mechanism is unknown — in regimes where cross-architecture diversity is critical (e.g., if single-architecture RMs all share the same blind spots), ENS may outperform WARM even under label noise.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation but does not attempt to quantify its significance or propose workarounds. Weight-matching techniques (e.g., Git Re-Basin, Ainsworth et al., 2022) exist for aligning weight spaces from different training runs and could potentially enable cross-architecture WA, but the paper does not discuss or test these. The homogeneity constraint is fundamental to the current WARM method — relaxing it would require a substantially different technical approach. The paper presents this as a tradeoff (efficiency + robustness vs. diversity of ensembling sources) rather than a limitation to be solved, which is honest but leaves practitioners without guidance on which factor dominates in their setting.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

WARM introduces a **conceptual reframing of the reward modeling problem in RLHF** by demonstrating that weight averaging and prediction ensembling are not merely computationally interchangeable (the first-order approximation view inherited from prior OOD generalization work) but are **qualitatively different estimators with distinct asymptotic behavior under label noise**. This reframing has several downstream consequences for how the field thinks about reward model robustness.

The most important shift is the **reconciliation of two previously separate paradigms** for building reliable reward models. The ensembling tradition (Christiano et al., 2017; Eisenstein et al., 2023; Coste et al., 2023) treats the problem as variance reduction: train multiple RMs, average their predictions, and the noise cancels. The invariance tradition (Arjovsky et al., 2019; IRM and its variants in domain generalization) treats the problem as causal feature identification: find predictive mechanisms that are stable across environments, because those are the ones that generalize under distribution shift. WARM's theoretical analysis in Section 4.3 shows that weight averaging actually **does both simultaneously** — the first-order Taylor approximation provides variance reduction (like ENS), while the `p_j^2` feature-probability squaring effect provides invariance regularization (suppressing run-specific mechanisms and preserving run-invariant ones). This synthesis matters because it provides a unified theoretical foundation for why weight averaging works, and suggests that the two paradigms — which were previously pursued by largely disjoint research communities — are complementary rather than competing.

This reframing also **changes the status of prediction ensembling from a gold-standard baseline to a potentially suboptimal choice** under realistic RLHF conditions. Prior to WARM, the implicit consensus (from Christiano et al., 2017 through Eisenstein et al., 2023) was that ENS represents the best achievable RM quality, with the only drawback being computational cost. WARM's corruption experiments (Figures 4, 5, 8(b)) demonstrate that ENS is specifically vulnerable to label noise in ways that WA is not — ENS linearly weights features according to `p_j`, meaning it preserves noise-correlated features that are learned by a subset of models. This is not a small quantitative difference; it represents a structural limitation of prediction averaging that cannot be overcome by increasing the ensemble size `M`. In the limit `M → ∞`, ENS converges to `y · Σ_j p_j · |z_j|^2` while WA converges to `y · Σ_j p_j^2 · |z_j|^2` — the gap is baked into the asymptotic behavior, not the finite-sample approximation. This means that **for deployments where preference labels are noisy** — which includes essentially all RLAIF pipelines (AI-generated labels with systematic biases) and many human-labeled datasets (with low inter-rater agreement reported at 72.6% for InstructGPT) — **WARM should be preferred over ENS even ignoring computational efficiency**, because it provides a qualitatively different and more appropriate inductive bias.

The paper also **provides a diagnostic decomposition of reward hacking into two separable axes**: distribution shift (the policy drifts OOD during RL) and label noise (the preference training data is corrupted). The clean-vs-corrupt experimental design isolates these axes and shows that they require different solutions. Under clean labels, WARM and ENS perform similarly because both address the distribution-shift axis through variance reduction — the gap is modest (Figure 8(a) shows WARM `M=2` and ENS `M=2` tracking closely). Under corrupted labels, WARM substantially outperforms ENS because the `p_j^2` mechanism directly addresses memorization of noisy labels — the gap is large (Figure 8(b), where WARM `M=2` at peak is ~1.0–1.5 control reward units above ENS `M=2`). This decomposition reframes the reward hacking mitigation problem from a single-axis challenge ("make the RM more robust") to a two-axis challenge ("make the RM robust to OOD inputs AND to label corruption"), and suggests that different technical approaches may be optimal depending on which axis dominates in a given deployment. For high-quality human-annotated preference data (low label noise), the distribution-shift axis dominates and efficient ensembling (possibly via WA) is sufficient. For AI-generated labels or crowd-sourced annotations (high label noise), the label-noise axis dominates and WA's specific corruption-robustness becomes essential. This diagnostic framework makes the paper's findings actionable beyond the specific WARM method: it tells practitioners what to measure and what to prioritize.

A more subtle landscape shift is the **elevation of weight space geometry from an implementation detail to a design resource**. The Baklava method (Figure 2) demonstrates that intermediate SFT checkpoints — which are typically discarded as byproducts of training — can be repurposed as diversity sources that respect the pre-training connectivity constraint. This reframes the diversity-connectivity tradeoff in weight averaging: rather than treating connectivity as a fragile constraint that limits how much diversity you can inject, Baklava treats it as a structural invariant that can be exploited to get diversity "for free" from existing training artifacts. This suggests a broader design principle: any source of variation that produces weights within the same convex loss basin (different checkpoints along a single training trajectory, different hyperparameters within mild ranges, different data orders) is a candidate diversity source, and the cost of exploring these sources is essentially zero beyond what is already spent during standard training. This shifts weight averaging from a method that requires careful tuning to avoid breaking connectivity, to a method where the default training pipeline already generates usable diversity. Future work on WA methods can build on this principle by designing training procedures that explicitly maximize the diversity of intermediate artifacts while guaranteeing connectivity, rather than treating connectivity as an after-the-fact compatibility check.

Finally, the paper **narrows the research agenda around reward model improvement in a productive way**. By demonstrating that verifier over-optimization is not a monolithic problem but decomposes into distribution-shift and label-noise components, the paper implicitly argues that efforts to improve RM training data quality (reducing label noise) and efforts to improve RM architecture (handling distribution shift) are partially substitutable. This has practical implications for resource allocation: if you have budget to either collect cleaner preference labels OR develop more sophisticated RM architectures, the paper's decomposition helps you decide which investment will yield larger marginal returns based on which axis is currently the bottleneck in your deployment. It also suggests that **improving verifier robustness to label noise — rather than developing ever-more-complex search algorithms or ensembling strategies** — may be the highest-leverage direction for mitigating reward hacking, echoing a similar finding in the test-time compute scaling literature where verifier over-optimization (rather than search algorithm sophistication) was identified as the primary bottleneck.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Scaling WARM to diverse architectures via weight matching.** The paper explicitly acknowledges that WARM is limited to RMs sharing the same architecture and pre-training, while ENS can combine heterogeneous models (Section 6). However, recent work on weight matching (Git Re-Basin, Ainsworth et al., 2022) has shown that the weight spaces of independently trained models can be aligned through permutation symmetries — effectively finding a transformation that maps one model's weights into the basin of another. A direct follow-up would test whether weight-matched RMs from different architectures (e.g., one PaLM-XXS and one T5-based RM, or RMs with different pre-training corpora) can be meaningfully averaged after alignment, and whether the `p_j^2` benefit persists across architectures. The experiment would require: (1) train RMs from two different model families on the same TL;DR preference dataset, (2) apply Git Re-Basin to align their weight spaces, (3) average the aligned weights, (4) evaluate on the clean and corrupt setups from the paper. A null result (WA fails despite weight matching) would suggest that the `p_j^2` mechanism depends on fine-grained weight-space proximity that permutation matching cannot recover. A positive result would dramatically expand WARM's applicability to multi-architecture deployments. The key metric: does cross-architecture WA preserve the WA > ENS gap on OOD data under label corruption?

**Sweeping corruption rates and noise structures to validate the `p_j^2` theory.** The paper tests a single corruption level (25% random label swap), but the theory in Section 4.3 makes a quantitative prediction: the WA-over-ENS gap should scale monotonically with the fraction of corrupted examples, because more corruption means more features with intermediate `p_j` (learned by some runs but not others), and the gap between `p_j` and `p_j^2` is maximized at `p_j = 0.5`. A clean experiment would sweep corruption rates from 0% to 50% in 10% increments on the TL;DR benchmark, measuring the WA-minus-ENS accuracy gap on OOD data at each rate. The theory predicts a concave relationship (gap starts near zero at 0%, grows as more features acquire intermediate `p_j`, saturates or declines at very high corruption where even invariant features become unreliable). Beyond uniform random corruption, testing structured noise models (systematically preferring longer summaries, injecting position bias into the preference labels, simulating labeler-specific error patterns) would determine whether real-world noise produces the same intermediate-`p_j` features that WA suppresses. A finding that WA's advantage vanishes under structured noise (because all RMs learn the same biased features, giving them `p_j ≈ 1`) would scope WARM's benefit to idiosyncratic label noise rather than systematic labeling biases. The experiment also needs to control for `M`: sweep `M ∈ {2, 4, 6, 8}` at each corruption rate to determine whether the asymptotic `p_j^2` vs. `p_j` behavior is approached in the finite-`M` regime used in practice.

**Dynamic weight selection: online identification of which RMs to average.** The paper's weight selection procedure (Figure 10) is static and requires an OOD evaluation dataset. In a production RLHF pipeline, the policy's output distribution shifts during RL training, meaning an RM that was high-quality on the initial OOD dataset may become unreliable later. A dynamic extension would periodically re-evaluate all `M` trained RMs on recent policy generations (using the control RM or a held-out scoring procedure) and update the WARM average to exclude RMs that have degraded. The key question: does the set of "best" RMs change over the course of RL training, and does dynamic re-weighting further delay reward hacking beyond static WARM? The experiment would track individual RM accuracy on policy-generated summaries at training step intervals (every 500 steps), recompute WARM using only the top-`k` RMs at each interval, and measure whether dynamic WARM pushes the control reward collapse further right (in KL or steps) compared to static WARM. This would also help answer a practical question the paper leaves open: how should a practitioner select the optimal checkpoint during RL training without oracle preference access? If dynamic WARM's own reward signal remains reliable for longer, it could serve as a better checkpoint selection metric.

**Combining WARM with active preference data collection.** The paper identifies distribution shift as a primary cause of RM unreliability, and notes that iterative data collection (retraining the RM on the evolving policy's outputs) is effective but expensive. WARM's efficiency — producing a single model that is more robust to both distribution shift and label noise — could reduce the frequency or cost of active data collection. The experiment: run RL fine-tuning with WARM as the proxy RM, periodically collect new preference labels on the policy's generations (e.g., every 2000 steps), fine-tune a new batch of `M` RMs on the augmented dataset, and compute a new WARM average. Compare against a baseline that uses a single RM with the same active data collection schedule. The prediction: WARM requires fewer active data collection rounds to achieve the same final policy quality, because its robustness to distribution shift and label noise allows it to remain reliable for longer on stale data. The metric is total labeling cost (number of human or AI preference judgments) to reach a target oracle win rate. A calibrated result — e.g., "WARM with active collection every 4000 steps matches the performance of a single RM with active collection every 2000 steps, reducing labeling cost by 50%" — would directly quantify the practical benefit.

**Stress-testing WARM on open-ended generation tasks where reward modeling is harder.** The paper's experiments are restricted to summarization, where quality has reasonably objective dimensions and the RM's base accuracy is relatively high (best individual RM reaches ~76% OOD accuracy on TL;DR). On tasks where reward modeling is intrinsically harder — such as dialogue (where preferences are highly subjective and context-dependent), creative writing (where quality is multi-dimensional and hard to verbalize), or safety-critical alignment (where rare failure modes dominate) — the RM's base accuracy may be much lower, and the relative contribution of WARM's variance reduction and `p_j^2` regularization may change. A replication on a dialogue benchmark (e.g., Anthropic's helpfulness/harmlessness data) or an instruction-following benchmark (e.g., AlpacaFarm or LMSYS-Chat-1M) would establish whether WARM's benefits scale to lower-accuracy RM regimes. The experiment should also vary the RM architecture size: if the base RM's accuracy is low because of limited capacity (PaLM-XXS is relatively small), does scaling to larger RMs (PaLM-XS, PaLM-S) change the WA-over-ENS gap? A finding that WARM's relative advantage shrinks as RM capacity increases would suggest that the `p_j^2` benefit is most impactful in the underparameterized regime where individual RMs are forced to rely on spurious features.

**WA of DPO policies as an alternative to WA of RMs.** The paper briefly mentions in Section 6 and Appendix A.2 that Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Rafailov et al., 2023) merges reward modeling and policy learning into a single stage, and that "the WA of DPO policies, theoretically equivalent to the WA of RMs, is a promising research direction with already significant empirical results on public benchmarks, as demonstrated in [Labonne, 2024]." A systematic comparison would train `M` DPO policies with the same diversity sources used in WARM (different learning rates, dropout, data orders, Baklava SFT initialization), weight-average the resulting policy parameters, and evaluate the averaged policy directly (without a separate RM). The key question is whether weight averaging DPO policies provides the same `p_j^2` robustness benefit as weight averaging RMs, or whether the implicit reward representation in DPO changes the dynamics of feature suppression. The experiment needs to compare: (1) WARM (WA of RMs) → standard RL, vs. (2) WA of DPO policies directly, vs. (3) ENS of DPO policies (averaging their output probabilities rather than weights), all under clean and corrupt preference labels, measuring oracle win rate against a SFT baseline. A finding that WA of DPO policies matches or exceeds WARM + RL would streamline the alignment pipeline by removing the separate RM training and RL stages.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**RLAIF pipelines with AI-generated preference labels.** The paper's RLAIF setup — using a PaLM-L model to generate preference labels for training PaLM-XXS RMs — is increasingly common in production LLM alignment, where human annotation is expensive and slow. AI-generated labels are known to introduce systematic noise: length bias, position bias, sensitivity to prompting strategy, and inconsistency across labeling models. WARM's `p_j^2` mechanism directly addresses the idiosyncratic memorization that this noise induces, as demonstrated in the corrupt-label experiments (Figure 8(b), where WARM `M=6` maintains ~6.5 control reward at peak vs. ~5.5 for the best individual RM). For a team deploying RLAIF at scale — training a reward model on millions of AI-labeled preferences to align a production chatbot — WARM provides a drop-in improvement: launch `M` parallel RM fine-tunings on the same preference data with mild hyperparameter sweeps (which would likely be run anyway during hyperparameter tuning), average the best `M` weights, and deploy a single RM. The training cost scales with `M` (10× for the `M=10` used in the paper's experiments) but inference cost is unchanged, and the amortized cost over millions of RLHF-trained outputs is negligible. The expected benefit: a ~15–20% relative improvement in control reward at peak RL performance (comparing WARM `M=6` to the best individual RM in Figure 8(a)) and a significantly delayed reward hacking collapse (WARM `M=6` sustains control reward above 9 to KL ≈ 600 vs. KL ≈ 300 for the best individual RM), translating to more stable RL training runs and fewer wasted compute cycles on runs that collapse early.

**Federated or privacy-preserving reward modeling.** The paper notes in Section 6 that WARM "follows the updatable machine learning paradigm, eliminating the need for inter-server communication, thus enabling embarrassingly simple parallelization of RMs. This facilitates its use in federated learning scenarios where the data should remain private." In a federated RLHF deployment — where different organizations or user clusters contribute preference data that cannot be centralized due to privacy constraints — each data silo can independently fine-tune an RM on its local preference data (starting from the same shared pre-trained initialization). The resulting RMs are then weight-averaged at a central server without any data leaving the local silos. This is qualitatively different from prediction ensembling in a federated setting: ENS would require each silo to run its RM on every inference query (or to send RM predictions to a central server, which leaks information about the query distribution). WA sends weights once and then runs inference on a single model anywhere. The `p_j^2` mechanism provides an additional privacy benefit that the paper discusses: "WA would add a layer of privacy and bias mitigation by reducing the memorization of private preference." Features that are specific to a single silo's preference data (e.g., idiosyncratic preferences of a particular user demographic) will have low `p_j` across the full population of silo-specific RMs and will be suppressed in the final WARM model, reducing the risk that the deployed RM encodes and exposes private preference patterns. This application is speculative — the paper does not run federated experiments — but the architecture directly supports it, and the memorization-reduction property is demonstrated in the corruption experiment (Figure 4(a), where WA accuracy on corrupted training data is lower than ENS, indicating reduced memorization of run-specific noise).

**Cost-efficient BoN sampling for LLM evaluation and output selection.** The BoN experiments (Section 5.1) demonstrate that WARM as a reward selector achieves up to 92.5% oracle win rate against random selection (Figure 7(a)) and that no other RM-based selection strategy beats WARM `M=6` (all methods have win rates below 50% against WARM `M=6` in Figures 7(c) and 7(d)). For practitioners using BoN sampling as a lightweight alignment method (generating `N` candidate outputs and selecting the best via the RM, without full RL fine-tuning), WARM provides a single-model drop-in replacement for the reward scorer that outperforms both individual RMs and prediction ensembles. The efficiency benefit is particularly relevant for BoN, where the RM must score `N` candidate outputs per prompt — with ENS, this means `M × N` RM forward passes per prompt; with WARM, it means `N` forward passes regardless of `M`. For large `N` (the paper tests `N=1000` on T5 generations) and large `M` (ENS with `M=6` or `M=10`), this is a substantial latency and cost reduction at deployment time, with no sacrifice in selection quality — in fact, with improved quality, since WARM `M=6` outperforms ENS `M=2` in BoN across all settings (Figure 6).

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions WARM against prediction ensembling (ENS) and establishes clear tradeoffs based on the experimental findings and theoretical analysis. The decision framework is:

**Prefer WARM over ENS when:**
- Preference labels are expected to contain noise, corruption, or inconsistencies (RLAIF-generated labels, crowd-sourced human annotations, multi-objective preferences with conflicting signals). The `p_j^2` mechanism provides structural robustness that ENS cannot match regardless of ensemble size. This is the paper's strongest comparative advantage. Evidence: Figures 4, 5, and 8(b) showing WA substantially outperforms ENS under 25% label corruption.
- Inference-time efficiency is a binding constraint — memory, latency, or per-query compute must be minimized. WARM produces a single model while ENS requires `M` parallel models. Evidence: Section 3.1 ("WARM is efficient, incurring no memory or computation costs, as it returns a single model"), and the BoN experiments where WARM `M=6` requires 1/6 the inference compute of a hypothetical ENS `M=6`.
- All available RMs share the same architecture and pre-training. WARM requires weight-space compatibility for averaging to be meaningful. Evidence: Remark 1 and the Baklava discussion confirming that shared pre-training is a prerequisite for LMC.

**Prefer ENS over WARM when:**
- Rewards must be combined from heterogeneous sources: different model architectures, different pre-training procedures, or different preference datasets. ENS averages scalar predictions and is agnostic to weight-space geometry. Evidence: Section 6 acknowledgment that "prediction ensembling can benefit from the diversity brought by combining RMs from various architectures and pre-trainings" — a capability WARM explicitly lacks.
- Uncertainty estimation is needed from the reward signal (e.g., using prediction disagreement across ensemble members to detect OOD inputs or to adaptively weight the reward). ENS provides per-input variance across RMs; WARM collapses this into a single point estimate. Evidence: Section 6 notes that ENS "can incorporate prediction disagreement into the reward to provide uncertainty estimation and limit model drift," though the paper also notes that Eisenstein et al. (2023) found simple averaging often performs comparably to more complex uncertainty-aware aggregation.

**Neutral / context-dependent:**
- When preference labels are clean and the primary challenge is distribution shift. The paper shows WARM and ENS perform similarly in the clean-label setting (Figures 3, 6(a,c), 8(a) — WARM `M=2` and ENS `M=2` often track closely), so the choice reduces to the efficiency-vs-flexibility tradeoff. If inference efficiency matters, use WARM; if cross-architecture diversity or uncertainty estimation matters, use ENS. Evidence: the first-order analysis (Observation 2, Figure 3) showing WA ≈ ENS under distribution shifts when labels are clean.
