Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (concise principles for reaching Staff engineer, drawn from his own experiences and popular Twitter thread):

**Impact is king**

The foundational principle is simple: focus on impact, defined as any measurable and objective outcome that benefits your company. The specific definition of impact will vary depending on the part of the business you work on, so a crucial early step is learning what your particular organization considers impactful. Promotions are not instantaneous rewards but a "byproduct of your elevated, sustained impact." The word "sustained" is key because promotions are a lagging indicator. You need to perform at the next level for a meaningful period of time first—often at least six months of consistent next-level performance before a promotion materializes.

**Leverage is how you multiply impact**

Having more impact with limited time requires leverage, which is what differentiates higher-level ICs from lower-level ones. The concept is captured by Archimedes’ principle: "Give me a lever long enough, and a place to stand, and I will move the earth." Software engineers increase their leverage through three primary vectors: people, writing, and code. People leverage comes from technical leadership, which means setting direction, reviewing designs and code, and growing others—learning to influence and sell your ideas allows you to multiply your impact through others. Writing gives leverage by influencing and helping people without your active, synchronous involvement. Not all code is created equal; high-leverage code solves problems that few others can or helps engineers move faster at scale.

High-level ICs can get their primary leverage from different sources based on their strengths. Tech leads coordinate and drive large cross-company initiatives, making people their primary source of leverage. Specialists write state-of-the-art code that pushes the industry forward, making code their primary source of leverage. You can focus on the type of leverage that best suits your strengths and still achieve excellent results.

**Your internal brand matters most**

You should obsess over the quality of your work because high-quality work is inherently more impactful in the long term and effectively markets itself. However, you cannot rely *only* on the quality of your work for recognition; you must actively share the results. "No one gets promoted unless their work is known." Embrace accountability and take projects under your own name—when the work goes well, your internal brand grows alongside it. The brand that matters most is your "internal brand": what people within your company think about your work and its value. When people hear "personal brand" their mind often goes to social media, but most of the top ICs he knows are not well-known outside of Meta—they are "legendary within the company though because people see the impressive work they are doing." Building this internal brand is a two-part process: doing great work and then letting others know about it.

**Help others help you**

Working with others is a necessity for doing anything of consequence, so being a good collaborator is non-negotiable. Being someone that others want to work with also makes it much easier to find mentors who will uplift you throughout your career. This principle extends to your relationship with your manager: be transparent, help them understand your goals, and get explicit clarity on what your gaps are to the next level. Soft skills are often underrated among software engineers, but since many engineers don't prioritize them, having strong communication and collaboration skills will help you stand out and lead.

**Put the work in**

Reading is faster than listening, so he advises fighting inefficient meetings and processes and protecting your focus time to do the work that truly matters. An early performance review taught him that *what* you work on is more important than *how hard* you work. He worked a tremendous number of hours but realized that even doubling them wouldn't have yielded the same impact as more senior engineers because his projects had a ceiling on how beneficial they could be. Still, you should work as hard as you can. Following your curiosity and doing work you genuinely enjoy is the key to making long hours sustainable—he worked around 60 hours a week but never felt he had a bad work-life balance because he loved what he was doing. And in a final, cheeky nod to Naval Ravikant's famous thread, he notes that when you finally get promoted, you'll realize the title wasn't what you were actually seeking in the first place.

---

Ryan’s entire framework is an exercise in professional physics—using **people, writing, and code leverage** as force multipliers on the raw **impact** you generate. His insistence on the **internal brand** reveals a calculated antidote to the modern obsession with public personas: the only audience that pays you is the one you work beside, and your reputation there is built by doing great work and then deliberately making sure it is known. The closing paradox—that promotions are a **lagging indicator** of a pursuit whose true reward isn’t the title itself—mirrors the discipline he preaches throughout: chase the work, not the optics, and the optics will eventually chase you back.
