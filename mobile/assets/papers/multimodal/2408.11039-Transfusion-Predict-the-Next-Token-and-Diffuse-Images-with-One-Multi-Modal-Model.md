# Transfusion: Predict the Next Token and Diffuse Images with One Multi-Modal Model

**ArXiv:** [2408.11039](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11039)

## 🎯 Pitch

A single transformer trained to both predict text tokens and diffuse image patches scales far better than quantizing images into discrete tokens—achieving 2× lower FID at matched compute and matching image-to-text performance at just 21.8% of the FLOPs—while generating images on par with dedicated diffusion models and text on par with language models.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Transfusion introduces a recipe for training a single unified multi-modal model that handles both discrete and continuous data by combining two distinct objectives — next-token prediction for text and diffusion for images — over shared transformer parameters. The authors pretrain models up to 7B parameters from scratch on a mixture of text and image data, demonstrating through controlled comparisons against Chameleon's quantization-based approach that Transfusion scales significantly better across all modality pairings: it achieves approximately 2× lower FID scores on text-to-image generation at matched FLOPs, matches Chameleon's image-to-text performance at 21.8% of the compute, and even reaches text perplexity parity at 50–60% of Chameleon's FLOPs. Scaling the recipe to 7B parameters and 2T multi-modal tokens produces a model that generates images on par with similar-scale diffusion models like DeepFloyd while simultaneously matching Llama 1 on text benchmarks, establishing that a single transformer can reap the benefits of both modalities with negligible parameter-sharing cost — though only on problems within the model's pretraining distribution, as the approach does not address fundamentally novel or out-of-distribution reasoning tasks.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Discrete and Continuous Modalities Live in Separate Worlds

The fundamental challenge this paper addresses is architectural: **there is no standard, principled way to train a single model that excels at both discrete data generation (like text) and continuous data generation (like images).** The two modalities are dominated by completely different modeling paradigms, each with its own training objectives, architectures, and decades of accumulated best practices. Language modeling — the task of predicting the next token in a sequence — has become the undisputed champion of text generation, powering models like GPT-4 [OpenAI et al., 2024] and Llama [Dubey et al., 2024]. Diffusion models [Ho et al., 2020, Rombach et al., 2022a], which learn to reverse a gradual noise-corruption process over continuous vectors, are the state of the art for generating images, video, and audio [Dai et al., 2023, Esser et al., 2024b, Bar-Tal et al., 2024].

The separation is not just academic — it has practical consequences. A model that can only generate text cannot produce an illustration to accompany a story. A model that can only generate images cannot write a caption explaining what it created. True multi-modal intelligence — the ability to perceive, reason about, and produce content across modalities — requires bridging this gap. But the gap is deep: language modeling operates over discrete tokens from a finite vocabulary using cross-entropy loss, while diffusion operates over continuous vectors using mean squared error on predicted noise. These objectives, data representations, and inference procedures are fundamentally different. The question is whether they can coexist in a single model without one modality's objective undermining the other.

This problem matters for several reasons that the paper makes clear (Section 1):

- **Unified understanding and generation**: Many real-world tasks involve both consuming and producing multiple modalities — answering questions about an image, generating an image from a description, editing an image based on text instructions, or producing interleaved text-and-image documents. A single model that handles all these tasks removes the need for pipeline systems that chain together modality-specific components, each introducing its own failure modes and integration complexity.

- **Parameter sharing and efficiency**: Separate models for text and images duplicate parameters that could potentially be shared. A transformer that processes both modalities can reuse its attention mechanisms, feed-forward layers, and learned representations across tasks. If the sharing is efficient — if the model doesn't degrade on text when trained on images and vice versa — this represents a substantial savings in total parameters and training compute.

- **Emergent cross-modal capabilities**: When a model is trained end-to-end on mixed-modal data, it may develop capabilities that neither modality-specific model would acquire alone. The paper's image editing experiment (Section 4.5) hints at this: a model pretrained on text-text, image-text, and text-image data can generalize to image-image generation (editing an image based on a text instruction) with only 8,000 fine-tuning examples.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper identifies three broad strategies that prior work has used to combine modalities, each with significant limitations:

#### Strategy 1: Grafting Pretrained Models Together

The most common approach in both image generation and vision-language understanding is to attach separate, independently pretrained models via adapter layers. State-of-the-art image generation models like DALL-E 2 [Ramesh et al., 2022], Imagen [Saharia et al., 2022], SDXL [Podell et al., 2023], and SD 3 [Esser et al., 2024b] use large pretrained text encoders (typically frozen) to encode prompts into latent vectors, which then condition a diffusion model that generates images. The text encoder and the image generator are trained separately, often on completely different datasets, and communicate only through a narrow bottleneck of conditioning vectors.

On the vision-language understanding side, models like Flamingo [Alayrac et al., 2022] and LLaVA [Liu et al., 2024] follow a similar pattern: take a pretrained language model, attach pretrained vision encoders through projection layers, and train only the adapters (or fine-tune select parts) on multi-modal data. For generation in both directions, GILL [Koh et al., 2024] grafts a diffusion model onto a language model, and DreamLLM [Dong et al., 2024] combines separate encoders and decoders for visual comprehension and generation.

**Where this falls short:** These are fundamentally pipeline architectures, not unified models. The text and image components have separate parameters, separate training procedures, and separate inductive biases. They cannot share learned representations across modalities during pretraining because they were never trained together. The text encoder in Imagen knows nothing about how its representations will be used for image generation — it was trained purely on text data. This fragmentation limits the potential for cross-modal learning and creates engineering complexity (multiple models to maintain, version, and deploy). Moreover, many of these models are asymmetric — they can consume one modality (e.g., text) and produce another (e.g., images), but cannot freely mix modalities or handle interleaved sequences.

#### Strategy 2: Quantizing Continuous Modalities into Discrete Tokens

An alternative that achieves true architectural unity is to force continuous data into the discrete paradigm of language models. The key idea: use a vector-quantized autoencoder (VQ-VAE) [Van Den Oord et al., 2017] to compress each image into a sequence of discrete tokens from a learned codebook, then train a standard autoregressive language model over the combined sequence of text tokens and image tokens. This approach was used by DALL-E [Ramesh et al., 2021], Parti [Yu et al., 2022], and CM3Leon [Yu et al., 2023]. Most recently, Chameleon [Chameleon Team, 2024] scaled this approach to 7B and 34B parameter models, demonstrating that early-fusion mixed-modal training is viable at scale.

**Where this falls short:** Quantization introduces an information bottleneck. A VQ-VAE must map every image patch to one of a finite set of codebook vectors (typically 8,192 or 16,384). This means that subtle visual details — smooth gradients, fine textures, precise colors — are lost during encoding, regardless of how good the VQ-VAE is. The paper explicitly measures the cost of this bottleneck through controlled comparisons: Chameleon models underperform Transfusion on image generation by a factor of 2× in FID and require approximately 5× more compute to match Transfusion's image captioning performance (Table 3, Figure 5).

Beyond the information loss, quantized training introduces optimization challenges. The Chameleon paper required architectural modifications to stabilize training — query-key normalization, post-normalization, denominator loss, and a lower learning rate — which the Transfusion authors show incur an efficiency cost (Table 4). Even with these modifications, quantized image tokens compete with text tokens for probability mass in the language modeling head's output distribution, potentially degrading text performance. Table 4 demonstrates this: compared to a pure text baseline, Chameleon loses 3 percentage points on the Llama 2 evaluation suite beyond what Transfusion loses, suggesting that forcing images into the discrete token paradigm harms text modeling.

#### Strategy 3: Using Diffusion as a Tool for Language Models

A third approach treats the diffusion model as an external tool that the language model can invoke, rather than integrating the two into a single model. Liu et al. [2023] and similar work let a language model generate text that triggers a separate image generation API. The language model never "sees" the image pixels — it only handles the text interface.

**Where this falls short:** This is not truly multi-modal modeling. The language model has no direct access to visual information, cannot learn from image data, and cannot develop internal representations that span modalities. It is a text model that happens to know how to format API calls. For tasks requiring deep visual understanding — describing the contents of an image in detail, answering compositional visual questions, or editing images based on fine-grained instructions — this approach fundamentally cannot leverage visual pretraining.

### The Gap: No Simple, End-to-End Unified Approach Matching State-of-the-Art in Both Modalities

The paper positions itself against this landscape by identifying a clear, previously unexplored idea: **what if we train a single model with both loss functions — language modeling and diffusion — applied to the appropriate modalities within a shared architecture?** The intuition is straightforward enough that the authors call it "simple, yet previously unexplored" (Section 6): text tokens get next-token prediction loss, image patches get diffusion loss, and both gradients flow through the same transformer parameters.

The conceptual breakthrough is recognizing that these two objectives need not be in conflict. Prior work either converted images to discrete tokens (forcing them into the language modeling objective) or kept architectures separate (avoiding the challenge of a shared model). Transfusion takes the third path: **keep the data in its native representation (discrete for text, continuous for images) and apply the objective best suited to each modality (classification for discrete, regression for continuous), but share the underlying neural network.**

Why was this not obvious before? The paper doesn't explicitly speculate, but several factors likely made this approach seem problematic:

1. **Different loss scales and dynamics**: Language modeling cross-entropy and diffusion MSE have different magnitudes, variances, and convergence properties. The paper addresses this with a balancing coefficient λ = 5 (Equation 4), chosen through preliminary experiments.

2. **Different attention patterns**: Text uses causal attention (each token attends only to previous tokens), while images typically use bidirectional attention (each patch attends to all other patches in the same image). The paper's "Transfusion attention" (Section 3, Figure 4) combines both: causal across the full sequence, bidirectional within each image. This is a simple but crucial design choice — Table 5 shows that removing bidirectional intra-image attention hurts FID from 20.3 to 61.3 when using linear patch encoding.

3. **Different inference procedures**: Language models sample token by token autoregressively; diffusion models denoise iteratively over many timesteps. The paper must design a decoding algorithm that switches between these modes when special boundary tokens (BOI/EOI) are encountered (Section 3, Inference).

4. **Representation mismatch**: Language models operate on discrete token embeddings; diffusion models operate on continuous latent vectors. The paper uses lightweight modality-specific encoding/decoding layers — either linear projections or U-Net blocks — to map between the shared transformer space and each modality's native representation, keeping the core transformer architecture unchanged.

### How Transfusion Positions Itself

The paper does not claim to invent new loss functions, new architectures, or new training techniques. Its contribution is integrative: **show that LM loss and diffusion loss can coexist in a single transformer without destructive interference, and that the resulting model scales better than quantization-based alternatives while matching or approaching specialized models in each modality.**

This positioning is reflected in the paper's experimental design:

**Against Chameleon (the quantized approach):** The paper runs rigorous controlled comparisons (same data, same VAE training, same model sizes, same FLOPs) to establish that continuous diffusion is superior to discrete tokenization for images, and that the continuous approach doesn't degrade text performance — in fact, it improves it (Table 3, Figure 5).

**Against modality-specific models:** The paper compares its 7B model to both text-only models (Llama 1, Llama 2) and image-only models (SDXL, DALL-E 2, DeepFloyd, SD 3) to establish that a single Transfusion model is competitive with specialized alternatives in each modality individually (Table 9). The claim is not that Transfusion beats the best text model or the best image model — it's that Transfusion performs on par with strong baselines in both, which no prior unified model had demonstrated.

**As a platform for future research:** The paper explicitly frames Transfusion as "a specific instantiation of a broader idea: combining a discrete distribution loss with a continuous distribution loss to optimize the same model" (Section 3, Training Objective). It mentions flow matching [Lipman et al., 2022] as a natural alternative to diffusion, and acknowledges that applying diffusion to discrete text generation [Li et al., 2022, Gat et al., 2024] is an active research area that could enable even tighter integration. The paper is presenting a recipe and a proof of concept, not a final solution.

### Confounding Factors the Paper Explicitly Controls

A key strength of the motivation is the care taken to isolate the effect of the core idea (continuous vs. discrete image representation) from confounding variables:

- **Same VAE training for both approaches:** The VAE (for Transfusion) and VQ-VAE (for Chameleon) are trained on identical data, compute, and architecture, differing only in the quantization layer and codebook loss. This ensures that any performance gap is due to the modeling approach, not the quality of the latent representation.

- **Same transformer architecture:** Both Transfusion and Chameleon use Llama-style transformer blocks with SwiGLU activations and RoPE positional encodings. Chameleon's additional stability modifications (QK normalization, etc.) are acknowledged and measured for their text performance cost (Table 4), but the core comparison controls for the language modeling aspect.

- **FLOPs-matched comparisons:** Rather than comparing model sizes, the paper compares at matched FLOPs (6ND), accounting for the fact that Transfusion uses fewer tokens per image (since images are represented continuously rather than as long sequences of discrete tokens). This prevents Transfusion from benefiting from shorter sequence lengths in the comparison.

- **Text performance degradation is measured and attributed:** Table 4 breaks down exactly how much text performance is lost at each step: the base Llama recipe, adding diffusion loss and image patches (Transfusion), the Chameleon stability modifications alone, and adding LM loss on quantized image tokens (full Chameleon). This decomposition reveals that quantized image tokens degrade text performance more than diffusion does, providing evidence for the paper's central claim that continuous representations are more compatible with text modeling.

### The Broader Significance

The paper's motivation connects to a larger question in machine learning: **should multi-modal models be homogeneous (all modalities converted to a single format) or heterogeneous (modalities processed in their native forms with specialized components)?** The quantization approach represents the homogeneous extreme — everything becomes a discrete token. The grafting approach represents the heterogeneous extreme — separate models with separate inductive biases. Transfusion represents a middle ground: **heterogeneous objectives and representations, but a homogeneous architecture and parameter space.**

This middle ground is appealing because it preserves the strengths of modality-specific modeling (diffusion for continuous data, autoregressive prediction for discrete data) while enabling parameter sharing and end-to-end training. If it works — and the paper's experiments suggest it does — it opens a path toward truly unified multi-modal models that don't force unnatural representations on any modality.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation (approachable technical breakdown)

Transfusion is a **training recipe** — not a new architecture, but a specific combination of existing components — that produces a single transformer model capable of ingesting and generating both text (discrete tokens) and images (continuous pixel data). The problem it solves is the deep incompatibility between the two dominant paradigms in generative AI: language models work with discrete tokens and cross-entropy loss, while diffusion models work with continuous vectors and mean-squared-error loss on noise. Transfusion's solution is to keep each modality in its native representation, apply the optimal loss for that modality (next-token prediction for text, diffusion for images), combine the loss values with a balancing coefficient, and route the gradients from both objectives through a single shared transformer — all while using a specially-designed attention mask that applies causal attention to the full interleaved sequence but bidirectional attention within each image.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The system has five major components, organized as a pipeline that converts raw data into a model capable of multi-modal generation:

1. **Data preprocessing pipeline** — converts raw text strings into sequences of integer token IDs using the Llama 2 tokenizer, and converts raw pixel images into sequences of continuous latent patch vectors using a pretrained VAE encoder (or VQ-VAE encoder for the Chameleon baseline). For mixed-modal examples, special `<BOI>` and `<EOI>` tokens bracket each image sequence, which is then spliced into the text sequence at the appropriate position.

2. **Modality-specific input encoders** — lightweight, non-shared components that map each modality's native representation into the shared $d$-dimensional vector space consumed by the transformer. For text, this is a standard embedding lookup table. For images, this is either a simple linear layer (with a timestep embedding added for diffusion) or a stack of U-Net down-sampling blocks that compress local $k \times k$ patches of latent pixel vectors into single transformer vectors.

3. **Shared transformer backbone** — a Llama-style decoder-only transformer (SwiGLU activations, RoPE positional encodings, pre-normalization) that processes the entire mixed-modal sequence. This component contains the vast majority of the model's parameters (>96% in the 7B configuration). It receives a sequence of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and produces a sequence of output vectors of the same dimensionality. The transformer itself does not know or care which vectors came from text versus images — it applies the same self-attention and feed-forward operations uniformly.

4. **Modality-specific output decoders** — lightweight, non-shared components that map the transformer's output vectors back to each modality's prediction space. For text, this is a linear projection to vocabulary size followed by softmax, producing a probability distribution over the next token. For images, this is either a linear layer or U-Net up-sampling blocks that expand each transformer output vector back into $k \times k$ latent patch vectors, which are then assembled into the full latent image and decoded by the frozen VAE decoder.

5. **Dual-objective training loop** — at each training step, the model receives a batch of mixed-modal sequences. Text tokens contribute a cross-entropy loss (`$L_\text{LM}$`) computed per token position. Image patches contribute a diffusion mean-squared-error loss (`$L_\text{DDPM}$`) computed per image (summed across its constituent patches). The two losses are added with a balancing coefficient `$\lambda = 5$` to form `$L_\text{Transfusion}$`, and gradients are computed through all parameters (shared transformer plus modality-specific encoders/decoders).

**Information flow at inference time:** A prompt (which may include text, images, or both) is encoded into a sequence of vectors → the shared transformer processes the entire sequence with Transfusion attention (causal across the sequence, bidirectional within each image) → when the model predicts a `<BOI>` token, the decoding algorithm switches to diffusion mode: pure Gaussian noise is appended to the sequence as image patch placeholders, the transformer denoises these patches over `$T$` timesteps (250 at inference, though the model was trained with 1,000), and at each timestep the predicted noise is used to overwrite the image patch vectors with a less-noisy version → when denoising completes, an `<EOI>` token is appended and decoding switches back to autoregressive text mode.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First, the data representation scheme** — how text and images are converted into sequences that can coexist within a single transformer's input. This is foundational because every subsequent component depends on this unified sequence format.

- **Second, the Transfusion attention mask** — the key architectural innovation that lets the transformer handle both causal (text) and bidirectional (image) dependencies within a single forward pass. This must come before the loss functions because the attention pattern determines which positions can influence which predictions.

- **Third, the dual training objective** — how language modeling loss and diffusion loss are computed on different parts of the same sequence, how they are combined, and why a balancing coefficient is necessary. This is the intellectual core of the paper.

- **Fourth, the modality-specific encoding and decoding layers** — the "glue" that maps between native modality representations and the shared transformer space, including the U-Net variant that compresses multiple latent pixels into single transformer vectors.

- **Fifth, the inference/decoding algorithm** — how the trained model generates mixed-modal outputs by switching between autoregressive sampling and iterative denoising based on special boundary tokens.

- **Sixth, the training configuration and optimization** — the concrete hyperparameters, data mixtures, and training recipes used to produce the models evaluated in Section 4.

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

This is primarily a **systems and methods paper** whose core idea is that a single transformer can be trained with two different loss functions on two different data types simultaneously, without destructive interference, provided the data is kept in its native representation and appropriate attention patterns are used.

---

#### Data Representation: Converting Modalities into a Unified Sequence

The fundamental challenge Transfusion must solve is representational: a transformer expects a sequence of vectors in `$\mathbb{R}^d$` as input, but the raw data consists of text strings (inherently discrete) and images (inherently continuous). The paper's solution is to convert each modality into a sequence of vectors using modality-specific preprocessing pipelines, then interleave them into a single sequence demarcated by special tokens.

**Text tokenization.** Each text string is tokenized into a sequence of discrete tokens from a fixed vocabulary using the Llama 2 tokenizer [Touvron et al., 2023b]. The specific vocabulary size is not stated in the paper, but Llama 2 uses a vocabulary of 32,000 tokens. Each token is represented as an integer index. At this stage, text is just a list of integers — no different from standard language model preprocessing. The tokenization is applied to captions, prompts, and any other text data before mixed-modal sequences are constructed.

**Image encoding via VAE.** Each raw image (256×256 pixels) is processed by a pretrained variational autoencoder (VAE) following Esser et al. [2021]. The VAE consists of a CNN encoder that compresses the image into a latent tensor and a CNN decoder that reconstructs the original image from the latent. The authors train an 86M-parameter VAE with a latent dimension of 8, meaning each spatial position in the latent grid is represented as an 8-dimensional continuous vector. The compression factor is 8×: a 256×256 pixel image becomes a 32×32 grid of 8-dimensional latent vectors. Each latent vector conceptually represents an 8×8 pixel patch in the original image, capturing its color, texture, and structural information in a compressed form.

The VAE training objective combines multiple losses:

$$L_\text{VAE} = L_1 + L_\text{LPIPS} + 0.5L_\text{GAN} + 0.2L_\text{ID} + 0.000001L_\text{KL}$$

where `$L_1$` is pixel-space L1 reconstruction loss, `$L_\text{LPIPS}$` is perceptual similarity loss based on deep features [Zhang et al., 2018], `$L_\text{GAN}$` is a patch-based discriminator loss, `$L_\text{ID}$` is a perceptual loss using Moco v2 internal features [Chen et al., 2020], and `$L_\text{KL}$` is the standard KL-divergence regularization term that encourages the encoder's output distribution to approximate a standard normal distribution. GAN training (adding the adversarial loss) is delayed until 50,000 steps to allow the VAE to achieve sufficient reconstruction quality first. The VAE is trained for 1M steps total.

**From latent grid to sequence of patches.** The 32×32×8 latent tensor is not fed directly to the transformer. Instead, it is converted into a flat sequence of "latent patches" — groups of spatially adjacent latent vectors that are processed together. The paper experiments with multiple patch sizes (Table 6):

- **No patching (1×1):** Each of the 1024 latent vectors becomes a separate element in the sequence. This is the finest granularity but produces the longest sequences.
- **2×2 patching:** Each 2×2 block of latent vectors (covering a 16×16 pixel region) is compressed into a single transformer vector. This produces 256 elements per image.
- **4×4 patching:** Each 4×4 block (32×32 pixels) → 64 elements per image.
- **8×8 patching:** Each 8×8 block (64×64 pixels) → 16 elements per image.

The compression from `$k \times k$` latent vectors to a single transformer vector is performed by the modality-specific encoder (either a linear projection or U-Net down blocks — see the dedicated section below). Importantly, the patches are sequenced in **left-to-right, top-to-bottom raster order**, preserving the 2D spatial structure as a 1D sequence that the transformer can process.

**Constructing mixed-modal sequences.** Given a caption-image pair, the paper constructs a single interleaved sequence as follows: the caption text tokens are placed first 80% of the time (image second), and the image is placed first 20% of the time. A special Beginning of Image (`<BOI>`) token is prepended to the image patch sequence, and an End of Image (`<EOI>`) token is appended. These special tokens are discrete tokens added to the vocabulary, similar to the separator tokens used in multi-turn dialogue. The result is a sequence like:

```
[text_token_1, text_token_2, ..., text_token_n, <BOI>, patch_vec_1, patch_vec_2, ..., patch_vec_m, <EOI>]
```

where `text_token_i` are integers, `patch_vec_j` are 8-dimensional continuous vectors, and `<BOI>`/`<EOI>` are integers.

**Critical detail: images are noised before patchification during training.** The paper specifies that noise `$\epsilon$` is added to the entire latent image `$x_0$` according to the diffusion forward process to produce `$x_t$` **before** the patching step. This means the patch vectors that enter the transformer are noisy versions of the latent representation, with the noise level determined by the sampled timestep `$t$`. The implications are: (1) the transformer must learn to denoise at the patch level, and (2) downstream tokens (e.g., captions that come after the image) condition on noisy images during training. The paper explores limiting this noise in Section 4.3.4.

**Why this representation scheme?** The key design choice is keeping images in continuous latent space rather than quantizing them. Alternatives would be: (1) quantize to discrete tokens (Chameleon's approach), which loses information but makes the sequence uniformly discrete; (2) train on raw pixels, which would be prohibitively expensive (the paper notes preliminary experiments showed Transfusion works with raw pixels but focuses on latent representations for efficiency); or (3) use a single vector to represent the entire image (like CLIP embeddings), which would lose spatial detail needed for generation. The chosen approach preserves continuous-valued spatial information while achieving an 8×8 compression in each spatial dimension through the VAE, making the sequence length manageable.

#### Transfusion Attention: Combining Causal and Bidirectional Dependencies

The transformer at the heart of Transfusion uses self-attention, which computes each output position as a weighted sum of all input positions based on learned similarity scores. Standard practice for language models is **causal masking**: position `$i$` can only attend to positions `$\leq i$`, preventing information leakage from future tokens during autoregressive training. Standard practice for image models (like ViT or DiT) is **bidirectional (unrestricted) attention**: every patch attends to every other patch, since images are not inherently sequential.

Transfusion must handle sequences containing both modalities, and applying purely causal attention would be suboptimal for images — a patch in the top-left corner should be able to attend to a patch in the bottom-right corner, since they are spatially related even though the bottom-right patch appears "later" in the left-to-right top-to-bottom sequence. The paper's solution is a hybrid attention mask that:

1. **Applies causal attention to the full sequence:** Position `$i$` can NEVER attend to position `$j$` if `$j > i$`. This preserves the autoregressive property needed for text generation during inference.

2. **Adds bidirectional attention within each individual image:** For any two positions `$i$` and `$j$` that both belong to the SAME image (i.e., they are both image patch vectors from the same `<BOI>...<EOI>` block), the causal restriction is lifted. Position `$i$` can attend to `$j$` even when `$j > i$`, and vice versa.

3. **Cross-image attention remains causal:** A patch in image #2 CANNOT attend to a patch in image #1 (since image #1 appears earlier in the sequence), consistent with causal ordering. A patch in image #1 can attend to a patch in image #2 only if image #2 appears earlier, which doesn't happen in the paper's setup since images are typically separated by text.

Figure 4 in the paper illustrates this: the attention mask has a block-triangular structure where blocks on the diagonal (corresponding to tokens within the same image) are fully dense (all-to-all attention), while off-diagonal blocks follow the standard causal pattern.

**Operational mechanics:** The attention mask is implemented as an additive bias matrix `$M$` of shape `[seq_len, seq_len]`, where `$M_{ij} = 0$` if position `$i$` is allowed to attend to position `$j$`, and `$M_{ij} = -\infty$` otherwise. Before the softmax in the attention computation, `$M$` is added to the attention logits, effectively zeroing out the attention weights for forbidden pairs.

**Why bidirectional intra-image attention matters:** Table 5 quantifies this. For the linear encoder variant at 0.76B parameters, FID on MS-COCO drops from 61.3 (causal-only) to 20.3 (with bidirectional intra-image attention) — a 3× improvement. The reason is intuitive: when a patch at position 15 in the image can only see patches 1–14, it has no information about what's below or to the right of it, crippling its ability to understand spatial context. Text doesn't suffer from this because language IS inherently sequential — future words genuinely shouldn't influence past words. But an image is a 2D structure flattened into a 1D sequence purely for computational convenience; flattening should not become an information bottleneck.

For the U-Net encoder variant, the FID gap is much smaller (16.8 vs. 16.7) because the U-Net down blocks themselves contain internal attention and convolutions that provide bidirectional context within the local window being compressed, partially compensating for the lack of explicit bidirectional attention in the transformer.

#### The Dual Training Objective: Language Modeling + Diffusion

The intellectual core of Transfusion is the simultaneous application of two fundamentally different loss functions to different parts of the same sequence, with gradients from both flowing through shared parameters. This section explains each loss individually and then their combination.

**Language modeling loss (`$L_\text{LM}$`):** Applied exclusively to text token positions. For each position `$i$` in the sequence where the ground-truth token is text (not `<BOI>`, `<EOI>`, or an image patch), the model computes a probability distribution over the vocabulary and evaluates the negative log-likelihood of the correct token:

$$L_\text{LM} = \mathbb{E}_{y_i}\left[-\log P_\theta(y_i \mid y_{<i})\right]$$

where `$y_i$` is the ground-truth text token at position `$i$`, `$y_{<i}$` are all preceding tokens in the sequence (which may include both text tokens and image patches), and `$P_\theta$` is the model's predicted distribution parameterized by all model parameters `$\theta$`.

**What it computes:** the standard next-token prediction cross-entropy loss used in every autoregressive language model. For each text position, the model's output vector (from the transformer's final layer at that position) is projected to vocabulary size via a linear layer and softmax, producing `$P_\theta(\cdot \mid y_{<i})$`. The negative log of the probability assigned to the correct token `$y_i$` is the loss for that position. The expectation is over the empirical distribution of training data (averaged across all text tokens in the batch).

**Why this form:** Cross-entropy is the maximum-likelihood objective for categorical distributions, and it's the standard loss that has been empirically validated across virtually all large language models. The paper does not modify or innovate on the LM loss — it uses it exactly as is, because the goal is to show that adding diffusion does not break language modeling.

**Important detail:** When the input at position `$i+1$` is a `<BOI>` token, no LM loss is computed at position `$i$` (since the model is predicting the `<BOI>` token, which is deterministic and uninteresting). The paper states: "When the input is a BOI token, we do not compute any loss."

**Diffusion loss (`$L_\text{DDPM}$`):** Applied exclusively to image patch positions. The paper follows the DDPM formulation of Ho et al. [2020], which trains a model to predict the noise that was added to a clean image. The key difference from standard diffusion is that the noise prediction is computed **per image, not per patch in isolation**, and the loss aggregates across all patches belonging to the same image.

The forward diffusion process (which creates training targets) works as follows: given a clean latent image `$x_0$` (the 32×32×8 tensor from the VAE), a timestep `$t \in \{1, ..., T\}$` is sampled uniformly (the paper uses `$T=1000$` during training). Gaussian noise `$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$` is sampled and mixed with `$x_0$` to produce the noised latent:

$$x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon$$

where `$\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} (1 - \beta_s)$` with `$\beta_s$` defined by the cosine noise schedule [Nichol and Dhariwal, 2021]. The cosine schedule sets `$\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \approx \cos(\frac{t}{T} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2})$`, meaning that at `$t=0$`, `$\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_0} \approx 1$` (clean image), and at `$t=T$`, `$\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_T} \approx 0$` (pure noise).

**What the noise schedule `$\bar{\alpha}_t$` controls:** At each timestep `$t$`, `$\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}$` determines what fraction of the original signal remains, and `$\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}$` determines the standard deviation of the added noise. The cosine schedule was chosen over the original linear schedule because prior work [Nichol and Dhariwal, 2021] showed it produces better samples by adding noise more slowly in the early timesteps (preserving more structure) and more rapidly in the later timesteps.

The noised latent `$x_t$` is then patchified using the modality-specific encoder (e.g., linear projection of 2×2 latent pixel blocks), producing a sequence of patch vectors that replace the image's positions in the mixed-modal sequence. The transformer processes the entire sequence and produces output vectors at each position. For positions corresponding to image patches, the model's output is passed through the modality-specific decoder to predict the noise. The diffusion loss is:

$$L_\text{DDPM} = \mathbb{E}_{x_0, t, \epsilon}\left[\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c)\|^2\right]$$

where `$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$` is the actual noise added during the forward process, `$\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c)$` is the model's noise prediction, `$t$` is the diffusion timestep (provided to the model via a timestep embedding added to each patch vector), and `$c$` is the conditioning context (the text tokens and/or other images appearing before the current image in the sequence).

**What it computes:** the mean squared error between the true noise that was added to the clean latent and the model's estimate of that noise. The expectation is over: (1) the distribution of clean images `$x_0$` in the training data, (2) the uniform distribution of timesteps `$t$`, and (3) the Gaussian distribution of noise `$\epsilon$`. In practice, this is implemented as: for each image in the batch, sample `$t$` and `$\epsilon$`, construct `$x_t$`, run the transformer, extract the output vectors at image patch positions, project them through the decoder, and compute the squared L2 distance to `$\epsilon$`. The MSE is averaged across all patches belonging to the image, producing a single scalar loss per image.

**Why MSE for noise prediction:** The DDPM formulation derives from a variational bound on the data likelihood. Under the Gaussian forward process, the optimal reverse process also produces Gaussian transitions, and predicting the noise `$\epsilon$` is equivalent to predicting the mean of the reverse Gaussian. MSE is the correct loss because the noise `$\epsilon$` is Gaussian-distributed, and MSE corresponds to maximizing the likelihood under a Gaussian assumption. Using cross-entropy (the LM loss) would be inappropriate here because the target is a continuous vector, not a discrete category.

**Why predict noise rather than `$x_0$` directly:** Ho et al. [2020] found empirically that predicting the noise `$\epsilon$` works better than predicting the clean image `$x_0$` directly. Intuitively, `$x_0$` is a complex, multi-modal target (a natural image), while `$\epsilon$` is simple Gaussian noise — the model only needs to identify the noise component in `$x_t$`, which is an easier learning problem. The clean image can be recovered from the noise prediction: `$\hat{x}_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}} (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon_\theta)$`.

**Combining the losses with a balancing coefficient (`$\lambda$`):**

$$L_\text{Transfusion} = L_\text{LM} + \lambda \cdot L_\text{DDPM}$$

where `$\lambda = 5$` is a hyperparameter chosen through preliminary experiments.

**What this formula does:** it simply adds the LM loss (averaged over all text tokens in the batch) and the diffusion loss (averaged over all images in the batch) after multiplying the diffusion loss by 5. Both contributions are scalars, and their sum is the total loss that gets backpropagated through all parameters — the shared transformer, the text embedding/decoding layers, and the image encoding/decoding layers.

**Why `$\lambda = 5$`?** The paper states this was chosen through "preliminary experiments" and does not provide an ablation. The motivation is likely that LM loss and diffusion loss have different typical magnitudes — cross-entropy on a vocabulary of 32K tends to be in the range of 2–10 nats, while MSE on normalized latent vectors might be much smaller or larger depending on the VAE's output scale. The coefficient `$\lambda = 5$` balances them so neither objective dominates the gradient updates. The paper explicitly leaves tuning of `$\lambda$` to future work, suggesting the specific value is not essential to the core claim.

**What happens to the shared parameters:** The transformer receives gradients from both objectives. For a parameter in the attention layers, its update is the sum of: (1) gradients from text positions where the model must improve next-token prediction, and (2) gradients from image patch positions where the model must improve noise prediction. There is no explicit mechanism to prevent interference — the experiments simply demonstrate empirically that interference is minimal, as evidenced by Transfusion matching Llama's text performance (Table 9) while simultaneously matching diffusion models' image quality.

**An important training detail: no LM loss on `<BOI>` input positions.** The paper states that when the input token at a position is `<BOI>`, no loss is computed at the PREVIOUS position. This makes sense because the model's task at position `$i$` is to predict token `$i+1$`. When `$i+1$` is `<BOI>`, the prediction is trivial (the model should learn to predict `<BOI>` deterministically when an image should be generated), so omitting the loss prevents the model from wasting capacity on an easy task and avoids biasing the text loss with a large number of low-variance `<BOI>` predictions.

#### Modality-Specific Encoders and Decoders: Bridging Native Representations and the Transformer Space

The transformer at the core of Transfusion operates on sequences of vectors in `$\mathbb{R}^{d_\text{model}}$` (where `$d_\text{model}$` ranges from 768 for the 0.16B model to 4096 for the 7B model — see Table 2). But raw data comes in incompatible formats: text tokens are integers, and latent image patches are 8-dimensional vectors arranged in a 2D grid. The modality-specific encoders and decoders are lightweight, non-shared neural network components that bridge this representational gap. They are "modality-specific" because text and images have fundamentally different structures, but "lightweight" because the paper wants the transformer to do the heavy lifting of cross-modal reasoning.

**Text encoder (input embedding):** A standard embedding lookup table `$E \in \mathbb{R}^{|V| \times d_\text{model}}$` where `$|V|$` is the vocabulary size (32K for Llama 2) and `$d_\text{model}$` is the transformer dimension. Each integer token ID is converted to its corresponding `$d_\text{model}$`-dimensional vector. The `<BOI>` and `<EOI>` tokens are included in this vocabulary and have learned embeddings. The embedding parameters are NOT shared with the image encoder — text and images are projected into the same `$\mathbb{R}^{d_\text{model}}$` space through completely separate pathways, but the transformer treats the resulting vectors identically.

**Text decoder (output projection):** A linear layer `$W_\text{out} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\text{model} \times |V|}$` followed by softmax, mapping the transformer's output vector at position `$i$` to a probability distribution over the vocabulary for predicting token `$i+1$`. This is standard for language models. Typically the input embedding and output projection share weights (weight tying), but the paper does not specify whether this is used.

**Image encoder: two variants.** The paper experiments with two architectures for converting a `$k \times k$` block of `$d_\text{latent}$`-dimensional latent vectors into a single `$d_\text{model}$`-dimensional transformer input vector. Both variants first add a **timestep embedding** to each latent patch vector before encoding. The timestep `$t$` is encoded using a sinusoidal positional encoding and projected to `$d_\text{latent}$` dimensions, then added element-wise to the patch vector. This is how the diffusion timestep information enters the transformer — without it, the model would have no way to know how much noise is present in the current image.

**Variant 1 — Linear encoder/decoder:** The simplest approach. A linear layer `$W_\text{enc} \in \mathbb{R}^{(k^2 \cdot d_\text{latent}) \times d_\text{model}}$` flattens the `$k^2$` latent vectors (each of dimension `$d_\text{latent}=8$`) into a single vector of length `$k^2 \cdot 8$` and projects to `$d_\text{model}$` dimensions. For the decoder, another linear layer `$W_\text{dec} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\text{model} \times (k^2 \cdot d_\text{latent})}$` maps the transformer output back to `$k^2$` patch vectors. The number of parameters is small: `$k^2 \cdot 8 \cdot d_\text{model} + d_\text{model} \cdot k^2 \cdot 8$`, which for `$k=2$` and `$d_\text{model}=4096$` is `$2^2 \cdot 8 \cdot 4096 \cdot 2 \approx 262K$` parameters — less than 0.3% of the 7B model's total.

**Variant 2 — U-Net encoder/decoder:** Replaces the linear layers with convolutional down-sampling (encoder) and up-sampling (decoder) blocks inspired by the U-Net architecture common in diffusion models [Nichol and Dhariwal, 2021, Saharia et al., 2022]. The U-Net down blocks apply a sequence of convolutions, residual connections, and potentially attention to compress the spatial information within each `$k \times k$` window into a single vector, while the U-Net up blocks do the reverse. Critically, the authors replace the U-Net's standard AdaLayerNorm (adaptive layer normalization conditioned on timestep) with regular layer normalization in their implementation. The U-Net layers add approximately 0.27B parameters across all model sizes — which is substantial for small models (doubling the 0.16B model's parameters) but only a 3.8% increase for the 7B model, almost identical to the token embedding parameters.

**Why U-Net blocks help:** Table 7 shows that U-Net encoding consistently outperforms linear encoding across all model sizes, and the benefit persists even when controlling for the parameter increase (the 7B model with linear encoding has 3.8% fewer total parameters than the U-Net version but underperforms it on image tasks). The authors attribute this to "inductive biases" — the U-Net's convolutions provide local spatial processing that makes the subsequent transformer's job easier. Rather than forcing the transformer to learn from scratch how to interpret 2D image structure, the U-Net preprocesses patches with operations designed for visual data. This is analogous to how CNNs were found to be better than MLPs for early vision tasks — the local connectivity and translation equivariance are useful priors.

**Why the parameter overhead is acceptable:** For large models (7B+), the U-Net parameters are a rounding error (3.8%) compared to the transformer parameters. The small-model results (Table 7) show that even when U-Net layers double the parameter count at 0.16B, the performance gains more than compensate — the U-Net 0.37B model (combined ~0.63B) achieves better FID than the linear 7B model (18.1 vs. 18.6), suggesting the architectural benefit is real and not just a capacity effect.

#### Inference/Decoding Algorithm: Switching Between Autoregressive and Diffusion Modes

A trained Transfusion model must be able to generate mixed-modal sequences — text interspersed with images — at test time. The paper's decoding algorithm reflects the dual nature of the training: it has two modes that are toggled by the presence of special boundary tokens.

**Text mode (autoregressive).** When the model is in text mode, it generates one token at a time using standard autoregressive sampling. The procedure:

1. The current sequence (which may include previously generated text tokens and generated images) is fed through the transformer, producing logits at the last position.
2. The logits are converted to a probability distribution via softmax.
3. A token is sampled from this distribution. The paper uses **greedy decoding** (selecting the highest-probability token) for text generation in all experiments, except for the Llama 2 evaluation suite where ranked classification is used (evaluating the probability of each answer choice and selecting the highest).
4. The sampled token is appended to the sequence, and the process repeats.

**Triggering image generation.** When the sampled token is `<BOI>`, the decoding algorithm recognizes that an image should be generated next. The algorithm:

1. Determines the desired image size (in the paper's experiments, always 32×32 latent pixels, corresponding to 256×256 output pixels after VAE decoding).
2. Appends `$n$` pure Gaussian noise vectors to the sequence, where `$n$` is the number of patch positions for the chosen patch size. For 2×2 patching, `$n = 256$`; for 8×8 patching, `$n = 16$`. These noise vectors represent `$x_T$` — the starting point of the reverse diffusion process.
3. Switches to diffusion mode.

**Diffusion mode (iterative denoising).** In diffusion mode, the model performs the reverse diffusion process over `$T_\text{inf}$` timesteps (the paper uses `$T_\text{inf} = 250$` at inference, though the model was trained with `$T_\text{train} = 1000$`). At each step `$t$` (descending from `$T_\text{inf}$` to 1):

1. The full sequence — text tokens, any previously generated images, and the current noisy image patches — is fed through the transformer. The noisy image patches have the timestep `$t$` embedded and added to them.
2. The transformer produces output vectors at the image patch positions. These are passed through the modality-specific decoder to produce the noise prediction `$\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c)$`.
3. The predicted noise is used to compute `$x_{t-1}$` using the DDPM reverse process update rule (which scales the predicted noise according to the noise schedule and subtracts it from `$x_t$`).
4. The image patch positions in the sequence are **overwritten** with the newly computed `$x_{t-1}$` values. The model conditions on the LATEST denoised version of the image — it cannot attend to previous timesteps of the same image. This is a design choice that mirrors standard diffusion inference, where only the current state is maintained.
5. After the final timestep (`$t=1$`), the denoised latent patches are assembled into the full latent image, decoded by the frozen VAE decoder, and the resulting pixel image is the model's output for that image.
6. An `<EOI>` token is appended to the sequence, and the algorithm switches back to text mode.

**Classifier-free guidance (CFG).** During image generation, the paper uses classifier-free guidance [Ho and Salimans, 2022], a technique that sharpens the generated images by contrasting the model's conditional and unconditional predictions:

$$\epsilon_\text{CFG} = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c) + \gamma \cdot (\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c) - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \varnothing))$$

where `$\gamma$` is the guidance scale, `$c$` is the conditioning context (the text and/or images preceding the generated image in the sequence), and `$\varnothing$` represents the unconditional case (typically implemented by replacing the conditioning context with a null token or empty sequence). The intuition: the term `$(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c) - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \varnothing))$` represents the direction in noise space that the conditioning pushes the prediction, and `$\gamma$` amplifies this push, making the generated image more strongly aligned with the conditioning at the cost of some diversity.

The paper sweeps `$\gamma$` values: `$\gamma = 5$` is used in the controlled comparison with Chameleon (following Chameleon's practice), `$\gamma = 3$` is used in ablation experiments (the authors note "This value is suboptimal for Transfusion"), and in the large-scale experiment (Section 4.4), they "follow the standard practice of tuning the coefficient for each benchmark."

**Why CFG is necessary:** Without CFG, diffusion models tend to produce blurry or generic images because the model learns to average over all possible images consistent with a (potentially ambiguous) conditioning. CFG sharpens the output by pushing it toward the high-density regions of the conditional distribution. The cost: each denoising step now requires TWO forward passes through the transformer — one with conditioning and one without — doubling the inference compute for image generation.

**Why 250 inference steps when trained on 1000:** The paper uses the DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models) sampling procedure, which allows skipping timesteps during inference. Training on 1000 timesteps gives the model exposure to noise levels at fine granularity, while inference at 250 steps is a standard speed-accuracy tradeoff. The paper does not ablate this choice.

**Handling multiple images in sequence:** The decoding algorithm can generate any interleaved pattern of text and images. After generating an image (completing the diffusion loop and appending `<EOI>`), the model returns to text mode and continues autoregressively. If another `<BOI>` is sampled, another image is generated. This enables multi-image outputs or images interspersed with explanatory text.

#### Training Configuration and Optimization

The paper specifies a comprehensive set of hyperparameters and training details that are essential for reproducibility and understanding the experimental scale.

**Model architecture (Table 2):** Five model sizes are trained, following the Llama architecture [Touvron et al., 2023a] with SwiGLU activation functions and RoPE positional encodings:

| Parameters | Layers | Embedding Dim (`$d_\text{model}$`) | Attention Heads |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.16B | 16 | 768 | 12 |
| 0.37B | 24 | 1024 | 16 |
| 0.76B | 24 | 1536 | 24 |
| 1.4B | 24 | 2048 | 16 |
| 7B | 32 | 4096 | 32 |

These configurations are taken directly from the Llama family of models and are not optimized for Transfusion specifically. The authors note that "while we use the transformer architecture in this work, Transfusion could potentially work with other architectures too, despite its name" (Section 3, Model Architecture footnote).

**Optimizer:** AdamW with `$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`, `$\epsilon = 10^{-8}$`. Learning rate `$3 \times 10^{-4}$`, warmed up linearly for 4000 steps and decaying to `$1.5 \times 10^{-5}$` using a cosine scheduler. Weight decay of 0.1. Gradient clipping by global norm with a threshold of 1.0.

**Batch and sequence configuration:** For controlled experiments (Sections 4.1–4.3), sequences of 4096 tokens in batches of 2M tokens (approximately 488 sequences per batch at max length, though some sequences will be shorter). Training runs for 250,000 steps, totaling 0.5T tokens processed. For the large-scale experiment (Section 4.4), batch size increases to 4M tokens and training extends to 500,000 steps, totaling 2T tokens.

**Data mixture for controlled experiments:** 50% text tokens (from the Llama 2 corpus, containing 2T tokens across diverse domains) and 50% image patches (from 380M licensed Shutterstock images with captions). Each image is center-cropped and resized to 256×256 pixels. Caption-image pairs are randomly ordered: caption first 80% of the time, image first 20% of the time. The 1:1 token ratio means that at lower patch compression (1024 patches per image), fewer total images are seen during training, while at higher compression (16 patches per image), more images are seen.

**Data mixture for large-scale experiment (Section 4.4):** Scales to 2T total tokens — 1T text tokens and approximately 3.5B caption-image pairs (at 256 patches per image, this is another ~1T patches). The image dataset is expanded beyond Shutterstock to include 220M publicly available images (prefiltered to exclude images containing people), with 80M Shutterstock images containing people upsampled for balance. Conceptual 12M (CC12M) is added, reaching a total mixture of 692M unique image-caption pairs per epoch. In the last 1% of the training schedule, the proportion of high-aesthetic-quality images is upweighted to improve final image quality.

**Initialization:** All model parameters are randomly initialized. No pretrained weights are used — this is training from scratch. This is important because it demonstrates that end-to-end unified training is viable without bootstrapping from pretrained unimodal models.

**Latent image representation details:** The VAE is an 86M-parameter CNN following Esser et al. [2021], trained for 1M steps with latent dimension 8. An image of 256×256×3 (RGB) is compressed to 32×32×8. For the VQ-VAE used in Chameleon baselines, the same architecture is used with one modification: a vector quantization layer is inserted after projecting encoder outputs to 8 dimensions, using a codebook of 16,384 token types, and the KL-divergence loss is replaced with the standard codebook commitment loss with `$\beta = 0.25$` and loss weight 1.0. The VQ-VAE training loss is:

$$L_\text{VQ-VAE} = L_1 + L_\text{LPIPS} + 0.5L_\text{GAN} + 0.2L_\text{ID} + L_\text{codebook}$$

Both VAEs are trained on identical data, compute budget, and architecture outside of the quantization layer, ensuring fair comparison.

**FLOP accounting:** The paper uses the approximation `$6ND$` for total training FLOPs, where `$N$` is parameter count and `$D$` is total tokens processed. This standard formula accounts for both the forward pass (approximately `$2ND$` FLOPs) and the backward pass (approximately `$4ND$` FLOPs per gradient computation). Since Transfusion represents images with fewer sequence elements than Chameleon (continuous patches vs. discrete tokens), the actual wall-clock time per training step would favor Transfusion. The paper explicitly removes this confounder by "using the theoretical FLOP calculation" for fair comparisons (Section 4.2).

#### Putting It All Together: The Training Step

To make the flow concrete, here is what happens in a single training step for a batch containing one example — a caption-image pair where the caption comes first:

1. **Preprocessing:** The caption is tokenized into `$[\text{"A"}, \text{"cute"}, \text{"cat"}]$` (3 token IDs). The image is encoded by the VAE into a 32×32×8 latent, patchified into 256 patches of 2×2 latent pixels each, and a `<BOI>` token is prepended and `<EOI>` appended. The full sequence is: `$[\text{"A"}, \text{"cute"}, \text{"cat"}, \text{<BOI>}, \text{patch}_1, ..., \text{patch}_{256}, \text{<EOI>}]$`.

2. **Diffusion noising:** A timestep `$t$` is sampled uniformly from `$\{1, ..., 1000\}$`. Gaussian noise `$\epsilon$` is sampled. The clean latent image is noised to `$x_t$` using Equation 2. The resulting noisy latent is patchified — this is the actual data that will occupy the `$\text{patch}_1, ..., \text{patch}_{256}$` positions.

3. **Encoding:** Text tokens are embedded via the text embedding table. Noisy image patches have timestep embeddings added and are projected through the U-Net down blocks (or linear layer). The result is a sequence of `$1 + 3 + 1 + 256 + 1 = 262$` vectors in `$\mathbb{R}^{d_\text{model}}$` (including the `<BOI>` and `<EOI>` tokens, which have learned embeddings).

4. **Transformer forward pass:** The sequence passes through all transformer layers with the Transfusion attention mask. The `<BOI>` token and all 256 patches within the image can attend to each other bidirectionally (they form a single dense block in the attention mask). Text tokens before `<BOI>` can attend to each other causally and can also attend to the image patches (since images appear later in sequence). Image patches can attend to earlier text tokens but not to `<EOI>` (which appears after them).

5. **Loss computation:** At position 0 (input "A"), the model predicts "cute" — LM loss is computed. At position 1 ("cute" → "cat") — LM loss. At position 2 ("cat" → `<BOI>`) — no loss (see the paper's rule). At positions 3–258 (the 256 image patches), no tokens are being predicted; instead, the model's output vectors at these positions are projected through the image decoder to predict the noise `$\epsilon$`. The MSE between predicted and true noise, averaged over all 256 patches, is the diffusion loss `$L_\text{DDPM}$` for this image. At position 258 (`<EOI>`), the model would normally predict whatever comes next, but the sequence ends here.

6. **Gradient computation:** `$L = L_\text{LM from text positions} + 5 \cdot L_\text{DDPM from image}$`. Gradients flow through the shared transformer parameters (updated by both losses), the text embedding/decoding parameters (updated only by LM loss), and the image U-Net parameters (updated only by diffusion loss). The `<BOI>` and `<EOI>` embedding parameters are updated by gradients from positions where they are predicted or where they attend to other positions.

7. **Parameter update:** AdamW applies the gradients with weight decay and gradient clipping.

**Why this works without destructive interference:** The paper's results suggest that the LM and diffusion losses are largely complementary rather than conflicting. Text positions primarily need the transformer to develop good language representations; image positions primarily need it to develop good visual representations and denoising capabilities. Since both tasks benefit from general-purpose features (attention to relevant context, compositional pattern recognition, etc.), the shared transformer can satisfy both simultaneously. The balancing coefficient `$\lambda = 5$` prevents either loss from dominating the gradient updates, and the modality-specific encoders/decoders give each modality a small amount of dedicated capacity to handle its unique requirements. The end result, as Table 4 shows, is that adding diffusion to a language model costs only 2 percentage points on the Llama evaluation suite (53.7% → 51.7%), while adding quantized image tokens costs nearly 5 points (53.7% → 48.9%).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Separate Objectives for Separate Modalities Over Shared Parameters — A Viable Third Path

The deepest conceptual move in this paper is rejecting a false dichotomy that has quietly structured multi-modal model research: either convert all modalities to a single format (typically discrete tokens) so a single loss function can be applied uniformly, OR keep modalities in separate architectures with separate parameters and training procedures, connecting them only through narrow interfaces. Transfusion demonstrates a third option: **keep each modality in its native continuous or discrete representation, apply the loss function best suited to that modality (diffusion for images, next-token prediction for text), and route both gradients through a single shared transformer.**

Prior to this work, the dominant assumption — evident in the architecture choices of DALL-E [Ramesh et al., 2021], Parti [Yu et al., 2022], CM3Leon [Yu et al., 2023], and Chameleon [Chameleon Team, 2024] — was that a unified model required a unified representation space, which for transformers meant discrete tokens. The quantization bottleneck was accepted as a necessary cost of architectural unity. The alternative — grafting pretrained models together, as in Imagen [Saharia et al., 2022], Flamingo [Alayrac et al., 2022], or GILL [Koh et al., 2024] — accepted architectural fragmentation as the cost of keeping modality-appropriate objectives.

What makes Transfusion's contribution significant beyond a simple "why not both?" observation is the empirical demonstration that **these objectives do not destructively interfere**. Table 4 shows the decomposition: adding diffusion loss and image patches to a Llama 2 training run costs only 2 percentage points on the Llama evaluation suite (53.7% → 51.7%), while adding quantized image tokens with LM loss costs nearly 5 points (53.7% → 48.9%). The diffusion objective, counterintuitively, is MORE compatible with text modeling than the discrete token approach, not less. This is a fundamental empirical finding — it could have gone the other way (MSE gradients from image patches might have destabilized the cross-entropy optimization of text), but it didn't.

This insight reframes the problem. The question is no longer "how do we force continuous data into a discrete mold?" or "how do we coordinate separate models?" but rather **"what is the minimal set of modality-specific components needed to allow a shared transformer to learn from heterogeneous objectives?"** The answer turns out to be quite minimal: modality-specific input/output projection layers (which can be as simple as a single linear layer) and a hybrid attention mask. Everything else — the entire transformer backbone, the optimization process, the inference procedure — can be shared.

I classify this as a **fundamental reframing** rather than an incremental improvement. It opens a design space that was not previously on the menu: what other pairs of loss functions can coexist in a shared architecture? Can flow matching loss replace diffusion? Can diffusion be applied to discrete text generation [Li et al., 2022, Gat et al., 2024], collapsing the distinction further? The paper explicitly gestures at this broader space in Section 3 (Training Objective), framing Transfusion as "a specific instantiation of a broader idea." The experiments validating this idea — particularly the scaling law comparisons in Figure 5 showing Transfusion outperforming Chameleon across every modality pairing — provide the evidence that the third path is not just viable but preferable.

---

### Innovation 2: Difficulty-Agnostic Scaling Laws Demonstrate That Continuous Representations Are Strictly More Compute-Efficient Than Quantized Ones

The paper's controlled scaling law experiments (Section 4.2, Figure 5, Table 3) produce a clean, monotonic result: **across all model sizes, all compute budgets, and all modality pairings, Transfusion outperforms Chameleon's quantization approach, and the gap does not close with scale.** This is not obvious a priori. One could imagine that quantization's information loss matters mainly for small models, and that large models learn to compensate for the VQ-VAE's discretization artifacts — the scaling trends in Figure 5 would then converge. They don't. The lines are close to parallel across six orders of magnitude of FLOPs, with Transfusion maintaining a consistent advantage.

What makes this a distinct innovation from #1 is the **diagnostic nature of the comparison**. By controlling for literally everything except the representation (same data, same VAE training, same transformer architecture, same FLOP counts, same inference procedures), the paper isolates quantization as the causal factor in the performance gap. This is a stronger claim than "Transfusion works well" — it is "quantization, as a strategy for multi-modal modeling, is Pareto-dominated by continuous diffusion when the downstream model is a transformer." The evidence for this claim goes beyond image metrics. Table 3 shows that Transfusion even achieves better TEXT perplexity than Chameleon, with a parity FLOP ratio of 0.489 on C4 — meaning Transfusion matches Chameleon's text performance using less than half the compute. This implies that quantized image tokens don't just hurt image generation (which might be expected) but actively interfere with text modeling, likely because they consume probability mass in the shared output softmax and force the model to spend capacity on a discretization task that doesn't generalize.

The significance of this finding extends beyond the Transfusion vs. Chameleon comparison. It provides empirical grounding for a general principle: **when building multi-modal generative models, preserving the native representation of continuous data (with an appropriate continuous loss function) is more parameter- and compute-efficient than forcing it into a discrete token vocabulary.** This principle, if it generalizes, has implications for other continuous modalities like audio (where discrete tokenization via tools like EnCodec is currently standard) and video.

I classify this as a **diagnostic finding with broad implications**, supported by the most rigorous controlled experiments in the paper. The parity FLOP ratios in Table 3 — 0.489 for text, 0.029 for image generation, 0.218 for image captioning — quantify the cost of quantization in a way that gives future practitioners concrete guidance. The 0.029 ratio for FID is particularly striking: Transfusion achieves in 2.9% of the FLOPs what Chameleon achieves on image generation. If replicated at larger scales and on other modalities, this finding would shift the default approach for unified multi-modal models away from tokenization.

---

### Innovation 3: Transfusion Attention — Bidirectional Within Images, Causal Across the Sequence — Is a Simple Architectural Pattern That Generalizes Beyond This Paper

The hybrid attention mask described in Section 3 and Figure 4 is technically simple — it's just an additive bias matrix with block-diagonal dense regions — but conceptually significant because it resolves a tension that any mixed-modal transformer must face. The tension: causal attention is correct for autoregressive text generation, but applying it to 2D images flattened into 1D sequences creates an artificial information bottleneck where patches cannot attend to spatially adjacent regions that happen to appear later in raster order. Bidirectional attention is correct for images, but applying it globally breaks the autoregressive property needed for text generation.

The prior approaches to this tension were unsatisfying. Chameleon applied full causal attention to everything (including quantized image tokens), accepting the suboptimal image modeling. Vision-language models like Flamingo and LLaVA side-stepped the issue by processing images in a separate encoder with full bidirectional attention, then feeding a single pooled representation to the language model — losing spatial granularity and preventing the image from attending to text during encoding. Diffusion transformers for image generation use full bidirectional attention but don't handle text generation at all.

Transfusion attention elegantly splits the difference: **every element in the sequence respects causal ordering globally, but within each image, the ordering is ignored.** This preserves the autoregressive training efficiency (causal masking allows computing all text losses in a single forward pass) while giving images the all-to-all attention they need. The ablation in Table 5 quantifies the importance: removing bidirectional intra-image attention (making everything causal) tanks FID from 20.3 to 61.3 when using linear encoding — a 3× degradation.

What makes this an innovation rather than an implementation detail is that it establishes a **design pattern** for how to handle heterogeneous attention requirements in a shared transformer. The pattern generalizes: any sequence containing segments with different dependency structures (e.g., text + video frames where frames within a clip are bidirectional, text + audio where spectrogram time steps might be bidirectional locally but respect causal ordering globally) can use the same block-diagonal masking approach. The paper doesn't explore this generalization, but the pattern is clear and reusable.

I classify this as an **architectural innovation with practical significance**. It's not as fundamental as #1 (the block-diagonal mask is a straightforward extension of standard attention) but it addresses a real problem that any mixed-modal autoregressive model must solve, and the ablation provides clear evidence that it matters. The fact that it works with both linear and U-Net encoding (Table 5) suggests it's a robust solution, not brittle to the choice of image processor.

---

### Innovation 4: Image Editing via Minimal Fine-Tuning — Evidence That Mixed-Objective Pretraining Produces Transferable Cross-Modal Abilities

Section 4.5 presents a small but conceptually important experiment: fine-tuning the 7B Transfusion model on only 8,000 image editing examples (input image + edit instruction → output image) enables the model to perform image-to-image generation, a modality pairing (image-text → image) that was not explicitly present during pretraining. The pretraining data only contained text-text, image-text, and text-image examples. The model had never seen an input-output pair where both were images.

This is significant because it suggests that **training with heterogeneous objectives on mixed-modal data produces representations that generalize compositionally to novel modality combinations.** The model learned something more abstract than "text before image means generate an image" or "image before text means generate a caption" — it learned a general notion of conditioning that transfers across modality boundaries. The edit instruction in Figure 6 ("Remove the cupcake on the plate") requires the model to understand the input image visually, parse the text instruction, identify the referenced object, and modify the image accordingly while preserving everything else. None of these sub-capabilities was explicitly trained in this combination, yet 8,000 examples suffice to unlock them.

The connection to prior work is instructive. LIMA [Zhou et al., 2024] showed that large language models can be fine-tuned on very few examples if the pretraining is sufficiently broad. Emu Edit [Sheynin et al., 2024] demonstrated image editing capabilities but with a specialized architecture and training procedure. Transfusion's result suggests that the combination of LM loss and diffusion loss during pretraining creates a model that is, in some sense, "waiting" for new modality pairings to be demonstrated — the building blocks (visual understanding from image-to-text training, visual generation from text-to-image training, instruction following from text-to-text training) are already in place and can be rapidly recombined.

I classify this as a **tantalizing preliminary finding** rather than a fully-established innovation. The experiment is limited (8K examples, manual examination of random outputs, no quantitative evaluation of editing quality), and the fine-tuning dataset comes from an existing benchmark (EmuEdit) rather than being independently constructed. But the conceptual implication — that mixed-objective pretraining promotes compositional generalization across modalities — is important and under-explored. If this finding holds up under rigorous evaluation, it would strengthen the case for Transfusion-style training beyond the efficiency arguments in innovations #1 and #2, suggesting that heterogeneous objectives create representations that are fundamentally more flexible than those from homogeneous (all-discrete or separately-trained) approaches.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses a mixture of text and image data. For text, it draws from the Llama 2 corpus [Touvron et al., 2023b], which contains 2T tokens across diverse domains. For images, the primary source is a collection of 380M licensed Shutterstock images with captions; each image is center-cropped and resized to 256×256 pixels. In the large-scale experiment (Section 4.4), this is augmented with 220M publicly available images (prefiltered to exclude people), with 80M Shutterstock images containing people upsampled for balance, plus Conceptual 12M (CC12M) [Changpinyo et al., 2021], reaching 692M unique image-caption pairs per epoch. For evaluation, the paper uses standard benchmarks listed in Table 1: Wikipedia (20M held-out tokens) and C4 [Raffel et al., 2019] for text-to-text perplexity; the Llama 2 evaluation suite (HellaSwag, PIQA, SIQA, WinoGrande, ARC-e, ARC-c, BoolQ) [Touvron et al., 2023b] for text understanding; MS-COCO 30k randomly selected prompts from the validation set [Lin et al., 2014] for text-to-image generation; the Karpathy test split of MS-COCO for image-to-text captioning (CIDEr) [Vedantam et al., 2015]; and GenEval [Ghosh et al., 2023] for fine-grained text-to-image alignment in the large-scale comparison.

- **Base model(s).** All Transfusion models use the Llama-style transformer architecture [Touvron et al., 2023a] with SwiGLU activations [Shazeer, 2020] and RoPE positional encodings [Su et al., 2024]. Five model sizes are trained from scratch, ranging from 0.16B to 7B parameters, with configurations specified in Table 2 (number of layers, embedding dimension, attention heads taken directly from the Llama family). The models are randomly initialized — no pretrained weights from unimodal models are used. For the baseline comparison, the paper trains Chameleon-style models [Chameleon Team, 2024] at the same sizes using quantized image tokens, with the same Llama backbone augmented with query-key normalization, post-normalization, and denominator loss to manage training instability. The VAE for Transfusion and VQ-VAE for Chameleon are both 86M-parameter CNNs trained on identical data, compute, and architecture, differing only in the quantization layer and codebook loss.

- **Metrics.** The evaluation suite (Table 1) measures performance across four modality pairings. For text-to-text: perplexity on held-out Wikipedia and C4 tokens (lower is better), and average 0-shot accuracy across 7 commonsense reasoning tasks in the Llama 2 evaluation suite (higher is better). For text-to-image: Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) [Heusel et al., 2017] to measure photorealism on 30k MS-COCO prompts (lower is better), and CLIP score [Radford et al., 2021] to measure prompt-image alignment on the same set (higher is better). For image-to-text: CIDEr score [Vedantam et al., 2015] on the MS-COCO Karpathy test split (higher is better). For the large-scale comparison (Section 4.4): GenEval score [Ghosh et al., 2023], which measures the model's ability to accurately depict objects, attributes, relationships, and counts specified in prompts (higher is better).

- **Baselines.** The primary baseline is **Chameleon** [Chameleon Team, 2024], a quantization-based approach that discretizes images via VQ-VAE and trains a standard autoregressive language model over the combined text-and-image token sequence. This is the "prominent open-science method for training a single mixed-modal model that can generate both text and images" at the time of writing. For text-only reference, the paper compares against **Llama 1** [Touvron et al., 2023a] and **Llama 2** [Touvron et al., 2023b] at 7B parameters. For image-only reference, the large-scale experiment (Table 9) compares against **Imagen** [Saharia et al., 2022], **Parti** [Yu et al., 2022], **SD 1.5** and **SD 2.1** [Rombach et al., 2022b], **DALL-E 2** [Ramesh et al., 2022], **SDXL** [Podell et al., 2023], **DeepFloyd** [Stability AI, 2024], and **SD 3** [Esser et al., 2024b]. For most of these image-only baselines, the paper reports results from the original publications; the baselines are not retrained or re-evaluated under controlled conditions.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For the controlled scaling law experiments (Section 4.2), compute is measured by total training FLOPs, approximated as `6ND` where `N` is parameter count and `D` is total tokens/patches processed during training. This accounts for both forward (`~2ND`) and backward (`~4ND`) passes. The paper explicitly notes that Transfusion uses fewer sequence elements per image than Chameleon (continuous patches vs. discrete tokens), which would give Transfusion a wall-clock advantage due to the quadratic cost of self-attention. The comparison uses "theoretical FLOP calculation" to remove this confounder and isolate the effect of the modeling approach. For fair comparison, all models within a size class use identical transformer configurations, and the VAEs for both approaches are trained on identical compute budgets. Inference cost is not used as a comparison metric; the paper only controls for training FLOPs.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The scaling law experiments regress linear trendlines on log-metric over log-FLOPs curves and estimate parity FLOP ratios (the ratio of Transfusion FLOPs to Chameleon FLOPs needed to achieve the same performance level). For the controlled comparison, the paper notes that smaller Chameleon models "perform poorly on image generation and understanding tasks, leading to outlier results that do not correlate with the emerging scaling law of larger Chameleon models." It therefore applies minimum performance thresholds below which data points are excluded: FID ≤100, CLIP ≥17, CIDEr ≥4. The exact number of excluded points is not specified. There is no explicit cross-validation, statistical significance testing, or confidence interval reporting anywhere in the experimental evaluation — all comparisons rely on single-point estimates and the consistency of trends across model sizes.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Controlled Scaling Law Comparison: Transfusion vs. Chameleon (Section 4.2, Figure 5, Table 3)

The central empirical claim of the paper is that Transfusion scales more efficiently than the quantization-based Chameleon approach across all modality pairings. Figure 5 presents six log-log plots — one per benchmark — showing performance (perplexity, accuracy, FID, CLIP score, or CIDEr) against total training FLOPs for both Transfusion and Chameleon models at multiple sizes. Each data point represents a model trained to completion; the lines are linear regressions over the included points.

**Text-to-text (C4 and Wikipedia perplexity, Llama 2 evaluation suite accuracy).** Transfusion consistently achieves lower perplexity and higher accuracy than Chameleon at matched FLOPs. At the largest scale (7B parameters, 0.5T tokens), Transfusion achieves C4 perplexity of 7.72 vs. Chameleon's 8.41; Wikipedia perplexity of 4.28 vs. 4.69; and Llama suite accuracy of 61.5% vs. 59.1% (Table 3). The parity FLOP ratios — the fraction of Transfusion FLOPs needed to match Chameleon's largest-model performance — are 0.489 for C4, 0.526 for Wikipedia, and 0.600 for the Llama suite. This means Transfusion reaches Chameleon 7B's text performance using roughly half to 60% of the training compute. The scaling trend lines (Figure 5) are close to parallel, suggesting the gap does not close with additional scale.

**Image-to-text (MS-COCO CIDEr).** The advantage is larger. Transfusion 7B achieves a CIDEr score of 27.2 vs. Chameleon 7B's 18.0, with a parity FLOP ratio of 0.218 — Transfusion matches Chameleon's captioning performance using less than a quarter of the compute.

**Text-to-image (MS-COCO FID and CLIP).** The gap is most dramatic on image generation. Transfusion 7B achieves FID of 16.8 and CLIP score of 25.5, while Chameleon 7B scores 29.6 and 24.3 respectively. The parity FLOP ratio for FID is 0.029 — Transfusion achieves the same FID as Chameleon 7B using approximately 2.9% of the FLOPs, or equivalently, Transfusion achieves roughly 2× lower FID at matched FLOPs. The CLIP parity FLOP ratio is 0.319.

**Scaling behavior of smaller models.** Looking at Figure 5, the Chameleon trendlines for image generation metrics (FID, CLIP, CIDEr) have gaps or missing points at the low-FLOP end due to the minimum performance thresholds. This indicates that small Chameleon models completely fail to learn meaningful image generation or understanding, while small Transfusion models produce usable outputs. For example, at FLOPs around 1e20, Transfusion achieves FID below 64 while Chameleon's corresponding models are below the FID ≤100 threshold and excluded from the regression. This is qualitative evidence that continuous representations make more efficient use of limited capacity — the model doesn't waste parameters and training steps learning to overcome quantization artifacts.

**Interpretation of parity FLOP ratios.** The ratios in Table 3 quantify the compute multiplier needed for Chameleon to match Transfusion: Chameleon requires roughly 2× the FLOPs for text (1/0.489 ≈ 2.04), 4.6× for image captioning (1/0.218 ≈ 4.59), and 34× for image generation (1/0.029 ≈ 34.5). The asymmetry — image generation benefits far more from continuous representations than image understanding does — is consistent with the information bottleneck hypothesis: generation requires reconstructing fine visual details from the latent representation, which quantization directly impairs, while understanding (CIDEr) can operate on coarser semantic information that survives quantization better.

#### Text Performance Decomposition: Why Transfusion Outperforms Chameleon Even on Text (Table 4)

Table 4 decomposes the text performance degradation of the 0.76B Transfusion and Chameleon models relative to the original Llama 2 recipe, which achieves C4 perplexity 10.1, Wikipedia perplexity 5.8, and Llama suite accuracy 53.7% when trained on 1M text tokens per batch. The decomposition reveals:

- **Starting point:** Llama 2 with text-only training.

- **Transfusion:** Adding diffusion loss and 1M image patches to the batch (keeping text at 1M tokens) increases C4 perplexity by 0.3 (10.1 → 10.4), Wikipedia perplexity by 0.2 (5.8 → 6.0), and decreases Llama suite accuracy by 2.0 points (53.7 → 51.7). This is the "parameter sharing cost" — the transformer must now serve two objectives.

- **Chameleon stability modifications alone:** Applying Chameleon's architectural changes (QK normalization, post-normalization, denominator loss, lower learning rate) to a text-only model increases C4 perplexity by 0.9 (10.1 → 11.0), Wikipedia by 0.5 (5.8 → 6.3), and decreases accuracy by 1.8 points (53.7 → 51.9). These modifications, introduced to stabilize training with quantized image tokens, impose a non-trivial cost on text performance even in their absence.

- **Full Chameleon:** Adding LM loss on quantized image tokens (1M image tokens alongside 1M text tokens) further increases C4 perplexity by 0.8 (11.0 → 11.8), Wikipedia by 0.5 (6.3 → 6.8), and decreases accuracy by 3.0 additional points (51.9 → 48.9). The total degradation from Llama 2 to Chameleon is +1.7 C4 perplexity and -4.8 accuracy points.

The key comparison: Transfusion's diffusion loss degrades text performance by 2.0 accuracy points, while Chameleon's approach degrades it by 4.8 points — more than double the penalty. This directly supports the paper's claim that "training on quantized image tokens degrades text performance more than diffusion" and provides evidence for the broader assertion that continuous representations are more compatible with text modeling than discrete tokenization. The authors hypothesize that this stems from "competition between text and image tokens in the output distribution" or that "diffusion is more efficient at image generation and requires fewer parameters, allowing Transfusion models to use more capacity than Chameleon to model text" (Section 4.2).

A limitation: Table 4 only reports results at 0.76B scale. Whether the decomposition holds identically at 7B is not shown, though the scaling trends in Figure 5 suggest the relative advantage persists.

#### Architecture Ablation: Attention Masking (Section 4.3.1, Table 5)

Table 5 compares causal-only attention (the standard autoregressive mask applied uniformly) against Transfusion attention (causal globally + bidirectional within each image) for 0.76B models with both linear and U-Net encoding, using 2×2 latent pixel patches. The headline finding:

- **With linear encoding:** Enabling bidirectional intra-image attention reduces FID from 61.3 to 20.3 — a 3× improvement. CIDEr improves from 12.7 to 16.0. Text metrics (C4 perplexity, Wikipedia perplexity, Llama accuracy) are essentially unchanged (10.4 vs. 10.4, 6.0 vs. 6.0, 51.4 vs. 51.7). This demonstrates that intra-image bidirectional attention is critical for image generation quality when using simple linear patch encoders, but irrelevant for text modeling — the bidirectional attention is contained within image segments and does not affect text tokens' information flow.

- **With U-Net encoding:** The FID gap nearly disappears (16.8 with causal vs. 16.7 with bidirectional). CIDEr still benefits (23.3 → 25.4). The U-Net down/up blocks contain internal bidirectional attention and convolutions that operate within the local patch window being compressed, partially compensating for the lack of explicit bidirectional attention in the transformer. However, U-Net + bidirectional still achieves the best overall performance across all metrics except Llama accuracy (51.9 vs. 52.0 for U-Net + causal, a negligible difference).

The non-obvious insight: bidirectional intra-image attention matters most when the image encoder is weak (linear). When the encoder already injects spatial inductive biases (U-Net), the transformer's attention pattern is less critical. This suggests a complementarity — if you're willing to pay the parameter cost of U-Net layers, you can simplify the attention mask; if you need maximum parameter efficiency (linear layers are essentially free), the hybrid attention mask is essential.

#### Architecture Ablation: Patch Size with Linear vs. U-Net Encoding (Section 4.3.2, Table 6)

Table 6 explores the trade-off between patch size (how many latent pixels are compressed into a single transformer vector) and performance for 0.76B models. The number of sequence elements per image ranges from 1024 (1×1 patches, no compression) to 16 (8×8 patches, maximum compression). Larger patches mean shorter sequences, allowing more images per batch and dramatically reducing inference compute (up to 64× fewer patches to denoise), but potentially losing spatial detail.

**With linear encoding:** Performance degrades monotonically as patch size increases. FID rises from 21.0 (1×1) to 20.3 (2×2) to 25.6 (4×4) to 43.5 (8×8). CIDEr drops from 12.0 to 16.0 (a local improvement at 2×2) then falls to 14.3 and 11.3. Text metrics also worsen: C4 perplexity rises from 10.3 to 11.7, Llama accuracy drops from 52.2 to 47.7. The linear encoder lacks the capacity to meaningfully compress spatial information at large patch sizes — forcing 64 latent pixels (8×8 grid) into a single transformer vector via a linear projection loses too much detail.

**With U-Net encoding:** The pattern reverses for image metrics. FID improves from 16.7 (2×2) to 16.0 (4×4) to 16.1 (8×8) — essentially flat, and all better than the 21.0 achieved by linear 1×1. CIDEr improves dramatically: 25.4 (2×2) → 29.9 (4×4) → 29.5 (8×8). However, text metrics still degrade: C4 perplexity rises from 10.3 to 11.4, Llama accuracy drops from 51.9 to 49.2. The authors hypothesize that U-Net models benefit from "the greater amount of total images (and diffusion noise) seen during training" — because each image occupies fewer sequence positions, the same total token budget exposes the model to more unique images, providing richer visual training signal. The text degradation is attributed to the model needing "to exert more resources (i.e., parameters) to learn how to process images with fewer patches."

The striking result is the 8×8 U-Net configuration: with only 16 patches per image, it achieves FID of 16.1 and CIDEr of 29.5 — competitive with or exceeding the best 2×2 linear configuration (FID 20.3, CIDEr 16.0) while requiring 16× fewer patches. This is the basis for the paper's claim that U-Net encoding can "decrease the serving costs by up to 64×" (the 8×8 configuration uses 16 patches vs. 1024 for 1×1). The 64× figure compares the extremes (1024 vs. 16), which is technically correct but somewhat misleading since the paper never evaluates a 1×1 U-Net variant for a direct comparison.

#### Architecture Ablation: Linear vs. U-Net Encoding Across Model Scales (Section 4.3.3, Table 7)

Table 7 addresses a critical confound: the U-Net layers add substantial parameters at small model sizes (doubling the 0.16B model, adding 71.3% to the 0.37B model), which could explain their performance advantage independent of architectural inductive biases. By scaling up to 7B, where the U-Net adds only 3.8% parameters (0.27B out of 7.3B total), the comparison isolates the architectural benefit.

The key pattern: **the U-Net advantage persists but shrinks as the transformer grows**. For FID at 0.16B: linear 37.6 vs. U-Net 18.8 (U-Net is 2× better). At 7B: linear 18.6 vs. U-Net 16.0 (U-Net is 1.16× better). The gap narrows because the larger transformer can partially compensate for the lack of spatial inductive biases — it has enough capacity to learn from scratch how to process image patches that a smaller transformer cannot. However, the gap does not close entirely. For CIDEr, the U-Net 1.4B model (combined 1.67B parameters) achieves 28.1, surpassing the linear 7B model's 27.2 — meaning the architectural benefit can substitute for roughly 5× more transformer parameters on this metric.

The paper interprets this as evidence that "there are indeed inductive bias benefits to U-Net encoding and decoding of images beyond the mere addition of parameters." This is a reasonable conclusion given the 7B results, but the effect size is modest (1.6 FID points, 2.3 CLIP points, 6.5 CIDEr points) relative to the dramatic gains at small scales. For practical deployment at 7B+, the choice between linear and U-Net encoding may be a matter of whether a 3.8% parameter overhead is worth a ~10% relative improvement on image metrics.

#### Image Noising Ablation: Limiting Noise When Images Precede Captions (Section 4.3.4, Table 8)

Table 8 evaluates a targeted intervention: in the 20% of training examples where the image appears before its caption (image → text ordering), the image is normally noised according to the standard diffusion process, meaning the caption must condition on a noisy image. The paper tests limiting the maximum diffusion timestep to `t = 500` (half the noise schedule) in these cases, so the caption conditions on a less-noisy (more informative) image.

The results for both 0.76B and 7B U-Net models: noise limiting significantly improves CIDEr (image captioning). At 0.76B: 25.4 → 29.4 (+4.0). At 7B: 33.7 → 35.2 (+1.5). Other metrics change by less than 1%: C4 perplexity (10.3 → 10.3 at 0.76B, 7.8 → 7.7 at 7B), FID (16.7 → 16.5, 16.0 → 15.7), and CLIP (25.4 → 25.4, 26.5 → 26.3) are essentially unchanged. This is an elegant demonstration that selectively reducing noise in specific sequence positions — those where the image provides conditioning for text — improves text generation quality without harming image generation. It suggests that the model can benefit from cleaner conditioning signals when generating captions, but that the standard full-noise training for image generation (which dominates because of the 80/20 split and the fact that images are always noised when they are the generation target) is sufficient.

The mechanism: during diffusion training, downstream tokens (captions) see noised versions of upstream images. At high noise levels (t near 1000), the image is essentially pure noise and provides no useful conditioning information. The caption model must learn to ignore the image in these cases, which could interfere with learning the image-text alignment needed for good captioning. Limiting the noise to t ≤ 500 ensures the image always retains some structure, making the conditioning task easier.

#### Large-Scale Comparison with Image Generation Literature (Section 4.4, Table 9)

The 7B Transfusion model trained on 2T tokens (1T text, ~1T image patches from 3.5B image-caption pairs, using U-Net encoding with 2×2 patches) achieves the following results compared to published models:

**Text performance:** Llama 2 evaluation suite average accuracy of 66.1%, matching Llama 1 (66.1%) and slightly below Llama 2 (66.3%), both of which were trained on similar text data distributions. The text tokens seen by Transfusion (1T) are comparable to Llama 1's 1.4T, suggesting text performance is not significantly degraded by the multi-modal training.

**Image generation (GenEval):** Transfusion scores 0.63, outperforming DALL-E 2 (0.52), SDXL (0.55), and DeepFloyd (0.61), while falling short of SD 3 (0.68). The paper notes that SD 3 used synthetic caption data via backtranslation, which alone boosted GenEval by 6.5% absolute at smaller scale (0.433 → 0.498); Transfusion only used natural captions, making direct comparison difficult.

**Image generation (MS-COCO FID):** Transfusion achieves FID of 6.78, competitive with Imagen (7.27) and DeepFloyd (6.66), and substantially better than DALL-E 2 (10.39) and Chameleon (26.74). However, Parti reports FID of 7.23 but with the significant caveat that it "samples 16 images for every prompt and then reranks with an auxiliary scoring model," which Transfusion does not do.

**Key context for interpreting Table 9:** The comparison is not controlled — these models differ in architecture, training data, compute budget, and inference procedures (CFG coefficients, number of diffusion steps, reranking, synthetic captions). The table's purpose is not to claim Transfusion is "better" than any specific model, but rather to demonstrate that a single unified model trained from scratch can achieve competitive performance with specialized models in both text and image generation. This is a "proof of viability" rather than a "state of the art" claim.

The comparison also highlights a limitation: Transfusion's 7B parameters with 0.27B U-Net layers (7.3B total) is trained for 2T tokens. The text-only Llama models were trained for 1.4T (Llama 1) and 2.0T (Llama 2) text-only tokens. The image-only models like SDXL use 2.6B + 0.8B (frozen text encoder) parameters but are specialized architectures. Transfusion achieves parity with both classes of models simultaneously, which no prior approach had demonstrated at this scale.

#### Image Editing via Fine-Tuning (Section 4.5, Figure 6, Appendix C)

Fine-tuning the 7B Transfusion model on 8,000 publicly available image editing examples (input image + edit instruction → output image, from the EmuEdit test set [Sheynin et al., 2024]) produces qualitative evidence of emergent image-to-image capabilities. Figure 6 shows four examples: removing a cupcake from a plate, changing a tomato to a green olive, writing "Zebra" in Arial bold on an image, and converting an image to cartoon style. The paper states that "manual examination of random examples... reveals that our fine-tuned Transfusion model performs image edits as instructed."

The quantitative results are not provided — there are no metrics, no comparisons to baselines, no evaluation of how often edits succeed vs. fail, and no assessment of how well the fine-tuned model preserves unedited regions. The experiment is qualitative and exploratory, intended to "assess how well the model can generalize to image-to-image generation, a scenario not covered during pretraining" rather than to make a strong claim about editing capabilities.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **λ coefficient (balancing language modeling and diffusion losses):** The paper uses λ = 5 in all experiments, chosen "following preliminary experiments" (Section 4.1). No ablation over λ values is reported. The paper states it leaves "further tuning of λ to future work." This is a significant gap — λ is the only mechanism preventing one loss from dominating the other, and the sensitivity of results to this choice is unknown.

- **CFG guidance scale:** The paper sweeps different classifier-free guidance coefficients across experimental settings but does not systematically ablate. In the controlled comparison (Section 4.2), CFG = 5 is used, following Chameleon's practice, but the authors note "This value is suboptimal for Transfusion." In ablations (Section 4.3), CFG = 3 is used. In the large-scale experiment (Section 4.4), CFG is tuned per benchmark. No table or figure shows performance as a function of CFG scale, so the sensitivity of image quality metrics to this hyperparameter is not characterized.

- **Number of diffusion timesteps at inference:** All experiments use 250 timesteps at inference (Section 4.1) while training with 1000 timesteps. No ablation over inference steps is reported; the speed-quality tradeoff for Transfusion specifically is not explored.

- **VAE latent dimension and training details:** The VAE uses latent dimension 8 and is trained for 1M steps. The paper does not ablate VAE capacity (e.g., latent dimension 4 or 16) or VAE training duration. The dependence of downstream Transfusion performance on VAE quality is not characterized — if the VAE is undertrained or overfitted, it's unclear how this would affect the Transfusion vs. Chameleon comparison.

- **Caption-first vs. image-first ordering (80/20 split):** The 80/20 split is stated but not ablated. The effect of this ratio on image captioning (CIDEr) vs. image generation (FID) is not explored. The noise limiting experiment (Section 4.3.4) partially addresses the image-first case but does not vary the frequency of image-first examples.

- **Data mixture ratio (50/50 text/image tokens):** All controlled experiments use a 1:1 token ratio. The effect of varying this ratio — e.g., 90/10 or 10/90 — on the trade-off between text and image performance is not studied. This is a practical concern: if deployment primarily requires text generation with occasional image generation, a text-heavy mixture might be more efficient.

- **Comparison with Chameleon using identical CFG and inference settings:** The controlled comparison (Section 4.2) uses CFG = 5 for both Transfusion and Chameleon, following Chameleon's practice, but the authors acknowledge this is suboptimal for Transfusion. If Transfusion performs better with CFG = 3 and Chameleon with CFG = 5, the controlled comparison may underestimate Transfusion's advantage. No results are reported showing both models at their individually optimal CFG settings.

- **No combination of PRM search or any test-time compute scaling:** This is not an ablation per se, but a notable absence. The paper explores no test-time compute strategies beyond standard autoregressive sampling for text and standard 250-step DDIM with CFG for images. Whether Transfusion benefits from best-of-N sampling, beam search, or iterative refinement at inference time is not investigated. This is particularly relevant given that Chameleon, as an autoregressive model over discrete tokens, could potentially benefit from temperature tuning and nucleus sampling in ways that Transfusion's diffusion-based image generation cannot directly replicate.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: Transfusion scales significantly better than quantizing images and training a language model over discrete image tokens.

**The experiments demonstrate this claim convincingly, within the studied regime.** The controlled comparison in Figure 5 and Table 3 establishes that across all six benchmarks (text-to-text, text-to-image, image-to-text) and across model sizes from ~0.1B to 7B, Transfusion outperforms Chameleon at matched FLOPs. The scaling trend lines are approximately parallel, suggesting the gap is not closing with scale. The parity FLOP ratios (0.029–0.600) quantify the advantage precisely.

**However, several caveats apply:**

(1) **Only one quantization-based baseline is tested.** Chameleon uses VQ-VAE with codebook size 16,384 and specific architectural modifications (QK normalization, post-normalization, denominator loss). The paper does not compare against other quantization approaches — different codebook sizes, different VQ-VAE architectures, or methods like FSQ (finite scalar quantization) that have been proposed as improvements over VQ-VAE. It's possible that a different quantization recipe would narrow the gap.

(2) **The Chameleon baseline may not be optimally tuned.** The paper notes that the Chameleon recipe required stability modifications to train, and Table 4 shows these modifications alone degrade text performance. The paper does not experiment with alternative stabilization strategies that might have lower text penalties. The choice of codebook size (16,384) and β (0.25) are taken from prior work without ablation.

(3) **The advantage is measured at training time, not inference time.** Transfusion requires iterative denoising (250 steps) during image generation, which is substantially more expensive than Chameleon's autoregressive image token generation. The paper does not account for inference cost differences in the comparison. If the goal is generating images at test time, Chameleon may be faster (one forward pass per token, ~1024 tokens per image) while Transfusion requires 250 forward passes. The paper's claim is about training efficiency and model quality, not inference speed, but this distinction is important for practitioners.

(4) **The 34× FLOP advantage for FID (parity ratio 0.029) is partly an artifact of Chameleon's poor absolute performance.** Chameleon 7B achieves FID of 29.6 on MS-COCO, which is substantially worse than even small diffusion models. If a better quantization approach achieved FID of, say, 20 at the same FLOPs, the parity FLOP ratio would be less dramatic.

#### Claim 2: Transfusion does not degrade text performance relative to text-only training; the parameter-sharing cost is negligible.

**The experiments partially support this claim, but the evidence is incomplete.** Table 4 shows that adding diffusion to a 0.76B Llama 2 training run costs 2.0 accuracy points on the Llama suite (53.7% → 51.7%). Table 9 shows that a 7B Transfusion trained for 2T tokens achieves 66.1% accuracy, matching Llama 1 (66.1%) but slightly below Llama 2 (66.3%).

**Weaknesses:**

(1) **The "negligible cost" claim is only demonstrated at 7B scale with 2T tokens.** The 0.76B model in Table 4 loses 2 points, which is a non-trivial degradation. The scaling trend (the gap between Llama and Transfusion text performance) is not shown across model sizes. It's possible that at 1.4B, the gap is still 1.5–2 points, which would be a meaningful cost for text-heavy deployments.

(2) **Llama 2's 66.3% accuracy was achieved with 2T text-only tokens, while Transfusion's 66.1% used 1T text tokens + 1T image patches.** The comparison is not FLOPs-matched — Transfusion sees half as many text tokens. If Transfusion were trained on 2T text tokens + image patches (a fairer comparison of total compute), its text performance might exceed Llama 2's, or it might saturate. Without this experiment, we cannot be sure whether adding images helps, hurts, or is neutral for text performance at matched text-token budgets.

(3) **The comparison only covers perplexity and commonsense reasoning.** Other language capabilities — code generation, mathematical reasoning, long-form generation, factual accuracy — are not evaluated. It's possible that multi-modal training degrades performance on specialized text tasks more than it does on the Llama evaluation suite.

#### Claim 3: U-Net encoding/decoding layers improve performance beyond their parameter contribution.

**The experiments provide reasonable evidence for this claim, with diminishing returns at scale.** Table 7 shows that U-Net encoding benefits persist at 7B where the parameter overhead is only 3.8%, and the U-Net 1.4B model (1.67B total) exceeds the linear 7B model on CIDEr (28.1 vs. 27.2). This demonstrates that architectural inductive biases can substitute for transformer parameters on image tasks.

**Weaknesses:**

(1) **The 7B linear baseline may not be optimally trained.** If the linear encoder requires more training steps to learn visual processing from scratch, and all models are trained for the same number of steps, the linear variant might be undertrained relative to the U-Net variant. The paper does not show training curves to assess convergence.

(2) **The U-Net configuration is not ablated.** The paper uses a specific U-Net architecture adapted from Nichol and Dhariwal [2021] with AdaLayerNorm replaced by regular layer norm. The effect of U-Net depth, width, or architectural variants is not explored. It's possible that a simpler convolutional encoder (a few conv layers without the full U-Net structure) would capture most of the benefit at lower parameter cost.

(3) **The claim that U-Net enables "up to 64×" compression (Section 4.3.2) is based on comparing 1×1 linear patches (1024 per image) to 8×8 U-Net patches (16 per image).** This is an apples-to-oranges comparison — the 1×1 linear variant was never directly compared to 8×8 U-Net in a controlled setup, and there is no 1×1 U-Net baseline to isolate the effect of compression from the effect of architecture. A fairer statement would be that U-Net with 8×8 patches (16 patches/image) achieves better performance than linear with 2×2 patches (256 patches/image), offering a 16× reduction in patch count.

#### Claim 4: A 7B Transfusion model generates images and text on par with similar-scale diffusion models and language models.

**The experiments support this claim for the specific models compared in Table 9, with important caveats about the lack of controlled comparison.** Transfusion 7B achieves GenEval 0.63 (vs. SDXL 0.55, DALL-E 2 0.52, DeepFloyd 0.61) and FID 6.78 (vs. Imagen 7.27, DeepFloyd 6.66). On text, it achieves Llama evaluation suite 66.1% (vs. Llama 1 66.1%, Llama 2 66.3%).

**Weaknesses:**

(1) **The comparisons are across different training data distributions, compute budgets, and architectures.** SD 3 achieves GenEval 0.68 using synthetic captions, which the paper acknowledges. Parti achieves FID 7.23 but uses reranking with an auxiliary model. The text baselines (Llama 1, Llama 2) were trained on text-only data with different mixtures and training durations. Transfusion's performance parity is impressive as a proof of concept, but it does not demonstrate Pareto-optimality — it's possible that scaling a specialized model further would yield better results at the same total cost.

(2) **Only one model scale (7B) is compared to external baselines.** The scaling laws in Figure 5 show Transfusion's performance at 0.16B–7B, but the external comparisons in Table 9 are only at 7B. Whether a 1.4B Transfusion matches 1.4B specialized models is unknown.

(3) **GenEval is the only fine-grained image generation benchmark reported.** Other standard benchmarks like DrawBench, T2I-CompBench, or human evaluation are not used. GenEval specifically measures object-centric compositional generation, which may favor certain model architectures.

(4) **Image quality metrics (FID, CLIP) are reported on MS-COCO 30k, which is a standard but limited benchmark.** MS-COCO prompts are relatively simple and descriptive; they may not stress the model's ability to handle complex compositional prompts, unusual concepts, or stylistic variation.

#### Overall Assessment

The paper's experiments are well-designed for their primary purpose: demonstrating that continuous diffusion + language modeling over shared parameters (Transfusion) is a viable and efficient alternative to quantization (Chameleon) for training unified multi-modal models. The controlled scaling law comparisons (Figure 5) are the strongest part of the paper — they isolate the key variable (continuous vs. discrete image representation) while controlling for data, compute, architecture, and VAE training.

Where the experiments are weaker is in establishing Transfusion's absolute performance relative to the state of the art. Table 9 is suggestive but not definitive — the comparisons are across different training setups, and the paper does not establish Pareto frontiers or compute-matched comparisons against specialized models. The image editing experiment (Section 4.5) is qualitative and preliminary.

Key missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:

1. **Ablation over λ (the balancing coefficient between LM and diffusion loss).** The paper uses λ = 5 based on preliminary experiments but never shows sensitivity. This is the single most important hyperparameter in the Transfusion recipe — too small and image generation suffers; too large and text modeling degrades.

2. **Training curves showing LM loss and diffusion loss separately over time.** This would reveal whether one loss converges faster, whether they interfere during specific phases of training, and whether early stopping could improve performance.

3. **Inference cost comparison with Chameleon.** The paper measures training FLOPs carefully but never compares wall-clock inference time or FLOPs for generating images. Since Transfusion uses 250 diffusion steps vs. Chameleon's single autoregressive pass, the practical deployment tradeoff is unclear.

4. **Comparison with other continuous approaches (flow matching, rectified flow).** The paper mentions flow matching as an alternative to diffusion but never experiments with it. Given that SD 3 uses rectified flow, this would be a relevant comparison.

5. **Scaling the U-Net encoder/decoder with model size.** Table 7 keeps U-Net parameters constant (0.27B) across all transformer sizes. Whether scaling U-Net parameters proportionally would yield further improvements is unexplored.

6. **Evaluation on additional image generation benchmarks** (DrawBench, T2I-CompBench, human evaluation) to provide a more complete picture of image quality and prompt alignment.

7. **Experiments on other continuous modalities** (audio, video) to test the generality of the Transfusion recipe beyond images. The paper's title and framing suggest generality, but the experiments are restricted to text + images.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Inference Cost of Diffusion Dominates Deployment Feasibility

**The assumption or constraint.** Transfusion generates images through iterative denoising — the paper uses 250 diffusion steps at inference time (Section 4.1), with classifier-free guidance requiring **two forward passes per step** (one conditional, one unconditional; Section 3, Inference). This means generating a single image requires **500 forward passes through the full 7B-parameter transformer**. In contrast, Chameleon generates images autoregressively token-by-token — approximately one forward pass per image token, with ~1024 tokens per image (using 1×1 discrete patches). The paper explicitly acknowledges this discrepancy: the controlled comparison in Section 4.2 uses "theoretical FLOP calculation" for training but **never measures or compares inference FLOPs or wall-clock time**.

**The consequence.** For any deployment where image generation latency or throughput matters, Transfusion's 250+ forward passes per image is a severe practical liability that the headline training-efficiency numbers (Table 3's parity FLOP ratios) completely mask. A practitioner reading "Transfusion matches Chameleon at 2.9% of the FLOPs for FID" (parity ratio 0.029) might reasonably conclude Transfusion is ~34× more efficient overall, but this is training FLOPs only. At inference, Transfusion may be **substantially slower** than Chameleon for image generation — even if the per-forward-pass cost is lower (Transfusion uses fewer tokens per image, reducing the quadratic attention cost), the 500× multiplier on forward passes is difficult to overcome. The paper provides no data to assess whether this is a 2× slowdown, a 10× slowdown, or worse relative to autoregressive generation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 4.1 (Inference) states the 250-step procedure. Section 4.2 explicitly removes the shorter-sequence advantage from the training comparison by using theoretical FLOPs. **No inference cost comparison is reported anywhere in the paper** — no tables, no figures, no wall-clock measurements for generating a single image or caption. The large-scale comparison (Table 9) reports FID and GenEval scores but not inference time or compute.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The patch-size ablation (Table 6) goes partway: U-Net with 8×8 patches reduces the sequence length per image to 16 patches, which decreases the per-forward-pass cost (fewer tokens → cheaper attention), but the 500 forward passes remain. The paper notes that the U-Net configuration can "decrease the serving costs by up to 64×" (Section 4.3.2) but this references the reduction in **tokens per forward pass** (from 1024 to 16), not the elimination of iterative denoising. The 64× figure is comparing the cheapest per-step configuration (8×8 U-Net) against the most expensive (1×1 linear) — it is not a comparison against autoregressive generation. Standard techniques for accelerating diffusion sampling (DDIM with fewer steps, distillation, consistency models) are not explored.

### λ = 5 Is Chosen Without Systematic Tuning, and There Is No Sensitivity Analysis

**The assumption or constraint.** The combined Transfusion loss is `L_Transfusion = L_LM + λ · L_DDPM` with `λ = 5` (Equation 4). The paper states this value was chosen "following preliminary experiments" (Section 4.1) and explicitly defers tuning to future work. There is **no ablation over λ values anywhere in the paper** — no table showing how text perplexity and image FID trade off as λ varies, no evidence that 5 is near-optimal, and no characterization of how sensitive the headline results are to this choice.

**The consequence.** λ is arguably the single most important hyperparameter in Transfusion — it is the **only knob controlling the relative priority of text and image objectives** during training. If λ is too small, the diffusion loss is drowned out by the LM loss, and image generation quality suffers. If λ is too large, gradient updates from image patches dominate, and text modeling degrades. Practitioners attempting to deploy Transfusion on a different data mixture (e.g., 90% text / 10% images, or with higher-resolution images) cannot know whether λ = 5 will transfer, or how to tune it. The paper's entire empirical case — that the two objectives do not interfere destructively — rests on an untuned hyperparameter that balances them.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 4.1 states λ = 5 is from preliminary experiments. **No ablation is reported.** The paper does not even report the range of λ values explored in the preliminary experiments, the criterion used to select 5, or whether performance is stable across a range (e.g., λ ∈ [1, 10]) or sharply peaked. Table 4 shows text degradation from adding diffusion (+2 accuracy points), which could potentially be reduced with a smaller λ, and Table 7 shows image metric improvements from U-Net encoding, which might interact with λ. Without a λ sweep, it is impossible to know whether the reported results reflect a near-optimal balance or a compromise point that could be substantially improved.

**Mitigation status.** None. The paper explicitly leaves this to future work and provides no guidance for practitioners. This is an acknowledged gap, which is better than an unacknowledged one, but it remains a significant limitation for anyone trying to reproduce or extend the work.

### No Evidence of Generalization Beyond a Single Text-Image Data Mixture and Model Family

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the same architecture family (Llama-style transformers with SwiGLU and RoPE), the same VAE architecture and latent dimension (8), the same image resolution (256×256), the same text tokenizer (Llama 2), and the same 50/50 text-to-image token ratio in controlled experiments. The paper's title and framing ("Predict the Next Token and Diffuse Images with One Multi-Modal Model") suggest generality across modalities, but the experiments are restricted to a single modality pairing (text + images) with a single architectural template. The authors acknowledge this indirectly in a footnote (Section 3, Model Architecture): "while we use the transformer architecture in this work, Transfusion could potentially work with other architectures too, despite its name" — but no evidence is provided.

**The consequence.** A practitioner wondering whether Transfusion applies to their domain — e.g., text + audio, text + video, or code + images with a different resolution — gets no empirical guidance. Several specific failure modes are plausible but unexplored: (1) Video generation requires temporal coherence across frames; does bidirectional attention within each video clip suffice, or does Transfusion attention need a third dimension (temporal + spatial)? (2) Audio spectrograms have different spatial structure from natural images; do U-Net inductive biases designed for 2D photos transfer? (3) Higher-resolution images (512×512 or 1024×1024) increase sequence length quadratically, and the paper's experiments max out at 32×32 latent grids (256×256 pixels) — does the approach scale to resolutions needed for production image generation? (4) The paper uses a fixed 50/50 token ratio; would a 90/10 text-heavy mixture (common in document understanding tasks) disproportionately degrade image generation, or vice versa?

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All experiments use text + images with the specific configurations listed in Section 4.1. **There are zero experiments on any other modality.** The "Transfusion" name implies generality but the evidence is restricted. The image editing experiment (Section 4.5) shows generalization to a new modality *pairing* (image+text → image) within the same modalities, which is promising but does not test generalization to new modalities entirely. The patch-size ablation (Table 6) provides some evidence of flexibility in image representation (16 to 1024 patches per image) but always at 256×256 resolution. The data mixture ratio is never varied. Model architecture is never varied — the paper uses Llama's exact layer counts, head counts, and embedding dimensions from Touvron et al. [2023a] without exploring whether a different architecture (e.g., a non-autoregressive text model, or a diffusion transformer architecture) would work better with the dual-objective approach.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim to have tested other modalities or architectures, and frames Transfusion as "a specific instantiation of a broader idea" (Section 3, Training Objective), explicitly inviting future work to explore flow matching, other modality pairs, and other architectures. This intellectual honesty is commendable but does not change the fact that the paper's empirical support is narrower than its conceptual ambition. The limitation matters because a practitioner evaluating whether to adopt Transfusion for their multi-modal problem cannot simply extrapolate from text+image results to, say, text+video without running their own experiments at scale.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Considered; All Prompts Receive Identical Compute

**The assumption or constraint.** At inference time, Transfusion uses a fixed generation procedure regardless of the prompt: 250 diffusion steps with CFG for every image, greedy decoding for text, the same number of generated tokens/patches regardless of prompt complexity. There is **no adaptive allocation** of inference compute — a simple prompt ("a red ball") receives the same 500 forward passes as a complex compositional prompt ("three spheres made of glass falling into the ocean, water splashing, sun setting"). The paper provides no mechanism for estimating prompt difficulty, varying the number of diffusion steps based on that difficulty, or trading off image generation quality against text generation quality per-example.

**The consequence.** For deployment scenarios where inference compute is the dominant cost (high-traffic APIs, on-device generation), this uniform allocation is wasteful. A substantial fraction of user prompts may be simple enough that 50 diffusion steps would suffice, or that CFG could be reduced or disabled without perceptible quality loss. Conversely, some prompts may require *more* than 250 steps for adequate quality — the paper's fixed budget may under-serve complex prompts. The paper has no way to distinguish these cases. This contrasts with dynamic compute allocation approaches that have been explored in text-only language models (e.g., adaptive computation time, early exiting) and in diffusion models (e.g., adaptive step counts based on prompt difficulty), none of which are evaluated or even mentioned.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper uses a fixed inference recipe (250 steps, CFG tuned per benchmark in Section 4.4, greedy decoding for text) throughout all experiments. There is no ablation over inference step count — Figure 5 and Tables 3, 5–9 all use 250 steps. The paper does not report the variance in image quality across prompts of different complexity, or analyze whether certain prompt types benefit more or less from the full 250-step procedure. A practitioner cannot tell whether reducing to 100 steps would cause a 1% or 20% degradation in FID, or whether that degradation is concentrated on specific prompt types.

**Mitigation status.** None. This limitation is not acknowledged. The paper's focus is on training efficiency and model quality, not inference-time compute optimization. However, given that inference cost is the most significant practical barrier to Transfusion deployment (see limitation #1), the absence of any difficulty-aware or adaptive inference strategy compounds the problem. A system that both (a) requires 500 forward passes per image AND (b) uses that budget indiscriminately on all prompts is doubly inefficient.

### Image Editing and Image-to-Image Capabilities Are Demonstrated Only Qualitatively on a Tiny Fine-Tuning Dataset

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 4.5 explores whether the 7B Transfusion model can perform image editing — generating an output image conditioned on an input image and a text instruction. The experiment fine-tunes the pretrained model on only 8,000 examples from the EmuEdit dataset [Sheynin et al., 2024], inspired by the LIMA finding that large language models can be fine-tuned on small datasets [Zhou et al., 2024]. The paper's claim is that the model "performs image edits as instructed" and that the "findings suggest that Transfusion models can indeed adapt to and generalize across new modality combinations."

**The consequence.** This claim is a key part of the paper's narrative about emergent cross-modal generalization — the idea that mixed-objective pretraining creates representations flexible enough to handle modality pairings not seen during training. However, **the evidence supporting this claim is extremely thin**: (1) only 8,000 examples, a tiny fine-tuning dataset by modern standards; (2) only qualitative evaluation via "manual examination of random examples" (Section 4.5) — no quantitative metrics (editing accuracy, image preservation rate, CLIP score, human evaluation) are reported; (3) only a handful of cherry-picked examples are shown in Figure 6 and Appendix C, with no information about failure cases or the selection process; (4) the model is fine-tuned on EmuEdit and evaluated on EmuEdit, so there is no demonstration of generalization to new editing tasks or datasets. A practitioner evaluating Transfusion for image editing has essentially no quantitative basis for comparison against specialized editing models or even against the Chameleon baseline.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 6 shows four editing examples (remove a cupcake, change a tomato, add text, convert to cartoon style). Appendix C (Figure 9) shows eight additional examples. All are qualitatively impressive but selected by the authors with no stated criteria. **No quantitative metrics are reported** — no FID between edited and ground-truth images, no CLIP score for edited images, no human preference study, no comparison to a baseline editing method, and no measurement of how often the model fails to follow the edit instruction or corrupts unedited regions. The paper does not report the size of the EmuEdit test set used for evaluation, the number of examples examined, or the failure rate.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is appropriately modest in its claims: "Despite the limitations of this experiment, the findings suggest that Transfusion models can indeed adapt to and generalize across new modality combinations. We leave further exploration of this promising direction to future research." (Section 4.5) This is the correct framing for a preliminary result. However, the limitation remains: a reader attracted by the paper's vision of a unified multi-modal model that handles any modality pairing will find the empirical support for image-to-image generation — a natural and important use case — to be a qualitative sketch rather than a rigorous evaluation. The gap between the paper's conceptual ambition (one model for all modality pairings) and its quantitative evidence (strong for text↔image, absent for image↔image) is particularly visible here.

### No Experiment Characterizes the Trade-off Between Text and Image Performance as Data Mixture or Training Duration Varies

**The assumption or constraint.** All controlled experiments in the paper use a **fixed 50/50 text-to-image token ratio** during training, and all models are trained for a fixed number of tokens (0.5T for controlled experiments, 2T for the large-scale experiment). There is no characterization of how the text/image performance frontier shifts as the data mixture varies, or as training progresses. A practitioner deciding how to allocate their multi-modal training budget — should 70% of tokens be text? 30%? — gets no guidance from the paper.

**The consequence.** This is a practical deployment question that the paper is well-positioned to answer but does not. The scaling law experiments (Figure 5) measure performance as total FLOPs increases with a fixed mixture, but they do not measure performance at a fixed FLOP budget with varying mixtures. This matters because different applications have different quality requirements per modality. A model for generating illustrated stories might need excellent text and good-enough images; a model for stock photo generation might need excellent images and only adequate text understanding. Without a Pareto frontier over mixture ratios, it is impossible to know whether the 50/50 ratio is near-optimal for balanced performance, or whether a 70/30 text-heavy mixture would substantially improve text with only minor image degradation (or vice versa). The temporal dynamic is also unknown: perhaps image quality improves faster early in training and saturates, suggesting a curriculum where the mixture shifts from image-heavy to text-heavy over time.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All experiments use a 1:1 token ratio (Section 4.1): "we sample 0.5T tokens (patches) from two datasets at a 1:1 token ratio." The large-scale experiment in Section 4.4 keeps this ratio: "1T text tokens and about 3.5B caption-image pairs at 256 patches per image" (which is approximately 1T image patches). The patch-size ablation (Table 6) does vary the effective number of images seen during training (larger patches → more images per fixed token budget), but this is confounded with the spatial compression loss. The loss curves for LM and diffusion are never plotted separately over training time, so there is no visibility into whether one objective converges faster than the other.

**Mitigation status.** None. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, does not suggest data mixture ratios as a direction for future work, and provides no heuristics for practitioners. Given that the core insight of the paper is that two loss functions can coexist without destructive interference, understanding how that coexistence depends on their relative weight in the training data (not just the loss coefficient λ) is a natural and important extension that is simply absent.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

Transfusion changes the conversation around multi-modal model architecture by demonstrating that the field's implicit assumption — unified models require unified representations — is empirically false. Prior to this work, the dominant approaches were either to quantize continuous modalities into discrete tokens (DALL-E, Parti, CM3Leon, Chameleon) or to keep modalities in separate architectures connected through narrow interfaces (Imagen, Flamingo, LLaVA, GILL). The first approach accepted an information bottleneck as the cost of architectural unity; the second accepted architectural fragmentation as the cost of keeping native representations. Transfusion introduces a **third path** that refuses both compromises: keep each modality in its native continuous or discrete form, apply the loss best suited to that modality, and route gradients from both through shared parameters.

This is less a paradigm shift than a **reframing that reopens a design space**. The paper does not introduce fundamentally new components — next-token prediction, diffusion, transformers, VAEs, and U-Nets are all established — but rather shows that these components can be combined in a way that was previously considered non-obvious or even unstable. The controlled scaling law experiments (Figure 5, Table 3) provide the empirical foundation: across six benchmarks and five model sizes, Transfusion consistently outperforms Chameleon's quantization approach, with parity FLOP ratios ranging from 0.029 to 0.600. These numbers convert a "why not both?" intuition into a quantitative argument that continuous representations are strictly more compute-efficient than quantization for images in a multi-modal transformer, and that diffusion loss interferes *less* with text modeling than discrete image token prediction does (Table 4).

The work also **resolves a latent contradiction** in the multi-modal modeling literature. On one side, quantization-based approaches (Parti, Chameleon) demonstrated that a single autoregressive model could generate both text and images, but with image quality that lagged behind specialized diffusion models. On the other side, grafted approaches (Imagen, DALL-E 2) achieved high image quality but at the cost of architectural complexity and the inability to freely interleave modalities. Transfusion shows that this trade-off is not fundamental — a single transformer trained with two objectives can match specialized models in each modality (Table 9: GenEval 0.63 vs. SDXL 0.55, Llama suite 66.1% vs. Llama 1 66.1%) while maintaining the architectural simplicity of a unified model. The "either/or" between quality and unity was an artifact of the quantization bottleneck, not an inherent constraint.

The implication for research prioritization is significant. **Quantization as a general strategy for multi-modal modeling is now empirically suspect**, at least for images with current VQ-VAE technology. The parity FLOP ratio of 0.029 for FID means Chameleon requires approximately 34× more training compute to match Transfusion's image generation quality. Even accounting for the uncontrolled variables in the comparison (Chameleon's specific architectural modifications, codebook size, and VQ-VAE training), an advantage this large makes it difficult to justify further investment in quantization-based unified models without a substantial advance in discrete autoencoding. Conversely, **research on hybrid objective training** — combining discrete distribution losses with continuous regression losses over shared architectures — becomes more attractive, not just for text and images but potentially for other modality pairs (text + audio, text + video, text + protein structures).

The paper also redirects attention toward a previously under-explored question: **what is the right attention pattern for heterogeneous sequences?** The Transfusion attention mask (causal globally, bidirectional within each image) is a simple solution that the ablation in Table 5 shows is critical — FID degrades from 20.3 to 61.3 when removed with linear encoding. This establishes that attention pattern design is a first-class concern in mixed-modal transformers, not an implementation detail. The block-diagonal masking pattern generalizes naturally: any sequence containing segments with different dependency structures can use the same approach, with dense diagonal blocks for each segment type that benefits from bidirectional context. This is a reusable design pattern, not a Transfusion-specific trick.

The **image editing experiment** (Section 4.5), despite its qualitative nature, hints at a deeper implication: mixed-objective pretraining may produce representations that compose across modality boundaries in ways that homogeneous training does not. A model trained only on text→text, image→text, and text→image examples generalizes to image+text→image editing with only 8,000 fine-tuning examples. This suggests that the combination of LM loss and diffusion loss teaches the transformer something more abstract than task-specific mappings — a general notion of conditioning that transfers across modality pairings. If this finding scales, it would make Transfusion-style training a foundation for few-shot adaptation to novel modality combinations, much as large language models showed few-shot generalization to novel text tasks.

However, the paper also **narrows the overclaiming window** for unified multi-modal models. Table 9 is honest about where Transfusion stands: competitive with but not surpassing specialized models, and still behind techniques like synthetic captioning (SD 3's +6.5% GenEval boost from backtranslation) that the paper did not employ. The inference cost limitation — 500 forward passes per image due to 250 diffusion steps with CFG — means that for deployment scenarios where latency dominates, Transfusion in its current form is not a drop-in replacement for autoregressive image generation. The paper does not solve the inference speed problem; it demonstrates that the training efficiency and quality can justify further work on inference acceleration.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Systematic exploration of the loss balancing coefficient λ.** The paper uses λ = 5 based on preliminary experiments and explicitly leaves tuning to future work (Section 4.1). A natural follow-up would train multiple 0.76B Transfusion models with λ ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50} on the controlled 0.5T token setup and plot the Pareto frontier of text perplexity vs. image FID. The key questions: does text performance monotonically improve as λ decreases, or is there a U-shaped curve where too little diffusion weight causes image gradients to become noise that harms text? Is there a λ that achieves better text performance than the text-only baseline (suggesting visual training benefits language)? Does the optimal λ change with model scale, data mixture, or training duration? This experiment would replace the paper's single-point "preliminary experiments" claim with a quantitative characterization that practitioners need to set λ for their own mixtures.

**Training a Transfusion model with flow matching instead of diffusion.** The paper mentions this explicitly (Section 3, Training Objective): "We leave further exploration of this space, such as replacing diffusion with flow matching [Lipman et al., 2022], to future work." Given that SD 3 [Esser et al., 2024b] uses rectified flow and achieves GenEval 0.68 (vs. Transfusion's 0.63), this is a high-priority extension. A controlled comparison would train two 1.4B Transfusion models under identical conditions (same data, same architecture, same compute budget) with the only difference being DDPM vs. flow matching loss on image patches. The metrics would be FID, CLIP, and GenEval on MS-COCO, plus text perplexity to check whether flow matching interferes with language modeling differently than diffusion. Since flow matching can enable faster sampling (fewer integration steps), this experiment would simultaneously address the inference cost criticism if the flow-based model matches diffusion quality at, say, 50 inference steps instead of 250.

**Scaling Transfusion to video generation.** The paper's experiments are restricted to text + images, but the conceptual framework explicitly includes "continuous elements (e.g. image, audio, and video data)" (Section 1). A video Transfusion would encode each frame through the same VAE, arrange frame patches in temporal order, and modify the attention mask to add bidirectional attention within each frame and potentially causal attention across frames (or a 3D block that allows bidirectional attention within a short clip). A concrete starting point: take the 7B Transfusion checkpoint from Section 4.4, add temporal attention layers (or modify the existing attention mask to handle frame sequences), and fine-tune on a video dataset like WebVid-10M or Kinetics at low resolution (64×64 latent frames). The key measurements would be FVD (Fréchet Video Distance) and text-video alignment scores, plus whether the model retains its text generation capabilities after video fine-tuning. A negative result — e.g., video training catastrophically degrades text or image generation — would be equally informative, suggesting capacity limitations or optimization conflicts at current scale.

**Characterizing the Pareto frontier of text/image data mixture ratios.** All controlled experiments in the paper use a 50/50 text-to-image token ratio. A systematic sweep would train multiple 1.4B Transfusion models with the same total token budget (e.g., 0.5T tokens) but varying ratios: 90/10, 70/30, 50/50, 30/70, 10/90 (text/image). The output would be a 2D plot with text perplexity on one axis and image FID on the other, showing the achievable trade-offs. Beyond the static mixture, a **curriculum experiment** would train with a 50/50 ratio for the first half of training and then shift to a text-heavy or image-heavy ratio for the second half, measuring whether a balanced foundation followed by specialization outperforms a fixed ratio. The paper's observation that U-Net models with larger patches "benefit from the greater amount of total images... seen during training" (Section 4.3.2) suggests that mixture ratios interact with architectural choices — systematic characterization would surface these interactions.

**Testing Transfusion on a text-only downstream task that stress-tests visual contamination.** Table 4 shows Transfusion loses 2 accuracy points on the Llama suite relative to text-only training at 0.76B, but the evaluation is limited to commonsense reasoning. A more targeted experiment would test whether the shared transformer parameters cause visual knowledge to "leak" into text generation in undesirable ways. For example: does a Transfusion model asked to describe an object (e.g., "What color is a typical fire hydrant?") show influence from its visual training that differs from a text-only baseline? More systematically, one could evaluate on text-only factual benchmarks (e.g., MMLU, TriviaQA) comparing Transfusion against a text-only Llama trained on the same text tokens. If Transfusion underperforms on factuality, it would suggest that the shared parameter space creates interference, even if perplexity and commonsense reasoning remain competitive. If it overperforms, it would suggest that visual training provides grounding that improves text understanding — a finding that would shift the justification for multi-modal training from "it doesn't hurt text much" to "it actually helps text."

**Adversarial evaluation of verifier over-optimization in the dual-objective setting.** This direction is inspired by the example paper's focus on verifier over-optimization, applied to Transfusion. When generating images with CFG, the model contrasts conditional and unconditional predictions to sharpen outputs. But CFG can also amplify artifacts or produce images that score well on automated metrics (FID, CLIP) while being perceptually worse. A diagnostic experiment: generate images from the 7B Transfusion model with CFG guidance scales ranging from 1.0 to 10.0, measure both automated metrics (FID, CLIP) and human preference scores (or at minimum, qualitative inspection by independent raters). The question is whether there exists a guidance scale where automated metrics improve but human judgments degrade — an analog of the PRM over-optimization phenomenon in the test-time compute paper. If such a regime exists, it would motivate research on guidance calibration or alternative decoding strategies specific to the Transfusion architecture.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Illustrated content generation with interleaved text and images.** A Transfusion model deployed as an API could accept a prompt like "Write a short story about a dragon and generate an illustration for each paragraph" and produce an interleaved output of text blocks and matching images in a single inference run, rather than requiring separate calls to a language model and an image generation model. The paper demonstrates that text and image generation quality can coexist (Table 9), making this viable today for creative writing, educational content, or marketing material generation. The key advantage over chaining separate models is coherence: because the same transformer generates both the text and the images, the model can plan the visual elements while writing the text, producing illustrations that match the narrative details rather than generic images from a separate prompt.

**On-device multi-modal assistants with small Transfusion models.** The scaling laws in Figure 5 show that even 0.16B–0.76B Transfusion models produce non-trivial image generation (FID 20–38) and text generation. While these small models are not production-quality for open-ended generation, they could power features like: generating simple icons or diagrams to accompany text responses, creating thumbnail previews of described scenes, or enabling basic image editing (remove object, change color) on-device without cloud round-trips. The 8×8 U-Net configuration (Table 6) requires only 16 patches per image, reducing the per-forward-pass cost to levels feasible on mobile hardware, and the image editing fine-tuning experiment (Section 4.5) demonstrates that even a small number of editing examples enable the capability.

**Training data generation for self-improving multi-modal systems.** The paper's demonstration that a Transfusion model generates both high-quality text and images (Table 9) makes it suitable as a data generator for bootstrapping better models. For example: a Transfusion model could be prompted to generate diverse image-caption pairs, which are then filtered by quality (e.g., using CLIP score and human preference models) and used to train the next generation of either Transfusion models or specialized models. The fact that the same model generates both modalities means the generated captions and images are inherently aligned — the model generated them together — potentially reducing the caption-image mismatch that plagues web-scraped datasets. This is analogous to how language models are used to generate training data for themselves (self-instruct, constitutional AI), extended to the multi-modal domain.

**Accessibility tools combining visual question answering and image description.** A Transfusion model fine-tuned for instruction following could serve as a unified backend for accessibility applications: a blind user takes a photo, the model generates a detailed text description of the scene, and then the user asks follow-up questions ("Is there a crosswalk visible?" "What color is the sign?"). The model handles both the image-to-text understanding and the text-to-text conversational reasoning in a single architecture, avoiding the pipeline complexity and latency of chaining separate vision and language models. The noise limiting experiment (Table 8) shows that limiting diffusion noise on images used for conditioning improves captioning quality (CIDEr +4.0 at 0.76B), suggesting that for understanding-focused deployments, the training recipe could be adjusted to provide cleaner visual conditioning signals without the full denoising capacity needed for generation.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions Transfusion against one primary alternative (Chameleon's quantization approach) and implicitly against the grafting paradigm (separate pretrained models connected through adapters). Based on the experimental evidence, the decision rules are:

- **Prefer Transfusion over quantization-based unified models (Chameleon, DALL-E, Parti) when:** (1) image generation quality is a priority — the parity FLOP ratio of 0.029 for FID means Transfusion achieves the same image quality with ~34× less training compute, or equivalently, substantially better image quality at matched compute; (2) you are training from scratch and can allocate a 50/50 text-to-image token budget; (3) the deployment can tolerate iterative denoising at inference (250 steps with CFG) or you plan to invest in diffusion acceleration techniques; (4) you want to avoid the architectural stability modifications that quantization-based training requires (Table 4 shows these alone cost ~1.8 accuracy points on text).

- **Prefer Chameleon-style quantization when:** (1) inference latency for image generation is the binding constraint, since autoregressive token generation (~1 forward pass per token) is potentially faster than 250+ diffusion steps per image — though this trade-off is not quantified in the paper; (2) you need to reuse existing language model infrastructure that only handles discrete tokens; (3) the image understanding (as opposed to generation) is the primary use case, since the CIDEr parity FLOP ratio of 0.218 is less dramatic, and quantization may be "good enough" for semantic-level visual tasks.

- **Prefer grafting approaches (separate pretrained text and image models) when:** (1) you have access to high-quality pretrained components (e.g., a state-of-the-art LLM and a state-of-the-art diffusion model) and want to leverage their existing capabilities without retraining from scratch; (2) the application requires only one cross-modal direction (e.g., text-to-image only) and doesn't benefit from truly interleaved generation; (3) the deployment environment can support the memory and serving complexity of multiple models; (4) the image generation quality requirements exceed what Transfusion achieves — Table 9 shows SD 3 outperforms Transfusion on GenEval (0.68 vs. 0.63), though with synthetic caption data that Transfusion did not use.

The paper does not explicitly compare against grafting approaches, so this final decision rule is extrapolated from the paper's demonstrated strengths (unified architecture, interleaved generation, competitive-with-specialized quality) and weaknesses (from-scratch training required, inference cost, no comparison against state-of-the-art pretrained components).
