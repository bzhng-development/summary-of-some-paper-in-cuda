# Low-probability Tokens Sustain Exploration in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward

**ArXiv:** [2510.03222](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03222)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper studies why exploration collapses during Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) training of LLMs on mathematical reasoning tasks, identifying the systematic elimination of valuable low-probability exploratory tokens—termed **reasoning sparks**—as the root cause.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies why exploration collapses during Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) training of LLMs on mathematical reasoning tasks, identifying the systematic elimination of valuable low-probability exploratory tokens—termed **reasoning sparks**—as the root cause. The authors introduce **Low-probability Regularization (Lp-Reg)**, which constructs a filtered proxy distribution that discards presumed noise tokens (e.g., "cost", "fine") before renormalizing, then applies a selective forward KL penalty to protect the remaining low-probability tokens (e.g., "wait", "however") from being extinguished by negative advantage signals. On Qwen2.5-32B-Base and Qwen3-14B-Base models evaluated across five math benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, MATH-500, Minerva, OlympiadBench), Lp-Reg achieves a 60.17% average accuracy, improving over prior entropy-control methods by 2.66%, while enabling stable on-policy training for 3,000 steps over 81,204 GPU-hours—a regime where baselines collapse—establishing that preserving exploration quality through selective low-probability token protection sustains RLVR scaling only when irrelevant noise is simultaneously filtered out.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RLVR Training Plateaus Because Exploration Dies

The fundamental question this paper tackles is concrete and practically urgent: **why do reinforcement learning systems for mathematical reasoning LLMs hit a performance ceiling and then collapse, even when we give them more training compute?** 

This isn't a minor inefficiency. The collapse is dramatic—as Figure 1b shows, standard GRPO training climbs to roughly 0.60 average accuracy, then plummets sharply, accompanied by a corresponding crash in policy entropy. The phenomenon has been observed across multiple independent efforts (Yu et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), suggesting it is a structural feature of RLVR training rather than an artifact of any particular implementation. The paper explicitly names this as a **training bottleneck** that limits the scalability of the entire paradigm:

> "a notable bottleneck emerges during RL training that limits its scalability, frequently culminating in a performance plateau and subsequent collapse. This failure is consistently accompanied by a rapid decay in policy entropy, indicating a severe loss of exploration capacity."

The practical stakes are high. If RLVR cannot scale, then the dominant approach for building advanced reasoning models—the approach behind systems like OpenAI O1 and DeepSeek-R1—hits a wall. Every additional GPU-hour beyond the collapse point is wasted. Understanding **why** exploration dies and **how** to sustain it therefore has direct implications for the economics and feasibility of training ever-more-capable reasoning models.

### Why Entropy Collapse Matters: Exploration vs. Exploitation in Language Models

To understand the significance, we need to step back and consider what entropy represents in this context. Policy entropy measures the spread of the probability distribution over the next token—high entropy means the model is uncertain and considers many options broadly; low entropy means it commits decisively to one or a few tokens. During RL training, the model learns from trial and error: it generates multiple solutions to math problems (sampling from its current policy), receives rewards for correct answers and penalties for incorrect ones, and updates its parameters to reinforce successful behaviors while discouraging failures.

The problem is that this process aggressively collapses entropy. Once the model finds a few strategies that sometimes work, the reinforcement signal pushes it to exploit those strategies more and more deterministically. Tokens that once appeared as low-probability alternatives—even at 2-3% probability—get pushed toward zero as the model "commits" to its high-probability patterns. This is efficient learning in the short term (the model becomes better at what it already knows), but destructive in the long term because these low-probability tokens are often the seeds of **new, better reasoning strategies**.

The connection to exploration is non-obvious and worth dwelling on. At first glance, one might think exploration requires the model to try completely random tokens—to occasionally say something nonsensical in the hope of stumbling upon brilliance. But this isn't how language model exploration works. The model cannot generate truly random tokens; its vocabulary is discrete and its probability distribution is shaped by pretraining. Real exploration happens when the model entertains low-probability but semantically coherent alternatives—words like "wait" that signal a reconsideration, or "however" that introduces a counterpoint, or "perhaps" that opens an alternative hypothesis. These tokens don't just add noise; they structure the reasoning process itself by branching into new logical paths. When the policy collapses these tokens to near-zero probability, it isn't just losing randomness—it's losing the capacity to **think differently about a problem**.

### Conflicting Prior Approaches: The Entropy-Control Paradox

The research community has not been blind to this problem. A series of recent methods have attempted to combat entropy collapse, but the paper argues they all share a fundamental flaw: they treat exploration as a problem of **insufficient randomness quantity** rather than **insufficient exploration quality**.

**Adaptive entropy regularization** (He et al., 2025) adds a bonus to the RL objective that rewards the policy for maintaining higher entropy. The logic is straightforward: if low entropy causes collapse, then explicitly incentivize the model to stay uncertain. This is essentially the Maximum Entropy RL framework (from classical reinforcement learning) ported to language models.

**Selective high-entropy training** ("80/20," Wang et al., 2025) takes a more targeted approach: restrict gradient updates to only the top 20% of tokens with the highest generation entropy. The insight is that most tokens carry little information about reasoning (function words, formatting characters), and only the uncertain, "forking" tokens—places where the model genuinely hesitates between alternatives—should drive exploration.

**Asymmetric clipping strategies** (Yu et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025) modify the PPO clipping mechanism to be less aggressive in suppressing low-probability actions. Clip-Higher, a core component of DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), uses an asymmetric clipping range that allows the policy to increase probabilities of low-probability tokens more freely than it can decrease them.

**KL-covariance methods** (Cui et al., 2025) apply KL-divergence penalties selectively to tokens where the covariance between log probabilities and advantages is high, in an attempt to prevent the policy from collapsing at precisely the points where exploration would be most valuable.

All of these methods share a common monitoring signal: **they watch policy entropy and try to keep it high**. And all of them show improved performance relative to vanilla GRPO—in Table 1, Clip-Higher, KL-Cov, and 80/20 all meaningfully outperform baseline GRPO on the 14B model. But the paper presents Figure 1b as a devastating counterexample to the entropy-centric worldview. When a pure entropy bonus is added to GRPO ("GRPO + Entropy Loss"), performance collapses **even faster** than the baseline. The entropy curve in that figure spikes initially (the bonus works mechanically), but accuracy plummets, and eventually entropy crashes anyway. This is the central paradox: **forcing the model to be more random doesn't prevent collapse—it accelerates it.**

### The Paper's Diagnostic Contribution: Reasoning Sparks vs. Noise

This is where the paper introduces its key diagnostic insight. The failure of entropy bonuses points to a deeper issue: not all low-probability tokens are created equal. Within the low-probability tail of the policy distribution, two categorically different classes of tokens coexist:

**Reasoning sparks** are semantically meaningful exploratory markers like "wait," "however," "perhaps," "alternatively," and "but." Their low-probability appearance in a given context signals that the model is entertaining a genuine alternative—a reconsideration, a counter-argument, or a shift in problem-solving strategy. These tokens serve as logical connective tissue that enables structured exploration. When the model says "Wait, perhaps I should..." it is pivoting from one reasoning approach to another. These pivots are the mechanism through which LLMs explore the space of possible solutions during chain-of-thought reasoning.

**Irrelevant noise** consists of tokens like "cost," "fine," "balanced," "ere," and "trans" that appear at low probability but carry no meaningful signal for mathematical reasoning. Their appearance is genuinely random in the colloquial sense—they don't open productive reasoning pathways; they are just artifacts of the pretraining distribution that happen to survive in the tail.

The distinction matters enormously because standard entropy-control methods **amplify both equally**. When an entropy bonus pushes the policy toward higher entropy, it lifts the probabilities of reasoning sparks and noise tokens alike. The noise tokens, once amplified, introduce destructive interference into the reasoning process—the model starts generating semantically irrelevant words that derail logical chains rather than enriching them. This explains the faster collapse under the entropy bonus: the model is being encouraged to explore, but its exploration is increasingly contaminated by nonsense, which leads to worse rewards, which leads to even more aggressive gradient updates trying to suppress the noise, which further collapses the meaningful low-probability tokens, and the whole system spirals.

### Evidence from the Paper's Analysis

The paper provides detailed evidence for this diagnostic in Figures 1c, 1d, and the extensive analysis in Section 6. Figure 1c shows the aggregated sampling probability distributions for five representative reasoning sparks ("but," "wait," "perhaps," "alternatively," "however") under three conditions: GRPO alone, GRPO with entropy loss, and Lp-Reg. Under standard GRPO, the distributions collapse rightward—these tokens go from being occasionally sampled at low probabilities (creating exploratory branching) to being sampled only at high probabilities, meaning they've lost their exploratory function and become deterministic filler. The entropy bonus does not fix this; the distributions remain collapsed. Under Lp-Reg, the distributions remain wide and healthy, with mass preserved in the low-probability tail.

Figure 1d shows the mirror image for irrelevant noise tokens ("cost," "fine," etc.): standard GRPO keeps them suppressed, the entropy bonus **amplifies** them dramatically (their low-probability sampling spikes), and Lp-Reg keeps them suppressed while preserving the reasoning sparks. The word cloud in Figure 11 crystallizes the distinction visually: low-probability tokens include "But," "Wait," "Perhaps," "Alternatively"—cognitive operators; high-entropy tokens include "sqrt," "times," newline characters—functional filler that carries no exploratory intent.

### The Statistical Foundation: A Distinction the Model Itself Makes

A crucial empirical finding that underpins the entire Lp-Reg design appears in Figure 14 (Section 6.3). Even within the low-probability range (0 to 0.1), meaningful exploratory tokens consistently exhibit **higher average probabilities** than irrelevant noise tokens. This gap—small but persistent across all training steps—means the model's own predictive distribution already encodes a noisy but usable signal for distinguishing reasoning sparks from noise. Tokens like "wait" might appear at 0.007 probability while tokens like "cost" appear at 0.004. Neither is likely, but the model "knows" (in the sense of its calibrated probability estimates) that "wait" is more contextually appropriate than "cost."

The paper attributes this to the **intrinsic confidence** of LLMs—their well-documented ability to assign calibrated probabilities that reflect genuine contextual appropriateness (Saurav et al., 2022; Nguyen et al., 2025). This is not a new observation in itself, but the paper's contribution is to recognize that this confidence gradient can serve as a **principled filtering mechanism** to separate exploration from noise, transforming an intractable quality problem into a tractable thresholding problem.

### How This Paper Repositions the Exploration Problem

Prior work treated entropy as both the diagnostic signal (we know exploration is failing because entropy is dropping) and the therapeutic target (we fix exploration by keeping entropy high). This paper argues that entropy is a **correlate, not a cause** of meaningful exploration. The actual mechanism driving sustained performance is the preservation of specific, semantically meaningful low-probability tokens—reasoning sparks—that enable structured exploration of reasoning paths.

This reframing has a direct methodological consequence: instead of trying to maintain high overall entropy (which amplifies noise), a successful method should **selectively regularize the policy to keep reasoning sparks alive while letting noise tokens die**. The challenge, as the paper frames it, is that reasoning sparks and noise tokens coexist in the same low-probability regime and cannot be distinguished by simple human labeling (it depends on context). The solution must therefore leverage the model's own confidence signal to automate the distinction.

### Positioning Relative to Existing Work

The paper positions Lp-Reg against a specific family of entropy-control methods (listed as baselines in Section 5.1.1) but also connects to two broader research threads:

**Entropy collapse in RLVR** (Yu et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025; MiniMax et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025) is the direct lineage. The paper acknowledges these methods as addressing the same bottleneck but argues they operate at the wrong level of abstraction—monitoring entropy rather than directly examining the next-token prediction distribution and the semantic character of its low-probability tail.

**Intrinsic confidence of LLMs** (Nguyen et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025; Agarwal et al., 2025) provides the theoretical foundation for the filtering mechanism. The paper builds on the observation that tokens with higher relative probabilities are typically more contextually appropriate. Interestingly, this prior work largely studied confidence in the context of **improving inference** (e.g., using confidence scores to select better outputs or prune generation), while this paper repurposes the same signal for **stabilizing training**—a novel application.

The paper also implicitly connects to the broader debate about exploration in discrete action spaces. Traditional RL exploration strategies (epsilon-greedy, count-based bonuses, curiosity-driven exploration) are designed for environments where actions are semantically neutral and any random action has at least theoretical value. Language, by contrast, has a highly structured action space where most random actions are actively harmful. This paper's contribution can be seen as adapting exploration to the semantic structure of language—exploring only through tokens that carry meaningful cognitive operators rather than through arbitrary randomization.

### Summary of the Gap

In short: RLVR training for mathematical reasoning suffers from an exploration collapse that manifests as entropy decay, but prior approaches that combat entropy decay directly (through bonuses or selective training) fail because they amplify two classes of low-probability tokens—valuable reasoning sparks and destructive noise—indiscriminately. The field lacks a method that can **selectively preserve meaningful exploratory tokens in the low-probability regime while filtering out semantically irrelevant noise**, and this gap is what Lp-Reg is designed to fill.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Lp-Reg is a **regularization add-on** for policy gradient algorithms (specifically GRPO) that protects valuable low-probability tokens from being extinguished during RL training of language models on mathematical reasoning. It solves the exploration-collapse problem by constructing a **filtered version of the model's own output distribution** that discards presumed noise tokens, then penalizing the model only when it pushes preserved low-probability tokens toward zero—creating a selective safety net that sustains exploration quality without amplifying random noise.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components that interact during each training step:

1. **Base Policy (π_θ)** — the language model being trained (e.g., Qwen3-14B-Base). It takes a math problem prompt as input and outputs a sequence of tokens representing a chain-of-thought solution. At each token position, it produces a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary.

2. **Proxy Distribution Constructor (π_proxy)** — a filtering mechanism that takes the current policy's output distribution at each token position, removes tokens whose probability falls below a threshold τ (presumed noise), and renormalizes the remaining probability mass. This produces a less-noisy reference distribution where reasoning sparks have amplified relative probability.

3. **Indicator Function (I[...])** — a three-condition gate that determines whether the regularization penalty applies to a given token: (a) is the token's probability below a batch-relative percentile threshold? (b) does the token survive the noise filter (π_proxy > 0)? (c) is the token receiving a negative learning signal (advantage < 0)? Only tokens satisfying ALL three conditions are regularized.

4. **Forward KL Penalty (D_KL(π_proxy || π_θ))** — the regularization function itself. It measures how much probability mass π_θ assigns to tokens that π_proxy considers plausible. Crucially, it uses the forward direction, which penalizes the policy only for discarding tokens entirely, without forcing it to match the proxy distribution exactly.

5. **Modified GRPO Objective** — the standard GRPO policy gradient combined with the selective KL penalty, with the importance sampling clipping adjusted to avoid suppressing low-probability actions.

Information flows as follows: a batch of prompts enters → the policy generates responses (sampling from π_θ_old in the off-policy case, or π_θ in the on-policy case) → rewards are computed via rule-based verification → advantages are estimated using group-relative normalization → for each token position, the proxy distribution is constructed from π_θ → the indicator determines which tokens receive regularization → the forward KL penalty is computed and added to the policy gradient loss → parameters are updated.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the modified GRPO objective (Equation 6),** because Lp-Reg is defined as a modification to this existing framework, and understanding the full loss function reveals what is being optimized, what constraints are applied, and how the three indicator conditions interact.
- **Second, the proxy distribution construction (Section 4.1, Equation 5),** because it is the core filtering mechanism that separates reasoning sparks from noise. We need to understand the threshold τ, the two variants (fixed vs. min-p), and the renormalization step before we can understand why the regularization works.
- **Third, the three indicator conditions in detail,** breaking down each gate—low-probability identification (δ^B_ρ), noise filtering (π_proxy > 0), and negative sampling restriction (A_i,t < 0)—to understand precisely which tokens receive protection and why.
- **Fourth, the forward KL divergence choice,** comparing D_KL(π_proxy || π_θ) against D_KL(π_θ || π_proxy) to understand the "soft guide" property and why strict imitation of the heuristic proxy would be harmful.
- **Fifth, the probability ratio clipping modification,** because removing the lower clip bound is a subtle but important design choice that prevents the objective from actively suppressing low-probability exploratory tokens.
- **Sixth, the integration into on-policy vs. off-policy training,** because the proxy distribution is constructed from different policy snapshots depending on the training regime, and this affects stability.
- **Seventh, hyperparameter configuration and sensitivity,** to give concrete numbers and show where the method has tuning flexibility versus where choices are constrained.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method-design paper** whose core idea is that you can sustain exploration during RLVR by constructing a filtered reference distribution from the model's own predictions and selectively penalizing the policy only when it eliminates valuable low-probability tokens from negative samples.

---

#### The Modified GRPO Objective (Equation 6)

The starting point is the standard GRPO objective (Equation 3 in the paper), which is a group-relative advantage actor-critic method where advantages are estimated by comparing rewards within a group of G independent responses sampled from the same prompt. Lp-Reg modifies this objective in two ways: it changes the importance sampling clipping, and it adds a selective KL regularization term.

The full Lp-Reg objective is:

$$J_{\text{Lp-Reg}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{B \sim \mathcal{D}, (q,a) \sim B, \{o_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|q)} \left[ \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^G |o_i|} \sum_{i=1}^G \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \left[ \text{clip}(r_{i,t}(\theta), 0, U) \cdot A_{i,t} - \beta \cdot \mathbb{I}\left[ \pi_\theta(o_{i,t}|q, o_{i,<t}) < \delta^B_\rho \land \pi_{\text{proxy}}(o_{i,t}|q, o_{i,<t}) > 0 \land A_{i,t} < 0 \right] \cdot D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\text{proxy}}(\cdot|q, o_{i,<t}) \| \pi_\theta(\cdot|q, o_{i,<t})) \right] \right]$$

where:

- $B$ is a mini-batch sampled from the training dataset $\mathcal{D}$.
- $(q, a)$ is a (question, reference answer) pair.
- $\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G$ are $G$ independent response sequences sampled from the behavior policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.
- $|o_i|$ is the length in tokens of the $i$-th response.
- $r_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}|q, o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}|q, o_{i,<t})}$ is the importance sampling ratio at token position $t$ — the ratio of the current policy's probability to the behavior policy's probability for generating that token given the context.
- $A_{i,t}$ is the group-relative advantage estimate for token $t$ in response $i$ (defined in Equation 2 as $A_{i,t} = \frac{R(o_i) - \text{mean}(G)}{\text{std}(G)}$ where $G = \{R(o_1), \ldots, R(o_G)\}$).
- $U$ is the upper clipping bound for the importance sampling ratio (set to $U = 10$ in all experiments).
- $\beta$ is the KL regularization coefficient (set to $\beta = 1.0$).
- $\mathbb{I}[\cdot]$ is the three-condition indicator function.
- $\delta^B_\rho$ is the dynamic low-probability threshold computed as the $\rho$-th percentile of sampling probabilities across all tokens in batch $B$.
- $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ is the filtered and renormalized proxy distribution.
- $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\text{proxy}} \| \pi_\theta)$ is the forward Kullback-Leibler divergence.

**What it computes:** For each token in each generated response, we first compute a standard policy gradient term: the clipped importance sampling ratio multiplied by the advantage. This term pushes the policy toward tokens from successful responses (positive advantage) and away from tokens from unsuccessful responses (negative advantage). Then, IF AND ONLY IF the token satisfies all three indicator conditions, we subtract a KL penalty term $\beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\text{proxy}} \| \pi_\theta)$. This penalty is positive (it increases the loss) when $\pi_\theta$ assigns low probability to tokens that $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ considers plausible, counteracting the gradient that would otherwise push those tokens toward zero. The expectation is averaged over all tokens in the batch.

**Why this form:** The additive structure (policy gradient term minus KL penalty) means the regularization only activates when the indicator fires; otherwise, standard GRPO updates proceed unchanged. This is crucial because applying the KL penalty indiscriminately to all tokens would (a) slow down learning on high-probability tokens that don't need protection, and (b) regularize positive samples where the policy is already doing the right thing, introducing noise without benefit. The three-condition gate ensures the penalty is surgically targeted at the specific token/context combinations where extinction risk is highest. The forward KL direction (explained in detail below) provides a "soft floor" rather than forcing the policy to match the heuristic proxy, preserving the model's freedom to shape its own distribution shape beyond the minimum protection threshold.

---

#### Proxy Distribution Construction (Equation 5)

The proxy distribution $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ is the central mechanism by which Lp-Reg distinguishes reasoning sparks from noise. It is constructed from the current policy $\pi_\theta$ in two steps: noise filtering and probability renormalization. The process is illustrated in Figure 2 with a concrete example.

**Step 1: Noise Filtering by Threshold τ.** For each token position (given the preceding context), the policy produces a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. We define a threshold $\tau$ and classify any token $o$ with $\pi_\theta(o|\cdot) \leq \tau$ as **presumed noise**. These tokens are eliminated from the proxy—they receive zero probability in $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ and therefore cannot trigger the regularization penalty (since $\pi_{\text{proxy}}(o|\cdot) > 0$ is a required condition in the indicator).

The paper explores two strategies for setting $\tau$:

- **Fixed threshold:** $\tau$ is a constant hyperparameter (e.g., $\tau = 0.02$). This is simple but ignores contextual variation—a probability of 0.02 might be relatively high in a flat distribution (where the peak is 0.05) but extremely low in a sharp distribution (where the peak is 0.98).
- **Min-p threshold (default):** Following Nguyen et al. (2025), $\tau = \kappa \cdot \max_{o' \in \mathcal{V}} \pi_\theta(o'|\cdot)$, where $\kappa \in (0, 1)$. This makes the filter adaptive: in sharp distributions (confident predictions), the threshold scales down proportionally, preserving only tokens close to the peak; in flat distributions (uncertain predictions), the threshold scales up, preserving a wider range of candidates.

The paper's primary experiments use min-p with $\kappa = 0.02$. The ablation in Figure 8 confirms that the dynamic min-p threshold outperforms a fixed threshold ($\tau = 0.02$), and Section 5.3 shows that both fixed and dynamic thresholds are significantly better than having no filter at all (the "Lp-Reg w/o τ" condition in Figure 8 leads to catastrophic entropy explosion and performance collapse).

**Why thresholding works as a noise filter.** The key empirical justification is Figure 14 in Section 6.3. This analysis shows that across all training steps, the average probability of meaningful exploratory tokens (e.g., "wait," "perhaps") within the low-probability range (0 to 0.1) is consistently higher than the average probability of irrelevant tokens (e.g., "cost," "fine"). While the absolute differences are small (on the order of 0.003-0.005), the existence of a consistent probabilistic gap means a threshold can serve as a **noisy but informative separator**: by setting $\tau$ appropriately, we discard a large fraction of the lowest-probability noise while retaining most meaningful exploratory tokens. This works because the model's intrinsic confidence—its calibrated probability estimates reflecting contextual appropriateness—already encodes a distinction between the two classes, even if imperfectly.

**Step 2: Probability Renormalization (Equation 5).** After filtering, the remaining tokens (those with $\pi_\theta(o|\cdot) > \tau$) form a truncated set. Their probabilities no longer sum to 1 because we removed the mass assigned to noise tokens. The renormalization step redistributes this discarded mass proportionally among the survivors:

$$\pi_{\text{proxy}}(o|\cdot) = \begin{cases} \frac{\pi_\theta(o|\cdot)}{\sum_{o' \text{ s.t. } \pi_\theta(o'|\cdot) > \tau} \pi_\theta(o'|\cdot)} & \text{if } \pi_\theta(o|\cdot) > \tau \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where the sum in the denominator runs over all tokens that survive the threshold filter (those with probability strictly greater than $\tau$).

**What it computes:** For each token position, take the current policy's raw probability distribution, remove all tokens with probability at or below $\tau$, and scale up the probabilities of the remaining tokens so they sum to 1. The output is a new probability distribution over the vocabulary where the relative ordering of survivor tokens is preserved (the ratio between any two survivors' probabilities is unchanged), but their absolute probabilities are amplified, and noise tokens have exactly zero probability.

**Why renormalization rather than re-sampling or masking.** The renormalization step serves two purposes. First, it makes $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ a valid probability distribution (summing to 1), which is required for the KL divergence to be well-defined. Second, and more subtly, it **amplifies the relative probability of reasoning sparks** within the proxy. Consider a token "wait" with $\pi_\theta(\text{wait}|\cdot) = 0.007$ and a token "cost" with $\pi_\theta(\text{cost}|\cdot) = 0.003$, in a distribution where the peak is at 0.4 and κ = 0.02, giving τ = 0.008. Under this threshold, "cost" is filtered out (its probability is below τ) while "wait" survives. In the raw policy, "wait" has probability 0.007; in the renormalized proxy, its probability increases because the discarded mass from "cost" and other noise tokens gets redistributed to the survivors. This amplification is critical: it means that when we later compute $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\text{proxy}} \| \pi_\theta)$, the penalty for driving "wait" toward zero is based on its **amplified proxy probability**, making the protection stronger for tokens the filter singles out as meaningful.

**Design choice: constructing π_proxy from the current policy.** The paper specifies that $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ is built from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ in the off-policy setting (where we use importance sampling) and from $\pi_\theta$ in the on-policy setting. This is a practical choice: in off-policy training, we have already sampled responses using $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and need consistency between the sampling distribution and the proxy reference. In on-policy training, we can use the current policy directly since there is no distribution shift between sampling and training.

#### The Three Indicator Conditions in Detail

The indicator function $\mathbb{I}[\cdot]$ in Equation 6 gates the KL penalty with three conditions that must ALL be true simultaneously. This triple gate is what makes the regularization selective rather than blanket. Let's examine each condition individually and understand why it is necessary.

**Condition 1: Low-probability identification — $\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}|q, o_{i,<t}) < \delta^B_\rho$.** This condition restricts regularization to tokens whose current sampling probability is in the lowest $\rho$-th percentile of all tokens in the current training batch $B$. The threshold $\delta^B_\rho$ is computed dynamically at each training step: we gather the sampling probabilities of every token across all responses in batch $B$, sort them, and take the $\rho$-th percentile as the cutoff. Only tokens with probability below this batch-relative threshold are considered "low-probability" and thus candidates for protection.

The parameter $\rho$ controls how wide a net to cast: $\rho = 1\%$ (used for Qwen3-14B-Base) means the bottom 1% of tokens by probability are eligible; $\rho = 0.5\%$ (used for Qwen2.5-32B-Base) means only the bottom half-percent. The sensitivity analysis in Figure 15 shows that performance is stable across $\rho \in \{0.005, 0.010, 0.015\}$.

**Why batch-relative rather than an absolute threshold.** Absolute probability thresholds fail because the policy's probability distribution changes dramatically over training—what counts as "low probability" at step 100 (when the model is still relatively uncertain) might be completely different from what counts at step 500 (when the model is more confident). By computing the threshold relative to the batch, the method automatically adapts to the current scale of the policy's probabilities. Additionally, using a percentile rather than an absolute value ensures a consistent proportion of tokens are protected regardless of distribution shifts.

**Why target only low-probability tokens.** The paper's core hypothesis (validated by Figure 7's ablation, "Highest H Regularization" vs. "Lowest π_θ Regularization") is that meaningful exploration tokens reside in the low-probability regime. Regularizing high-probability tokens is counterproductive because (a) they already survive the RL optimization naturally, and (b) they are often common function words or formatting characters whose regularization would pollute the learning signal. The ablation in Figure 7 confirms this: applying the same regularization mechanism to the top 1% highest-entropy tokens (rather than the bottom 1% lowest-probability tokens) leads to worse performance than no regularization at all.

**Condition 2: Noise filtering — $\pi_{\text{proxy}}(o_{i,t}|q, o_{i,<t}) > 0$.** This condition ensures that only tokens that survive the proxy distribution's noise filter receive regularization. Since noise tokens are assigned exactly zero probability in $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ (by Equation 5), they automatically fail this condition and are excluded from protection.

**Why this condition is critical.** The ablation "Lp-Reg w/o τ" in Figure 8 demonstrates the catastrophic failure when this filter is removed: removing the $\pi_{\text{proxy}} > 0$ gate means ALL tokens below the probability threshold, including pure noise, receive regularization. This leads to entropy explosion (the model is penalized for suppressing genuinely irrelevant tokens), performance collapse, and unstable training dynamics. The filter is not an optimization—it is necessary for the method to function at all.

Theoretically, this condition embodies the paper's key insight: exploration quality matters more than exploration quantity. By filtering out noise, we ensure that the regularization budget is spent exclusively on tokens that are more likely to be meaningful, avoiding the entropy-bonus failure mode where amplifying all low-probability tokens indiscriminately accelerates collapse.

**Condition 3: Negative sample restriction — $A_{i,t} < 0$.** This condition restricts regularization to tokens receiving a negative advantage signal. Recall from Equation 2 that $A_{i,t}$ is the group-relative advantage: for a token in a response that received a low reward (below the group mean), $A_{i,t}$ is negative, signaling that the policy should reduce its probability of generating that token in that context. For tokens in successful responses (above-mean reward), $A_{i,t}$ is positive, signaling that the policy should increase their probability.

By requiring $A_{i,t} < 0$, the regularization only intervenes when the learning signal is actively trying to suppress a token—it pushes back against the negative gradient, saying "don't eliminate this token entirely even though the current trajectory was unsuccessful." When the advantage is positive, the learning signal is already reinforcing the token, so no protection is needed.

**Why restrict to negative samples only.** The ablation in Figure 9 ("Regularize Neg." vs. "Regularize Pos. & Neg.") shows that applying regularization to both positive and negative samples leads to slower learning than restricting it to negative samples only. The paper's explanation is twofold. First, the primary threat to reasoning sparks comes from **over-penalization**: negative rewards cause the policy to aggressively suppress tokens associated with failed reasoning attempts, and these tokens may be structurally sound even though the specific trajectory was unsuccessful. Second, adding regularization to positive samples introduces noise into the gradient without providing additional exploratory benefit—the policy already preserves these tokens naturally because they're associated with success. The "Regularize Pos. & Neg." condition still outperforms no regularization (GRPO baseline), confirming that protecting low-probability tokens is beneficial overall, but the negative-only restriction is more efficient.

**Why all three conditions must hold simultaneously (logical AND).** The three conditions target complementary risks: Condition 1 identifies tokens whose low probability puts them at risk of extinction; Condition 2 filters out tokens where extinction would actually be beneficial (noise elimination); Condition 3 identifies tokens receiving an active suppression signal. A token that is low-probability AND survives the noise filter AND receives a negative advantage is exactly the profile of a reasoning spark at risk of elimination. Removing any one condition breaks the selectivity: without Condition 1, high-probability tokens get regularized unnecessarily; without Condition 2, noise tokens get protected and destabilize training; without Condition 3, positive tokens get regularized, introducing gradient noise without benefit.

#### The Forward KL Divergence Choice

The regularization function in Equation 6 is $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\text{proxy}} \| \pi_\theta)$, the **forward** KL divergence from the proxy to the policy (also written as KL(proxy || policy)). This is a critical design choice that the paper explicitly contrasts with the reverse direction $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{proxy}})$ in the ablation of Figure 10.

The forward KL divergence is defined as:

$$D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\text{proxy}} \| \pi_\theta) = \sum_{o \in \mathcal{V}} \pi_{\text{proxy}}(o|\cdot) \log \frac{\pi_{\text{proxy}}(o|\cdot)}{\pi_\theta(o|\cdot)}$$

where the sum runs over all tokens in the vocabulary, but in practice only tokens with $\pi_{\text{proxy}}(o|\cdot) > 0$ contribute (since $0 \cdot \log(0/\pi_\theta)$ is defined as 0 by convention, and $\pi_{\text{proxy}}(o) \cdot \log(\pi_{\text{proxy}}(o)/0)$ is infinite).

**What it computes:** For each token position, we measure how much "surprise" a model that believes the true distribution is $\pi_\theta$ would experience upon learning that the actual distribution is $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$. For tokens where $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ assigns non-zero probability but $\pi_\theta$ assigns very low probability, the term $\pi_{\text{proxy}}(o) \cdot \log(\pi_{\text{proxy}}(o)/\pi_\theta(o))$ becomes large because $\log(1/\pi_\theta(o))$ grows as $\pi_\theta(o)$ shrinks. In practical terms, the forward KL imposes a **heavy penalty when $\pi_\theta(o|\cdot)$ approaches zero for any token $o$ that $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ considers plausible**, effectively placing a soft floor under those tokens' probabilities.

**Why forward KL rather than reverse KL.** The reverse KL, $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{proxy}})$, measures the divergence in the opposite direction—it penalizes deviations of $\pi_\theta$ from $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ everywhere, including at tokens where $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ assigns non-zero probability and $\pi_\theta$ assigns higher probability. This has the effect of **forcing the policy to strictly imitate the proxy distribution**, pushing $\pi_\theta$ toward $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ across all tokens equally. The forward KL is more permissive: it only penalizes the policy for assigning low probability to tokens the proxy considers plausible, without penalizing the policy for assigning higher-than-proxy probability to those tokens or for exploring tokens that the proxy assigns zero probability to (since $\pi_{\text{proxy}}(o) = 0$ zeroes out the contribution regardless of $\pi_\theta(o)$).

The paper's argument for preferring forward KL is that $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ is a **heuristic reference, not a target distribution**. It's constructed through a simple filtering operation on the current policy, which means it inherits all the biases, miscalibrations, and approximations of that policy. Strictly imitating this heuristic target (as the reverse KL would enforce) would constrain the policy's ability to discover genuinely better distributions. The forward KL acts as a "soft guide": it prevents catastrophic forgetting of plausible tokens while allowing the policy to reshape its distribution freely otherwise.

The empirical validation in Figure 10 is clear: forward KL significantly outperforms reverse KL, with reverse KL showing performance only marginally better than the no-regularization GRPO baseline. This confirms that the permissive, floor-providing nature of the forward KL is essential to the method's success.

**Connection to avoiding token elimination.** The mathematical property that makes forward KL effective for this specific task is its **mode-covering** behavior. In the forward direction, $D_{\text{KL}}(P \| Q)$ penalizes $Q$ for not covering all modes of $P$—it's large when $P$ has non-zero probability in regions where $Q$ is near-zero. Applied here, this means $\pi_\theta$ is penalized for assigning probabilities near zero to tokens that $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ considers plausible. This is exactly the "don't extinguish reasoning sparks" objective. If the proxy says "wait" has 0.01 probability and the policy pushes "wait" to 0.0001, the forward KL term becomes substantial, pushing back against the gradient that would eliminate it. If the policy pushes "wait" to 0.02 instead, no penalty is incurred (the KL term is small because $\pi_\theta$ covers that mode, even if it overshoots relative to the proxy).

#### Probability Ratio Clipping Modification

The paper modifies the standard GRPO clipping in Equation 6 by changing $\text{clip}(r_{i,t}, 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon)$ to $\text{clip}(r_{i,t}, 0, U)$. This is a subtle but important change that prevents the objective from actively suppressing low-probability exploratory tokens.

In standard PPO/GRPO clipping, the importance sampling ratio $r_{i,t} = \pi_\theta(o_{i,t}) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t})$ is clipped to the range $[1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon]$, typically with $\epsilon = 0.2$. When the advantage is negative ($A_{i,t} < 0$), the objective uses $\text{clip}(r_{i,t}, 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) \cdot A_{i,t}$, which is maximized (i.e., least negative) when the clipped ratio is at its minimum, $1 - \epsilon$. This means the policy gradient pushes probabilities downward, but the clipping creates a floor: the policy can only reduce a token's probability to $(1 - \epsilon) \cdot \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t})$ before the gradient signal cuts off.

The problem is that $\epsilon = 0.2$ with a lower bound of $1 - \epsilon = 0.8$ means the policy can still reduce any token's probability by 20% per update. Over multiple updates, this compounds: a token that starts at probability 0.01 would be reduced to approximately $0.01 \times (0.8)^k$ after $k$ negative-advantage updates, rapidly approaching zero. For reasoning sparks that happen to appear in unsuccessful trajectories, this standard clipping mechanism still enables extinction.

The modified clipping uses a **lower bound of 0 instead of $1 - \epsilon$** and an **upper bound of $U = 10$**. Setting the lower bound to 0 removes the implicit pressure to decrease probabilities—when $r_{i,t}$ is small (the current policy assigns lower probability than the behavior policy), the clipped ratio cannot go below zero, so the gradient from the clipped term vanishes for tokens the policy has already reduced. This effectively defers to the KL penalty (when active) to be the sole mechanism preventing extinction, rather than having the clipping mechanism fight against the KL penalty.

The upper bound of $U = 10$ serves only for numerical stability: without an upper clip, extremely large importance sampling ratios could cause gradient explosion. This upper clip is set high enough (10× the behavior policy probability) that it doesn't constrain normal probability increases for successful trajectories.

**Why this matters.** In standard GRPO, there's a tension between the policy gradient term (trying to suppress low-probability tokens in unsuccessful trajectories) and any entropy regularization (trying to keep probabilities high). The clipping mechanism in standard PPO partially mitigates this tension by capping how much a probability can decrease in a single update, but it doesn't solve the multi-step extinction problem. Lp-Reg's approach is to remove the lower clip entirely and rely on the selective KL penalty to provide token-specific protection, creating a cleaner separation of concerns: the policy gradient optimizes for reward, the KL penalty prevents extinction of protected tokens, and the clipping (now only an upper bound) provides numerical stability.

#### Integration into On-Policy vs. Off-Policy Training

The paper specifies that Lp-Reg works in both on-policy and off-policy settings, but with different constructions of the proxy distribution:

- **Off-policy:** Responses are sampled from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ (the behavior policy at the start of the rollout), and the proxy distribution $\pi_{\text{proxy}}$ is constructed from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ as well. The current policy $\pi_\theta$ is updated through importance sampling. This is the standard GRPO setup with mini-batches: after collecting a rollout of responses, the data is split into mini-batches, and gradient updates use importance sampling ratios to correct for the distribution shift between $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and the updated $\pi_\theta$.

- **On-policy:** Responses are sampled from and the proxy is constructed from the current policy $\pi_\theta$ directly. There is no importance sampling (or equivalently, $r_{i,t} = 1$ for all tokens). This requires re-sampling after every gradient update, which is more computationally expensive per step but eliminates off-policy correction errors.

The paper reports (Figure 4, Table 1) that on-policy training consistently outperforms off-policy training for all methods, not just Lp-Reg. This is attributed to the inherent stability of on-policy updates—they avoid the distribution shift between data-sampling and training policies that requires importance sampling corrections, which in turn avoids the variance and bias introduced by clipping those corrections.

For Lp-Reg specifically, the on-policy variant has an additional advantage: the proxy distribution is built from the exact same policy that generated the responses, so the noise filtering is perfectly aligned with the data distribution. In the off-policy variant, the proxy is built from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, but the regularization acts on $\pi_\theta$, creating a slight mismatch—the proxy reflects what the old policy considered noise, which may differ from what the current policy considers noise after several gradient updates.

The training settings (Section 5.1.2) specify: global batch size of 256, mini-batch size of 32 (resulting in 8 gradient updates per rollout for off-policy methods), group number $G = 8$ (meaning 8 independent responses are sampled per prompt for advantage estimation), constant learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-6}$ without warmup, maximum response length of 8,192 tokens, and training on the Dapo-Math-17K dataset (Yu et al., 2025).

**Design choice: 8 gradient updates per rollout for off-policy.** This is a standard GRPO setting that balances sample efficiency against off-policy degradation. More updates per rollout would increase sample efficiency (extracting more learning from each set of samples) but would increase the off-policy distribution shift (the current policy diverges further from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ with each update), degrading the quality of importance sampling corrections. Eight updates is a common compromise in the literature.

#### Hyperparameter Configuration and Sensitivity

The paper specifies the following hyperparameters for Lp-Reg (Section 5.1.2):

- **Low-probability percentile ρ:** 1% for Qwen3-14B-Base, 0.5% for Qwen2.5-32B-Base. This controls the batch-relative threshold $\delta^B_\rho$ below which tokens are candidates for regularization.
- **Min-p ratio κ:** 0.02 for both model scales. This controls the noise filtering threshold $\tau = \kappa \cdot \max_{o' \in \mathcal{V}} \pi_\theta(o'|\cdot)$.
- **KL coefficient β:** 1.0 for both model scales. This weights the regularization penalty relative to the policy gradient term.
- **Upper clip bound U:** 10. This provides numerical stability for extreme importance sampling ratios.
- **Learning rate:** $1 \times 10^{-6}$, constant (no warmup, no decay).
- **Group size G:** 8. This is the number of independent responses sampled per prompt for group-relative advantage estimation.

**Hyperparameter sensitivity (Figure 15).** The paper conducts sensitivity analyses for ρ and κ:

- **ρ sensitivity (Figure 15a):** ρ ∈ {0.005, 0.010, 0.015} shows comparable final performance for the Qwen3-14B model, with training dynamics (response length, train entropy, average test accuracy) closely clustered across all three settings. This indicates Lp-Reg is robust to the precise scope of tokens being protected, provided it's within a reasonable range.

- **κ sensitivity (Figure 15b):** κ ∈ {0.01, 0.02, 0.03} shows tightly clustered training curves and final performance on Qwen3-14B, confirming that the method is not sensitive to the exact filtering threshold as long as it captures the probabilistic gap between exploratory and noise tokens (Figure 14).

**The proxy construction in practice (Appendix B.4, Figures 20-22).** A concrete case study shows what tokens the min-p filter preserves versus discards. In one example response from a Qwen3-14B model trained with Lp-Reg, the threshold $\tau = 0.026208$ preserves tokens like "Wait" and "Then" (received probability > τ) while filtering tokens like "We," "also," and "that" (received probability < τ). The preserved tokens include the reasoning sparks that structure the chain of thought; the filtered tokens are functional words that don't encode exploratory intent. This example illustrates the practical operation of the filtering mechanism and confirms it makes semantically meaningful distinctions in real generated text.

**Design choice: different ρ for different model scales.** The paper uses ρ = 1% for the 14B model and ρ = 0.5% for the 32B model. The authors do not explicitly justify this difference, but a plausible motivation (consistent with the paper's framework) is that larger models have richer tail distributions—a 32B model may assign non-trivial probability to more diverse low-probability tokens, some of which are genuine reasoning sparks and some of which are noise. Using a smaller ρ (0.5% instead of 1%) targets a more selective slice of the tail, potentially avoiding noise tokens that would otherwise be included and destabilize training. The performance results (Table 1: Lp-Reg achieves 52.54% on 32B vs. 60.17% on 14B) suggest this choice works in practice even if the optimization is not formally derived.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Exploration from "Quantity of Randomness" to "Quality of Preserved Reasoning Tokens"

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is a reframing of what exploration means for LLMs during RLVR training. Prior work—spanning adaptive entropy regularization (He et al., 2025), selective high-entropy training (Wang et al., 2025), asymmetric clipping (Yu et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025), and KL-covariance methods (Cui et al., 2025)—treated the exploration collapse as a problem of **insufficient randomness**. The diagnostic signal was entropy (which measures the spread of the probability distribution), and the therapeutic target was to keep entropy higher, longer. This framing was natural given the RL literature, where exploration is classically managed through entropy bonuses or epsilon-greedy randomization.

Lp-Reg argues this framing is not merely suboptimal—it's actively harmful. The key evidence is Figure 1b: adding a pure entropy bonus to GRPO causes performance to collapse **faster** than no intervention at all. This is a striking negative result that cannot be explained within the entropy-as-exploration framework. If exploration is simply about having more randomness, why would more randomness accelerate failure?

The paper's diagnostic insight resolves this paradox by introducing a **qualitative dimension** to the exploration problem. Not all low-probability tokens serve the same function. The distribution tail contains two categorically distinct classes: **reasoning sparks** (semantically meaningful cognitive operators like "wait," "however," "perhaps") that enable structured exploration by branching into alternative reasoning paths, and **irrelevant noise** (contextually inappropriate tokens like "cost," "fine," "balanced") whose amplification derails coherent reasoning. Entropy-based methods, by design, cannot distinguish between these classes—they lift all tail probabilities equally, amplifying noise along with genuine exploration signals. The faster collapse under entropy bonuses occurs because the amplified noise contaminates the reasoning process, leading to lower rewards, which triggers more aggressive gradient updates suppressing the tail, which further collapses meaningful exploration, creating a feedback loop.

This reframing is **fundamental, not incremental**. It changes the exploration problem from a continuous optimization challenge (how much randomness to inject) to a filtering challenge (which low-probability tokens to preserve and which to let die). It implies that successful exploration strategies must be **semantically aware**—they must leverage the model's own understanding of contextual appropriateness to distinguish productive branching from nonsense—rather than merely statistically aware. The paper provides evidence for this distinction throughout Section 6: the word cloud in Figure 11 shows that high-entropy tokens are dominated by formatting characters and function words, while low-probability tokens are dominated by cognitive operators; the individual token distribution plots in Figure 17 show that reasoning sparks are systematically eliminated under GRPO and not restored by entropy loss; and Figure 14 demonstrates a consistent probabilistic gap between exploratory and noise tokens that the model's own confidence encodes.

The significance extends beyond this paper's method. If the diagnosis is correct, it means the entire research direction of entropy-control methods for RLVR exploration is built on a proxy signal (entropy) that is correlational rather than causal, and that future progress requires methods operating at the semantic level of individual token distributions rather than the aggregate level of entropy statistics. This is a conceptual pivot analogous to the shift in computer vision from handcrafted features to learned representations, applied to the reinforcement learning training dynamics problem.

### Innovation 2: The Proxy Distribution as a Self-Supervised Filtering Mechanism

The paper's second conceptual innovation is the idea that the model's own predictive distribution contains sufficient signal to separate exploration from noise, and that this signal can be operationalized through a simple thresholding-and-renormalization operation—the proxy distribution π_proxy—without any external annotation, auxiliary model, or task-specific engineering.

Prior work on confidence-based filtering (Nguyen et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025) had established that LLMs produce calibrated probability estimates where higher-probability tokens are generally more contextually appropriate. But this prior work applied confidence signals at **inference time**—to improve decoding quality, prune generation paths, or select among candidate outputs. The paper's insight is to repurpose this same signal at **training time** as a stabilization mechanism, using the model's confidence gradient within the low-probability regime to automatically filter out noise tokens before they can be amplified by regularization.

What makes this distinctive is its **self-supervised, policy-intrinsic** nature. Lp-Reg does not require:
- Human annotations of which tokens are "exploratory" versus "noise" (which would be impractical across contexts where the same token can play different roles).
- A separate classifier or verifier to distinguish token classes.
- Task-specific vocabularies or heuristics about which words matter for math reasoning.

Instead, it relies on the empirical regularity documented in Figure 14: that across all training steps, meaningful exploratory tokens in the low-probability range (0 to 0.1) consistently exhibit higher average probabilities than irrelevant tokens. This gap—small in absolute terms (on the order of 0.003-0.005) but persistent across training—means a probability threshold τ can serve as a **principled, noisy separator**. The min-p variant (τ = κ · max probability) makes this threshold adaptive to the distribution's sharpness, which is important because the model's confidence calibration changes as training progresses and across different contexts.

The proxy distribution construction is a **fundamental architectural contribution** rather than an incremental trick because it solves a problem that would otherwise be intractable. Without some filtering mechanism, any method attempting to protect low-probability tokens faces an unavoidable tradeoff: protect too narrowly and you miss reasoning sparks that happen to be at very low probability in certain contexts; protect too broadly and you amplify noise, destabilizing training (as demonstrated by the "Lp-Reg w/o τ" ablation in Figure 8, which collapses catastrophically). The proxy distribution provides a way to navigate this tradeoff that is grounded in the model's own competence rather than in arbitrary hyperparameters, making the approach scalable across model sizes (demonstrated on both 14B and 32B variants) and training regimes (both on-policy and off-policy).

The innovation's significance is amplified by its demonstrated robustness. The hyperparameter sensitivity analysis in Figure 15 shows that both ρ (the low-probability percentile) and κ (the min-p ratio) can vary across reasonable ranges without substantially affecting final performance, suggesting the method captures a genuine structural property of the training dynamics rather than exploiting a fragile hyperparameter sweet spot.

### Innovation 3: Negative-Sample-Only Regularization as a Principle of Efficient Exploration Protection

The third conceptual innovation is the identification that exploration-preserving regularization should be applied **exclusively to tokens receiving negative learning signals**, not uniformly to all low-probability tokens. This is a non-obvious design principle that the paper validates both empirically (Figure 9) and theoretically.

The intuition is straightforward once stated but was not obvious before the paper's analysis: reasoning sparks are most at risk when they appear in **unsuccessful trajectories**. When a response containing "Wait, perhaps..." leads to a wrong answer, the negative advantage signal pushes the policy to reduce the probability of all tokens in that response, including the exploratory markers that initiated productive reconsideration. These markers were not the cause of the failure—the specific mathematical content that followed was—but they get caught in the gradient's blast radius. In successful trajectories, reasoning sparks already enjoy positive reinforcement (they appear in contexts that led to correct answers), so they face no extinction threat.

The paper frames this as a principle of **efficient protection**: target the regularization budget at the specific mechanism that causes extinction (over-penalization of tokens in failed attempts) rather than at a general property of the distribution. This contrasts with entropy-control methods that maintain high entropy uniformly across all samples, positive and negative alike, which wastes capacity on tokens that were never at risk and introduces noise into the gradient for successful trajectories.

The empirical validation in Figure 9 is clean: "Regularize Neg." (negative samples only) achieves faster learning than "Regularize Pos. & Neg." (all samples), and both substantially outperform "Regularize None" (GRPO baseline). The paper quantifies this as faster convergence to the same performance level, which in practice translates to more efficient use of training compute—a marginal but meaningful improvement.

This innovation is **incremental but principled**—it's a refinement of the regularization strategy that follows directly from the diagnostic analysis of *why* reasoning sparks die, rather than an arbitrary hyperparameter choice. It connects to the broader RL literature on credit assignment: if the problem is that negative rewards penalize structurally sound exploratory behaviors that happened to precede incorrect answers, then the solution should target the penalty mechanism rather than trying to maintain high entropy everywhere. This is a form of **structured credit assignment** applied to discrete token sequences, where the structure comes from the semantic distinction between "structural" tokens (which organize reasoning) and "content" tokens (which determine correctness), even though the method doesn't explicitly label these categories.

### Innovation 4: Forward KL as "Soft Floor" Rather Than "Strict Imitation"

The choice of forward KL divergence D_KL(π_proxy || π_θ) over the reverse direction D_KL(π_θ || π_proxy) represents a conceptual insight about the appropriate relationship between a heuristic reference distribution and the policy being regularized. The paper articulates this as a distinction between providing a **soft floor** (forward KL) versus demanding **strict imitation** (reverse KL), and demonstrates the practical consequences of this distinction through the ablation in Figure 10.

This insight matters because π_proxy is not a ground-truth target. It is a mechanically constructed reference that inherits all the imperfections of the current policy's probability estimates—miscalibrations, context-dependent errors, and the limitations of simple threshold-based filtering. If the regularization forced the policy to match this imperfect reference (as reverse KL would, by penalizing any deviation of π_θ from π_proxy in either direction), it would constrain the policy's ability to discover genuinely better distributions and potentially lock in errors from the proxy construction. The forward KL, by contrast, only penalizes the policy for assigning near-zero probability to tokens that the proxy considers plausible—it imposes a floor, not a ceiling.

The mathematical property that makes this work is forward KL's **mode-covering** behavior: D_KL(P || Q) grows large when Q is near-zero in regions where P has non-trivial mass, but is indifferent to Q having mass in regions where P is zero. Translated to the Lp-Reg context: the penalty fires when the policy tries to eliminate tokens that survive the proxy's noise filter (reasoning sparks), but imposes no penalty on the policy for exploring tokens that the proxy filtered out (which is fine—the proxy might filter out tokens that are actually useful in certain contexts, and the policy should retain the freedom to discover this).

The empirical magnitude of the difference (Figure 10) underscores that this is not a minor implementation detail: reverse KL performs only marginally better than no regularization at all, while forward KL sustains the substantial performance gains that define Lp-Reg's advantage. This suggests that **the permissiveness of the forward KL is load-bearing**—the method works because it protects without constraining, and the protection mechanism itself would be counterproductive if coupled with a matching requirement.

This insight is **conceptual rather than performance-driven**, but it has practical implications for future method design: any method that constructs a reference distribution from the policy itself (whether through filtering, smoothing, or other transformations) should regularize in the forward direction to avoid forcing the policy to imitate its own biases. This principle may generalize beyond Lp-Reg to other self-supervised regularization schemes in RL for language models.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the Dapo-Math-17K dataset (Yu et al., 2025) for training, consisting of 17,000 mathematical problems. Evaluation is conducted across five diverse mathematical reasoning benchmarks: AIME24, AIME25, MATH-500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021), OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024a), and Minerva Math (Lewkowycz et al., 2022). This five-benchmark suite tests both competition-level difficulty (AIME) and broader mathematical reasoning (MATH-500, OlympiadBench, Minerva), providing a comprehensive assessment that goes beyond single-benchmark evaluation common in prior work.

- **Base model(s).** Two model scales are tested: Qwen2.5-32B-Base and Qwen3-14B-Base (Yang et al., 2025). These represent state-of-the-art open-weight base models at different parameter counts, chosen to demonstrate that the method scales across model sizes. The 14B model is trained for approximately 1,000 steps and the 32B model for approximately 800 steps in the main comparisons, with an extended 3,000-step run on the 32B model to test scaling stability. Using base models rather than instruction-tuned variants is deliberate—RLVR is applied to instill reasoning capabilities from scratch, and base models provide a clean starting distribution where the "reasoning sparks" phenomenon (low-probability exploratory tokens from pretraining) can be studied without contamination from prior fine-tuning.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **test accuracy (%)** on each benchmark, computed as the fraction of problems for which the model's final answer matches the ground truth after extracting and parsing boxed answers. For AIME24 and AIME25 (which have smaller test sets of 30 problems each), the paper employs **sampled decoding** with temperature 0.6, generating 16 independent responses per problem and reporting the best performance to obtain robust estimates despite small sample sizes. For MATH-500, OlympiadBench, and Minerva (which have larger test sets), **greedy decoding** (temperature 0) is used for evaluation. The paper also reports **average accuracy** across all five benchmarks as a summary statistic. Training dynamics are monitored through additional metrics: **train entropy** (the policy's per-token entropy averaged over training samples), **response length** (mean number of tokens in generated responses), and the training accuracy curve.

- **Baselines.** Six methods are compared, representing the spectrum of exploration-management approaches in RLVR:

  - **GRPO** (Shao et al., 2024a): The standard Group Relative Policy Optimization algorithm, serving as the foundational baseline with no special exploration mechanism. Evaluated in both on-policy and off-policy variants.
  
  - **GRPO + Entropy Loss**: GRPO augmented with a Maximum Entropy RL-style policy entropy bonus, with entropy coefficient 0.002. This represents the classical approach to maintaining exploration by directly rewarding randomness. Included specifically to test whether indiscriminate entropy incentives help or hurt.
  
  - **Clip-Higher** (Yu et al., 2025): A core component of DAPO that modifies the PPO clipping range asymmetrically—allowing probability increases up to a higher ceiling than probability decreases—to encourage higher entropy. Evaluated off-policy.
  
  - **GSPO** (Zheng et al., 2025): Group Sequence Policy Optimization, which modifies the clipping mechanism to operate at the sequence level rather than the token level, promoting higher training entropy. Evaluated off-policy.
  
  - **KL-Cov** (Cui et al., 2025): Applies a selective KL-divergence penalty to tokens where the covariance between log probabilities and advantages is highest, aiming to prevent entropy collapse at critical decision points. Evaluated off-policy.
  
  - **Selective High-Entropy Training (80/20)** (Wang et al., 2025): Restricts policy gradient updates to only the top 20% of tokens with the highest generation entropy, based on the observation that most tokens are low-entropy function words that don't need updating. Evaluated in both off-policy and on-policy variants.

  The baselines were chosen to span multiple design philosophies: direct entropy maximization (GRPO + Entropy Loss), asymmetric gradient constraints (Clip-Higher, GSPO), covariance-based selective regularization (KL-Cov), and entropy-based selective training (80/20). All baseline hyperparameters follow their original public implementations to ensure faithful reproduction.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures training cost in two units. **Training steps** are defined consistently across all methods as one full rollout (collecting a batch of G=8 responses per prompt for a global batch size of 256), regardless of how many gradient updates are performed per rollout. For off-policy methods, each step includes 8 gradient updates (mini-batch size 32 on the rollout data); for on-policy methods, each step includes one gradient update (the data is used once). A step is therefore not FLOPs-equivalent across on-policy and off-policy methods—off-policy methods extract more gradient updates per unit of generation compute. The paper reports GPU-hours for scale: approximately 8,000 GPU-hours on 32 NVIDIA H20 GPUs for 1,000 steps of 14B training, 16,000 GPU-hours on 64 H20 GPUs for 800 steps of 32B training, and 81,204 GPU-hours for the extended 3,000-step 32B run with Lp-Reg. All methods are given the same rollout budget per step, making step-count comparisons fair in terms of generation cost but not in terms of total FLOPs (since off-policy methods perform more gradient updates per step). The paper argues this is appropriate because the generation cost (sampling responses) dominates the training FLOPs for these model scales.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation—all methods are trained once on the fixed Dapo-Math-17K training set and evaluated on the standard test sets. For AIME24 and AIME25 (30 problems each), the reported accuracy is the best score achieved across all training checkpoints, evaluated with 16 samples per problem to reduce variance from the small test set size. For the larger benchmarks (MATH-500: 500 problems, OlympiadBench, Minerva), greedy decoding at each checkpoint provides a deterministic accuracy estimate. The paper implements **early stopping** for models that experience training collapse, defined as a performance drop greater than 10% in accuracy, to conserve computational resources—this means some baseline methods (particularly GRPO + Entropy Loss) are evaluated at fewer total steps than Lp-Reg, which the paper acknowledges by reporting "the best score among the total training for each method" in Table 1. The training curves in Figures 4, 5, and 6 show performance at every step, allowing comparison at matched step counts rather than only at final performance.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Performance Across Benchmarks and Model Scales

The headline result appears in Table 1: **Lp-Reg (on-policy) achieves 60.17% average accuracy on the Qwen3-14B-Base model**, representing a 2.66 percentage point improvement over the next-best method (80/20 on-policy at 57.51%) and a 2.74 point improvement over the best off-policy method (KL-Cov at 57.29%). On the Qwen2.5-32B-Base model (trained for 800 steps), Lp-Reg (on-policy) achieves 52.54% average accuracy, outperforming the next-best methods (80/20 off-policy at 51.49%, Clip-Higher off-policy at 51.48%) by approximately 1 percentage point.

The per-benchmark breakdown reveals that Lp-Reg's advantage is not uniform across all tasks. On MATH-500 with Qwen3-14B, Lp-Reg (on-policy) achieves 94.40%, compared to Clip-Higher (off-policy) at 95.00%—Clip-Higher actually edges out Lp-Reg on this benchmark at this model scale. On AIME24 (the most challenging competition benchmark), Lp-Reg (on-policy) achieves 50.83%, substantially ahead of the next-best (KL-Cov off-policy at 49.17%). On OlympiadBench (the largest and most diverse benchmark), Lp-Reg (on-policy) reaches 68.44% versus 65.78% for 80/20 (on-policy). The improvement is concentrated on the most difficult benchmarks (AIME and OlympiadBench) rather than on MATH-500 where many methods already exceed 90%.

A notable pattern in Table 1 is that **on-policy training consistently outperforms off-policy training across methods**. GRPO (on-policy) achieves 57.43% on Qwen3-14B versus 51.13% for GRPO (off-policy)—a 6.3 point gap. 80/20 (on-policy) achieves 57.51% versus 55.88% for 80/20 (off-policy). Lp-Reg (on-policy) achieves 60.17% versus 57.08% for Lp-Reg (off-policy). This pattern holds on Qwen2.5-32B as well, though the gaps are smaller. The paper attributes this to the inherent stability advantage of on-policy updates, which avoid distribution shift between the data-generating policy and the training policy, eliminating the variance introduced by importance sampling corrections.

#### Extended Scaling: 3,000 Steps on Qwen2.5-32B

Figure 3 demonstrates what the paper frames as Lp-Reg's defining capability: **stable continuous scaling for 3,000 training steps (81,204 GPU-hours)** on Qwen2.5-32B-Base, a regime where baseline methods collapse. The figure shows three panels:

- **Average test accuracy** (bottom panel): Performance climbs from approximately 0.20 to roughly 0.58 over the 3,000 steps, with the curve showing a characteristic shape—rapid initial improvement, a plateau or slight dip around steps 500-1,000, and then continued gradual improvement through the remaining 2,000 steps. There is no collapse point; the accuracy curve remains stable or improving throughout.

- **Train entropy** (middle panel): Entropy follows a multi-phase trajectory that the paper identifies as a signature of healthy exploration-exploitation balance. It decreases initially (as the model learns core reasoning patterns), then **gradually increases** as performance improves (reflecting renewed exploration at higher capability levels), and finally stabilizes in a moderate range (0.3-0.5). This contrasts sharply with the monotonic entropy decay observed under standard GRPO (Figure 5, middle panel), and equally with the artificial entropy inflation of Clip-Higher.

- **Response length** (top panel): The mean response length grows from approximately 2,000 tokens early in training to 6,000-8,000 tokens by step 3,000, with continued upward trend. This is consistent with the model learning to produce longer, more deliberative chain-of-thought reasoning—a hallmark of successful RLVR training observed in systems like DeepSeek-R1.

The paper does not show equivalent 3,000-step curves for baseline methods because they collapsed earlier (training was stopped per the early-stopping protocol), but the contrast with the 1,000-step curves in Figures 4, 5, and 6 (where multiple baselines already show plateauing or declining accuracy) establishes the qualitative difference: Lp-Reg extends the viable training horizon by roughly 3× beyond where entropy-control methods saturate or collapse.

Clip-Higher shows the steepest initial improvement but also the most volatile trajectory, dipping substantially around step 400 before recovering. KL-Cov shows the steadiest improvement among the entropy-control methods.

Lp-Reg shows the distinctive **multi-phase trajectory**: initial decrease, subsequent gradual increase, and stabilization in a moderate range (~0.2-0.4).

#### Per-Benchmark Breakdown (Table 1 Deep Dive)

The strongest results for Lp-Reg (on-policy, Qwen3-14B) come on OlympiadBench (68.44%) and AIME24 (50.83%). On OlympiadBench, the gap to the next-best on-policy method (80/20 at 65.78%) is 2.66 percentage points—matching the overall average improvement. On AIME24, the gap to the next-best method (KL-Cov off-policy at 49.17%) is 1.66 points. On AIME25, Lp-Reg achieves 37.92% versus 34.79% for KL-Cov (off-policy) and 34.58% for 80/20 (off-policy).

On Minerva Math, Lp-Reg (on-policy, Qwen3-14B) achieves 49.26%, which is actually **below** the best off-policy methods (GSPO at 50.74%, GRPO off-policy at 49.26%—tied). This suggests the gains from exploration preservation may be less impactful on benchmarks where the primary challenge is factual mathematical knowledge (Minerva includes questions requiring recall of theorems and constants) rather than multi-step reasoning structure, consistent with the paper's framing that reasoning sparks primarily benefit structured chain-of-thought exploration.

On the Qwen2.5-32B model, the pattern is more mixed. Lp-Reg (on-policy) achieves the highest OlympiadBench score (61.19%) and competitive AIME24 (38.12%), but on MATH-500 it achieves 90.00% versus 90.20% for Lp-Reg (off-policy). The advantage over baselines is narrower at this scale—the best off-policy methods (80/20, Clip-Higher) are within 0.5-1.0 percentage points of Lp-Reg on average.

#### The Entropy Trajectory as a Diagnostic

The paper emphasizes that Lp-Reg's entropy trajectory (Figures 5 and 6, middle panels) is not merely a side effect but a **diagnostic signature** of healthy training. Standard GRPO entropy decays monotonically toward zero—the model becomes increasingly deterministic. Clip-Higher entropy increases monotonically—the model is artificially pushed toward randomness. Lp-Reg entropy decreases, then increases, then stabilizes—a pattern the paper interprets as: Phase 1 (decrease): the model converges on effective reasoning patterns; Phase 2 (increase): as the model's capability improves, it naturally re-engages with exploration, considering a wider range of alternatives at higher competence levels; Phase 3 (stabilization): the regularization maintains a healthy balance without forcing further entropy inflation.

The paper does not present formal statistical tests of this trajectory interpretation, but the qualitative alignment with the accuracy curves (which continue improving through Phase 2 and Phase 3) provides circumstantial support. The fact that this trajectory is replicated across both model scales (14B in Figure 5, 32B in Figure 6) suggests it is not an artifact of a particular hyperparameter configuration.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Low-probability token targeting vs. high-entropy token targeting (Figure 7):** The paper's core conceptual claim—that targeting low-probability tokens is categorically different from and superior to targeting high-entropy tokens—is tested by applying the same forward KL regularization mechanism to the top 1% highest-entropy tokens instead of the bottom 1% lowest-probability tokens. The result: "highest H regularization" not only fails to improve over the no-regularization GRPO baseline, it performs **worse** than GRPO, with entropy collapsing after an initial spike and accuracy declining. The mean response length also degrades. This is a strong negative result that validates the paper's diagnostic: high-entropy tokens (dominated by formatting characters and function words, as shown in Figure 11) are the wrong target for exploration preservation, and regularizing them pollutes the learning signal. The contrast is stark—the same regularization mechanism, applied to low-probability tokens, sustains training; applied to high-entropy tokens, it accelerates collapse.

**Noise filtering necessity (τ ablation, Figure 8):** Three conditions are compared: Lp-Reg with dynamic min-p threshold (default), Lp-Reg with fixed threshold (τ = 0.02), and Lp-Reg without any noise filter (w/o τ). Removing the filter entirely causes **catastrophic failure**: entropy explodes to values exceeding 1.2 (compared to ~0.4 for the filtered variants), mean response length collapses to near zero, and average test accuracy plummets to ~0.20—far below the GRPO baseline. This ablation demonstrates that the noise filter is not an optimization but a **necessary condition** for the method to function. The fixed threshold variant performs better than no filter but worse than the dynamic min-p threshold, with its accuracy curve tracking below the dynamic variant throughout training. This supports the paper's argument that adaptiveness (via min-p) provides a more robust separation between reasoning sparks and noise across varying distribution shapes.

**Negative sample vs. all sample regularization (Figure 9):** Three conditions are compared: no regularization (GRPO baseline), regularization applied to negative samples only (Regularizing Neg., the default), and regularization applied to both positive and negative samples (Regularizing Pos. & Neg.). Both regularized variants substantially outperform no regularization, confirming that protecting low-probability tokens is beneficial regardless of targeting strategy. However, "Regularizing Neg." achieves **faster learning** than "Regularizing Pos. & Neg."—its accuracy curve is consistently above the all-sample variant throughout training, and the gap is most pronounced in the middle training regime (steps 400-800). The paper explains this as removing unnecessary noise from gradient updates on positive samples, where the tokens are not at risk of elimination anyway. The final performance gap narrows by step 1,000 but "Regularizing Neg." maintains a small advantage.

**Forward KL vs. reverse KL (Figure 10):** Comparing D_KL(π_proxy || π_θ) (forward, the default) against D_KL(π_θ || π_proxy) (reverse). The reverse KL variant performs only marginally better than the no-regularization GRPO baseline, with its accuracy curve essentially tracking the baseline for most of training and showing a final accuracy of approximately 0.58 versus ~0.60 for forward KL. This is a key validation of the paper's design philosophy: π_proxy is a heuristic reference, not a target distribution to imitate. The reverse KL forces imitation of this imperfect reference and constrains the policy's ability to discover better distributions; the forward KL provides only a soft floor, preventing extinction without restricting optimization freedom.

**Hyperparameter sensitivity for ρ and κ (Figure 15):**
- **Low-probability percentile ρ (Figure 15a):** Tested at ρ ∈ {0.005, 0.010, 0.015} on Qwen3-14B. All three values produce closely clustered training dynamics—the accuracy curves, entropy trajectories, and response length trajectories are nearly indistinguishable. The final average test accuracy differs by less than 1 percentage point across the three settings. This demonstrates robustness to the scope of token protection: whether 0.5% or 1.5% of tokens receive regularization, the method's behavior is stable.
- **Min-p ratio κ (Figure 15b):** Tested at κ ∈ {0.01, 0.02, 0.03} on Qwen3-14B. Again, all three values produce highly similar training curves, with final performance tightly clustered. This demonstrates that the exact placement of the noise-filtering threshold is not critical—any reasonable κ that captures the probabilistic gap between exploratory and noise tokens (Figure 14) suffices.

**Training dynamics of regularized tokens (Figure 16):** The paper analyzes how the regularization mechanism evolves during training. The probability threshold δ^B_ρ (the batch-relative percentile cutoff) **decreases** steadily from approximately 0.07 at step 128 to approximately 0.02 at step 768. Simultaneously, the regularization ratio (the fraction of low-probability tokens that survive the noise filter and thus receive regularization) declines from approximately 27% to approximately 8%. The paper interprets this trend as evidence that: (1) the extreme low-probability tail becomes increasingly dominated by noise as training progresses, so fewer tokens pass the π_proxy > 0 filter; (2) meaningfully exploratory tokens are lifted into higher-probability regions (above δ^B_ρ) as the model learns to recognize their value, reducing the need for active protection.

### Critical Assessment

The paper's central claims are: (1) that the elimination of low-probability reasoning sparks is the key mechanism behind RLVR exploration collapse; (2) that Lp-Reg selectively preserves these sparks while filtering noise, enabling sustained exploration; (3) that this leads to state-of-the-art performance and extended training stability; (4) that entropy-based methods fail because they cannot distinguish reasoning sparks from noise.

#### Claim 1: Reasoning spark elimination drives collapse

The evidence for this claim comes from the distributional analyses in Figures 1c, 1d, 11, 12, 13, 14, and 17-19. These analyses are detailed and multi-faceted—they show probability density evolution, probability-entropy scatter plots, token frequency tracking, and the probabilistic gap between token classes. The observation that GRPO systematically shifts reasoning spark distributions from low-probability to high-probability (Figure 1c, Figure 17) is well-documented at the individual token level.

However, a critical inferential gap exists: the paper demonstrates **correlation** (reasoning sparks are eliminated as performance plateaus) but not **causation** (eliminating reasoning sparks causes the plateau). The ablation in Figure 7 shows that regularizing high-entropy tokens doesn't work, and the ablation in Figure 9 shows that regularizing low-probability tokens helps, but neither directly proves that reasoning spark preservation is the **mechanism** by which performance is sustained. It is possible that Lp-Reg works through a different channel—for instance, by providing a general regularizing effect on the policy's tail distribution that stabilizes optimization regardless of whether the preserved tokens are semantically meaningful. The paper's case study (Figures 20-22) and word cloud analysis (Figure 11) provide suggestive evidence linking the semantic character of preserved tokens to reasoning structure, but they don't constitute a controlled test of the mechanism.

**What would strengthen this claim:** An experiment that ablates the semantic character directly—for instance, comparing Lp-Reg's performance when the proxy distribution preserves tokens that are demonstrably reasoning sparks versus when it preserves an equal number of tokens matched for probability but not for semantic function. This would be difficult to design (since token function is context-dependent), but is the kind of experiment that would distinguish "Lp-Reg works because it preserves tokens with certain probability properties" from "Lp-Reg works because it preserves tokens with certain reasoning functions."

#### Claim 2: Lp-Reg selectively preserves reasoning sparks while filtering noise

The evidence for selective preservation is substantial. Figure 1c/d show the aggregated probability distributions for both token classes, demonstrating that Lp-Reg maintains a wide low-probability tail for reasoning sparks while keeping noise suppressed. The individual token plots (Figures 17 and 18 in Appendix B.1) confirm this pattern at the single-token level across five representative sparks and five representative noise tokens. Figure 14 demonstrates the existence of the probabilistic gap that the filtering exploits, and the ablation "Lp-Reg w/o τ" (Figure 8) shows that the filter is load-bearing—without it, training collapses.

The weakness is in the semantic categorization itself. The paper defines "reasoning sparks" through a fixed set of five tokens: "but," "wait," "perhaps," "alternatively," and "however." Similarly, "noise" is operationalized through a fixed set: "cost," "fine," "balanced," "ere," and "trans." These sets are small and chosen by the authors based on their semantic intuitions about mathematical reasoning. There is no systematic methodology for identifying which tokens should count as reasoning sparks, and the same token might be a spark in one context and noise in another ("but" could introduce a productive counterargument or could be a grammatical filler). The paper acknowledges this context-dependence implicitly—the filtering is done per-token-position based on local probability, not on global token identity—but the analysis in Section 6 treats token identity as fixed. This means the demonstrated "preservation of reasoning sparks" is really a demonstration that certain pre-selected tokens are preserved, not that all or even most context-appropriate exploratory tokens are preserved.

**What would strengthen this claim:** A more systematic token analysis covering a larger vocabulary of potential reasoning sparks, with some methodology for validating (perhaps through human annotation or through correlation with successful reasoning trajectories) which tokens actually function as sparks in context. Additionally, showing that the preserved tokens actually correlate with correct reasoning—i.e., that responses where Lp-Reg preserves low-probability "wait" tokens are more likely to be correct than responses where those tokens don't appear—would connect the distribution-level finding to behavioral outcomes.

#### Claim 3: State-of-the-art performance and extended training stability

The performance claims (60.17% average, 2.66% improvement over prior methods, stable 3,000-step scaling) are directly supported by Table 1 and Figures 3-6. The 2.66 percentage point improvement over 80/20 on-policy is meaningful at this performance level, though not transformative—the absolute gap narrows on easier benchmarks where many methods exceed 90%, and the advantage is concentrated on harder benchmarks (AIME24: +3.54 points over 80/20 on-policy; OlympiadBench: +2.66 points).

Several qualifications are necessary. First, the paper reports "the best score among the total training for each method" (Table 1), which is a form of oracle selection—in practice, one would need a validation set to select the best checkpoint, and the effective performance might be lower. The training curves in Figure 4 show that Lp-Reg's advantage is partly due to its continued improvement in later steps while baselines plateau; at step 800, the gap is smaller than at step 1,000, and some baselines match Lp-Reg at intermediate points.

Second, the total training steps differ across methods due to the early-stopping protocol. Methods that collapsed (like GRPO + Entropy Loss) were stopped early, meaning their "best score" was selected from fewer total steps. The paper acknowledges this by reporting steps (800 for 32B, 1,000 for 14B as the standard training duration), but the asymmetry still favors methods that maintain stability longer—which is part of the method's claimed advantage, but complicates the interpretation of the final performance numbers as a pure "accuracy" comparison.

Third, the 3,000-step scaling on Qwen2.5-32B (Figure 3) is impressive but is shown **only for Lp-Reg**—there are no baseline curves for comparison because the baselines collapsed earlier. This makes it hard to determine whether Lp-Reg's stability at 3,000 steps is a qualitative breakthrough or just a quantitative extension of what careful hyperparameter tuning could achieve with other methods given enough compute. The fact that the accuracy curve in Figure 3 is still improving at step 3,000 (albeit slowly) is promising but doesn't demonstrate that no other method could achieve similar stability if pushed.

**What would strengthen this claim:** Training runs for at least one strong baseline (e.g., 80/20 or Clip-Higher) with equivalent compute budget to see whether they also stabilize given enough steps, or whether they genuinely hit a hard ceiling that Lp-Reg breaks through. Additionally, reporting confidence intervals or variance across multiple training seeds would address concerns about whether the 2.66% improvement is reliable or within the noise of training stochasticity.

#### Claim 4: Entropy-based methods fail because they cannot distinguish sparks from noise

The evidence for this claim is strong and multi-pronged. Figure 1b directly shows that GRPO + Entropy Loss collapses faster than GRPO—an unambiguous demonstration that indiscriminate entropy incentives are harmful in this setting. Figure 1d shows the mechanism: entropy loss amplifies the low-probability sampling of irrelevant noise tokens. Figure 7 shows that targeting high-entropy tokens specifically (rather than low-probability tokens) with regularization fails. Figure 11 shows that high-entropy tokens are semantically different from low-probability tokens, dominated by formatting characters rather than cognitive operators. This is the best-evidenced claim in the paper.

However, the claim is somewhat of a straw man against the strongest entropy-based methods. The baselines include GRPO + Entropy Loss (which is indeed a naive entropy-maximization approach) but the more sophisticated entropy-based methods (80/20, Clip-Higher, KL-Cov) all substantially outperform GRPO in Table 1. These methods are not simply "maximizing entropy"—80/20 selectively trains on high-entropy tokens, Clip-Higher uses asymmetric clipping to allow exploration without forcing it, and KL-Cov targets regularization based on covariance with advantages. The paper's framing sometimes conflates "GRPO + Entropy Loss" (which clearly fails) with "entropy-based methods" in general (which mostly succeed, just not as well as Lp-Reg). The gap between Lp-Reg and the best entropy-based method (80/20) is 2.66 percentage points—meaningful, but not the kind of paradigm-invalidating difference the paper's rhetoric sometimes suggests.

#### Missing experiments and analyses

Several experiments would have strengthened the paper's contributions:

- **Multi-seed training runs:** All results appear to be from single training runs. Given the known stochasticity in RL training, variance across seeds could be substantial, and without seed statistics, it's unclear whether the 2.66% gap is reliable.

- **Ablation of reasoning spark identity:** Does Lp-Reg's performance depend on the specific set of tokens it preserves, or would it work equally well if it preserved a random subset of low-probability tokens? This would directly test the "reasoning spark" hypothesis versus the alternative that any selective low-probability preservation stabilizes training.

- **Verifier quality analysis:** The paper uses rule-based verification but doesn't analyze how verifier reliability affects Lp-Reg's performance. If the verifier produces noisy rewards, the negative advantage signal might penalize reasoning sparks more aggressively, potentially making Lp-Reg even more important—or the opposite, if noisy rewards make the negative-sample restriction less meaningful.

- **Compute-matched comparison with entropy methods:** The paper reports that off-policy methods use 8× more gradient updates per step than on-policy methods. A FLOPs-matched comparison (giving off-policy methods fewer total steps to equalize total compute) would clarify whether Lp-Reg's on-policy variant is truly more efficient or just benefits from more generation budget.

- **Analysis on non-math reasoning tasks:** All experiments are on mathematical reasoning. The reasoning spark concept (tokens like "wait," "however") should generalize to other reasoning domains (code generation, logical puzzles, scientific reasoning), but this is untested. Without evidence from other domains, it's unclear whether Lp-Reg addresses a mathematical-reasoning-specific phenomenon or a general property of RLVR training.

#### Summary

The experimental section provides robust evidence that Lp-Reg outperforms existing entropy-control methods on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, that its noise-filtering mechanism is necessary for stability, and that the forward KL + negative-sample-only design choices contribute to performance. The evidence for the specific "reasoning spark" mechanism—as opposed to a more general low-probability preservation effect—is suggestive but not conclusive, relying on qualitative token analyses and small, hand-selected token sets. The performance advantage over the best baseline (80/20) is meaningful but not overwhelming at 2.66%, and the absence of multi-seed statistics and domain diversity limits the generalizability of the findings. The 3,000-step scaling demonstration is the paper's most distinctive empirical contribution, showing training stability far beyond what standard methods achieve, but the lack of equivalent-scale baseline comparisons weakens the claim that this stability is uniquely enabled by Lp-Reg's mechanism rather than by other aspects of the training configuration.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The "Reasoning Spark" Construct Is Defined by a Hand-Selected Set of Five Tokens

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper operationalizes the concept of "reasoning sparks"—the valuable low-probability exploratory tokens that Lp-Reg is designed to protect—through a fixed set of five tokens: "but," "wait," "perhaps," "alternatively," and "however." Similarly, "irrelevant noise" is operationalized through another fixed set: "cost," "fine," "balanced," "ere," and "trans." All of the paper's distribution-level analyses in Section 6 (Figures 1c, 1d, 11, 12, 13, 14, 17–19) and its central mechanistic claims rely on these ten hand-selected tokens as representatives of the two categories. No systematic methodology is described for how these tokens were selected, whether they exhaust the space of reasoning sparks in mathematical reasoning contexts, or whether the same tokens function consistently as sparks versus noise across different contexts within the training data.

**The consequence.** The validity of the paper's central diagnostic claim depends on these ten tokens being representative. If the actual population of reasoning sparks is much larger and more heterogeneous—including tokens that don't follow the probabilistic distinction documented in Figure 14—then Lp-Reg may be protecting only a subset of genuine reasoning sparks while failing to protect others, or it may be protecting tokens that are not actually reasoning sparks but happen to fall in the right probability range. More fundamentally, the paper's narrative that Lp-Reg works *because* it preserves reasoning sparks relies on the semantic interpretation of these tokens. If Lp-Reg's performance gains come instead from a more generic effect—such as stabilizing the policy's tail distribution regardless of which tokens are preserved—then the mechanistic explanation is incorrect even if the method is effective. A practitioner evaluating whether to adopt Lp-Reg needs to know whether its success transfers to reasoning domains where the "spark" tokens are different (e.g., code generation might use "refactor" or "alternatively" but in different distributional patterns), and the current analysis provides no methodology for predicting which tokens will serve as sparks in a new domain.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The probabilistic gap between the two token classes is documented in Figure 14, showing that across training steps, the mean probability of the five exploratory tokens consistently exceeds that of the five irrelevant tokens within the low-probability range (0 to 0.1). The individual token distribution plots in Figures 17 and 18 confirm that Lp-Reg preserves the low-probability tail for each of the five spark tokens while suppressing it for each of the five noise tokens. The word cloud in Figure 11 shows that low-probability tokens include many semantically meaningful words beyond the selected five (e.g., "Then," "Alternatively," "But," "Wait"), but the paper does not analyze whether all of these additional tokens exhibit the same probabilistic separation from noise. The case study in Figures 20–22 qualitatively illustrates that tokens like "Wait" and "Then" are preserved while "We," "also," and "that" are filtered, but this is a single example.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to validate the representativeness of its spark/noise token sets, either through human annotation (do independent raters agree these tokens serve exploratory functions in context?), through correlation with behavioral outcomes (do responses containing preserved low-probability "wait" tokens show higher rates of successful reasoning pivots?), or through systematic vocabulary analysis (what proportion of the low-probability vocabulary do these ten tokens represent?). The limitation is not acknowledged by the authors, and no future work is suggested on token categorization methodology. A practitioner must therefore treat the "reasoning spark" explanation as a plausible mechanistic hypothesis supported by suggestive evidence on a small token sample, rather than as a validated causal account.

---

### Single-Domain Evaluation: Only Mathematical Reasoning Is Tested

**The assumption or constraint.** All training and evaluation in the paper uses mathematical reasoning data: the Dapo-Math-17K training set and five math benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, MATH-500, Minerva, OlympiadBench). The paper does not evaluate on any non-math reasoning task such as code generation, logical deduction, scientific reasoning, or multi-step planning. The authors do not claim generalizability beyond math, but they also do not explicitly acknowledge this as a limitation—the framing of "reasoning sparks" and the method's applicability to RLVR more broadly implies domain-generality that is not tested.

**The consequence.** It is unknown whether Lp-Reg's mechanism—or even the concept of reasoning sparks—transfers to other reasoning domains. Mathematical reasoning has specific characteristics that may make the spark/noise distinction particularly salient: it involves structured symbolic manipulation with clear verification signals, and the "cognitive operators" identified as sparks ("wait," "however," "perhaps") serve as pivot points in multi-step derivations. In code generation, the equivalent exploratory tokens might be different ("refactor," "simplify," "optimize") and might not exhibit the same probabilistic gap relative to noise tokens. In open-ended reasoning tasks without clean verifiable rewards, the negative-sample restriction (Condition 3 in the indicator) may behave differently because the advantage signal is noisier. More concretely, a practitioner deploying Lp-Reg for a non-math application has no guidance on whether to expect similar 2.66% gains, whether the hyperparameters (ρ = 1% for 14B, ρ = 0.5% for 32B, κ = 0.02) transfer to other domains, or whether the "reasoning spark" vocabulary needs to be redefined.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no evidence whatsoever regarding domain transfer. All figures, tables, and analyses are exclusively on mathematical reasoning data. The extended scaling run (Figure 3, 3,000 steps) is on Qwen2.5-32B with math data. The per-benchmark breakdown (Table 1) shows some variation across math benchmarks (e.g., Lp-Reg's advantage is larger on OlympiadBench than on MATH-500), but this is variation within a single domain family, not cross-domain evaluation.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not mention domain generalization as a limitation or as future work. A practitioner must assume that Lp-Reg's effectiveness is demonstrated only for mathematical reasoning RLVR and that any application to other domains would require independent validation. This is a significant gap because RLVR is increasingly applied to code generation (via unit test verification), where the method's applicability is both plausible and untested.

### Difficulty Estimation and the Hardest Problems Remain Unsolved

**The assumption or constraint.** Lp-Reg operates by protecting low-probability tokens that are candidates for exploration. This mechanism fundamentally assumes that the base model already has the *capacity* to explore productively—that is, that there exist reasoning paths in the model's output distribution that, if explored, would lead to correct solutions. The method does not create new capabilities; it prevents existing exploratory capacities from being extinguished during training. The paper does not explicitly analyze how Lp-Reg's benefit varies with problem difficulty, but the implicit assumption is that the base model's pretrained distribution contains sufficient reasoning capacity across the difficulty spectrum of the training data.

**The consequence.** If a problem is fundamentally beyond the base model's capabilities—if the model's pretrained distribution assigns effectively zero probability to any correct reasoning path—then preserving low-probability tokens cannot help, because the tokens being preserved don't lead anywhere useful. The paper does not provide a difficulty-stratified analysis, so it is unknown whether Lp-Reg's gains are concentrated on easy-to-medium problems (where the base model has latent capacity that exploration can surface) and offer minimal benefit on the hardest problems (where no amount of exploration compensates for missing capability). This is analogous to the finding in the compute-optimal test-time scaling literature that test-time compute cannot substitute for pretraining on the hardest problems. For a practitioner, this means Lp-Reg may extend training stability and improve average performance without addressing the ceiling on the most challenging problems—which might be the problems of greatest practical interest.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not stratify results by problem difficulty. The per-benchmark breakdown in Table 1 provides some indirect signal: AIME24 and AIME25 (competition-level, hardest) show meaningful gains (50.83% vs. 49.17% on AIME24, 37.92% vs. 34.79% on AIME25 for Qwen3-14B), while MATH-500 (easier, more standard problems) shows 94.40% vs. 95.00%—Lp-Reg actually underperforms Clip-Higher on this easier benchmark at the 14B scale. This mixed pattern is suggestive but not conclusive without within-benchmark difficulty binning. The training dynamics curves (Figures 4, 5) show that all methods plateau at different levels, implying some residual difficulty ceiling, but there is no analysis of whether Lp-Reg shifts that ceiling or only extends the approach to it.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss difficulty-dependent effects or capability boundaries. A difficulty-stratified analysis (similar to the quintile analysis in the compute-optimal test-time scaling paper) would substantially strengthen the practical guidance for practitioners deciding where to invest in Lp-Reg versus alternative approaches.

### The Headline 3,000-Step Scaling Result Has No Equivalent-Budget Baseline Comparison

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's most distinctive empirical contribution is the demonstration that Lp-Reg enables stable on-policy training for approximately 3,000 steps (81,204 GPU-hours) on Qwen2.5-32B-Base (Figure 3). This is presented as evidence that Lp-Reg breaks through the stability ceiling that causes other methods to collapse. However, the paper does not run any baseline method for an equivalent number of steps or GPU-hours. Baseline methods were stopped early when they experienced training collapse, defined as a performance drop greater than 10% in accuracy (Section 5.1.2). The total steps and GPU-hours consumed by baselines are therefore substantially lower than Lp-Reg's extended run.

**The consequence.** The claim that Lp-Reg uniquely enables extended scaling is confounded with total compute budget. It is possible that some entropy-control methods (particularly 80/20 or Clip-Higher, which showed strong performance in the standard-length runs) would also stabilize given enough steps—perhaps the collapse observed at 800–1,000 steps is a transient phase that would self-correct with additional training, or perhaps hyperparameter adjustments (learning rate decay, entropy coefficient scheduling) that were not explored could extend their viable training horizon. Without an equivalent-budget baseline, the 3,000-step result demonstrates that Lp-Reg *can* scale that far, not that Lp-Reg is *necessary* to scale that far. This weakens the paper's strongest stability claim and makes it harder for practitioners to assess whether the marginal benefit of Lp-Reg over the best baseline (2.66% at 1,000 steps) justifies the cost of extended training or whether similar gains could be achieved by simply training 80/20 or Clip-Higher for more steps.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 shows Lp-Reg's trajectory alone. Figures 4, 5, and 6 show baseline trajectories terminating at 800–1,000 steps with plateauing or declining accuracy, but these terminations are due to either the planned training duration (800 steps for 32B, 1,000 for 14B) or early stopping after collapse. The paper does not report what the collapse threshold was for each baseline or at what step it occurred. The best baseline methods (80/20 on-policy at 57.51%, KL-Cov off-policy at 57.29% on 14B) show plateauing but not catastrophic collapse in Figures 4 and 5, leaving open the possibility that they would continue to improve slowly or stabilize with more training.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation or discuss the need for compute-matched scaling comparisons. The early-stopping protocol is described as a practical measure to conserve computational resources (Section 5.1.2), which is understandable given the cost, but the absence of even a single strong baseline run at extended scale means the central stability claim rests on an asymmetric comparison. The paper suggests future work on combining Lp-Reg with other techniques (Section 7 mentions this implicitly) but does not specifically call for equivalent-budget baseline comparisons.

### Selective Categorization: Entropy-Based Methods Are Partially Straw-Manned

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's narrative frames a dichotomy: entropy-based methods (which aim to maintain high overall randomness) versus Lp-Reg (which selectively preserves meaningful low-probability tokens). The strongest evidence for the failure of entropy-based approaches is the GRPO + Entropy Loss baseline, which adds a uniform entropy bonus to the GRPO objective and collapses faster than vanilla GRPO (Figures 1b, 4 right panel). The paper then generalizes this finding to characterize the broader class of entropy-control methods as suffering from the same flaw—inability to distinguish reasoning sparks from noise.

**The consequence.** This framing obscures the fact that several entropy-based baselines in the paper's own evaluation **substantially outperform vanilla GRPO and come within 2.66% of Lp-Reg**. 80/20 (Wang et al., 2025) achieves 57.51% on Qwen3-14B (on-policy), KL-Cov (Cui et al., 2025) achieves 57.29% (off-policy), and Clip-Higher (Yu et al., 2025) achieves 56.16% (off-policy). These methods are not simple entropy maximizers—80/20 selectively trains only on high-entropy tokens, KL-Cov targets regularization based on covariance with advantages, and Clip-Higher uses asymmetric clipping to permit exploration without forcing it. They represent different strategies for addressing the exploration bottleneck, and their strong performance suggests they are already partially addressing the noise-amplification problem through their own selectivity mechanisms. The paper's rhetorical contrast between "indiscriminate entropy boosting" and "selective spark preservation" collapses somewhat when applied to these more sophisticated baselines. A practitioner choosing between Lp-Reg and 80/20 (a 2.66% gap) faces a different decision than choosing between Lp-Reg and GRPO + Entropy Loss (a ~10% gap), and the paper's framing sometimes conflates these comparisons.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 shows that 80/20 on-policy achieves 57.51% versus Lp-Reg on-policy at 60.17% on Qwen3-14B. On Qwen2.5-32B, the gap narrows to 52.54% versus 51.49% (80/20 off-policy) and 51.48% (Clip-Higher off-policy)—a difference of approximately 1 percentage point. The training curves in Figures 4, 5, and 6 show that the performance trajectories of these strong baselines and Lp-Reg are often closer than the paper's narrative suggests, particularly in the middle training regime. The paper does not conduct an ablation that would distinguish whether Lp-Reg's advantage over 80/20 comes from the filtering mechanism (separating sparks from noise) or from the forward KL formulation (soft floor vs. 80/20's gradient restriction), or from the negative-sample targeting, or from a combination that the more sophisticated entropy methods partially replicate.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the strong performance of baseline methods in the results section (Table 1, Figures 4–6) but the Introduction and Section 2 framing emphasizes the failure of entropy-based methods using GRPO + Entropy Loss as the primary example. The paper does not explicitly analyze *why* 80/20, KL-Cov, and Clip-Higher succeed where GRPO + Entropy Loss fails, or whether their mechanisms implicitly perform some form of implicit noise filtering that partially converges with Lp-Reg's explicit filtering. This leaves open the possibility that the 2.66% gap represents the marginal benefit of explicit filtering over implicit filtering, rather than a paradigm-level difference in approach to exploration.

### Computational Overhead of Difficulty Estimation and On-Policy Training Is Not Accounted for in Efficiency Comparisons

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper reports performance primarily in terms of training steps (with steps defined consistently as one rollout) and final accuracy, with GPU-hour totals provided for scale context. On-policy Lp-Reg training consumes one gradient update per rollout, while off-policy baselines consume 8 gradient updates per rollout (mini-batch size 32 on rollout data of batch size 256). This means that at matched step counts, on-policy Lp-Reg performs substantially fewer gradient updates than off-policy baselines like Clip-Higher, KL-Cov, and GSPO—each of which extracts 8 times as many parameter updates from the same generation budget. The paper argues (Section 5.1.2) that a step is defined as a rollout for fairness, but this definition hides a substantial asymmetry in total optimization compute.

**The consequence.** The reported performance comparisons in Table 1 and Figures 4–6 favor on-policy methods because they are evaluated after equal generation cost but unequal optimization cost. An off-policy method that achieves 57.29% (KL-Cov) using 8 gradient updates per step is arguably more sample-efficient than an on-policy method that achieves 60.17% (Lp-Reg) using 1 gradient update per step, because the off-policy method amortizes generation cost over more learning. If budgets were measured in total FLOPs (generation + gradient computation) rather than in steps, the gap between on-policy Lp-Reg and the best off-policy baselines might narrow or reverse. The paper does not provide FLOPs-normalized comparisons.

Furthermore, on-policy training requires re-sampling responses after every gradient update, which incurs additional overhead in distributed training systems (synchronization, data loading, communication) that is not reflected in the per-step GPU-hour accounting. The paper reports total GPU-hours for the runs (8,000 for 14B, 16,000 for 32B standard, 81,204 for the extended 32B run) but does not break down how much of this is generation versus gradient computation, making it difficult for practitioners to estimate the cost of replicating the on-policy results in their own infrastructure.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 reports results for both on-policy and off-policy variants of Lp-Reg, GRPO, and 80/20, allowing within-method comparisons across training regimes. On-policy consistently outperforms off-policy for the same method (Lp-Reg: 60.17% on-policy vs. 57.08% off-policy on 14B; GRPO: 57.43% on-policy vs. 51.13% off-policy). The paper attributes this to the stability advantage of on-policy updates (Section 5.2), not to unequal optimization budgets. However, the off-policy numbers use 8 gradient updates per rollout, meaning they represent a different point on the sample-efficiency curve—it is possible that off-policy performance would improve with more rollouts and fewer gradient updates per rollout, or that on-policy performance would degrade if restricted to the same total FLOPs budget.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not provide FLOPs-matched comparisons or analyze the tradeoff between generation cost and gradient update count. The training settings section (5.1.2) describes the off-policy mini-batch configuration (8 gradient updates per rollout) but does not discuss its implications for efficiency comparisons. A practitioner evaluating whether on-policy Lp-Reg is worth the additional generation cost (re-sampling after every gradient update) versus running more gradient updates on fewer rollouts with an off-policy method has no quantitative guidance from the paper. The 3,000-step extended run (81,204 GPU-hours) is particularly affected by this ambiguity—a substantial fraction of that cost is generation, and it is unknown whether an off-policy method with equivalent total FLOPs (fewer steps, more gradient updates per step) would achieve comparable stability.
