Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (the host of The Peterman Post shares his own story of leaving Meta to pursue passion projects full-time):

**Early career: Amazon vs. Meta**

Ryan spent a few months at Amazon before joining Meta over seven years ago. At Amazon, he worked in a small satellite office on a team that wasn’t using source control, and much of what he built never saw the light of day. The contrast with Meta was immediate and stark: within weeks, he was learning rapidly and trusted to land code that hit production the same day. The people he worked alongside were "incredible." He recalls a specific pattern — when a fix for a site-wide outage seemed impossible, "someone (usually Haixia) dropped a diff that saved the day." That culture of excellent work was inspiring, but the deepest gratitude he holds is for the intrinsic excitement of the work itself: "Working on something you're intrinsically motivated by is one of life's greatest pleasures."

**The podcast begins as a side project**

About a year ago, Ryan started The Peterman Post — a podcast focused on software engineering career stories — as a way to practice speaking and share the narratives that inspired him. Everything felt new, and he was learning constantly. After work, he'd find himself thinking about ways to make the podcast better. The initial goal was modest: post once a month and see how it went. After only a few episodes, two things became clear: people genuinely liked the content, and he enjoyed working on it even more than he'd expected. The project had crossed from curiosity into something deeper. "Every now and then, I’d wonder what it’d be like to work on my passion project full time."

**The financial reality of a passion project**

Ryan is candid about the economics. YouTube has paid $14,994.63 so far, against $17,600 spent on video editing. For some episodes — he singles out the Boris episode — he also paid for travel, hotels, and production crews, adding roughly another $8,000 in costs across in-person recordings. The project has only lost money. He doesn't offer that for sympathy, but to be transparent. The cushion that made it possible was Meta stock: his unvested equity had appreciated significantly, and he used that to fund the podcast. But that same unvested stock also tied him to Meta financially — a tether he'd eventually have to cut.

**The reorg that triggered the decision**

A few months ago, a major reorg at Mark Zuckerberg's level moved Ryan and many engineers in his org. Initially, he was excited. The reorg made structural sense, and his new org was building ML infrastructure — a technical challenge that genuinely interested him. But the more he thought about building up a new team (he was a manager in the new org), the more "off" he felt. He kept returning to thoughts of his passion project and the dream of doing it full time. A specific guilt crystallized: "it felt selfish to build a team and then leave if my podcast kept growing." So before people were moved around, **he decided to go for his dream early** — pulling the trigger before the timing looked fully "responsible." He acknowledges the unvested stock he left behind: "I will miss it, I'm sure, but hopefully it's not something I will think about at the end of my life."

**Two passion projects, six months of runway**

Ryan now has two projects. The first is **The Peterman Pod** itself, which he plans to keep focused on people's career stories — it remains "a great excuse to get to talk to heroes of mine." He names Jeff Dean and John Carmack as dream guests. The second is **Compose**, an ergonomic keyboard project born from personal necessity. He's used ergonomic keyboards for roughly five years due to wrist pain, and after extensive research could never find exactly what he was looking for. His framing: "what I'm building is the ergonomic keyboard that I wish existed." He shares photographs of an actual prototype — not renders, but a physical build.

He has earmarked roughly six months of money at his current burn rate to fund this new direction. If he wants to continue beyond that horizon, he'll need to generate revenue. His immediate idea is to find a sponsor for the podcast, with a clear boundary: "I'll make sure it is genuine and helpful. I won't promote anything I don't use myself." He closes with gratitude, acknowledging that being able to consider working on passion projects full time is only possible because of the audience's support.

---

His decision to **go for his dream early** mirrors the intrinsic motivation he prized at Meta — working on something you're genuinely excited by, even when the financial picture is incomplete. The tension between the unvested stock that funded his podcast and the same stock that tethered him to the job is a through-line: he ultimately chose the shorter runway of Compose and The Peterman Pod over the longer financial horizon of Meta equity, betting that meaning would outweigh the money he left behind. His boundary for sponsorship — "I won't promote anything I don't use myself" — extends that same authenticity into the revenue model he'll need to make the leap sustainable.
