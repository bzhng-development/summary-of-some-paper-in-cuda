Here are the takeaways from this James Cowling interview (CTO at Convex and formerly the most senior engineer at Dropbox, known for building massive-scale storage systems and a philosophy of radical simplicity):

**Beginnings and the PhD as a forge for the mind**

Cowling’s career rests on two parallel threads: abstractions—designing simple models for complex problems—and large-scale transactional systems. He describes transactions as “one of the most incredible abstractions we’ve invented” because they manage concurrency, the hardest problem in computer science. His PhD thesis, Granola, tackled distributed transaction coordination through what he called **independent transactions**. The insight was about eliminating points of coordination to maximize throughput: two parties processing pure functions propose timestamps and choose the maximum to serialize safely, avoiding the blocking of two-phase commit. The work earned a citation in Google’s Spanner paper—then Spanner came out and “everyone kind of forgot about my paper.” His master’s work on Byzantine fault tolerance and an influential paper revisiting View Stamp Replication (an algorithm predating Paxos that later influenced companies like Tiger Beetle) filled out his graduate career.

Cowling frames the PhD not as an extension of college but as a deeply personal developmental crucible. He believes most people shouldn’t do one if they just want to be engineers, but there’s a singular value: “You reach a certain point in time when you have a problem that you’re facing that no one else in the world knows the answer to... you can’t chat about it with folks, you can’t ask your advisor because your advisor doesn’t know either.” This forces a comfort with uncertainty. He feels lucky to have gone through grad school before large language models existed, so he had to learn to think through the discomfort himself. Since leaving academia, his work has been innovative but “our goal was never research. Our goal was just to solve problems.” That distinction—advancing knowledge versus solving problems—is the line he draws between the two worlds.

Testing Granola happened in a charmingly pre-cloud era: a rack of servers at MIT and the communal academic system Planet Lab, which was “continually having problems” as a free-for-all. He’d sleep next to his desk and wake at random hours to check Planet Lab’s status and kick off benchmarks. He notes that Google “ruined systems research” (said with affection) around this time by shifting program committees to expect realistic, Gmail-scale benchmarks that grad students couldn’t produce, favoring papers about giant systems over papers about ideas—a transition he thinks obscured some pure intellectual value.

**Dropbox and the physical reality of exabyte-scale storage**

Cowling joined Dropbox in 2010. One early concept he worked on was **multi-homing**—having data exist authoritatively in multiple locations. He distinguishes between primary-secondary replication (writes commit in one region, replicate lazily to another for business continuity, with a window of potential data loss) and active-active multi-homing (a write isn’t externalized until it lands in all regions, yielding zero downtime if an entire region fails). At Dropbox, the block storage system achieved true multi-region active-active replication. But he delivers a caution that’s become a personal mantra: “systems are all about trade-offs.” The speed of light is fixed; synchronously writing across the US adds ~60 ms to every commit path. His advice to most companies asking how to adopt active-active multi-homing is blunt: “Don’t do it... I would generally recommend most people do not make the trade-off to have partition tolerance or multi-region availability.” That level of investment only makes sense when you’re storing several exabytes for hundreds of millions of customers.

The durability math was extreme. Dropbox advertised “at least 12 nines of durability” to customers, but internal models showed around 24 nines—meaning “according to the model, the universe will be extinct before any data is lost.” This was achieved through **erasure coding**: breaking files into fragments, combining them with a custom Vandermonde encoding matrix, and spreading them across different racks, power feeds, hard drive manufacturers, and regions to account for correlated failure patterns. A file might be split into many fragments, but you could reconstruct it from reading only a subset (e.g., six of nine in a simplified scheme), which actually makes reads faster than non-replicated storage because you fire requests to all fragments and return as soon as the fastest respond. In practice they also kept a local cached copy for low-latency reads near a user’s home region.

The sheer physicality of the work comes through in his stories. They had to calculate how many amps a rack’s circuit breaker could handle based on access patterns. A bad batch of hardware with too-frequent disk failures would cause re-replication to spike, and he’d get messages from the data center team saying “Hey, we’re running the racks really hot right now.” They operated so close to the edge that they built a system called **Trampoline**, which in a worst-case capacity crunch would simply dump 30 petabytes onto S3 as an elastic escape hatch—then move it back when the crisis passed. “You can run your capacity way closer to the edge if you’re willing, under worst-case scenarios, just to dump... on S3 and then move it back when it’s done.”

**The AWS migration, Magic Pocket, and the cascading failure that drove a Rust rewrite**

The project to migrate off Amazon S3—the storage system code-named **Magic Pocket**—had been a desire since before Cowling arrived. It was driven strategically (owning the file system in the file-sync-and-share category) and financially (massive cost savings pre-IPO). Amazon had offered “very, very, very good discounted rates,” but ultimately Dropbox built a system more efficient than S3 for its specific workload. He calls it “a $1 billion project” and “the largest ever data migration in history at that time.” They used experimental **Shingled Magnetic Recording** disks and operated off draft specs from manufacturers. Amazon noticed the exodus—at one point peering bandwidth between Amazon and Dropbox hit 764 gigabits per second, and Cowling received a “slightly nasty email” calling the pattern of massive reads and few writes “super weird.” He didn’t reply.

The system’s architecture shifted through languages. The prototype was in Python (which he defends as “pretty efficient for I/O”). Then they moved to Go before it hit general availability. But Go’s runtime and unpredictable memory usage became a liability in a storage system where an out-of-memory error looks like a disk failure. A real failure cascade occurred when a band (he thinks De La Soul) released an album on Dropbox, causing a spike in load that made nodes OOM. Those nodes restarted, which looked like disk failures, which triggered re-replication across many replicas—turning “one fire hose worth of load” into “seven fire hoses worth of load” and creating a **congestion collapse** scenario. They rewrote the storage nodes in Rust (also pre-GA), ripped out the file system layer on the disks entirely, and directly addressed the disk heads using a new instruction set called ZBC (Zone Block Control). The resulting system, **Discotech**, shifted the cost pie chart so that “almost all” the money went to disks themselves.

The migration project was built by a team of only three to six engineers. Cowling’s core principle was a “very thick layer of validation checks” combined with a **dark launch**: double-writing data in both S3 and Magic Pocket, with a six-month clock that would reset to zero if any incident or data loss occurred. When a bug slipped through to production, he went to the VP and said they were resetting the clock at a cost of “double-digit millions.” The response was “great, thank you, that’s good, I trust you”—a moment he cites as exemplary of Dropbox’s cultural values. He also describes the **FMEA** (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis) process: a giant spreadsheet enumerating every bad thing that could happen—from a truck crash that delayed server deliveries by six weeks to existential threats like a data center fire—and designing protections around each.

**The gospel of simplicity and the war against complexity-driven incentives**

Cowling’s central design philosophy is that “simple systems are way harder to design than complex systems.” The highest compliment: when after the fact, people say “Oh, isn’t that the obvious way of doing it?” At Dropbox, he cites the block-index database: a cluster of 1,000 MySQL nodes, indexed by block ID, mapping blocks to disk locations. Every new hire from academia or other companies would say “this is not very sophisticated—you could use a Patricia trie or a distributed hash table.” His counter: because the mapping lives in one giant table, “if I want to validate what happened, if I want to check all the data is where it’s meant to be, I just walk over the table and check.” **Designing for validation is very important. Designing for understanding is very important.** Simplicity scales not just in queries per second but in years—simple systems survive feature additions, requirement changes, and team turnover; over-optimized, complex systems do not.

He becomes visibly frustrated discussing promotion systems that reward complexity. The incentives at large companies can be perverse: people chase OKR checkmarks disconnected from real problem-solving, build things because “the VP told me to,” and produce systems where the engineer doesn’t even know who the user is. “The best engineering comes from a deep understanding of why.” At Convex, the mantra is “everything exists for the why. Don’t build a fancy load balancer unless it’s needed.” He pleads with engineers stuck in such environments to fight the nihilism: “I do see a lot of nihilism, a lot of defeatedness... But I think it does matter. If I think of the happiest times in my life, it’s been dedicating myself to a cause... not trying to get promoted, just trying to do the right thing and then assuming I’m going to get promoted. If not, go somewhere else.”

He introduces the concept of **system bias**: teams oriented around defending their own system rather than solving the problem the system was meant to address. His concrete fix at Dropbox: the day Magic Pocket shipped, he renamed the team from “Magic Pocket Team” to “Storage Team,” physically renaming emails, channels, repos. “It’s the job of the storage team to advocate for moving back [to S3]” if that ever made sense. He compares it to a “Puppet team” that advocates for Puppet instead of for infrastructure management. Orienting identity around a problem, not a system, removes the threat to one’s career from even considering alternatives.

**Leadership: why “leading by example” is a trap and the oversight-accountability slider**

Cowling argues that leading by example is “a very passive thing to do.” His own early attempt: he wanted his team to respond quickly to pages, so he’d be first to every incident, writing up reports, jumping on bugs. But from the team’s perspective, “all they see is that the lead is just doing all these jobs, and they don’t know... ‘Maybe that’s James’s job.’” Passive modeling doesn’t work; you have to explicitly give people ownership. He describes a **slider between oversight and accountability**. When someone is new, you’re in oversight mode. As you dial oversight down, you must dial accountability up: explicit conversations where you say, “Okay, cool, you’ve got this project. Let me know when it’s going to get done. What’s the plan? It’s on you.” Most people genuinely like accountability; they like owning their work.

He also warns against becoming the micromanaging bottleneck—a trajectory “almost every high-achieving leader goes through.” The art is figuring out which tasks are **paper cuts** (safe to let someone fail at and learn from) versus arm-losing risks (where failure would be catastrophic). Let people experience small failures and feed that back into their growth.

For broader organizational dynamics, his core principle is **100% alignment on the why**. Most conflict is well-intentioned people debating how to do something while holding different assumptions about why they’re doing it. At Convex—and he argues at any good engineering culture—you don’t lead through authority. A senior person should “use the benefit of their experience to be able to articulate, to win the hearts and minds.” You can’t change someone’s mind in a meeting; you have to identify the disagreement, provide information, and let it sit so the person can reflect and come back a week later. “It doesn’t matter how smart you are, it doesn’t matter how right you are; are you effective?”

**Mentoring the most senior: finding your own brand and the human side**

Mentoring staff-plus engineers moves beyond standard feedback patterns into helping each person “become the best version of themselves.” There is no archetype for a senior principal engineer; you have to be your own brand. Cowling’s brand is strategic collaboration, simplicity, and abstraction; his coding “fell off a cliff.” Others are the deep-science problem-solvers. His role is to help people identify strengths and become “more spiky,” while simultaneously working on the interpersonal and organizational understanding that many deeply technical people still lack. He has to remind senior engineers constantly that “you can’t change someone’s mind in a meeting”—the ego and neurological reality don’t work that way. The hardest problems at that level involve getting 10 or 20 people to work together.

**Career advice for the AI era: train your mind and reject the doomerism**

Cowling is blunt about the market: “There is incredible demand in Silicon Valley for senior engineers, and it is getting harder for junior engineers to succeed and grow.” Architecture, simplicity, and design are still the domain of human beings. He rejects the idea that not knowing engineering is somehow an advantage. “Software engineering is an intellectual discipline that helps you think... It’s like doing weights with your mind.” He acknowledges the **passivity trap** of agentic tools: if you let the robot lift the weight for you, you don’t grow. His advice: when facing a new problem, “try to think of a solution yourself and then go check with a large language model.” He admits he’s personally struggled with the same thing—finding it harder to read books because his brain has been “fried by the stimulation economy.”

He frames a thought experiment for junior engineers who believe AGI makes learning irrelevant: In one year, imagine AGI arrives. Two people face that year—one who gave up on problem-solving and just fed the machine, and one who kept training their mind. “Who is going to be better placed for the future? The person who’s been training their mind.” He compares it to proving a theorem in a math degree that’s already been proven; the point is the mental development. He also notes, with some exasperation, that the AI labs themselves still do whiteboard coding interviews and are “desperately hiring engineers.” He wishes his industry peers would “stop it with the real doomer, human elimination kind of narrative” and instead get behind the idea of enabling people to do more cool things. He dismisses the permanent-underclass anxiety and “tech tabloidism”—obsessing over every new model release or Jensen Huang quote—as noise: “It’s like reading about Beyoncé, but you’re a nerd... Just be building stuff. Just do real work.”

**Starting Convex: the abstraction floor rises again**

Cowling left Dropbox after eight years, amicably, feeling he had outgrown what he could learn there. Convex started pre-agentic-era from an observation: the real differentiator in a project’s success is the quality of its abstractions, and the hardest problem in engineering is **distributed state management**—storing state reliably and reasoning about concurrent modifications. Convex is a transactional database where transactions are written in TypeScript as stored procedures, with serializable isolation and automatic client-side reactivity. The platform’s eventual value in the agentic era was an accident of timing: “What humans find hard is also what agents find hard.” Coding agents struggle with large codebases, action at a distance, and race conditions—exactly the problems Convex’s abstractions make disappear.

He describes Convex as “a new operating system in some respects,” with its own distributed database tracking read and write ranges for efficient WebSocket subscriptions. They’re currently building lower-level primitives—singletons, **fork**-like APIs for background processes—to support million-job background workloads. A fork in this context means a background singleton that processes aggregates, goes to sleep on a condition variable, and wakes only when new work arrives, rather than constantly polling. He finds this design work “super hard” in a way that Claude can’t help with, which he loves. His framing: “Engineering is science with constraints. I’m not particularly interested in constraint-free environments. That’s art. I like craft and engineering.”

**Personal arc, Silicon Valley consulting, and the cost of obsession**

His most intellectually stimulating work wasn’t the PhD modeling but “working on a very large storage system with a team where things were going wrong... where the rubber hits the road.” He was a paid consultant ($400 total, for visa reasons) for the TV show *Silicon Valley*, starting in season two. Mike Judge had been a software engineer himself, and the show was fanatical about technical accuracy—farming real stories from companies, building realistic rack diagrams, and weaving actual industry betrayals into plotlines. “A lot of people I know can’t watch that show because it’s just so creepily accurate.”

His career regret isn’t a missed billionaire opportunity (he, like many veterans, has several of those stories), but underinvesting in his personal life. “It’s been a lot of sacrifice, to be blunt. I’ve been on call my whole career. I’ve carried a laptop almost every day.” He cautions against performative hustle culture. His advice to his younger self: “It’ll be okay. Don’t sweat the small stuff as much... careers are long. You probably won’t, ideally, max out your growth for 20-plus years as an engineer.” The 22-year-old billionaire stories are “not real, not repeatable, not that healthy.” He doesn’t read many tech books—after the PhD, “most of my learning has been by doing”—and believes there’s nothing like a real problem to grow as an engineer.

---

Across Cowling’s career, a few themes echo relentlessly: simplicity as the hardest and most valuable design goal, the primacy of solving real problems over chasing artificial incentives, and the conviction that engineering is fundamentally a discipline for training the mind—one that demands deliberate, uncomfortable practice rather than outsourcing to the latest tool. His story is a reminder that the most durable systems, teams, and careers are built not on complexity or credentialism, but on a deep, almost obsessive commitment to doing the right thing, aligning everyone on the *why*, and never mistaking the noise of tech tabloidism for the real work of building.
