Here are the takeaways from this Rong Yan interview (went from PhD researcher to Senior Director at Snapchat in 3 years, now CTO/co-founder of HeyGen, guided by a decades-long North Star to build an AI-first company):

**Joining Facebook — choosing engineering over research**

Rong Yan’s career begins with an intentional fork. After a PhD in computer vision and machine learning and three years as a research scientist at IBM, he concluded the industrial research model—a 50/50 mix of research and engineering—was unsustainable. Around 2009 he faced three paths: become a faculty member at a top school, join a quantitative trading firm, or go into software engineering. He chose engineering because he wanted to “create things that will influence more people than just doing trading,” and he wanted a place where engineering was a first-class citizen. In the financial world, “engineering is always second class.” Facebook fit both criteria, and it was still a fast-growing smaller company in the early 2010s. The move was non-conventional among his IBM Research peers, who mostly went into academia or other industrial labs. “I think it was the right call,” he says—a pattern of deliberately ignoring conventional wisdom that recurs through his whole story.

**Job hopping into a director role at Square**

When he joined Square, he took a director-level offer directly—he didn’t get promoted into it. Square, being smaller, was willing to give him a chance to perform at that level. He started with a team of about 25 and grew it to over 50 within a year, building out machine learning and data science infrastructure. The transition from line manager to director taught him the core distinction: **directing vs. managing**. A director is “about directing instead of managing”—setting strategic direction, collecting resources across departments, and solving the hardest organizational problems. It’s a different philosophy from managing ICs. “It took me some time to adjust,” but after that exercise he felt he’d become a better manager at a different altitude. He’s transparent that he didn’t get promoted into the role; he was hired into it, and the company’s smaller size made that possible—but the on-the-job learning is what cemented the skills.

**The director skill gap — earning trust when you’re the new layer**

The hardest gap early on was being a director in a new environment where he was the *junior* person in the company, yet leading senior people who knew far more detail than he did. He candidly says, “I don’t think I did well in the first half a year.” His people started wondering why the layer existed if he wasn’t adding value. The turning point came when he reframed his job around what his direct reports needed: “I started to understand what they are looking for, and then I align my work towards that.” He analogizes it to being a psychological doctor—diagnosing what’s blocking someone’s growth and addressing it. Some of his reports had hoped to be promoted into his role, and the way to handle that resentment was to help them see his arrival was actually a *better* situation for their careers, not a blocker: “you need to find common ground between you and your direct report, so you are not a blocker for their career, but actually a promoter for their career.”

His playbook for building a team in a new leadership role centers on one concept: **trust as the foundational layer**. He cites Patrick Lencioni’s *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team*, noting the most foundational layer for a successful team is trust. So the first thing he does in any new role is build trust relationships—people have to genuinely believe he’s there to help them. Then he executes a few concrete things to prove it, especially unblocking problems they couldn’t unblock themselves. That visibly demonstrates the value of having upper management in place. The early underperformance itself taught him the difference between a director and a manager: a manager knows the details best; a director in a new org is the *junior* person in domain knowledge and must provide value through strategic direction and organizational unblocking instead.

**The North Star strategy — filling skill gaps intentionally**

The through-line of his career moves is a **North Star goal** he set years ago: to become the CTO of an AI company. He reverse-engineered what he was missing from that goal. Coming from a research background, he had gaps in understanding industry operations, infrastructure, backend, frontend, and product. “I need to choose my career to help me fill those gaps step by step.” Before Snapchat, he was always in data and machine learning—every position was related to data and ML.

His most striking example came at Snapchat, where he had two director offers on the table—Director of Data or Director of Camera. The data role was the comfortable, obvious choice given his entire background. He intentionally chose Camera to learn iOS and Android programming from scratch, spending two months just learning iOS. “I think this is a very unconventional choice,” he acknowledges, but the North Star required understanding product and working with PMs and designers. Traditional intra-company progression makes it hard to jump from backend to product-facing teams; he saw this as a deliberate opportunity to challenge himself. The first two years at Snapchat were the happiest period of his career precisely because he was learning new things every day—“I really enjoyed that time. I was learning new things and developing my knowledge base every single day.”

Ryan notes that most people define career strategy around external goals—maximizing level or compensation—while Rong designed around a personal mission. Rong says there’s no right or wrong, but his litmus test is: “If I look back at my career and feel regret for not doing these things, I’d rather do it now.” That future-regret filter is the heuristic that cuts through conventional wisdom for him.

**Domain knowledge — the Facebook bootcamp imprint on leadership**

A formative culture shock from his Facebook days shapes how he leads today. Facebook required every engineering hire, including VP-level hires, to go through a six-week bootcamp fixing bugs and writing pull requests. He vividly recalls “a VP-level hire sitting right next to me, doing the same things I was doing: finding bugs, fixing bugs, writing pull requests. She was doing that for six weeks.” At IBM, VPs never coded; “they probably only write PPTs at the end.” The experience ingrained in him that **being technical and data-driven is critical for success at any level**. He still writes code and reviews code every week—“at least two or three paragraphs”—because he’s not comfortable making strategic decisions without understanding the details. “I want to make sure I understand the details so that I know I’m not making things up.”

To scale himself, he uses **first-principles weekly triage**: every Monday he identifies the top three things he must achieve and only focuses on those. Sometimes one of those three is deep-diving into technical details. He recalibrates themes against his North Star, acknowledging that everyone has the same hours; “the best people are not just spending more time; they’re really good at allocating their time. They understand the priority of each direction and spend the right time on each priority.”

**LA vs. SF — a broader view of users and the world**

Snapchat’s culture, he found, was surprisingly similar to Facebook’s—“moving fast, breaking things, getting things done. Done is better than perfect”—because many early employees came from the Bay Area or Seattle. But living in Los Angeles broadened his perspective in a way Silicon Valley couldn’t. In the Bay Area, every dinner conversation was about tech stocks and startups; “the topics were almost always the same.” In LA, his neighbors included a medical doctor and Michael Jackson’s cryptographer. That exposure helped him understand that “a lot of your users are not just techies or AI people”—they’re ordinary people who can still benefit from your product, which shapes how you should build.

**Scaling Snapchat — growing from 100 to 3,000 people**

He joined Snapchat as roughly the 100th employee, and the company grew to about 3,000 in two years. His own team grew to 250 engineers organically alongside the company. The rapid scaling was what he calls a “painful period for fast growth.” His key lesson: **every time you double the team, it’s almost like building a new company**. The culture and processes for a 100-person company are entirely different from those for a 3,000-person company, and cultures “start to clash with each other, and in a good way.” Leadership has to be “very resilient and adaptive” rather than holding onto old ways. Seeing how to grow a small team into a large one, and what processes had to be introduced, was itself a major growth experience for him.

**Evan Spiegel — pixel-level bar and personal connection**

Working closely with Evan Spiegel, two traits stood out. First, an extraordinarily high bar for product, “even at the pixel level”—Spiegel would point out pixel-level issues in demos, which reminded Rong of Steve Jobs. That led to his rule: “if we’re building a demo, we need to build an EverReady demo, not a normal demo.” Second, Spiegel placed unusual weight on personal connections with engineers. He once told a leader that “no matter what happens, you should never fire the first 15 engineers in the company because they are the founding members.” Years after Snapchat moved out of its original Blue House, Spiegel bought the house back and started hosting board meetings there. Rong connects this to the product itself: “Snapchat inherently is a tool that helps people build more intimate connections and create conversations with each other.”

**Recruiting at higher levels — personal connections and radical transparency**

He has never proactively looked for a job; everything has come inbound through personal connections or recruiter outreach. He prioritizes personal connections because he expects every company to go through up cycles and down cycles. “To go through a down cycle, I want to work with people I like and think alike with so we can work together as a team.” He evaluates far more seriously anything that comes through personal connections, and his advice is that “building professional network connections early is a very useful exercise. You never know when these opportunities will come, but you want to capture them when they arrive.”

When evaluating roles, he doesn’t optimize for team size or company valuation. In fact, “I actually want fewer people to report to me right now.” Everything is anchored against the North Star. He’s also grown skeptical of titles: “levels and titles are things the company gives you; they are not yours.” He watched senior VPs fade from relevance the moment they left their companies, remembered only as “ex-VP of something.” What fulfills him is being remembered for building a product that made lives better. “I’m waiting for the time when two people can make a $1 billion company, and it’s probably going to come pretty soon. You may not have any direct reports at that time, and that’s totally okay because I’m making a lot of impact.”

His recruiting philosophy is **radical transparency**. He tells candidates explicitly that there’s only a 1% chance of success, because he believes the winning strategy is honest upfront expectation-setting. “When you go to a battlefield with soldiers, you want all soldiers to understand the difficulties they’re going to face, but they’re pumped for it.” He’s filtering for the 1–5% of people who truly want to go on that journey, wanting them to know the truth so they won’t “leave the company when they start to see some slight difficulties.”

**On promotions and luck — control what you can control**

His advice on career growth has a clear center of gravity: **don’t make promotions the objective**. He’s mentored people who examine every bullet point of the next level’s criteria and ask their managers when they’ll get promoted. “That’s very wrong.” When you chase promotions, you tie your happiness to something you don’t fully control—especially as a manager, where organizational structure and company strategy heavily constrain growth. “The only thing you can do is to do your best in the positions you have, and everything else will be lagging indicators.” Control the things you can control: make an impact in your current role, drive toward work you love long-term, and avoid future regret. If luck brings promotions, great. If not, “sometimes something will come afterward. You can even find your own company at some point and maybe even be richer at that time.”

**Advice to a younger self: ignore conventional wisdom**

If he could go back to his just-graduated self, he’d say: “You don’t need to always follow conventional wisdom.” He chose CMU over Princeton against his father’s advice because CMU was stronger in computer science—it was the right call. He joined Facebook when almost no one from the research community would consider a small software engineering company (“Fair was created back in 2014, but five years earlier, it was very difficult for researchers to reset their careers and join a software engineering company like that”). Those contrarian choices, anchored to a clear North Star, let him “see new opportunities that no one else can see” and increased his odds of winning the long game. The path is less comfortable, but “only people who can think differently early” get those asymmetric outcomes.

On the one regret he’d name: his career progression wasn’t as smooth as it could have been. If he’d known he’d end up in software engineering, he might not have done the PhD. But the larger lesson is that you can’t predict what will happen in ten years—“I cannot even predict what AI is going to do in the next three months.” The real skill is never taking anything for granted, being able to learn new knowledge quickly, and treating short-term setbacks as adjustment moments rather than defeats: “this is just a moment for adjustment. Learn how to adjust, and you’ll be a better self afterward.”

---

His **North Star** strategy—reverse-engineering a CTO role and deliberately taking the uncomfortable Camera job to fill skill gaps—is the engine behind every unconventional move. It pairs with his **trust as the foundational layer** playbook from Lencioni, which he uses to turn skeptical direct reports into allies, and his **radical transparency** recruiting philosophy that filters for the 1% who sign up for the real odds. Together they form a career philosophy where you don’t chase promotions (they’re lagging indicators you can’t control) but instead make every decision pass the future-regret test: “If I look back at my career and feel regret for not doing these things, I’d rather do it now.” The bootcamp imprint—that even VPs must stay technical and data-driven—and the first-principles weekly triage are how he scales that philosophy into daily practice.
