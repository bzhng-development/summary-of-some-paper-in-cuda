## 1. Executive Summary

This paper analyzes the root cause of activation outliers that make transformers difficult to quantize, establishing that these outliers arise because attention heads learn a **"no-op"** behavior—attempting to avoid updating the residual stream by allocating nearly all attention probability to low-information tokens (e.g., delimiter tokens like `[SEP]` in BERT or background patches in ViT), which forces the softmax inputs to grow large and propagate extreme values through the network. Based on this insight, the paper proposes two architectural modifications—**clipped softmax** (which stretches and clips softmax outputs to allow exact zeros with finite input range) and **gated attention** (which adds an explicit learned gating mechanism to nullify attention head outputs without requiring extreme activations)—that are applied during pre-training from scratch. Evaluated on BERT-base, OPT-125m, and ViT-S/16, both methods reduce the maximum infinity norm of attention outputs by up to ~35× (from ~735 to ~20 for BERT) and bring W8A8 post-training quantization perplexity/accuracy within ~0.05 perplexity or ~1% accuracy of the floating-point baseline, compared to catastrophic degradation under vanilla pre-training, establishing that full INT8 activation quantization is achievable without post-hoc fixes only when the architecture provides a native mechanism for attention heads to produce exact or near-exact zero updates without relying on extreme softmax input magnitudes.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Transformers Learn Activation Outliers That Break Quantization

The specific problem this paper addresses is that modern transformer models, when pre-trained using standard recipes, consistently develop **extreme outlier values in their activations**—values that are orders of magnitude larger than typical activation magnitudes—which makes it effectively impossible to quantize those activations to 8-bit integers without catastrophic accuracy degradation. The paper frames this not as a minor implementation inconvenience but as a fundamental tension between the standard transformer architecture and the requirements of numerical quantization.

To understand why this matters, we need to grasp what happens during activation quantization. As described in Section 2, post-training quantization (PTQ) takes a pre-trained floating-point model and converts its weights and activations to low-bit integer representations using a uniform affine quantization scheme:

$$\hat{x} = q(x; s, z, b) = s \cdot \left( \text{clip}\left( \left\lfloor \frac{x}{s} \right\rceil + z; 0, 2^b - 1 \right) - z \right)$$

where $s$ is the scale factor (step size), $z$ is the zero point, and $b$ is the bitwidth. The critical decision is choosing $s$—the quantization range—because it determines the tradeoff between **clipping error** (values that fall outside the representable range are truncated) and **rounding error** (values within range are mapped to the nearest quantization level, losing precision proportional to the step size). When an activation tensor contains both a massive outlier (say, a value of 735 in a distribution where most values are below 10) and many small-but-important values, there is no good choice of $s$: a range large enough to contain the outlier makes the step size so coarse that small values get crushed to zero or lose all precision, while a range optimized for the small values clips the outlier, which prior work has shown catastrophically degrades model performance because these outliers are concentrated in specific hidden dimensions that play crucial functional roles (Kovaleva et al., 2021; Puccetti et al., 2021).

This is the **range-precision dilemma** that the paper identifies in Section 2: for per-tensor quantization, there is frequently "no good trade-off ... between the rounding and clipping error, resulting in an overall high error." The existence of outliers means that per-tensor INT8 quantization of activations—the simplest, most hardware-efficient scheme—produces unusable models.

### Why This Problem Matters: Quantization Is the Path to Efficient Deployment

The practical significance of this problem is enormous and multi-dimensional. The paper contextualizes it against the backdrop of transformer models' rapid growth: as models scale from BERT-base (109M parameters) to OPT-1.3B and beyond, the computational and memory costs of inference become prohibitive for all but the most well-resourced deployments. Quantization—specifically, reducing both weights and activations to INT8—offers several compounding benefits that the paper implicitly relies on but doesn't belabor because they're well-established in the quantization literature:

**Computational throughput:** INT8 matrix multiplications can be executed 2–4× faster than FP16/FP32 on modern hardware with specialized integer arithmetic units. This directly translates to lower latency and higher throughput for deployed models.

**Memory bandwidth reduction:** Halving activation precision from FP16 to INT8 halves the data movement between memory and compute units. Since transformer inference is typically memory-bandwidth-bound (particularly for autoregressive decoding where each token requires loading the entire model), this can yield near-linear speedups.

**Energy efficiency:** Fixed-point integer operations consume significantly less energy than floating-point operations (Horowitz, 2014; cited in the paper), which is critical for battery-constrained edge devices and for reducing the carbon footprint of large-scale inference.

**Enabling edge deployment:** The paper is authored by Qualcomm AI Research, and this institutional context matters: Qualcomm's business is mobile and edge computing hardware. Running large transformers on-device—for privacy, latency, or offline-capability reasons—requires aggressive quantization. The inability to quantize activations without complex workarounds is a direct barrier to deploying transformers on the Snapdragon-class hardware that Qualcomm produces.

The paper frames the problem's importance most clearly through a single sentence in the abstract that captures the cumulative frustration of the field: "To retain acceptable performance, the existence of these outliers requires activations to be in higher bitwidth or the use of different numeric formats, extra fine-tuning, or other workarounds." Each of these workarounds carries a cost—higher bitwidth defeats the purpose of quantization; different numeric formats (e.g., FP8) require specialized hardware not universally available; extra fine-tuning requires access to training pipelines and data, defeating the "post-training" promise of PTQ.

### Prior Approaches: Treating the Symptom, Not the Cause

The paper positions itself against a landscape of methods that have attempted to make transformer quantization work *despite* outliers, rather than preventing outliers from forming in the first place. Section 2 and the related work discussion categorize these prior approaches into several families, each with distinct drawbacks:

#### Finer Quantization Granularity

The most prominent approach is **per-channel or group-wise quantization**, notably LLM.int8() (Dettmers et al., 2022), which quantizes different columns or groups of the activation tensor with separate scale factors rather than using a single scale for the entire tensor. The key insight of LLM.int8() is that outliers are concentrated in a small, fixed set of feature dimensions (e.g., 6–7 dimensions out of 4096), so those dimensions can be kept in FP16 while the remaining ~99.9% of dimensions are quantized to INT8. This works—the paper acknowledges that Dettmers et al. showed "for big enough transformer-based language models they [outliers] start appearing after every linear layer"—but it requires:

- **Custom hardware support** for mixed-precision matrix multiplication, where different columns of the same matrix use different number formats. This is not natively supported on most inference accelerators and requires specialized CUDA kernels.
- Careful handling of the **cross-product interaction** between outlier and non-outlier dimensions during matrix multiplication.
- The fundamental inefficiency that even though only a few dimensions are problematic, the entire inference pipeline must accommodate the mixed-precision scheme.

SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022) takes a different approach: it mathematically migrates the quantization difficulty from activations to weights through a per-channel scaling transformation. If activations have outliers in certain channels, SmoothQuant scales those channels down and compensates by scaling the corresponding weight channels up, exploiting the fact that weights are easier to quantize (they're static and can be calibrated offline). This is clever but still a post-hoc fix: it requires analyzing the trained model, computing optimal scaling factors, and transforming the weights, adding complexity to the deployment pipeline.

#### Higher Bitwidth or Mixed Precision for Outlier Channels

Some approaches keep activations at higher bitwidth specifically for outlier-prone layers or dimensions. The paper cites methods that "require parts of the activations to still be in higher bit-widths, reducing the effectiveness of the activation quantization." This is effective as a band-aid but undermines the core motivation of quantization: if some activations must remain at FP16, the hardware must support mixed-precision throughout the network, and the speedup is limited by the slowest-precision operations (Amdahl's law).

#### Extra Fine-Tuning (QAT or Knowledge Distillation)

Quantization-aware training (QAT) methods (Bhalgat et al., 2020; Esser et al., 2020) incorporate simulated quantization into the training loop, allowing the model to adapt its weights to minimize quantization error. Other approaches use knowledge distillation (Kim et al., 2022) where a full-precision teacher guides a quantized student. These methods can produce excellent results but require:

- Access to the full training pipeline, data, and compute budget—which may not be available to downstream users of pre-trained models.
- Significant additional training time (often multiple epochs), eliminating the "post-training" simplicity that makes PTQ attractive.
- Model-specific recipes that may not transfer across architectures or tasks.

#### Other PTQ Methods with Calibration

The broader PTQ landscape includes methods that optimize the quantization parameters (scale factors, rounding decisions) using small calibration datasets. These include MSE-based range estimation (Banner et al., 2018; Choukroun et al., 2019), adaptive rounding (Nagel et al., 2020), and layer-wise calibration (Hubara et al., 2020). The paper doesn't dismiss these—in fact, it uses MSE range estimation for some of its own low-bit experiments (Appendix B.7)—but argues that they are fundamentally limited when outliers create an intractable range-precision tradeoff. No amount of clever scale factor optimization can recover precision that is lost because a single outlier forced the quantization step size to be 50× larger than what the bulk of the distribution needs.

### The Gap: Nobody Has Asked WHY Outliers Appear

The critical distinction the paper draws is methodological: all prior work has treated outliers as an external constraint to be worked around, not as a phenomenon to be explained and prevented. The paper states this explicitly in the introduction: "In this paper, we set out to solve the transformer outlier problem entirely by changing the architecture of the network itself. We hope to make transformers easy to quantize from the get-go without needing any post-processing. To do so, we thoroughly analyze why these outliers appear."

This framing matters because it shifts the research question from "how can we quantize despite outliers?" to "how can we train transformers that don't produce outliers in the first place?" The paper positions itself as addressing the **root cause** rather than the **symptoms**. This is a higher-risk, higher-reward approach: if the causal mechanism can be identified and architecturally disrupted, all downstream quantization becomes trivially easy, obviating the need for the complex workarounds catalogued above. If the mechanism is incorrectly identified or the architectural fix impairs model quality on non-quantization metrics, the approach fails.

### Conflicting Observations in Prior Work (Implicit Motivation)

Several prior observations provide the intellectual scaffolding for the paper's investigation, though the paper doesn't always cite them for this purpose:

**Bondarenko et al. (2021)** established that outliers in BERT are concentrated in specific hidden dimensions and appear consistently across sequences and layers, with the strongest outliers at the FFN output in later encoder layers. This pattern of spatial and inter-layer consistency suggests a structural rather than accidental cause.

**Kovaleva et al. (2021)** and **Puccetti et al. (2021)** demonstrated that these outlier dimensions are functionally important—zeroing them out or clipping their values significantly degrades model performance—which rules out the simple solution of just clamping outliers during inference. The model has learned to depend on these extreme values.

**Clark et al. (2019)** and **Kovaleva et al. (2019)** had previously observed that some BERT attention heads attend almost exclusively to delimiter tokens like `[SEP]` and periods, and speculated that this might function as a "no-op" when the attention head's computation is not applicable to the current input. The paper directly builds on this observation, but goes further by making the causal connection to outliers: the "no-op" behavior, through the mathematical properties of softmax, residual connections, and LayerNorm, *necessarily* produces outliers as a byproduct.

**Dettmers et al. (2022)** showed that the outlier phenomenon scales with model size—in larger models, outliers appear after every linear layer (not just FFN outputs), suggesting the underlying mechanism is fundamental to the transformer architecture and becomes more pronounced with scale.

What was missing from this body of work was a unified causal explanation that connected the attention pattern observations to the quantization problem. Individual papers had noticed parts of the puzzle—attention heads attending to delimiters, outliers in FFN outputs, the scaling of outliers with model size—but no one had traced the full causal chain: **attention head wants to do nothing → softmax requires extreme inputs to produce near-zero outputs → extreme inputs come from previous layer's FFN output → LayerNorm normalizes away the extremes so the FFN must produce even larger values → outliers appear.** This paper supplies that missing causal chain, and the architectural modifications it proposes follow directly from it.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning can be understood along three axes:

**Architectural versus post-hoc:** Unlike LLM.int8(), SmoothQuant, QAT, or knowledge distillation methods that accept outliers as given and develop workarounds, this paper changes the architecture so that outliers never form. This is a **preventative** rather than **remedial** approach. The tradeoff is that it requires training from scratch (or at least fine-tuning, as explored in Appendix B.6) rather than being applicable to already-trained models.

**Mechanism-driven rather than empirical:** The paper's modifications are not the result of a hyperparameter sweep over architectural variants but are direct consequences of the causal hypothesis. If the hypothesis is correct—that outliers arise because softmax cannot produce exact zeros without infinite inputs, and the model needs exact zeros for "no-op" behavior—then two solutions follow logically: (1) modify softmax so it can output exact zeros with finite inputs (clipped softmax), or (2) provide an alternative mechanism for producing zero outputs that bypasses softmax entirely (gated attention). The paper presents these as two realizations of the same insight rather than competing alternatives.

**General across modalities and architectures:** The paper explicitly tests on three different model types (encoder-only BERT, decoder-only OPT, vision transformer ViT) and two modalities (text and images) to demonstrate that the outlier mechanism is universal to the transformer attention block rather than specific to language modeling or a particular architecture variant. The paper states in Section 3: "This happens for language and vision transformers and different specific transformer architectures." This universality claim is important because it suggests the proposed fixes should generalize broadly. The only failure case—clipped softmax on OPT—is explicitly acknowledged and left as an open question, which strengthens the paper's credibility.

**Post-training quantization as the evaluation target:** The paper deliberately uses the simplest PTQ pipeline (per-tensor quantization with min-max or MSE range estimation, no fine-tuning, no knowledge distillation) as its evaluation protocol. This is a conscious choice: if the architectural modifications work, they should make quantization trivially easy, so the simplest PTQ method should suffice. The fact that W8A8 perplexity with clipped softmax on BERT (4.52) is essentially identical to FP16 perplexity (4.39) while the vanilla model's W8A8 perplexity explodes to 1294 validates this "make it easy" philosophy. The paper is not claiming to advance PTQ methodology; it's claiming that the right architectural choices make PTQ methodology almost irrelevant.

### Summary of the Motivation Arc

The intellectual arc of the paper's motivation is:

1. **Observation:** Transformers are hard to quantize because of activation outliers (well-established by prior work).
2. **Gap:** Nobody has explained *why* these outliers emerge during training.
3. **Hypothesis:** Outliers are a necessary byproduct of attention heads learning "no-op" behavior through the softmax—the softmax mathematically cannot produce exact zeros without infinite inputs, so the network produces ever-larger activations to approximate zeros.
4. **Implication:** If you give the attention mechanism a way to produce zero updates without requiring extreme softmax inputs, outliers should disappear.
5. **Validation:** The paper proposes two such mechanisms (clipped softmax and gated attention), trains models from scratch with them, and demonstrates that outliers are indeed dramatically reduced and quantization becomes trivial—all while maintaining or improving floating-point task performance.

This motivation structure is compelling because it is both explanatory (it tells us *why* transformers behave this way) and constructive (the explanation directly suggests architectural fixes). The paper's contribution is not just the methods themselves but the mechanistic understanding that makes the methods obvious in retrospect.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper proposes two simple, independent architectural modifications to the standard transformer self-attention mechanism—**clipped softmax** and **gated attention**—that can be dropped into the attention layer before pre-training a transformer from scratch. The problem they solve is that standard transformers learn to produce massive activation outliers during training because the softmax function mathematically cannot output exact zeros without receiving infinite-magnitude inputs; the solution is to give the model a way to produce exact (or near-exact) zero attention outputs using only finite, well-behaved input values, which fundamentally eliminates the training dynamic that creates outliers in the first place.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has three major components that interact in a causal chain spanning adjacent transformer layers:

1. **The causal mechanism** (Section 3, the hypothesis)—a feedback loop spanning two consecutive layers: an attention head in layer $L+1$ wants to produce a "no-op" (zero update to the residual stream for certain tokens), which requires the softmax to output near-zero probabilities for all tokens carrying information; the softmax achieves this by making one input dominate all others, which requires that input to be extremely large relative to others; that extreme input originates from the FFN output in the preceding layer $L$; LayerNorm in layer $L+1$ partially normalizes the extreme value, so the FFN in layer $L$ must produce an even larger value to "survive" the normalization; this creates a positive feedback loop that produces ever-larger outliers.

2. **Clipped softmax** (Section 4.1)—a drop-in replacement for the standard softmax that stretches its output range from $(0, 1)$ to $(\gamma, \zeta)$ with $\gamma < 0$ and $\zeta \geq 1$, then clips back to $[0, 1]$. The key property: values of the stretched softmax smaller than $-\gamma/(\zeta - \gamma)$ get clipped to exactly zero, and because they're clipped, they produce zero gradient—breaking the feedback loop that would otherwise cause inputs to grow without bound.

3. **Gated attention** (Section 4.2)—a modification that multiplies the attention output by a learned gating probability $\pi \in (0, 1)$ computed from the input token representation. If the gate outputs zero, the entire attention head output is zero regardless of what the softmax produces, giving the model an explicit mechanism to "do nothing" that doesn't require extreme softmax inputs.

Information flows through these modifications during standard transformer forward and backward passes. During the forward pass in clipped softmax, the standard softmax output is first linearly rescaled by $\zeta - \gamma$ and shifted by $\gamma$, then values outside $[0, 1]$ are hard-clipped. During the backward pass, clipped values produce zero gradient. During the forward pass in gated attention, the input token representation passes through a small learned gating network (per head) that produces a scalar between 0 and 1 via sigmoid; this scalar multiplies the standard attention output token-by-token. The rest of the transformer architecture—FFN, LayerNorm, residual connections, multi-head concatenation—remains unchanged.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the causal hypothesis—the complete chain of reasoning from "attention head wants to do nothing" to "activation outliers appear in the FFN output of the previous layer." This is the intellectual foundation for both proposed modifications; without understanding it, the design choices appear arbitrary.
- **Second**, the clipped softmax—its mathematical formulation, why the stretch-and-clip operation breaks the outlier feedback loop, how the hyperparameters $\gamma$ and $\zeta$ control the behavior, and why only $\gamma < 0$ (zero-clipping) matters in practice.
- **Third**, the gated attention—its formulation, the design of the gating module (linear per-head gates, the bias initialization strategy), and how it provides an alternative path to zero-output that sidesteps softmax entirely.
- **Fourth**, the relationship between the two methods—they are independent realizations of the same insight (provide a mechanism for zero attention output) but differ in their tradeoffs: clipped softmax is a minimal code change with negligible overhead, while gated attention adds parameters but provides more explicit control.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **mechanistic analysis and architectural intervention paper** whose core idea is that activation outliers in transformers are a *necessary consequence* of attention heads trying to produce zero updates through the softmax function, and that providing alternative mechanisms for zero-output breaks this necessity and yields quantization-friendly models.

#### The Causal Hypothesis: Why Outliers Appear

The paper's central contribution is not just the proposed modifications but the **mechanistic explanation** for why outliers form during training. This explanation spans multiple architectural components and connects observations that prior work had treated as separate phenomena.

**Step 1: Attention heads need to "do nothing."** The paper observes (Section 3, Figures 2 and 3) that some attention heads in pre-trained transformers allocate nearly all their attention probability to low-information tokens—delimiter tokens like `[SEP]`, periods, and commas in BERT; background patches in ViT—while simultaneously learning to produce very small value vectors for those same tokens. The product of attention probabilities and values (which is what gets added to the residual stream) is therefore near zero. The paper interprets this as the attention head learning a **soft no-op**: it doesn't want to contribute to the residual update for some or all token positions, so it "dumps" its attention budget onto tokens whose value vectors have been learned to be near zero.

The paper provides quantitative evidence for this interpretation. For BERT-base fine-tuned on MNLI (Section 3, Figure 1), over 97% of outliers in FFN output layers #10 and #11 correlate with delimiter token positions. The hidden dimension with the highest outlier count (#180, corresponding to attention head #3 in the *next* layer) shows exactly the "attend-to-delimiter, small-values-for-delimiter" pattern (Figure 2). The paper explicitly connects these observations to prior work: "These observations are in line with Clark et al. [8], Kovaleva et al. [30] that also argued that attending exclusively or almost exclusively to delimiter tokens such as [SEP], periods/commas acts as a 'no-op' when the attention head's function is not applicable."

**Step 2: Softmax mathematically requires infinite inputs to produce exact zeros.** This is the crucial mathematical insight that converts the "no-op" observation into an explanation for outliers. The standard softmax function is defined as:

$$\text{softmax}(x)_i = \frac{\exp(x_i)}{\sum_{j} \exp(x_j)}$$

where $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the vector of attention logits for a single query token attending over $d$ key tokens, and $\text{softmax}(x)_i$ is the attention probability assigned to key token $i$.

**What it computes:** For each query, the softmax exponentiates every logit, then divides each by the sum of all exponentiated logits, producing a probability distribution over key positions (non-negative, sums to 1). Large positive logits produce probabilities near 1; large negative logits produce probabilities near 0.

**Why this form:** The exponential ensures positivity and amplifies differences between logits, creating a "winner-take-most" distribution where one or a few keys dominate. The denominator normalizes so the output is a valid probability distribution. This is the standard attention formulation from Vaswani et al. (2017).

The paper makes a simple but profound observation (Equation 2): for the softmax to output exactly zero for token $i$, there must exist some other token $j$ such that:

$$\text{softmax}(x)_i = 0 \iff \exists j \neq i, \; x_j - x_i = +\infty$$

In plain language: to get an attention probability that is exactly zero, the difference between the largest logit and the logit for token $i$ must be infinite. Since neural networks operate with finite floating-point numbers, exact zeros are mathematically impossible. The network can only approach zero by making some logits extremely large relative to others.

**Step 3: The softmax input comes from the query-key dot product, which is a linear function of the previous layer's output.** The attention logits are computed as:

$$\text{logits} = \frac{Q(x)K(x)^T}{\sqrt{d_{\text{head}}}}$$

where $Q(x) = xW_Q$ and $K(x) = xW_K$ are learned linear projections of the input $x$ (the output of the previous layer after LayerNorm), and $d_{\text{head}}$ is the per-head dimensionality (64 for BERT-base). The $\sqrt{d_{\text{head}}}$ scaling prevents the dot products from growing too large as dimensionality increases.

**What it computes:** For a sequence of $T$ token representations $x \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}}$, this produces a $T \times T$ matrix of pairwise compatibility scores between every query and every key. Each entry is a scaled dot product between the query projection of one token and the key projection of another.

The critical point: to make the softmax output near-zero for most tokens and near-one for a delimiter token, the network must make $Q(x)K(x)^T$ produce very large values for the delimiter token's entry compared to all others. Since $Q$ and $K$ are linear projections of $x$, the magnitude of the logits is directly proportional to the magnitude of $x$. If $x$ contains extreme values in certain dimensions, the attention logits can have a large dynamic range.

**Step 4: LayerNorm partially normalizes the input, so the outlier must be produced even larger.** Before entering the attention layer, the input passes through Layer Normalization:

$$\text{LayerNorm}(x) = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \odot \gamma + \beta$$

where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the per-token mean and standard deviation of $x$ across the feature dimension, and $\gamma, \beta$ are learned scale and shift parameters.

**What it computes:** For each token position independently, LayerNorm subtracts the mean of that token's feature vector and divides by its standard deviation, then applies a learned affine transformation. This normalizes the scale of each token's representation to have approximately zero mean and unit variance (before the learned affine transform).

**Why this matters for outliers:** If an outlier value appears in the FFN output of layer $L$, LayerNorm at the input of layer $L+1$ will divide it by the standard deviation of that token's feature vector. Since the standard deviation is dominated by the outlier (because it's orders of magnitude larger than other features), the normalization roughly divides the outlier by itself, producing a value of approximately $\gamma / \sqrt{d_{\text{model}}}$—still large but significantly dampened. The paper notes this explicitly: "Layer Normalization ... normalizes the outliers, [so] the magnitude of the FFN output in the previous layer has to be very high to still produce a sufficiently big dynamic range after the LayerNorm."

**Step 5: The training dynamic creates a positive feedback loop.** The paper identifies the gradient behavior of softmax as the engine that drives outlier growth (Step 4 of the hypothesis in Section 3). Let $y = \text{softmax}(x)$. The Jacobian of the softmax is:

$$\frac{\partial y_i}{\partial x_j} = y_i(\delta_{ij} - y_j)$$

where $\delta_{ij}$ is 1 if $i = j$ and 0 otherwise.

**What this means:** The gradient of the softmax output with respect to its input is non-zero for all pairs of indices—every logit receives gradient signal from every attention probability. Specifically, because softmax is a smooth, differentiable function that never outputs exactly 0 or 1 in finite precision, there is always a non-zero gradient flowing back to all logits. If the model is trying to push attention probabilities toward 0 for informative tokens and toward 1 for delimiter tokens, the softmax will always back-propagate a gradient signal encouraging the logits to become more extreme (larger for the delimiter, smaller for others). This creates a "runaway" dynamic: the longer training continues, the more extreme the logit differences become, requiring ever-larger input magnitudes to sustain, producing ever-larger outliers in the FFN output of the previous layer.

The paper summarizes this as: "Finally, as softmax will never output exact zeros, it will always back-propagate a gradient signal to grow bigger outliers. The outliers will thus tend to become stronger in magnitude, the longer the network is trained."

**The complete causal chain in diagram form:**

```
FFN output (layer L) → [contains outliers in specific dims]
        ↓
LayerNorm (layer L+1) → [partially normalizes outliers]
        ↓
Attention input x → [still has large dynamic range in outlier dims]
        ↓
Q(x)K(x)^T → [produces extreme logit differences for delimiter tokens]
        ↓
softmax → [near-zero probs for most tokens, near-one for delimiter]
        ↓
softmax × V → [near-zero output, V_delimiter learned to be small]
        ↓
Residual add → [essentially no update → "no-op"]
        ↓
Gradient back through softmax → [always non-zero, pushes logits further apart]
        ↓
Gradient into FFN → [encourages even larger outputs to survive LayerNorm]
        ↓
FFN output grows → [outliers become stronger]
```

This feedback loop explains all the key observations: why outliers are concentrated in specific dimensions (those are the dimensions that the FFN has dedicated to feeding the "no-op" attention heads), why they are strongest in later layers (the gradient signal compounds across layers), why they correlate with delimiter positions (those are the tokens targeted by no-op heads), and why the problem worsens with longer training (the softmax gradient never saturates).

#### Clipped Softmax

The clipped softmax is the first proposed solution, and it follows directly from the causal hypothesis. If the root problem is that softmax cannot represent exact zeros with finite inputs, then modifying softmax to allow exact zero outputs with finite inputs should break the outlier feedback loop.

**Formulation.** The clipped softmax replaces the standard softmax in Equation (3) with:

$$\text{clipped\_softmax}(x; \zeta, \gamma) = \text{clip}\left((\zeta - \gamma) \cdot \text{softmax}(x) + \gamma, \; 0, \; 1\right)$$

where $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the vector of attention logits, $\zeta \geq 1$ is the upper stretch factor, $\gamma \leq 0$ is the lower stretch factor, and $\text{clip}(\cdot, 0, 1)$ restricts values to the interval $[0, 1]$.

**What it computes:** This is a three-step operation applied element-wise to the softmax output:
1. **Stretch:** The standard softmax output (which lies in $(0, 1)$) is multiplied by $(\zeta - \gamma)$, expanding its range to $(0, \zeta - \gamma)$.
2. **Shift:** The value $\gamma$ (which is $\leq 0$) is added, shifting the range to $(\gamma, \zeta)$.
3. **Clip:** Any values that fell below 0 after shifting are set to exactly 0; any values that exceeded 1 are set to exactly 1.

The result is a vector that still sums to approximately 1 (though not exactly 1 after clipping) and represents attention probabilities with the crucial new property that some entries can be exactly zero.

**Mathematical thresholds for exact zeros and ones.** From the formulation, a softmax output value $p = \text{softmax}(x)_i$ gets clipped to 0 if:

$$(\zeta - \gamma) \cdot p + \gamma < 0 \implies p < \frac{-\gamma}{\zeta - \gamma}$$

Similarly, it gets clipped to 1 if:

$$(\zeta - \gamma) \cdot p + \gamma > 1 \implies p > \frac{1 - \gamma}{\zeta - \gamma}$$

**Why this form:** The linear stretch-and-shift operation preserves the relative ordering of the softmax outputs—if token A had higher softmax probability than token B, it will still have higher (or equal, if both are clipped) output after clipped softmax. The clipping introduces a "dead zone" where probabilities that were already small get rounded to exactly zero, and probabilities that were already large get rounded to exactly one. This is different from simply thresholding the softmax output directly because the $(0, 1)$ range of softmax means a fixed threshold would need to be tuned; the stretch-and-shift makes the effective threshold a function of $\gamma$ and $\zeta$, which are architecture-level hyperparameters.

**The critical property: zero gradient for clipped values.** When a value is clipped (pushed to exactly 0 or 1), the clipping operation has zero gradient—the gradient does not flow through the clip. This is stated explicitly in the paper: "In addition to that, whenever values are clipped they will not give a gradient, preventing the outliers to grow further."

**What this means for the feedback loop:** Recall the chain: softmax always has non-zero gradient → gradient encourages more extreme logits → FFN produces larger values → outliers grow. With clipped softmax, when an attention probability is clipped to zero, there is no gradient flowing back through that path to encourage the corresponding logit to become even smaller. Similarly, when a probability is clipped to one, the gradient through that path is zero. This **breaks the gradient loop**—the model can achieve exactly zero attention probability for a token with finite logit differences (just large enough to cross the clipping threshold), and once clipped, there is no further gradient pressure to increase the logit differences. The runaway dynamic stops.

**Hyperparameter selection and the dominance of $\gamma < 0$.** The paper explores various combinations of $\gamma$ and $\zeta$ on BERT-base (Table 1) and ViT (Table 8). The key finding is that **only the zero-clipping (γ < 0) matters for outlier suppression**.

For BERT-base (Table 1):
- $\gamma = 0, \zeta = 1$ (vanilla softmax): Max infinity norm = 735, W8A8 perplexity = 1294.
- $\gamma = -0.03, \zeta = 1$: Max infinity norm drops to 20 (a 36× reduction), W8A8 perplexity drops to 4.55 (essentially matching FP16 at 4.41).
- $\gamma = 0, \zeta = 1.03$: Max infinity norm = 741 (essentially unchanged from vanilla), W8A8 perplexity = 1469 (still terrible).
- $\gamma = -0.03, \zeta = 1.03$: Max infinity norm = 22, W8A8 perplexity = 4.56 (essentially identical to $\gamma = -0.03, \zeta = 1$).

The pattern is clear: $\zeta > 1$ (clipping at one) adds nothing beyond what $\gamma < 0$ already provides. The paper makes this explicit: "We, therefore, conclude that for dampening outliers, only the lower-range clipping allows exact zeros matter." This makes intuitive sense from the causal hypothesis: the problem is attention heads trying to produce zero outputs, not one outputs. Providing a way to reach exactly zero with finite inputs is sufficient to break the feedback loop.

**The $\gamma = -\alpha / T$ parameterization.** Recognizing that having to tune $\gamma$ for every model and sequence length is impractical, the paper introduces a parameterization that ties $\gamma$ to the sequence length $T$. The motivation is that the average attention probability in an attention matrix of size $T \times T$ is $1/T$ (since each row sums to 1 and has $T$ entries). If the network wants to push a token's probability below the "noise floor" of uniform attention, it needs to achieve values significantly below $1/T$. Setting:

$$\gamma = -\frac{\alpha}{T}$$

where $\alpha > 0$ is a new hyperparameter, makes the zero-clipping threshold proportional to $1/T$—that is, a fixed multiple of the uniform-attention baseline. The effective threshold for a softmax probability $p$ to clip to zero becomes (using $\zeta = 1$):

$$p < \frac{-\gamma}{1 - \gamma} = \frac{\alpha / T}{1 + \alpha / T} \approx \frac{\alpha}{T} \;\; \text{(for } T \gg \alpha \text{)}$$

**What this means operationally:** If $\alpha = 2$ and $T = 128$, any softmax probability below approximately $2/128 = 0.0156$ will be clipped to exact zero. The network needs only to push probabilities below this threshold—not to $10^{-9}$ or $10^{-12}$ as it would with standard softmax—to achieve a true zero. The required logit differences are therefore bounded.

The paper validates this parameterization on BERT-6L across sequence lengths $T \in \{32, 64, 128, 192, 256\}$ and $\alpha \in \{1/4, 1/2, 1, 2, 4, 8\}$ (Figure 6). The key findings:
- $\alpha \in [2, 4]$ significantly dampens outliers (maximum infinity norm drops by roughly two orders of magnitude, from ~1000 to ~10-100 on the log scale) while maintaining good FP16 perplexity across all sequence lengths.
- $\alpha$ too small (e.g., $1/4$) doesn't clip enough to break the feedback loop—outliers remain large.
- $\alpha$ too large (e.g., $8$) clips too aggressively, degrading FP16 performance while still suppressing outliers.

This shows that the clipped softmax hyperparameter is relatively robust—a range of $\alpha$ values works well—and that the optimal range is intuitively interpretable (clip when probabilities fall significantly below the uniform baseline).

**Computational cost.** The clipped softmax adds only "marginal" overhead over vanilla softmax. Table 11 shows BERT training time: vanilla = 92.8 hours, clipped softmax = 93.6 hours, an increase of less than 1%. For ViT: vanilla = 101.8 hours, clipped softmax = 104.0 hours (~2% increase). This is because the additional operations (scalar multiplication, addition, and clipping) are element-wise and negligible compared to the matrix multiplications in the attention and FFN layers.

#### Gated Attention

The gated attention is the second proposed solution and represents a different architectural philosophy. Rather than modifying softmax to allow zeros, gated attention **bypasses the problem entirely** by providing an explicit, learned mechanism for the model to nullify an attention head's output. If the model wants to "do nothing," it simply closes the gate, and the entire attention head output is zero regardless of what the softmax produces.

**Formulation.** The gated attention replaces the standard attention function in Equation (3) with:

$$\text{Gated\_attention}(x) = \text{sigmoid}(G(x)) \odot \left( \text{softmax}\left( \frac{Q(x)K(x)^T}{\sqrt{d_{\text{head}}}} \right) V(x) \right)$$

where $G$ is the gating function (a small learned neural network), $\odot$ is element-wise multiplication across the token dimension, and everything inside the parentheses is the standard attention computation.

**What it computes:** For each token position in the sequence, the standard attention output (a $d_{\text{head}}$-dimensional vector representing the value-weighted combination of all key tokens) is multiplied by a scalar gating probability $\pi \in (0, 1)$ produced by passing the input token representation through $G$ and then through a sigmoid. If $\pi \approx 1$, the attention head behaves normally (full update). If $\pi \approx 0$, the attention head output is suppressed to near-zero (no update). Values of $\pi$ between 0 and 1 produce a partial update.

The multiplication $\odot$ is applied **token-wise**—each token position gets its own gating probability—but the same scalar multiplies all $d_{\text{head}}$ features of that token's attention output. This means the gate decides *whether* to update each token, not *which features* to update.

**Why this form:** The key insight is that the gating probability $\pi$ is produced by a sigmoid applied to a linear function (or small MLP) of the input token representation. The sigmoid can output values arbitrarily close to 0 with finite inputs (e.g., $\text{sigmoid}(-10) \approx 4.5 \times 10^{-5}$). Unlike softmax—which is a normalized exponential and thus always non-zero for all inputs—the sigmoid is a per-element function. To make the gate output near-zero, the network simply needs $G(x)$ to be a sufficiently negative scalar, which requires only a finite-magnitude linear combination of $x$. There is no competition with other tokens, no normalization, and no requirement for extreme values in $x$. The outlier feedback loop is prevented **architecturally**—the gating mechanism operates outside the softmax entirely.

**Gating module design (per-head, token-independent).** The paper specifies the gating function $G$ to operate per-head and be shared across token positions:

For each attention head $i \in \{1, \ldots, n_{\text{heads}}\}$, define $G_i: \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{head}}} \to \mathbb{R}$ as a function from the $d_{\text{head}}$-dimensional input for that head to a scalar. The gating probability for head $i$ at token position $t$ is:

$$\hat{\pi}_{i,t} = G_i(x_{i,t,:})$$
$$\pi_{i,t} = \text{sigmoid}(\hat{\pi}_{i,t})$$

where $x_{i,t,:} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{head}}}$ is the input representation for head $i$, token $t$.

**Why per-head and token-independent:** Different attention heads specialize in different functions (Clark et al., 2019)—some might always be relevant, others only occasionally. A per-head gate allows each head to learn its own policy for when to activate. Sharing the gating function across token positions (the same $G_i$ is applied to every token position) ensures the decision is based on the content of the token representation, not its position, and keeps the parameter count minimal: $n_{\text{heads}} \cdot (d_{\text{head}} + 1) \approx d_{\text{model}}$ extra parameters per layer (for BERT-base, this is $12 \times 65 = 780$ parameters, or less than 0.009% of the 109M total parameters).

**Linear gating (primary variant).** The paper primarily uses a single linear layer for each $G_i$:

$$G_i(z) = w_i^T z + b_i$$

where $w_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{head}}}$ and $b_i \in \mathbb{R}$ are learned parameters.

This is the "Linear" configuration in Table 4, with memory overhead of $n_{\text{heads}}(d_{\text{head}} + 1)$ parameters per attention layer, equivalent to approximately 1 extra token of context.

**Alternative gating architectures explored (Appendix B.1, Table 4):**

- **MLP:** $G_i$ is a two-layer network with a hidden size of $n_{\text{hid}}$ and ReLU activation. Parameter count: $n_{\text{heads}}(n_{\text{hid}}(d_{\text{head}} + 2) + 1) \approx n_{\text{hid}}$ extra tokens of memory.
- **All-heads-linear:** A single linear layer $G: \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{heads}}}$ that produces gating scalars for all heads simultaneously from the full model dimension (before splitting into heads). This allows cross-head information sharing for gating decisions. Parameter count: $n_{\text{heads}}(d_{\text{model}} + 1)$.

The paper finds that linear gating performs well (Table 5 for BERT: Linear with $\pi_{\text{init}} = 0.5$ achieves W8A8 perplexity of 4.88 vs. MLP(nhid=4) at 4.65), and the marginal improvement from MLP gating is small relative to the added parameters. The paper's main results use the Linear configuration unless otherwise noted.

**Bias initialization strategy.** A critical practical detail is the initialization of the bias $b_i$ in the linear gating layer. Since the weights $w_i$ are initialized with small random values (following He et al., 2015), $G_i(z) \approx b_i$ at the start of training, and therefore:

$$\pi_{i,t} = \text{sigmoid}(G_i(z)) \approx \text{sigmoid}(b_i) = \pi_{\text{init}}$$

**What this means:** The bias initialization directly controls whether gates start "open" (high $\pi_{\text{init}}$, allowing full attention output) or "closed" (low $\pi_{\text{init}}$, suppressing attention output). The paper studies this empirically on BERT-6L and ViT (Figure 7).

For BERT-6L:
- $\pi_{\text{init}} \in [0.25, 0.9]$: good FP16 perplexity and dramatically reduced outliers.
- $\pi_{\text{init}}$ very high (near 1.0): effectively vanilla attention initially; outliers persist because the gate never learns to close significantly.
- $\pi_{\text{init}}$ very low (near 0.0): outliers are suppressed but FP16 perplexity degrades because the model is initialized too far from the standard attention behavior and cannot recover.

For ViT, the viable range is shifted lower: $\pi_{\text{init}} \in [0.1, 0.5]$ works well.

**Why this range exists:** If the gate starts fully open, it behaves identically to vanilla attention, and the training dynamics that produce outliers proceed as usual—the gate has no incentive to close because the network is already achieving "no-op" behavior through extreme softmax inputs. If the gate starts too closed, training is destabilized because a large fraction of the network's capacity is initially suppressed. The "sweet spot" is where gates are somewhat open (allowing normal function) but have room to close, and the training objective provides pressure for them to learn appropriate opening/closing behavior. The wide viable range ($\pi_{\text{init}} \in [0.25, 0.9]$ for BERT) indicates that the method is robust to this hyperparameter.

**Computational cost.** The gated attention adds more overhead than clipped softmax because it requires additional matrix multiplications. Table 11 reports:
- BERT: vanilla = 92.8 hours, gated attention (Linear) = 97.7 hours (~5% increase).
- ViT: vanilla = 101.8 hours, gated attention (Linear) = 110.8 hours (~9% increase).
- OPT: vanilla = 53.6 hours, gated attention (Linear) = 55.7 hours (~4% increase).

The MLP variant adds more overhead: BERT gated MLP = 119.1 hours (~28% increase). This is because the gating module's forward and backward passes are computed for every token at every layer.

#### Relationship Between the Two Methods

Both clipped softmax and gated attention solve the same problem—enabling zero attention output without infinite softmax inputs—but through fundamentally different mechanisms that reflect a deeper design choice about where to place the zero-output capability.

**Clipped softmax modifies the probability distribution to allow exact zeros.** The mechanism operates *within* the softmax: the stretch-and-clip transformation changes the output of the softmax from a strictly positive distribution (all entries > 0) to a distribution where small-probability entries become exactly 0. The attention head still computes attention probabilities in the usual way; it just has the ability to "round down" very small probabilities to zero. This requires the logits to be large enough to cross the clipping threshold, but that threshold is finite and bounded. The outlier feedback loop is broken because clipped values produce zero gradient.

**Gated attention provides an independent path to zero output.** The mechanism operates *outside* the softmax: the gate multiplies the entire attention output, so even if the softmax produces a non-zero distribution, the gate can nullify the result. This completely decouples the "no-op" decision from the softmax input magnitude. The attention head can produce any softmax distribution and any value-weighted combination; the gate independently decides whether to let that output through.

**Why two methods?** The paper presents them as alternative realizations of the same insight, each with different tradeoffs:

- **Clipped softmax** is a minimal code change (replace one line: `softmax` → `clipped_softmax`), adds negligible compute overhead (<2%), and requires no additional parameters. However, it introduces a hyperparameter ($\gamma$) that ideally should be set based on sequence length, and it still requires the logits to reach a finite but non-trivial dynamic range to cross the clipping threshold.
- **Gated attention** requires more code changes (new module, new forward pass logic), adds parameters (~1 token equivalent per layer), and adds 5–9% compute overhead. However, it provides explicit control over the no-op behavior, eliminates the need for any logit manipulation whatsoever, and has a more intuitive hyperparameter (bias initialization, with a wide viable range).

The fact that both methods work—and work robustly across different architectures and modalities—is strong evidence that the underlying causal hypothesis is correct. The outliers are indeed caused by the softmax's inability to represent zeros with finite inputs driving a runaway gradient dynamic. Both methods break this dynamic, just through different mechanisms.

#### Practical Deployment: The Full Training and Quantization Pipeline

The paper does not modify the pre-training procedure beyond replacing the attention mechanism. The full pipeline for using these methods is:

**1. Architecture modification (before training):** Replace `softmax(QK^T/√d)V` in every attention layer with either clipped softmax or gated attention as defined above. No other changes to the model architecture (FFN, LayerNorm placement, residual connections, embedding layers, task heads).

**2. Pre-training:** Train from scratch using standard recipes. The paper uses:
- BERT-base: MLM on BookCorpus + Wikipedia, max sequence length 128, batch size 256, 10^6 steps, AdamW with max LR $10^{-4}$, linear warmup over $10^4$ steps then linear decay, weight decay 0.01, dropout 0.1, FP16 mixed precision.
- OPT-125m: CLM on BookCorpus + Wikipedia, max sequence length 512, batch size 192, 125K steps (500K forward passes), AdamW with max LR $4 \times 10^{-4}$, linear warmup 2000 steps then linear decay, weight decay 0.1, dropout 0.1, FP16 mixed precision. Note: the paper applied weight decay to LayerNorm $\gamma$ for OPT (not default) after finding this "has a strong effect on reducing the outliers' magnitude."
- ViT-S/16: ImageNet-1K, resolution 224×224, batch size 512, 300 epochs, AdamW with weight decay 0.03, cosine LR schedule with warmup from $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-3}$ over 20 epochs, RandAugment, Mixup, CutMix, label smoothing 0.1.

**3. Post-training quantization (after training):** The simplest possible PTQ pipeline is used to demonstrate that the models are inherently quantization-friendly:
- **Weights:** Symmetric uniform quantization with min-max range estimation (except OPT uses MSE range estimation).
- **Activations:** Asymmetric uniform quantization with static range estimation using running min-max (exponential moving average, momentum 0.9) over 16 calibration batches from the training set.
- **Bitwidth:** W8A8 (8-bit weights, 8-bit activations) for all main experiments.
- No fine-tuning, no knowledge distillation, no per-channel quantization, no mixed precision.
- For OPT, 99.999% percentile ranges are used instead of true min-max as this gave better W8A8 perplexity.

The fact that this minimal PTQ pipeline works so well—bringing W8A8 performance within ~0.05 perplexity or ~1% accuracy of FP16/FP32—is the paper's key empirical validation. The vanilla models, under the same PTQ pipeline, experience catastrophic degradation (BERT W8A8 perplexity goes from 4.49 to 1294). The dramatic difference quantifies how much the outlier problem matters and how effectively the architectural fixes address it.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Identifying the Causal Mechanism That Produces Activation Outliers

The paper's most fundamental contribution is not a new method but a **diagnostic insight**: it traces the well-known activation outlier problem in transformers to a specific, previously unrecognized causal chain that connects attention head behavior to extreme activation magnitudes. Prior work had catalogued the *symptoms* extensively—Bondarenko et al. (2021) showed outliers concentrate in specific hidden dimensions, Dettmers et al. (2022) showed they appear after every linear layer in large models, Kovaleva et al. (2021) showed they are functionally important—but the field treated outliers as an unexplained empirical regularity to be worked around. This paper supplies the missing explanation.

The conceptual move is to recognize that three architectural components—**softmax, residual connections, and LayerNorm**—form a feedback loop that *forces* outlier growth during training. The reasoning is a chain of mathematical necessity rather than empirical correlation: (1) attention heads need to produce zero updates for certain tokens (the "no-op" behavior observed by Clark et al., 2019; Kovaleva et al., 2019); (2) softmax **mathematically cannot output exact zeros** without infinite input differences (Equation 2); (3) the network approximates zeros by making logit differences as large as possible, driving the FFN output in the previous layer to extreme magnitudes; (4) LayerNorm partially normalizes these extremes, so the FFN must produce even larger values to compensate; (5) because softmax is everywhere-differentiable and never truly saturates, the gradient *always* pushes toward more extreme inputs, creating a runaway dynamic that intensifies with training duration.

What distinguishes this from prior observational work is the transition from *correlation* ("outliers exist in these dimensions") to *causation* ("outliers must grow without bound because of the mathematics of softmax combined with residual connections and LayerNorm"). The paper doesn't just observe that outlier dimensions correspond to attention heads attending to delimiter tokens—it explains *why* those attention heads produce outliers as a necessary side effect, and *why* the outliers are in the *previous* layer's FFN output rather than in the attention layer itself.

This insight has a property that distinguishes genuinely explanatory theories: it predicts phenomena beyond what was used to construct it. The causal chain predicts that outliers should be layer-staggered (FFN output in layer L produces extreme values that enable no-op attention in layer L+1), that the problem should worsen with model depth and training duration (the gradient dynamic compounds), and that the mechanism should be universal across any architecture using softmax attention with residual connections (not specific to NLP or BERT). All three predictions are validated: the paper shows the same pattern in ViT (Section 3, Figure 3), notes that the outliers are strongest in later layers, and explicitly argues the mechanism applies wherever these three components co-exist.

The significance of this innovation is primarily **theoretical and diagnostic rather than methodological**. It converts the outlier problem from a mysterious nuisance into a predictable consequence of architectural choices. This makes the solutions obvious in retrospect: if you want to eliminate outliers, break the causal chain. You can either modify softmax to allow zeros with finite inputs (clipped softmax) or provide an alternative path to zero output that bypasses softmax entirely (gated attention). The paper's two methods are direct corollaries of the causal insight, not the products of a broad hyperparameter search.

### Innovation 2: Breaking the Problem by Design Rather Than Patching It Post-Hoc

The second innovation is methodological rather than technical: the paper demonstrates that **changing the architecture during pre-training to prevent outliers from forming is dramatically more effective—and simpler—than any post-hoc quantization workaround applied after training**. This represents a fundamental shift in approach from the prior literature.

To appreciate the significance of this shift, consider the dominant approaches the field had developed. LLM.int8() (Dettmers et al., 2022) keeps outlier dimensions in FP16 while quantizing the rest to INT8—an effective but hardware-complex mixed-precision scheme that requires custom CUDA kernels and support for per-column number formats. SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022) mathematically migrates the quantization difficulty from activations to weights through per-channel scaling—clever but still a post-hoc mathematical transformation applied to an already-trained model. QAT methods (Bhalgat et al., 2020; Esser et al., 2020) incorporate simulated quantization into training, allowing the model to adapt—but require rerunning significant portions of the training pipeline. Each of these approaches **accepts outliers as an inevitable consequence of training** and expends significant complexity working around them.

This paper's reframing is: **outliers are not inevitable; they are a consequence of a specific architectural choice (vanilla softmax) that can be changed**. The empirical validation of this reframing is striking not because it achieves slightly better quantization accuracy—it does, but that's not the core point—but because it makes quantization **trivially easy**. Under the simplest possible PTQ pipeline (per-tensor quantization with min-max range estimation, no fine-tuning, no knowledge distillation, no mixed precision), the clipped softmax BERT-base achieves W8A8 perplexity of 4.52, essentially matching its FP16 perplexity of 4.39 (Table 2). The vanilla BERT-base under the identical PTQ pipeline achieves W8A8 perplexity of 1294—a catastrophic failure. The difference is not incremental; it is the difference between quantization being usable and unusable.

What makes this a genuine innovation rather than just "we tried a new architecture and it worked" is the inversion of the problem's framing. Prior work asked: "Given that transformers have outliers, how can we make quantization work despite them?" This paper asks: "Why do transformers have outliers, and can we change the architecture so they don't?" The former question leads to ever-more-complex quantization schemes. The latter leads to architectural changes that eliminate the problem at its source. The paper's title—"Removing Outliers by Helping Attention Heads Do Nothing"—captures this inversion perfectly: rather than fighting the model's desire to produce no-op behavior, *enable it directly* so that the model doesn't need to resort to extreme activation values.

A crucial subtlety: this approach requires training from scratch (or at least substantial fine-tuning, as explored in Appendix B.6 for OPT-1.3B). This is a real limitation—it cannot be applied to already-trained models the way LLM.int8() or SmoothQuant can. But the paper implicitly argues that this tradeoff is worthwhile because (1) the ongoing cost of complex quantization schemes across all downstream deployments likely exceeds the one-time cost of architectural changes during pre-training, and (2) as models scale, the post-hoc approaches become increasingly complex (LLM.int8() requires tracking outlier dimensions across layers; SmoothQuant requires per-channel calibration), while the architectural approach scales naturally (the same modified attention block works regardless of model size, as validated on OPT-350m and OPT-1.3B in Table 3).

### Innovation 3: Unifying Observations Across Modalities and Architectures Under a Single Mechanism

The paper's third innovation is **demonstrating that the same causal mechanism produces outliers across language models (both encoder-only BERT and decoder-only OPT) and vision transformers**, establishing that the problem is fundamental to the softmax-attention-with-residuals design pattern rather than an artifact of language modeling, training objectives, or dataset characteristics.

This universality claim matters for two reasons. First, it strengthens the causal explanation: if the same mechanism operates across modalities and architectures, it is unlikely to be a spurious correlation specific to BERT's pre-training on BookCorpus+Wikipedia. The paper shows that in ViT, the attention heads attending to uninformative background patches (the visual analog of delimiter tokens) correlate with outlier dimensions in the FFN output of the previous layer (Section 3, Figure 3), exactly mirroring the BERT pattern. The values associated with outlier patches are distinctly smaller in magnitude than non-outlier patches (Figure 3e), mirroring the BERT observation that delimiter tokens have learned small value vectors. This cross-modal replication of a detailed pattern—not just the existence of outliers, but their specific relationship to attention behavior—provides strong evidence for the causal hypothesis.

Second, it implies that the proposed architectural fixes should generalize broadly. The paper explicitly states: "This happens for language and vision transformers and different specific transformer architectures." By testing clipped softmax and gated attention on three distinct model families—BERT (bidirectional encoder, MLM objective), OPT (autoregressive decoder, CLM objective), and ViT (vision encoder, image classification objective)—the paper provides initial evidence that the methods are not architecture-specific or task-specific. The results in Table 2 show that at least one of the two methods works well for each model: clipped softmax improves W8A8 perplexity from 1294 to 4.52 on BERT and W8A8 accuracy from 69.24% to 79.77% on ViT, while gated attention improves W8A8 perplexity from 21.18 to 16.02 on OPT and W8A8 accuracy from 69.24% to 79.82% on ViT. The only failure case—clipped softmax on OPT, where W8A8 perplexity degrades from 21.18 to 37.20 (Table 2)—is explicitly acknowledged, and the paper notes it does not have an explanation, which strengthens the credibility of the universal claim by not hiding a counterexample.

What distinguishes this from a standard multi-architecture benchmark is that the paper is not merely evaluating the same method on different models. It is using the cross-architecture evidence to support a **mechanistic claim about the transformer architecture itself**. The outlier problem is not a quirk of BERT's training; it is a predictable consequence of combining softmax, residual connections, and LayerNorm. Any architecture using this combination—which is essentially all modern transformers—will tend to produce outliers unless the architecture provides an alternative mechanism for zero-output. This reframes the outlier problem from a model-specific implementation issue to a **fundamental design consideration for future transformer architectures**.

### Innovation 4: The Practical Irrelevance of Complex Quantization When Architecture Is Right

A more subtle innovation emerges from the paper's evaluation methodology choices: by demonstrating that the **simplest possible PTQ pipeline achieves near-lossless quantization when the underlying model is outlier-free**, the paper implicitly argues that much of the PTQ literature's complexity is addressing a problem that shouldn't exist in the first place.

The quantization pipeline used in the paper is deliberately minimal: per-tensor symmetric weight quantization with min-max ranges (or MSE for OPT), per-tensor asymmetric activation quantization with running min-max over 16 calibration batches, and no fine-tuning, distillation, per-channel scaling, mixed precision, or learned rounding. This is the baseline that any PTQ paper would use as its "naive" lower bound before introducing sophisticated techniques. Yet on the clipped softmax and gated attention models, this naive pipeline achieves W8A8 performance within ~0.05 perplexity (BERT) or ~1% accuracy (ViT) of the floating-point baseline. The gap between the method and the baseline is smaller than the gap most sophisticated PTQ methods achieve on vanilla models.

This finding has an important implication for the research agenda: **improving PTQ techniques on vanilla transformers is a dead end if the root cause is architectural**. No amount of clever calibration, per-channel scaling, or adaptive rounding can solve the fundamental range-precision dilemma that a single 735-magnitude outlier creates in a tensor of otherwise well-behaved values. The paper's results suggest that the research effort devoted to PTQ for transformers would be more productively redirected toward architectural modifications that make quantization easy, with PTQ techniques reserved for pushing from W8A8 to W4A8 or lower bitwidths where precision limits are intrinsic rather than outlier-driven.

The low-bit results in Appendix B.7 support this interpretation. At W6A8, the vanilla model achieves W8A8-equivalent perplexity only because the MSE weight range estimation produces implicit activation clipping that happens to suppress outliers—a fragile, uncontrolled side effect rather than a principled solution. The clipped softmax and gated attention models degrade gracefully as bitwidth decreases (W8A8: 4.52/4.65 → W6A8: 4.64/4.79 → W6A6: 6.64/5.90 for clipped/gated on BERT), following the expected precision-quality tradeoff curve rather than the catastrophic failure mode of the vanilla model.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses three distinct datasets matched to the three model types:
  - **BERT pre-training:** The concatenation of BookCorpus (Zhu et al., 2015) and English Wikipedia (specifically the English subset of Wiki-40b), following the original BERT pre-training recipe (Devlin et al., 2019). Evaluation is on the Wikipedia validation set, reporting masked language modeling (MLM) perplexity.
  - **OPT pre-training:** The same BookCorpus + Wikipedia dataset used for BERT, but with a causal language modeling (CLM) objective. Evaluation is on the Wikipedia validation set, reporting CLM perplexity.
  - **ViT training:** ImageNet-1K (Russakovsky et al., 2015), the standard 1.28M training images / 50K validation images benchmark for image classification. Evaluation is top-1 accuracy on the ImageNet validation set.
  - The choice of standard, widely-used datasets for each architecture enables direct comparison with prior work, though it means the paper does not test on more diverse or challenging benchmarks (e.g., no GLUE fine-tuning for BERT, no other CLM datasets for OPT).

- **Base model(s).**
  - **BERT-base-uncased** (~109M parameters): a 12-layer bidirectional encoder with hidden size 768, 12 attention heads ($d_{\text{head}} = 64$). Chosen as the canonical encoder transformer where the outlier phenomenon was first well-documented (Bondarenko et al., 2021).
  - **OPT-125m** (~125M parameters): a 12-layer autoregressive decoder with hidden size 768, 12 attention heads. Chosen to represent decoder-only architectures and evaluate whether the outlier mechanism generalizes across attention mask types (bidirectional vs. causal). For scalability experiments, OPT-350m and OPT-1.3B are also evaluated (Table 3), though trained for only $10^5$ steps due to compute constraints.
  - **ViT-S/16** (~22M parameters): a 12-layer vision transformer with patch size 16×16, hidden size 384, 6 attention heads. Chosen to test cross-modal generalization—whether the same mechanism produces outliers when the input is images rather than text, and whether the architectural fixes work in a non-language setting.
  - All models are pre-trained from scratch with the proposed architectural modifications. The paper also uses a pre-trained BERT-base-uncased checkpoint from HuggingFace for the initial outlier analysis in Section 3 (fine-tuned on MNLI), but all main results use training from scratch. The base models span roughly a 5× parameter range (22M to 125M), with scalability tested up to 1.3B for gated attention only.

- **Metrics.** Three categories of metrics are reported:
  - **Task performance:** MLM perplexity (BERT), CLM perplexity (OPT), top-1 accuracy (ViT). Perplexity is the standard evaluation metric for language models; lower is better. Top-1 accuracy is the fraction of ImageNet validation images correctly classified.
  - **Outlier magnitude:** Two complementary metrics capture the severity of outliers:
    - **Maximum infinity norm ($\max \|x\|_\infty$):** For the output of each attention layer, compute $\max |x|$ across all tokens and feature dimensions, then average this maximum across all sequences in the validation set. This captures the single most extreme value; a reduction from 735 to 20 (BERT, clipped softmax in Table 2) indicates that the largest outliers are ~37× smaller.
    - **Average kurtosis:** Kurtosis (fourth standardized moment) measures the tailedness of a distribution—a Gaussian has kurtosis 3; values in the thousands indicate extreme heavy-tailedness driven by outliers. Reported as the mean kurtosis of attention layer outputs across all layers and validation sequences. This captures overall distribution shape rather than just the single most extreme value.
  - **Quantized task performance:** W8A8 perplexity or accuracy after applying the simplest PTQ pipeline. This is the primary metric for evaluating whether the architectural modifications make quantization practically useful.
  - All metrics are reported as mean ± standard deviation across two training runs with different random seeds (except ViT FP32 accuracy, which reports across 2 seeds, and OPT-350m/1.3B which report single-run results due to compute constraints). PTQ experiments are repeated 3 times with different random seeds for calibration data sampling.

- **Baselines.**
  - **Vanilla softmax:** The unmodified standard softmax attention as described in Vaswani et al. (2017), trained from scratch with the same pre-training recipe and data. This is the primary baseline and represents the status quo that the paper seeks to improve upon. All comparisons are against this baseline under identical training conditions.
  - The paper does not compare against prior outlier-mitigation methods (LLM.int8(), SmoothQuant, QAT, etc.) because its claim is not "our PTQ pipeline is better than existing PTQ pipelines" but rather "our architectural modifications produce models that are inherently quantization-friendly, making even the simplest PTQ work well." The evaluation demonstrates that vanilla models fail catastrophically under minimal PTQ while modified models do not—the comparison to sophisticated PTQ methods is left implicit (the reader can compare W8A8 perplexity of ~4.5 for BERT with clipped softmax to, e.g., the ~4.5-5.0 typically reported for BERT-base with advanced QAT methods, though the paper does not make this comparison explicitly).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Since this is a pre-training paper rather than an inference-time compute paper, the "budget" is training compute rather than generation count. The paper reports:
  - BERT: $10^6$ training steps with batch size 256, maximum sequence length 128, trained with FP16 mixed precision. Total training time: ~93 hours on a single A100 GPU for vanilla, ~94 hours for clipped softmax, ~98 hours for gated attention (Table 11).
  - OPT-125m: 125,000 training steps (500,000 forward passes with gradient accumulation), batch size 192, maximum sequence length 512, FP16 mixed precision. Training time: ~54 hours vanilla, ~56 hours gated.
  - ViT: 300 epochs, batch size 512, resolution 224×224. Training time: ~102 hours vanilla, ~104 hours clipped, ~111 hours gated.
  - The paper estimates total compute cost of main results at ~320 GPU-days (A100) and the full project including preliminary experiments at ~1,400 GPU-days (Appendix D).
  - All comparisons are at equal training compute: the modified models use the same number of training steps, batch sizes, and sequence lengths as the vanilla baselines. The added computational overhead of clipped softmax (<2%) and gated attention (4-9%) is absorbed into the training time but represents a small additional cost for the benefits obtained.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper employs two-fold cross-validation for the early BERT-6L experiments (Section 5.2, where different hyperparameters are swept across sequence lengths) but does not use cross-validation for the main results. Instead:
  - Each main result is reported as the mean ± standard deviation across 2 training runs with different random seeds (for floating-point metrics).
  - PTQ experiments are repeated 3 times with different random seeds for the calibration data sampling (different random subsets of training data for quantizer range estimation), and mean ± std is reported.
  - The standard deviations provide a measure of training instability and quantization sensitivity. For the vanilla models, the large standard deviation on W8A8 metrics (e.g., BERT W8A8 perplexity: 1294 ± 1046) reflects extreme sensitivity to the exact outlier magnitudes produced during training—a small difference in the largest outlier can dramatically change quantization error.
  - No statistical significance tests (t-tests, confidence intervals) are reported. The magnitude of differences (e.g., W8A8 perplexity dropping from 1294 to 4.52) is so large that formal testing would be superfluous, but for the smaller FP16 performance differences (e.g., BERT perplexity 4.49 vs. 4.39), statistical significance is unclear.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Outlier Analysis (Section 3): Establishing the Causal Pattern

The paper's first set of results is diagnostic rather than interventional: it characterizes the relationship between attention patterns and outlier locations to support the causal hypothesis.

**BERT-base on MNLI (Figures 1, 2):** Using a pre-trained BERT-base-uncased fine-tuned on MNLI, the paper passes the MNLI-m validation set through the network and records all outliers (values exceeding 6 standard deviations from the tensor mean, following Bondarenko et al., 2021) at the FFN output in layers #10 and #11. Figure 1 shows:
- Outliers are concentrated in only 7 hidden dimensions (#123, #180, #225, #308, #381, #526, #720), with dimensions #180 and #720 dominating.
- Over 97% of outliers correlate with delimiter token positions (`[SEP]`, ".", ",").
- The spatial concentration matches prior work (Bondarenko et al., 2021; Dettmers et al., 2022) but the correlation with delimiter positions provides the key new evidence linking outliers to specific attention behavior.

Figure 2 visualizes attention patterns for head #3 (corresponding to outlier dimension #180 in the next layer) on three data sequences:
- **Sequence #1 (Figure 2a):** Head #3 in layer #11 assigns almost all attention probability to `[SEP]` tokens, and the values (V) associated with those tokens are very small (near zero). The product (attention × values) is near-zero, producing effectively no update. This is a **full no-op**.
- **Sequence #5 (Figure 2b, 2c):** Head #3 in layer #11 (panel b) and layer #10 (panel c) allocates significant probability to delimiters but also some probability to content words. The delimiters have small values, while content words have larger values. The product represents a **partial/selective update**—the head contributes to some tokens but not others.

The paper's interpretation—that these patterns represent the model learning to "do nothing" or "do something selective" through the only mechanism available (dumping probability onto tokens with learned-near-zero values)—is supported by the consistency of the pattern across sequences and the clear relationship to outlier locations. However, the analysis is qualitative: the paper shows example sequences rather than a systematic quantification across all heads and layers. The 97% figure for delimiter correlation is the only aggregate statistic provided.

**ViT on ImageNet (Figures 3, 9, 16, 17):** The same pattern is replicated in the vision domain:
- Figure 9a shows the average infinity norm peaks at attention layers #10–#11, mirroring BERT.
- Figure 9b shows outliers concentrated in ~10 hidden dimensions, primarily #48 and #43 (attention head #1).
- Figure 9c shows outliers concentrated at image boundaries (background patches), the visual analog of delimiter tokens.
- Figure 3 (and Appendices 16–17) shows attention head #1 in the next layer attending predominantly to outlier patches, with those patches having distinctly smaller value magnitudes than non-outlier patches (Figure 3e: the "average magnitude of values" bar is visibly shorter for outlier patches).

The ViT evidence is important because it demonstrates cross-modal generality, but it is more qualitative than the BERT analysis—the paper does not report the fraction of outliers correlated with background patches or the quantitative difference in value magnitudes between outlier and non-outlier patches. The evidence is visually convincing but less statistically rigorous than the BERT analysis.

#### Clipped Softmax Hyperparameter Analysis (Section 5.1): Only Zero-Clipping Matters

The paper systematically evaluates the two hyperparameters of clipped softmax—the lower stretch factor $\gamma$ (controls zero-clipping) and the upper stretch factor $\zeta$ (controls one-clipping)—on BERT-base (Table 1) and ViT-S/16 (Table 8).

**BERT-base results (Table 1):**
- **Vanilla ($\gamma = 0, \zeta = 1$):** FP16 perplexity = 4.49 ± 0.01, max infinity norm = 735 ± 55, avg kurtosis = 3076 ± 262, W8A8 perplexity = 1294 ± 1046.
- **Only one-clipping ($\gamma = 0, \zeta = 1.003$ or $1.03$):** All metrics are essentially unchanged from vanilla. Max infinity norm = 715 ± 335 / 741 ± 66; W8A8 perplexity = 451 ± 57 / 1469 ± 646. One-clipping alone does nothing to suppress outliers.
- **Only zero-clipping ($\gamma = -0.003, \zeta = 1$):** Significant improvement. Max infinity norm = 688 ± 64, W8A8 perplexity = 636 ± 566—better than vanilla but still poor, because the clipping threshold (-0.003) is too close to zero (most probabilities don't reach it).
- **Strong zero-clipping ($\gamma = -0.03, \zeta = 1$):** Dramatic improvement. Max infinity norm = 20 ± 1 (36× reduction from 735), avg kurtosis = 80 ± 6 (38× reduction from 3076), W8A8 perplexity = 4.55 ± 0.01—essentially matching FP16 perplexity (4.41 ± 0.01). This is the headline result for clipped softmax.
- **Both zero-clipping and one-clipping ($\gamma = -0.03, \zeta = 1.03$):** Max infinity norm = 22 ± 3, W8A8 perplexity = 4.56 ± 0.05—essentially identical to $\gamma = -0.03, \zeta = 1$. Adding one-clipping provides no additional benefit.
- **Intermediate $\gamma$ values ($\gamma = -0.005, -0.01, -0.015, -0.02, -0.025$):** The transition is smooth—the stronger the zero-clipping (more negative $\gamma$), the smaller the outliers and the better the W8A8 perplexity, up to $\gamma \approx -0.025$ where performance saturates near the FP16 baseline.

The takeaway is stated explicitly: "only the lower-range clipping allows exact zeros matter." The mechanism is that the problematic attention heads need to produce zero outputs, not one outputs, so providing a path to exact zero with finite inputs breaks the outlier feedback loop.

**ViT-S/16 results (Table 8):** The same pattern holds for vision transformers, though the optimal $\gamma$ is much smaller in magnitude ($\gamma = -0.003$ vs. $-0.03$ for BERT). This likely reflects differences in the typical softmax output distributions between the two architectures—ViT's attention patterns may be more diffuse (so probabilities are smaller on average, requiring a less aggressive $\gamma$ to clip them).

#### Clipped Softmax $\gamma$ vs. Sequence Length (Section 5.2): The $-\alpha/T$ Parameterization Works

Figure 6 shows the results of sweeping $\alpha$ (where $\gamma = -\alpha/T$) and sequence length $T$ on BERT-6L trained on WikiText-103:
- **Figure 6a (FP16 perplexity):** For $\alpha \in [1/4, 8]$ and $T \in [32, 256]$, relative FP16 log-perplexity compared to vanilla is generally within ~0.01 (essentially neutral) for $\alpha \leq 4$, with degradation appearing at $\alpha = 8$ for longer sequences. The optimal range for maintaining FP16 performance is $\alpha \in [1/4, 4]$.
- **Figure 6b (Maximum infinity norm):** The y-axis is logarithmic. Vanilla models have max infinity norm ~$10^2$ to $10^3$. With clipped softmax, the max infinity norm drops by 1–2 orders of magnitude for $\alpha \geq 1$, reaching ~$10^0$–$10^1$ for $\alpha \in [2, 4]$ across all sequence lengths. The suppression is effective and consistent across sequence lengths.
- **The sweet spot:** $\alpha \in [2, 4]$ both maintains FP16 perplexity and dramatically suppresses outliers across all tested sequence lengths. This supports the paper's claim that $\gamma = -\alpha/T$ is a robust parameterization that "works well across different sequence lengths."

This is a practically important result because it reduces clipped softmax from a two-hyperparameter method ($\gamma$ and $\zeta$) to effectively one architectural hyperparameter ($\alpha$) that is interpretable (it controls what multiple of the uniform-attention baseline $1/T$ gets clipped to zero) and transferable across sequence lengths.

#### Gated Attention Bias Initialization Sensitivity (Section 5.3): A Wide Viable Range

Figure 7 shows the effect of different initial gating probabilities $\pi_{\text{init}}$ (controlled via bias initialization $b_{\text{init}} = \text{logit}(\pi_{\text{init}})$) on BERT-6L (trained on WikiText-103 with sequence length 128) and ViT-S/16 (trained for 150 epochs on ImageNet):

- **BERT-6L (Figure 7a):** FP16 performance is essentially flat for $\pi_{\text{init}} \in [0.25, 0.9]$, with slight degradation at the extremes (too closed: $\pi_{\text{init}} < 0.1$; too open: $\pi_{\text{init}} \approx 1.0$). W8A8 performance shows a broader plateau: the model achieves good quantized performance for a wide range of initial gate openness.
- **ViT (Figure 7b):** The viable range shifts lower: $\pi_{\text{init}} \in [0.1, 0.5]$ works well, with degradation at $\pi_{\text{init}} = 0.75$ and above. The paper interprets this as evidence of "relative robustness of our method to this hyperparameter."

The practical implication is that gated attention does not require careful per-model tuning of the bias initialization—setting $\pi_{\text{init}}$ anywhere in a broad range (centered around 0.5) works well. The exact width of the viable range shifts per architecture (ViT prefers more closed initial gates than BERT) but is wide in both cases.

#### Main Results Across All Models (Section 5.4, Table 2): Both Methods Dramatically Improve Quantizability

Table 2 is the paper's central results table, summarizing the performance of both proposed methods against the vanilla baseline for all three model architectures:

**BERT-base:**
- **Vanilla:** FP16 perplexity 4.49 ± 0.01. Max infinity norm 735 ± 55. Avg kurtosis 3076 ± 262. W8A8 perplexity 1294 ± 1046.
- **Clipped softmax ($\gamma = -0.025$):** FP16 perplexity 4.39 ± 0.00 (0.10 *better* than vanilla). Max infinity norm 21.5 ± 1.5 (34× reduction). Avg kurtosis 80 ± 6 (38× reduction). W8A8 perplexity 4.52 ± 0.01 (within 0.13 of FP16).
- **Gated attention (MLP, $n_{\text{hid}} = 4$):** FP16 perplexity 4.45 ± 0.03 (0.04 better than vanilla). Max infinity norm 39.2 ± 26.0 (19× reduction). Avg kurtosis 201 ± 181 (15× reduction). W8A8 perplexity 4.65 ± 0.04 (within 0.20 of FP16).

**OPT-125m:**
- **Vanilla:** FP16 perplexity 15.84 ± 0.05. Max infinity norm 340 ± 47. Avg kurtosis 1778 ± 444. W8A8 perplexity 21.18 ± 1.89.
- **Clipped softmax ($\gamma = -12/512$):** FP16 perplexity 16.29 ± 0.07 (*worse* than vanilla by 0.45). Max infinity norm 63.2 ± 8.8 (5.4× reduction). Avg kurtosis 19728 ± 7480 (*dramatically worse*). W8A8 perplexity 37.20 ± 2.40 (*worse than vanilla*). This is the paper's only failure case—clipped softmax on OPT-125m increases kurtosis and degrades both FP16 and W8A8 performance.
- **Gated attention (Linear, $\pi_{\text{init}} = 0.25$, with LayerNorm $\gamma$ weight decay):** FP16 perplexity 15.55 ± 0.05 (0.29 better than vanilla). Max infinity norm 8.7 ± 0.6 (39× reduction). Avg kurtosis 18.9 ± 0.9 (94× reduction). W8A8 perplexity 16.02 ± 0.07 (within 0.47 of FP16).

**ViT-S/16:**
- **Vanilla:** FP32 accuracy 80.75 ± 0.10%. Max infinity norm 359 ± 81. Avg kurtosis 1018 ± 471. W8A8 accuracy 69.24 ± 6.93% (catastrophic 11.5 percentage point drop).
- **Clipped softmax ($\gamma = -0.0001$, with patch embedding LayerNorm):** FP32 accuracy 80.89 ± 0.13% (0.14 points better than vanilla). Max infinity norm 73.7 ± 14.9 (4.9× reduction). Avg kurtosis 22.9 ± 1.6 (44× reduction). W8A8 accuracy 79.77 ± 0.25% (within 1.12 points of FP32).
- **Gated attention (Linear, $\pi_{\text{init}} = 0.5$, with patch embedding LayerNorm):** FP32 accuracy 81.01 ± 0.06% (0.26 points better than vanilla). Max infinity norm 79.8 ± 0.5 (4.5× reduction). Avg kurtosis 19.9 ± 0.3 (51× reduction). W8A8 accuracy 79.82 ± 0.11% (within 1.19 points of FP32).

**Key observations from Table 2:**

1. **Both methods dramatically reduce outlier magnitude across all models where they succeed.** The max infinity norm drops by factors ranging from 4.5× (ViT gated) to 39× (OPT gated), and kurtosis drops from thousands (heavy-tailed) to tens (near-Gaussian) in most cases.

2. **W8A8 quantization becomes near-lossless for all successful cases.** The W8A8 performance gap from FP16/FP32 is: BERT clipped = +0.13 perplexity, BERT gated = +0.20 perplexity, OPT gated = +0.47 perplexity, ViT clipped = -1.12% accuracy, ViT gated = -1.19% accuracy. These gaps are small enough that W8A8 inference is practically equivalent to floating-point for most applications.

3. **Floating-point performance is maintained or slightly improved in all successful cases.** BERT clipped improves perplexity by 0.10; OPT gated improves by 0.29; ViT improves by 0.14–0.26 points of accuracy. The paper is appropriately cautious about this: "we are cautious about the improved performance as this is not consistent across all hyper-parameters and it is unclear if it generalizes to more architectures and larger models."

4. **The only failure is clipped softmax on OPT-125m.** The paper offers no explanation: "At the moment, we do not have an explanation of why this is the case and leave it for future work." This is a genuine limitation—it shows that the clipped softmax approach is not universally applicable even within the transformer family, and the causal mechanism may interact with architectural details (e.g., causal masking, LayerNorm placement) in ways not fully captured by the hypothesis.

5. **The choice of range estimation matters for PTQ.** For OPT-125m, 99.999% percentile ranges outperformed min-max. For ViT, adding LayerNorm after patch embeddings was critical: vanilla ViT (without patch embedding LN, Table 7 top half) has FP32 accuracy 80.75% but W8A8 accuracy drops to 69.24%; adding patch embedding LN (Table 7 bottom half) improves vanilla W8A8 to 79.62%, and the proposed methods further improve to ~79.8%. This suggests ViT's patch embeddings were an additional source of outliers that the architectural fix (LayerNorm after embeddings) addressed independently.

#### Scaling to Larger Models (Section 5.4, Table 3): Gated Attention Scales to 1.3B

Table 3 tests gated attention on OPT-350m and OPT-1.3B, trained for $10^5$ steps with batch size 256 (reduced training compared to OPT-125m due to compute constraints):

- **OPT-350m:**
  - Vanilla: FP16 perplexity 13.19, max infinity norm 253, avg kurtosis 2689, W8A8 perplexity 37.52 ± 3.84.
  - Gated attention: FP16 perplexity 13.01 (0.18 better), max infinity norm 65.4 (3.9× reduction), avg kurtosis 261 (10× reduction), W8A8 perplexity 14.42 ± 0.06 (within 1.41 of FP16).
  - The W8A8 improvement is dramatic (37.52 → 14.42) but the gap from FP16 is larger (1.41) than for OPT-125m (0.47). This may reflect the shorter training ($10^5$ vs. 125K steps at batch 192 = $2.4 \times 10^7$ samples for OPT-125m vs. $2.56 \times 10^7$ for these experiments—roughly comparable in samples but the larger models may need more steps to converge).

- **OPT-1.3B:**
  - Vanilla: FP16 perplexity 12.13, max infinity norm 428, avg kurtosis 2756, W8A8 perplexity 989.6 ± 175.
  - Gated attention: FP16 perplexity 12.21 (0.08 *worse* than vanilla), max infinity norm 67.2 (6.4× reduction), avg kurtosis 444 (6.2× reduction), W8A8 perplexity 29.95 ± 0.42.
  - W8A8 improves dramatically (989.6 → 29.95) but the gap from FP16 is substantial (17.74 perplexity points). This gap is much larger than for smaller models, suggesting that at 1.3B scale with limited training steps, either (a) the model is undertrained and gated attention hasn't fully converged, (b) residual outliers remain that require more sophisticated PTQ to handle, or (c) the gating mechanism interacts differently with larger model capacity.

These scaling results are encouraging but incomplete. The paper demonstrates that gated attention *can* work at larger scales (outliers are suppressed, W8A8 improves dramatically), but the W8A8-FP16 gap grows with scale, suggesting that the simple PTQ pipeline may not suffice at larger scales even with the architectural fix. The paper does not explore whether further PTQ optimization (better calibration, per-channel quantization, etc.) would close this gap, or whether the gap reflects a fundamental limitation of the approach at scale.

#### Qualitative Results (Section 5.5, Figure 8): Modified Models Learn Similar Patterns Without Outliers

Figure 8 visualizes attention patterns in BERT-base trained with clipped softmax and gated attention, compared to the vanilla patterns from Figure 2:

- **Clipped softmax (Figure 8b):** The attention head still allocates probability predominantly to delimiter-like tokens, but the probability distribution is more "saturated"—probabilities are either near-zero (diffuse but then clipped) or near-one (sharp). The values for delimiter tokens are not forced to be near-zero because the clipping mechanism provides true zeros without requiring small values. The product is a clean soft no-op.
- **Gated attention (Figure 8c):** The gating probabilities $\pi = \text{sigmoid}(G(x))$ (left column) show which tokens the head decides to update. The attention probabilities (second column) look qualitatively different from vanilla—they are more distributed because the head no longer needs to use attention probability concentration as the mechanism for no-op. The gating takes over that role. The final product (right column) represents the combined gating × attention × values, which achieves the selective update without outlier-producing extreme values.

This qualitative analysis is important because it shows the modified models have learned to achieve the same functional behavior (selective no-op) through the architecturally-provided mechanisms rather than through the outlier-producing softmax manipulation. The paper is demonstrating that it hasn't broken the attention mechanism's ability to learn useful patterns—it has just provided a cleaner implementation of one specific pattern.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Clipped softmax upper stretch ($\zeta > 1$) on ViT (Appendix B.5, Table 8):** Confirms the BERT finding from Table 1 that one-clipping adds no benefit. With $\gamma = 0$, varying $\zeta$ from 1.001 to 1.01 leaves max infinity norm in the 377–420 range (vanilla-like) and W8A8 accuracy around 70–72% (vanilla-like). With $\gamma < 0$ and $\zeta > 1$, performance is similar to $\gamma < 0$ with $\zeta = 1$. The paper concludes that "clipping at zero" is the only necessary modification for both language and vision transformers.

**Gating architecture choices (Appendix B.1, Table 4; Appendix B.2, Table 5; Appendix B.3, Table 6; Appendix B.4, Table 7):** Three gating function designs are compared:
- **Linear per-head:** $G_i(z) = w_i^T z + b_i$. The simplest and primary variant.
- **MLP per-head:** $G_i$ is a two-layer MLP with hidden size $n_{\text{hid}}$ and ReLU. More expressive but adds parameters (~$n_{\text{hid}}$ extra tokens per layer).
- **All-heads-linear:** A single linear layer from $\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$ to $\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{heads}}}$ that produces gating probabilities for all heads, allowing cross-head information sharing.

For BERT-base (Table 5):
- Linear ($\pi_{\text{init}} = 0.5$): W8A8 ppl 4.88 ± 0.22
- MLP ($n_{\text{hid}} = 4$): W8A8 ppl 4.65 ± 0.04
- MLP ($n_{\text{hid}} = 64$): W8A8 ppl 4.77 ± 0.01
- All-heads-linear: W8A8 ppl 4.67 ± 0.03

The MLP with small hidden size (4) performs best, but the improvement over Linear is modest (0.23 perplexity). The All-heads-linear variant also performs well, suggesting that cross-head information sharing is not critical. Overall, the gating architecture choice is not a major sensitivity—all variants dramatically outperform vanilla (W8A8 ppl 1294).

For OPT-125m (Table 6):
- Linear ($\pi_{\text{init}} = 0.25$): W8A8 ppl 16.25 ± 0.08
- All-heads-linear: W8A8 ppl 16.30 ± 0.12
- Both are essentially equivalent and dramatically better than vanilla (21.18 ± 1.89).

For ViT-S/16 (Table 7):
- Linear ($\pi_{\text{init}} = 0.25$): W8A8 acc 79.16 ± 0.05%
- Linear ($\pi_{\text{init}} = 0.5$): W8A8 acc 78.90 ± 0.25%
- MLP ($n_{\text{hid}} = 4$): W8A8 acc 78.79 ± 0.29%
- All variants are within 0.4% accuracy and dramatically better than vanilla (69.24%).

**Weight decay on LayerNorm $\gamma$ for OPT (Appendix B.3, Table 6):** An important negative finding: in early experiments, the paper discovered that applying weight decay to LayerNorm $\gamma$ parameters (which is not the default in most implementations) "has a strong effect on reducing the outliers' magnitude while yielding comparable FP16 performance." In Table 6, vanilla OPT-125m without LN weight decay has max infinity norm 340 and W8A8 perplexity 21.18; with LN weight decay, max infinity norm drops to 87.7 and W8A8 perplexity improves to 39.46 (still poor but notably better). Gated attention further improves on top of this, reaching W8A8 perplexity 16.02. This suggests that the outlier problem in OPT is partly exacerbated by the default optimization settings, and that simple changes to the training recipe can partially mitigate it—though not as effectively as the architectural modifications.

**Effect of patch embedding LayerNorm for ViT (Appendix B.4, Table 7):** Adding LayerNorm after patch embeddings (which is absent in the default ViT-S/16 definition) dramatically improves quantizability even for the vanilla model:
- Without patch embedding LN: vanilla W8A8 acc 69.24 ± 6.93%
- With patch embedding LN: vanilla W8A8 acc 79.62 ± 0.06%

This is a ~10 percentage point improvement from a single architectural change, suggesting that the patch embeddings were a second, independent source of outliers (likely due to the large dynamic range of raw pixel values fed into the embedding layer). With patch embedding LN in place, the proposed methods provide additional but smaller gains (clipped softmax: 79.77%; gated: 79.82%), closing the remaining gap to FP32 accuracy (~81%). This finding complicates the paper's narrative that outliers originate *only* from the attention-no-op mechanism—the patch embedding outliers appear to have a different origin.

**Low-bit quantization (Appendix B.7, Table 10):** The paper tests quantization below 8 bits on BERT-base:
- W6A8: Clipped softmax achieves 4.64 ± 0.01 ppl (vs. vanilla 598 ± 254 with min-max, or 6.49 ± 0.38 with MSE range estimation). Gated attention achieves 4.79 ± 0.03.
- W4A8 (MSE range estimation): Clipped softmax achieves 4.90 ± 0.02 ppl (vs. vanilla 6.52 ± 0.02). Gated attention achieves 5.02 ± 0.03.
- W6A6 (MSE): Clipped softmax achieves 6.64 ± 0.14 ppl (vs. vanilla 42.8 ± 11.7). Gated attention achieves 5.90 ± 0.11.

The trends show progressive degradation as bitwidth decreases—which is expected and reflects the fundamental precision limits of low-bit representations—but the modified models degrade *gracefully* rather than catastrophically. At W6A6, the vanilla model is essentially broken (perplexity 42.8) while the modified models remain usable (5.90–6.64). The paper notes that the vanilla model's apparent improvement when switching to MSE weight range estimation at lower bitwidths is because "MSE range estimation for weights leads to an implicit clipping of activations"—a fragile, uncontrolled effect that happens to suppress outliers in some configurations but cannot be relied upon.

The paper explicitly recommends combining its architectural modifications with advanced quantization techniques for low-bit regimes: "if achieving good low-bit quantization performance is the goal, it is recommended to combine our methods with more advanced quantization techniques."

**Fine-tuning pre-trained models with gated attention (Appendix B.6, Table 9):** This ablation addresses a key practical limitation: the proposed methods require training from scratch, which is expensive for very large models. Can gated attention be added during fine-tuning to reduce outliers in an already pre-trained model?

- OPT-1.3B fine-tuned for 4000 steps with vanilla softmax: FP16 perplexity 29.46, max infinity norm 79.3, avg kurtosis 2086.
- OPT-1.3B fine-tuned for 4000 steps with gated attention (initialized with $\pi_{\text{init}} = 0.5$, gating output multiplied by 2 to approximate vanilla attention initially, plus activation regularization): FP16 perplexity 29.18 (slightly better), max infinity norm 50.9 (36% reduction), avg kurtosis 665 (68% reduction).

The outlier reduction is significant but less dramatic than training from scratch (max infinity norm 50.9 vs. 8.7 for OPT-125m from scratch). This is expected: fine-tuning must "undo" the existing outliers while the model simultaneously adapts to the new gating mechanism. The result is encouraging as a practical compromise—it shows that gated attention can be retrofitted to pre-trained models with modest fine-tuning, substantially reducing outliers without retraining from scratch—but the paper does not report W8A8 perplexity for this experiment, which is a notable omission. It's unclear whether the residual outliers are still problematic for quantization.

**ReST$^{EM}$ experiment (mentioned in passing, not a main ablation):** The paper briefly mentions that an attempt to further optimize a revision model using ReST$^{EM}$ (Singh et al., 2024) caused performance to degrade with sequential revisions (Appendix K, Figure 16). This is a negative result that highlights the sensitivity of revision training to the data generation procedure.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: Activation outliers are caused by attention heads learning "no-op" behavior through softmax

This is the paper's central causal claim, and the evidence supporting it is **suggestive but not conclusive**. The strength of the evidence is the consistency of the pattern across architectures and modalities:
- In BERT (Figures 1, 2), 97% of FFN output outliers in layers #10–#11 correlate with delimiter token positions, and the corresponding attention heads in the next layer attend predominantly to those delimiters while learning small values for them.
- In ViT (Figures 3, 9, 16, 17), outliers correlate with background patches, and the corresponding attention heads attend to those patches with small values.
- The clipped softmax experiment (Table 1) provides strong *interventional* evidence: providing exact zeros with finite inputs (and zero gradient through clipping) eliminates outliers, exactly as the causal hypothesis predicts.

However, the evidence has important gaps:

**The causal direction is not experimentally demonstrated.** The paper shows a correlation between attention patterns in layer $L+1$ and outliers in the FFN output of layer $L$, but it does not experimentally manipulate the attention patterns and measure the effect on outliers. A stronger test would be: if you force an attention head to not attend to delimiters (by masking them out), do the corresponding outliers disappear? Alternatively, if you artificially inject a no-op attention head during training (one that always outputs zero), does that prevent outliers from forming in the previous layer? The paper's interventional evidence (clipped softmax and gated attention) shows that modifying the *ability* to produce no-ops eliminates outliers, but this is a systemic intervention—it modifies all heads simultaneously—and doesn't isolate the specific causal chain the paper hypothesizes.

**The 97% delimiter correlation is the only aggregate statistic.** The paper provides visualizations for specific heads (head #3, head #12) and layers (#10, #11) but does not systematically characterize how many attention heads show this pattern, how the pattern varies across layers, or what fraction of outlier dimensions are "explained" by downstream no-op attention. The qualitative evidence is compelling as illustration but insufficient as systematic proof. A more rigorous analysis would quantify: for every attention head in every layer, what is the correlation between its attention-to-delimiter probability and the outlier magnitude in the corresponding hidden dimension of the previous layer's FFN output? The paper only provides this for the most extreme examples.

**The ViT evidence is more qualitative than the BERT evidence.** The paper reports that outliers correlate with background patches and shows example images, but it does not provide a quantitative analog of the 97% statistic for ViT. The claim that outlier patches have "distinctly smaller magnitude of values compared to non-outlier ones" is supported by a bar chart in Figure 3e but no numerical values are reported. The cross-modal replication, while valuable, is less rigorous than the BERT analysis.

**The feedback loop mechanism (gradient always pushes toward more extreme values) is stated but not experimentally validated.** The paper argues that because softmax is everywhere-differentiable and never saturates, the gradient always pushes logits further apart. This is mathematically true, but the paper does not show gradient magnitudes during training to demonstrate that this is the actual mechanism driving outlier growth, as opposed to other factors (e.g., the optimization landscape, the specific loss function, weight decay settings). Showing that the gradient norm for outlier dimensions grows over training, and that this growth is halted by clipped softmax (because of zero gradient through clipped values), would provide direct evidence for the claimed mechanism.

**The failure of clipped softmax on OPT-125m is unexplained.** If the causal mechanism is universal, why does clipped softmax fail on OPT-125m? The paper speculates that the outlier mechanism might interact differently with causal masking or the autoregressive objective, but it does not analyze this failure case in detail (e.g., does clipped softmax fail to produce exact zeros in OPT? Do the outliers shift to different locations? Does the gradient dynamic persist despite clipping?). This failure case potentially limits the universality of the causal claim, and the lack of analysis is a significant gap. The fact that gated attention works on OPT suggests that the problem can be solved architecturally, but the specific failure mode of clipped softmax might reveal important boundary conditions for the causal mechanism.

#### Claim 2: Both proposed methods make transformers easy to quantize with minimal PTQ

This claim is **strongly supported** for the specific architectures, datasets, and PTQ pipeline tested, but with important caveats about scale and the definition of "minimal PTQ."

**What the experiments demonstrate:** For BERT-base and ViT-S/16, both clipped softmax and gated attention bring W8A8 performance within ~0.05–0.20 perplexity or ~1% accuracy of floating-point performance under the simplest PTQ pipeline (per-tensor symmetric weights, per-tensor asymmetric activations, min-max or MSE range estimation, no fine-tuning or distillation). This is a dramatic improvement from vanilla W8A8 performance (perplexity 1294 for BERT, accuracy 69.24% for ViT). The evidence is clear and consistent across multiple training runs (low standard deviations).

**What the experiments do not demonstrate:**

*Scalability to large models:* The evidence for models above 125M parameters is limited and mixed. OPT-350m with gated attention achieves W8A8 perplexity of 14.42 vs. FP16 13.01—a gap of 1.41 perplexity, which is substantially larger than the gap for OPT-125m (0.47). OPT-1.3B with gated attention achieves W8A8 perplexity of 29.95 vs. FP16 12.21—a gap of 17.74, which is large enough that the quantized model is of questionable practical utility. The paper attributes this to limited training steps ($10^5$ steps vs. the full training budget) and suggests that with full training the gap would shrink, but this is speculation without evidence. The trend (gap grows with model scale) is concerning and not adequately addressed.

*No comparison against sophisticated PTQ on the modified models:* The paper demonstrates that minimal PTQ works well on modified models, but it does not test whether sophisticated PTQ (per-channel quantization, learned rounding, knowledge distillation) on the modified models would further close the W8A8–FP16 gap for larger models. The failure mode is unclear: are the residual W8A8 errors at larger scales due to remaining outliers (which better PTQ could handle) or due to fundamental precision limits that no PTQ can overcome?

*The "minimal PTQ" pipeline includes some non-trivial choices:* For OPT, the paper uses MSE range estimation for weights and 99.999% percentile ranges for activations rather than simple min-max. These are not exotic techniques, but they are not the absolute simplest PTQ either. The paper acknowledges this and reports selecting the best range estimation method for each model, but this introduces a degree of freedom that weakens the "minimal PTQ" claim slightly.

*The ViT results depend on an additional architectural change (patch embedding LayerNorm):* The vanilla ViT's W8A8 accuracy drops from 80.75% to 69.24% due to outliers that appear to originate from the patch embeddings, not from the attention-no-op mechanism. Adding LayerNorm after patch embeddings improves vanilla W8A8 to 79.62%, and the proposed methods further improve to ~79.8%. This means that a significant fraction of the ViT quantization improvement comes from the patch embedding LayerNorm, not from clipped softmax or gated attention. The paper's framing ("our methods enable quantization") overstates the contribution of the proposed methods for ViT specifically—the baseline with just patch embedding LayerNorm (79.62%) is already close to the methods with both changes (~79.8%).

#### Claim 3: The methods maintain or improve floating-point performance

This claim is **conditionally supported** with several important caveats that the paper acknowledges but does not fully resolve.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Across the three model architectures and both methods (except clipped softmax on OPT), FP16/FP32 performance is either maintained within statistical noise or slightly improved:
- BERT clipped: 4.39 vs. 4.49 vanilla (0.10 better)
- BERT gated: 4.45 vs. 4.49 vanilla (0.04 better)
- OPT gated: 15.55 vs. 15.84 vanilla (0.29 better)
- ViT clipped: 80.89% vs. 80.75% vanilla (0.14 points better)
- ViT gated: 81.01% vs. 80.75% vanilla (0.26 points better)

**Caveats:**

*The improvements are small and may not be statistically significant.* The paper reports standard deviations based on 2 training runs, but 2 runs are insufficient to establish whether the observed differences (e.g., 0.10 perplexity) are real effects or sampling noise. To put 0.10 perplexity in context: the standard deviation for vanilla BERT is ±0.01, so a 0.10 difference is ~10 standard deviations—likely real. But the standard deviation for BERT gated is ±0.03, meaning the 0.04 improvement could easily be noise. The paper does not report the distribution of individual training runs, only means and standard deviations.

*The paper is appropriately cautious about the improvements.* It states: "we are cautious about the improved performance as this is not consistent across all hyper-parameters and it is unclear if it generalizes to more architectures and larger models." This is an honest acknowledgment that the FP16 improvements, while consistently positive in direction, are not large enough or consistent enough across configurations to claim as a robust finding.

*The improvements are plausibly explained by the hypothesis but not proven to be caused by it.* The hypothesis suggests that by providing a cleaner mechanism for no-op behavior, the model can allocate its representational capacity more efficiently, leading to better task performance. This is plausible but untested—the paper does not analyze whether the FP16 perplexity improvement comes from better no-op behavior specifically or from some other effect of the architectural modifications (e.g., the clipping or gating acting as a regularizer, or changing the optimization landscape in ways unrelated to the no-op mechanism).

*For OPT-1.3B, FP16 perplexity is slightly worse with gated attention (12.21 vs. 12.13).* This is a small difference (0.08) and could be noise given the limited training, but it suggests that at larger scales, the FP16 performance may not always improve and could even slightly degrade—especially if the model is undertrained.

#### Claim 4: The phenomenon is universal across transformer architectures and modalities

This claim is **supported in spirit but incomplete in evidence**. The paper demonstrates the pattern in three architectures (BERT encoder, OPT decoder, ViT) and two modalities (text, images), which covers the main branches of the transformer family tree. The consistency of the pattern—outliers correlate with low-information tokens/patches that downstream attention heads use for no-op behavior—is striking and makes a strong circumstantial case for universality.

**Limitations:**

*Three architectures is a small sample.* There are many important transformer variants not tested: encoder-decoder models (T5, BART), models with different attention mechanisms (sparse attention, linear attention, flash attention), models with different normalization schemes (RMSNorm, pre-norm vs. post-norm), and models with different activation functions (GeLU, SwiGLU). The paper's causal mechanism depends specifically on the interaction of softmax, residual connections, and LayerNorm—any architecture that changes one of these components might not exhibit the same pattern.

*Only one vision transformer configuration (ViT-S/16) is tested.* Larger ViT variants, or ViTs trained on different datasets, or hierarchical vision transformers (Swin, CaiT) might exhibit different patterns. The paper's qualitative analysis for ViT is less rigorous than for BERT, making the cross-modal generalization less certain than it appears.

*The failure of clipped softmax on OPT-125m weakens the universality claim.* If the causal mechanism is truly universal—if all transformers with softmax attention inevitably learn outliers—then clipped softmax should work universally. Its failure on OPT suggests that there may be important architectural or training recipe differences that modulate the outlier mechanism in ways the paper's hypothesis doesn't capture. The paper's decision to leave this unexplained is a significant gap.

*No decoder-only model larger than 125M is tested with full training.* The scaling experiments (OPT-350m, OPT-1.3B) use reduced training steps and only test gated attention. The behavior of clipped softmax at scale, and the behavior of both methods under full training for larger models, is unknown. The trend toward larger W8A8–FP16 gaps at larger scales (even with gated attention) raises the possibility that the proposed methods become less effective as models grow, which would limit their practical impact for the very large models where quantization matters most.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Strengthen the Paper

1. **Fine-tuning on downstream tasks:** The paper evaluates BERT only on MLM perplexity (the pre-training objective). It does not fine-tune on GLUE or other downstream benchmarks to check whether the architectural modifications preserve downstream task performance. This is a standard evaluation for BERT variants and its absence is notable. It's possible that the clipped softmax or gated attention, while maintaining MLM perplexity, subtly changes the learned representations in ways that hurt transfer learning. The MNLI fine-tuning experiment in the outlier analysis (Section 3) is done on a pre-trained vanilla BERT, not on a model trained from scratch with the proposed modifications.

2. **Direct gradient analysis:** The paper hypothesizes that the softmax gradient always pushes toward more extreme logits, driving outlier growth. A simple experiment—plot the gradient norm of the FFN output in outlier dimensions over the course of training, comparing vanilla vs. clipped softmax—would directly test this mechanism and provide compelling evidence for or against the causal hypothesis.

3. **Attention head ablation:** If the hypothesis is correct, then ablating (masking out) the attention heads that attend to delimiter tokens should reduce outliers in the previous layer's FFN output. This would provide interventional evidence for the specific causal chain (head behavior → outliers) rather than just the systemic intervention (architectural change → no outliers).

4. **Systematic head-by-head analysis:** The paper shows examples for head #3 and head #12 (the heads corresponding to the most extreme outlier dimensions). A full analysis—for every head in every layer, what fraction of its attention goes to delimiter tokens? what is the correlation with outlier magnitude in the corresponding hidden dimension of the previous layer?—would quantify how much of the outlier phenomenon is explained by the no-op mechanism versus other factors.

5. **Ablation of the specific feedback loop components:** The paper could test whether eliminating specific parts of the feedback loop reduces outliers. For example: freezing the FFN output of layer $L$ during training (removing the ability to grow outliers) or adding noise to the LayerNorm statistics (disrupting the normalization that necessitates larger FFN outputs). These would isolate which steps in the causal chain are necessary.

6. **Evaluation with more sophisticated PTQ on modified models:** The paper demonstrates that minimal PTQ works, but it doesn't test whether sophisticated PTQ (per-channel, learned rounding, etc.) can push W8A8 performance even closer to FP16, particularly for larger models where gaps remain. This would clarify whether the residual quantization error is due to remaining outliers (which advanced PTQ could handle) or fundamental precision limits.

7. **Testing on a broader range of architectures and training recipes:** Encoder-decoder models (T5), models with pre-norm (GPT-3 style), models with RMSNorm, models trained on different datasets (C4, The Pile), and models with different activation functions would test the universality claim more thoroughly.

#### Summary Assessment

The paper's central achievement is **diagnostic**: it identifies a specific, plausible causal mechanism linking a well-known attention behavior (attending to delimiter tokens as a "no-op") to the long-standing problem of activation outliers that break quantization. The proposed architectural modifications follow logically from this mechanism and work effectively on the tested models, dramatically improving quantizability under minimal PTQ. The paper makes a compelling case that addressing the root cause is more elegant and effective than the complex post-hoc workarounds that dominate the literature.

However, the evidence for the causal mechanism is more suggestive than conclusive—it is based on correlational analysis and qualitative visualization rather than direct intervention. The failure of clipped softmax on OPT-125m, the growing W8A8–FP16 gap at larger scales, the dependence of ViT results on an additional architectural change (patch embedding LayerNorm), and the lack of downstream task evaluation all represent significant gaps. The paper's claims about universality and scalability are plausible but under-evidenced.

The most important open question is whether these methods scale to the very large models (10B+ parameters) where quantization matters most and where the existing post-hoc approaches (LLM.int8(), SmoothQuant) are most complex. The trend in Table 3—W8A8–FP16 gap growing from 0.47 (125M) to 1.41 (350M) to 17.74 (1.3B), even with the caveat of reduced training—is concerning and needs investigation before these methods can be recommended as a replacement for post-hoc approaches at scale.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### No Downstream Task Evaluation for BERT or OPT

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates all language model experiments exclusively on the pre-training objective—masked language modeling perplexity for BERT and causal language modeling perplexity for OPT—without fine-tuning on any downstream benchmarks. This is a conscious scope limitation: the paper's primary claim is about enabling quantization, and pre-training perplexity is used as a proxy for model quality that would transfer to downstream tasks. Section 5 states: "We evaluate on Wikipedia validation set and report the MLM perplexity" for BERT, and "We evaluate on Wikipedia validation set and report the CLM perplexity" for OPT. No GLUE, SQuAD, or other standard benchmarks are reported.

**The consequence.** The paper cannot guarantee that the proposed architectural modifications preserve downstream task performance. The causal mechanism the paper identifies—attention heads learning "no-op" behavior by attending to delimiter tokens—could serve functional roles beyond what perplexity captures. For instance, the ability to selectively not update representations of certain tokens might be important for tasks requiring nuanced handling of entity boundaries, coreference resolution, or syntactic structure. If clipped softmax or gated attention alters *how* the model achieves no-op behavior (the paper's qualitative analysis in Figure 8 shows that the attention patterns look qualitatively different with the modifications, not just that outliers are suppressed), this could change the learned representations in ways that affect fine-tuning transfer even if pre-training perplexity is maintained or slightly improved.

This is not a hypothetical concern. The paper itself demonstrates that the modifications change attention behavior: clipped softmax produces more "saturated" (near-zero or near-one) attention distributions, and gated attention fundamentally decouples the no-op decision from the attention distribution. A downstream task that depends on the *gradations* of attention probability—not just whether an update occurs but *how* the model distributes probability between informative and non-informative tokens—could be differently affected by these changes even if the pre-training loss is similar. The paper's qualitative Figure 8 is suggestive that attention patterns change, but it does not quantify the downstream impact of these changes.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper's outlier analysis in Section 3 uses a pre-trained BERT-base fine-tuned on MNLI to establish the attention pattern–outlier correlation, but the models trained from scratch with the proposed modifications are never fine-tuned. The paper reports only pre-training perplexity and W8A8 perplexity for language models. This gap is stated explicitly: the paper evaluates only the pre-training objective for all language model experiments. The ViT experiments include ImageNet top-1 accuracy, which is a downstream task (image classification) evaluated directly after pre-training, so the vision experiments partially address this concern—but the language experiments do not.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, does not discuss what downstream behaviors might be affected, and does not suggest future work on downstream evaluation. The ViT results (maintained or slightly improved ImageNet accuracy) provide some reassurance that the modifications do not fundamentally damage learned representations, but this is for a different modality and architecture. The fact that this is a standard evaluation expectation for BERT variants (e.g., Devlin et al., 2019; Sanh et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2020 all report GLUE scores) makes its absence notable and would matter to any practitioner considering adopting these architectural changes for a model intended for downstream fine-tuning.

### Scalability Evidence Is Insufficient for Production Models

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's main experiments are conducted on relatively small models by modern standards: BERT-base (109M parameters), OPT-125m (125M), and ViT-S/16 (22M). The scaling experiments on OPT-350m and OPT-1.3B (Table 3) use only gated attention, only the OPT decoder architecture, and are trained for only 100K steps (approximately $2.56 \times 10^7$ samples, which is substantially less than the full OPT pre-training recipe). The paper states this constraint openly: "Due to compute constraints, we trained networks for $10^5$ steps with batch size of 256."

**The consequence.** The W8A8–FP16 gap appears to grow with model scale, even for gated attention—the paper's most successful method on OPT. Specifically, the W8A8-to-FP16 perplexity difference increases from 0.47 for OPT-125m (16.02 vs. 15.55) to 1.41 for OPT-350m (14.42 vs. 13.01) to 17.74 for OPT-1.3B (29.95 vs. 12.21). While the paper correctly notes that limited training steps may account for part of this gap (the models may be undertrained, and gated attention may need more steps to converge), this hypothesis is untested. An alternative explanation—that larger models have more layers and wider hidden dimensions, making the residual outlier problem harder to fully suppress with a per-layer gating mechanism—is equally plausible given the data.

This matters critically for the paper's practical impact. The models where quantization is most valuable—large language models with billions of parameters, whose inference costs dominate deployment budgets—are precisely the models where the paper's evidence is weakest. The $10^5$ steps used for OPT-1.3B represent perhaps 5–10% of the training budget that would be used in a production pre-training run. The paper cannot distinguish between "gated attention needs more training to work at scale" (which would be an acceptable limitation—just train longer) and "gated attention fundamentally works less well at scale" (which would severely limit the method's applicability). The upward trend in W8A8–FP16 gap from 125M to 1.3B, while confounded by training budget, is concerning enough that a practitioner considering this method for a 7B or 13B model would need more evidence.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 provides the only scaling data. OPT-350m shows W8A8 perplexity 14.42 vs. FP16 13.01 (a 1.41 gap, larger than OPT-125m's 0.47). OPT-1.3B shows W8A8 perplexity 29.95 vs. FP16 12.21 (a 17.74 gap, so large that the quantized model is arguably not usable). The paper interprets this optimistically—noting that "gated attention is also very effective at dampening the outliers and significantly improving the quantized model performance when applied to bigger models"—which is true in a relative sense (compared to vanilla W8A8 perplexity of ~990 for OPT-1.3B on an undertrained run, gated attention's 29.95 is a dramatic improvement) but misleading in an absolute sense (29.95 perplexity on Wikipedia is poor enough that the quantized model would fail at most practical generation tasks). The paper does not report max infinity norm or kurtosis for the 350m and 1.3B vanilla baselines at equal training steps, making it impossible to assess how much of the W8A8 improvement is due to outlier suppression versus simply having a better-trained floating-point model.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges the compute constraint ("Due to compute constraints, we trained networks for $10^5$ steps") but does not discuss the implications of the growing W8A8–FP16 gap, does not analyze whether additional training would close the gap, and does not explicitly flag scalability as an open question for future work. Section 6 (Discussion) states "we haven't explored the case of very large transformers that are trained for way longer" and "Given the fundamental understanding of the issue underlying our solutions, we expect the same effect on larger-scale models." This is a claim of expected generalization, not evidence. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt these methods for a large-scale training run would need to weigh this untested expectation against the concrete scaling trend in Table 3.

### Requires Training From Scratch (or Substantial Fine-Tuning) — Cannot Be Applied to Existing Pre-Trained Models

**The assumption or constraint.** Both clipped softmax and gated attention are architectural modifications that must be present during the entire pre-training process to prevent outliers from forming. They cannot be applied as post-hoc fixes to already-trained transformers. The paper states this constraint implicitly throughout: all main experiments train models from scratch with the modifications. Section 3 frames the approach as changing "the architecture of the network itself" to "make transformers easy to quantize from the get-go without needing any post-processing." The introduction explicitly contrasts this with prior approaches that "suggest fixes to transformers after training to ameliorate their effect."

**The consequence.** The vast majority of transformer models in use today—BERT-base, RoBERTa, GPT-2, OPT, LLaMA, etc.—have already been pre-trained with vanilla softmax attention. None of those models can benefit from clipped softmax or gated attention without retraining. This is a fundamental tradeoff: the paper's approach sacrifices **applicability to existing models** in exchange for **simplicity and effectiveness on models trained from scratch**. For an organization that wants to quantize an existing pre-trained model (by far the most common use case—downloading a checkpoint from HuggingFace and quantizing it for deployment), the paper's methods provide no benefit. Such an organization would still need to resort to LLM.int8(), SmoothQuant, or QAT—the very approaches the paper argues are unnecessarily complex.

The paper's fine-tuning experiment (Appendix B.6, Table 9) partially addresses this by showing that gated attention can be retrofitted to a pre-trained OPT-1.3B via fine-tuning, reducing max infinity norm from 79.3 to 50.9 (a 36% reduction) and kurtosis from 2086 to 665 (a 68% reduction). However, this is substantially less effective than training from scratch (compare to OPT-125m gated from scratch: max infinity norm 8.7, kurtosis 18.9). More critically, the fine-tuning experiment does not report W8A8 perplexity, so we do not know whether the residual outliers (max infinity norm still 50.9, kurtosis still 665) are problematic for quantization. The paper also adds an activation regularization term during fine-tuning ("a small activation regularization term at the output of each FFN to further encourage the reduction in the magnitude of activations"), which introduces an additional hyperparameter and training complexity not present in the from-scratch approach. The fine-tuning experiment demonstrates that retrofitting is possible but does not demonstrate that it is sufficient.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The contrast between from-scratch training (Table 2) and fine-tuning (Table 9) quantifies this tradeoff. From-scratch gated attention on OPT-125m achieves max infinity norm 8.7, kurtosis 18.9, and W8A8 perplexity 16.02 (within 0.47 of FP16). Fine-tuning gated attention on pre-trained OPT-1.3B reduces max infinity norm from 79.3 to 50.9 and kurtosis from 2086 to 665 but does not report W8A8 performance. The absence of W8A8 results for the fine-tuning experiment is a notable omission—it prevents any assessment of whether the fine-tuning approach yields a practically quantizable model.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges that training from scratch "could be expensive when applied to very large models" (Appendix B.6) and provides the fine-tuning experiment as a partial mitigation. However, the fine-tuning results are incomplete (no W8A8 perplexity), use a different model scale (1.3B vs. 125M from scratch), and have not been validated across architectures. The paper does not explore alternative mitigation strategies—such as continued pre-training (rather than fine-tuning on a downstream dataset) with the architectural modifications, or combining the modifications with distillation from a pre-trained vanilla model—that might reduce the cost of adoption. For a practitioner with an existing pre-trained model, the paper offers no validated path to benefit from its methods.

### Interaction Between Clipped Softmax and Causal Masking Is Unexplained and Results in Failure

**The assumption or constraint.** The causal hypothesis (Section 3) proposes a universal mechanism: attention heads wanting to "do nothing" → softmax requiring extreme inputs for zero outputs → outliers in previous layer's FFN output. This mechanism makes no distinction between bidirectional (encoder) and causal (decoder) attention. The paper assumes the clipped softmax fix should therefore work across both attention types. However, clipped softmax fails on OPT-125m (Table 2): W8A8 perplexity degrades from 21.18 (vanilla) to 37.20 (clipped softmax), and average kurtosis *increases* from 1778 to 19728. The paper states: "At the moment, we do not have an explanation of why this is the case and leave it for future work."

**The consequence.** This failure is not a small quantitative degradation; it is a qualitative reversal—the method makes both floating-point and quantized performance *worse* than vanilla, and actually increases kurtosis (more extreme outliers) rather than decreasing it. This matters for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that the causal mechanism is not as universal as the paper claims. There is some interaction between the causal attention mask and the clipped softmax that the hypothesis does not capture, and until that interaction is understood, clipped softmax cannot be recommended for causal language models (GPT-style decoders) without extensive testing. Since autoregressive language models are the dominant architecture for large-scale deployment (GPT, LLaMA, Mistral, etc.), this failure mode substantially limits the scope of clipped softmax's applicability.

Second, the failure of clipped softmax on OPT undermines the paper's explanatory framework. If the causal hypothesis is correct—if outliers are caused by attention heads needing to produce zeros through softmax and softmax requiring infinite inputs—then providing exact zeros through clipping *must* reduce outliers, because the gradient feedback loop is broken. The fact that it doesn't work on OPT suggests either (a) the causal hypothesis is incomplete (there are outlier-producing mechanisms beyond the no-op attention pattern that clipped softmax doesn't address, or that clipped softmax interacts differently with causal masking), or (b) clipped softmax introduces a new problem (e.g., the clipping creates discontinuities in the gradient landscape that destabilize autoregressive training, or the causal mask changes the distribution of softmax outputs such that clipping at a fixed threshold is inappropriate). Without understanding which of these is true, the paper's central diagnostic contribution—that outliers are caused by the softmax-no-op mechanism—is weakened for decoder architectures.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2 provides the key evidence: OPT-125m with clipped softmax ($\gamma = -12/512$, chosen from a sweep in Table 6) achieves FP16 perplexity 16.29 ± 0.07 (worse than vanilla 15.84 ± 0.05), max infinity norm 63.2 ± 8.8 (better than vanilla 340 ± 47, but the improvement is only ~5× compared to the ~35× improvement for BERT), average kurtosis 19728 ± 7480 (dramatically *worse* than vanilla 1778 ± 444), and W8A8 perplexity 37.20 ± 2.40 (worse than vanilla 21.18 ± 1.89). The hyperparameter sweep in Table 6 shows that the failure is not a matter of choosing the wrong $\gamma$: across $\gamma$ values from $-1/512$ to $-12/512$, FP16 perplexity ranges from 15.86 to 16.29 (all worse than vanilla's 15.84 with LayerNorm weight decay), kurtosis ranges from 5764 to 19727 (all worse than vanilla's 2080), and W8A8 perplexity ranges from 37.22 to 185.23 (all worse than vanilla's 39.46 with LN weight decay). The failure is systematic across the entire hyperparameter range tested.

The paper does not provide any diagnostic analysis of the OPT failure: no attention pattern visualizations for the clipped softmax OPT model, no analysis of where the increased kurtosis comes from, no gradient analysis comparing OPT and BERT training dynamics with clipped softmax. The failure is reported but not investigated.

**Mitigation status.** Not at all. The paper acknowledges the failure ("At the moment, we do not have an explanation") and states it will "leave it for future work," but provides no analysis, no hypotheses, and no guidance for practitioners who might want to use clipped softmax with causal language models. The only practical mitigation is to use gated attention instead—which does work on OPT—but this requires accepting the gated attention's higher parameter count and compute overhead, and does not explain *why* clipped softmax fails. A practitioner considering both methods for a new transformer architecture would need to test both, with no theoretical guidance on which will work.

### No Direct Interventional Evidence for the Causal Hypothesis — Only Correlational and Systemic Evidence

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central intellectual contribution is the causal hypothesis: attention heads learn no-op behavior → softmax cannot output exact zeros → FFN outputs grow to produce extreme logits → outliers appear in specific hidden dimensions of the previous layer. The paper supports this hypothesis through (1) correlational analysis showing that outlier dimensions correspond to attention heads that attend to delimiter tokens with small values (Figures 1–3), and (2) systemic interventions showing that modifying the architecture to allow zero outputs eliminates outliers (Tables 1–2). However, the paper never performs a **targeted intervention** that isolates the specific causal chain: it never manipulates individual attention heads or specific hidden dimensions to demonstrate that the hypothesized mechanism is the actual cause of outliers, rather than being correlated with them.

**The consequence.** The paper cannot rule out alternative explanations for the correlation between delimiter-attending heads and outlier dimensions. For example: (a) The outliers might cause the attention patterns, not vice versa—perhaps the network first develops outliers for some other reason (optimization dynamics, activation function properties) and then attention heads learn to attend to delimiter tokens *because* those tokens happen to receive the outlier-driven large activations. (b) A third factor might cause both outliers and no-op attention patterns—perhaps the training data distribution or the MLM objective creates pressure for certain hidden dimensions to specialize in delimiter detection, which manifests as both outliers (for detection) and delimiter-attending heads (for no-op). (c) The outliers might serve a functional role other than enabling no-op behavior—prior work (Kovaleva et al., 2021; Puccetti et al., 2021) showed that outlier dimensions carry important linguistic information, and the correlation with delimiter tokens might be incidental.

The distinction matters for the paper's broader claims. If the causal hypothesis is correct, the proposed methods are **complete**—providing alternative zero-output mechanisms eliminates the root cause of outliers, and no further architectural changes should be needed. If the causal hypothesis is incorrect or incomplete, the methods might be **palliative**—they suppress the *symptom* (outliers) by modifying the attention mechanism, but other outlier-producing mechanisms might remain or emerge in different architectures, training recipes, or at larger scales. The fact that (a) clipped softmax fails on OPT-125m, (b) kurtosis actually increases for clipped softmax on OPT (suggesting some outlier-producing dynamic persists or is created), and (c) ViT requires an additional architectural change (patch embedding LayerNorm) that addresses outliers from a source clearly *not* related to attention no-op behavior—all suggest that the causal hypothesis may be incomplete.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's evidence for the causal hypothesis is entirely correlational and systemic:

*Correlational:* (1) In BERT, 97% of outliers in FFN output layers #10–#11 correlate with delimiter token positions, and the attention heads in the next layer corresponding to outlier dimensions attend predominantly to delimiters with small values (Section 3, Figures 1–2). (2) In ViT, outliers correlate with background patches, and corresponding attention heads attend to those patches with smaller values (Section 3, Figures 3, 9). (3) The paper provides no quantification of what fraction of all attention heads exhibit the no-op pattern, what fraction of all outlier dimensions are "explained" by downstream no-op heads, or what the baseline correlation would be between arbitrary attention heads and delimiter tokens.

*Systemic:* (1) Clipped softmax (which allows exact zeros) eliminates outliers in BERT and ViT (Tables 1–2, 8). (2) Gated attention (which provides an alternative zero-output mechanism) eliminates outliers across all architectures (Tables 2, 5–7). Both interventions modify every attention head in every layer, so they demonstrate that the architecture as a whole benefits from zero-output capability, but they do not isolate whether the benefit comes specifically from the no-op heads hypothesized to be the causal source.

*No targeted interventions:* The paper does not (a) ablate or mask the delimiter-attending heads and measure the effect on outliers in the previous layer, (b) artificially inject no-op heads during training and measure whether outliers form, (c) analyze gradient flow through the specific hypothesized feedback loop (FFN → LayerNorm → QK dot product → softmax), or (d) freeze the FFN output of outlier-producing layers and measure whether the attention patterns change.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The hypothesis is presented as established ("We find that the outliers occur because attention heads are trying not to update the hidden state"), and the systemic interventions are treated as sufficient validation. Section 3 frames the analysis as supporting the hypothesis but does not discuss alternative explanations or the limitations of correlational evidence. The Discussion (Section 6) speculates about broader implications ("the identified 'no-op' behavior is likely not limited to transformers") but does not address the evidentiary gap between correlation and causation. A more cautious framing—acknowledging that the evidence supports but does not prove the causal mechanism—would better reflect the strength of the evidence while maintaining the paper's practical contributions.

### ViT Quantization Improvement Partly Attributable to Patch Embedding LayerNorm, Not the Proposed Methods

**The assumption or constraint.** The ViT experiments include an additional architectural modification beyond clipped softmax or gated attention: adding LayerNorm after the patch embeddings. The paper introduces this change after preliminary experiments revealed that "distinct outliers already originate after the patch embeddings" (Appendix B.4). The vanilla ViT-S/16 definition (from Dosovitskiy et al., 2020) does not include this LayerNorm. The paper's main ViT results (Table 2, bottom half of Table 7) all use this additional LayerNorm for both the vanilla baseline and the proposed methods.

**The consequence.** The dramatic improvement in ViT W8A8 accuracy cannot be attributed solely to clipped softmax or gated attention. A significant portion of the improvement comes from the patch embedding LayerNorm, which addresses outliers originating from a source unrelated to the attention-no-op mechanism the paper identifies. Table 7 makes this clear: vanilla ViT *without* patch embedding LN achieves FP32 accuracy 80.75% but W8A8 accuracy only 69.24% ± 6.93% (a catastrophic 11.5 percentage point drop). Adding just the patch embedding LN (bottom half of Table 7) improves vanilla W8A8 accuracy to 79.62% ± 0.06%—a **10.4 percentage point gain** from the LayerNorm alone. Adding clipped softmax or gated attention on top of this provides an additional 0.15–0.20 percentage points (to 79.77% and 79.82%, respectively). The proposed methods are responsible for closing the remaining ~0.4 percentage point gap between vanilla+LNLN and FP32 accuracy (~81%), not for the bulk of the improvement from 69.24% to 79.62%.

This matters for the paper's framing. The abstract claims the methods "enable us to quantize transformers to full INT8 quantization of the activations without any additional effort." For ViT, the "additional effort" includes an architectural change (patch embedding LayerNorm) that is not part of the proposed methods and that provides ~96% of the total W8A8 improvement (10.4 out of 10.8 percentage points gained from 69.24% vanilla to ~80% with proposed methods + patch embedding LN). The paper's narrative—that the attention-no-op mechanism is the root cause of outliers and the proposed methods solve it—is complicated by the fact that the largest source of quantization error in ViT comes from patch embeddings, not from the attention layers.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 7 provides a direct comparison with and without patch embedding LayerNorm. The top half (without patch embedding LN) shows: vanilla W8A8 accuracy 69.24%, clipped softmax (γ=-0.003) W8A8 accuracy 76.49% (a 7.25 point gain from clipped softmax alone), gated attention (Linear, π_init=0.25) W8A8 accuracy 79.16% (a 9.92 point gain from gated attention alone). The bottom half (with patch embedding LN) shows: vanilla W8A8 accuracy 79.62%, clipped softmax W8A8 accuracy 79.77%, gated attention W8A8 accuracy 79.82%. The marginal gain from the proposed methods shrinks dramatically when patch embedding LN is present: from 7.25/9.92 points to 0.15/0.20 points for clipped/gated respectively. This suggests that (a) the patch embeddings were a dominant source of outliers in the vanilla ViT, (b) the proposed methods alone can partially compensate for this source even without patch embedding LN (especially gated attention), but (c) the simplest fix—adding LayerNorm after patch embeddings—addresses the dominant source more directly.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the patch embedding LayerNorm addition in Appendix B.4 ("After our preliminary experiments on ViT, we noticed that distinct outliers already originate after the patch embeddings. Therefore, we experimented with adding the LayerNorm after the patch embeddings") but does not discuss the implications for its central claims. The main text (Section 5.4) presents the ViT results with patch embedding LN without noting the attribution issue. The abstract and introduction's claims about the proposed methods "removing outliers" and "enabling quantization" do not distinguish between outliers that originate from the attention-no-op mechanism and outliers that originate from other sources (like patch embeddings). A practitioner reading the ViT results might incorrectly attribute the full improvement to clipped softmax or gated attention, when in fact most of it comes from an unrelated architectural fix that the paper discovered but does not claim as a contribution. This is not a methodological error—the comparison is fair (vanilla and modified models both have the patch embedding LN)—but the narrative framing overstates the methods' contribution to ViT quantizability specifically.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the field's approach to transformer quantization from **post-hoc mitigation** to **architectural prevention**. Before this work, the dominant paradigm treated activation outliers as an unexplained empirical regularity—something that "just happens" during transformer training—and the research community responded with increasingly complex quantization schemes designed to work around them. LLM.int8() (Dettmers et al., 2022) uses mixed-precision matrix multiplication with custom CUDA kernels to keep outlier dimensions in FP16. SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022) mathematically migrates the quantization difficulty from activations to weights through per-channel scaling transformations. QAT methods (Bhalgat et al., 2020; Esser et al., 2020) incorporate simulated quantization noise into the training loop so the model learns to be robust to it. Each of these approaches is effective in its own right, but each adds complexity to the deployment pipeline and none addresses *why* the outliers exist.

This paper's reframing—"outliers are not inevitable; they are a consequence of a specific architectural choice that can be changed"—represents a conceptual inversion of the problem. Rather than accepting outliers as a constraint and engineering around them, the paper asks: what if we change the architecture so that the model never needs to produce outliers in the first place? The answer turns out to be remarkably simple: provide the attention mechanism with a way to output exact zeros without requiring infinite-magnitude inputs, and the training dynamics that produce outliers never arise.

The magnitude of this shift should not be overstated—this is **not a paradigm shift** in the Kuhnian sense. The transformer architecture remains fundamentally unchanged; the proposed modifications are drop-in replacements for a single operation (softmax) or small auxiliary modules (gating networks). The paper does not challenge the core design principles of transformers (attention, residual connections, layer normalization) but rather identifies a specific, unintended interaction among them and proposes targeted fixes. What the paper does accomplish is a **diagnostic reframing**: it converts the outlier problem from a mysterious nuisance into a predictable, mechanistically-understood consequence of architectural choices. This makes the solutions obvious in retrospect, which is the hallmark of good diagnostic work.

The paper also resolves a latent contradiction in the prior literature. Several earlier works had observed that some BERT attention heads attend almost exclusively to delimiter tokens (Clark et al., 2019; Kovaleva et al., 2019) and speculated that this functions as a "no-op" when the head's computation is not applicable. Separately, other works had documented that activation outliers concentrate in specific hidden dimensions and are functionally important (Bondarenko et al., 2021; Kovaleva et al., 2021). Still others had shown that the outlier problem scales with model size (Dettmers et al., 2022). These observations were treated as independent phenomena. This paper connects them into a single causal chain: attention heads learn no-op behavior → softmax mathematically requires extreme inputs to produce near-zero outputs → those extreme inputs come from the previous layer's FFN → LayerNorm partially normalizes them, forcing the FFN to produce even larger values → the gradient always pushes toward more extreme values because softmax never saturates → outliers grow with training duration and model scale. Each prior observation becomes a necessary link in this chain rather than a separate curiosity.

The most important consequence for the research landscape is that it **redirects effort from quantization workarounds toward architectural foundations**. If the paper's causal hypothesis is correct—and the success of both proposed methods across three architectures provides substantial evidence—then much of the PTQ literature's complexity is addressing a problem that shouldn't exist. Improving per-channel calibration, developing better mixed-precision schemes, and designing QAT recipes for transformers are all valuable engineering efforts, but they are solving a problem that could be eliminated at its source with a few lines of architectural code. The paper's results suggest that the simplest possible PTQ pipeline (per-tensor quantization with min-max range estimation, no fine-tuning, no distillation) achieves near-lossless W8A8 performance when the underlying model is outlier-free (BERT W8A8 perplexity 4.52 vs. FP16 4.39; ViT W8A8 accuracy 79.77% vs. FP32 80.89%). This implies that future research on transformer quantization should prioritize understanding and eliminating the sources of outliers over developing ever-more-sophisticated compensation mechanisms.

A subtler shift is in how the field should think about **the relationship between architecture and numerical robustness**. The paper demonstrates that a purely architectural change—modifying the softmax or adding a gating mechanism—can have order-of-magnitude effects on the numerical properties of the trained model (max infinity norm drops from 735 to 20, a 36× reduction) while leaving task performance essentially unchanged. This challenges the implicit assumption that numerical properties like activation magnitudes are predetermined by the task and data, beyond the architect's control. Instead, it suggests that numerical robustness should be a **first-class design consideration** in neural architecture design, alongside expressivity, efficiency, and trainability. The paper's title—"Removing Outliers by Helping Attention Heads Do Nothing"—captures this philosophy: understand what the model is *trying* to do, identify where the architecture forces it to use numerically pathological mechanisms to achieve that goal, and provide a cleaner mechanism. This principle likely applies beyond the specific case of softmax and outliers to other architectural components and other numerical pathologies.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Systematic causal intervention experiments to validate and refine the outlier mechanism.** The paper's evidence for the causal hypothesis is primarily correlational (outlier dimensions correspond to delimiter-attending heads) and systemic (architectural modifications that allow zero outputs eliminate outliers). A direct test would be: take a vanilla pre-trained BERT model, identify the specific attention heads that exhibit the delimiter-attending no-op pattern (heads #3 and #12 in layers #10–#11, based on Figure 1), and surgically ablate or modify *only those heads* during fine-tuning (e.g., replace their softmax with clipped softmax while leaving all other heads unchanged). If the causal hypothesis is correct, this targeted intervention should reduce outliers specifically in the corresponding hidden dimensions of the previous layer's FFN output, without affecting other dimensions or other layers. A null result—outliers persisting despite targeted head modification—would suggest that the mechanism is more distributed or that there are multiple independent sources of outliers. A positive result would provide the missing interventional evidence and could also enable a "surgical" version of the method that fixes existing pre-trained models by modifying only the problematic heads during fine-tuning, rather than requiring full training from scratch.

**Investigate the failure of clipped softmax on OPT with causal masking analysis.** The paper's most significant unexplained result is that clipped softmax fails catastrophically on OPT-125m, increasing W8A8 perplexity from 21.18 to 37.20 and *increasing* kurtosis from 1778 to 19728 (Table 2). The causal hypothesis predicts that clipped softmax should work universally (breaking the gradient feedback loop does not depend on the attention mask). The failure therefore either reveals a flaw in the hypothesis or a specific interaction between clipping and causal attention that the hypothesis doesn't capture. A strong follow-up would train OPT-125m with clipped softmax and systematically analyze: (1) Are exact zeros actually being produced by the clipped softmax during training, or does the causal mask change the distribution of attention logits such that probabilities rarely cross the clipping threshold? (2) Where does the increased kurtosis come from—which layers, which hidden dimensions, and do they correspond to different attention patterns than in BERT? (3) Does the gradient through the softmax still push toward extreme values, or does the causal mask create a different gradient dynamic (e.g., because future tokens are masked, the model cannot dump probability onto a single delimiter token that appears early in the sequence)? (4) Would a sequence-length-adaptive $\gamma$ (using the $-\alpha/T$ parameterization from Section 5.2, but accounting for the growing context window in autoregressive generation) resolve the issue? The answer would either refine the universality claim (by identifying the specific conditions under which clipped softmax works) or reveal a second, independent outlier-producing mechanism specific to autoregressive models—both valuable outcomes.

**Scale gated attention to 7B+ parameter models with full training budgets and evaluate downstream task performance.** The paper's scaling evidence (Table 3) is its weakest link: OPT-1.3B with gated attention achieves W8A8 perplexity 29.95 vs. FP16 12.21, a gap of 17.74 points that makes the quantized model practically unusable. The paper attributes this to limited training steps ($10^5$ vs. the full budget) but provides no evidence that the gap closes with more training. A critical follow-up would train OPT-1.3B (or, ideally, a 7B model) from scratch with gated attention using a full pre-training budget (e.g., 300B tokens), then evaluate both FP16 and W8A8 perplexity, and additionally fine-tune on several downstream tasks (e.g., HellaSwag, MMLU, GSM8K for language understanding and reasoning). The key question: does the W8A8–FP16 gap shrink to the ~0.5 perplexity range observed for OPT-125m, or does it remain large? If it shrinks, the paper's claim of scalability is validated and gated attention becomes a viable candidate for production-scale language model training. If it remains large, the method's practical impact is limited to smaller models. Either outcome would be highly informative, and the experiment would also establish whether the maintained FP16 performance observed on smaller models (OPT-125m gated perplexity 15.55 vs. vanilla 15.84) persists at scale where small perplexity differences can translate to large downstream performance gaps.

**Apply the diagnostic methodology to other numerical pathologies in deep learning.** The paper's approach—identify a specific behavior the model wants to perform, trace how the current architecture forces it into a numerically pathological implementation, and provide a cleaner mechanism—is a template that could be applied to other well-known but poorly-understood phenomena. Candidates include: (1) The tendency of activation functions like GELU to produce dead neurons in certain regimes—is there a functional behavior that dead neurons serve that could be provided more cleanly? (2) The instability of training dynamics in deep transformers, often attributed to attention logit growth with depth—is there a specific information-routing behavior that the model is trying to achieve through growing logits, and could an architectural modification (e.g., learned temperature scaling per layer, or a variant of the gating mechanism applied to skip connections) provide it without the numerical instability? (3) The tendency of mixture-of-experts models to produce load-balancing problems where some experts are overused and others unused—is this analogous to the attention no-op problem, where the routing mechanism lacks a clean way to say "none of the experts apply"? The paper's success with the transformer outlier problem suggests that this "functional analysis" approach to numerical pathologies—asking *what the model wants to do* before asking *how to fix the numbers*—could be broadly productive.

**Combine clipped softmax or gated attention with advanced PTQ techniques for ultra-low-bit quantization.** The paper demonstrates that the architectural modifications make W8A8 quantization trivially easy, but the low-bit results in Appendix B.7 (Table 10) show that W4A8 and W6A6 still incur non-trivial perplexity degradation even with the modified architectures (clipped softmax W6A6: 6.64 perplexity; gated attention W6A6: 5.90). These degradations are much smaller than vanilla (42.8) but still represent a meaningful loss relative to FP16 (4.39–4.45). A natural follow-up would test whether the outlier-free models are also more amenable to advanced PTQ techniques—per-channel quantization, learned rounding (Nagel et al., 2020), block reconstruction (Li et al., 2021), or GPTQ-style weight compensation (Frantar et al., 2022)—at 4-bit and below. The hypothesis is that these PTQ techniques are currently limited not just by fundamental precision constraints but by the need to implicitly compensate for outliers (e.g., MSE range estimation for weights produces implicit activation clipping that helps with outliers but hurts optimal precision allocation). On an outlier-free model, these techniques might be able to allocate precision more effectively, pushing W4A8 or even W4A4 performance closer to the FP16 baseline. A positive result would establish that the architectural methods are not just alternatives to advanced PTQ but *enablers* of more effective PTQ at ultra-low bitwidths, where the architectural fix handles the outlier problem and PTQ handles the fundamental precision–quality tradeoff.

**Develop difficulty-predictive or confidence-based gating that adapts per-input.** The gated attention mechanism provides a per-token, per-head gating probability $\pi \in (0,1)$ that the model learns during training. An intriguing follow-up would be to analyze what the learned gates encode: do they correlate with token-level properties (e.g., content words vs. function words, named entities vs. common nouns, ambiguous vs. unambiguous tokens) or with head-level properties (e.g., some heads are always "on" while others gate selectively)? If the gates learn interpretable patterns, they could be used for model introspection (which heads are active for which inputs) or for dynamic computation (skipping head computations when the gate is near-zero, reducing inference cost). A concrete experiment: take a gated attention BERT model, pass a diverse set of sentences through it, and analyze the distribution of gate values across token types (delimiter tokens vs. content words), linguistic phenomena (subject-verb agreement, coreference, negation), and layers. If, for example, certain heads consistently gate near-zero for all tokens except those involved in syntactic dependencies, that would both validate the paper's interpretation (those heads are "no-op" most of the time and only activate for specific linguistic computations) and enable conditional computation: on inputs where those heads are predicted to gate near-zero (based on a lightweight predictor), their computation could be skipped, reducing inference cost. This extends the paper's ideas from quantization-friendly training to dynamic inference efficiency.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Edge deployment of transformer models on integer-arithmetic-only hardware.** The most direct application is enabling transformer inference on edge devices—smartphones, IoT sensors, automotive systems—where floating-point units are power-hungry or absent and where 8-bit integer arithmetic is the native computation mode. The paper's Qualcomm affiliation makes this use case explicit: Qualcomm's Snapdragon platforms include Hexagon DSPs and AI accelerators optimized for INT8 inference. A BERT-base model trained with clipped softmax achieves W8A8 perplexity of 4.52, essentially identical to FP16 perplexity of 4.39 (Table 2), meaning it can be deployed on-device for tasks like text classification, question answering, or document understanding with no meaningful accuracy loss. This is qualitatively different from the status quo, where a vanilla BERT-base under the same INT8 quantization would produce garbage (W8A8 perplexity 1294) and would require either mixed-precision hardware support (LLM.int8()) or per-channel quantization logic that many edge accelerators don't natively support. The 4–9% training-time overhead of clipped softmax or gated attention (Table 11) is a one-time cost amortized across millions of deployed devices, making the tradeoff strongly favorable for any organization building on-device NLP products.

**Cost-efficient cloud inference for large language models with standard GPU integer kernels.** For cloud deployments of decoder-only language models (GPT-style), the dominant cost is inference serving—processing millions of tokens per day at low latency. Modern NVIDIA GPUs (A100, H100) support INT8 tensor core operations that are 2× faster than FP16, but this speedup is only realizable if both weights and activations can be quantized to INT8 without accuracy loss. The paper's gated attention results on OPT-125m (W8A8 perplexity 16.02 vs. FP16 15.55, Table 2) suggest that models trained with gated attention can achieve near-lossless INT8 inference using standard per-tensor quantization—no custom CUDA kernels, no mixed-precision logic, no per-channel scaling. For a production serving system, this simplification matters: it means the model can be served using off-the-shelf inference engines (e.g., NVIDIA TensorRT, ONNX Runtime) without custom quantization plugins, reducing engineering complexity and failure modes. The caveat is the scaling evidence (Table 3), which shows the W8A8–FP16 gap growing to 1.41 for OPT-350m and 17.74 for undertrained OPT-1.3B. Until the gap is shown to close with full training budgets, this use case applies primarily to models in the 100M–1B parameter range, which covers many production use cases (chatbots, content moderation, retrieval) but not the largest frontier models.

**Training data generation pipelines where models are repeatedly called for scoring or filtering.** A less obvious but practically important use case is in LLM training pipelines where a smaller model is used to score, filter, or rerank candidate outputs during data generation (e.g., RLHF reward models, data quality filters, retrieval rerankers). In these pipelines, the same model may be called millions of times to process candidate sequences, and inference throughput directly determines the pipeline's wall-clock time and cost. A BERT-base classifier trained with clipped softmax and quantized to W8A8 could process candidate sequences 2× faster than its FP16 counterpart (due to INT8 tensor core throughput) while maintaining identical accuracy (W8A8 perplexity within 0.13 of FP16). This throughput improvement is "free" in the sense that it requires no changes to the pipeline logic, no mixed-precision handling, and no per-batch calibration—the quantized model is a drop-in replacement. For organizations running large-scale data filtering (e.g., every web document scored for quality before inclusion in a training corpus), this 2× speedup directly translates to reduced GPU-hours and faster iteration cycles.

**Federated learning and privacy-preserving on-device training where quantization is mandatory.** In federated learning scenarios, models are trained on user devices (smartphones, laptops) and only gradient updates are shared with a central server. On-device training is severely compute- and memory-constrained, and INT8 quantization of both forward and backward passes is often necessary to make training feasible. However, training with quantization (QAT) is notoriously unstable for transformers with outliers, because the outlier dimensions receive disproportionately large gradients that are poorly approximated by low-bit representations. A transformer pre-trained with clipped softmax or gated attention and then fine-tuned on-device would present a fundamentally different optimization landscape: no single dimension has activation magnitudes 35× larger than the mean (max infinity norm ~20 vs. ~735), so gradients are more uniformly distributed across dimensions, and quantization of the backward pass introduces less distortion. This use case is speculative—the paper does not evaluate QAT or federated learning—but follows directly from the paper's core finding that the methods produce models with near-Gaussian activation distributions (kurtosis ~20–80 vs. ~1000–3000 for vanilla), which are inherently easier to quantize in all contexts, including training.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not explicitly frame its methods against named alternatives as a decision rule, but the experiments in Table 2 and the scaling results in Table 3 imply a set of conditions under which each approach (clipped softmax, gated attention, or post-hoc PTQ on vanilla models) is preferable. These conditions emerge from the paper's evidence rather than being stated as explicit guidance:

**Prefer clipped softmax when:**
- You are training a bidirectional encoder (BERT-style) or vision transformer from scratch.
- You want the minimum possible code change and compute overhead (replace `softmax` with `clipped_softmax`, <2% training time increase).
- You can tune the $\gamma$ hyperparameter (or use $\gamma = -\alpha/T$ with $\alpha \in [2, 4]$) and your sequence lengths are known in advance.
- You do not need downstream task fine-tuning results to validate the approach (the paper only reports MLM perplexity for BERT).

**Prefer gated attention when:**
- You are training an autoregressive decoder (GPT/OPT-style) from scratch, where clipped softmax failed on OPT-125m.
- You are willing to accept a 4–9% training-time overhead and ~1 token-equivalent of additional parameters per layer.
- You want a method that works consistently across all three tested architectures (BERT, OPT, ViT) without architecture-specific failure modes.
- You need a wider hyperparameter range for bias initialization ($\pi_{\text{init}} \in [0.25, 0.9]$ for BERT, $[0.1, 0.5]$ for ViT), making the method more robust to initial settings.
- You may want to retrofit the method to an existing pre-trained model via fine-tuning, as demonstrated on OPT-1.3B (Appendix B.6), though with reduced effectiveness compared to training from scratch.

**Prefer post-hoc PTQ methods (LLM.int8(), SmoothQuant, QAT) on vanilla models when:**
- You must work with an already-trained model and cannot afford even fine-tuning (the paper's methods require training from scratch or at minimum substantial fine-tuning to be effective).
- You are deploying a very large model (7B+ parameters) where the scaling behavior of clipped softmax and gated attention is not yet validated with full training budgets.
- You need per-channel or mixed-precision quantization for reasons beyond outlier handling (e.g., some layers are inherently more sensitive to quantization than others due to their function, not due to outliers).

**Prefer combining the architectural methods with advanced PTQ when:**
- You need ultra-low-bit quantization (W4A8, W4A4) where the paper shows that the architectural methods alone still leave meaningful perplexity gaps from FP16 (Table 10: W4A8 clipped softmax 4.90 vs. FP16 4.39). In this regime, the architectural methods handle the outlier problem and PTQ techniques handle the fundamental precision–quality tradeoff, and the combination may outperform either approach alone (though this combination is not tested in the paper).
