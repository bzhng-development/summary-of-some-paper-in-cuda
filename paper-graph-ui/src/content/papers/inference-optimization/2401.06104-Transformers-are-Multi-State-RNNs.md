# Transformers are Multi-State RNNs

**ArXiv:** [2401.06104](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06104)

## 🎯 Pitch

Decoder-only transformers are secretly just unbounded multi-state RNNs—and you can fix their hidden state size to slash the key-value cache by 8× with almost no performance loss, turning them into bounded RNNs that run 4.8× faster.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper redefines decoder-only transformers as **unbounded multi-state RNNs (MSRNNs)** — a generalized RNN variant whose hidden state is a matrix that grows with each new token — and demonstrates that they can be converted into **bounded MSRNNs** by applying a compression policy to fix the size of their key-value cache. The authors introduce a novel, training-free compression policy called **TOVA (Token Omission Via Attention)** (at each decoding step, drop the token with the lowest attention score from the cache) and evaluate it against several baseline policies on long-range language modeling (PG-19 perplexity), long-range understanding (SQuALITY summarization and QASPER QA), and text generation tasks using LLaMA-2, Mistral, and Yi models. TOVA achieves within 0.4 perplexity points of the full 4,096-token model using only 1/8 of the cache size on PG-19, yields a 4.8× throughput increase with a multi-state size of 512, and successfully extrapolates to 70K tokens — establishing that transformers routinely act as bounded MSRNNs in practice even when their theoretical capacity is unbounded, though retrieval-intensive tasks require retaining up to half of the full multi-state to match uncompressed performance.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: The KV Cache Is a Computational Bottleneck That Grows Without Bound

The specific problem this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **decoder-only transformer LLMs maintain a key-value (KV) cache that grows linearly with sequence length during autoregressive generation, creating an unbounded memory requirement that limits throughput and constrains deployment.** Every new token generated requires computing attention over *all* previous tokens' key and value vectors, and in standard implementations, these vectors are stored in a cache to avoid recomputation (Radford et al., 2019; Pope et al., 2022). For a sequence of length $t$, the KV cache stores $2 \times t \times d$ values per layer, where $d$ is the hidden dimension. At sequence lengths of 4,096 tokens — the training context length for models like LLaMA-2 — this translates to gigabytes of memory just for the KV cache (Table 1 reports 2.18 GB for a single batch at 4,096 tokens). At 70K tokens, the memory footprint becomes prohibitive for most deployment hardware.

This problem matters for both practical and theoretical reasons:

**Practical impact: Throughput and accessibility.** The KV cache memory consumption is the dominant bottleneck for inference batch size. As the authors demonstrate in Table 1, moving from a full 4,096-token cache to a compressed 512-token cache increases the maximum batch size on a single V100 GPU from 8 to 139 — a ~17× increase — leading to 4.8× higher total throughput. This directly translates to lower serving costs, higher utilization of expensive GPU hardware, and broader accessibility of LLMs for users with limited hardware resources (a point the authors highlight in their Ethics Statement). For applications like long-document summarization, multi-turn dialogue, or story generation where sequences can stretch to tens of thousands of tokens, the memory bottleneck becomes the primary barrier to deployment.

**Theoretical significance: Do transformers actually need all those tokens?** The unbounded KV cache implies that transformers store *every* token they have ever seen, in contrast to RNNs, which maintain a fixed-size hidden state that is updated recursively. If transformers can be compressed to bounded memory without meaningful performance loss, it suggests that their apparent "infinite memory" is largely redundant — that they, like RNNs, maintain a compact representation of the past rather than faithfully attending to every historical token. This has profound implications for how we understand transformer architectures: are they fundamentally different from RNNs, or are they just RNNs with an inefficiently large state?

### The Conceptual Gap Between Transformers and RNNs

The paper is motivated by a specific conceptual tension in the NLP community. Transformers (Vaswani et al., 2017) are widely considered to be architecturally distinct from recurrent neural networks (RNNs; Elman, 1990). The standard narrative holds that:

- **Transformers** have direct, non-recurrent access to every token in the sequence through the self-attention mechanism. They do not maintain a "state" that compresses history — they just look at everything.
- **RNNs** maintain a hidden state vector that is updated at each step, compressing all historical information into a fixed-size representation. They do not have direct access to individual past tokens.

This distinction is treated as foundational in the literature. But the authors identify a subtle flaw in this narrative: decoder-only transformers, when generating autoregressively, *do* carry forward a state from one step to the next — the KV cache. At step $t$, the cache contains key-value pairs for tokens $1$ through $t-1$, and the new token's representation is computed using this cached state plus the current input. This is structurally analogous to an RNN: a state from the previous step is combined with a new input to produce an output and an updated state. Equation (8) in the paper formalizes this by writing the transformer layer as:

$$x^{l+1}_t, (\mathbf{K}^l_t, \mathbf{V}^l_t) = f^l_{\text{TRANS}}\left(x^l_t, (\mathbf{K}^l_{t-1}, \mathbf{V}^l_{t-1})\right)$$

which has exactly the same functional form as the RNN recurrence in Equation (1), except the state is a matrix $(\mathbf{K}^l_t, \mathbf{V}^l_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times d}$ rather than a vector $\mathbf{h}^l_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$.

The key difference is not *whether* a state exists, but *how large* that state is. An RNN's state has fixed size regardless of sequence length — it is **bounded**. A transformer's KV cache grows with every new token — it is **unbounded**. The paper introduces the **Multi-State RNN (MSRNN)** formalism to capture this spectrum: an RNN variant where the state is a matrix with rows corresponding to individual "sub-states," and the number of rows $g(t)$ can be either bounded ($g(t) \leq k$ for some constant $k$) or unbounded ($g(t) = t$). Transformers are the unbounded case; traditional RNNs are the bounded case with $g(t) = 1$.

This reframing is not merely taxonomic. It opens the door to asking a new question: **what happens if we take an unbounded MSRNN (a transformer) and force it to be bounded by limiting its number of states?** Can it still function?

### Where Prior Compression Approaches Fall Short

Several existing methods can be understood as bounded MSRNN compression policies, but the paper identifies specific limitations in each:

**Window attention (Wang et al., 2019)** implements a simple First In First Out (FIFO) strategy: keep the most recent $k$ tokens and drop everything older. While conceptually simple, this is catastrophically bad for language modeling. The authors report in Appendix A (Table 3) that Window with a 64-token multi-state produces perplexity of **4,812** on PG-19, compared to 17.65 for the truncated baseline and 9.53 for TOVA. This is not a minor degradation — it is complete collapse. The reason is straightforward: many tasks require attending to information from early in the sequence (the beginning of a book, the initial instructions in a prompt), and a pure recency window discards all of that context. Window is not a viable general-purpose compression strategy.

**Window+i (Xiao et al., 2023; Han et al., 2023)** improves on Window by retaining the first $i$ tokens (typically 1–4) in addition to the recent window, based on the observation that initial tokens — particularly the very first token — serve as "attention sinks" that receive disproportionately high attention scores throughout generation. This is a significant improvement: Table 3 shows Window+4 achieves 10.28 PPL at size 64, compared to 4,812 for Window. However, Window+i introduces a strong inductive bias: the non-window tokens it keeps are *always* the earliest ones, regardless of content. The authors argue this is an overly rigid assumption — some early tokens may be irrelevant (e.g., boilerplate text at the start of a document), while some middle tokens may be critical (e.g., a proper noun introduced mid-document that is referenced throughout). Window+i cannot distinguish between these cases.

**H2O (Heavy-Hitter Oracle; Zhang et al., 2023)** introduces a more dynamic approach: it keeps a fixed window of recent tokens plus additional tokens selected by aggregating attention scores across the entire generation history and retaining those with the highest cumulative scores. This is a substantial conceptual advance — the policy is content-aware rather than purely position-based. However, the authors identify two limitations:

1. **Aggregation bias toward early tokens:** Because H2O sums attention scores across all previous decoding steps, tokens that appear earlier automatically accumulate higher scores simply by virtue of being around for more steps. This creates an implicit positional bias in favor of early tokens, even if their content is not inherently important.
2. **Fixed window ratio:** H2O typically allocates a fixed proportion (e.g., half) of the cache to recent tokens, making it partially rigid.

**TOVA's positioning relative to these baselines:** The authors present TOVA as a policy that makes **fewer assumptions** than any of the above. It does not fix a window of recent tokens (unlike all three baselines). It does not aggregate scores across time steps (unlike H2O). It does not hard-code a preference for early tokens (unlike Window+i). Instead, TOVA makes a single, simple decision at each step: look at the attention scores from the *current* query to all cached tokens, and drop the one with the lowest score. The policy is entirely **local in time** (only the current step's attention matters) and entirely **content-driven** (the importance of each cached token is reassessed at every step based on its relevance to the new query). If the attention pattern changes over the course of generation — for instance, if a token that was unimportant for the first 1,000 steps suddenly becomes relevant — TOVA can adapt, while H2O's accumulated scores would lag behind this change.

The paper implicitly argues that this minimal-assumption approach is not just simpler but *better* because it lets the model's own attention mechanism — which was trained to identify relevant context — determine what to keep, rather than imposing external heuristics about what "should" be important.

### Prior Work on the Transformer-RNN Connection

The idea that transformers can be viewed through an RNN lens is not entirely new, and the paper situates itself carefully relative to prior attempts at this connection.

**Katharopoulos et al. (2020)** showed that transformers can be written as RNNs and proposed a *linear transformer* that explicitly implements a recurrent computation with linearized attention, enabling $O(1)$ memory per step rather than $O(t)$. However, this requires training a new model from scratch with a modified attention mechanism — it cannot be applied to existing pretrained transformers. The authors' approach differs in that it operates on *any pretrained decoder transformer* without architectural modification or retraining.

**Peng et al. (2022)** presented a framework called "Attention with Bounded-memory Control" (ABC) that interprets several transformer variants (Linformer, window attention) as instances of bounded-memory transformers. Again, this work treats the memory as a single unified state without an explicit mapping from tokens to sub-states, and it requires dedicated training. The MSRNN formalism in the current paper provides a more explicit decomposition: each row of the multi-state matrix corresponds to a specific token, making the connection between "which states are kept" and "which tokens are attended to" transparent.

**Hutchins et al. (2022)** and **So et al. (2022)** proposed hybrid architectures that incorporate recurrent elements into transformers, but again require training from scratch. **Sun et al. (2023)** and **Peng et al. (2023)** modified self-attention to perform recurrence at inference time, but these are architectural modifications, not analyses of existing models.

The key differentiator for this work is that it does not propose a new architecture, a new training procedure, or even a modification to the attention mechanism. It simply observes that the KV cache *already is* an RNN multi-state, and that compressing it with a simple attention-based policy can recover most of the full model's performance. This makes the approach immediately applicable to any pretrained transformer decoder — a point the authors emphasize by demonstrating TOVA across three different model families (LLaMA-2, Mistral, Yi) without any model-specific tuning.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's position can be understood as operating at two levels simultaneously:

**Level 1: A conceptual reframing.** The MSRNN formalism is not presented as a technical contribution per se, but as a *lens* that makes the problem of KV cache compression natural. Once you see transformers as unbounded MSRNNs, the question "how do we reduce the KV cache size?" becomes "how do we convert an unbounded MSRNN into a bounded one?" — which is a well-defined operation (apply a compression policy that selects which states to retain). This framing unifies several previously disconnected ideas (window attention, H2O, TOVA) as different answers to the same fundamental question.

The paper is essentially arguing that the transformer-RNN distinction is a false dichotomy — or at least, a distinction that applies to the encoder case (where full bidirectional attention is genuinely non-recurrent) but not to autoregressive decoding. This is a provocative claim intended to shift how the field thinks about transformer inference.

**Level 2: A practical compression method.** TOVA is presented not as a theoretically optimal policy but as an empirically effective one that outperforms existing training-free baselines. The paper emphasizes that TOVA is **training-free**, meaning it can be applied immediately to any pretrained model without fine-tuning, data collection, or architectural changes. This is significant because many KV cache reduction methods (e.g., learning which tokens to prune; Anagnostidis et al., 2023) require additional training, which is expensive and may not transfer across models or tasks.

The paper explicitly scopes its contribution to **off-the-shelf models** (Section 5.1): "As our focus is on the capacity of off-the-shelf models, we only consider baseline policies that operate on pretrained LLMs and require no additional training." This positions TOVA in a distinct category from learned compression approaches, which the authors briefly acknowledge in Section 8.

### The Underlying Empirical Question: Do Transformers Actually Use Their Full Memory?

Beneath the conceptual reframing and the practical compression method lies a deeper empirical question that motivates the paper: **do pretrained transformer decoders actually need access to every token they've seen, or do they already function internally as if they had bounded memory?** The experiments are designed to test this by progressively shrinking the cache and measuring when performance degrades.

If transformers genuinely relied on their unbounded capacity, we would expect a smooth, monotonic relationship between cache size and performance — every reduction in cache size hurts because useful information is being lost. If instead transformers act as bounded MSRNNs, we would expect a plateau: performance stays flat or nearly flat until the cache is compressed below some threshold, after which degradation sets in rapidly.

The results (Figures 3, 4, 5) show something closer to the plateau scenario. On PG-19 language modeling, TOVA with only 512 cache slots (1/8 of the full 4,096) produces perplexity within 0.4 points of the full model. On SQuALITY summarization, TOVA with 512–1,024 slots achieves ROUGE scores within 1 point of the topline. Even on the retrieval-intensive QASPER task, which forces the model to locate specific facts in long documents, half the cache size (2,048) is sufficient for comparable performance.

This plateau behavior is the paper's most significant empirical finding, and it supports the central claim: transformers, despite having the *capacity* for unbounded memory, **often act in practice as bounded MSRNNs**, maintaining a compressed representation of the past rather than faithfully attending to every historical token. The fact that a simple attention-score-based policy can select which tokens to keep without substantial performance loss suggests that the model's attention mechanism is already doing this selection implicitly — TOVA just makes it explicit and enforces it as a hard constraint.

The authors are careful not to overclaim this finding. They note that on retrieval-intensive tasks (QASPER) and hard generation tasks, larger cache sizes do matter, and that the conversion to a bounded MSRNN "reintroduces the inherent challenges associated with RNNs, as they encounter difficulties with retrieving distant information" (Section 6.4). This is an honest acknowledgment that bounded memory has costs, particularly for tasks that require precise access to specific tokens seen long ago. The point is not that bounded MSRNNs are *always* equivalent to unbounded ones, but that the degradation is surprisingly mild and task-dependent, which has both practical implications (you can compress aggressively on many tasks) and theoretical implications (transformers don't use their full memory as much as we might assume).

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper builds a **compression system for the key-value cache of pretrained decoder-only transformer LLMs** that enables them to operate with a fixed memory budget rather than a cache that grows without bound with each generated token. The system solves the problem of unbounded memory consumption during autoregressive generation by treating the transformer as a multi-state RNN and applying a training-free policy—called TOVA—that, at each decoding step, identifies and drops the cached token with the lowest attention score from the current query, thereby maintaining a constant-size memory footprint without any model retraining, fine-tuning, or architectural modification.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has three major components working together during autoregressive decoding:

1. **The Transformer Decoder (LLM)** — This is the frozen, pretrained language model (LLaMA-2, Mistral, or Yi) that generates tokens one at a time. Internally, each layer computes query, key, and value projections for the current token, uses the key and value projections to update a cache (the multi-state), and computes attention over all cached keys and values to produce the next token representation.

2. **The Multi-State Cache (the bounded MSRNN memory)** — This is a fixed-capacity matrix per layer that stores key-value pairs for selected historical tokens. The cache has a maximum size of $k$ slots (e.g., 512, 1,024, or 2,048), where each slot corresponds to the key and value vectors of a specific token. When the cache is full and a new token arrives, the compression policy must evict one existing entry before adding the new one.

3. **The Compression Policy (TOVA)** — This is the decision rule that selects which cached token to evict at each step. TOVA operates after the attention scores for the current query are computed: it identifies the cached key with the lowest attention score from the current query, removes that key-value pair from the cache, and then inserts the new token's key-value pair. The policy is applied independently per layer and uses attention scores averaged across attention heads within each layer.

Information flows as follows: A new token is generated → the model computes its query, key, and value vectors → the query attends to all keys currently in the cache, producing attention scores → TOVA identifies the cached token with the lowest score and evicts it → the new token's key and value are inserted into the cache, maintaining the fixed capacity → the attention output is computed using the updated cache → the feedforward network produces the next hidden state → the process repeats for the next token.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the MSRNN formalism (Section 3.1 of the paper) — defining what a multi-state RNN is, how it generalizes standard RNNs, and why the distinction between bounded and unbounded $g(t)$ is the key abstraction that bridges transformers and RNNs.
- **Second**, the equivalence proof (Section 3.2) — showing step-by-step that the decoder transformer recurrence can be rewritten in MSRNN form with $g(t) = t$, establishing that transformers *are* unbounded MSRNNs.
- **Third**, the compression mechanism (Section 3.3) — explaining how to convert an unbounded MSRNN into a bounded one by setting $g(t) = \min(t, k)$ and applying a compression policy when capacity is exceeded, and showing how several existing methods (Window, H2O) fit this framework.
- **Fourth**, the TOVA policy in full detail (Section 4) — the specific eviction rule, its per-layer and per-head design choices, and its relationship to baseline policies.
- **Fifth**, the extrapolation mechanism (Section 7.2) — the positional encoding adjustment that allows bounded MSRNNs to process sequences longer than the training context length.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **conceptual reframing and empirical demonstration paper**. Its core idea is that decoder-only transformers *already are* RNNs with a matrix-valued state, and that making this state bounded via a simple attention-based compression policy recovers nearly all performance while dramatically reducing memory. The MSRNN formalism provides the intellectual scaffolding; TOVA provides the practical compression method; and the experiments demonstrate that the theoretical equivalence has practical consequences.

#### The Multi-State RNN (MSRNN) Formalism

The paper begins by defining a generalized RNN variant called a **Multi-State RNN (MSRNN)**. The purpose of this definition is to create a single mathematical framework that encompasses both traditional RNNs and decoder transformers, so that the compression operation can be stated as moving from one point in this space (unbounded) to another (bounded).

**The defining equation.** An MSRNN layer is defined by the following recurrence:

$$x^{l+1}_t, \mathbf{H}^l_t = f^l_{\text{MSRNN}}(x^l_t, \mathbf{H}^l_{t-1})$$

where $x^l_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the representation of the current token at layer $l$ and time $t$, $\mathbf{H}^l_t \in \mathbb{R}^{g(t) \times d}$ is the multi-state matrix at layer $l$ after processing token $t$, and $f^l_{\text{MSRNN}}$ is the layer-specific update function.

**What it computes:** At each time step $t$, the layer takes two inputs — the current token's representation from the previous layer and the multi-state matrix from the previous time step — and produces two outputs: an updated token representation (which becomes input to the next layer) and an updated multi-state matrix (which is fed back into the same layer at time $t+1$). Each row of $\mathbf{H}^l_t$ is called a **single-state**, and the matrix as a whole is the **multi-state**.

**Why this form:** The standard RNN recurrence (Equation 1 in the paper) is $x^{l+1}_t, \mathbf{h}^l_t = f^l_{\text{RNN}}(x^l_t, \mathbf{h}^l_{t-1})$ where $\mathbf{h}^l_t$ is a *vector*. The MSRNN generalization replaces the vector with a matrix, but preserves the exact same functional signature: the state from the previous step and the current input produce the output and the new state. This means any analysis or algorithm that operates on RNNs can potentially be lifted to MSRNNs by operating on matrices instead of vectors. The matrix formulation is also critical for transparency: each row corresponds to a specific token, making it possible to ask "which tokens are in the state?" — a question that is meaningless for a standard RNN's compressed vector state.

**The capacity function $g(t)$.** The function $g: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{N}$ controls the number of rows in the multi-state matrix as a function of the sequence position $t$. This is the key design parameter that distinguishes different types of MSRNNs:

- If $g(t) = 1$ for all $t$, the multi-state matrix has exactly one row at every step. In this case the MSRNN reduces to a standard (single-state) RNN. The single row is repeatedly overwritten, forcing all historical information into a fixed-size compressed representation.
- If $g(t) \leq k$ for some constant $k$ and for all $t$, the multi-state matrix has at most $k$ rows regardless of sequence length. This is a **bounded MSRNN** — it has a fixed memory capacity that does not grow with the sequence.
- If $g(t) = t$, the number of rows equals the current sequence position. The matrix grows by one row at each step. This is an **unbounded MSRNN** — it has infinite memory capacity, with every token since the start of the sequence stored in a dedicated row.

The crucial insight is that $g(t)$ is a *choice*, not an inherent property of the architecture. A transformer implements $g(t) = t$ by default, but we can *choose* to impose $g(t) \leq k$ by discarding rows when the matrix exceeds size $k$. This operation — reducing $g(t)$ from unbounded to bounded — is what the paper means by "converting transformers into bounded MSRNNs."

**A subtle point about the term "state."** The authors deliberately use the term "multi-state" rather than "memory" or "context window." This is because each row of $\mathbf{H}^l_t$ functions analogously to the hidden state in an RNN: it is a representation that is carried forward from previous steps and used to compute the output at the current step. In a standard RNN, the single hidden state must compress all historical information. In an MSRNN, the compression is distributed across multiple rows, each preserving a different aspect of the history (specifically, each row preserves information about a different token). The bounded MSRNN thus faces the same fundamental challenge as an RNN: with limited capacity, it must decide what to keep and what to forget.

#### Transformers as Unbounded MSRNNs

The paper then demonstrates that a decoder-only transformer layer can be rewritten exactly in the MSRNN form with $g(t) = t$. This is not an approximation or an interpretation — it is a direct algebraic equivalence.

**The transformer layer decomposition.** Recall that a standard transformer decoder layer (ignoring layer normalization, residual connections, and multiple heads for clarity) operates as follows. At time $t$, the layer receives the current token representation $x^l_t$ and has access to the cached key and value matrices from all previous tokens, denoted $\mathbf{K}^l_{t-1}, \mathbf{V}^l_{t-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{(t-1) \times d}$. It computes:

$$\mathbf{q}^l_t = x^l_t \mathbf{W}^l_Q \quad\quad \mathbf{k}^l_t = x^l_t \mathbf{W}^l_K \quad\quad \mathbf{v}^l_t = x^l_t \mathbf{W}^l_V$$

where $\mathbf{W}^l_Q, \mathbf{W}^l_K, \mathbf{W}^l_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ are the learned projection matrices. The key and value vectors for the current token are then appended to the cache:

$$\mathbf{K}^l_t = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{K}^l_{t-1} \\ \mathbf{k}^l_t \end{bmatrix} \quad\quad \mathbf{V}^l_t = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{V}^l_{t-1} \\ \mathbf{v}^l_t \end{bmatrix}$$

where $\mathbf{K}^l_t, \mathbf{V}^l_t \in \mathbb{R}^{t \times d}$ are the updated cache matrices. The attention output is computed using the query and the full cache:

$$\mathbf{a}^l_t = \text{Softmax}\left(\mathbf{q}^l_t (\mathbf{K}^l_t)^T\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times t}$$

$$x^{l+1}_t = \text{FF}^l\left(\mathbf{a}^l_t \mathbf{V}^l_t\right)$$

where $\mathbf{a}^l_t$ contains the attention weights from the current token to all previous tokens, and the weighted sum over values produces the attention output, which is then passed through the feedforward network $\text{FF}^l$ to produce the output representation.

**Rewriting in MSRNN form.** The key observation is that the cache update (appending $\mathbf{k}^l_t$ and $\mathbf{v}^l_t$ to the existing matrices) and the attention-and-feedforward computation together match the signature of $f^l_{\text{MSRNN}}$. Specifically, define the multi-state as the concatenation of the key and value matrices:

$$\mathbf{H}^l_t = (\mathbf{K}^l_t, \mathbf{V}^l_t)$$

This is a single conceptual object — it is what is preserved from one step to the next. The transformer recurrence becomes:

$$x^{l+1}_t, (\mathbf{K}^l_t, \mathbf{V}^l_t) = f^l_{\text{TRANS}}\left(x^l_t, (\mathbf{K}^l_{t-1}, \mathbf{V}^l_{t-1})\right)$$

which is exactly the MSRNN form from Equation (5). The growth function is $g(t) = t$, since the cache has $t$ rows after $t$ tokens.

**What this equivalence means operationally.** At each decoding step:

1. The current token $x^l_t$ enters the layer.
2. The layer retrieves the cached key-value pairs from all previous tokens, $(\mathbf{K}^l_{t-1}, \mathbf{V}^l_{t-1})$.
3. It projects $x^l_t$ into query, key, and value vectors.
4. It computes attention weights between the query and all cached keys.
5. It computes the weighted sum of cached values.
6. It passes this through the feedforward network to produce $x^{l+1}_t$.
7. It appends the new key and value vectors to the cache, producing $(\mathbf{K}^l_t, \mathbf{V}^l_t)$ for the next step.

Steps 2–7 are the function $f^l_{\text{TRANS}}$. Step 7 is the "state update" that distinguishes an RNN from a stateless function: the output of the layer at time $t$ includes not just the token representation but also the updated cache, which becomes part of the input at time $t+1$.

**Why this reframing matters, concretely.** Once the transformer is written in this form, it becomes obvious that the only thing preventing it from being a bounded RNN is the fact that step 7 appends without bound. If we modify step 7 to instead *replace* one existing row when the cache is full, we have exactly a bounded MSRNN with $g(t) \leq k$. The rest of the computation — the attention, the feedforward, the query-key-value projections — remains completely unchanged. This is the key insight that makes TOVA possible: **the transformer architecture already supports bounded recurrence; we just need to change the cache management policy, not the model itself.**

**Relationship to the KV cache implementation.** The paper notes that in practice, the key and value matrices are cached for efficiency (avoiding recomputation), but the recurrence argument applies equally to non-cached implementations: at each step, the model still computes attention over all previous tokens, which means it necessarily has access to their representations. The cache is an implementation optimization, not a conceptual difference. The MSRNN formulation works whether or not the cache is physically stored.

#### Converting Transformers into Bounded MSRNNs

The conversion from unbounded to bounded is conceptually simple: replace the append operation with a capacity-limited append that, when the multi-state has reached size $k$, first evicts one row before inserting the new one.

**The bounded growth function.** The MSRNN capacity function becomes:

$$g(t) = \min(t, k)$$

For the first $k$ tokens ($t \leq k$), the cache grows normally — each new token adds a row, exactly as in the unbounded case. At step $t = k+1$, the cache is full, and any further growth requires eviction.

**The eviction operation.** When $t > k$, before appending $\mathbf{k}^l_t$ and $\mathbf{v}^l_t$, the cache must drop one existing row. Let $j$ be the index of the row to remove. The update becomes:

$$\mathbf{K}^l_t = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{K}^l_{t-1, 0:j-1} \\ \mathbf{K}^l_{t-1, j+1:k} \\ \mathbf{k}^l_t \end{bmatrix} \quad\quad \mathbf{V}^l_t = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{V}^l_{t-1, 0:j-1} \\ \mathbf{V}^l_{t-1, j+1:k} \\ \mathbf{v}^l_t \end{bmatrix}$$

where the notation $\mathbf{K}_{0:j-1}$ means rows 0 through $j-1$, and $\mathbf{K}_{j+1:k}$ means rows $j+1$ through $k$ (inclusive of $k$). Row $j$ is simply removed. The resulting matrices have exactly $k$ rows: $k-1$ retained from the previous step plus the one new row.

**What different policies change.** The only difference between compression policies is the choice of $j$ — which row to evict. All other aspects of the computation (the query-key-value projections, the attention mechanism, the feedforward network) are identical across policies. The paper presents four policies:

- **Window:** $j = 0$ always — evict the oldest token. This is a First In First Out (FIFO) queue.
- **Window+i:** $j$ is chosen from the non-window portion of the cache (rows $i$ through $k-1$), preserving the first $i$ rows indefinitely. Specifically, the policy distinguishes between "protected" rows (indices $0$ through $i-1$, which are never evicted) and "window" rows (indices $i$ through $k-1$). When a new token arrives, the oldest window row is evicted — this is the one with the smallest original sequence position among the window rows.
- **H2O:** The cache is split into two segments: a "heavy-hitter" segment and a "recent" segment. The recent segment (typically the last $k/2$ positions) is managed as a window — the oldest recent token is evicted when the recent segment is full. The heavy-hitter segment holds tokens with the highest *cumulative* attention scores (aggregated across all previous decoding steps), updated dynamically. When a recent token ages out of the recent window, it may be promoted to the heavy-hitter segment if its cumulative attention score is high enough to displace an existing heavy-hitter token.
- **TOVA:** $j = \arg\min_i (\mathbf{a}^l_{t,i})$ — evict the cached token with the lowest attention score from the *current* query. No aggregation across time steps, no fixed window, no protected positions.

**The MSRNN perspective on existing methods.** The authors make the important observation that Window, Window+i, and H2O can all be understood as specific eviction rules within the bounded MSRNN framework. This is not just a classification exercise — it reveals the common structure underlying these seemingly different approaches. They are all solving the same problem (selecting which $k$ states to keep out of $t$ possible states) under different heuristics. This unifies the KV cache compression literature under a single formalism and makes it clear that TOVA is not a fundamentally different kind of thing — it is a different answer to the same question.

**A critical design choice: training-free policies only.** The paper explicitly restricts its scope to policies that operate on pretrained LLMs without any additional training (Section 5.1): "As our focus is on the capacity of off-the-shelf models, we only consider baseline policies that operate on pretrained LLMs and require no additional training." This rules out learned approaches such as Anagnostidis et al. (2023), which train a model to predict which tokens can be pruned. The rationale is practical: training-free policies can be deployed immediately on any existing model without data collection, GPU hours for fine-tuning, or risk of catastrophic forgetting. The cost is that the policy cannot adapt to the specific model's behavior — it must rely on a hand-designed heuristic. TOVA's heuristic (attention score from the current query) is intentionally minimal, relying on the fact that the attention mechanism was *already trained* to identify relevant tokens, so we can simply reuse the attention scores as importance estimates without any additional learning.

#### The TOVA (Token Omission Via Attention) Policy

TOVA is the paper's proposed compression policy. Its core decision rule is elegant in its simplicity: **at each decoding step, after computing attention scores for the current query against all cached keys, remove the cached token that received the lowest attention score.**

**The formal eviction rule.** When $t > k$ (the cache is full), let $\mathbf{a}^l_t \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times k}$ be the attention weight vector for the current query over the $k$ cached keys. The index to evict is:

$$j = \arg\min_i \left(\mathbf{a}^l_{t,i}\right)$$

where $i$ ranges over $0, 1, \ldots, k-1$ (the positions in the current cache). The row at position $j$ is removed, and the new token's key-value pair is inserted at the end (position $k-1$ after removal).

**What this computes:** For each of the $k$ tokens currently in the cache, the attention mechanism has already computed a scalar weight representing how relevant that token is to the current query. These weights are non-negative and sum to 1 (they are the output of a softmax). The minimum weight corresponds to the token that the attention mechanism judges to be *least relevant* to the current generation step. By removing this token, TOVA discards the cached information that the model itself considers least important right now.

**Why this form:** There are several alternative heuristics that seem plausible but have specific flaws:

- **Evicting the oldest token (Window):** Ignores content entirely. The oldest token might be the most important one (e.g., the initial instruction in a prompt, the title of a book).
- **Evicting the token with lowest cumulative attention (H2O):** Aggregates over all past steps. A token that was irrelevant for the first 1,000 steps might become highly relevant at step 1,001 (e.g., the model returns to a topic introduced early in the text). Cumulative scoring would have already evicted it.
- **Evicting the token with the lowest average attention:** Same problem as cumulative, though less severe — early steps where the token was irrelevant still penalize its average.
- **Evicting the token with the lowest attention from the *most recent* query:** This is exactly TOVA. It answers the question: "if I had to drop one token right now, which one would the model miss the least?" The attention mechanism was explicitly trained to answer this question — its job is to identify which past tokens are relevant to the current generation step. Using attention scores for eviction reuses a signal that is already computed (zero additional cost) and inherently task-aligned.

**Per-layer independence.** TOVA is applied independently at each transformer layer. This is crucial because different layers attend to different types of information: lower layers tend to attend to local syntactic context, while higher layers attend to longer-range semantic dependencies (as shown in the layer-wise visualizations in Appendix G, Figures 12–13). By making eviction decisions independently per layer, TOVA allows each layer to keep the tokens that are relevant to *its* specific function. For example, a lower layer might retain recent tokens for local syntax, while a higher layer might retain a key named entity from 2,000 tokens ago for coreference resolution.

**Per-head vs. per-layer aggregation.** The paper explores two variants:

- **TOVA-head:** Each attention head independently computes its own attention scores and evicts based on its own minimum. This means different heads can retain different tokens in their caches.
- **TOVA-layer:** The attention scores are first averaged across all heads within the layer, producing a single importance score per cached token. The token with the lowest *averaged* score is evicted, and all heads remove the same token. This is the variant that performs best.

The paper's explanation for why the layer version outperforms the head version (Appendix A): averaging across heads "requires agreement among all heads to determine the importance of specific tokens," providing a more robust signal. Individual heads may have noisy or specialized attention patterns — a token that one head ignores might be critical for another head. Evicting based on a single head's minimum risks removing a token that is important for some other head. Averaging mitigates this by ensuring that a token is only evicted if it is genuinely unimportant to *all* heads in the layer.

**The absence of a fixed recent window.** Unlike Window, Window+i, and H2O, TOVA does not hard-code a window of recent tokens that are always kept. Instead, if recent tokens are important for the current generation step, the attention mechanism will assign them high scores and TOVA will keep them. If some recent tokens are unimportant (e.g., a punctuation mark, a boilerplate phrase), TOVA can evict them even though they are recent. This is the "fewer assumptions" property the authors emphasize: TOVA lets the data (the attention scores) determine what to keep, rather than imposing a structural prior that recency always matters.

**Which attention scores are used?** The paper states that TOVA uses the attention scores for the *last* query in the current sequence — that is, the attention from the most recently generated token to all cached tokens. This is because, during autoregressive decoding, the most recent token is the one being generated, and its attention pattern reflects what the model currently needs. The scores are computed as part of the normal forward pass, so TOVA adds no additional computation beyond the comparison and eviction logic.

**A PyTorch-style implementation.** Appendix B (Algorithm 1) provides a concrete implementation in a few lines of code. The key operations are:

1. Check if the current cache size exceeds the maximum.
2. If so, average the attention weights for the last query token across all heads (producing a vector of length equal to the current cache size).
3. Find the index of the minimum value in this averaged vector.
4. Concatenate all cache rows before and after the minimum index, effectively removing that row.

The implementation assumes batch size 1 for simplicity, though the paper notes that batching is supported in practice.

#### Bounding the First $k$ Tokens and the Append-Then-Evict Pattern

An important detail about the TOVA procedure during autoregressive generation: the cache does not compress immediately. For the first $k$ tokens, TOVA does nothing — the cache grows normally, just like an unbounded transformer. Only when the $(k+1)$-th token is generated does the policy activate: it computes attention scores, evicts the lowest-scoring cached token, and inserts the new token.

This means the *effective* behavior of the bounded MSRNN with TOVA is:
- Steps $1$ through $k$: Act as an unbounded MSRNN, accumulating tokens in the cache.
- Steps $k+1$ onward: At each step, compute attention scores, evict the lowest, and insert the new token.

This pattern is visible in Figure 2 of the paper. The early steps show the cache filling up (green cells appearing for each new token). Once the cache reaches capacity (the horizontal line representing $k$), every subsequent step involves one eviction (red cell, which becomes transparent in the next row) and one insertion (new green cell).

A consequence of this design: the tokens present in the cache at step $t$ are not necessarily the $k$ most recent tokens, nor are they the $k$ tokens with the highest cumulative attention. They are a dynamically selected set that has survived repeated rounds of attention-based eviction. A token that has been in the cache since step 1 can remain there until step 4,096 if it consistently receives high attention scores — and indeed, Figure 9 shows that the very first token is almost never evicted, surviving for all 4,096 steps regardless of multi-state size.

#### Extrapolation Beyond Training Context Length

Transformer LLMs are typically trained with a fixed maximum sequence length (4,096 tokens for the models used in the paper). The positional encodings (typically Rotary Position Embeddings, RoPE) are trained only for positions within this range. When a sequence exceeds the training length, the positional encodings for positions $> 4,096$ are out-of-distribution, and the model's performance degrades — a phenomenon known as the length extrapolation problem (Press et al., 2022).

TOVA, by compressing the cache, interacts with positional encodings in a specific way. The cache retains some old tokens (e.g., the first token, which persists throughout) while continuously inserting new tokens. This means the model sees a mixture of very old and very new tokens, with positional gaps between them that can be large — for example, at step 50,000, the cache might contain the first token (position 1) alongside recent tokens (positions 49,990–50,000), with a gap of nearly 50,000 positions between adjacent cached tokens.

If the model's positional encodings are used naively, this large gap would correspond to a positional difference far beyond what was seen during training, causing the model to mishandle the relative positions. The paper introduces a **positional encoding adjustment** to mitigate this.

**The gap compression function.** Let $g$ be the gap in original sequence positions between two adjacent tokens in the cache. For example, if the cache contains tokens at original positions 1 and 50,000, the gap is $g = 49,999$. The paper compresses this gap using the function:

$$g_{\text{compressed}} = \begin{cases} g & \text{if } g \leq 10 \\ \ln(\ln(g)) & \text{if } g > 10 \end{cases}$$

The new positional encoding is computed as if the tokens were spaced by $g_{\text{compressed}}$ rather than $g$. For the example above, $\ln(\ln(49,999)) \approx \ln(10.82) \approx 2.38$, so the model sees the token at position 50,000 as if it were at position $1 + 2.38 \approx 3$ (or, after rounding, a very small positional distance from the first token).

**What this computes:** The double logarithm heavily compresses large gaps while leaving small gaps unchanged (the threshold of 10 preserves local positional sensitivity for nearby tokens). A gap of 50,000 positions becomes approximately 2.38 positions; a gap of 1,000 becomes approximately 1.93; a gap of 100 becomes approximately 1.53. All large gaps map to a narrow range of small values.

**Why this form:** The authors state that "preliminary experiments with other compression functions, e.g., $\ln(g)$ and $\sqrt{g}$, showed inferior results." This suggests that the extremely aggressive compression of $\ln(\ln(g))$ is necessary to bring large positional gaps into the range that the model's positional encodings can handle. A single logarithm compresses a gap of 50,000 to approximately 10.82, which is still a fairly large positional difference that might be poorly handled by a model trained on sequences of length 4,096. The double logarithm compresses it to approximately 2.38, which is well within the training distribution. The threshold of 10 for small gaps preserves the model's ability to distinguish between adjacent and near-adjacent tokens, which matters for local syntactic processing.

**Why this matters for TOVA:** The positional encoding adjustment is essential for the extrapolation experiments in Section 7.2. Without it, the bounded MSRNN would see positional encodings far outside its training range and would likely fail catastrophically, not because of the cache compression per se but because of the positional encoding mismatch. The fact that TOVA with this adjustment extrapolates to 70K tokens (Figure 7) demonstrates that the compression policy itself is viable at extreme lengths — the limiting factor for naive extrapolation is the positional encoding scheme, not the cache management policy.

#### Relationship to the Full System: Putting It All Together

The complete system for deploying a bounded MSRNN with TOVA during autoregressive generation works as follows:

**Initialization:** The LLM is loaded from a pretrained checkpoint without any modification. A maximum cache size $k$ is chosen (e.g., 512, 1,024). The cache is initially empty.

**For each new token to generate:**

1. **Token embedding:** The newly generated token is embedded and passed through the transformer layers sequentially.
2. **Per-layer computation:** At each layer $l$:
   - The token representation $x^l_t$ is projected into query $\mathbf{q}^l_t$, key $\mathbf{k}^l_t$, and value $\mathbf{v}^l_t$ vectors using the layer's pretrained weight matrices.
   - The query attends to all keys currently in the cache for this layer, producing attention scores $\mathbf{a}^l_t$ (a vector of length equal to the current cache size). This is the standard scaled dot-product attention.
   - The attention output is computed as the weighted sum of cached values using these scores.
   - The feedforward network processes the attention output to produce $x^{l+1}_t$.
   - **Cache management (TOVA):**
     - The new key $\mathbf{k}^l_t$ and value $\mathbf{v}^l_t$ are appended to the cache.
     - If the cache now exceeds size $k$:
       - The attention scores $\mathbf{a}^l_t$ are averaged across all heads in this layer.
       - The index $j$ with the minimum averaged score is identified.
       - The row at index $j$ is removed from both the key and value caches.
     - Otherwise (cache size $\leq k$), no eviction occurs.
3. **Output projection:** The final-layer hidden state $x^L_t$ is projected to vocabulary logits, and the next token is sampled.

**For extrapolation beyond training length:** When the total sequence length exceeds the training context window (4,096 tokens), the positional encodings for the cached tokens are recomputed using the compressed gap function $g_{\text{compressed}}$ described above. This is a one-time modification to how positional indices are assigned to cached tokens — the model weights, the attention mechanism, and the TOVA policy are unchanged.

**Key properties of this system:**

- **Training-free:** No fine-tuning, no data collection, no model modification. The same pretrained weights are used throughout.
- **Online:** Eviction decisions are made at each step based only on information already computed during the forward pass. There is no separate preprocessing or difficulty estimation step.
- **Adaptive:** The set of retained tokens changes as the attention pattern changes over the course of generation. A token that was important for steps 100–500 can be evicted at step 501 if it becomes irrelevant.
- **Layer-specific:** Each layer maintains its own independent cache and makes its own independent eviction decisions, allowing different layers to retain different tokens optimized for their different functional roles.
- **Head-averaged:** Eviction decisions within each layer are based on attention scores averaged across heads, providing robustness against noisy individual head attention patterns.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Transformers Don't Just Resemble RNNs — They *Are* RNNs, With a Matrix-Valued Hidden State

**What's distinctive at the idea level:** The paper makes a conceptual move that, once stated, feels obvious — but it represents a fundamental reframing that the field had not internalized. The dominant narrative treats transformers and RNNs as architectural opposites: transformers attend to everything directly (no state, no recurrence), while RNNs compress history into a hidden vector (state, recurrence). The paper's intervention is to point out that this distinction collapses under scrutiny of how decoder transformers actually operate during autoregressive generation. The key-value cache *is* a hidden state — it is preserved from one step to the next, it is updated with each new token, and the next token's computation depends on it. The only difference is its shape: a matrix with one row per historical token rather than a fixed-size vector.

This is not a cute analogy. It is a direct algebraic equivalence formalized through the **Multi-State RNN (MSRNN)** abstraction: a generalized RNN whose hidden state $\mathbf{H}^l_t \in \mathbb{R}^{g(t) \times d}$ has a variable number of rows controlled by the capacity function $g(t)$. A standard RNN is the special case $g(t) = 1$. A decoder transformer is the special case $g(t) = t$. Once you accept this framing, the question "how do we reduce the KV cache?" becomes "how do we convert an unbounded MSRNN into a bounded one ($g(t) \leq k$)?" — which is a well-defined, principled operation rather than an ad hoc engineering hack.

**Comparison to prior work:** Before this paper, the connection between transformers and RNNs had been explored but always required *architectural modification*: Katharopoulos et al. (2020) proposed linearized attention to make transformers recurrent, requiring training from scratch. Peng et al. (2022) presented bounded-memory transformers as a design framework for new architectures. Hutchins et al. (2022) and So et al. (2022) built hybrid transformer-RNN models. All of these approached the connection by saying "we can *make* transformers more like RNNs by changing how they work." This paper inverts that logic: transformers *already are* RNNs — you just need to look at the KV cache through the right lens. The MSRNN formalism doesn't propose a new architecture; it *describes* the existing one in terms that make the compression problem natural.

**Significance beyond performance:** This is a theoretical reframing with immediate practical consequences. By unifying transformers and RNNs under a single formalism, the paper provides intellectual scaffolding that:
- Explains *why* existing KV cache compression methods (Window, H2O) work — they are all instances of bounding $g(t)$, differing only in their eviction rule.
- Makes the design space for future compression policies transparent — any bounded MSRNN needs an eviction rule, and the quality of that rule determines performance.
- Connects the KV cache compression problem to the long-standing RNN literature on memory management, forgetting, and the difficulties of retaining information over long sequences (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997; Arjovsky et al., 2016). The authors explicitly note that "the conversion of a transformer into an RNN reintroduces the inherent challenges associated with RNNs, as they encounter difficulties with retrieving distant information" (Section 6.4), which is not a failure of TOVA but a fundamental property of bounded memory that the MSRNN framing makes legible.

**Evidence anchor:** The equivalence is established mathematically in Sections 3.1–3.2 through direct algebraic rewriting, not through empirical approximation. The fact that TOVA with 1/8 of the cache nearly matches full-model performance on PG-19 (Figure 3: 7.41 PPL at 512 tokens vs. 7.16 at 4,096, a difference of only 0.25) demonstrates that the bounded MSRNN is not just formally equivalent but practically viable — the model's behavior under bounded memory is close to its behavior under unbounded memory.

**Incremental or fundamental?** Fundamental. This is a rare case where a paper changes how you *think about* an existing architecture rather than proposing something new. After reading it, the standard view that "transformers don't have recurrence" feels like a category error — of course they do, you just weren't looking at the right thing.

### Innovation 2: The Right Eviction Policy Is the One That Asks the Model What It Needs *Right Now* — Not What It Needed in the Past

**What's distinctive at the idea level:** Once you accept the bounded MSRNN framing, the central design question becomes: *which cached token do you evict when the cache is full?* All prior approaches answer this question with some form of aggregation over time. Window (Wang et al., 2019) aggregates nothing — it uses a purely positional rule (oldest first). Window+i (Xiao et al., 2023; Han et al., 2023) aggregates nothing for the window portion but hard-codes protection for the first $i$ positions. H2O (Zhang et al., 2023) aggregates attention scores *across all previous decoding steps* and retains tokens with the highest cumulative scores.

TOVA's distinctive move is to reject aggregation entirely. The eviction decision is made based on the attention scores from the **current query only** — a purely local, instantaneous signal. The conceptual insight is that the attention mechanism was *trained* to answer exactly this question: "which past tokens are relevant to the current generation step?" The token with the lowest current attention score is, by definition, the one the model currently needs the least. Evicting it should minimize the harm to the current step's computation.

**Comparison to prior work:** H2O represents the state of the art among training-free compression policies before this paper. Its core idea — aggregate attention over time to identify "heavy hitters" — is intuitively appealing: tokens that have been important for many steps should continue to be important. But this intuition has a specific failure mode: *a token that was irrelevant for steps 1–1,000 can become highly relevant at step 1,001*. In a long document, the model might discuss one topic for hundreds of tokens, introduce a new topic, and then return to the earlier topic. During the intermediate discussion, tokens from the first topic receive low attention scores — and H2O's cumulative scoring progressively penalizes them, potentially evicting them before they become relevant again. TOVA, by using only the current attention scores, avoids this hysteresis: when the model returns to the earlier topic, those tokens' attention scores spike, and they are protected from eviction *at that moment*.

This is a subtle but critical distinction. H2O optimizes for *historical importance* (was this token useful over the whole sequence so far?), while TOVA optimizes for *current relevance* (is this token useful for the very next token we're about to generate?). The evidence that TOVA consistently outperforms H2O (Table 3: TOVA-layer achieves 7.71 PPL at 256 tokens vs. 8.21 for H2O-head and 8.22 for H2O-layer) suggests that current relevance is a better proxy for future importance than historical importance — the model's attention patterns shift over time, and an aggregated score is a lagging indicator of what matters now.

**Significance beyond performance:** This innovation establishes a design principle that generalizes beyond TOVA: **for dynamic eviction policies, local signals beat aggregated signals because attention patterns are non-stationary.** Future work on KV cache compression should be skeptical of any method that averages over time, because the relevance of a token at step $t$ may be completely uncorrelated with its relevance at step $t+1000$. The fact that TOVA, the simplest possible local policy (just look at the argmin of the current attention vector), outperforms more complex aggregation-based methods is a strong empirical case for this principle.

**Evidence anchor:** Table 3 (Appendix A) provides the cleanest comparison. At multi-state size 256, TOVA-layer achieves 7.71 PPL vs. 8.21 for H2O-head and 8.22 for H2O-layer. At size 128, the gap widens: 8.32 vs. 8.97. The absolute improvements are modest but consistent across all cache sizes and models (Figures 3, 4, 5). The extrapolation result in Figure 7 extends this finding to extreme lengths: TOVA maintains similar perplexity at 70K tokens as at 10K, while Window+4 degrades — evidence that the local, content-driven eviction policy scales to lengths far beyond training.

**Incremental or fundamental?** Incremental but important. TOVA is a specific eviction rule, not a new class of algorithms. But the principle it embodies — *use the model's own current attention to decide what to forget* — is a conceptual contribution that should influence future work. It is the natural eviction rule once you accept the MSRNN framing: if the transformer is an RNN, its attention mechanism is its gating function, and TOVA simply makes that gating explicit and enforces it as a hard capacity constraint.

### Innovation 3: Transformers Routinely Act as Bounded-Memory Systems, Even When They Don't Have To

**What's distinctive at the idea level:** The paper's deepest empirical finding is not that TOVA works well — it's that the full, uncompressed transformer works *almost as well* with dramatically less memory. This is a **diagnostic result** about the nature of pretrained transformers, not just a demonstration of a compression method. The key evidence is the plateau behavior in Figures 3–5: reducing the cache from 4,096 tokens to 512 tokens (an 8× reduction) causes only a 0.25 PPL degradation on PG-19 (Figure 3, LLaMA-2: 7.16 → 7.41). On SQuALITY summarization, reducing to 512–1,024 tokens loses less than 1 ROUGE point (Figure 4). On text generation, reducing to 1,024 tokens makes TOVA indistinguishable from the full model in 94% of GPT-4 evaluations (Figure 6).

If transformers genuinely needed access to every historical token to maintain their performance, we would expect a smooth monotonic degradation as cache size decreases — every eviction would remove some useful information, and the loss would accumulate. Instead, we see a threshold: compression down to a certain level causes almost no degradation, and then degradation accelerates below that threshold. This is the signature of a system that already operates with an effective memory much smaller than its theoretical capacity.

**Comparison to prior work:** The dominant assumption in the field — implicit in the design of the transformer architecture and the practice of caching all KV pairs — is that the full attention over all tokens is necessary for the model's performance. After all, the model was *trained* with access to all tokens; removing any should hurt. This paper challenges that assumption directly. It shows that at inference time, the model's internal attention mechanism spontaneously focuses on a small subset of tokens, and a policy that simply retains that subset and discards the rest recovers nearly all performance.

This is related to but distinct from the sparsity patterns observed in attention matrices by prior work (Clark et al., 2019; Hassid et al., 2022). Those works showed that many attention heads focus on specific positions or tokens (e.g., BERT often attends to punctuation, the first token, or adjacent tokens). This paper goes further: it shows that you can *enforce* sparsity by physically deleting low-attention tokens from the cache, and the model barely notices. The attention sparsity is not just an observational curiosity — it is functionally redundant information that can be discarded with minimal cost.

**Significance beyond performance:** This finding has important theoretical implications for how we understand transformers. The fact that the model can operate with 1/8 of its cache suggests that during pretraining, the transformer learned to represent and manipulate information using a *compressed internal state* rather than by faithfully attending to every token it has ever seen. The full KV cache is, in effect, a wasteful representation — most of the stored tokens are not actually used by the attention mechanism in practice. This aligns with the paper's MSRNN narrative: transformers act like RNNs not just formally but *behaviorally*, maintaining a compact working memory rather than a complete transcript of the past.

It also has practical implications for pretraining. If models trained with full attention at inference time only actually use a fraction of their cache, perhaps they could be trained with bounded attention from the start — reducing training cost and memory while potentially achieving similar performance. The paper does not explore this direction, but the diagnostic result makes it a natural follow-up question.

**Evidence anchor:** The full picture comes from comparing across tasks. On PG-19 (Figure 3), TOVA with 512 tokens matches the full model within 0.3–0.4 PPL for all three model families. On SQuALITY (Figure 4), TOVA with 512–1,024 tokens is within 1 ROUGE point. On QASPER (Figure 5) — a retrieval task that forces the model to locate specific facts — the required cache size is larger (at least 2,048 tokens, half the full context) but still substantially compressed. The task-dependence is itself informative: retrieval tasks stress bounded memory more than language modeling or summarization, exactly as RNN theory would predict (the difficulty of retrieving distant information with bounded memory is a classic RNN failure mode).

**Incremental or fundamental?** Fundamental as a diagnostic finding. The paper is not just proposing a compression method; it is demonstrating something about how transformers *actually work* that contradicts the prevailing assumptions. The bounded-MSRNN behavior is an empirical property of pretrained transformers, not a capability that TOVA creates — TOVA merely reveals it.

### Innovation 4: The Attention Mechanism Is a Sufficient Eviction Oracle — No Learned Importance Estimator Needed

**What's distinctive at the idea level:** Many approaches to reducing transformer memory in other contexts require learning a separate model to decide what to keep. For example, Anagnostidis et al. (2023) train a dynamic context pruning module. Token merging and pruning methods (Yun et al., 2023; Berchansky et al., 2023) often use learned importance scores. Even H2O, despite being training-free, computes a derived statistic (cumulative attention) that is not directly produced by the standard forward pass.

TOVA's innovation on this axis is the demonstration that **the attention scores already computed during the normal forward pass are a sufficient signal for cache eviction — no separate importance estimator, no additional forward passes, no learned parameters, no hyperparameter tuning beyond the cache size $k$.** The attention mechanism was trained to identify relevant tokens from the past; TOVA repurposes this existing capability for memory management without any additional training.

**Comparison to prior work:** This is not the first training-free compression policy (Window and Window+i are even simpler), but it is the first that achieves strong performance without imposing structural priors (fixed windows, protected early tokens) while using only information already available in the forward pass. H2O requires maintaining and updating cumulative attention scores as a separate data structure. TOVA's eviction decision uses exactly the attention weights computed for the current query — no state beyond what the transformer already maintains.

The key design choice that enables this is **the rejection of temporal aggregation**. By using only the current attention scores, TOVA avoids the need to maintain running statistics of token importance. This makes the policy extremely lightweight to implement: Algorithm 1 (Appendix B) is essentially an argmin over the averaged attention weights followed by a concatenation of the cache tensors to remove one row. The computational overhead is negligible compared to the attention computation itself.

**Significance beyond performance:** This finding establishes that the attention mechanism is a *general-purpose relevance oracle* for its own context — not just a mechanism for computing output representations. The attention scores reflect what the model "wants" to look at, and using those scores to decide what to remember is a natural, zero-cost approach to memory management. This principle could generalize beyond KV cache compression: attention scores could guide which context to include in retrieval-augmented generation, which documents to keep in a multi-turn dialogue, or which examples to select for in-context learning.

The fact that TOVA works well without any task-specific tuning (the same policy is applied to language modeling, summarization, QA, and story generation without modification) further supports the claim that attention scores are a robust, general-purpose importance signal.

**Evidence anchor:** TOVA's performance across all tasks and model families (Figures 3–6) using only the cache size $k$ as a tunable parameter. The ablation in Appendix A (Table 3) shows that adding explicit token protection (TOVA-layer+1, TOVA-layer+4) does not improve over the base TOVA-layer policy — the attention scores alone are sufficient, and the policy automatically retains the important early tokens (Figure 9 shows the first token is kept through the entire sequence without being explicitly protected).

**Incremental or fundamental?** Incremental as a method, but the *principle* it validates — that attention scores are a sufficient importance signal for content-driven memory management — is a conceptual contribution that should influence future work on efficient transformer inference. It suggests that the path to better compression policies is not more complex importance estimators but better attention mechanisms (or better use of the existing ones).

### Innovation 5: The First Token Is Special, But You Don't Need to Hard-Code That — Dynamic Attention-Based Eviction Recovers It Automatically

**What's distinctive at the idea level:** Prior work (Xiao et al., 2023; Han et al., 2023) established that the very first token in a sequence often serves as an "attention sink" — it receives disproportionately high attention scores throughout generation, and retaining it is crucial for model stability. Based on this finding, Window+i policies explicitly protect the first $i$ tokens from eviction. This is a structural prior: *assume* the first token matters, and design the policy accordingly.

TOVA demonstrates that this structural prior is unnecessary. Because the first token *does* receive consistently high attention scores, TOVA — which evicts based on attention scores — automatically retains it without any special-case code. Figure 9 shows this directly: the first token (position 0) is kept for all 4,096 decoding steps across all multi-state sizes (256, 512, 1024, 2048). Other early tokens (positions 1–25) are evicted far more rapidly, with their lifespan proportional to their positional distance from the start and the cache size.

The significance is not just that TOVA replicates a known finding — it's that TOVA *discovers* the importance of the first token from the attention scores rather than having it programmed in. This is a stronger result: it confirms that the attention mechanism genuinely considers the first token important, and that a policy which trusts the attention mechanism will reproduce the same behavior that previous work had to manually encode.

**Comparison to prior work:** Window+i requires choosing $i$ — how many early tokens to protect. Xiao et al. (2023) show that $i = 4$ works well, but this is an empirical choice that might not generalize across models, tasks, or sequence lengths. TOVA eliminates this hyperparameter: the attention mechanism decides which early tokens to keep, and it turns out to keep the first token (and to a lesser extent a few others) without being told to. The authors note in Appendix A that "when we enhance our TOVA policy with the explicit preservation of $i$ initial tokens, the results remain relatively unchanged, implying that our policy inherently retains the crucial tokens."

**Significance beyond performance:** This innovation demonstrates a methodological principle: **let the model tell you what matters, rather than imposing your assumptions about what should matter.** Window+i encodes a hypothesis ("early tokens are important") that turns out to be partially correct (the first token matters a lot, tokens 2–25 much less so). TOVA, by not encoding any positional hypothesis, discovers a more nuanced pattern: the first token is critically important, but other early tokens vary in importance depending on content (Figure 8 and Appendix G show that some early tokens are retained for thousands of steps while adjacent ones are quickly evicted). The part-of-speech analysis in Table 2 reveals that proper nouns, possessive endings, and punctuation marks tend to be retained longer — a content-driven pattern that no position-based policy could capture.

**Evidence anchor:** Figure 9 provides the quantitative evidence: the first token's average number of steps kept equals the full sequence length (4,096) for all cache sizes, while the second token is evicted much earlier (dropping from ~3,500 steps at cache size 2,048 to ~2,500 at size 256). Table 2 shows that POS tags, quotation marks, and proper nouns last 2–4× longer than average tokens. Table 3 (Appendix A) shows that TOVA-layer+1 and TOVA-layer+4 achieve nearly identical perplexity to TOVA-layer across all cache sizes, confirming that explicit protection doesn't help.

**Incremental or fundamental?** Incremental as a specific finding, but methodologically significant. It's a clean demonstration that attention-based eviction recovers known patterns automatically, which validates the approach and suggests it will generalize to other patterns that we haven't yet hand-coded into policies. If a future model develops a different attention pattern (e.g., attending more to section headings than to the first token), TOVA would adapt automatically, while Window+i would need to be reconfigured.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses four distinct evaluation settings, each designed to probe a different aspect of long-range performance. For **language modeling**, the primary metric is perplexity on the PG-19 test set (Rae et al., 2020), which consists of 100 full-length books averaging approximately 70K tokens each — a widely used benchmark for evaluating long-range language models (So et al., 2022; Hutchins et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023). For **long-range understanding**, the paper selects two tasks from the ZeroSCROLLS benchmark (Shaham et al., 2023): SQuALITY (Wang et al., 2022), a question-focused summarization dataset, and QASPER (Dasigi et al., 2021), a question-answering dataset based on scientific papers from the S2ORC corpus (Lo et al., 2020) that functions as a retrieval task since answering its questions requires locating specific details in long documents. For **text generation**, the authors prompt models to generate long stories, sampling 100 unique stories per model variant using different random seeds. Due to the inherent difficulty of evaluating open-ended story quality, they employ GPT-4 (gpt-4-0613) as a pairwise evaluator (Chiang et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023): for each seed, GPT-4 compares the story generated by the compressed model against the story generated by the full model, with each pair presented twice in alternating order to account for GPT-4's known positional bias (Wang et al., 2023), and a win is counted only if the same approach is preferred in both positions. For **extrapolation** evaluation, the paper reports average perplexity on the first 70K tokens of all PG-19 books that contain at least that many tokens (52 books total), applying a positional encoding adjustment to handle context lengths beyond the training window.

- **Base model(s).** The paper experiments with three leading transformer decoder LLM families to establish robustness across architectures. For language modeling: LLaMA-2-7B (Touvron et al., 2023b), Mistral-7B (Jiang et al., 2023), and Yi-6B (Young et al., 2024) — all approximately 7B parameter models. For long-range understanding tasks, which require instruction-following capability, the paper uses three instruction-tuned variants: LLaMA-2-7B-chat (Touvron et al., 2023b), Mistral-7B-Instruct (Jiang et al., 2023), and neural-chat-7B (Lv et al., 2023) — a fine-tuned variant of Mistral-7B optimized for instruction tasks. For text generation, the paper uses MythoLogic-13B (Padar, 2023), a LLaMA-2-13B variant fine-tuned specifically for story generation. The choice of three distinct model families at the 7B scale is deliberate: it tests whether the bounded MSRNN behavior is an artifact of a particular pretraining recipe or a general property of transformer decoders.

- **Metrics.** For language modeling, the paper reports **perplexity** on the PG-19 test set, computed using efficient masking (Appendix E describes modifying the attention mask to incorporate the different MSRNN policies while evaluating all tokens in parallel). For SQuALITY summarization, the paper reports the **geometric mean of ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L** scores, computed against the gold summary as specified in Shaham et al. (2023). For QASPER question answering, the paper reports **F1 score** following the standard evaluation protocol from Dasigi et al. (2021). For text generation, the paper reports pairwise **win rate** as judged by GPT-4, with ties counted separately (the evaluator is asked to choose between "Response (A)", "Response (B)", or "equal quality"). For extrapolation, the paper reports average perplexity over all tokens across the full 70K-token sequences.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares TOVA against four compression policies, all of which operate in a training-free manner on pretrained LLMs. **Window** (Wang et al., 2019) implements a First In First Out (FIFO) strategy: when the multi-state reaches capacity, the oldest cached state (corresponding to the earliest token) is discarded, retaining only the most recent tokens. **Window+4** retains a fixed window of recent tokens but additionally protects the first 4 cached states from eviction, following the finding from Xiao et al. (2023) and Han et al. (2023) that a small number of initial tokens serve as "attention sinks" that are critical for model stability. **H2O** (Heavy-Hitter Oracle; Zhang et al., 2023) splits the cache into a recent window and a "heavy-hitter" segment; the heavy-hitter segment retains tokens with the highest cumulative attention scores aggregated across all previous decoding steps, dynamically updating which tokens are promoted from the recent segment. The paper evaluates both head-wise and layer-wise variants of H2O, finding they perform similarly and using the head-wise version as recommended by the original authors. The **Full model (topline)** uses the complete, unbounded KV cache with the full training sequence length of 4,096 tokens. For the long-range understanding tasks, an additional baseline is included: the model is presented with a truncated version of the input example, where the truncation length equals the multi-state size (i.e., the model sees only the first $k$ tokens of the document with no compression, corresponding to an unbounded MSRNN with a shorter sequence length rather than a bounded MSRNN with compression).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The primary resource metric is the **multi-state size** — the number of key-value pairs retained in the cache — expressed as either an absolute number of slots or as a fraction of the full training context length (4,096 tokens). For language modeling, the paper examines multi-state sizes in exponential scales of $2^j$ for $j \in \{6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12\}$, corresponding to sizes 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024, 2048, and 4096. For long-range understanding, sizes of $2^j$ for $j \in \{8, 9, 10, 11, 12\}$ (256 through 4096) are used, with the lower end starting at 256 because smaller sizes proved insufficient for coherent text understanding. For text generation, three size points are evaluated: 256, 512, and 1024 tokens. The paper also reports actual memory consumption (in GB) and maximum batch size on a single V100 GPU (Table 1), demonstrating the practical throughput implications: a reduction from 4,096 to 512 cache slots reduces memory from 2.18 GB to 0.28 GB per batch element and increases maximum batch size from 8 to 139 — a 17× improvement — yielding 4.8× higher overall throughput when decoding 512 sequences totaling 2 million tokens.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ a cross-validation protocol for policy selection — TOVA has no learned parameters or hyperparameters to tune beyond the multi-state size $k$. All evaluation is performed directly on the specified test sets. For the text generation evaluation, the paper implements a positional bias correction: each pair of stories is presented to GPT-4 twice with alternating order, and a win is only counted if the same model is preferred in both positions, with cases where preferences differ counted as ties. For the PG-19 language modeling evaluation, texts are split into chunks of 4,096 tokens, and efficient masking (described in Appendix E) is applied to parallelize perplexity computation across all tokens while respecting the per-policy attention patterns. All experiments use bfloat16 precision on Nvidia V100 GPUs (Appendix E).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Language Modeling (PG-19 Perplexity)

The headline result for language modeling is that **TOVA achieves within 0.4 perplexity points of the full 4,096-token model using only 512 cache slots — 1/8 of the original cache size — across all three model families tested** (Figure 3). At the full context size (4,096), all policies converge to the same perplexity as the topline by definition (they retain all tokens). The differences emerge at compressed sizes, where TOVA consistently and substantially outperforms all baselines.

For LLaMA-2-7B (Figure 3, left): at multi-state size 512, TOVA achieves 7.41 PPL compared to 7.16 for the full model (a gap of 0.25), while the next-best baseline (Window+4) requires 2,048 slots to achieve comparable performance. At size 256, TOVA achieves 7.71 PPL versus 8.19 for Window+4 and 8.21 for H2O-head — a meaningful gap of approximately 0.5 PPL points. At the smallest size tested (64), TOVA achieves 9.53 PPL versus 10.28 for Window+4 and 10.22 for H2O-head. The Window baseline catastrophically fails at small multi-state sizes: 4812.27 PPL at size 64 (Table 3), confirming that pure recency-based eviction destroys the model's ability to process text — it is not merely degraded but fundamentally broken.

For Mistral-7B (Figure 3, center): the pattern is nearly identical. TOVA with 512 slots achieves approximately 7.8 PPL versus 7.5 for the full model. Window+4 and H2O require 1,024–2,048 slots to match this performance.

For Yi-6B (Figure 3, right): TOVA with 512 slots achieves approximately 9.7 PPL versus 9.2 for the full model — again within 0.5 PPL. The relative ranking of policies is preserved across all three model families.

**Task-dependence of compression ratio:** The paper notes that the compression ratio achievable without meaningful degradation varies by task — language modeling tolerates 8× compression (512/4096), while retrieval-intensive tasks like QASPER require 2× compression (2048/4096) for comparable performance. This task-dependence is treated as a finding rather than a limitation of TOVA: it reveals that different tasks stress the bounded memory to different degrees, consistent with classic RNN limitations on long-range retrieval.

#### Long-Range Understanding: SQuALITY Summarization

For the SQuALITY summarization task, **TOVA consistently outperforms all baselines and achieves ROUGE scores within 1 point of the full model using 1/8 to 1/4 of the full multi-state size** (Figure 4). The topline (full 4,096-token model) achieves approximately 19.5–20.0 ROUGE (geometric mean of ROUGE-1/2/L) across the three instruction-tuned models.

For LLaMA-2-7B-chat (Figure 4, left): TOVA with 512 tokens achieves approximately 19.2 ROUGE versus approximately 19.7 for the topline — a gap of approximately 0.5 points. Window+4 at 512 tokens achieves approximately 17.5 ROUGE, and the truncated baseline (no compression, just shorter input) achieves approximately 17.0. The gap between TOVA and Window+4 is substantial: at 512 tokens, TOVA outperforms Window+4 by approximately 1.7 ROUGE points. At 256 tokens, TOVA achieves approximately 17.8 ROUGE while Window+4 drops to approximately 17.2. Notably, TOVA with 1,024 tokens essentially matches the topline (approximately 19.6–19.7).

For Mistral-7B-Instruct (Figure 4, center): TOVA with 512 tokens achieves approximately 18.8 ROUGE versus approximately 19.5 for the topline. Window+4 at the same size achieves approximately 17.0 — a gap of approximately 1.8 points favoring TOVA. TOVA reaches within one point of the topline at 512–1,024 tokens (1/8 to 1/4 of full context).

For neural-chat-7B (Figure 4, right): TOVA with 512 tokens achieves approximately 19.2 ROUGE versus approximately 20.2 for the topline. The pattern holds: TOVA dominates at all multi-state sizes, and the gap is largest at intermediate compression ratios (256–1,024) where the baseline policies drop off more steeply.

The truncated baseline (presenting the model with a shorter input rather than compressing a long input) consistently underperforms all compression policies at the same multi-state size. This is a non-obvious result: it indicates that seeing a compressed version of the full document is better than seeing only the beginning at full resolution. The compression policies, particularly TOVA, are effectively condensing the document by retaining the most salient tokens throughout rather than simply truncating.

#### Long-Range Understanding: QASPER Question Answering

QASPER is designed as a retrieval task — answering its questions requires locating specific facts within long scientific papers. This makes it a stress test for bounded memory: if critical details from early in the document are evicted from the cache, the model cannot answer. The results (Figure 5) confirm that **QASPER is substantially harder for bounded MSRNNs than SQuALITY or language modeling, with TOVA requiring half the full multi-state size (2,048 tokens) rather than 1/8 to achieve comparable performance to the topline, while still maintaining a large and consistent advantage over all baselines**.

For LLaMA-2-7B-chat (Figure 5, left): the topline achieves approximately 23.5 F1. TOVA with 2,048 tokens achieves approximately 22–23 F1 — within approximately 1 point. At 1,024 tokens, TOVA drops to approximately 19 F1, and at 512 tokens to approximately 15 F1. Window+4 at 1,024 tokens achieves approximately 14 F1, a gap of approximately 5 F1 points compared to TOVA at the same size. The truncated baseline performs substantially worse: at 2,048 tokens it achieves approximately 21 F1 (close to TOVA), but drops precipitously at smaller sizes (approximately 12 F1 at 512 tokens).

For Mistral-7B-Instruct (Figure 5, center): the results are notably different in scale — Mistral-Instruct achieves approximately 41 F1 at the topline, dramatically outperforming LLaMA-2-chat. TOVA with 2,048 tokens achieves approximately 39–40 F1; at 1,024 tokens approximately 36 F1; at 512 tokens approximately 30 F1. Window+4 at 1,024 tokens achieves approximately 27 F1 — roughly a 9-point gap. The gap between TOVA and baselines is even larger for Mistral-Instruct than for LLaMA-2-chat, suggesting that the attention-based eviction policy better preserves the model's strong retrieval capability.

For neural-chat-7B (Figure 5, right): the overall F1 scores are lower (topline approximately 32 F1). TOVA with 2,048 tokens achieves approximately 30 F1; at 1,024 tokens approximately 24 F1; at 512 tokens approximately 18 F1. Window+4 at 1,024 tokens achieves approximately 17 F1 — approximately a 7-point gap.

**Why QASPER is harder:** The paper attributes the larger required cache size on QASPER to the nature of the task. Summarization (SQuALITY) requires understanding the gist of a document — a compressed representation of the overall content suffices. Retrieval QA (QASPER) requires pinpointing specific sentences, named entities, or numerical values that may appear once in a 4,096-token document and never be repeated. If TOVA evicts the token containing that specific detail (e.g., the exact percentage reported in a results section), the model has no way to answer correctly. This is explicitly connected to the classic RNN limitation on distant information retrieval (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997; Arjovsky et al., 2016): "the conversion of a transformer into an RNN reintroduces the inherent challenges associated with RNNs, as they encounter difficulties with retrieving distant information" (Section 6.4).

#### Text Generation: Story Quality Evaluation

The text generation evaluation adds a qualitatively different dimension: rather than measuring accuracy or perplexity on a fixed test set, it probes whether TOVA-compressed models can produce coherent, high-quality long-form creative text. **TOVA with a multi-state size of 1,024 is preferred by or ties with the full (4,096) model in 94% of pairwise comparisons, and even with only 256 tokens, TOVA wins or ties in 53% of comparisons** (Figure 6).

The evaluation uses MythoLogic-13B, a LLaMA-2-13B variant fine-tuned for story generation, prompted to "Write a very long story (at least 4,000 words) with at least 20 named characters, spanning 3 countries and 9 cities, with at least 10 chapters and a lot of plot twists" (full prompt in Table 4, Appendix C). For each of 100 random seeds, a story is generated with the full model and with TOVA at three multi-state sizes (256, 512, 1,024), and GPT-4 judges which is better.

At 1,024 tokens: TOVA wins in 5% of cases, ties in 6% of cases, and loses in 88% of cases — meaning that in 11% of comparisons, TOVA is judged equal to or better than the full model despite using only 1/4 of the cache. This 88% "loss" rate may seem high, but it is important to note that these are extremely long, complex stories, and even the full model struggles with coherence over thousands of words.

At 512 tokens: TOVA wins 10%, ties 19%, loses 71% — TOVA is preferred or ties in 29% of comparisons.

At 256 tokens: TOVA wins 6%, ties 47%, loses 47% — effectively a coin flip between TOVA and the full model, despite using 1/16 of the cache.

**A note on story length:** The paper observes that limiting the multi-state size reduces the average length of generated stories: 1,566 tokens for the full model, maintained at 1,566 for 1,024 multi-state size, but dropping to 1,503 at 512 and 1,361 at 256. The authors do not draw strong conclusions from this, but it suggests that compressed memory leads the model to produce shorter outputs — possibly because it "forgets" plot elements or character details and has trouble sustaining long-range narrative coherence, or possibly because the positional encoding adjustment and cache constraints create an implicit bias toward shorter generation.

**GPT-4 evaluation limitations:** The paper explicitly acknowledges the limitations of using GPT-4 as an evaluator (Section "Limitations"): "Evaluating models on long text generation is computationally expensive and might limit others from reproducing our results. Further, the evaluation of such task is extremely complicated, even for humans. We therefore resort to GPT-4... We recognize that this is far from perfect, and will most likely not catch the full breadth of evaluating text quality." This is a fair caveat — GPT-4's pairwise preferences are a noisy proxy for story quality, and the 47% tie rate at 256 tokens may partly reflect the evaluator's inability to distinguish between two imperfect long stories rather than genuine equivalence.

#### Memory and Throughput Efficiency

Table 1 quantifies the practical impact of cache compression on deployment efficiency. **TOVA with multi-state size 512 — which performs comparably to the full model on language modeling and summarization — reduces GPU memory consumption from 2.18 GB to 0.28 GB per batch element, enables increasing batch size from 8 to 139 on a single V100 GPU, and yields 4.8× higher throughput when decoding at maximum batch size.**

The memory reduction is nearly linear in the cache size: halving the cache roughly halves the memory footprint (2,048 tokens: 1.11 GB; 1,024 tokens: 0.56 GB; 512 tokens: 0.28 GB; 256 tokens: 0.15 GB). The throughput gain (4.8×) is less than the batch size improvement (17×) because larger batches have some overhead and the GPU's computational throughput has limits — but a 4.8× speedup is practically significant for production deployments.

**What the memory accounting includes:** The paper notes that LLM decoding memory consumption has three components: the model weights (fixed), the KV cache (grows with sequence length and batch size), and activations. TOVA reduces only the KV cache component, but since this is the dominant bottleneck for batch size in long-sequence decoding, the practical impact is substantial. For a model with 7B parameters in bfloat16, the weights alone consume approximately 14 GB. The KV cache at batch size 1 with 4,096 tokens consumes an additional 2.18 GB (Table 1), which may seem modest — but with batch size 8, the total cache memory reaches 17.44 GB, which combined with the 14 GB for weights exceeds a V100's 32 GB capacity. Compressing the cache by 8× (from 4,096 to 512) reduces the per-batch cache from 2.18 GB to 0.28 GB, allowing much larger batches before hitting the GPU memory ceiling.

#### Extrapolation Beyond Training Context Length

The extrapolation experiments test whether bounded MSRNNs can handle sequences dramatically longer than the training context (4,096 tokens), and whether TOVA's eviction policy remains effective at these extreme lengths. **TOVA with a multi-state size of 512 successfully extrapolates to 70K tokens with less than 0.5 PPL degradation compared to its performance at 10K tokens, while consistently outperforming Window+4 at all lengths** (Figure 7).

The positional encoding adjustment (the double-logarithmic gap compression described in Section 3) is essential for this result. Without it, the model would see positional encodings far outside its training range and would likely fail entirely. The fact that TOVA *with* the adjustment maintains stable perplexity out to 70K tokens demonstrates two things: (1) the compression policy itself is viable at extreme lengths — the attention-based eviction logic continues to select appropriate tokens even when the generation has been running for tens of thousands of steps; and (2) the main barrier to length extrapolation is the positional encoding scheme, not the cache management policy.

At 10K tokens, TOVA achieves approximately 6.85 PPL; at 70K tokens, approximately 7.25 PPL — a degradation of approximately 0.4 PPL points. Window+4 starts at approximately 7.05 PPL at 10K and degrades to approximately 7.55 at 70K — a gap that grows from approximately 0.2 PPL at 10K to approximately 0.3 PPL at 70K. The fact that the gap *widens* with length suggests that TOVA's content-driven eviction becomes relatively more important as sequences grow longer: when the cache retains only 512 tokens out of 70,000, selecting the right 512 tokens matters enormously, and TOVA's attention-based selection improves over Window+4's position-based selection.

**The extrapolation limitation:** The paper only compares TOVA against Window+4 for extrapolation, stating that "as models struggle to extrapolate to such long contexts, we only compare TOVA with Window+4, which has been shown to support such contexts" (Section 7.2). Comparisons against H2O at these lengths are not reported, likely because H2O's cache management becomes more complex at extreme lengths (the cumulative attention scores would need to be maintained over 70K steps). The lack of an H2O comparison at extreme lengths is a minor omission.

#### Peak Memory Comparison: Compressed Cache vs. Truncation

An implicit but informative comparison runs throughout Figures 3, 4, and 5: the performance of bounded MSRNNs (compressed cache) versus unbounded MSRNNs with truncated input (shorter sequence length). In Figure 4 (SQuALITY), the "Baseline" line in each plot corresponds to truncating the input to the multi-state size with no compression. For example, at 512 tokens, the truncated baseline feeds only the first 512 tokens of the document while TOVA feeds the entire 4,096-token document but compresses the cache to 512 slots. TOVA substantially outperforms truncation at all sizes for all models. The same pattern appears in Figures 5 and 10–11.

This is a practically important finding: if you have a fixed memory budget for the KV cache, you are better off **processing the full document with a compressed cache** than **processing only the beginning of the document with full attention**. The compressed cache acts as a dynamic summarization mechanism, retaining the most attention-relevant information from throughout the document, while truncation simply discards everything after the first $k$ tokens regardless of its importance.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The ablation studies are primarily presented in Appendix A (Table 3) and Appendix J (Figures 15a, 15b), with additional analysis in Section 7.3 (Figures 8, 9 and Table 2).

**Per-head vs. per-layer attention aggregation for TOVA:** The paper compares TOVA-head (each attention head independently evicts based on its own attention scores) against TOVA-layer (attention scores are averaged across all heads within a layer before eviction, and all heads remove the same token). Table 3 shows that TOVA-layer consistently and substantially outperforms TOVA-head at all multi-state sizes. At size 256, TOVA-layer achieves 7.71 PPL vs. 9.55 for TOVA-head — a gap of 1.84 PPL. At size 128, the gap is 1.23 PPL (8.32 vs. 9.55). At size 512, the gap narrows to 0.49 PPL (7.41 vs. 7.90). The paper attributes this difference to the "more robust selection mechanism employed by the layer version, which requires agreement among all heads to determine the importance of specific tokens" (Appendix A). Individual attention heads may have noisy or highly specialized attention patterns; a token that one head ignores might be critical for another head, and head-wise eviction risks removing tokens that are important for some subset of heads. Layer-wise averaging mitigates this.

**Comparison between H2O-head and H2O-layer:** Table 3 shows that H2O-head and H2O-layer produce nearly identical perplexity across all multi-state sizes (e.g., at size 256: 8.21 vs. 8.22; at size 128: 8.97 vs. 8.97). The authors follow Zhang et al. (2023) in using the head-wise version for all main experiments.

**Explicit protection of initial tokens in TOVA (TOVA-layer+i):** The paper tests whether adding explicit protection of the first $i$ tokens to TOVA (analogous to the Window+i strategy) improves performance. Table 3 shows that TOVA-layer+1 and TOVA-layer+4 achieve nearly identical perplexity to the base TOVA-layer at all cache sizes. At size 256: TOVA-layer 7.71, TOVA-layer+1 7.71, TOVA-layer+4 7.72. At size 128: 8.32, 8.31, 8.33. At size 64: 9.53, 9.53, 9.63. The differences are negligible. This confirms that TOVA's attention-based eviction *already* retains the important initial tokens without being explicitly told to — Figure 9 provides the mechanistic evidence (the first token is kept for the entire sequence). Adding explicit protection is redundant.

**Window+i for different $i$ values:** The paper compares Window+1 and Window+4 in Table 3. Window+4 slightly outperforms Window+1 at most cache sizes (e.g., at size 128: 8.98 vs. 8.97), but the differences are small. Window alone (no protected tokens) is catastrophic, confirming that retaining at least the first token is essential for stability. This replicates Xiao et al. (2023)'s finding that the first token serves as an "attention sink," while demonstrating that TOVA discovers and preserves this pattern without configuration.

**Revision history in verifier context (not applicable to this paper):** This is a reference to the reference example's structure; this paper does not involve verifiers or revision models. No equivalent ablation exists.

**Task type as an implicit ablation:** The paper's use of three qualitatively different long-range tasks (language modeling, summarization, and retrieval QA) implicitly ablates the effect of task type on the required cache size. The finding that retrieval QA (QASPER) requires a larger cache (2,048 tokens, or 1/2 of full) than language modeling or summarization (512 tokens, or 1/8 of full) is a robustness check on the claim that transformers act as bounded MSRNNs: the degree of boundedness is task-dependent, and tasks that require precise retrieval of specific distant tokens stress the bounded memory more severely.

**Model family as an implicit ablation:** The consistency of results across LLaMA-2, Mistral, and Yi — three independently trained model families with different pretraining data, tokenizers, and architectural details — serves as a robustness check that the bounded MSRNN behavior is not an artifact of a particular pretraining recipe. The relative ranking of policies (TOVA > H2O ≈ Window+4 >> Window) is preserved across all three families for language modeling (Figure 3) and across instruction-tuned variants for understanding tasks (Figures 4, 5).

**Positional encoding adjustment ablation:** The paper mentions that "preliminary experiments with other compression functions, e.g., ln(g) and sqrt(g) showed inferior results" (Section 7.2) compared to the double-logarithmic compression ln(ln(g)). This is not presented as a formal ablation table but as a design justification. Single logarithmic compression of a gap of 50,000 positions yields approximately 10.82 — still well outside the training distribution for a 4,096-context model. Double logarithmic compression yields approximately 2.38, which is within the training distribution. The threshold of 10 for small gaps preserves local positional sensitivity. The absence of a formal ablation table for this choice is a minor weakness — quantitative comparisons against linear, sqrt, and ln would have been informative.

**Part-of-speech analysis of retained tokens (Table 2, Appendix H):** The paper analyzes which tokens tend to be kept longest by TOVA by mapping each retained token to its part-of-speech tag using NLTK (Bird et al., 2009). The full results (Table 5 in Appendix H) reveal that possessive endings (POS tag, 1,734 steps average at cache size 1,024), quotation marks (", 1,413 steps), dollar signs ($, 1,276 steps), closing parentheses (), 1,161 steps), periods (., 1,117 steps), and proper nouns (NNPS, 1,042 steps) are retained substantially longer than the average token (897 steps). At the bottom of the table, function words and determiners (DT, 734 steps; TO, 734 steps; MD, 754 steps) are retained for the shortest durations. This content-driven pattern confirms that TOVA is not simply approximating a positional heuristic — it is genuinely making content-based retention decisions, and the tokens kept longest are semantically significant (proper nouns, possessive markers, structural punctuation) while the tokens dropped earliest are syntactically functional but semantically lightweight (determiners, modals, infinitival "to").

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "Transformers can be conceptualized as unbounded multi-state RNNs" and "converted into bounded multi-state RNNs"

This claim is established mathematically in Section 3, not experimentally. The experiments demonstrate the *practical viability* of the conversion — that a transformer with a bounded cache performs nearly as well as one with an unbounded cache — which is a different claim from the conceptual equivalence. The mathematical equivalence (Equations 6–8) is a direct algebraic rewriting of the transformer recurrence and requires no experimental validation. The experiments validate the *utility* of the conceptual reframing by showing that the bounded version works well.

The evidence supporting practical viability is strong but task-conditional. On language modeling, 8× compression yields near-parity (Figure 3). On summarization, 4–8× compression is sufficient (Figure 4). On retrieval QA, only 2× compression is achievable without significant degradation (Figure 5). This gradation is informative rather than concerning — it precisely characterizes the boundary conditions of bounded transformer memory.

A missing experiment would be to test whether models *trained* with bounded attention from the start (i.e., pretrained as bounded MSRNNs) perform differently from pretrained unbounded models that are compressed at inference time. The current results show that pretrained unbounded models *tolerate* compression; they do not show that bounded pretraining would be equally effective.

#### Claim 2: "TOVA outperforms several baseline compression policies" and "achieves results nearly on par with the full model, using in some cases only 1/8 of the original cache size"

This claim is well-supported across all tested tasks and model families. The evidence is:

- Language modeling (Figure 3, Table 3): TOVA outperforms Window, Window+i, and H2O at all cache sizes across three model families. The margin over the best baseline (H2O or Window+4) ranges from approximately 0.5 PPL at small cache sizes (64–128) to approximately 0.1–0.2 PPL at larger sizes (1,024–2,048). The "1/8" figure specifically refers to matching full-model perplexity at 512 tokens (1/8 of 4,096) with a gap of 0.25–0.4 PPL.

- SQuALITY (Figure 4): TOVA outperforms Window+4 by 1.5–2.0 ROUGE points at intermediate cache sizes, and achieves within 1 ROUGE point of the topline at 512–1,024 tokens.

- QASPER (Figure 5): TOVA outperforms Window+4 by 5–9 F1 points at intermediate cache sizes. The "1/8" figure does NOT hold here — TOVA requires 2,048 tokens (1/2 of full) for comparable performance, which the paper honestly reports.

- Text generation (Figure 6): TOVA at 1,024 tokens is competitive with the full model (preferred or tied in 11% of cases, but note the high 88% loss rate).

**A genuine weakness:** The "1/8" headline figure applies to the easiest tasks (language modeling, summarization) but not to retrieval QA. The paper is transparent about this in the body text but the abstract claims "in some cases only 1/8 of the original cache size" — the qualification "in some cases" is doing important work. Readers focusing only on the abstract may overgeneralize.

#### Claim 3: "4.8× higher throughput"

This claim is supported by Table 1 and is a direct consequence of the memory reduction from 2.18 GB (4,096 tokens) to 0.28 GB (512 tokens). The throughput calculation assumes maximum batch size utilization on a single V100 GPU. The 4.8× figure is context-specific (V100, 32 GB, LLaMA-2-7B, bfloat16, 2M total tokens decoded) and would vary with different hardware (A100 with 80 GB would have a different memory ceiling) and different model sizes. The paper is explicit about the measurement conditions in Table 1's caption, which is appropriate.

A nuance not explored: the throughput improvement comes from increased batch size, which is enabled by reduced per-sequence memory. This assumes that there is sufficient demand to fill the larger batches — if the inference workload is latency-bound (single queries, interactive use), the larger batch capacity may not translate to throughput gains. The paper implicitly assumes a throughput-oriented batch inference setting.

#### Claim 4: "TOVA allows running on dramatically longer contexts, up to 70K tokens"

This claim is supported by Figure 7, but with an important caveat: the extrapolation to 70K tokens requires a positional encoding adjustment (the double-logarithmic gap compression) that is independent of TOVA. The claim conflates two mechanisms: TOVA (the cache eviction policy) and the positional encoding adjustment (which enables length extrapolation in general). A model with Window+4 and the same positional encoding adjustment also extrapolates to 70K tokens (Figure 7), just with worse perplexity. So TOVA does not *enable* the extrapolation — the positional encoding adjustment does. TOVA *improves* extrapolation performance relative to baselines.

The paper would be more precise to claim that "TOVA combined with a positional encoding adjustment enables effective processing of sequences up to 70K tokens, outperforming Window+4 at all lengths." The current claim in the abstract ("TOVA allows running on dramatically longer contexts") is slightly misleading in its attribution of causality.

#### Claim 5: "Not all recent tokens are important to retain, and some may be safely dropped" and "the importance of keeping the very first token"

These claims from the analysis (Section 7.3) are well-supported by the visualization and quantitative analysis. Figure 8 shows that only 73–76% of retained tokens are from the recent window, meaning 24–27% are older tokens. Figure 9 shows the first token is retained for the entire sequence while other early tokens are evicted rapidly. Table 2/5 shows content-driven retention patterns (POS tags, proper nouns retained longer; determiners, modals evicted faster). These are observational findings about TOVA's behavior, not causal claims about transformer attention in general — they describe what TOVA keeps, which reflects what the attention mechanism scores highly.

**Missing analysis:** The paper does not examine whether the tokens TOVA retains are the *causally* important ones — that is, whether evicting those tokens would actually hurt performance. This would require counterfactual experiments (e.g., manually removing the first token and measuring the performance drop). The current analysis is correlational: TOVA retains tokens with high attention scores, and TOVA works well. This is suggestive but not proof that the retained tokens are the *right* tokens to keep. It is possible that TOVA works adequately despite occasionally evicting important tokens, because most tokens are redundant in practice (as argued in Innovation 3).

#### Experimental Design Limitations

**Single precision and hardware configuration:** All experiments use bfloat16 on V100 GPUs (Appendix E). The throughput and memory numbers in Table 1 are specific to this configuration. The paper does not report results on A100 or H100 GPUs, which have different memory bandwidth and capacity characteristics and are more representative of current production deployments.

**Limited test set sizes:** PG-19 contains 100 books, but the paper splits them into 4,096-token chunks for evaluation, yielding many test samples. ZeroSCROLLS test sets for SQuALITY and QASPER are not specified by exact size in the paper, but the original ZeroSCROLLS paper reports approximately 1,000–2,000 examples per task. The text generation evaluation uses only 100 stories per model variant, which is a relatively small sample for pairwise preference evaluation — the 5% win rate at 1,024 tokens (Figure 6) is based on 5 out of 100 comparisons, which has wide confidence intervals.

**No evaluation on standard short-range benchmarks:** The paper focuses exclusively on long-range tasks to trigger the compression policies. It does not report performance on standard short-range benchmarks (e.g., MMLU, HellaSwag, GSM8K) to verify that cache compression doesn't degrade performance on tasks where the cache would not fill up anyway. For sequences shorter than the multi-state size, TOVA does nothing (the cache never reaches capacity), so short-range performance should be identical to the full model — but this is not empirically verified.

**Missing baseline: random eviction:** A simple sanity-check baseline is absent: randomly evicting a token at each step (rather than the one with lowest attention). If random eviction performs comparably to TOVA, it would undermine the claim that attention scores are a meaningful importance signal. The Window baseline (always evict the oldest) already serves as a partial test — it shows that positional heuristics are much worse than attention-based heuristics. But a random baseline would directly test whether *any* non-positional heuristic works, or whether attention-based selection specifically matters.

**Missing baseline: learnable or oracle eviction:** The paper claims TOVA is training-free and therefore practical, but does not compare against an upper bound such as "evict the token that would have the largest negative impact on the final answer if removed" (which would require ground-truth labels and is an oracle, not a practical method). Such an oracle would characterize how much room for improvement remains beyond TOVA's attention-based heuristic.

**No inter-policy combination experiments:** The paper compares TOVA against H2O and Window+4 as separate policies, but does not test hybrid policies that combine elements (e.g., TOVA with a small fixed window, or H2O with TOVA's current-attention-based eviction rather than cumulative scores). The design space of eviction policies is large, and the paper's exploration covers only a few points.

**Extrapolation evaluation is thin:** The extrapolation results (Figure 7) compare only TOVA against Window+4, on a single model (LLaMA-2-7B), with a single multi-state size (512), using only perplexity as the metric. No understanding or generation tasks are tested at extrapolated lengths. The 70K-token ceiling is determined by PG-19 book lengths, not by TOVA's inherent limits — it is unclear whether TOVA would degrade gracefully or catastrophically at, say, 200K tokens.

#### Summary of Evidence Strength by Claim

The strongest claims — TOVA outperforms baseline compression policies across multiple tasks and model families, and bounded MSRNNs can match full-model performance with substantially compressed caches on language modeling and summarization — are robustly supported by Figures 3–6 and Table 3, with consistent patterns across three model families and multiple tasks.

The more specific headline claims require qualification: "1/8 of the original cache size" applies to PG-19 and SQuALITY but not QASPER. "4.8× higher throughput" is hardware-specific (V100) and workload-specific (throughput-oriented batch decoding). "70K tokens" extrapolation conflates TOVA with positional encoding adjustment and is only tested against one baseline on one task.

The conceptual claims (transformers are MSRNNs, TOVA makes fewer assumptions than prior policies) are well-argued but not empirically testable in a direct sense — they are frameworks and design philosophies whose value is measured by the practical results they enable.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Problem Is Replaced by a Hard Capacity Constraint — But We Don't Know the Optimal Constraint

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central claim is that transformers can be converted from unbounded to bounded MSRNNs with minimal performance loss, but the entire framework rests on choosing the multi-state size $k$ — the one hyperparameter that controls the memory-performance tradeoff. The paper sweeps $k$ across exponential scales ($2^6$ through $2^{12}$) and shows that performance degrades gracefully for many tasks, but offers no mechanism for *selecting* $k$ given a specific model, task, and performance requirement. There is no difficulty estimator, no budget allocator, and no principled criterion for determining when $k$ is "large enough." Instead, the paper presents performance curves as a function of $k$ and lets the reader observe where the plateau ends.

This is stated explicitly in the paper's approach: TOVA "makes fewer assumptions: it neither fixes a window of recent token-states, nor favors early ones" (Section 5.1). The flip side of making fewer assumptions is that **the user must supply the single most consequential design parameter** — the cache size — with no guidance beyond sweeping and observing.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deploying TOVA faces a cold-start problem: for a new model, a new task, or a new sequence length distribution, they have no way to predict whether $k = 512$ (which works for LLaMA-2 on PG-19) will be sufficient or catastrophic without running their own sweep. The required $k$ varies substantially by task: 512 (1/8 of full) for language modeling (Figure 3), 512–1,024 for summarization (Figure 4), 2,048 (1/2 of full) for retrieval QA (Figure 5), and 1,024 for text generation (Figure 6). This 4× range in the required cache size across tasks means that a single deployment serving multiple task types must either provision for the worst case (losing the efficiency gains on easier tasks) or implement task-specific cache sizes (requiring a routing mechanism the paper does not provide).

Furthermore, the extrapolation experiments (Section 7.2) use a single cache size ($k = 512$) for all sequence lengths from 10K to 70K tokens, but do not explore whether larger caches would be needed for even longer sequences. At 70K tokens, the cache retains only 512 out of 70,000 tokens — 0.7% of the context. The fact that this works for perplexity evaluation on PG-19 is impressive but does not guarantee it would work for tasks requiring precise retrieval over such distances.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The task-dependence of required cache size is visible across Figures 3–5: the point at which TOVA diverges from the topline varies by task. On PG-19 (Figure 3), the divergence begins around $k = 256$–$512$ (the curve starts bending upward). On QASPER (Figure 5), divergence is visible even at $k = 2,048$ for some models. The paper acknowledges this in Section 6.4: "the other two tasks, text generation and retrieval QA, seem to require larger multi-state sizes." No systematic study of how to choose $k$ is presented.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this. It provides performance curves for a range of $k$ values, which is the honest approach for a research paper introducing a new method, but leaves the practical deployment question — "what $k$ should I use?" — unanswered. The authors do not suggest a method for predicting the required cache size from task properties, model properties, or a small calibration set. This is a significant gap between the research contribution and practical deployability.

### The Method Can Only Forget Tokens; It Cannot Recover Them

**The assumption or constraint.** TOVA makes **irreversible** eviction decisions: once a token's key-value pair is removed from the cache, it is gone forever. The model cannot "remember" it later, even if the generation context shifts and that token becomes relevant again. The policy operates under a strict capacity constraint with no mechanism for recovery or retrieval from secondary storage.

This is inherent in the bounded MSRNN formulation: $g(t) = \min(t, k)$ means that at step $t > k$, exactly $k$ tokens are retained. Any token evicted at step $s$ is permanently inaccessible at all steps $s' > s$. The only exception is if the same or similar information is reintroduced through new tokens (e.g., a proper noun mentioned again), but the original cached representation of that token is lost.

**The consequence.** For tasks where the model needs to refer back to multiple distinct pieces of information that are spread across a long context — especially when the relevant tokens are separated by long stretches of text that fill the cache with other tokens — TOVA can permanently lose access to critical information before it is needed. This is the classic RNN long-range dependency problem (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) that the paper explicitly acknowledges the transformer-MS RNN conversion reintroduces.

Concretely: consider a long document that introduces 20 named characters in the first 1,000 tokens, then follows one character's subplot for 3,000 tokens, then returns to another character introduced early on. With a cache size of 512, TOVA must evict tokens during the 3,000-token subplot. If the early mention of the returning character was evicted during the subplot (because attention scores during that subplot focused on different tokens), the model has no way to recover that character's name or attributes when they become relevant again. The generation quality may degrade — the model might invent a new name, contradict earlier descriptions, or produce incoherent narrative.

The paper's text generation results provide indirect evidence for this failure mode: TOVA with 256-token multi-state produces shorter stories (1,361 tokens average vs. 1,566 for the full model; Section 6.3) and loses to the full model in 47% of pairwise comparisons (Figure 6). The paper does not analyze *why* TOVA loses in these cases, but irreversible forgetting of early story elements is a plausible mechanism.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 6.4 contains the key acknowledgment: "the conversion of a transformer into an RNN reintroduces the inherent challenges associated with RNNs, as they encounter difficulties with retrieving distant information." The QASPER results (Figure 5) show the largest gap between TOVA and the topline among all tasks — retrieval QA is precisely the task where irreversible eviction of a single critical detail most hurts performance. Figure 9 provides mechanistic evidence: while the first token is retained indefinitely, other early tokens (positions 2–25) are evicted far more rapidly, with average retention dropping steeply as a function of position.

The paper does not provide a direct experiment demonstrating the irreversibility failure mode (e.g., by constructing a synthetic task where a token evicted at step $s$ is needed at step $s' \gg s$, and showing that TOVA fails while the full model succeeds). This would strengthen the diagnosis.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate irreversible forgetting. It does not explore hybrid approaches where evicted tokens are stored in a compressed form (e.g., a secondary, smaller cache, or a learned summary vector) that could be consulted later. The bounded MSRNN formalism, as implemented, is a pure fixed-capacity cache with hard eviction. Future work on combining TOVA with some form of secondary storage or retrieval augmentation is implicitly suggested by the limitation but not explicitly proposed.

### The Extrapolation Result Depends on a Positional Encoding Adjustment That Is Not Evaluated Independently

**The assumption or constraint.** The extrapolation to 70K tokens (Section 7.2, Figure 7) requires two mechanisms working together: (1) TOVA for cache compression, and (2) a **positional encoding adjustment** that compresses large positional gaps using $g_{\text{compressed}} = \ln(\ln(g))$ for gaps greater than 10. The paper evaluates these mechanisms jointly — TOVA + positional adjustment vs. Window+4 + positional adjustment — but never evaluates the positional adjustment independently or ablates its contribution to the overall result.

This is a confound: the 70K-token extrapolation might be primarily enabled by the positional encoding adjustment, with TOVA providing a secondary improvement over baselines. The paper's abstract claim that "TOVA allows running on dramatically longer contexts, up to 70K tokens" could be misinterpreted as TOVA being the enabling mechanism, when in fact the positional adjustment is the necessary condition for any extrapolation, and TOVA only improves performance within that regime.

**The consequence.** A practitioner who reads the abstract and skips Section 7.2 might conclude that TOVA alone enables 70K-token contexts, which is false. The positional encoding adjustment is an independent component with its own design choices (the $\ln(\ln(g))$ function, the threshold of 10) and its own failure modes. If the positional encoding adjustment fails for a particular model or task, TOVA provides no additional extrapolation capability. Moreover, the specific adjustment function was selected based on "preliminary experiments" (Section 7.2) that are not reported in detail — other functions (single $\ln$, square root) showed "inferior results," but the magnitude of the inferiority and the experimental conditions are not provided.

Additionally, the extrapolation evaluation is thin: it compares only TOVA against Window+4, only on LLaMA-2-7B, only at $k = 512$, and only on perplexity. The behavior of TOVA at, say, 70K tokens on QASPER-style retrieval or story generation — where irreversible forgetting and the compressed positional encoding might interact in complex ways — is completely unknown.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 7 shows that TOVA outperforms Window+4 at all lengths from 10K to 70K, but both policies use the same positional encoding adjustment. There is no curve for either policy *without* the positional adjustment, so the independent contribution of each component cannot be estimated. The paper states in Section 7.2: "As models struggle to extrapolate to such long contexts, we only compare TOVA with Window+4, which has been shown to support such contexts" — but the justification for only comparing two policies is the difficulty of the setting, not a principled experimental design choice. The missing ablations (no positional adjustment, different gap compression functions, different cache sizes at extrapolated lengths) limit the strength of the extrapolation claim.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address the confound. The positional encoding adjustment is described as a necessary technical detail for making bounded MSRNNs work beyond the training length, but its contribution is not isolated. The abstract attributes the extrapolation capability to TOVA, which is an overstatement relative to the evidence presented.

### The Method Is Evaluated on a Single Modality (English Text) and a Narrow Set of Model Sizes (~7B Parameters)

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use English-language text datasets (PG-19 books, SQuALITY stories, QASPER scientific papers) and models in the ~7B parameter range (LLaMA-2-7B, Mistral-7B, Yi-6B, with the exception of MythoLogic-13B for text generation). The paper does not evaluate TOVA on code generation, multilingual text, mathematical reasoning, multi-turn dialogue, or any modality beyond natural language. It does not evaluate on models significantly larger than 7B parameters (the largest tested is 13B).

The paper acknowledges this scope limitation in the "Limitations" section: "our evaluation framework focuses on English tasks. It is not unlikely that languages with more flexible word order will make different use of the attention mechanism, and thus might require a larger multi-state size." This is the only explicit acknowledgment of the language limitation; the model size limitation is not discussed.

**The consequence.** The bounded MSRNN behavior demonstrated in the paper might not generalize to all settings where KV cache compression would be valuable:

- **Larger models:** Models in the 70B–175B parameter range (e.g., LLaMA-2-70B, GPT-3) have more attention heads and more layers, potentially developing more complex attention patterns. The attention-based eviction that works well for 7B models might be too aggressive or too conservative for larger models, because the relationship between attention score magnitude and token importance could change with scale. The paper's finding that layer-wise averaging outperforms head-wise eviction (Appendix A, Table 3) might not hold at larger scales where individual heads have more specialized functions.

- **Code generation:** Code has highly structured long-range dependencies (function definitions referenced hundreds of lines later, import statements at the top of files). Evicting a function signature or import statement from the cache could make subsequent code impossible to generate correctly. The required cache size for code might be substantially larger than for natural language.

- **Languages with flexible word order:** The paper's own caveat is important: languages where syntactic dependencies span longer distances (e.g., German with separable prefix verbs, Japanese with sentence-final predicates) might require retaining different types of tokens for longer. The part-of-speech analysis (Table 2) showing that determiners and modals are evicted quickly might not transfer to languages where these categories carry more information.

- **Multi-turn dialogue:** Dialogue involves repeated references to entities introduced many turns ago, with topic shifts and returns. This is precisely the scenario where irreversible forgetting (Limitation 2) could be most damaging.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no evidence for generalization beyond English text and the 7B–13B size range. The three tested model families (LLaMA-2, Mistral, Yi) show consistent behavior (Figures 3–5), which provides some evidence of robustness across pretraining recipes, but they are all in the same size class. The consistency across models is encouraging but insufficient to establish generalization to substantially different scales or modalities.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the language limitation explicitly in the Limitations section, but does not acknowledge the model size limitation. Both are presented as scope constraints rather than as gaps to be addressed by future work. The paper does not suggest experiments or analyses that would test generalization, nor does it provide any theoretical argument for why the findings should transfer to larger models or different modalities. Given the practical importance of KV cache compression for large-scale LLM deployment, the lack of evaluation on models larger than 13B is a significant omission.

### The Cache Compression Is Applied Uniformly; There Is No Mechanism for Adaptive Reallocation Across Layers or Sequence Positions

**The assumption or constraint.** TOVA applies the same policy (evict the token with the lowest current attention score) uniformly across all transformer layers, with the same multi-state size $k$ for every layer. This is a flat, non-hierarchical allocation of the total memory budget: if the total cache budget is $k \times L$ slots (where $L$ is the number of layers), each layer gets exactly $k$ slots, regardless of whether some layers need more memory than others.

The paper's own analysis suggests this is suboptimal. The layer-wise visualizations in Appendix G (Figures 12–13) show that different layers exhibit qualitatively different attention patterns. Lower layers tend to attend to recent, local context (the token retention pattern in Figures 12–13 looks more "window-like" for early layers), while higher layers attend to longer-range dependencies and retain a more diverse set of tokens. Despite this heterogeneity, every layer is constrained to the same fixed capacity.

**The consequence.** Under a fixed total memory budget, the flat allocation wastes capacity on layers that don't need it while starving layers that do. For example, a lower layer that mostly attends to the previous 50 tokens has no use for a 512-slot cache — 50 slots would suffice, and the remaining 462 are wasted. Meanwhile, a higher layer that needs to attend to 1,000+ tokens across the document is forced into 512 slots, potentially evicting important information. A smarter allocation that gives lower layers 64 slots and higher layers 960 slots — keeping the total constant — would likely achieve better overall performance.

The paper does explore one dimension of layer-wise variation: TOVA makes eviction decisions independently per layer. But the capacity $k$ is fixed and identical across all layers. The flat allocation is particularly problematic for retrieval tasks (QASPER), where higher layers likely need to retain many widely scattered tokens to answer fact-seeking questions, while lower layers may only need local context for syntactic processing.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The layer-wise visualizations in Appendix G (Figures 12–13) show that retention patterns vary systematically across layers. Layers 0–5 show dense retention of recent tokens with sparse retention of older ones. Middle layers (10–20) show more distributed retention. Higher layers (25–31) show even more diverse patterns. Figure 8 (final layer only) is explicitly noted as showing the pattern for the final layer alone — the paper presents it as a representative example, but the variation across layers is visible in the appendix figures.

The paper does not report any experiment with variable cache sizes across layers, nor does it report per-layer performance metrics that would quantify how much each layer suffers from compression. The uniform allocation is an implicit assumption of the entire experimental design, not a justified design choice.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the possibility of heterogeneous cache sizes across layers, nor does it report any ablation or analysis that would justify uniform allocation. This is a missed opportunity, given that the MSRNN formalism naturally supports layer-specific capacity functions $g_l(t)$, and that the per-layer visualization data was collected. Future work on heterogeneous cache allocation — or even dynamic reallocation during generation — is a natural extension that the paper does not explicitly suggest.

### The Method Evaluates "Performance" Using Metrics That May Mask Degradation in Unmeasured Dimensions

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates TOVA using standard automatic metrics: perplexity for language modeling, ROUGE for summarization, F1 for QA, and GPT-4 pairwise preference for story generation. These metrics capture specific aspects of output quality (token-level prediction accuracy, n-gram overlap with a reference, exact-match answer correctness, relative preference between two outputs) but are known to be incomplete proxies for the full spectrum of model capabilities, particularly for open-ended generation.

The text generation evaluation (Section 6.3) is the most direct attempt to measure qualitative degradation, but it is acknowledged as limited: "Evaluating models on long text generation is computationally expensive and might limit others from reproducing our results. Further, the evaluation of such task is extremely complicated, even for humans. We therefore resort to GPT-4 to compare the output... We recognize that this is far from perfect, and will most likely not catch the full breadth of evaluating text quality" (Limitations section).

**The consequence.** The headline results — TOVA matches the full model within 0.4 PPL on PG-19, within 1 ROUGE point on SQuALITY, wins or ties in 11–53% of story comparisons — may give an overly optimistic impression of the quality preservation. Several dimensions of degradation are plausible but not measured:

- **Factual consistency:** In story generation, TOVA with 256-token cache might produce internally inconsistent narratives (characters changing names, locations shifting without explanation) that a GPT-4 pairwise evaluator fails to catch or weighs less heavily than other qualities.
- **Logical coherence:** The QASPER F1 metric measures whether the final answer contains the correct tokens, but not whether the reasoning path that led to that answer was valid. A compressed model might produce the right answer for the wrong reasons more often than the full model.
- **Calibration and confidence:** The paper does not measure whether TOVA-compressed models become overconfident in incorrect answers (a known failure mode for models operating with degraded context).
- **Diversity and repetition:** The observation that TOVA stories are shorter (1,566 → 1,361 tokens at $k=256$) suggests a systematic effect on output length. The paper does not measure repetition rate, topic diversity, or lexical diversity.

The story generation results (Figure 6) are particularly revealing when read carefully: even at $k=1,024$ (1/4 of full context), TOVA loses to the full model in 88% of comparisons. The 5% win rate and 6% tie rate mean that in 94% of comparisons, GPT-4 either prefers the full model or finds them equal — but only 6% are equal. In the remaining 88%, the full model is strictly preferred. This is a substantial qualitative gap that the "nearly on par" framing in the abstract understates.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The story length data (Section 6.3) provides direct evidence of a measurable effect: average length drops from 1,566 to 1,503 ($k=512$) to 1,361 ($k=256$). The GPT-4 evaluation (Figure 6) shows the preference gap. The paper does not report any analysis of *why* GPT-4 prefers the full model's stories — whether due to coherence, creativity, factual consistency, or other factors. For the other tasks, the standard metrics are reported without supplementary analysis of error types or failure modes specific to compressed models.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through honest acknowledgment in the Limitations section, but not through additional experiments or analyses. The paper recognizes the evaluation limitation for text generation specifically, but does not discuss the broader issue of whether standard automatic metrics adequately capture the degradation caused by cache compression. The disconnect between the strong automatic metric results on PG-19/SQuALITY and the 88% loss rate on story generation at 1,024 tokens suggests the metrics are measuring different things, and the paper does not reconcile this. A pairwise evaluation for summarization or QA (e.g., having humans or GPT-4 compare TOVA outputs to full model outputs on SQuALITY or QASPER) would help characterize whether the ROUGE/F1 parity reflects genuine quality preservation or metric insensitivity.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

**This work introduces a conceptual reframing rather than a paradigm shift — but it is a reframing with immediate, practical consequences.** The paper's central move is to redescribe the decoder transformer architecture in terms that make the KV cache compression problem natural and the design space for compression policies transparent. Prior to this work, KV cache compression was typically approached as an engineering optimization problem: how do we reduce memory usage while minimizing performance degradation? The dominant methods (Window, Window+i, H2O) were developed under this framing, each introducing heuristics about what "should" be kept (recent tokens, early tokens, tokens with high cumulative attention). The MSRNN formalism changes the question from "how do we compress the cache?" to "how do we convert an unbounded RNN into a bounded one?" — which is a well-defined operation on a known mathematical object. This recharacterization matters because it provides a **unifying framework** that reveals Window, Window+i, H2O, and TOVA as different answers to the same fundamental question (which eviction rule to apply), rather than as unrelated heuristics. The value of this unification is not merely taxonomic — it makes the design space transparent, enabling systematic comparison and suggesting natural extensions (e.g., layer-specific capacity, dynamic reallocation) that would not be obvious if each policy were treated as a distinct approach.

**The more impactful contribution is the empirical finding that TOVA — a policy that makes fewer assumptions than any prior approach — outperforms all of them.** This is not just "our method is better." It establishes a **design principle** that should influence future work on attention-based memory management: *the model's own current attention scores are a sufficient importance signal for cache eviction, and structural priors (fixed windows, protected early tokens, temporal aggregation) generally hurt rather than help because they override the attention mechanism's trained relevance judgments.* The evidence for this principle is the consistent pattern in Table 3 and Figures 3–5: the more structural assumptions a policy makes, the worse it performs. Window (strongest structural prior: recency is all that matters) catastrophically fails. Window+4 (relaxes the prior to protect the first 4 tokens) improves dramatically but still underperforms. H2O (adds content awareness via cumulative attention, but retains a fixed window and temporal aggregation) improves further. TOVA (no structural prior, no temporal aggregation, purely current-attention-driven) is best. This progression is a strong empirical case for minimizing assumptions and trusting the attention mechanism.

**The work resolves a latent contradiction in the transformer-RNN relationship.** Before this paper, the dominant narrative held that transformers and RNNs are fundamentally different architectures — transformers access all tokens directly, RNNs compress history into a state. Several works (Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Peng et al., 2022; Hutchins et al., 2022) had noted connections between the two but always required architectural modification or retraining to realize them. This created a peculiar situation: the connection was recognized as theoretically interesting but practically irrelevant for existing models. TOVA resolves this by showing that the connection is not just theoretical — it is *operational*. Any pretrained decoder transformer *already functions as an RNN*; you just need to bound its state to see it. The practical success of TOVA (matching full-model performance with 1/8 of the cache on multiple tasks) validates the MSRNN framing in the strongest possible way: it leads to a simple, effective method that improves on the state of the art. This makes the transformer-RNN connection a first-class design principle rather than a theoretical curiosity.

**The diagnostic finding — that transformers routinely act as bounded-memory systems — shifts how we should think about transformer capacity.** The field has implicitly assumed that the full KV cache is necessary for transformer performance because the model was trained with access to all tokens. The plateau behavior in Figures 3–5 challenges this assumption directly: on language modeling and summarization, 7/8 of the cached tokens are functionally redundant. This is not obvious a priori. The fact that a simple attention-based eviction policy can select which 1/8 to keep with minimal performance loss suggests that transformers learn to operate with an effective memory far smaller than their theoretical capacity, and that the attention mechanism already performs implicit importance-weighted compression during the forward pass. TOVA simply makes this implicit compression explicit and enforces it as a hard capacity constraint. This finding should influence how we design future transformer models: if most of the cache is redundant at inference time, perhaps it was redundant at training time as well, and models could be trained with bounded attention from the start — reducing training cost and potentially improving generalization by forcing the model to learn more efficient context representations.

**The work also redirects attention in the KV cache compression literature.** Prior to this paper, there was genuine uncertainty about what kind of eviction policy would work best. H2O's cumulative attention scoring was a significant advance over purely positional methods, and it was reasonable to hypothesize that more sophisticated importance estimation (learned pruning, clustering, quantization) would yield further gains. TOVA demonstrates that the simplest possible content-driven policy — argmin of current attention — already outperforms more complex approaches. This suggests that future effort should be directed not at building more elaborate importance estimators, but at understanding *why* the current attention signal is sufficient, and whether improvements in attention mechanism design (e.g., training objectives that produce more calibrated attention scores) would translate to better compression. It also suggests that the remaining performance gap between TOVA and the full model may require fundamentally different approaches — perhaps hierarchical cache architectures that preserve some information about evicted tokens, or training procedures that explicitly optimize for bounded-memory performance.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Training transformers as bounded MSRNNs from scratch.** The paper demonstrates that pretrained unbounded transformers *tolerate* being converted to bounded MSRNNs at inference time. A natural next step is to train transformers with bounded attention from the start — that is, with a fixed-size KV cache applied during pretraining, using TOVA or a similar eviction policy. If bounded pretraining achieves comparable performance to unbounded pretraining (on standard benchmarks, not just the long-range tasks tested here), it would dramatically reduce pretraining memory costs and potentially produce models that are more robust to compressed inference by design. A strong experiment would replicate the pretraining of a 7B-scale model (e.g., following the LLaMA recipe) with bounded attention at sizes of 512, 1024, and 2048 slots, and compare downstream performance against the unbounded baseline on both short-range and long-range tasks. The key question is whether bounded pretraining hurts the model's ability to learn long-range dependencies during training, or whether — as the inference-time results suggest — most of the attention during pretraining is already sparse and the capacity reduction has minimal impact. The paper's own results provide a lower bound: inference-time compression of 8× works well for language modeling, suggesting that pretraining with similar compression might be viable.

**Hierarchical cache architectures with secondary storage.** The paper identifies irreversible forgetting as the primary failure mode for bounded MSRNNs (Section 6.4), and the QASPER results (Figure 5) demonstrate that retrieval-intensive tasks are most affected. A natural extension is a two-level cache: a small primary cache managed by TOVA (e.g., 512 slots) for tokens with high current attention, and a larger secondary cache (e.g., 4,096 slots) that stores compressed representations of evicted tokens — perhaps by pooling key-value vectors across evicted token groups, or by training a lightweight encoder to summarize evicted segments. When the model's attention pattern shifts and previously-evicted information becomes relevant, the secondary cache could be searched or the compressed representation could be expanded back into the primary cache. A strong experiment would test this on QASPER: does a 512-slot primary cache augmented with a 3,584-slot compressed secondary cache recover the performance of the full 4,096-slot model? The key metric is whether the hierarchical approach closes the gap between TOVA and the topline on retrieval tasks while maintaining the memory savings on tasks where 512 slots suffice.

**Layer-adaptive cache sizing.** The paper's layer-wise visualizations (Appendix G, Figures 12–13) show that lower layers attend more locally (dense retention of recent tokens) while higher layers attend more broadly (distributed retention across the full sequence). Despite this, all experiments use a uniform cache size $k$ across all layers. A natural optimization is to allocate cache capacity proportionally to each layer's attention sparsity: lower layers get small caches (e.g., 64–128 slots), higher layers get large caches (e.g., 1,024–2,048 slots), with the total memory budget held constant. A strong experiment would measure per-layer "attention entropy" or "effective cache utilization" (the number of unique tokens that receive >1% attention weight) under the full model, then allocate cache sizes inversely proportional to sparsity, and compare performance against uniform allocation at the same total memory budget. The hypothesis is that heterogeneous allocation recovers more of the full model's performance, particularly on retrieval tasks where higher layers need access to many scattered tokens. The paper's per-layer retention visualizations provide the necessary data to estimate optimal per-layer capacities without additional experiments.

**Targeted stress-tests of the irreversibility failure mode.** The paper observes that bounded MSRNNs struggle with retrieval (QASPER results, Section 6.4) and attributes this to the reintroduction of RNN-like difficulties with distant information. But there is no controlled experiment isolating this failure mode. A strong follow-up would construct synthetic sequences where a critical piece of information is introduced at position $p_1$, followed by a long distractor segment of length $L$, followed by a query that requires retrieving the information from $p_1$. By varying $p_1$, $L$, and the cache size $k$, one could precisely characterize the conditions under which TOVA irrevocably loses access to critical information. The experiment would measure: (1) at what distance $L$ does TOVA with cache size $k$ drop below 90% retrieval accuracy? (2) does the failure occur because the token is evicted (which can be verified by instrumenting the cache) or because the positional encoding adjustment for large gaps degrades the model's ability to use distant tokens even when they are retained? This would distinguish between two mechanisms (cache eviction vs. positional encoding degradation) that are confounded in the current results. The outcome would inform whether future work should focus on better eviction policies, secondary storage, or positional encoding schemes for long-range retrieval.

**Cross-modal and cross-lingual evaluation of TOVA.** The paper is scoped to English text with 7B-scale models, and the authors acknowledge that "languages with more flexible word order will make different use of the attention mechanism, and thus might require a larger multi-state size" (Limitations). This is an empirically testable hypothesis. A strong follow-up would replicate the PG-19 perplexity experiment across 5–10 typologically diverse languages (e.g., German, Japanese, Arabic, Finnish, Turkish) using multilingual models like BLOOM or Aya, measuring the compression ratio (the smallest $k$ that achieves within 0.5 PPL of the full model) for each language. If the paper's hypothesis is correct, languages with longer syntactic dependency distances should require larger cache fractions. The same experiment could be extended to code generation (using HumanEval or MBPP with long-context prompts), where structured long-range dependencies (function definitions, imports) might require substantially larger caches than natural language. Results from these experiments would determine whether TOVA's compression ratio is task-dependent (as already shown) or fundamentally language- and modality-dependent, which has direct implications for deployment in multilingual or code-generation settings.

**Combining TOVA with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).** The irreversible forgetting limitation suggests a natural synergy: rather than trying to retain all important tokens in the KV cache, offload the retrieval problem to an external retrieval system. In a RAG setup, the model receives retrieved documents concatenated to its input. TOVA would compress the KV cache during generation, potentially evicting parts of retrieved documents that are not relevant to the current generation step. But if later generation steps require information from evicted document segments, the model could re-issue a retrieval query rather than relying on the cache. A strong experiment would compare TOVA-only, RAG-only, and TOVA+RAG on a long-form QA task (e.g., a variant of QASPER where the full paper is provided as retrieved context) to test whether TOVA and RAG are complementary. The hypothesis is that TOVA handles within-document attention compression while RAG handles across-document retrieval, and the combination achieves better accuracy-memory tradeoffs than either alone.

**Pretraining with attention-based eviction to improve attention calibration.** The paper finds that TOVA works because attention scores reflect token importance — but this is an empirical property of pretrained transformers, not a guarantee. If attention scores are miscalibrated (e.g., a token receives high attention but is not actually important for downstream task performance), TOVA would retain the wrong tokens and performance would degrade. An interesting follow-up would train transformers with an auxiliary loss that explicitly optimizes attention scores to be good predictors of token importance for eviction — for example, by training with a bounded cache during pretraining and adding a loss term that penalizes evicting tokens that later turn out to be needed (measured by whether future queries attend strongly to them). This would produce models whose attention scores are more directly useful for TOVA-style compression, potentially enabling more aggressive compression ratios. The experiment would compare a standard-pretrained model with TOVA against an eviction-aware-pretrained model with TOVA, measuring the compression ratio achievable at equivalent perplexity. Negative results (eviction-aware pretraining doesn't help) would be equally informative, suggesting that standard pretraining already produces well-calibrated attention scores for this purpose.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Batch inference for cost-sensitive LLM deployments.** The most directly actionable application of TOVA is in throughput-optimized batch inference settings. Organizations running large-scale LLM inference — for example, generating summaries for thousands of documents, scoring candidate answers, or producing synthetic training data — are typically bottlenecked by GPU memory, which limits batch size. TOVA with 512-slot caches reduces KV cache memory by 8× (Table 1: 2.18 GB → 0.28 GB per sequence at 4,096 tokens), enabling proportionally larger batch sizes. On a V100 GPU, the maximum batch size increases from 8 to 139, yielding 4.8× higher throughput. For a deployment processing 10 million sequences of length 4,096, the difference between 8-sequence batches (1.25M forward passes) and 139-sequence batches (~72K forward passes) translates directly to GPU-hours and cost. The 4.8× throughput figure is hardware-specific (V100, 32 GB), but the scaling relationship — batch size scales inversely with per-sequence cache size — holds across GPU configurations. The key practical caveat is that TOVA requires no model modification, fine-tuning, or calibration, making it immediately deployable on any existing transformer-based inference pipeline with a single hyperparameter (the cache size $k$) that can be selected based on the task's tolerance for compression.

**Enabling long-context processing on consumer hardware.** The extrapolation results (Figure 7) demonstrate that TOVA combined with a simple positional encoding adjustment (the double-logarithmic gap compression) enables processing contexts up to 70K tokens — and potentially longer — on models trained with only 4K-token contexts. This is practically significant for users with consumer-grade GPUs (e.g., RTX 3090 with 24 GB) who currently cannot run long-context inference because the KV cache exceeds available memory. With TOVA at 512 slots per layer, a 7B model's cache memory is reduced from multiple gigabytes to hundreds of megabytes (Table 1: 0.28 GB at 512 tokens), making 70K-token processing feasible on a single consumer GPU. The throughput would be low (batch size 1, no parallelism), but for interactive applications — analyzing a long document, summarizing a book chapter, processing a multi-turn conversation history — the ability to process the full context at all outweighs throughput concerns. The caveat is that the extrapolation has been tested only for perplexity on narrative text (PG-19); performance on structured tasks (QA, summarization) at 70K tokens is unverified and may require larger cache sizes or further positional encoding refinements.

**Dynamic quality-cost tradeoffs in LLM serving infrastructure.** Modern LLM serving systems (e.g., vLLM, TGI) could integrate TOVA as a per-request cache compression policy, where the multi-state size $k$ is selected dynamically based on the estimated complexity of the request or the user's latency/cost requirements. For simple requests (e.g., "summarize this paragraph"), a small $k$ (128–256) could be used, reducing memory and increasing throughput. For complex requests (e.g., "answer detailed questions about this 15-page contract"), a larger $k$ (1,024–2,048) could be allocated. The paper provides the performance curves (Figures 3–5) needed to make these tradeoffs: on PG-19, TOVA with 256 tokens achieves 7.71 PPL vs. 7.16 for the full model — a 0.55 PPL degradation for a 16× memory reduction. A serving system could expose a "quality" slider that maps to cache size, allowing users to trade accuracy for speed and cost. The implementation would be straightforward: TOVA is training-free and requires only an attention-score argmin and cache concatenation operation (Algorithm 1, Appendix B), adding negligible compute overhead to each forward pass. The challenge is request routing: predicting the appropriate $k$ for a given request without running it first, which would require a lightweight difficulty estimator or a default-to-small-then-expand strategy not explored in the paper.

**Mitigating the memory bottleneck in multi-turn dialogue and agent systems.** Conversational AI systems and LLM-based agents accumulate long interaction histories over multiple turns (user messages, model responses, tool outputs, retrieved documents). The KV cache grows linearly with the total conversation length, quickly exceeding GPU memory in long-running sessions. TOVA offers a principled way to bound the cache while retaining the most conversationally relevant history. The paper's finding that the first token and semantically significant tokens (proper nouns, possessive markers, structural punctuation; Table 2) are preferentially retained suggests that TOVA would naturally preserve key entities, names, and discourse markers from earlier in the conversation while discarding redundant or less informative turns. A deployment could set $k$ based on the expected conversation length and available GPU memory, confident that TOVA's attention-based selection will retain the most relevant context without requiring conversation-specific tuning. The risk — irreversible loss of information from early turns that becomes relevant later — is precisely the retrieval failure mode that the paper identifies (Section 6.4), and would need to be monitored or mitigated through explicit memory mechanisms (e.g., the model generating explicit summaries of key information before it is evicted, or a secondary storage system as suggested in the follow-up research section).

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions TOVA explicitly against other training-free compression policies (Window, Window+i, H2O) and implicitly against the full (unbounded) transformer. The tradeoffs are clear enough to articulate a decision rule:

- **Prefer TOVA over other training-free compression policies (Window, Window+i, H2O) when:** You can afford the negligible additional compute of an argmin over attention scores at each decoding step (TOVA) compared to the simpler but less performant position-based heuristics (Window, Window+i). The evidence is Table 3 and Figures 3–5: TOVA consistently outperforms all baselines across all cache sizes, models, and tasks tested, with the largest margins at intermediate compression ratios (e.g., +1.5–2.0 ROUGE points on SQuALITY at 512 tokens, +5–9 F1 points on QASPER at 1,024 tokens). The only reason to prefer a baseline would be implementation simplicity in a context where the attention scores are not easily accessible (e.g., a heavily optimized inference framework that doesn't expose per-step attention weights).

- **Prefer TOVA over the full (unbounded) model when:** You are operating under a GPU memory constraint that limits batch size, throughput, or maximum sequence length, and your task tolerates the compression ratio you plan to use. Use the paper's task-specific guidance: 1/8 of full context (512 tokens for a 4K-trained model) is safe for language modeling and summarization (PG-19: 0.4 PPL gap; SQuALITY: under 1 ROUGE point); 1/2 of full context (2,048 tokens) for retrieval QA (QASPER: under 1 F1 point); 1/4 of full context (1,024 tokens) for text generation (Figure 6: competitive in 11% of cases, though with a high 88% loss rate relative to the full model, so user-facing generation at this compression ratio may be risky). The throughput improvement is directly proportional to the cache compression ratio up to the GPU's maximum batch capacity (Table 1: 4.8× at 512 tokens on V100).

- **Prefer the full model over TOVA when:** You have sufficient GPU memory for your target batch size and sequence length, and you cannot tolerate any degradation. This is most relevant for retrieval-intensive tasks (QASPER: even at 2,048 tokens, TOVA is 0.5–1 F1 point below the topline for some models; Figure 5), for very long-context retrieval beyond what has been tested with TOVA, or for user-facing applications where the 88% full-model preference in story generation (Figure 6) is unacceptable. Additionally, if you are evaluating on a task or language not tested in the paper, the safe default is the full model until you have conducted your own TOVA sweep — the paper provides no out-of-distribution generalization guarantees.

- **Do not use purely positional policies (Window, Window+i) as a strong baseline for future work without including TOVA.** The paper demonstrates that Window catastrophically fails (4,812 PPL at 64 tokens; Table 3) and that Window+4, while much better, is consistently outperformed by TOVA. Future work proposing new compression policies should compare against TOVA as the state-of-the-art training-free baseline, not against Window+i, which is now obsolete as a competitive method.
