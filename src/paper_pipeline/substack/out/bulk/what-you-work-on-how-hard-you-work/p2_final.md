Here are the takeaways from this post on accelerated career growth (a friend reached Staff / IC6 at Google by 28 while working less than 40 hours a week):

**The central insight: selectivity over grind**

The entire framework rests on one compressed claim: a friend got to Staff at Google by 28 while averaging under 40 hours a week, and the secret wasn’t grinding harder but being ruthless about *what* he worked on. "We all have the same amount of time. You can only get so much done if you grind with all your waking hours. So, the only way to have Staff+ impact is to be picky about what you work on." You can't out-grind the clock—the real multiplier is what you choose to say yes to.

**Skill one: understanding what results the org cares about most**

The first skill is learning to assess the impact of every task before committing. The friend turned down work he knew "wouldn’t be impactful enough," so each project he did take on kept the promotion door open even though his plate wasn’t overflowing. Ryan learned this lesson as a mid-level (IC4) engineer: he stopped blindly accepting work, and after enough conversations with his manager about senior (IC5) expectations, he knew that small features wouldn't be enough for promotion. The way to build this understanding is to discuss potential projects with a manager or tech leads, and to reference leveling guides (he links his own writing on IC3→IC4, IC4→IC5, and IC5→IC6 expectations at FAANG) to calibrate what "impactful" actually means at each rung.

**Skill two: saying “no” without causing thrash**

Simply dropping work isn't an option — "otherwise, you'll cause people problems." The **best way to say no is to overcommunicate**: explain that you have something more urgent or impactful on your plate. A good manager will be happy you're prioritizing bigger impact over a small task. But he's careful to say you can't say no to everything that doesn't directly help your career: "you’d be a terrible team player, which will bite you long term." You evaluate case-by-case, protecting time strategically while maintaining the relationships that make long-term career growth possible.

**The extreme case — a Distinguished engineer (IC9) who negotiated his way out of oncall**

A mentor once told him about a Distinguished engineer (IC9) at Google who was "so selfish with his time that he was the only person in the org to negotiate his way out of oncall and didn't answer most people who asked him questions." That left a bad taste in the mentor's mouth. Ryan frames the takeaway as a tradeoff, not a judgment: the IC9 wasn't necessarily wrong—he achieved the highest individual-contributor level—but the story makes visible the relational cost extreme time-protection can exact. His own stance: "evaluate case-by-case but still lean toward protecting your time."

**The closing thesis**

"If you don’t protect your time, you can’t get promoted no matter how much you work." Working as hard as you can isn't the strongest lever for impact by itself — selectivity is. Doing both — choosing high-impact work *and* protecting time to execute — is the multiplier that accelerates a career.

---

The **best way to say no is to overcommunicate** playbook and the IC9 cautionary tale form two sides of the same coin: time-protection is essential, but executed without social awareness it burns the relationships that Staff+ roles require. His framework doesn't pretend the tradeoff disappears at higher levels—it just insists that **selectivity** is the non-negotiable foundation, and that the soft skill of saying no without alienating people is what separates sustainable acceleration from a short-lived sprint.
