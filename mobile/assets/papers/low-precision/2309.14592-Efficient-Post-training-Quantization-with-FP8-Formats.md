# Efficient Post-training Quantization with FP8 Formats

**ArXiv:** [2309.14592](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14592)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper studies the advantages of FP8 floating-point formats for post-training quantization across 75 unique network architectures and 200+ tasks spanning NLP, computer vision, speech, and recommendation.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies the advantages of FP8 floating-point formats for post-training quantization across 75 unique network architectures and 200+ tasks spanning NLP, computer vision, speech, and recommendation. The authors examine three FP8 representations—E5M2, E4M3, and E3M4—each trading off dynamic range against precision, and develop a unified **quantization workflow** combining a standard quantization scheme (per-channel weight scaling, per-tensor activation scaling, applied to Convolution/Linear/Embedding ops) with an extended quantization scheme (expanded operator coverage including LayerNorm and BatchNorm, mixed FP8 formats, and dynamic quantization) tuned via an accuracy-driven automatic feedback loop. FP8 formats achieve 92.64% workload pass rate (within 1% relative accuracy loss vs. FP32) compared to INT8's 65.87%, with E4M3 reaching 96.32% coverage on NLP models while E3M4 performs marginally better on computer vision tasks (78.95% vs. 73.68%), establishing that FP8 formats are a broadly superior post-training quantization alternative whose format choice should be domain-dependent.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: INT8 Quantization Fails on Modern Workloads

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is that **INT8 quantization—the industry-standard approach for efficient neural network inference—is inadequate for a large and growing fraction of modern deep learning architectures**, particularly large language models (LLMs) and transformer-based vision models. The authors report that INT8 post-training quantization achieves only a 65.87% workload pass rate, meaning roughly one-third of models fail to stay within 1% relative accuracy loss of their FP32 baselines when quantized to INT8. For production deployment at datacenter scale and on edge devices, this is a serious practical bottleneck: models that cannot be quantized must run at higher precision (FP16 or FP32), consuming more memory, bandwidth, and energy, which directly translates to higher operational costs, lower throughput, and inability to deploy on resource-constrained hardware.

This gap matters because the trend in deep learning is toward architectures that are *increasingly hostile to INT8 quantization*. As the paper notes in Section 1, INT8 has been "particularly effective for computer vision tasks such as object detection and image classification" and has seen "widely deployed in production." However, the arrival of transformer-based LLMs and diffusion models has shifted the landscape. These models exhibit activation distributions with extreme outliers—values that are orders of magnitude larger than the bulk of the distribution—particularly in layers following LayerNorm operations. INT8's uniform quantization grid, which allocates equal spacing between all representable values, is fundamentally mismatched to these long-tailed distributions: the outlier values stretch the quantization step size, forcing the majority of (small, precise) values to be coarsely quantized, which degrades model accuracy.

### Why This Matters: The Production Deployment Bottleneck

The practical stakes are high and multidimensional:

- **Datacenter inference cost.** Large language models like LLaMA-65B and Bloom-176B are expensive to serve. Quantization to 8-bit halves memory bandwidth requirements and enables higher batch sizes, directly reducing cost-per-query. If INT8 quantization causes unacceptable accuracy degradation on a language modeling task, the model must run at FP16, roughly doubling the required memory and compute. For organizations serving millions of inference requests daily, this doubles infrastructure cost.

- **Edge and on-device deployment.** Resource-constrained devices (phones, IoT, embedded systems) have strict memory and power budgets. A model that cannot be quantized to 8-bit may simply not fit. The paper explicitly connects to this: INT8 quantization was intended to make deep learning feasible on CPUs and edge devices, but its limitations on modern architectures restrict which models can actually be deployed there.

- **Operator coverage beyond matmuls.** The paper emphasizes that neural networks spend "significant fraction of their execution time in memory-bound operations such as LayerNorm, BatchNorm and element-wise operators such as Add and Mul" (Section 3.2). These operations are not computationally intensive per operation, but they dominate memory traffic. Quantizing them would reduce memory bandwidth pressure, but prior INT8 attempts "were unsuccessful in maintaining the model accuracy" for these operators (Section 3.2). Without quantizing these ops, the end-to-end speedup from quantization is limited—you get faster matrix multiplications, but the surrounding memory-bound operations remain in high precision, creating a bottleneck.

- **The post-training constraint.** The paper focuses specifically on **post-training quantization (PTQ)**, where quantization is applied to a pre-trained model without any retraining or fine-tuning. This is "the preferred approach used in production" (Section 1) because it avoids the engineering complexity, computational cost, and data requirements of quantization-aware training (QAT). Any solution that requires QAT would be substantially less practical for deployment teams working with third-party or open-source models where training pipelines are not accessible. INT8 PTQ's failure on many modern models means practitioners face an uncomfortable choice: accept accuracy degradation, switch to QAT (if feasible), or run at higher precision.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper situates itself against a rich landscape of INT8 quantization techniques, each developed to address specific shortcomings of vanilla INT8 quantization. The authors acknowledge these advances but argue they are ultimately insufficient for modern workloads.

**Asymmetric quantization** (Jacob et al., 2018; Krishnamoorthi, 2018; Bhalgat et al., 2020) allocates different numbers of representable values for positive and negative ranges using a non-zero offset, which helps when the tensor distribution is not centered at zero. This partially addresses the dynamic range problem but does nothing about outliers—if a few extreme values exist in either the positive or negative tail, the quantization step size is still dominated by those values, and the densely populated central region still suffers from coarse quantization.

**Per-channel and per-group scaling** (Zhou et al., 2016; Mellempudi et al., 2017; Jacob et al., 2018) extends dynamic range by computing independent scale factors for each output channel or each group of elements within a tensor. Per-channel weight scaling is widely adopted and effective because weight distributions often differ substantially across channels. However, per-channel *activation* scaling, while beneficial for accuracy, requires "special kernel implementations that are likely to incur higher compute overheads" (Section 3.1), making it impractical for production deployment. The paper explicitly excludes per-channel activation scaling from its study for this reason.

**Outlier clipping via calibration** (Sung et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2019b; Choi et al., 2018; Esser et al., 2020) attempts to reduce the impact of outliers by truncating extreme values before determining the quantization range. Methods include KL divergence minimization (which chooses a clipping threshold that makes the quantized value distribution most similar to the original), MSE minimization (which directly optimizes for reconstruction accuracy), and percentile-based clipping (which discards a fixed percentage of extreme values). The paper evaluates these methods and finds they "did not provide any additional benefits" over simple max scaling when applied to FP8 formats (Section 3, Range Calibration paragraph), suggesting the problem is not just about clipping outliers better—it is about the fundamental representational capacity of the format itself.

**Mathematical transformations for outlier redistribution** (Wei et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2022) take a more sophisticated approach: rather than clipping outliers, these methods mathematically transform the model to shift the outlier magnitude from activations (where they are most damaging) into weights (where per-channel scaling can handle them). SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022) is the most prominent example, introducing a scaling factor that smooths activation outliers by transferring their magnitude to adjacent weight tensors. The paper uses SmoothQuant as a baseline for its NLP INT8 experiments (Section 4.2.1), which makes the INT8 numbers stronger than vanilla INT8 would achieve—and yet FP8 still substantially outperforms INT8+SmoothQuant.

**Non-uniform quantization** (Miyashita et al., 2016; Zhou et al., 2017; Cai et al., 2017; Fang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020) directly addresses INT8's uniform grid limitation by assigning more precision to important regions of the data distribution. These methods can theoretically match the distribution-matching properties of floating-point formats, but they typically require custom hardware or lookup-table-based implementations that are difficult to accelerate on standard compute units (GPUs, TPUs). FP8, by contrast, is being actively standardized and implemented in hardware (Micikevicius et al., 2022 report that NVIDIA's H100 GPU includes native FP8 support), giving it a practical deployment advantage that non-uniform INT8 methods lack.

Despite these decades of research on INT8 quantization, the authors demonstrate that INT8's workload pass rate remains at 65.87% (Table 2), and the failures are concentrated precisely in the model classes that are most important today: large language models and transformer-based architectures. Figure 4 shows that INT8's accuracy loss distribution has substantially higher variance on CV models than FP8 formats, with notable failures on EfficientNet, MobileNetV3, and ViT—models that are widely deployed in production.

### The FP8 Opportunity

The paper positions FP8 not as an incremental improvement over INT8, but as a **qualitatively different approach** that leverages the properties of floating-point representation to solve the outlier problem at the format level rather than through workarounds. Floating-point formats allocate representational grid points non-uniformly: they densely pack values near zero (where the bulk of weight and activation mass lies) and sparsely cover the tails (where outliers live), using exponent bits to scale the representable range. This is shown concretely in Figure 1: the FP8 formats concentrate grid points in the center of the distribution (the $3\sigma$ region), while INT8's uniform grid wastes representation on the tails.

The paper studies three FP8 variants spanning the dynamic range vs. precision tradeoff:

- **E5M2** (5 exponent bits, 2 mantissa bits): Maximum dynamic range (max value 57,344, min subnormal $1.5 \times 10^{-5}$), lowest precision (only 4 representable values per exponent step). Follows IEEE-like encoding with support for infinity. Best for representing extreme outliers without any scaling or calibration—the paper notes it "does not require Range Calibration because it has sufficient dynamic range" (Section 3).

- **E4M3** (4 exponent bits, 3 mantissa bits): Moderate dynamic range (max value 448, min subnormal $1.9 \times 10^{-3}$), moderate precision (8 values per exponent step). Uses extended encoding that reclaims the ±infinity bit patterns for additional representable values, sacrificing IEEE infinity support for more numeric range. This is the format that NVIDIA's H100 natively supports alongside E5M2 (Micikevicius et al., 2022).

- **E3M4** (3 exponent bits, 4 mantissa bits): Lowest dynamic range (max value 30, min subnormal $1.5 \times 10^{-2}$), highest precision (16 values per exponent step). Same extended encoding approach as E4M3. Pushes the tradeoff furthest toward precision at the expense of range.

The key insight driving the paper is that **different model types and different tensor types (weights vs. activations) have different requirements along this tradeoff spectrum**. Weights tend to have compact, near-normal distributions that benefit from precision (more mantissa bits), while NLP activations contain outliers that require dynamic range (more exponent bits). This domain-dependence is the empirical foundation for the paper's recommendations: E4M3 for NLP (where its balance of range and precision handles activation outliers while preserving weight fidelity) and E3M4 for CV (where activations tend to be well-behaved and precision matters more).

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as a **comprehensive empirical study** rather than a novel algorithmic contribution. It is not proposing a new quantization *method*—per-channel scaling, calibration, and operator fusion are all established techniques. Instead, it contributes:

1. **Scale of evidence.** With 75 architectures, 200+ tasks, and 20+ datasets across NLP, CV, speech, and recommendation, the paper provides by far the largest-scale empirical comparison of FP8 vs. INT8 post-training quantization in the literature. Prior work (Micikevicius et al., 2022; Kuzmin et al., 2022) studied FP8 on smaller sets of models (e.g., Micikevicius et al. examine GPT-3 6.7B as their representative LLM) or focused primarily on training rather than inference.

2. **A unified workflow.** The paper develops a generalized quantization recipe (the "standard scheme" for common ops plus the "extended scheme" for architecture-specific tuning) that works across domains without per-model manual intervention. The extended scheme's automatic tuning loop (Figure 2) for operator coverage, mixed formats, and static vs. dynamic quantization is presented as a framework that can be applied to new architectures.

3. **Domain-specific format recommendations.** Prior work recognized that different FP8 formats have different dynamic range/precision tradeoffs, but did not systematically establish which formats work best for which domains. The paper's finding that E4M3 achieves 96.32% NLP pass rate while E3M4 achieves 78.95% CV pass rate (Table 2) provides actionable guidance for practitioners choosing FP8 formats.

4. **Demonstration of expanded operator coverage.** The paper shows that FP8 formats can quantize LayerNorm, BatchNorm, and element-wise operations—operators that INT8 historically could not handle—enabling end-to-end 8-bit inference pipelines where INT8 would require mixed-precision fallbacks for these ops. This is shown quantitatively in Figure 9, where adding operator coverage incrementally (Conv/Linear → +BatchMatMul/MatMul → +Embedding → +LayerNorm) shows E4M3 maintaining tight accuracy loss while INT8 degrades.

The paper builds directly on the FP8 training literature (Wang et al., 2018; Mellempudi et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2019; Micikevicius et al., 2022) and the FP8 inference studies by Noune et al. (2022) and Kuzmin et al. (2022), but extends them to a production-oriented PTQ setting with practical constraints (no retraining, per-tensor activation scaling for hardware efficiency, automatic tuning for deployment). It also connects to the INT8 outlier literature (Wei et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2022; Dettmers et al., 2022) by showing that FP8's non-uniform representation provides an alternative to algorithmic outlier mitigation—rather than transforming the model to hide outliers, use a format that naturally tolerates them.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper presents a **generalized post-training quantization framework** that replaces INT8 with 8-bit floating-point (FP8) formats by applying a configurable pipeline of scaling, calibration, and operator-level decisions—the system is not a single new algorithm but a *workflow* that can be applied to arbitrary pre-trained models to convert them to FP8 inference with minimal accuracy loss. The core problem it solves is that INT8's uniform quantization grid cannot handle the long-tailed activation distributions and outliers characteristic of modern LLMs and transformers, and the solution is to exploit FP8's **non-uniform representation**—where grid points are dense near zero and sparse in the tails—plus a systematic tuning procedure that selects format assignments, operator coverage, and calibration strategies per model.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The quantization framework, depicted in Figure 2, has five major components connected in a sequential workflow:

1. **Range Calibration** — Determines the scaling factor for each tensor by measuring its maximum absolute value from a calibration dataset. For E5M2 this step is skipped (its dynamic range is already sufficient); for E4M3 and E3M4, simple max scaling is used. The authors tested KL divergence, MSE minimization, and percentile-based clipping as alternatives but found "they did not provide any additional benefits" (Section 3, Range Calibration paragraph).

2. **Standard Quantization Scheme** — The default configuration applied to a common subset of operators (Convolution, Linear, Embedding). This uses per-channel weight scaling and per-tensor activation scaling with a max-based scale factor, and optionally keeps the first and last layers of convolutional networks in higher precision (these layers constitute "< 1% of the total computation" but are "more sensitive to quantization"). This scheme is designed to be "identical to INT8 quantization scheme, allowing a fair accuracy comparison" (Section 3.1).

3. **BatchNorm Calibration** (CV models only) —A recalibration step that recomputes BatchNorm running mean and variance statistics using a forward pass of calibration data through the already-quantized model, compensating for "the variance shift caused by quantization" (Section 3). This uses training-time data augmentation transforms and a recommended sample size of 3,000.

4. **Extended Quantization Scheme** — An incremental, architecture-specific tuning layer applied on top of the standard scheme. It consists of three independently-selectable components applied in a feedback loop until accuracy targets are met:
   - **Expanded operator coverage**: quantizing additional operators beyond Conv/Linear/Embedding, including BatchMatMul, MatMul, LayerNorm, and element-wise operations (Add, Mul).
   - **Mixed FP8 formats**: assigning different FP8 representations to different tensors within the same model (e.g., E4M3 for activations and E3M4 for weights) based on whether each tensor is "range-bound" (has outliers needing large dynamic range) or "precision-bound" (has a compact distribution needing fine granularity).
   - **Dynamic quantization** (for E4M3 and E3M4): computing scale factors on-the-fly for each input rather than using pre-calibrated static scales. "We studied the accuracy impact of dynamic quantization on all FP8 formats and found that it offers no additional benefits to E5M2 but observed a noticeable improvement in accuracy for E4M3 and E3M4 formats on selected models" (Section 3.2).

5. **Accuracy-Driven Automatic Tuning Loop** — A feedback mechanism that evaluates the quantized model against the FP32 baseline, identifies the operator types or individual operators causing the largest accuracy degradation, and selectively applies extended scheme components to those operators. The tuning strategy enables "automatic tuning for the best accuracy, performance or Pareto optimal" (Appendix A.1) by searching over combinations of tuneable parameters (operator inclusion/exclusion, format assignment, static vs. dynamic quantization) with a "customized search space based on our experiment result" to narrow down the options.

The information flow is straightforward: Calibration data → Range Calibration produces scale factors → Standard Scheme applies default quantization → BatchNorm Calibration (CV only) adjusts running statistics → The tuning loop iteratively tests accuracy, identifies problematic operators, and applies Extended Scheme components to them → The final quantized model is emitted when accuracy meets the 1% relative loss criterion or no further improvements are found.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the FP8 value representation and its core advantage**, including the density equation that formalizes *why* floating-point formats naturally handle long-tailed distributions—this is the mathematical foundation that makes everything else work.
- **Second, the scale factor computation**, since it is the shared mechanism across all FP8 formats and tensor types, and the design choice between per-channel (weights) and per-tensor (activations) scaling reveals important hardware-aware tradeoffs.

#### The FP8 Value Representation and Its Core Advantage

The paper studies three 8-bit floating-point formats—E5M2, E4M3, and E3M4—whose binary layouts are specified in Table 1. Each format allocates 1 bit for the sign, `e` bits for the exponent, and `m` bits for the mantissa (fraction), with the notation `EeMm` encoding the allocation. The formats share a common mathematical representation defined by the standard floating-point equation:

$$x = (-1)^s \times 2^{2^e - b} \times \left(1 + f_1 \times 2^{-1} + f_2 \times 2^{-2} + \dots + f_m \times 2^{-m}\right)$$

where `$s \in \{0, 1\}$` is the sign bit, `$e$` is the exponent bit-width (5, 4, or 3), `$b$` is the exponent bias (15 for E5M2, 7 for E4M3, 3 for E3M4), and `$f_i \in \{0, 1\}$` are the `$m$` mantissa bits.

**What it computes:** Given a specific bit pattern in an 8-bit register, this equation reconstructs the real number it represents. The sign bit `$s$` determines whether the number is positive or negative. The biased exponent `$2^e - b$` produces a power-of-two scaling factor—for E4M3 with `$b=7$`, exponent bit patterns 0000 through 1110 map to actual exponents from `$2^{-7}$` to `$2^{7}$`, giving a dynamic range spanning roughly `$1.9 \times 10^{-3}$` to 448. The mantissa bits `$f_1, \dots, f_m$` provide `$2^m$` equally-spaced subdivisions *within* each exponent interval, so the step size between adjacent representable numbers doubles with each increment of the exponent.

**Why this form:** The crucial property is that the step size between adjacent representable values is **not constant**—it grows with the magnitude of the represented value. Within a single exponent interval `$[2^k, 2^{k+1})$`, the spacing is `$2^{k - m}$` (there are `$2^m$` grid points spanning the interval of width `$2^k$`). This means that near zero (small exponent `$k$`), the representable values are densely packed, while in the tails (large `$k$`), they are sparse. This is the opposite of INT8, which allocates 256 uniformly-spaced values regardless of the data distribution. The consequence, visible in Figure 1's center plot, is that FP8 formats concentrate the majority of their representational budget inside the `$3\sigma$` region of a normal distribution—exactly where most weight and activation values lie—while still covering extreme outliers (up to 57,344 for E5M2) at the cost of coarsely representing those sparse tail regions.

**The density equation formalizes this property.** The appendix derives an expression for the representational density of an `E(e)M(m)` format:

$$D_{E(e)M(m)} = 2^{m - \text{Floor}[\log_2 N]}$$

where `$N$` is the decimal value being represented. This equation states that the number of representable values per unit interval around `$N$` decreases in inverse proportion to `$N$` itself: a value of `$N = 1$` benefits from `$2^m$` grid points in its exponent interval, while a value of `$N = 1024$` has `$2^{m-10}$` grid points—over a thousand times fewer. As the paper states in Appendix A.1, "the smaller the number N the denser the number of values being represented. On the contrary, the larger the number N the sparser the number of values being represented. Therefore, we always prefer to examine the histogram of our tensor's value and make sure always to represent the high frequency part of our tensor on the lower range on FP8 with higher density, which is in sharp contrast to INT8 with uniform density."

**Special value encoding differences.** Table 1 details how the three formats handle special values. E5M2 follows IEEE-like encoding: it supports subnormals (for graceful underflow below `$1.5 \times 10^{-5}$`), a single NaN pattern (all exponent bits = 11111 with any non-zero mantissa), and both `$\pm$`Infinity (exponent = 11111, mantissa = 0). E4M3 and E3M4 use **extended encoding** that reclaims the `$\pm$`Infinity bit patterns for additional representable numbers—this is why Table 1 shows "NO" for Infinity support in these formats. Instead, E4M3 and E3M4 designate a single all-ones bit pattern (exponent bits all 1, mantissa bits all 1) as NaN, gaining seven additional representable values in E4M3 and fifteen in E3M4 compared to an IEEE-compliant encoding.

**Exponent bias as a range-shifting mechanism.** The exponent bias `$b$` shifts the numeric range covered by the format without changing its precision characteristics. E5M2 uses `$b=15$` to center its range around large values (max = 57,344), E4M3 uses `$b=7$` for a moderate range (max = 448), and E3M4 uses `$b=3$` for a compact range (max = 30). The paper does not explore variable exponent biases (unlike Noune et al., 2022 and Kuzmin et al., 2022 who studied per-tensor bias tuning), instead using fixed standard biases—this is a deliberate simplification for the production PTQ setting where per-tensor bias search would add calibration complexity.

#### Scale Factor Computation

All FP8 formats require a scaling operation before quantization because the exponent bias is fixed and may not match the tensor's actual magnitude distribution. The scale factor maps the tensor's values into the representable range of the chosen FP8 format. The paper uses a simple max-based scaling:

$$s = \frac{\text{float\_max}}{\text{max\_}T}$$

where `$\text{float\_max}$` is the maximum representable value of the selected FP8 format (57,344 for E5M2, 448 for E4M3, 30 for E3M4—see Table 1), and `$\text{max\_}T = \max(|x|)$` is the calibrated absolute maximum value of the tensor `$T$` estimated from the calibration dataset.

**What it computes:** For each tensor, this equation produces a single scalar `$s$` such that multiplying every element of the tensor by `$s$` scales the maximum absolute value to exactly `$\text{float\_max}$`. The scaled tensor values then fall within `$[-\text{float\_max}, \text{float\_max}]$`, the representable range of the FP8 format. Quantization proceeds by rounding each scaled value to the nearest representable FP8 number (which, because FP8 values are non-uniformly spaced, involves finding the appropriate exponent interval and then rounding the mantissa). At runtime, dequantization reverses this: the stored FP8 value is converted back to a higher-precision number and divided by `$s$`.

**Why this form:** Max scaling is the simplest possible calibration strategy—it uses a single statistic (the maximum absolute value) and guarantees that no value will be clipped (since the scaled maximum exactly hits the format's ceiling). The paper explicitly compares this against more sophisticated INT8-style calibration methods: "We also examined more sophisticated range-calibration methods such as KL divergence, MSE error and percentile which did not provide any additional benefits" (Section 3). This is a notable finding because these methods are standard in INT8 quantization precisely to handle outliers—KL divergence clipping, for example, intentionally truncates outliers to reduce the step size for in-distribution values. The fact that FP8 does not benefit from these methods confirms that FP8's tapered precision (dense near zero, sparse in tails) already handles outliers without needing to clip them—the sparse representation in the tails naturally limits the damage outliers cause to the quantization error of in-distribution values.

**Per-channel vs. per-tensor scaling.** The paper makes different recommendations for weights and activations based on a combination of empirical evidence and hardware practicality:

- **Weights: per-channel scaling is recommended** because it "can reduce rounding errors by effectively utilizing the full encoding space for each channel" (Section 3.1). Each output channel of a weight tensor gets its own scale factor `$s_c$`, computed from `$\text{max\_}T_c$` (the maximum absolute weight in that channel). This is practical because weight tensors are static—scale factors can be pre-computed once and stored alongside the quantized weights without runtime overhead.

- **Activations: per-tensor scaling is used** because, although "per-channel activation scaling can benefit INT8 quantization" (Section 3.1, citing Xiao et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022; Dettmers et al., 2022), such methods "may require special kernel implementations that are likely to incur higher compute overheads." A per-tensor scale is a single scalar per activation tensor that can be efficiently applied as part of the matrix multiplication (by folding it into the weight scaling factors or using it as a post-multiplication rescaling). Per-channel activation scaling would require different scale factors for different elements of the same input row, which breaks standard GEMM (General Matrix Multiply) implementations and requires custom kernels.

**E5M2 exception.** The paper states that "E5M2 uses direct quantization and does not require Range Calibration because it has sufficient dynamic range to handle outliers" (Section 3). This means E5M2 with its 57,344 max value can represent virtually any activation or weight value encountered in practice without any scaling at all—the raw values are directly converted to FP8 without a scale factor. This simplifies the workflow (one less calibration step) at the cost of reduced precision (only 2 mantissa bits, giving just 4 representable values per exponent interval).

#### The Standard Quantization Scheme

The standard quantization scheme is the **default configuration applied uniformly across all architectures** to the "common subset of operators including Convolution, Linear and Embedding" (Section 3.1). It is designed to be minimal and broadly applicable—the paper explicitly states "this scheme is also identical to INT8 quantization scheme, allowing a fair accuracy comparison" (Section 3.1), meaning that any difference in accuracy or pass rate between FP8 and INT8 under the standard scheme is attributable to the format itself, not to differences in what is quantized or how.

**Operator coverage.** The standard scheme quantizes three operator types:
- **Convolution** (2D convolution layers in CNNs)
- **Linear** (fully-connected layers, including the feed-forward and attention projection layers in transformers)
- **Embedding** (lookup table layers that map discrete token IDs to continuous vectors)

These three cover the matrix multiplication operations that dominate computational cost in deep learning inference. Operations not covered by the standard scheme—LayerNorm, BatchNorm, element-wise Add and Mul, activation functions—remain in higher precision (FP16 or FP32), which limits end-to-end speedup but avoids accuracy degradation from quantizing operations that are known to be sensitive or difficult to quantize with INT8.

**Weight scaling.** Per-channel scaling is applied to all weight tensors. For a weight tensor with shape `[out_channels, in_channels]` (for Linear) or `[out_channels, in_channels, kernel_h, kernel_w]` (for Convolution), each output channel dimension gets an independent scale factor `$s_c$` computed from the maximum absolute value in that channel's slice of the tensor. This is important because weight magnitudes can vary substantially across channels—a channel with small weights would be poorly represented if forced to share a scale factor with a channel containing large weights, since the large weights would push the shared scale factor up and leave the small weights quantized to very few discrete levels. Per-channel scaling gives each channel its own full encoding space.

**Activation scaling.** Per-tensor scaling is applied, producing a single scale factor for the entire activation tensor. For a Linear layer's input with shape `[batch, seq_len, in_features]`, one scale factor covers all elements. This is the hardware-compatible choice: the scale can be folded into the next layer's weight quantization or applied as a single scalar multiply after the matrix operation, requiring no changes to the GEMM kernel.

**Scale factor computation.** The scale factors are computed from calibration data—a subset of the training or validation dataset—using the max-scaling equation. The "calibrated absmax value of the tensor" (`$\text{max\_}T$`) is the maximum absolute value observed across all calibration samples that flow through that tensor. This is a one-time offline computation: the calibration dataset is passed through the FP32 model once, maximum absolute values are recorded for every tensor to be quantized, and scale factors are derived and stored.

**First and last operator handling.** The paper maintains the "first convolution and the last fully-connected layers" in higher precision for convolutional neural networks (Section 3.1), following a well-established practice in the INT8 quantization literature (Han et al., 2015b; Choi et al., 2018; Micikevicius et al., 2022). The justification is that these two operators "typically constitute < 1% of the total computation" but "are more sensitive to quantization." The sensitivity arises because:
- The first convolution operates on raw input data (e.g., RGB pixel values in [0, 255]), which has a different distribution than the normalized intermediate activations that follow BatchNorm layers.
- The last fully-connected layer produces the final class logits, and quantization error in these logits directly impacts the softmax output and final prediction, whereas quantization error in intermediate layers can be partially compensated for by the network's redundancy.

The paper notes that this exception is "only applicable to convolutional neural networks"—transformer-based NLP models do not have an equivalent exemption, since their input embeddings and output projection layers are themselves Linear layers covered by the standard scheme.

**Accuracy criterion.** The standard scheme's success is measured against a threshold: a model "passes" if its quantized accuracy is within 1% relative loss of the FP32 baseline. This is the criterion used throughout the paper for computing pass rates (Table 2). "Relative loss" means a model with FP32 accuracy of 0.80 passes if the quantized accuracy is at least 0.792 (i.e., 0.80 × (1 - 0.01)). This is a stricter and more practically meaningful criterion than absolute accuracy difference—a 1% relative loss on a model with 0.50 FP32 accuracy requires quantized accuracy ≥ 0.495 (only 0.005 absolute difference), while on a 0.95-accuracy model it allows a 0.0095 absolute drop.

#### The Extended Quantization Scheme

The extended quantization scheme is **applied incrementally to address the specific needs of individual architectures** through three independent mechanisms that can be activated selectively via the automatic tuning loop (Section 3.2). Unlike the standard scheme which is uniform across models, the extended scheme represents architecture-specific optimization.

##### Expanded Operator Coverage

The paper identifies that "neural networks spend significant fraction of their execution time in memory-bound operations such as LayerNorm, BatchNorm and element-wise operators such as Add and Mul" (Section 3.2). These operations are not computationally intensive in terms of FLOPs (they perform simple arithmetic on each element independently), but they are **memory-bound**: their execution time is dominated by reading input tensors from memory and writing results back, not by the arithmetic itself. When these operations remain in FP16/FP32 while surrounding compute-heavy operations (matmuls, convolutions) are quantized to FP8, the system must constantly convert between formats—loading FP8 data, upcasting to FP16/FP32 for the memory-bound op, performing the operation, and then potentially downcasting back to FP8 for the next compute op. This conversion overhead erodes the speedup from quantization.

The paper shows that FP8 formats can quantize these operators **without sacrificing model accuracy**, which INT8 historically could not achieve. The specific additional operator types covered are:

- **BatchMatMul (BMM)** and **MatMul (MM):** Generalized matrix multiplication operations beyond standard Linear layers. BatchMatMul performs matrix multiplication over batched dimensions and is common in multi-head attention mechanisms. MatMul covers explicit matrix multiply nodes (as opposed to Linear which bundles the multiply with an optional bias add).

- **Embedding and EmbeddingBag:** While Embedding was already in the standard scheme, EmbeddingBag (a variant that computes mean or sum over embeddings without materializing the full lookup result) is added. These appear in recommendation models like DLRM where embedding tables are large and memory-bound.

- **LayerNorm:** The normalization operation ubiquitous in transformer architectures. LayerNorm computes `(x - μ) / σ` along the feature dimension for each token independently, then applies learned scale and shift parameters. It is present after every attention and feed-forward sublayer in transformers like BERT and LLaMA. Prior INT8 attempts to quantize LayerNorm were "unsuccessful in maintaining the model accuracy" (Section 3.2), likely because LayerNorm's division by standard deviation amplifies small quantization errors in the normalized output.

- **BatchNorm** (ones that cannot be folded into preceding Convolution layers): In architectures like DenseNet where BatchNorm appears in dense blocks without a following convolution that can absorb the BatchNorm parameters, the normalization must be computed explicitly at inference time rather than being folded. Quantizing it reduces memory traffic.

- **Element-wise Add and Mul:** These are residual connection additions (Add) and gating multiplications (Mul) that appear in attention mechanisms, highway connections, and various architectural patterns. They are pure memory-bound operations—each involves one arithmetic operation per element—making format conversion overhead proportionally large.

Figure 9 shows the incremental impact of adding these operators for both CV and NLP models across all FP8 formats and INT8. The key pattern: E4M3 maintains tight accuracy loss (within 1–2%) as operators are added (Conv/Linear → +BMM/MM → +Embedding/EmbeddingBag → +LayerNorm), while INT8 shows larger and more variable degradation, particularly on LayerNorm addition for NLP models. This is direct evidence for the paper's claim that FP8's "non-uniform representation handles these operators without sacrificing model accuracy."

##### Mixed FP8 Formats

The observation motivating mixed formats is that **different tensors within the same model have different distributional characteristics** that favor different FP8 tradeoffs. Figure 3 illustrates this:

- **NLP activations** (left panel) contain outliers—a small fraction of values much larger than the bulk of the distribution—making them "range-bounded." They need sufficient dynamic range (exponent bits) to represent these outliers without clipping, even at the cost of reduced precision in the densely-populated center.

- **CV activations** (center panel) tend to be more compact, without extreme outliers, making them "precision-bounded." They benefit from more mantissa bits to finely distinguish between the many values concentrated near the distribution center.

- **Weights** (right panel) from both CV and NLP models "tend to follow normal distributions with lots of values near zero," making them precision-bounded as well. They benefit from mantissa bits over exponent bits.

The mixed FP8 formats strategy assigns different FP8 representations to different tensors: **E4M3 or E5M2 for range-bound tensors** (NLP activations) and **E3M4 for precision-bound tensors** (weights, CV activations). The paper states "we balance this trade-off by assigning E5M2 or E4M3 format for range-bound tensors and E3M4 for precision-bound tensors" (Section 3.2).

Figure 8 provides a concrete example using a Linear operator from BERT-base on the MRPC task. The figure compares the quantization error (MSE) on the input tensor, weight tensor, and output tensor under single-format quantization (E5M2, E4M3, E3M4) vs. mixed formats. The key result: using E4M3 for the activation (the range-bound input) and E3M4 for the weight (the precision-bound weight) reduces output MSE from 5,617 to 919—a 6× reduction compared to the next-best single format. This is because E4M3's extra exponent bit handles the activation outliers, while E3M4's extra mantissa bit preserves weight precision, and the combination produces a more accurate matrix multiplication result than either format alone could achieve.

The mixed format scheme is the "E4M3 and E3M4" bar in Figure 8's rightmost grouping. The paper reports specific accuracy improvements from mixed formats in Table 5: Bert-Base on MRPC recovers FP32 accuracy exactly (0.9069 quantized = 0.9069 FP32), Bert-Large on RTE improves from 0.7329 (single E4M3) to 0.7365 (mixed), and Longformer on MRPC improves from 0.9113 to 0.9143. Funnel on MRPC is the most dramatic case—single E3M4 collapses to 0.3704 (vs. 0.9225 FP32), but mixed formats recover to 0.9233, even slightly exceeding FP32 accuracy (likely due to the regularization effect of quantization noise).

**Why mixed formats work when single formats fail.** The Funnel case illustrates the mechanism. E3M4 has a max representable value of 30 (Table 1). If Funnel's activations contain outliers exceeding 30 in magnitude, those values get clipped to 30, causing severe accuracy degradation. E4M3 has a max value of 448, which can accommodate the outliers, but its reduced precision (3 mantissa bits vs. 4) may degrade weight representation. Mixed formats assign E4M3 to the activation (solving the clipping problem) and E3M4 to the weight (preserving precision), resolving both issues simultaneously.

##### Static vs. Dynamic Quantization

The paper uses **static quantization as the default method throughout the study because it is computationally more efficient** (Section 3.2). In static quantization, the scale factor for each activation tensor is pre-computed from calibration data and fixed at inference time. This means the scale factor can be folded into weight quantization or applied as a pre-computed constant, requiring no runtime computation.

**Dynamic quantization** computes the scale factor on-the-fly for each input batch (or even each individual input) during inference. For an activation tensor, dynamic quantization observes the actual values for this specific input, computes `max_T` from those values, derives the scale factor, and applies quantization. This is more expensive—it requires a reduction operation (finding the maximum) over the entire activation tensor at runtime—but can improve accuracy because the scale factor is tailored to the specific input distribution rather than being a one-size-fits-all estimate from calibration data.

The paper's findings on dynamic vs. static are format-dependent (Section 3.2):

- **E5M2: dynamic quantization "offers no additional benefits"** because its dynamic range is already so large that precise scaling is unnecessary—any reasonable scale factor will bring values into the representable range without clipping.

- **E4M3 and E3M4: dynamic quantization shows "a noticeable improvement in accuracy for selected models."** Table 6 quantifies this: Bert-Base on MRPC with E4M3 improves from 0.9072 (static) to 0.9151 (dynamic), a +0.87% relative gain; Bert-Large on RTE with E4M3 improves from 0.7329 to 0.7401 (+0.98%).

The mechanism behind this improvement is that static calibration may underestimate `max_T` if the calibration dataset does not contain the most extreme activations present at deployment time. If a deployment input contains an outlier larger than any calibration sample, static quantization clips that outlier (causing information loss), while dynamic quantization observes it and adjusts the scale factor accordingly. This is particularly relevant for NLP models where activation outliers can be input-dependent—a sentence with unusual token combinations may produce larger activation spikes than any calibration example.

**Why dynamic quantization matters for pass rates.** Table 2 shows that E4M3 static achieves 96.32% NLP pass rate, while E4M3 dynamic achieves only 92.11%. This appears to contradict the per-model accuracy improvements in Table 6, but the explanation is that dynamic quantization is being applied selectively (by the tuning loop) only to models that benefit, while the pass rate counts all models. The tuning loop's job is to decide when dynamic quantization is worth the overhead—for models where static already meets the 1% criterion, dynamic is not activated.

#### BatchNorm Calibration (CV Models Only)

This step applies exclusively to computer vision models with BatchNorm layers that cannot be folded into preceding convolution layers (Section 3, workflow description, and Appendix A.1 discussion of Figure 7). The paper states that "retuning BatchNorm parameters (mean and variance) to compensate for the variance shift caused by quantization, has significantly improved the inference accuracy" (Section 3), citing Sun et al. (2019) as the precedent.

**The variance shift problem.** BatchNorm layers store running estimates of the mean `$\mu$` and variance `$\sigma^2$` of their inputs, accumulated during training via exponential moving averages. At inference time, BatchNorm normalizes using these stored statistics: `$y = \gamma \cdot (x - \mu) / \sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon} + \beta$`, where `$\gamma$` and `$\beta$` are learned parameters. However, after quantization is applied to the preceding layers, the distribution of values arriving at the BatchNorm layer changes—the quantization noise shifts the mean and increases the variance of the inputs. The stored `$\mu$` and `$\sigma^2$` from FP32 training become stale, causing the normalization to be miscalibrated. This is the "variance shift" problem.

**The recalibration procedure.** BatchNorm calibration recomputes `$\mu$` and `$\sigma^2$` by running a forward pass of calibration data through the **already-quantized** model (not the FP32 model) and accumulating the per-channel statistics that arrive at each BatchNorm layer. These new statistics replace the training-time running estimates. The paper's key finding is the importance of calibration data quality:

- **Training-time data augmentation** (random crops, flips, color jitter) produces more effective calibration data than **inference-time transforms** (center crop, resize). Figure 7 shows this: for ResNet-50 with only 300 calibration samples, "training transform" yields approximately 0.3% accuracy loss while "inference transform" yields roughly 1.5% loss—a 5× difference.

- **Sample size matters but plateaus.** Figure 7 compares 300 samples vs. 3,000 vs. 10,000 with different transforms. With training transform, 300 samples is often sufficient, but the paper "recommend[s] sample size of 3K with training transform for achieving best results across a wide range of networks" (Section 4.3.1).

Why training augmentations help: randomized augmentations produce diverse feature distributions that better cover the range of activation statistics the quantized model will encounter at deployment. Inference transforms produce a narrow, canonical view of the data that may not fully exercise the BatchNorm statistics, leading to stale estimates that degrade accuracy on real (varied) inputs.

**Models affected.** The BatchNorm calibration step is primarily needed for architectures where BatchNorm layers appear in non-foldable positions—for example, DenseNet's dense blocks where BatchNorm is followed by concatenation rather than convolution, preventing folding. For standard ResNet-style architectures where every BatchNorm is immediately followed by a convolution layer, the BatchNorm parameters can be mathematically absorbed (folded) into the convolution weights and biases at inference time, eliminating the need for explicit BatchNorm computation and thus for recalibration.

#### The Automatic Tuning Loop

The tuning loop (Figure 2, "Extended Quantization Scheme" feedback arrow) is the **procedural mechanism that makes the workflow general across 75 architectures** without per-model manual intervention. It operates as follows:

1. **Quantize with standard scheme.** Apply the default configuration (per-channel weight scaling, per-tensor activation scaling, max-based calibration, Conv/Linear/Embedding operators only). For CV models, also apply BatchNorm calibration.

2. **Evaluate accuracy.** Run the quantized model on the evaluation dataset and compute accuracy relative to FP32 baseline.

3. **Check pass criterion.** If accuracy is within 1% relative loss of FP32, the model passes and no extended quantization is needed. This is the common case—the standard scheme alone accounts for most of the 92.64% E4M3 pass rate.

4. **If accuracy is insufficient,** the tuning loop enters an iterative optimization phase. It identifies which operators or operator types are causing the largest accuracy degradation. The paper describes this as "operator level" fallback analysis (Appendix A.1): "we have to fallback some operators to high precision to let the quantized model meet the accuracy goal." The tuning loop evaluates candidate operator quantizations, identifies those whose quantization causes disproportionate accuracy loss, and applies extended scheme components to them:
   - For operators that are inherently sensitive: apply **expanded operator coverage** selectively (e.g., keep LayerNorm in FP16 but quantize other added ops).
   - For tensors with outlier problems: apply **mixed FP8 formats** (assign more exponent bits to outlier-heavy tensors).
   - For models where static calibration is insufficient: enable **dynamic quantization** for selected tensors.

5. **The search space** is "based on the combination of all tune-able parameters by default" but with a "customized search space based on our experiment result" that narrows down the options based on prior knowledge (Appendix A.1). For example, if prior experiments show that NLP models rarely benefit from E5M2, the search space for an NLP model might exclude E5M2 options to reduce tuning time.

6. **The tuning targets** are described as "automatic tuning for the best accuracy, performance or Pareto optimal" (Appendix A.1). This means the loop can optimize for:
   - **Best accuracy** (maximize pass rate, potentially at the cost of keeping more operators in high precision).
   - **Best performance** (quantize as many operators as possible while staying within the accuracy threshold).
   - **Pareto optimal** (find the frontier of accuracy vs. operator coverage tradeoffs).

The tuning loop is not described in algorithmic detail—the paper does not specify, for example, whether it uses grid search, random search, or Bayesian optimization over the tune-able parameter space. However, the implication of "customized search space" and the scale of the study (75 architectures × multiple format options × multiple operator combinations) suggests a structured approach that prunes obviously inferior configurations early.

**What makes this "automatic."** The key is that the tuning logic is driven by the observed accuracy gap, not by human intuition about which operators are "important." The system programmatically tests quantizing different subsets of operators, measures the accuracy impact, and selects the configuration that meets the target. This is what allows the same workflow to handle a CNN for ImageNet classification, a transformer for machine translation, and a diffusion model for image generation—each with different operator types, tensor distributions, and sensitivity patterns—without per-architecture expert tuning.

#### The INT8 Baseline Setup

To ensure a fair comparison, the paper configures INT8 quantization using the best practices established in the literature, matching the FP8 workflow's structure where possible and applying INT8-specific optimizations where they are known to help.

**Static vs. dynamic quantization per domain.** Table 2 shows that INT8 uses **static quantization for CV models and dynamic quantization for NLP models**. This split is motivated by the different outlier characteristics: NLP activations have severe outliers that make static calibration unreliable (dynamic quantization can adapt per-input), while CV activations are generally well-behaved enough for static scales. This is the same pattern observed by practitioners in the INT8 quantization community—dynamic quantization is standard for transformer-based NLP models (it is, for example, the default in PyTorch's quantization API for BERT), while static quantization is preferred for CNNs where calibration data is representative.

**SmoothQuant for NLP models.** The paper enables SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022) on all NLP INT8 models "with the default smoothing alpha value" and explicitly notes that "alpha tuning is out of scope in this paper" (Section 4.2.1). SmoothQuant is a mathematical transformation that shifts the quantization difficulty from activations to weights: it multiplies activations by a per-channel scaling factor `$s$` and divides the corresponding weight columns by the same factor, leaving the matrix multiplication output unchanged but smoothing activation outliers into the weights where per-channel scaling can handle them. Enabling SmoothQuant makes the INT8 NLP baseline **stronger** than vanilla INT8—it is a state-of-the-art LLM quantization technique—which makes FP8's superior NLP pass rate (96.32% E4M3 vs. 67.65% INT8+SmoothQuant in Table 2) more meaningful: FP8 outperforms INT8 *even after applying the best available INT8-specific mitigation*.

**Operator coverage for INT8.** The INT8 baseline follows the same standard scheme as FP8 (Conv/Linear/Embedding) and does not quantize LayerNorm, BatchNorm, or element-wise ops—the paper notes that "previous attempts to quantize these operators using integer approximation were unsuccessful in maintaining the model accuracy" (Section 3.2). This means INT8's lower pass rate is not because it quantizes more aggressively and fails, but because even with conservative operator coverage (fewer ops quantized than FP8's extended scheme can handle), it still cannot match FP8's accuracy on many models.

**Scale factor computation for INT8.** Like FP8, INT8 uses max-based calibration, but the paper also references the more sophisticated INT8 methods (KL divergence, MSE, percentile) as alternatives that were evaluated for FP8 and found unnecessary. For INT8, these sophisticated methods are often *necessary* to achieve acceptable accuracy—the fact that INT8 pass rate is only 65.87% even with the best available calibration and SmoothQuant highlights the fundamental representational limitation.

**The 1% relative loss criterion.** The same accuracy threshold applies to both INT8 and FP8, making pass rates directly comparable. A model "passes" under INT8 if it meets the same 1% relative loss standard—INT8's 65.87% pass rate means that for 34.13% of the evaluated workloads, INT8 quantization (with best practices) causes more than 1% relative accuracy degradation compared to FP32. For these models, INT8 is not a viable deployment option without quantization-aware training (which is out of scope for this paper's PTQ focus).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Quantization from an Outlier Mitigation Problem to a Format Selection Problem

The dominant conceptual framework in the post-training quantization literature prior to this work treated quantization as an **outlier mitigation problem**. The underlying assumption—reflected across two decades of INT8 research—was that 8-bit integer representation is fundamentally adequate for neural network inference, and that failures on modern architectures could be resolved by *working around* its limitations: clip outliers better (KL divergence, MSE, percentile-based calibration), redistribute them across tensors (SmoothQuant, outlier channel splitting), or allocate more bits to important values (non-uniform quantization, per-group scaling). Each generation of INT8 methods addressed a specific failure mode within the same paradigm, but the underlying format remained untouchable—it was the fixed constraint around which all algorithmic ingenuity had to work.

This paper makes a **fundamental reframing**: it argues that the format itself is the design variable, not the constraint. Instead of asking "how can we make INT8 work despite its uniform grid?", it asks "which of several available FP8 formats best matches the distributional characteristics of this specific model's tensors?" The shift is from a mitigation mindset—deploying increasingly sophisticated workarounds against a format's limitations—to a selection mindset—choosing the right tool for the job from a family of formats spanning different dynamic range vs. precision tradeoffs.

**Intellectual move.** The key diagnostic insight is contained in Figure 1, which is not merely an illustrative plot but a **conceptual argument rendered visually**. By showing that FP8 formats naturally concentrate representational grid points in the high-density region (the 3σ band of a normal distribution) while still reaching far into the tails, the figure demonstrates that the outlier problem—which INT8 required algorithmic heroics to address—is *inherently solved at the representational level* by floating-point's tapered precision. The paper then operationalizes this observation through the density equation in Appendix A.1 (`$D_{E(e)M(m)} = 2^{m - \lfloor \log_2 N \rfloor}$`), which formalizes *why* smaller values automatically get higher precision: it is not a heuristic or a tuning choice; it is a mathematical property of the floating-point number system.

**Comparison to prior work.** Prior FP8 studies (Micikevicius et al., 2022; Kuzmin et al., 2022; Noune et al., 2022) had documented that FP8 formats can work for specific models or training tasks, but they did not frame FP8 as a *replacement for the INT8 paradigm* in production PTQ settings. Micikevicius et al. focused on training and examined a narrower set of inference models (GPT-3 6.7B as their representative LLM). Kuzmin et al. explored variable exponent biases and even narrower formats (E2M5), but again on a limited model set. Noune et al. studied FP8 numerical properties without the systematic workload coverage analysis. None of these works positioned FP8 as a **drop-in replacement for INT8 across the full spectrum of production models**, nor did they provide the evidence to make that claim stick. This paper's 75-architecture, 200+-task scale transforms FP8 from a research curiosity into an empirically validated **default alternative to INT8**.

**Significance beyond raw performance.** This reframing has downstream consequences for how the field prioritizes research. If the outlier problem is solved at the format level, the return on investment for developing increasingly clever INT8 mitigation techniques diminishes sharply. Why spend research effort on designing a better clipping algorithm when switching to E4M3 already provides a 96.32% pass rate on NLP models without any clipping at all? The paper's finding that "sophisticated range-calibration methods such as KL divergence, MSE error and percentile... did not provide any additional benefits" over simple max scaling for FP8 (Section 3) is evidence for this: once the format handles outliers, the calibration problem simplifies dramatically. This is not an incremental improvement—it collapses an entire subfield of INT8 calibration research into a non-problem for FP8.

**Evidence anchor.** Table 2's headline result—FP8 E4M3 achieves 92.64% overall pass rate vs. INT8's 65.87%—is the quantitative manifestation of this reframing. The gap is too large to be explained by incremental improvements; it signals that INT8 was fundamentally mismatched to a substantial fraction of modern workloads, and FP8 formats resolve the mismatch.

---

### Innovation 2: Domain-Conditioned Format Selection as a Deployment Principle

The paper's second distinctive contribution is the empirical demonstration that **the optimal FP8 format is domain-dependent**, and that this dependence follows a consistent, explainable pattern tied to distributional characteristics of tensors in different model families. This establishes format selection as a first-class deployment decision—analogous to choosing a learning rate or optimizer—rather than a one-size-fits-all default.

**Intellectual move.** The paper identifies a clean **causal mechanism** linking domain to format choice, grounded in Figure 3's distribution visualizations. NLP models produce activation tensors with prominent outliers (the long-tailed histograms in the left panel), which demand dynamic range—hence more exponent bits. CV model activations lack these extreme outliers (center panel), making their distributions "precision-bounded" and favoring mantissa bits. Weights across both domains are consistently compact and near-normal (right panel), uniformly favoring precision. This maps directly to format recommendations: E4M3 (balanced range/precision) for NLP where activation outliers need the extra exponent bit, E3M4 (maximum precision) for CV where no tensor demands large dynamic range, and E5M2 only when range is critical and precision acceptable.

This is a **diagnostic model**, not just a set of recommendations. It tells a practitioner *how to think about* format selection for a new model: examine the activation distributions. If they exhibit long-tailed outliers, reach for E4M3. If they are compact, use E3M4. This generalizes beyond the specific architectures studied—it provides a principle that can be applied to future model families whose distributions may not have been directly tested.

**Comparison to prior work.** Prior FP8 work acknowledged that different formats have different tradeoffs, but treated format selection as an empirical hyperparameter to be swept rather than a principled choice driven by domain characteristics. Micikevicius et al. (2022) recommended E4M3 for forward-pass activations and weights, and E5M2 for gradients during training, but this recommendation was based on the training dynamics (gradients need range), not on domain-specific inference characteristics. The mixed-format literature (Sun et al., 2019) proposed using two formats within the same model for training, but did not map the choice to domain-level properties. This paper is the first to provide a **domain-level taxonomy** of format suitability backed by large-scale evidence.

The domain-dependence finding also has a **negative-result component** that is intellectually important: the paper does not claim that one format dominates across all tasks. E4M3 achieves 96.32% on NLP but only 73.68% on CV; E3M4 achieves 78.95% on CV but only 92.11% on NLP (Table 2). This near-symmetry demonstrates that the format choice is not about one format being "better" in general, but about **fit to distribution**. This is a more nuanced and practically useful finding than declaring a universal winner.

**Significance beyond raw performance.** This finding changes how deployment engineers should approach quantization. Rather than defaulting to INT8 (or even to a single FP8 format) and debugging failures case-by-case, they should characterize their model's tensor distributions and select the format accordingly. The paper's workflow automates this through the tuning loop, but the *principle*—that domain dictates format—is what makes the automation possible. If format choice were arbitrary or model-idiosyncratic, the tuning loop would need to brute-force search over all formats for every model. Because the choice is domain-predictable, the search space can be pruned ("customized search space based on our experiment result"), making the workflow tractable at scale.

**Evidence anchor.** Table 2's domain-specific pass rates and Table 5's mixed-format accuracy improvements provide the quantitative backbone. The mixed-format results are particularly telling: assigning E4M3 to activation (range) and E3M4 to weight (precision) within the same model improves over either single format for selected NLP models (Table 5), confirming that the domain-level recommendation emerges from per-tensor distribution analysis rather than being an arbitrary heuristic.

### Innovation 3: Expanded Operator Coverage as a Distinct Advantage of Floating-Point Quantization

A third conceptual contribution is the demonstration that **FP8 formats enable quantization of operator types that INT8 cannot handle**, transforming quantization from a matmul-only optimization into an end-to-end inference pipeline transformation. This is not merely an accuracy improvement on existing quantized operators—it is a *qualitative expansion* of what can be quantized in a neural network.

**Intellectual move.** The paper identifies that while matrix multiplications (Conv, Linear) dominate FLOP count, **memory-bound operations** (LayerNorm, BatchNorm, element-wise Add/Mul) dominate memory traffic and can become the bottleneck in quantized inference pipelines. When these operations remain in FP16 while surrounding operations use INT8, the system incurs format conversion overhead at every memory-bound op boundary—loading quantized data, upcasting, computing, and potentially downcasting again. The "expanded operator coverage" finding shows that FP8 formats eliminate this overhead by quantizing the memory-bound operations directly, enabling **homogeneous 8-bit pipelines** where data stays in FP8 through the entire network.

This is a departure from the standard quantization narrative, which focuses exclusively on compressing weights and accelerating matmuls. The paper argues that operator coverage is an equally important dimension of quantization quality—not just "how accurate are the quantized matmuls?" but "what fraction of the total execution time can be kept in 8-bit?" This reframes the quantization problem from a layer-level optimization to a graph-level optimization.

**Comparison to prior work.** The INT8 literature has a long history of failed attempts to quantize normalization and element-wise operations. Bhandare et al. (2019) and Kim et al. (2021) attempted integer-only BERT quantization but "were unsuccessful in maintaining the model accuracy" for LayerNorm and similar operations (Section 3.2). The standard INT8 deployment recipe—reflected in frameworks like PyTorch's quantization API and TensorRT's INT8 mode—keeps normalization layers in FP16/FP32, accepting the mixed-precision overhead as unavoidable. The paper's claim that FP8 "can handle these operators without sacrificing model accuracy" (Section 3.2) is therefore a **direct refutation of a long-standing assumption** in the quantization community that normalization operations are inherently quantization-hostile.

**Why this matters.** The practical consequence is significant: in transformer architectures, LayerNorm appears after every multi-head attention sublayer and every feed-forward sublayer. A typical BERT-base model has 12 transformer layers × 2 sublayers = 24 LayerNorm operations per forward pass, plus additional LayerNorms in the embedding and pooler layers. Each LayerNorm reads its input tensor, computes mean and variance reductions, normalizes, and applies affine parameters—all memory-bound operations. Keeping these in FP16 while the surrounding Linear layers use INT8 creates 24+ format transitions per forward pass, each requiring data movement and conversion. FP8's ability to keep LayerNorm in 8-bit eliminates these transitions, potentially yielding end-to-end speedups that exceed what matmul quantization alone could achieve. The paper does not report end-to-end latency measurements (a notable omission), but the architectural argument is sound.

**Evidence anchor.** Figure 9 provides the incremental ablation: on NLP models, adding BatchMatMul/MatMul, then Embedding/EmbeddingBag, then LayerNorm to the set of quantized operators shows E4M3 maintaining tight accuracy loss (within ~1-2%) while INT8 shows larger degradation, particularly on LayerNorm addition. The "E4M3 Static" panel in Figure 9(b) is the key visualization—the accuracy loss distribution barely shifts as operators are added, demonstrating that FP8's operator coverage expansion comes at negligible accuracy cost.

---

### Innovation 4: Mixed FP8 Formats Within a Single Model as a Distribution-Matching Strategy

While prior work explored using multiple FP8 formats for training (Sun et al., 2019 used E5M2 and E4M3 for different tensors during training), this paper contributes the insight that **assigning different FP8 formats to different tensors within the same inference model based on their distributional role—range-bounded vs. precision-bounded—is a systematic strategy for matching representational capacity to local data characteristics**. This generalizes beyond the training-specific gradient/activation/weight distinction into a domain- and tensor-type-aware allocation framework.

**Intellectual move.** The paper decomposes every tensor in a network along one axis: does this tensor contain outliers that require dynamic range (range-bounded), or is its distribution compact near zero (precision-bounded)? This binary classification—illustrated in Figure 3—maps directly to format choice: range-bounded tensors get formats with more exponent bits (E4M3 or E5M2), precision-bounded tensors get formats with more mantissa bits (E3M4). The mixed-format strategy is then simply: classify each tensor, assign accordingly.

The intellectual contribution is not the idea of using two formats (which Sun et al. already did), but the **diagnostic criterion** for assignment. Sun et al.'s training work assigned formats based on tensor *role* (gradient vs. weight vs. activation) under the assumption that role determines distribution. This paper's scheme assigns formats based directly on the *observed distribution* (range-bounded vs. precision-bounded), which is more fundamental and more generalizable—it would work for any tensor in any architecture regardless of its semantic role, as long as the distribution can be characterized.

**The Funnel case study and its implications.** Table 5's most striking result is Funnel on MRPC: single E3M4 collapses to 0.3704 accuracy (vs. 0.9225 FP32), while mixed formats achieve 0.9233, slightly *exceeding* FP32. This is a **diagnostic story in miniature**: E3M4's max representable value is 30 (Table 1). Funnel's activations apparently contain values exceeding 30 that get catastrophically clipped under single E3M4 quantization, destroying the model's ability to make predictions. Mixed formats assign E4M3 to those outlier-heavy activations (max = 448, sufficient to avoid clipping) while keeping E3M4 for the precision-bounded weights. The result is not just recovery of FP32 accuracy, but a slight improvement—likely because quantization noise acts as a regularizer.

This case study demonstrates something non-obvious: that **format mismatch on a single tensor type can cause complete model failure**, and that mixed formats can resolve this without any change to the model architecture, training procedure, or calibration method—just by allocating representational resources where the data demands them.

**Evidence anchor.** Figure 8's MSE analysis on BERT-base (MRPC) provides the mechanistic evidence: mixed formats (E4M3 for input activation + E3M4 for weight) reduce the output MSE by roughly 6× compared to the next-best single format (E4M3 for both). The paper quantifies this in absolute numbers: output MSE drops from 5,617 (E4M3 single) to 919 (mixed). Table 5 then shows that this MSE reduction translates to accuracy gains: Bert-Base MRPC recovers to exact FP32 accuracy (0.9069), Bert-Large RTE improves by 0.0036 over single E4M3, and Longformer MRPC improves by 0.0030.

### Innovation 5: The Difficulty of Format Selection Is Data-Dependent, Not Model-Dependent

A subtler but practically important insight emerges from the **negative results and distributional analyses** throughout the paper: the difficulty of quantizing a model to FP8 is determined not by the model's architecture or size per se, but by the *distributional properties of its tensors*. This decouples quantization difficulty from model scale and architecture complexity, and recasts it as a data characterization problem.

**Intellectual move.** Figure 5 plots accuracy loss by model size on both CV and NLP workloads, with model size encoded as ball diameter (on a logarithmic scale). The key pattern visible in these plots is that **model size does not predict quantization success**. Large models (Bloom-176B, LLaMA-65B) achieve comparable or better FP8 accuracy than some small NLP models, while certain small CV models (EfficientNet-B0, MobileNetV3) are among INT8's worst failures. The failures are concentrated in models with specific activation distribution characteristics (extreme outliers for NLP, unusual activation patterns for EfficientNet/MobileNet), not in models of particular sizes.

This matters because the prevailing intuition—amplified by the LLM quantization literature (Dettmers et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2022)—was that larger models are *harder* to quantize because their activations contain larger and more frequent outliers. The paper's data shows this intuition is **wrong for FP8**. Large LLMs (Bloom-176B, LLaMA-65B) quantize to FP8 with near-zero accuracy loss (Table 3: Bloom-176B E4M3 achieves 0.6757 vs. 0.6777 FP32, a 0.30% relative loss; LLaMA-65B E4M3 achieves 0.7914 vs. 0.7908 FP32, a 0.08% relative *gain*). The outliers in these large models are large in absolute magnitude, but FP8's tapered precision handles them naturally—the format's dynamic range covers the outliers while its precision near zero covers the dense central distribution.

**Comparison to prior work.** The INT8 LLM quantization literature (LLM.int8() by Dettmers et al., 2022; SmoothQuant by Xiao et al., 2022) explicitly frames large language models as the *hard case* for quantization, requiring specialized techniques (mixed-precision decomposition for outlier features, mathematical smoothing transformations). The paper's finding that these same models quantize trivially to FP8 E4M3—without any of these specialized techniques—implies that **the perceived difficulty of LLM quantization was an artifact of INT8's format limitations, not an intrinsic property of large models**. This is a significant reframing: LLMs are not hard to quantize; they are hard to quantize *to INT8*. FP8 makes the problem straightforward.

**Evidence anchor.** Table 3's large-model results (Bloom-7B1, Bloom-176B, LLaMA-65B) show FP8 E4M3 and E3M4 achieving accuracy within 1% of FP32 on all three models, with several configurations (Bloom-176B E4M3 at 0.6757 vs. 0.6777; LLaMA-65B E4M3 at 0.7914 vs. 0.7908) showing negligible differences. Figure 5's size-stratified scatter plots further support the decoupling: FP8 accuracy loss shows no systematic trend with model size in either CV or NLP panels.

**Practical significance.** This finding has direct consequences for deployment planning. If quantization difficulty scales with model size, then deploying larger models would require proportionally more quantization engineering effort—a scaling law for quantization difficulty. The paper's data suggests this is false for FP8: a 176B-parameter model is approximately as easy to quantize as a 7B-parameter model of the same architecture. This removes a barrier to deploying very large models in 8-bit precision, and suggests that FP8 quantization workflows developed on smaller models will transfer to larger ones without modification.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on more than 20 different datasets spanning 200+ tasks. For NLP, representative datasets include lambada-openai (language modeling accuracy), mrpc, cola, sts-b, sst2 (text classification tasks), samsum (summarization), WMT EN RO (machine translation), and wikitext (language modeling). For CV, datasets include ImageNet ILSVRC 2012 and CIFAR-10 (image classification), Kaggle Carvana Image Masking Challenge (image segmentation), and COCO2014 (object detection). For speech, LibriSpeech is used. For recommendation, Criteo Terabyte is used. All evaluations use standard dataset splits as provided by their respective benchmarks.

- **Base model(s).** The study covers 75 unique model architectures drawn from "a combination of diversity and popularity from mainstream hubs such as Hugging Face Models and Torch Vision, as well as individual models from Github based on their popularity" (Section 4.1). NLP models include Bloom (7B1 and 176B), LLaMA (65B), Bert (Base and Large), DistilBert, Longformer, Funnel, MarianMT, DialogGPT, Pegasus, and Xlm-Roberta-Base, among others. CV models include ResNet, DenseNet, VGG, GoogleNet, ShuffleNet, EfficientNet, MobileNet, ViT, U-Net, and YoloV3. Speech models include HuBERT and wav2vec 2.0. The recommendation model is DLRM. The models were "selected randomly from a pool" (Section 4.1) to ensure diversity rather than cherry-picking favorable cases. There is no single base model family—the diversity of architectures is itself the object of study to demonstrate the workflow's generality.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is **workload pass rate**: the percentage of models that achieve ≤1% relative accuracy loss compared to their FP32 baseline. "Relative loss" means a model with FP32 accuracy `A` passes if its quantized accuracy is at minimum `A × (1 - 0.01)`. This is stricter than absolute accuracy difference—for a model with 0.50 FP32 accuracy, the allowable absolute drop is only 0.005. Individual model accuracy is reported using the standard metric for each task: accuracy for classification tasks, FID score (lower is better) for Stable Diffusion image generation, and task-specific metrics for others. The paper also reports qualitative metrics for generative tasks—example text outputs and example generated images are provided for subjective comparison.

- **Baselines.** The primary quantitative baseline is **INT8 quantization** using best practices: static quantization for CV models and dynamic quantization for NLP models (Section 4.2.1, Table 2). For NLP INT8 baselines, SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022) is enabled "with the default smoothing alpha value" and the paper notes "alpha tuning is out of scope in this paper" (Section 4.2.1). All FP8 formats (E5M2, E4M3, E3M4) are evaluated in both static and dynamic configurations against their respective INT8 equivalents. The FP32 model serves as the accuracy reference for computing relative loss. There is no direct comparison against other FP8 quantization pipelines (e.g., the recipe from Micikevicius et al., 2022), nor against non-uniform INT8 methods or quantization-aware training approaches.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not measure or report inference latency, throughput, or FLOP counts. The comparison between FP8 and INT8 is based purely on **model accuracy** under the assumption that both formats reduce memory bandwidth and storage requirements equivalently (both are 8-bit). The operator coverage expansion (Section 3.2, Figure 9) implicitly accounts for compute coverage—quantizing more operator types yields greater end-to-end speedup—but no quantitative speedup or memory reduction numbers are reported. The calibration cost for computing scale factors (one forward pass over calibration data) is not quantified separately, though it is a fixed offline cost. The automatic tuning loop's search cost is not discussed.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report cross-validation, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests. The 75 models are evaluated once each under each configuration, and pass/fail is a binary determination based on a 1% relative loss threshold. No multi-seed or repeated measurement protocol is described. For calibration-dependent steps (range calibration, BatchNorm calibration), the calibration data is sampled once. The paper does not report variance across calibration data samples or across random seeds.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Pass Rate Comparison: FP8 vs. INT8

The headline result, presented in Table 2, is the workload pass rate across all 75 models. **FP8 E4M3 static achieves 92.64% overall pass rate, compared to INT8's 65.87%**—a 26.77 percentage point advantage. This means over one-quarter of all evaluated models that fail under INT8 quantization succeed under FP8 E4M3 without any format-specific tuning beyond the standard quantization scheme.

Breaking this down by domain:
- **NLP models:** E4M3 static achieves 96.32% pass rate vs. INT8 (dynamic NLP)'s 67.65%—a 28.67 percentage point gap. This is the domain where FP8 shows its strongest advantage.
- **CV models:** E3M4 (both static and dynamic) achieves 78.95% pass rate vs. INT8 (static CV)'s 57.89%—a 21.06 percentage point gap. The advantage is substantial but smaller than for NLP.

**Format ranking within FP8.** Across all domains, the pass rate ordering is: E4M3 static (92.64%) > E3M4 static (90.04%) = E3M4 dynamic (90.04%) > E4M3 dynamic (88.74%) > E5M2 (74.89%). The E5M2 result—74.89% pass rate without any range calibration—is notable because E5M2 uses "direct quantization" (Section 3), meaning no scale factor computation or calibration data is needed at all. It already exceeds INT8's 65.87% despite having the lowest precision (only 2 mantissa bits) of any format tested, demonstrating that dynamic range alone can solve a significant fraction of the INT8 failure cases.

**Domain-format interaction.** The pass rate ordering differs by domain:
- **NLP:** E4M3 static (96.32%) > E3M4 static (92.11%) = E3M4 dynamic (92.11%) > E4M3 dynamic (92.11%) > E5M2 (78.42%) > INT8 (67.65%).
- **CV:** E3M4 static (78.95%) = E3M4 dynamic (78.95%) > E4M3 static (73.68%) > E4M3 dynamic (71.05%) > INT8 (57.89%) > E5M2 (55.26%).

The crossover—E4M3 dominates NLP while E3M4 dominates CV—is the paper's primary evidence for domain-conditioned format selection. E5M2's reversal is particularly informative: on CV, E5M2 (55.26%) actually *underperforms* INT8 (57.89%), suggesting that for compact-activation CV models, extreme dynamic range at the expense of precision is harmful.

#### Accuracy Loss Variability

Figure 4 presents box-plot-style visualizations of accuracy loss dispersion across models for each format, separated by CV and NLP. The key patterns:

- **INT8 shows higher variability** than FP8 formats on both CV and NLP workloads. For CV, INT8's accuracy loss extends beyond 6% for some models (the upper whisker and outlier points in Figure 4, left panel), while E4M3 and E3M4 cluster tightly within 0–2% loss with "very few outliers." The paper explicitly names EfficientNet, MobileNetV3, and ViT as models where INT8 is particularly ineffective (Figure 4 caption).

- **E4M3 and E3M4 show less variability** than E5M2. The tradeoff between range and precision is visible: E5M2's low precision (2 mantissa bits) causes wider accuracy loss variation even though it eliminates clipping failures. E4M3 and E3M4's higher mantissa bit counts produce tighter accuracy distributions.

- **NLP models show tighter FP8 distributions than CV models.** This is partially explained by the fact that NLP models in the study are predominantly transformer-based architectures where the outlier problem (which FP8 handles well) is the dominant failure mode, while CV models include diverse architectures (CNNs, vision transformers, EfficientNet with squeeze-and-excitation) with varied sensitivity patterns.

#### Representative Individual Model Accuracy

Table 3 reports accuracy for 10 representative models spanning both domains and model sizes, comparing FP32 baseline against all three FP8 formats and INT8. The bold formatting in the table indicates configurations achieving ≤1% relative loss.

Key observations from Table 3:

- **Large NLP models quantize cleanly to FP8.** Bloom-176B E4M3 achieves 0.6757 vs. 0.6777 FP32 (a 0.30% relative loss); LLaMA-65B E4M3 achieves 0.7914 vs. 0.7908 FP32 (a 0.08% relative *gain*). Bloom-7B1 E4M3 achieves 0.5748 vs. 0.5764 (0.28% loss). All three large models pass the 1% criterion easily under FP8.

- **INT8 failures are visible on specific models.** Bert-Base on STS-B drops from 0.8975 (FP32) to 0.8809 (INT8), a 1.85% relative loss exceeding the threshold. DistilBert on MRPC drops from 0.8916 to 0.9042—this is actually a *gain* over FP32, likely a regularization effect (the paper reports similar gains elsewhere, e.g., Funnel mixed formats achieving 0.9233 vs. 0.9225 FP32 in Table 5). LLaMA-65B INT8 drops catastrophically to 0.7155 vs. 0.7908 FP32, a 9.52% relative loss—this is the clearest single-model failure case illustrating why INT8 is inadequate for large LLMs despite SmoothQuant being enabled.

- **E3M4 and INT8 can produce anomalous improvements.** Bert-Large COLA improves from 0.6257 (FP32) to 0.6282 (E3M4) and 0.6389 (INT8). DistilBert MRPC improves across all FP8 formats. These likely reflect the well-known regularization effect of quantization noise, where small perturbations can improve generalization, but the paper does not analyze this phenomenon.

#### Model Size and Quantization Difficulty

Figure 5 plots accuracy loss against model size (log-scale, represented by ball diameter) for all 75 models, separated by CV (top panel) and NLP (bottom panel). The model size categories are: tiny (≤32 MB), small (32–384 MB), medium (384–512 MB), and large (>512 MB).

The primary finding is that **model size does not predict quantization success for FP8 formats**. In the NLP panel (Figure 5, bottom), FP8 accuracy loss shows no systematic trend with increasing model size—the E4M3 and E3M4 points remain clustered within 0–2% loss from the smallest to the largest models. INT8, by contrast, shows increased variability at larger sizes, with some large models experiencing 4–8% accuracy loss.

In the CV panel (Figure 5, top), the pattern is similar: FP8 formats maintain consistent accuracy loss across all size ranges, while INT8 shows large accuracy losses (6–10%) for selected models across the small-to-medium size range—these are the EfficientNet, MobileNetV3, and ViT failures noted in the Figure 4 caption.

The paper's interpretation (discussed in Section 4.3 and implicit in these plots) is that quantization difficulty is **distribution-dependent, not size-dependent**—a model fails under INT8 not because it is large, but because its tensor distributions contain characteristics (outliers, unusual activation ranges) that INT8's uniform grid cannot accommodate.

#### Generative Quality: Stable Diffusion

Figure 6 shows images generated by Stable Diffusion with the prompt "A photo of an astronaut riding a horse on Mars" under FP32, three FP8 formats (with static and dynamic variants), and INT8 (static and dynamic). The paper uses FID score (lower is better) to quantify generation quality.

FID scores:
- FP32 reference: FID 45
- E3M4 static: FID 36 (best, *lower than FP32*)
- E3M4 dynamic: FID 40
- E4M3 static: FID 43
- E4M3 dynamic: FID 40
- E5M2 static: FID 50
- E5M2 dynamic: FID 51
- INT8 static: FID 86 (worst)
- INT8 dynamic: FID 58

The pattern is clear: **all FP8 formats achieve better FID scores than INT8**, with E3M4 static actually improving over the FP32 baseline (36 vs. 45). INT8 static produces a severely degraded image (FID 86). The paper notes that "FP8 formats achieve superior image quality compared to INT8, as indicated by the green arrow" and that "E4M3 and E3M4 produce smoother images and generate more intricate details, particularly on the astronaut" (Section 4.2.2). Appendix Figures 11 and 12 provide additional samples with different prompts ("A delicious ceviche cheesecake slice" and "The spirit of a tamagotchi wandering in the city of Paris"), showing similar patterns: FP8 formats consistently produce better FID scores than INT8, with E3M4 and E4M3 typically outperforming E5M2.

The Stable Diffusion workflow also demonstrates the **expanded operator coverage** capability: the FP8 configurations in Figure 6 quantize Conv2D, Linear, and LayerNorm (as indicated by the operator labels in the figure), with only the last Linear layer kept in higher precision. The paper labels indicate which ops are quantized: "FP8 Ops: Conv2d, Linear - Last Linear" in some configurations, and "+ LayerNorm" in others, showing incremental operator coverage.

#### Generative Quality: Text Generation with Bloom

Table 4 (in the main text) and Table 7 (in the Appendix) show text generated by Bloom with 32 input tokens and beam search of size 4. The prompt begins: "Once upon a time, there existed a little girl, who liked to have adventures..."

The key qualitative differences:

- **FP32 output** (Table 7): The model produces a coherent narrative about the girl going on a trip, encountering obstacles (no flight, no bus, no train, no car), with a repetitive "When she got there, she found out..." structure that is monotonous but logically connected.

- **INT8 output**: The text degenerates into extreme repetition—"She saw many strange things. She saw many strange people. She saw many strange animals. She saw many strange sights. She saw many strange smells. She saw many strange sounds..." repeated in a pattern. The paper identifies this as a failure mode where INT8 quantization causes the model to get stuck in a repetitive loop.

- **E3M4 static output** (Table 4 condensed version): "One day, she decided to go on an adventure. She packed her suitcase and went to the airport. She boarded a plane and flew to New York City. There, she met a man, and they had a great time together." This is coherent, progresses the narrative, and avoids the repetition seen in INT8.

- **E5M2 output** (Table 7): Similarly repetitive to INT8, with "they had a wonderful time" repeated five times for different locations (seaside, mountains, city, countryside, zoo). E5M2's low precision (2 mantissa bits) appears to cause a milder form of the same repetition pathology.

- **E4M3 and mixed FP8 outputs** (Table 7): These produce the most diverse and coherent narratives. Mixed FP8 output includes varied actions (took a taxi, unpacked suitcase, washed face, brushed teeth, put on makeup, went to restaurant, sat down, ordered) that form a complete scene, superior to all single-format outputs.

The paper's commentary (Section 4.2.2) notes: "E3M4 shows better response than INT8 with more comprehensive content and few repeated tokens." The full outputs in Table 7 reveal that the repetition problem is format-dependent: INT8 produces the most severe repetition, E5M2 produces moderate repetition, and E4M3/E3M4 produce coherent text. This aligns with the distribution-matching argument—formats with more mantissa bits better preserve the logit distribution that controls token sampling, reducing the probability of the model collapsing into repetitive patterns.

#### Expanded Operator Coverage Impact

Figure 9 presents the incremental accuracy impact of expanding operator coverage beyond the standard scheme (Conv, Linear) to include BatchMatMul/MatMul, Embedding/EmbeddingBag, and LayerNorm. The figure is split into CV models (Figure 9a) and NLP models (Figure 9b), with accuracy loss shown for each format as operators are added.

**CV models (Figure 9a):** The operator expansion tested is Conv/Linear → + keeping first & last ops in FP32 (the standard scheme configuration) → + removing the first & last op exception (all Conv/Linear quantized). The key findings:
- E4M3 static and dynamic maintain accuracy loss within approximately 2% across all configurations.
- E3M4 static and dynamic show slightly larger variability but remain within 3–4% for most models.
- INT8 shows 6–8% accuracy loss on some models even under the conservative standard scheme, and the variance increases with broader operator coverage.
- E5M2 shows the largest variability, with some models experiencing >8% loss—consistent with its low precision being insufficient for CV weight distributions.

**NLP models (Figure 9b):** The operator expansion is more aggressive: Conv/Linear → +BatchMatMul/MatMul → +Embedding/EmbeddingBag → +LayerNorm. The key findings:
- **E4M3 static maintains accuracy loss within approximately 1–2% across all levels of operator expansion.** The distribution of accuracy loss barely shifts as BatchMatMul, MatMul, Embedding, and LayerNorm are added to the quantized set. This is the primary evidence for the claim that FP8 "can handle these operators without sacrificing model accuracy" (Section 3.2).
- **E3M4 static shows a slight degradation** when LayerNorm is added (the rightmost bar in the E3M4 static panel extends to approximately 4.5% loss for some outlier models), suggesting that E3M4's limited dynamic range (max = 30) becomes a constraint when normalizing layers produce occasional larger activations.
- **INT8 degrades noticeably** as operators are added, particularly with LayerNorm. The INT8 panel's accuracy loss distribution widens and shifts rightward with each operator addition.
- **E4M3 dynamic and E3M4 dynamic show similar patterns** to their static counterparts but with slightly wider variance, consistent with the Table 2 finding that dynamic quantization has slightly lower pass rates than static for these formats on NLP.

This figure is central to the paper's argument that FP8 enables end-to-end 8-bit pipelines while INT8 cannot. The fact that E4M3 static can absorb LayerNorm quantization with negligible accuracy impact—where INT8 could not—directly supports the expanded operator coverage claim.

#### Mixed FP8 Formats

Figure 8 quantifies the per-tensor MSE impact of mixed FP8 formats on a Linear operator from BERT-base (MRPC task). For the input tensor, weight tensor, and output tensor, MSE is reported under single-format quantization (E5M2, E4M3, E3M4) and under mixed formats (E4M3 for input activation, E3M4 for weight).

The output MSE values illustrate the benefit:
- E5M2: output MSE = 56,132 (extremely high, reflecting low precision)
- E4M3: output MSE = 5,617
- E3M4: output MSE = 22,109 (activation outliers likely clipped, since E3M4 max = 30)
- **Mixed (E4M3 input + E3M4 weight): output MSE = 919**

The mixed-format configuration reduces output MSE by approximately 6× compared to the next-best single format (E4M3). The input tensor MSE is 0.16 (E4M3) vs. 0.13 (E3M4)—comparable, since both have sufficient precision for the activation distribution—but the weight tensor MSE is 982 (E4M3) vs. 309 (E3M4), a 3.2× improvement from the extra mantissa bit. The mixed configuration achieves the best of both: E4M3's dynamic range for the activation (MSE 0.16) and E3M4's precision for the weight (MSE 309), yielding the lowest output MSE.

Table 5 translates this MSE analysis into accuracy improvements on four NLP models. Mixed formats (E4M3 activations, E3M4 weights) achieve the highest accuracy among all FP8 configurations for Bert-Base (MRPC: 0.9069, matching FP32 exactly), Bert-Large (RTE: 0.7365 vs. 0.7329 single E4M3), and Longformer (MRPC: 0.9143 vs. 0.9113 single E4M3). The Funnel (MRPC) case is dramatic: single E3M4 collapses to 0.3704 accuracy (likely due to activation clipping at max value 30), while mixed formats achieve 0.9233, a complete recovery. The mixed configuration also outperforms E5M2 (0.9215) and single E4M3 (0.9207).

#### Static vs. Dynamic Quantization

Table 6 reports per-model accuracy improvements from switching from static to dynamic quantization for E4M3 and E3M4 formats on selected NLP models:
- Bert-Base MRPC (E4M3): 0.9072 → 0.9151 (+0.87% relative improvement)
- Bert-Base COLA (E4M3): 0.6033 → 0.6058 (+0.41%)
- Bert-Large RTE (E4M3): 0.7329 → 0.7401 (+0.98%)
- Xlm-Roberta-Base MRPC (E3M4): 0.8919 → 0.8962 (+0.48%)

These are models where the static calibration scale factors underestimated the activation range for some inputs, causing clipping that dynamic quantization avoids by computing scales per-input. The improvements are modest but consistent—dynamic quantization never reduces accuracy in these cases.

However, the overall pass rate comparison in Table 2 tells a different story: E4M3 static (92.64%) outperforms E4M3 dynamic (88.74%) in aggregate. This apparent contradiction (dynamic helps individual models but reduces aggregate pass rate) is explained by the tuning loop: dynamic quantization is applied selectively only to models that benefit, while the pass rate counts all models. Models that pass under static quantization may actually fare worse under dynamic quantization if the per-input scale factors introduce variance that static scales avoid. The tuning loop's job is to determine per-model whether the dynamic quantization benefit justifies the runtime cost.

#### BatchNorm Calibration Ablation

Figure 7 evaluates the effectiveness of BatchNorm calibration on CV models by comparing accuracy loss under different calibration configurations:
- **300 samples + training transform** (training-time data augmentations): lowest accuracy loss across most models
- **3K samples + training transform**: comparable, slightly better for some models; recommended by the paper
- **3K samples + inference transform** (center crop, resize): substantially worse than training transform at equal sample size
- **10K samples + training transform**: comparable to 3K samples, suggesting diminishing returns beyond 3K

The models shown (ResNet18 through EfficientNet-B0) exhibit varying sensitivity. ResNet18 with 300 samples + training transform shows approximately 0.5% accuracy loss, while the 3K + inference transform configuration shows approximately 2.5% for the same model. DenseNet121, DenseNet169, and EfficientNet-B0 show the largest sensitivity. The paper concludes: "We found training transform to be more effective even at smaller sample sizes (<3K). However, we recommend sample size of 3K with training transform for achieving best results across a wide range of networks" (Section 4.3.1).

The mechanism behind training transform superiority: randomized augmentations (random crops, flips, color jitter) produce more diverse activation statistics that better cover the distribution the quantized model will encounter at deployment. Inference transforms (deterministic center crop + resize) produce a narrow, canonical view that may not fully exercise the BatchNorm statistics.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Range calibration method comparison**: The paper reports (Section 3, Range Calibration paragraph) that for E4M3 and E3M4, "we found simple max scaling to be sufficient for handling outliers. We also examined more sophisticated range-calibration methods such as KL divergence, MSE error and percentile which did not provide any additional benefits." This is a negative result that simplifies the FP8 workflow: unlike INT8 where KL divergence or percentile clipping is often necessary, FP8's tapered precision makes max scaling adequate, eliminating the need to tune calibration hyperparameters. Specific accuracy numbers for the alternative calibration methods are not reported.

**First and last operator quantization**: Section 4.3.1 reports the impact of quantizing (rather than keeping in high precision) the first and last operators of convolutional networks. "For convolutional networks, quantizing the first and last operators reduced the Pass Rate for E5M2 and E4M3 formats by 25% and 15% respectively. However, E3M4 can maintain a Pass Rate of 70% even with the first and last operators quantized." This is a non-trivial finding: E3M4's higher precision (4 mantissa bits) makes it robust enough to quantize the input and output layers that are traditionally kept in FP32. The paper does not report which specific models account for the 25% and 15% pass rate drops, nor the accuracy impact on individual models.

**Alpha tuning for SmoothQuant**: The paper explicitly states that "alpha tuning is out of scope in this paper" (Section 4.2.1) and uses the default SmoothQuant alpha value for all INT8 NLP baselines. This is a significant qualification: SmoothQuant's performance is known to be sensitive to the alpha hyperparameter, which controls how much of the quantization difficulty is shifted from activations to weights. The INT8 NLP pass rate of 67.65% could potentially be higher with per-model alpha tuning, making this a conservative but fair baseline choice (the paper does not tune FP8 hyperparameters per-model either, using the standard workflow uniformly).

**SmoothQuant effect on FP8**: The paper does not apply SmoothQuant to FP8 models. This is a deliberate design choice—FP8's tapered precision is supposed to handle outliers without transformation—but it means there is no direct evidence that SmoothQuant does not further improve FP8 accuracy. If SmoothQuant could push FP8's already-high pass rate even higher, that would strengthen the case for FP8; if it degrades FP8 accuracy, that would demonstrate FP8's self-sufficiency in handling outliers. Neither hypothesis is tested.

**PRM800k dataset incompatibility (not applicable)**: This paper does not involve process reward models or the PRM800k dataset—this is a note for readers comparing across the two papers.

**Statistical rigor limitations**: The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical significance for any accuracy comparison. The pass/fail determination uses a hard 1% relative loss threshold with no sensitivity analysis (e.g., what happens to pass rates at 0.5% or 2% thresholds?). The 75 models are treated as individual data points with no accounting for architectural relatedness (e.g., Bert-Base and Bert-Large share architecture and are not independent observations). For the qualitative generation results (Stable Diffusion images, Bloom text), no inter-rater reliability or multiple-seed analysis is provided—the outputs shown are single samples.

**Calibration data quantity sensitivity**: Beyond the BatchNorm calibration study (Figure 7), the paper does not systematically vary calibration data quantity for the range calibration step or report sensitivity. The BatchNorm study suggests that 3K samples are sufficient, but whether range calibration (max scaling) requires 100, 500, or 3K samples is not investigated. This matters for deployment: smaller calibration datasets reduce offline processing cost.

**Hardware implementation consideration**: All experiments use "software emulation" (Section 4.1) with an "FP8 Emulation Toolkit, which provides a reference implementation that runs FP32 hardware." The paper does not report whether accuracy results are identical to what native FP8 hardware would produce, nor does it discuss potential discrepancies from hardware-specific rounding behavior or denormal handling. This is standard practice for pre-hardware quantization research but should be noted as a limitation for practitioners expecting identical behavior on native FP8 hardware.

**Missing end-to-end latency/throughput measurements**: The paper does not report any performance measurements—inference latency, throughput, memory bandwidth reduction, or FLOP reduction. The claims about expanded operator coverage reducing format conversion overhead (Section 3.2) and the efficiency implications of dynamic vs. static quantization are purely architectural arguments without empirical performance validation. This is the most significant missing evaluation dimension: the paper demonstrates that FP8 quantization can be accurate, but does not demonstrate that it is fast.

**Absence of comparison to FP16 or BF16 baselines**: All accuracy comparisons are against FP32. The paper does not report FP16 or BF16 accuracy as intermediate points—these formats are commonly used for inference when INT8 fails, so comparing FP8 pass rates against FP16/BF16 pass rates (which would be nearly 100%) would better contextualize the practical benefit.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "FP8 formats overall provide higher accuracy, better workload coverage compared to INT8 (92.64% vs. 65.87%)."

**What was tested:** The pass rate comparison in Table 2 covers 75 models under the standard quantization scheme (Conv/Linear/Embedding operators, per-channel weight scaling, per-tensor activation scaling, max-based calibration) with SmoothQuant enabled for INT8 NLP baselines. This is a head-to-head comparison under equivalent operator coverage and the best available INT8 techniques.

**What the experiments actually demonstrate:** The results show that FP8 E4M3 static achieves substantially higher pass rates than INT8 on this specific set of 75 models, with the largest gap on NLP models (96.32% vs. 67.65%). This is genuine evidence that FP8 is more robust to the distributional characteristics of modern architectures.

**Limitations and caveats:**
- The INT8 baseline, while using best practices (SmoothQuant with default alpha, dynamic quantization for NLP), does not include per-model hyperparameter optimization. Per-model alpha tuning for SmoothQuant could improve INT8's NLP pass rate. The gap might narrow, but the overall pattern likely holds.
- The 1% relative loss threshold is arbitrary. The paper does not report pass rates at other thresholds, so the sensitivity of the conclusion to this specific criterion is unknown.
- The 75-model set, while large and diverse, is not a random sample from any defined population. The models were "selected randomly from a pool" of popular Hugging Face and Torch Vision models, which introduces a popularity bias—architectures are included because they are widely used, not because they represent the full space of possible model designs.
- The paper does not evaluate whether INT8 quantization-aware training (QAT) could recover the failing models. This is acknowledged as out of scope (the paper studies PTQ), but it means the 65.87% figure is a PTQ-specific limitation, not an absolute statement about INT8's capability.

#### Claim 2: "FP8 formats can handle more operations such as LayerNorm and BatchNorm" (expanded operator coverage).

**What was tested:** Figure 9 shows incremental accuracy impact as operators are added to the quantized set. E4M3 static maintains accuracy with LayerNorm quantization on NLP models; INT8 degrades.

**What the experiments actually demonstrate:** The results demonstrate that E4M3 can approximately maintain accuracy when LayerNorm is quantized for the NLP models in the study, while INT8 cannot. This is strong evidence for the specific claim about LayerNorm.

**Limitations and caveats:**
- The figure shows *distributions* of accuracy loss, not per-model results. An individual model might still fail with LayerNorm quantized even under FP8—the data shows outlier points extending to ~2.5% loss for E4M3 static with full operator coverage on NLP.
- No end-to-end latency measurements are provided to quantify the performance benefit of quantizing these additional operators. The architectural argument that memory-bound operations create format conversion overhead is sound, but the magnitude of the benefit is unmeasured.
- BatchNorm quantization is only tested on CV models where BatchNorm cannot be folded. For standard ResNet-style architectures where BatchNorm folding is possible, the benefit of BatchNorm-specific FP8 quantization is irrelevant—the op is eliminated at inference time, not quantized.

#### Claim 3: "E4M3 is better suited for NLP models, whereas E3M4 performs marginally better than E4M3 on computer vision tasks."

**What was tested:** Table 2 pass rates by domain. Figure 3 activation distribution comparisons. Table 5 mixed-format accuracy improvements on NLP models.

**What the experiments actually demonstrate:** The domain-format interaction is clearly present: E4M3 NLP pass rate (96.32%) exceeds E3M4 NLP pass rate (92.11%), while E3M4 CV pass rate (78.95%) exceeds E4M3 CV pass rate (73.68%). The distributional explanation (NLP activations have outliers requiring range; CV activations are compact requiring precision) is supported by Figure 3.

**Limitations and caveats:**
- The "marginally better" characterization for CV understates the 5.27 percentage point gap—this is a meaningful difference, not marginal.
- The CV pass rates overall are substantially lower than NLP pass rates for all formats, including INT8. This suggests CV models have failure modes beyond the range-vs-precision tradeoff that the paper's distribution-based explanation does not fully capture. Only 78.95% of CV models pass even under the best FP8 format (E3M4), meaning over 20% of CV models fail PTQ entirely. The paper does not deeply analyze *which* CV models fail under FP8 or why.
- The domain distinction (NLP vs. CV) is coarse. Within NLP, there are diverse architectures (encoder-only BERT, decoder-only LLaMA, encoder-decoder MarianMT) that may have different format preferences. The study's pass rates aggregate across this diversity, potentially masking intra-domain variation.
- The recommendation to use E3M4 for CV is based on pass rate but the model-level accuracy comparisons in Table 3 show that E4M3 is competitive or better on specific CV models (ResNet-50: E4M3 0.7592 vs. E3M4 0.7604, nearly identical). The pass rate advantage may be driven by a subset of CV models where E3M4's precision is critical, while many CV models work equally well with either format.

#### Claim 4: "Mixed FP8 formats... are worthwhile exploring to produce an optimal FP8 model" (from Summary).

**What was tested:** Table 5 shows accuracy improvements on four NLP models. Figure 8 shows MSE reduction on one BERT-base operator.

**What the experiments actually demonstrate:** Mixed formats clearly help on the tested NLP models, with the Funnel case showing a dramatic recovery from 0.3704 to 0.9233. The mechanism (range for activations, precision for weights) is validated by the per-tensor MSE analysis.

**Limitations and caveats:**
- Only four NLP models are shown with mixed-format results (Table 5). The generalizability to the other 71 models is not established. Mixed formats might not help (or might even hurt) on models where single-format quantization already works well.
- Mixed formats are not systematically evaluated on CV models, where the range-vs-precision split between activations and weights might be different.
- The paper does not report whether the automatic tuning loop successfully discovers the mixed-format configuration for models that need it, or whether these results required manual format selection. This is critical for the claim that the workflow is "generalized"—if mixed formats work but must be manually configured, the automation claim is weakened.

#### Claim 5: "Our work is the first to study this problem across 200+ tasks and 75+ models demonstrating the scalability of our approach."

**What was tested:** The paper does evaluate 75 architectures on 200+ tasks with 20+ datasets.

**What the experiments actually demonstrate:** The scale of the study is genuinely large by quantization literature standards. Prior FP8 inference studies (Micikevicius et al., 2022; Kuzmin et al., 2022) evaluated at most a handful of models per domain. The claim to "first" at this scale is likely correct.

**Limitations and caveats:**
- Scale does not automatically imply rigor. The lack of statistical measures (confidence intervals, significance tests) and the absence of sensitivity analysis (varying the 1% threshold, varying calibration data quantity beyond the BatchNorm study) mean the conclusions are descriptive rather than inferential.
- "Scalability of our approach" could be interpreted as computational scalability (cost of tuning grows manageably with model count) or as robustness scalability (the approach continues to work as models grow larger). The paper provides evidence for robustness scalability (large models quantize just as well as small ones, Figure 5). It does not provide evidence for computational scalability—the tuning loop's runtime cost as a function of model count is not reported.

#### Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:

1. **Latency/throughput benchmarks.** The paper argues that FP8's expanded operator coverage yields end-to-end performance benefits by reducing format conversion overhead, but presents no wall-clock measurements. A simple comparison of ResNet-50 or BERT-base inference latency under (a) FP32, (b) INT8 with standard operator coverage, and (c) FP8 with expanded operator coverage would ground the architectural argument in empirical performance data.

2. **Comparison against FP16/BF16 accuracy.** Since FP16/BF16 are the fallback when INT8 fails, knowing how FP8 accuracy compares to these formats would complete the deployment decision tree. If FP8 accuracy is nearly indistinguishable from FP16 for most models, the case for FP8 over FP16 (which would be the actual deployment alternative, not FP32) is strong.

3. **Sensitivity to the 1% threshold.** Varying the pass/fail threshold (e.g., 0.5%, 1%, 2%) would reveal whether FP8's advantage over INT8 is robust or dependent on this specific cutoff. If INT8 improves disproportionately at relaxed thresholds, the practical advantage of FP8 may be smaller than the 92.64% vs. 65.87% numbers suggest.

4. **Per-model SmoothQuant alpha tuning for INT8.** The paper uses default alpha, noting tuning is out of scope. A single experiment with alpha-tuned INT8 on a subset of failing NLP models would indicate whether the gap is fundamental or can be partially closed with better INT8 hyperparameters.

5. **Calibration data quantity ablation for range calibration.** The BatchNorm calibration study (Figure 7) is thorough, but whether max-based range calibration for FP8 requires 100, 500, or 3K samples is not examined. If 100 samples suffice for max scaling, the calibration cost advantage over INT8 (which often needs large calibration sets for KL divergence estimation) would be an additional practical benefit.

6. **Mixed-format evaluation on CV models.** The mixed-format results are NLP-only. A corresponding CV experiment (e.g., E3M4 for weights, E4M3 for activations in a model where activation outliers exist) would test whether the mixed-format strategy generalizes across domains or is NLP-specific.

7. **Quantitative analysis of which CV models fail under FP8.** The 78.95% CV pass rate for E3M4 means ~21% of CV models fail. Identifying which architectures fail, and hypothesizing why (activation range constraints? weight precision constraints? specific operator types?), would strengthen the practical guidance and reveal FP8's current limitations.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The End-to-End Performance Benefit of FP8 Over INT8 Is Not Measured

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central value proposition is that FP8 quantization enables *efficient inference*—the title begins with "Efficient Post-training Quantization"—yet the entire experimental evaluation is based on model accuracy and pass rates. No latency, throughput, memory bandwidth, or FLOP reduction measurements are reported for any model under any configuration. This is not an oversight the paper hides; it is explicit about the evaluation scope. Section 4.1 states the experiments use "a software emulation framework" with "an FP8 Emulation Toolkit, which provides a reference implementation that runs FP32 hardware." All results are simulation-based accuracy measurements, not hardware performance measurements.

**The consequence.** A practitioner reading this paper cannot determine whether FP8 quantization actually makes their model *run faster* or *consume less memory* compared to INT8 quantization, or even compared to the FP32 baseline. The expanded operator coverage argument—that quantizing LayerNorm and element-wise ops in FP8 eliminates format conversion overhead that INT8 incurs—is architecturally sound, but the magnitude of the benefit is unquantified. It is entirely possible that, on current hardware, FP8 matrix multiplication kernels are less optimized than mature INT8 kernels, such that FP8 with expanded operator coverage is actually *slower* than INT8 with mixed-precision fallbacks despite keeping more of the graph in 8-bit. The claim that FP8 is an "efficient alternative to INT8" (Section 1) conflates representational efficiency (number of bits) with computational efficiency (wall-clock speed), and only the former is measured.

Several factors make this concern non-trivial:

- INT8 matrix multiplication kernels have been optimized for over a decade on CPUs, GPUs, and dedicated accelerators. FP8 hardware support is nascent—the NVIDIA H100 is the first widely-available GPU with native FP8 tensor core support, and its ecosystem (cuDNN, TensorRT, PyTorch integration) was immature at the time of this paper's experiments.
- The per-channel weight scaling recommended for FP8 (Section 3.1) may require different kernel implementations than per-tensor scaling, potentially trading accuracy for throughput.
- Dynamic quantization for E4M3 and E3M4 (which the paper shows can improve accuracy, Table 6) requires a runtime max-reduction over each activation tensor before quantization, adding overhead that is not present in static quantization. The paper does not quantify this overhead.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper provides zero performance measurements. The word "latency" does not appear in the paper, nor do "throughput," "speedup," "bandwidth," "FLOPS" (in a measured sense), or "wall-clock." The only performance-related claims are architectural arguments:
- Section 3.2 states that memory-bound operations "spend significant fraction of their execution time" but provides no profiling data to substantiate this for any specific model.
- Section 3.1 notes that per-channel activation scaling "may require special kernel implementations that are likely to incur higher compute overheads" but applies this caution only to INT8, not FP8.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge the absence of performance measurements as a limitation. The exclusive focus on accuracy is presented as self-evidently sufficient—the workflow aims to meet an accuracy criterion, and the assumption is that meeting this criterion enables efficient deployment. Future work is needed to close the gap between accuracy validation and performance validation, ideally on native FP8 hardware rather than software emulation. The nearest the paper comes to addressing this is listing "contribute our recipes and implementation to the open source community" as future work (Section 5), which could enable third-party performance evaluation.

---

### The Calibration and Tuning Cost Is Not Accounted For in the "Efficiency" Claim

**The assumption or constraint.** The FP8 quantization workflow involves several offline preprocessing steps whose computational cost is never quantified or amortized into the efficiency analysis:

1. **Range calibration:** Determining scale factors requires running calibration data through the FP32 model to compute max absolute values per tensor. While cheaper than training, this still requires a forward pass over potentially thousands of samples—the BatchNorm calibration study (Figure 7, Section 4.3.1) recommends 3,000 samples, and range calibration for max scaling may require comparable quantities, though the paper does not specify.

2. **BatchNorm calibration (CV models):** This requires a separate forward pass through the already-quantized model to recompute running mean and variance statistics, using 3,000 augmented samples. This is an additional computational cost on top of range calibration.

3. **Automatic tuning loop:** The extended quantization scheme involves iterative search over operator coverage, format assignment, and static vs. dynamic quantization configurations, with each candidate configuration requiring accuracy evaluation on the full validation set. The paper states the tuning "targets best accuracy, performance or Pareto optimal" (Appendix A.1) and searches over "the combination of all tune-able parameters" with a "customized search space based on our experiment result." The number of configurations evaluated per model is not reported, nor is the wall-clock time or FLOP cost of this tuning.

**The consequence.** The claimed efficiency advantage of FP8 over INT8 is based purely on pass rate (92.64% vs. 65.87%, Table 2) under the standard scheme *after* tuning. But the tuning process itself consumes computation—potentially substantial amounts if the search space is large. For a production deployment where a model is quantized once and then served millions of times, the offline tuning cost can be amortized and is largely irrelevant. However, for scenarios where models are frequently updated, where calibration data must be periodically refreshed, or where the tuning loop must be run per-hardware-target, the unaccounted cost could dominate the one-time inference savings.

More subtly: the *INT8* baseline also requires calibration and could benefit from tuning (e.g., per-model SmoothQuant alpha optimization, which the paper does not perform). If the FP8 tuning loop is more expensive than INT8 tuning (because it searches over a larger space of format combinations and operator coverage options), the fair comparison should account for the total pipeline cost (calibration + tuning + inference), not just inference accuracy.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides specific calibration recommendations (3K samples for BatchNorm, Figure 7; training-time transforms preferred, Section 4.3.1) but does not report the computational cost of these steps. The tuning loop is described procedurally (Section 3.2, Appendix A.1) but without algorithmic detail or cost quantification. The BatchNorm calibration ablation (Figure 7) compares accuracy at different sample sizes but does not report the wall-clock time per sample size.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge calibration or tuning cost as a limitation, nor does it propose methods to reduce these costs. The "standard quantization scheme" is designed to work without tuning for the majority of models (it accounts for most of the 92.64% pass rate), which reduces the fraction of models needing the expensive tuning step, but the cost of range calibration and BatchNorm calibration applies to all models regardless of whether tuning is needed. A practitioner would need to benchmark these steps independently to understand the total cost of deploying FP8 quantization.

---

### No Evidence That the Domain-Specific Format Recommendations Generalize Beyond the Studied Model Set

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's actionable recommendation—"E3M4 and E4M3 as the default FP8 format for CV and NLP models respectively" (Section 5)—is derived from pass rate comparisons on 75 models (38 NLP, 34 CV, plus speech and recommendation). While this is a large study by quantization literature standards, the model set has specific selection biases that limit the generalizability of the domain-format mapping:

1. **Selection from popularity hubs.** Section 4.1 states the models were "selected randomly from a pool of a combination of diversity and popularity from mainstream hubs such as Hugging Face Models and Torch Vision, as well as individual models from Github based on their popularity." This introduces a popularity bias—the models studied are those widely used at the time of the research, which may share architectural conventions (e.g., most NLP models are transformer-based, most CV models are CNN-based) that correlate with distributional properties. A genuinely random sample from the space of all possible architectures might show different format preferences.

2. **Architectural homogeneity within domains.** The NLP model set is dominated by BERT-family and GPT-family transformer architectures. The CV model set spans CNNs and vision transformers but is weighted toward ResNet-derived architectures. The distributional properties that drive format preference—activation outliers in NLP, compact activations in CV—may be specific to currently-popular architectures rather than intrinsic to the application domain. A future CV architecture that incorporates attention mechanisms differently might exhibit NLP-like outliers and benefit from E4M3.

3. **No held-out architecture families.** The paper evaluates its format recommendations on the same 75 models used to derive them. There is no separate validation set of "future" architectures released after the study to test whether the domain-format mapping holds for unseen model designs.

**The consequence.** A practitioner applying the E4M3-for-NLP, E3M4-for-CV recommendation to a model outside the studied set—particularly an architecture with unusual tensor distributions, a novel attention mechanism, or a non-standard normalization scheme—has no principled basis for confidence that the recommendation applies. The paper's diagnostic model (examine activation distributions for outliers—if present, use E4M3; if compact, use E3M4) partially addresses this by providing a principle rather than just a rule, but the principle has not been validated on out-of-distribution architectures.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides per-domain pass rates (Table 2) and activation distribution visualizations (Figure 3) that support the domain-format mapping for the studied models. However, there is no cross-domain outlier analysis—for example, testing whether a CV model that *does* exhibit activation outliers (perhaps a vision transformer with particular normalization choices) would benefit from E4M3 over E3M4. The paper observes that CV models have lower overall pass rates than NLP models (78.95% E3M4 vs. 96.32% E4M3), suggesting there are CV-specific failure modes not explained by the range-vs-precision narrative, but these are not analyzed.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed. The paper provides a *principle* for format selection (range-bounded vs. precision-bounded tensors, Figure 3) that can be applied to new architectures by examining their tensor distributions, rather than requiring blind trust in the domain-format mapping. However, the validation of this principle is limited to the studied model set, and the paper does not test whether practitioners, given only the distribution visualizations, would independently arrive at the same format assignments. The paper's future work (Section 5) mentions applying the recipes "to more diverse LLM models (e.g., BioGPT, Llama2 Chat, Code Llama)" but does not address the need for validation on architecturally distinct non-LLM models.

---

### The INT8 Baseline Is Not Fully Optimized, Potentially Overstating FP8's Advantage

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central comparison—FP8 vs. INT8 pass rates—depends on the INT8 baseline being representative of the best achievable INT8 PTQ accuracy. The paper configures INT8 using best practices: static quantization for CV, dynamic quantization for NLP, SmoothQuant enabled with default alpha for NLP models. However, several INT8 optimizations that could improve the baseline are explicitly excluded:

1. **SmoothQuant alpha tuning.** Section 4.2.1 states "SmoothQuant is enabled on NLP models with the default smoothing alpha value" and explicitly notes "alpha tuning is out of scope in this paper." The SmoothQuant paper (Xiao et al., 2022) demonstrates that per-model alpha tuning can substantially improve INT8 accuracy—alpha controls the migration strength of quantization difficulty from activations to weights, and the optimal value depends on the specific activation outlier characteristics of each model. Using the default alpha for all NLP models likely underserves some models where a tuned alpha would close the gap with FP8.

2. **Per-channel activation scaling exclusion.** Section 3.1 acknowledges that "per-channel activation scaling can benefit INT8 quantization" (citing Xiao et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022; Dettmers et al., 2022) but excludes it because it "may require special kernel implementations that are likely to incur higher compute overheads." The paper applies this exclusion symmetrically—FP8 also uses per-tensor activation scaling—so it is a fair comparison in terms of operator constraints. However, if the research question is "can FP8 replace INT8 as the production quantization format?", then the baseline should reflect the best INT8 accuracy achievable, even with custom kernels, since the claim is about format superiority, not kernel availability.

3. **No INT8-specific calibration tuning.** The paper reports that sophisticated calibration methods (KL divergence, MSE, percentile) "did not provide any additional benefits" *for FP8*, and therefore uses simple max scaling. For INT8, these methods *do* provide benefits—the INT8 quantization literature extensively documents accuracy improvements from KL divergence and percentile-based calibration—but the paper does not report whether these methods were applied to the INT8 baseline or whether max scaling was used for both formats to keep the comparison controlled. If max scaling was used for INT8, the baseline is weaker than what practitioners would deploy.

**The consequence.** The 92.64% vs. 65.87% pass rate gap may be partially attributable to suboptimal INT8 hyperparameters rather than fundamental format limitations. While it is unlikely that fully-optimized INT8 would close the entire gap—the Bloom-176B, LLaMA-65B, and Stable Diffusion results show FP8 achieving accuracy that seems qualitatively beyond INT8's reach—the magnitude of the advantage could be smaller than reported. This matters for practitioners making format adoption decisions: if FP8 is only marginally better than well-tuned INT8, the switching cost (new hardware requirements, immature kernel libraries, retooling deployment pipelines) might not be justified.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2's INT8 pass rates (67.65% NLP, 57.89% CV) and Figure 4's INT8 accuracy loss distributions are the relevant data. The paper does not include an ablation where INT8 alpha is tuned per-model, so the sensitivity of the pass rate to this hyperparameter is unknown. The paper also does not report which calibration method was used for INT8's scale factor computation.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through transparency—the paper explicitly states that alpha tuning is out of scope and that SmoothQuant uses default alpha. A practitioner aware of this can mentally adjust the INT8 pass rate upward to account for potential gains from tuning, though the magnitude of the adjustment is unknown without additional experiments. The paper's choice to match operator coverage (same ops quantized) and activation scaling granularity (per-tensor for both) between INT8 and FP8 is methodologically sound for a controlled comparison, even if it understates INT8's achievable accuracy with custom kernels.

---

### FP8 Quantization of Computer Vision Models Remains Substantially Less Reliable Than NLP

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper positions FP8 as a broadly superior alternative to INT8, but the pass rate data tells a more nuanced story: **FP8 excels on NLP models (96.32% E4M3 pass rate) but achieves only 78.95% pass rate on CV models (best case, E3M4)**. This means over one in five CV models fail FP8 post-training quantization under the studied workflow—still a substantial improvement over INT8's 57.89% CV pass rate, but far from the near-universal success on NLP. The assumption that FP8 is a "drop-in replacement for INT8 across the full spectrum of production models" is only partially supported for CV.

The paper does not deeply analyze *which* CV models fail under FP8 or *why*. The Figure 4 caption mentions that "INT8 shows higher variability for CV models than E4M3 and E3M4 due to its ineffectiveness on models such as EfficientNet, MobileNetV3, and ViT," but does not state which models fail under FP8. If the same models that fail INT8 (EfficientNet, MobileNetV3, ViT) also fail FP8—just with smaller accuracy degradation—then FP8's CV advantage is quantitative (less accuracy loss) rather than qualitative (making previously unquantizable models quantizable).

**The consequence.** A practitioner deploying CV models cannot rely on the 78.95% pass rate as a guarantee that their specific model will quantize successfully. The practical workflow would need to include a fallback strategy for the 21% of models that fail: either quantization-aware training (which the paper explicitly excludes from scope), keeping those models in FP16/BF16, or accepting accuracy degradation beyond 1% relative loss. The paper provides no guidance on which CV architectures are at risk of failure or what mitigation steps to take when a CV model fails FP8 PTQ.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2 provides the CV pass rates: E3M4 static 78.95%, E4M3 static 73.68%, INT8 static 57.89%. Figure 5 (top) shows accuracy loss by model size for CV models, but does not label individual model failures. Figure 9 (left) shows the distribution of accuracy loss for CV models under expanded operator coverage, but again aggregates across models.

The gap between CV and NLP pass rates is not explained by the paper's distribution-based analysis. Figure 3 shows that CV activations are "precision-bounded" (compact, no outliers), which predicts that formats with more mantissa bits (E3M4) should work well—and E3M4 does achieve the highest CV pass rate, consistent with the prediction. But the prediction does not explain why 21% of CV models still fail under E3M4. Possible explanations not explored in the paper include: (a) certain CV architectures have weight distributions that require more dynamic range than E3M4 provides; (b) the first/last operator sensitivity is more severe for CV models than anticipated; (c) BatchNorm calibration is insufficient for some architectures; or (d) some CV tasks have stricter accuracy requirements where the 1% relative loss threshold is violated by even small quantization errors.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not analyze CV failure cases, does not provide per-architecture CV results beyond the representative models in Table 3, and does not offer guidance for mitigating CV-specific FP8 failures. The future work (Section 5) focuses entirely on expanding NLP coverage ("more diverse LLM models"), with no mention of improving CV pass rates or understanding CV-specific failure modes. This is a significant gap given that CV remains a major deployment domain for quantization.

---

### The Paper Provides No Guidance on Handling Models Where Post-Training FP8 Quantization Fails

**The assumption or constraint.** The quantization workflow described in the paper (Figure 2, Sections 3.1–3.2) is a *best-effort* pipeline: it applies a standard scheme, optionally extends coverage and applies mixed formats through automatic tuning, and checks whether the quantized model meets the 1% relative accuracy threshold. If the model passes, it is deployed. If it fails, the paper provides no systematic guidance on what to do next. The extended quantization scheme's tuning loop (Section 3.2) is described as searching over operator coverage, format assignment, and static vs. dynamic quantization options—but it is framed as a procedure to *find* a working configuration, not as a procedure that might *fail to find* one. The failure case—a model where no combination of the available tuning options achieves the accuracy target—is not discussed.

**The consequence.** For models that fail FP8 PTQ (roughly 7% of NLP models and 21% of CV models under the best format choices, per Table 2), the paper leaves the practitioner with no actionable path forward. The options would logically include: (a) quantization-aware training (QAT), which the paper explicitly excludes from scope; (b) using FP16/BF16 inference, which negates the efficiency benefits; (c) accepting accuracy degradation beyond the 1% threshold, with unknown downstream consequences; or (d) manually debugging the quantization failure, which requires expertise the paper's automated workflow was designed to eliminate.

This limitation is particularly consequential because the paper frames itself as providing a *production-ready* workflow. A production deployment requires predictable behavior: the engineer needs to know, before starting the quantization process, whether their model is likely to succeed and what the recourse is if it fails. The paper provides the pass rate statistics (which answer "how likely?") but not the failure mode analysis (which would answer "what then?").

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The pass rate data in Table 2 implicitly identifies the failure fraction (100% minus pass rate). However, the paper does not:
- Identify *which specific models* fail under the best FP8 configuration.
- Characterize *why* they fail (clipping, precision loss, operator sensitivity, distribution mismatch).
- Test whether combining *all* extended scheme options (mixed formats + dynamic quantization + maximum operator coverage) can recover some of the failing models. Table 2 reports pass rates for individual format + quantization approach combinations, but does not report the pass rate after the full tuning loop has been applied.
- Test whether relaxing the 1% threshold to 2% or 5% would bring the failing models into acceptable range, and what the accuracy-impact tradeoff would be.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's conclusion (Section 5) frames the results as demonstrating "the effectiveness across 75 unique network architectures" and recommends format choices for practitioners, but does not acknowledge that a non-trivial fraction of architectures do not achieve the accuracy target. The future work focuses on expanding to more models, not on improving the workflow for models where it currently fails. This is a missed opportunity: even a brief analysis of the failure cases (e.g., "the failing CV models are predominantly lightweight architectures like MobileNetV3 and EfficientNet, suggesting that FP8's dynamic range may be insufficient for their particular activation patterns") would provide actionable guidance and suggest directions for improvement.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the field's default mental model for post-training quantization away from **mitigation** and toward **format selection**. The decades-long INT8 research tradition treated the uniform integer grid as a fixed constraint and developed increasingly sophisticated workarounds—KL-divergence clipping, SmoothQuant-style outlier redistribution, per-channel activation scaling with custom kernels—each addressing a specific failure mode without questioning whether the format itself was the right choice. The paper's core reframing is that the format is a design variable, not a constraint: given a family of FP8 representations spanning different dynamic range vs. precision tradeoffs (E5M2, E4M3, E3M4), the problem reduces to choosing the right format for the right tensor, guided by a simple diagnostic (range-bounded vs. precision-bounded distributions, Figure 3). This is not a paradigm shift in the Kuhnian sense—the quantization workflow itself (scale factors, calibration, operator selection) is structurally similar to INT8 pipelines—but it is a **methodological reframing** that collapses an entire subfield of outlier mitigation research into a format-selection decision. The finding that "sophisticated range-calibration methods such as KL divergence, MSE error and percentile… did not provide any additional benefits" (Section 3) over simple max scaling for FP8 is the clearest evidence: once the format handles outliers natively, the calibration problem simplifies dramatically.

The paper also **reconciles a standing contradiction** in the quantization literature: why do some models quantize easily to INT8 while superficially similar models fail catastrophically? The INT8 literature provided case-by-case explanations (outlier magnitude, LayerNorm amplification, weight distribution skew), but the underlying thread was elusive. This paper's per-domain pass rates (Table 2) and distributional analysis (Figure 3) provide a unified explanation: failures concentrate in models whose tensor distributions are mismatched to the uniform grid—NLP models with activation outliers, CV models like EfficientNet and MobileNetV3 with unusual activation patterns. FP8's tapered precision resolves the mismatch, and the residual failures (the ~21% of CV models that still fail E3M4) point to distributional characteristics beyond the range-vs-precision axis that merit further investigation. This explanation is more satisfying than the INT8 literature's catalog of failure modes because it is **mechanistic rather than empirical**: it says *why* a model fails based on measurable distributional properties, not just *that* it fails.

The most consequential redirection of research attention is away from **sophisticated INT8 calibration and outlier mitigation algorithms** and toward **verifier-quality-like questions for FP8**: format selection, operator coverage expansion, and hardware-software codesign. If FP8 with max scaling and per-tensor activation quantization achieves 92.64% pass rate across 75 diverse architectures (Table 2), the marginal return on developing yet another INT8 clipping algorithm shrinks substantially. Research investment shifts toward questions the paper opens but does not answer: how to predict format suitability without running the full quantization pipeline, how to handle the CV models that still fail FP8, and how to codesign FP8 kernels that make the expanded operator coverage benefit real in wall-clock terms. The INT8 research community does not become obsolete—there are deployment scenarios where INT8 hardware is the only option—but the burden of proof shifts: a new INT8 method must now demonstrate that it closes the gap with FP8, not just that it improves over vanilla INT8.

Perhaps the most understated implication is for **hardware roadmap decisions**. The paper provides large-scale evidence that FP8 formats are not merely viable but *superior* to INT8 for post-training quantization of modern architectures. For hardware vendors deciding which 8-bit formats to support in next-generation accelerators, this tilts the balance toward including native FP8 tensor core support (as NVIDIA did with H100) rather than investing solely in INT8. The finding that E4M3 and E3M4 serve complementary domains (NLP vs. CV) further suggests that hardware should support multiple FP8 formats, not a single one—a design choice with silicon area and instruction set implications.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**End-to-end latency and throughput benchmarking of FP8 vs. INT8 on native hardware.** The paper's most glaring omission is the absence of any performance measurement—latency, throughput, memory bandwidth reduction, or energy consumption. A direct follow-up would benchmark a representative subset of the 75 models (e.g., ResNet-50, BERT-base, LLaMA-7B, Stable Diffusion) on hardware with native FP8 support (NVIDIA H100 or equivalent), comparing three configurations: FP32 baseline, INT8 with best-practice calibration (SmoothQuant + per-model alpha tuning + dynamic quantization for NLP), and FP8 with the paper's recommended workflow (standard scheme + extended scheme with operator coverage expansion). The key measurements would be: (a) end-to-end inference latency at batch size 1 (for interactive applications) and batch size 32 (for throughput-oriented serving), (b) peak memory consumption, and (c) the latency contribution of memory-bound operators (LayerNorm, element-wise ops) under each configuration, to quantify the benefit of expanded operator coverage. This experiment would directly test the paper's central claim that FP8 is an "efficient alternative to INT8" in the sense that matters for deployment—wall-clock speed—rather than just representational efficiency.

**Characterization of CV models that fail FP8 post-training quantization.** The paper reports that 21% of CV models fail even the best FP8 configuration (E3M4, 78.95% pass rate, Table 2) but provides no analysis of which models fail or why. A focused diagnostic study would take the failing CV models, profile their tensor distributions at every layer, and identify the specific failure mechanism: Is it activation clipping (values exceeding E3M4's max of 30)? Weight precision loss (distributions requiring more than 4 mantissa bits)? First/last operator sensitivity that persists despite keeping those layers in FP32? BatchNorm calibration insufficiency? The experimental design would involve: (a) per-layer quantization with accuracy measurement to localize which operators cause the degradation, (b) per-tensor max value logging to identify cases where E3M4's dynamic range (max value 30) is exceeded, and (c) testing whether switching specific tensors to E4M3 (relaxing the range constraint) or E5M2 (eliminating it entirely) recovers accuracy. The output would be a taxonomy of CV failure modes under FP8, which would inform format selection guidance (the paper currently recommends E3M4 uniformly for CV, but the failures suggest this is insufficient for ~21% of models) and potentially motivate per-tensor mixed-format strategies for CV analogous to what the paper demonstrates for NLP (Table 5).

**Validation of the format selection diagnostic on out-of-distribution architectures.** The paper's domain-format mapping (E4M3 for NLP, E3M4 for CV) is derived from pass rates on 75 models that share architectural conventions (BERT-family transformers for NLP, ResNet-family CNNs for CV). A stress test would apply the workflow to architecturally distinct models not represented in the study: vision-language models (e.g., CLIP, which combines transformer text encoders with vision encoders), graph neural networks, speech models beyond HuBERT/wav2vec (e.g., Whisper with its encoder-decoder architecture), and multimodal models (e.g., LLaVA). For each, the experiment would: (a) characterize per-tensor distributions to predict which FP8 format should work best, (b) apply the standard quantization scheme and measure pass rate, (c) test whether the distribution-based prediction matches the empirical best format, and (d) if the prediction fails, analyze why (e.g., does a vision transformer with certain normalization choices exhibit NLP-like activation outliers?). This would test whether the paper's diagnostic model (range-bounded vs. precision-bounded, Figure 3) generalizes beyond the studied architectures or whether it is an artifact of the specific architectural conventions in the 75-model set.

**Quantifying the tradeoff between calibration dataset size and FP8 quantization accuracy.** The paper recommends 3,000 samples with training-time transforms for BatchNorm calibration (Section 4.3.1, Figure 7) but does not systematically vary calibration dataset size for the range calibration step (max scaling) that applies to all FP8 formats. A practical calibration cost study would measure: for a representative set of 10 models (5 NLP, 5 CV), sweep calibration dataset sizes from 1 to 10,000 samples, measure the resulting quantized model accuracy, and determine the sample size at which accuracy saturates for each format. The hypothesis is that FP8 max scaling requires substantially fewer calibration samples than INT8 KL-divergence calibration because it does not need to estimate distribution shape—only the maximum absolute value. If max scaling saturates at, say, 100 samples while INT8 KL calibration requires 1,000+, this would be an additional practical advantage of FP8 (faster offline calibration) that the paper does not currently claim. The experiment would also test whether the calibration dataset needs to be domain-representative (e.g., does ImageNet calibration data work for a fine-tuned downstream CV model?) or whether a generic dataset suffices for max-based scaling.

**Applying the FP8 quantization workflow to quantization-aware training (QAT) for the models that fail PTQ.** The paper explicitly restricts itself to post-training quantization, but the 7.36% of NLP models and 21.05% of CV models that fail FP8 PTQ (Table 2) represent a deployment gap. A natural extension would test whether applying the same FP8 workflow in a QAT setting—where the model is fine-tuned for a small number of steps with simulated FP8 quantization—can recover the failing models. The experiment would: (a) select the 5-10 worst-failing models from the study (those with >2% relative accuracy loss under the best FP8 configuration), (b) apply QAT with the paper's standard quantization scheme (per-channel weight scaling, per-tensor activation scaling, max calibration) for a small number of epochs on the training data, (c) measure whether the quantized accuracy reaches the 1% threshold, and (d) compare the QAT cost (training FLOPs, wall-clock time) against the alternative of keeping the model in FP16. This would determine whether the FP8 format advantage extends to the QAT regime, potentially enabling FP8 deployment for models that cannot be quantized post-training, and would provide a complete deployment decision tree: PTQ first; if it fails, try QAT; if that fails, fall back to FP16.

**Cross-hardware reproducibility of FP8 quantization accuracy.** The paper uses software emulation (FP8 Emulation Toolkit running on FP32 hardware, Section 4.1) and acknowledges that "hardware implementation consideration" is a limitation. Different hardware implementations of FP8 may use different rounding modes (round-to-nearest-even vs. round-to-nearest vs. stochastic rounding), different denormal handling policies (flush-to-zero vs. gradual underflow), and different fused operation semantics (e.g., whether a matrix multiplication accumulates in higher precision before rounding to FP8). A reproducibility study would quantize 5 representative models (e.g., ResNet-50, BERT-base, LLaMA-7B, Stable Diffusion, DLRM) using the paper's workflow on at least three hardware platforms or emulation frameworks: the paper's FP8 Emulation Toolkit, NVIDIA H100 native FP8 tensor cores via cuDNN/PyTorch, and an independent FP8 emulation library (e.g., the one from Micikevicius et al., 2022). The goal is to establish whether the paper's accuracy numbers are portable across implementations or specific to its emulation framework. Discrepancies would reveal which aspects of FP8 quantization are implementation-sensitive (likely rounding and denormal handling) and inform the standardization of FP8 inference behavior.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient serving of large language models in production.** Organizations deploying LLMs like LLaMA-65B or Bloom-176B for customer-facing applications face a direct tradeoff: serving in FP16 requires approximately 130 GB of memory for LLaMA-65B (65B parameters × 2 bytes), while FP8 halves this to ~65 GB. The memory reduction enables either (a) serving the same model on fewer GPUs, reducing infrastructure cost, or (b) serving a larger batch size on the same hardware, increasing throughput. The paper's Table 3 shows that LLaMA-65B quantized to E4M3 achieves 0.7914 accuracy vs. 0.7908 FP32 on lambada-openai—a 0.08% relative *improvement*—meaning the memory savings come at zero accuracy cost. For a service handling millions of queries daily, halving the GPU count directly halves the cloud compute bill. This is actionable today: the quantization recipe (per-channel weight scaling, per-tensor activation scaling, max calibration, E4M3 format) is fully specified in Section 3.1, and the workflow requires no retraining or fine-tuning.

**On-device deployment of vision models on mobile and edge hardware.** The paper shows that E3M4 quantization of CV models like MobileNetV2 and ShuffleNetV2 maintains accuracy within tight bounds (Figure 7, Figure 5 top panel), while INT8 fails on the same lightweight architectures (Figure 4). For mobile application developers deploying image classification or object detection on-device, FP8 E3M4 offers a path to 8-bit inference that INT8 could not provide for these model families. The specific benefit: a model that previously required FP16 (e.g., 14 MB for MobileNetV2) can be deployed at 7 MB in FP8 without accuracy degradation beyond the 1% threshold, fitting into tighter memory budgets on low-end devices. The BatchNorm calibration procedure (Section 4.3.1, Figure 7) provides the exact recipe: use 3,000 calibration samples with training-time data augmentation to recompute running statistics after quantization. This is production-ready for teams already using post-training quantization pipelines.

**End-to-end 8-bit inference pipelines for transformer-based applications.** The paper's expanded operator coverage capability—quantizing LayerNorm, element-wise Add/Mul, and Embedding operations in addition to Linear layers (Figure 9b, Section 3.2)—enables homogeneous FP8 inference where data remains in 8-bit format through the entire transformer forward pass. This matters for applications with strict latency budgets (real-time translation, voice assistants, interactive chatbots) where format conversion overhead at LayerNorm boundaries would add per-layer latency. A BERT-base model has 24 LayerNorm operations per forward pass; keeping these in FP16 while surrounding Linear layers use INT8 adds 24 format conversions, each requiring memory reads and type casting. FP8 eliminates these conversions by quantizing LayerNorm directly. The paper's Figure 9b shows E4M3 static maintaining accuracy within ~1-2% loss even with full operator coverage (+LayerNorm). For a deployment team building a real-time NLP pipeline, this means they can quantize the entire model graph to FP8 and achieve both the memory savings of 8-bit and the latency reduction of eliminating format transitions—without the accuracy cost that INT8 incurred when attempting the same.

**High-quality image generation with reduced memory footprint.** The Stable Diffusion results (Figure 6, Appendix Figures 11-12) demonstrate that FP8 quantization not only preserves generation quality but can slightly *improve* it (E3M4 static achieves FID 36 vs. FP32 FID 45). For creative applications generating images at scale—game asset generation, advertising creative production, architectural visualization—this means the diffusion model can run on a single GPU with half the memory, doubling the batch size and throughput, while producing subjectively equal or better images. The FID scores provide quantitative confidence: E3M4 static and dynamic achieve FID 36-40, versus INT8 dynamic at 58 and INT8 static at 86 (severely degraded). The workflow specifics (Conv2D + Linear quantized, last Linear kept in FP32, LayerNorm optionally quantized, per-channel weight scaling) are directly applicable to Stable Diffusion deployments using the paper's standard + extended scheme recipe. For a studio generating thousands of images per hour, halving the GPU memory requirement per model instance allows either running more instances on the same hardware or using cheaper GPUs with less VRAM.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper frames FP8 post-training quantization against INT8 post-training quantization as the primary alternative, with FP16/FP32 as the fallback when quantization fails entirely. The decision rules that emerge from the data are:

- **Prefer FP8 E4M3 static quantization when deploying NLP models (especially transformer-based LLMs) in production:** The 96.32% NLP pass rate under E4M3 static (Table 2) means near-certain success without tuning. Large models (Bloom-176B, LLaMA-65B) quantize with negligible accuracy loss (Table 3: ≤0.30% relative loss), making this a safe default. Use the standard quantization scheme (per-channel weight scaling, per-tensor activation scaling, max calibration, Conv/Linear/Embedding operators) and apply the extended scheme only if the 1% accuracy threshold is not met.

- **Prefer FP8 E3M4 static quantization when deploying computer vision models**, but with the explicit caveat that ~21% of CV models may still fail (Table 2: 78.95% pass rate). The paper does not provide a diagnostic to predict which CV models will fail, so deployment teams should budget for testing and potential fallback. Apply BatchNorm calibration with 3,000 training-transform-augmented samples (Figure 7) as a mandatory step—the paper shows this is the difference between passing and failing for several CV architectures.

- **Prefer INT8 only when FP8 hardware support is unavailable** or when the model-specific accuracy under well-tuned INT8 has already been validated and meets deployment requirements. The paper shows INT8 achieves only 65.87% overall pass rate (Table 2), but this is an aggregate: specific models may quantize adequately to INT8 with SmoothQuant and per-model alpha tuning (which the paper does not explore). If a deployment pipeline already has a validated INT8 configuration for a specific model, the switching cost to FP8 (new calibration, retesting, potential kernel compatibility issues) may not be justified by the accuracy improvement alone.

- **Use mixed FP8 formats (E4M3 activations + E3M4 weights) as a targeted intervention for NLP models that fail single-format quantization**, rather than as a default. The paper shows this configuration recovers models like Funnel (Table 5: from 0.3704 E3M4 to 0.9233 mixed) but only demonstrates it on four NLP architectures. The automatic tuning loop (Section 3.2) is designed to discover this configuration when needed, so manual mixed-format assignment should be unnecessary if the tuning infrastructure is in place.

- **Prefer FP16/BF16 inference over any 8-bit quantization when latency budgets are extremely tight and model size is not a constraint**, or when the model falls into the ~7% (NLP) or ~21% (CV) of cases where FP8 PTQ fails the 1% threshold and QAT is not feasible. The paper provides no latency measurements, so the wall-clock speedup from FP8 over FP16 is unquantified and may be smaller than the 2× memory bandwidth reduction would suggest, especially on hardware with immature FP8 kernel support.
