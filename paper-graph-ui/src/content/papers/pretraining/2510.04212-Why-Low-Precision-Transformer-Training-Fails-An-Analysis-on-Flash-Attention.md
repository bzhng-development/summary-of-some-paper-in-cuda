# Why Low-Precision Transformer Training Fails: An Analysis on Flash Attention

**ArXiv:** [2510.04212](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04212)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper analyzes a long-standing failure case where training transformers with flash attention in BF16 precision leads to catastrophic loss explosion, providing the first mechanistic explanation for this instability.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper analyzes a long-standing failure case where training transformers with flash attention in BF16 precision leads to catastrophic loss explosion, providing the first mechanistic explanation for this instability. Using a GPT-2 model (12 layers, 768 embedding dimension) pre-trained on OpenWebText, the authors trace the failure to two intertwined phenomena: the emergence of **similar low-rank representations** within the attention mechanism (structurally consistent matrices across tokens and training steps that prevent error cancellation) and the **compounding effect of biased rounding errors** inherent in BF16 arithmetic (systematic negative bias when adding numbers with the same sign causes overflow in the significand during the `P̄V` computation). The biased rounding errors act as coefficients for the low-rank representations, causing them to accumulate as a biased gradient update that increases the spectral norm of weights and activations, ultimately derailing training. A minimal modification to flash attention—conditionally adjusting the safe softmax normalization factor when multiple identical maxima are detected—eliminates the biased rounding and restores training stability, validating the analysis and establishing that the failure is a deterministic consequence of numerical arithmetic rather than a random artifact.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Low-Precision Flash Attention Training Spontaneously Explodes

The fundamental question this paper addresses is a deceptively simple one: **why does training a standard GPT-2 model with flash attention in BF16 precision suddenly and catastrophically fail after thousands of stable steps?** This failure manifests as a loss explosion — the validation loss jumps from normal values (around 3–4) to infinity or NaN, and the training run is effectively destroyed (Figure 2). The failure has been reported repeatedly in open-source communities for over two years (nanoGPT Issue 303, 2023; nanoGPT Issue 524, 2024; nanoGPT Issue 554, 2024; flash-attention Issue 337, 2024) and has remained without a mechanistic explanation. Users encountering this bug receive empirical advice — "switch to FP32," "don't use flash attention," or "use a different precision for the attention computation" — but no one has explained *why* these workarounds help, or what the underlying physical cause of the explosion actually is.

This gap matters for several interconnected reasons that go far beyond a single GitHub issue:

**Low-precision training is not optional — it is how modern LLMs are trained.** Training state-of-the-art models requires fitting models into GPU memory and keeping compute costs manageable. The standard approach is mixed-precision training (Micikevicius et al., 2017), where most operations run in 16-bit formats (BF16 or FP16) while a master copy of weights is maintained in FP32. BF16 has become the dominant format because it shares FP32's 8-bit exponent range, eliminating the gradient underflow problems that plague FP16 (Kalamkar et al., 2019). Every major model family — GPT-3, PaLM, LLaMA, Chinchilla, Gopher — has been trained with BF16. The paper emphasizes this ubiquity in Section A.1 of the appendix, noting that BF16 "has become the default 16-bit format for many large-scale training frameworks." If BF16 training can silently corrupt training dynamics through a mechanism nobody understands, then large-scale training runs worth millions of dollars are operating with an uncharacterized failure mode lurking in their arithmetic.

**Flash attention has become indispensable for long-context training.** The standard attention mechanism has $O(N^2)$ memory complexity with respect to sequence length $N$, making it prohibitively expensive for the long contexts (8K, 32K, 128K tokens) that modern models require. Flash attention (Dao et al., 2022; Dao, 2024) reduces this to $O(N)$ by tiling the computation and fusing operations into a single GPU kernel, avoiding the materialization of the full $N \times N$ attention score matrix. The paper notes in Section 2.2 that this has made flash attention "a cornerstone algorithm for efficient transformer training, making it indispensable for handling the long contexts required by modern large-scale models." The combination of flash attention and BF16 is effectively the default training configuration for modern LLMs. When this combination fails, the entire training pipeline is compromised.

**Existing stabilization techniques are empirical patches without theoretical grounding.** The community has developed a suite of workarounds — QK normalization (Henry et al., 2020), QK-clipping (Kimi-Team, 2025), Gated Attention (Qiu et al., 2025) — that are "often empirically linked to phenomena such as large spectral norms of weights, large activations, and attention sinks" (Section 3.1). But these fixes were developed through trial and error, not through understanding. The paper explicitly criticizes this state of affairs: "The absence of a clear causal chain from numerical error to loss explosion has left the community reliant on ad-hoc patches rather than principled solutions, hindering progress in robust low-precision training." This is a strong claim: the field is flying blind with respect to numerical stability, applying fixes that work for reasons nobody fully understands.

**The industry is pushing toward even lower precision (FP8, FP4).** The paper situates its analysis in a broader trajectory. As Section 1 notes, "in industrial practice, it is common to use BF16 for memory-bound operations like flash attention while pushing compute-bound operations like FFNs to even lower precisions such as FP8." If the community cannot explain why BF16 flash attention fails, moving to even more aggressive quantization (FP8 training, FP4 training) will only amplify these unknown failure modes. Wang et al. (2025) and Tseng et al. (2025) are already exploring FP4 training; Fishman et al. (2024) and Peng et al. (2023) are working on FP8. Understanding the BF16 failure case is therefore not just about fixing one bug — it is about establishing an analytical framework that can *scale down* to lower precisions and prevent similar catastrophes before they occur in production training runs.

### Prior Approaches and Their Limitations

**The prevailing diagnosis: "numerical instability."** The community's understanding of this failure has been vague and phenomenological. Users reporting the bug describe it as "gradient explosion when training with bfloat16," "training loss becomes NaN," or "loss diverges." The implicit assumption has been that lower precision introduces random noise that occasionally compounds catastrophically — an essentially stochastic explanation. This paper demonstrates that this assumption is wrong: the failure is deterministic, driven by systematic bias in rounding operations, not by random accumulation of floating-point error.

**Empirical workarounds that trade off efficiency.** The practical advice in GitHub issues and community forums has been:
- Revert to standard (non-flash) attention at the cost of $O(N^2)$ memory and slower training.
- Run the attention computation in FP32 while keeping the rest of the model in BF16, losing the memory and speed benefits of low precision for the attention module.
- Apply QK normalization to constrain the scale of attention scores.
- Use QK-clipping to prevent extreme values in the pre-softmax scores.

None of these approaches explain *why* they work. QK normalization might prevent training failure, but does it fix the root cause or merely mask it? Could it fail at larger scales or different precisions? The authors explicitly note in the Discussion (Section 5) that their analysis "provides a compelling explanation for the effectiveness of established stabilization techniques" — specifically, that "techniques like QK normalization and Gated Attention disrupt [the low-rank] structure," preventing errors from compounding even though they don't eliminate the rounding errors themselves. This insight was completely absent before this paper.

**The literature on low-precision training stability focuses on other mechanisms.** Prior academic work on training instability in low precision has largely addressed different failure modes:

- **Gradient underflow in FP16**: Micikevicius et al. (2017) identified that FP16's limited exponent range causes many gradients to underflow to zero, solved by loss scaling. This is a *range* problem, not a *precision* problem, and BF16 mostly fixes it.

- **Optimizer divergence**: Molybog et al. (2023) showed that Adam can diverge when the update direction becomes uncorrelated with the true descent direction, and Huang et al. (2025) proposed SPAM to detect gradient spikes and reset momentum. These are *optimizer-level* analyses that don't address *arithmetic-level* bias.

- **Activation outliers**: Fishman et al. (2024) observed that SwiGLU activation amplifies outliers during long FP8 training runs, and proposed Smooth-SwiGLU as a fix. This is an *activation function* problem.

- **FP8 scaling factor management**: Perez et al. (2023), Peng et al. (2023), and Balanc¸a et al. (2024) developed dynamic per-tensor scaling to make FP8 training viable. These address the *range* limitations of 8-bit formats.

What was missing from all prior work was a **mechanistic analysis that traces a training failure from the high-level symptom (loss explosion) down through the computational graph to the individual floating-point operations that cause systematic bias.** This paper fills exactly that gap.

**The closest prior work: Golden et al. (2024).** The paper cites Golden et al. (2024), "Is Flash Attention Stable?" which investigated numerical stability of flash attention but did not isolate the specific BF16 rounding mechanism that causes training failure. The current paper goes substantially further by (1) identifying the exact operation (`P̄V` in the forward pass), (2) characterizing the conditions under which it produces biased error (multiple identical maxima in the pre-softmax scores combined with same-sign entries in the value matrix), (3) showing how this error propagates through the backward pass into a biased weight update, and (4) demonstrating that the bias accumulates specifically because of structural similarity in the low-rank representations that the weight update affects.

**The specific failure case is well-defined and reproducible.** A key strength of this paper's approach is that it focuses on a single, specific, reproducible failure rather than trying to characterize stability in general. The authors reproduce the failure using a GPT-2 architecture (12 layers, 12 attention heads, embedding dimension 768, context length 1024) trained on OpenWebText with the AdamW optimizer. They use a clever reproducibility trick: "For deterministic reproducibility, we deviate from a standard random data loader by recording and reusing the exact sequence of data batches from an initial run that led to the failure" (Section 3.1). This eliminates data-related randomness as a confounding variable and enables the kind of precise, ablation-driven analysis that follows. Without this control, it would be impossible to isolate the numerical mechanism from other sources of variation.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

**Not a new training algorithm or stabilization technique — a mechanistic explanation.** The paper is explicitly not proposing a new method as its primary contribution. The stabilized flash attention (SFA) modification in Section 4 is presented as a *validation experiment* that confirms the analysis, not as the main contribution. The abstract frames the contribution as "the first mechanistic explanation for a long-standing and unresolved failure case," and the analysis (Sections 3.2 and 3.3) occupies the bulk of the paper. This is unusual in ML conference papers, which typically emphasize novel methods and benchmark gains. The positioning here is closer to a systems or numerical analysis paper: the value is in *understanding*, with the practical fix serving as evidence that the understanding is correct.

**A blueprint for diagnosing numerical instabilities, not just a fix for one bug.** The paper explicitly frames its analytical methodology as generalizable. Section 5 states: "Our analytical workflow — locate the source of error, find similar update directions, trace back to root causes — provides a blueprint for diagnosing similar numerical instabilities in other architectures, scales, and low-precision formats." Appendix F expands on this, providing a three-step methodology: (1) isolate the error source through targeted high-precision substitutions, (2) identify error accumulation mechanisms by analyzing gradient error structural patterns, (3) trace to root arithmetic cause by examining low-level format operations. The paper positions itself as establishing a *paradigm* for numerical debugging in deep learning, not solving one isolated incident.

**Connecting architectural phenomena to arithmetic mechanisms.** A distinctive aspect of this paper's positioning is how it links high-level architectural observations about transformers (attention sinks, spectral norm growth, QK normalization effectiveness) to low-level hardware arithmetic (BF16 rounding with sticky bits). The Discussion (Section 5) makes this explicit: attention sinks "are more likely to produce attention probabilities of 1, which triggers the biased rounding error," and QK normalization "disrupts the underlying structure" that allows errors to compound. This bridge between architecture and arithmetic was previously missing — practitioners knew that attention sinks were associated with instability and that QK normalization helped, but not *why* or *how*. The paper's mechanistic analysis fills this conceptual gap.

**An alternative to the "add more stabilization" paradigm.** The broader motivation is to shift the field from reactive patching to principled design. The existing approach to numerical stability — add QK normalization, then clipping, then gated attention, then gradient scaling — is an arms race against unknown failure modes. By establishing a causal framework for understanding *why* one specific failure occurs, this paper enables a more systematic approach: rather than guessing what might help, practitioners can analyze where biased rounding errors could accumulate, check whether low-rank structures provide an accumulation pathway, and design targeted interventions that address root causes. The stabilized flash attention modification is elegant precisely because it is so minimal — a few lines of conditional logic in the forward pass — yet it completely resolves the failure. This is only possible because the fix targets the exact mechanism of bias, not a downstream symptom.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper is primarily a **diagnostic investigation** rather than a method paper—it builds a systematic analytical pipeline to trace a training failure from its high-level symptom (loss explosion) down to individual BF16 floating-point rounding operations. The problem it solves is explaining *why* a seemingly innocuous combination (BF16 precision + flash attention + standard GPT-2 architecture) produces catastrophic training instability after thousands of stable steps, and the solution takes the shape of a three-stage reverse-engineering process: isolate the module causing the error → identify how the error accumulates across training steps → trace the accumulation to a specific arithmetic bias in BF16 addition.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The analytical system has five components that operate in a diagnostic cascade rather than as a deployed pipeline:

1. **Reproducible failure harness** — a controlled training setup (GPT-2 on OpenWebText with fixed data ordering) that deterministically reproduces the BF16 flash attention loss explosion, eliminating data randomness as a confounding variable.
2. **Error source isolator** — a suite of targeted ablation experiments that systematically replace specific operations (tiling, individual layers, individual attention heads, individual computation terms like `δ = rowsum(dO ∘ O)`) with high-precision equivalents to narrow down the failure locus.
3. **Gradient error structural analyzer** — mathematical decomposition of the difference between high- and low-precision weight gradients (Equation 2) into a weighted sum of rank-1 matrices, revealing that the error accumulates in a consistent low-rank direction `R` across tokens and training steps.
4. **Arithmetic bias tracer** — per-operation analysis of the `P̄V` product in the forward pass, examining how BF16 addition of same-sign numbers with sticky-bit interactions produces systematic negative rounding errors when multiple attention probabilities equal exactly 1.
5. **Validating intervention** — a minimal modification to the safe softmax that conditionally adjusts the normalization factor when multiple identical maxima are detected, preventing `P̄` elements from being exactly 1 and thus eliminating the biased rounding at its source.

Information flows backward through the failure's causal chain: we start from the consequence (loss explosion), isolate the module (layer 2's flash attention), identify the specific computation (`δ` using low-precision `O`), decompose the gradient error pattern (low-rank accumulation), and trace to the root arithmetic event (biased rounding in BF16 `P̄V` addition with sticky bits).

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the reproducible failure case setup (Section 3.1 of the paper), because all subsequent analysis depends on having a controlled, deterministic instance of the failure to dissect—the model architecture, training hyperparameters, and the specific reproducibility mechanism that freezes data ordering.
- **Second**, the systematic isolation experiments that narrow the failure source from "somewhere in the training pipeline" to "the BF16 computation of `O` in the forward pass of flash attention layer 2, specifically affecting certain attention heads" (Section 3.2), because these experiments provide the precise coordinates for the detailed mathematical analysis.
- **Third**, the gradient error decomposition (Section 3.3.1) that derives the difference between high- and low-precision weight gradients as a weighted sum of low-rank matrices, and the empirical evidence that these low-rank matrices are structurally similar across tokens and training steps—explaining *why* the error accumulates rather than cancels.
- **Fourth**, the rounding error analysis (Section 3.3.2) that traces the positive bias in `(δ_lp − δ_hp)[T]` to specific BF16 addition operations in the `P̄V` product, with a concrete worked example showing exactly how overflow, right-shift, and sticky-bit-driven round-up produce systematic negative error in `O`.
- **Fifth**, the stabilization modification (Section 4) that conditionally adjusts the safe softmax to prevent `P̄` entries from being exactly 1, confirming that the identified mechanism is both necessary and sufficient for the failure.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

#### The Reproducible Failure Harness

**Model architecture.** The authors use a **GPT-2 Small** architecture with 12 transformer layers, 12 attention heads per layer, an embedding dimension of 768, and a context length of 1024 tokens. This is a standard, well-documented architecture (Radford et al., 2019) that is small enough to iterate on quickly but large enough to exhibit the failure—the community bug reports originated from similar-scale GPT-2 training runs, so reproducing at this scale directly addresses the reported issue.

**Training configuration.** The optimizer is **AdamW** with `β₁ = 0.9`, `β₂ = 0.95`, and **zero weight decay**. The learning rate follows a **cosine schedule** with a 2000-iteration linear warmup to a peak of `1 × 10⁻³`, decaying to `1 × 10⁻⁵`. Global gradient clipping is applied with a **maximum norm of 1.0**. Training uses **automatic mixed precision**: the forward pass runs in BF16, the backward pass runs in FP32, and the optimizer maintains FP32 master weights. This is the standard PyTorch mixed-precision recipe, not a custom or exotic configuration.

**Hardware and parallelism.** Training runs on **4 NVIDIA A100 (80GB) GPUs** using PyTorch's Distributed Data Parallel (DDP). Each GPU processes a micro-batch of `32`, and with gradient accumulation over `4` steps, the **effective global batch size** is `524,288` tokens per optimization step.

**Data and the reproducibility trick.** The model trains on the **OpenWebText** dataset (Gokaslan et al., 2019). Critically—and this is a key methodological choice—the authors do not use a standard random data loader. Instead, they **record and reuse the exact sequence of data batches** from an initial run that led to the failure. This means every subsequent experiment processes identical data in identical order, eliminating any possibility that differences between experimental runs are caused by seeing different training examples. This is what makes the ablation analysis in Section 3.2 scientifically valid: when the authors replace a low-precision computation with a high-precision one and observe that training stabilizes, the *only* difference between the two runs is the precision of that computation. No other variable changes.

**Why this configuration matters.** The failure consistently manifests as a **sudden loss explosion after several thousand training steps** (visible in Figure 2). The training proceeds normally—validation loss decreases smoothly from ~11 to ~3-4—until roughly step 6,500-7,000, at which point the loss spikes to infinity or NaN. This temporal pattern is important: it rules out initialization-related problems (the model trains fine initially) and suggests a gradual accumulation of some corrupting influence that eventually overwhelms the training dynamics. The reproducibility of this pattern under the fixed-data regime is what enables the kind of precise, step-by-step diagnostic that follows.

#### Systematic Error Source Isolation (Section 3.2)

This section describes a sequence of experiments that progressively narrow the failure's origin from the entire training system to a single computation in a single attention head. The methodology is **ablation by precision substitution**: for each candidate operation, the authors run two versions of training—one with the operation in BF16 (the failing configuration) and one with that specific operation elevated to FP32 while everything else remains in BF16. If the FP32-substituted run trains stably, the operation is implicated.

**Step 1: Ruling out tiling.** Flash attention's distinguishing feature is its block-wise (tiled) processing. To determine whether this tiling causes the failure—perhaps through some interaction between block boundaries and numerical precision—the authors disable tiling by setting the block size equal to the full sequence length (1024). This forces the algorithm to compute attention over the entire matrix at once, eliminating any tiling-specific behavior. The training **still fails** with the same loss explosion pattern. This result cleanly rules out tiling as the cause, and it simplifies all subsequent analysis: the authors can work with non-tiled flash attention, ignoring block-partitioning details and focusing purely on the core mathematical operations.

**Step 2: Localizing to a single layer.** To determine which part of the model harbors the failure, the authors monitor the **spectral norm** (the largest singular value) of weight matrices across all layers during training. This reveals "an anomalous spike specifically within the second layer's attention" (Section 3.2). Two confirmatory experiments establish causality:

- Using flash attention **only in layer 2** (with standard attention elsewhere) is sufficient to reproduce the training failure.
- Replacing flash attention with standard attention **in layer 2** (while retaining it in all other layers) restores training stability.

These are strong, symmetric results: layer 2's flash attention is both necessary and sufficient for the failure. This narrows the diagnostic focus from 12 layers to 1.

**Step 3: Localizing to the computation of `δ`.** The backward pass of flash attention computes an intermediate term `δ = rowsum(dO ∘ O)`, where `dO` is the gradient of the loss with respect to the attention output and `O` is the forward-pass attention output. The operation `rowsum(dO ∘ O)` means: multiply `dO` and `O` element-wise (producing a matrix of the same shape), then sum across the feature dimension for each token position (producing a vector of length `N`). This `δ` term is then used to compute `dS`, the gradient of the attention scores.

The authors discover that replacing this efficient computation with the mathematically equivalent alternative `δ = rowsum(dP ∘ P)` (where `P = softmax(QKᵀ)` and `dP = dO Vᵀ`) **restores training stability**. Why is this diagnostic? Because the alternative formulation avoids using the forward pass's BF16-computed `O`; it recomputes the attention probabilities `P` and computes `δ` from them. This indicates that the **low-precision `O` from the forward pass** carries numerical errors that contaminate `δ`.

**Step 4: Pinpointing the forward-pass `O` computation.** Two further experiments nail down the exact error source:

- Recomputing `O` as `P V` in FP32 within the backward pass (instead of reusing the forward pass's BF16 `O`) stabilizes training. This means the backward pass itself is fine—the problem is that the stored `O_lp` is corrupted.
- Computing `O` entirely in FP32 during the forward pass, while keeping all other operations in BF16, also stabilizes training. This isolates the error to the **BF16 computation of `O` in the forward pass**.

This produces **Claim 1:** "Low-precision `δ_lp = rowsum(dO ∘ O_lp)` causes training failure." The chain is: BF16 introduces errors in `O` during forward → these errors propagate into `δ` during backward → corrupted `δ` produces corrupted gradients → weight updates become systematically biased → training diverges.

**Step 5: Localizing within the layer to specific attention heads.** The query projection weight matrices `W_Q` across different heads show highly unequal spectral norms (Figure 3). Head 8 exhibits the largest spectral norm at `6.27`, with heads 1, 7, 9, 11, and 12 also showing elevated values (`4.34`, `4.12`, `5.32`, `4.64`, `5.39` respectively). Selectively computing `O` in high precision for these outlier heads is sufficient to restore stability. Since head 8's spectral norm is the highest, subsequent detailed analysis focuses on this single head.

**Why this narrowing is scientifically rigorous.** Each step eliminates a class of alternative explanations. Tiling isn't the cause. The problem isn't distributed across the model—it's localized to one layer. It's not a general backward-pass issue—it's specifically the stored `O` from forward. It's not even all attention heads in layer 2—a handful of heads account for the failure. This produces a precise diagnostic target: the BF16 computation of `O` in the forward pass of flash attention, in layer 2, with head 8 being the strongest contributor. All subsequent analysis operates at this level of specificity.

#### Gradient Error Decomposition and Low-Rank Accumulation (Section 3.3.1)

This section derives the mathematical pathway by which errors in the forward-pass `O` become biased weight updates that accumulate across training steps. The analysis proceeds in three stages: deriving the gradient error formula, establishing that the error decomposes into a structured low-rank form, and demonstrating empirically that this structure prevents the error from canceling over time.

**Stage 1: Deriving the high-precision vs. low-precision gradient difference.**

The gradient of the query projection matrix `W_Q` is computed via the chain rule through: loss → attention output `O` → attention scores `S` → query vectors `Q` → query projection `W_Q`. The critical intermediate is `dS`, the gradient with respect to the pre-softmax attention scores:

$$dS = \alpha P \circ (dP - \delta)$$

where `α` is the attention scaling factor (typically `1/√d`), `P = softmax(α Q Kᵀ)` is the attention probability matrix, `dP = dO Vᵀ` is the gradient with respect to `P`, and `δ = rowsum(dO ∘ O)` is the term identified in Section 3.2.

**What this equation computes:** It distributes the output gradient `dO` backward through the softmax. The term `dP - δ` represents the gradient after the softmax normalization: `dP` is the raw upstream signal, and subtracting `δ` (broadcast across columns) enforces the constraint that each row of `P` sums to 1. The Hadamard product with `P` downweights gradients for token pairs with near-zero attention. The result `dS` is a matrix of the same shape as the attention scores, telling us how each pre-softmax score should change to reduce the loss.

**Why this form:** The softmax function has a specific gradient structure: `∂L/∂S_ij = P_ij (∂L/∂P_ij - Σ_k P_ik ∂L/∂P_ik)`. The subtraction of the row-wise weighted sum `δ` is not an approximation—it is the exact derivative of the row-wise normalization in softmax. Omitting `δ` would produce gradients that ignore the competitive nature of attention (increasing one token's attention necessarily decreases others).

Now, since `δ` is the only term that differs between the high-precision (FP32) and low-precision (BF16) backward passes (all other quantities are computed identically), the error in the score gradient is:

$$dS_{hp} - dS_{lp} = \alpha P \circ (\delta_{lp} - \delta_{hp})$$

**What this computes:** The high-precision score gradient `dS_hp` uses the correct `δ_hp` (computed from FP32 `O_hp`), while the low-precision gradient uses the corrupted `δ_lp` (computed from BF16 `O_lp`). The difference is exactly the attention probability matrix `P`, scaled row-wise by the error vector `δ_lp - δ_hp`.

The query gradient `dQ = dS K` propagates this error:

$$dQ_{hp} - dQ_{lp} = (dS_{hp} - dS_{lp}) K = \alpha \cdot \text{diag}(\delta_{lp} - \delta_{hp}) (P K)$$

**Key transformation:** The Hadamard product `P ∘ (δ_lp - δ_hp)` applies the error vector row-wise, multiplying each row `i` of `P` by `(δ_lp - δ_hp)[i]`. This is exactly equivalent to pre-multiplying `P` by a diagonal matrix `diag(δ_lp - δ_hp)` where the diagonal entries are the elements of the error vector. The notation `diag(v)` means: create an `N×N` matrix with vector `v` on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere. When you multiply `diag(v) P`, each row `i` of `P` gets scaled by `v[i]`.

**What this means operationally:** The error in the query gradient is the error vector `δ_lp - δ_hp` acting as a row-wise scaling on `P K` (the attention-weighted key matrix). If a particular token position `T` has a large positive error `(δ_lp - δ_hp)[T]`, then the `T`-th row of `P K` gets amplified in the gradient error.

Finally, the weight gradient `dW_Q = dQᵀ X` (where `X` is the input to the attention layer) gives:

$$dW^Q_{hp} - dW^Q_{lp} = \alpha \sum_{T=1}^{N} (\delta_{lp} - \delta_{hp})[T] \cdot (PK)[T]^{\top} X[T]$$

**What this equation computes:** The total gradient error for `W_Q` is a sum over all `N` token positions. For each token `T`, the contribution is the scalar coefficient `(δ_lp - δ_hp)[T]` multiplied by the rank-1 matrix `(PK)[T]ᵀ X[T]`. A rank-1 matrix is the outer product of two vectors: the `T`-th row of `PK` (a vector in key-value space) and the `T`-th row of `X` (a vector in input feature space). Each such outer product is a `d×d` matrix (where `d = 768` is the embedding dimension) but has only one independent direction in its row and column spaces.

**Why this decomposition matters:** It separates the error into two factors: (1) the **coefficients** `(δ_lp − δ_hp)[T]`, which depend only on numerical precision, and (2) the **direction matrices** `(PK)[T]ᵀ X[T]`, which depend only on the model's representations. If either factor behaves pathologically—systematically biased coefficients, or structurally aligned direction matrices—the error accumulates. If both behave randomly (zero-mean coefficients, uncorrelated directions), the errors cancel.

**Stage 2: Empirical observation that `(PK)[T]ᵀ X[T]` is structurally similar across tokens and training steps.**

Figure 4 provides the key empirical evidence. Panels (a) and (d) show the matrix `PK` for head 8 at two different training steps (6,610 and 6,619) and two different batch indices (190 and 209). Panels (b) and (e) show the corresponding input matrices `X`. The visual patterns—alternating bands of positive and negative values along the feature dimension—are nearly identical across these different contexts. Panels (c) and (f) show the rank-1 outer product `(PK)[T]ᵀ X[T]` for specific token positions (token 50 at step 6,610 and token 718 at step 6,619). The resulting `d×d` matrices exhibit strong similarity, particularly at specific input feature dimensions (columns 546 and 678 are highlighted).

Because these rank-1 error matrices `(PK)[T]ᵀ X[T]` are similar across different tokens `T` (within a batch) and across different training steps, the total gradient error can be approximated as:

$$dW^Q_{hp} - dW^Q_{lp} \approx \alpha \left(\sum_{T=1}^{N} (\delta_{lp} - \delta_{hp})[T]\right) R$$

**What this approximation means:** All the individual rank-1 error contributions point in roughly the same direction `R` in weight space. The net effect is not `N` independent random perturbations that average out—it is a single coherent update whose magnitude is proportional to the **sum of the coefficients** `Σ_T (δ_lp - δ_hp)[T]`. If this sum is consistently positive across training steps, the weight matrix is pushed in the `R` direction each iteration, accumulating rather than canceling.

**Stage 3: The cumulative coefficient sum is positively biased.**

Figure 5(a) plots the **cumulative sum** of `Σ_T (δ_lp - δ_hp)[T]` over training steps 6,580 to 6,680 (the period just before loss explosion). The sum is **consistently positive**, growing from approximately 0 to roughly `0.00038` over 100 steps. This is the smoking gun: the errors do not oscillate around zero or cancel out. Each training step adds a small positive increment to the weight matrix along direction `R`.

This produces **Claim 2:** "Weight update in low-precision training is biased by `(δ_lp - δ_hp)[T] R`, which arises from the structurally similar matrices (denoted as `R`) across tokens and training steps, and its positively-biased coefficient `(δ_lp - δ_hp)[T]`. This bias accumulates error, preventing cancellation, increasing weight spectral norm and activation, and leading to loss explosion."

**The mechanism in plain language:** The low-precision gradient at each step contains a small error. This error happens to point in approximately the same direction `R` every step (because `PK` and `X` have consistent structure). The error also happens to be systematically positive rather than zero-mean (because of the bias in `δ_lp - δ_hp`). Over hundreds of steps, this consistent push in direction `R` grows the weight matrix's magnitude in that direction. The spectral norm increases. Activations passing through this weight get amplified. The feedback loop intensifies until training collapses.

The remainder of Section 3.3.2 traces this positive bias in the coefficients back to its origin in BF16 arithmetic.

#### Tracing the Positive Bias to BF16 Rounding Error (Section 3.3.2)

This section establishes the final link in the causal chain: where does the positive bias in `(δ_lp - δ_hp)[T]` come from? The answer involves a specific interaction between the attention probability matrix `P̄` (unnormalized, pre-softmax), the value matrix `V`, and how BF16 addition handles overflow when adding numbers of the same sign.

**Step 1: Locating the large error contributors.**

The error in `δ` at token position `T` is `(δ_lp - δ_hp)[T] = rowsum(dO[T,:] ∘ (O_lp[T,:] - O_hp[T,:]))`. The authors focus on token `T = 718` at training step 6,619, where the error contribution is large and positive.

Figure 5(b) shows `O[T,:]` and `dO[T,:]` for this token. Both exhibit large-magnitude values at specific feature dimensions—most notably features 20 and 29, where both `O` and `dO` are strongly negative. Figure 5(c) zooms in on the error: `O_lp[T,:] - O_hp[T,:]` (the difference between low-precision and high-precision output) is most negative at exactly these same feature dimensions. The product `dO[T,i] × (O_lp[T,i] - O_hp[T,i])` at feature `i=20` is therefore `(negative) × (negative) = positive`, contributing positively to `(δ_lp - δ_hp)[T]`.

The key observation is that **`O_lp` is systematically more negative than `O_hp`** (`O_lp[T,i] < O_hp[T,i]`). This systematic bias toward more negative values is what makes the product with `dO` (which shares the same sign pattern) consistently positive rather than random.

**Step 2: Narrowing to the `P̄V` product.**

The attention output `O` is computed from an intermediate unnormalized output `Ō` via safe softmax:

$$\bar{O} = \bar{P} V, \quad O = \bar{O} / \text{rowsum}(\bar{P})$$

where `P̄ = exp(S - rowmax(S))` is the exponentiated, shifted attention score matrix (unnormalized probabilities). The authors find that computing only the `P̄V` product in FP32 (everything else in BF16) is sufficient to stabilize training. This pinpoints the error to the matrix multiplication `P̄V`.

**Step 3: Analyzing element-wise error in `P̄V`.**

For a specific output element `Ō[T,i]` (token `T`, feature `i=20`), the low-precision and high-precision computations differ:

$$\bar{O}_{lp}[T,i] - \bar{O}_{hp}[T,i] = \left(\sum_{t} \bar{P}_{lp}[T,t] V[t,i]\right)_{lp} - \left(\sum_{t} \bar{P}_{hp}[T,t] V[t,i]\right)_{hp}$$

The subscript `(·)_lp` means: the summation is performed in FP32 (for accumulation precision), but the final result is rounded to BF16. The subscript `(·)_hp` means: the entire computation stays in FP32. The inputs `P̄` and `V` are themselves BF16 values, so `P̄_lp = P̄_hp` and the only difference is the precision of the accumulation and the final rounding.

**Step 4: The role of `P̄[T,t] = 1`.**

The authors compute the **cumulative error** as the sum over token positions `t` progresses (Equation 5). Figure 6(b) and (c) visualize this cumulative error alongside the attention probabilities `P̄`. The critical observation: **large negative jumps in the error occur precisely at token positions `t` where `P̄[T,t] = 1`**.

When does `P̄[T,t] = 1`? This happens when the pre-softmax score `S[T,t]` is the maximum value in row `T`, because `P̄[T,t] = exp(S[T,t] - max(S[T,:])) = exp(0) = 1`. If a row of `S` has **multiple** identical maximum values, then `P̄` has multiple entries equal to 1.

When `P̄[T,t] = 1`, the product `P̄[T,t] V[t,i]` is simply `V[t,i]`—the value matrix entry, unchanged. Figure 6(a) shows that for the problematic feature `i=20`, **most entries of `V[:,20]` are negative**. So when summing the `P̄V` product across tokens, the terms where `P̄ = 1` contribute negative BF16 numbers.

**Step 5: The BF16 addition overflow and biased rounding mechanism.**

The core arithmetic problem occurs when adding two BF16 numbers with the **same sign** (both negative, in this case). Here is what happens at the hardware level:

1. **Exponent alignment:** The two numbers' exponents are compared. If they have the same exponent (which is common when both come from `V` entries of similar magnitude), no alignment shift is needed.

2. **Significand addition:** The significands (the fractional parts, including the implicit leading 1) are added. When adding two numbers with the same sign, the sum's significand can **overflow**. For example, adding `1.xxxx` and `1.yyyy` produces `10.zzzz` — the leading `10` means the result no longer fits in the normalized `1.xxx` format.

3. **Normalization:** The significand must be shifted right by one bit (dropping the least significant bit) and the exponent incremented by one. This right shift is where the rounding decision happens.

4. **Sticky bit interaction:** Because the summation is accumulated in FP32 (which has 23 fraction bits), many small terms (from token positions where `P̄ < 1`) have already been added to the running sum before the next big negative `V[t,i]` arrives. These small terms activate the **sticky bit** — a hardware flag indicating that the discarded bits during previous rounding operations were non-zero. According to the IEEE 754 round-to-nearest-even rule, if the sticky bit is set and the rounding bit (the bit shifted out during normalization) is 1, the result **rounds up**.

5. **Round-up for negative numbers means more negative:** "Rounding up" means increasing the magnitude. For a negative number, increasing the magnitude means making it **more negative**. So the BF16 result is more negative than the true FP32 sum.

Conversely, when the rounding bit is 0, the result rounds down, introducing a **positive** error. But the authors note that this positive error is typically smaller because the numbers being added in these cases are usually smaller. This **asymmetry** — large negative errors from rounding up on large-magnitude negative operands, small positive errors from rounding down on small operands — is what creates the **systematic negative bias** in `Ō`.

**Step 6: A worked concrete example.**

The paper provides a specific bit-level example to make this mechanism concrete (Section 3.3.2, "Analysis of Rounding Error in Fig. 6"):

The FP32 accumulator currently holds the sum of several previous terms, represented as:
- BF16 value: `11000000000110100000000000000000` in binary, which decodes as `−2.40625`
- Plus small residual: approximately `∼0.00087` that has activated the sticky bit

The next term to add is another BF16 value: `11000000000100110000000000000000` = `−2.296875`.

Their full FP32 representations (with the residual embedded in the fraction bits of the first operand) are:
- First operand: `11000000000110100000111000101110` (−2.4071154594421387)
- Second operand: `11000000000100110000000000000000` (−2.296875)

Both have the same exponent (`10000000` = 128 in biased representation, actual exponent 1), so no alignment shift is needed. The significands are added:

```
(−1.00110100000111000101110) + (−1.00100110000000000000000)
= −10.01011010000111000101110
```

The result overflows the normalized format (the `10.xxx` pattern means an extra bit to the left of the binary point). Normalization requires:
- Shifting the significand right by 1 bit: `10.0101101` → `1.00101101` (the last `1` becomes the rounding bit)
- Incrementing the exponent: `10000000` → `10000001`

The exact FP32 result would be `1 10000001 00101101000011100010111`, which decodes to `−4.703990459442139`.

To store this in BF16, we must round to 7 fraction bits. The significand's fraction is `0010110`, with the rounding bit being `1` (the next bit). Because there are also non-zero bits beyond the rounding bit (the sticky bit is effectively set), the round-to-nearest-even rule **rounds up**:

```
BF16 fraction before rounding: 0010110
After adding 1:                  0010111
```

The final BF16 result is `1100000010010111` = `−4.71875`.

The rounding error is `−4.71875 − (−4.703990459442139) = −0.014759540557861328`. This single addition introduces a negative error of approximately `−0.015`. When such events occur for every token position where `P̄ = 1` (and there can be multiple per attention head), and across all attention heads where this pattern occurs, these negative errors accumulate to produce the systematic negative bias in `O` that ultimately drives the positive bias in `δ_lp - δ_hp`.

**Step 7: Why `P̄ < 1` doesn't cause biased rounding.**

The paper includes a crucial remark (Remark 1): when `P̄[T,t] < 1`, the product `P̄[T,t] V[t,i]` has a mix of zero and non-zero bits in its significand (because `P̄` is not exactly a power of 2). When this product is rounded to BF16, the rounding direction depends on the specific bit pattern and is equally likely to round up or down. There is no systematic bias. The problem **only** arises when `P̄[T,t] = 1`, because then the product is exactly `V[t,i]` with no additional bit-level variation to randomize the rounding direction.

This produces **Claim 3:** "With more than one `P̄[T,t] = 1` and negative `V[t,i]`, the addition in `P̄V` can cause overflow of significand. This necessitates a right shift and a round-up of significand due to sticky bit, introducing negative rounding error in `O` and leading to positive `(δ_lp - δ_hp)[T]`."

#### The Stabilized Flash Attention Modification (Section 4)

This section describes the validation experiment: a minimal change to flash attention that prevents `P̄` entries from being exactly 1, thereby eliminating the biased rounding at its source. The modification is applied **only to the forward pass** (Algorithm 3, with the changed lines highlighted in magenta); the backward pass remains unchanged.

**The core idea: conditional dynamic maximum adjustment.** The safe softmax operation computes `P̄ = exp(S - m)` where `m` is a row-wise maximum (or running maximum in the tiled version). The problem occurs when a row of `S` has multiple entries equal to the maximum, causing `exp(0) = 1` at those positions. The fix: when multiple maxima are detected, **adjust `m` to a value different from the maximum**, so that `S - m` is strictly negative at all positions and `exp(S - m) < 1` everywhere.

**Why a naive fixed offset fails (Appendix C).** One might think to simply subtract a small constant `ε` from the maximum: `m = max(S) - ε`. This would make `P̄ < 1` but introduces a new problem: all entries of `P̄` would be systematically rounded down by the same amount during BF16 conversion, creating a **fixed bias** in `P̄`. Since `V` entries often share the same sign, this fixed bias in `P̄` would propagate into `Ō = P̄ V` as a systematic error, reintroducing the very problem the fix aims to solve. The adjustment must be **dynamic** and conditional to avoid introducing new systematic biases.

**The conditional logic.** The modification (presented in the paper as pseudocode and detailed in Algorithm 3) works as follows:

For each row, compute:
- `rm = rowmax(S)`: the maximum score in the row
- `rs = rowsum(rm - S ≤ ε)`: count how many entries in the row are within `ε` of the maximum (where `ε` is a small tolerance like `10⁻³` to handle floating-point imprecision)

Then, adjust the normalization factor `m` conditionally:
- If `rm > 0` AND `rs > 1` (positive maximum, multiple occurrences): set `m = β × rm` where `β > 1`
- If `rm < 0` AND `rs > 1` (negative maximum, multiple occurrences): set `m = 0`
- Otherwise: leave `m = rm` unchanged

**What this does:** When multiple maxima exist and the maximum is positive, scaling by `β > 1` makes the new maximum in the exponent `rm - β rm = -(β-1)rm`, which is strictly negative. All entries of `P̄` become strictly less than 1. When the maximum is negative and repeated, setting `m = 0` ensures the maximum exponent is `rm - 0 = rm`, which is negative, again guaranteeing `P̄ < 1`. In all other cases (single maximum, or no repeated maximum), the standard safe softmax proceeds unchanged.

**Why `β` cannot be too small or too large.** The authors set `β ∈ [2, 8]` in experiments. If `β` is very close to 1 (say, `1.0001`), then `-(β-1)rm` is very close to zero, and `exp(-(β-1)rm)` could round to exactly 1 in BF16, defeating the purpose. If `β` is very large, `-(β-1)rm` could be a large negative number, causing `exp(-(β-1)rm)` to **underflow** to zero in BF16. This would make the normalization factor zero and cause division by zero when computing `O`. The range `[2, 8]` provides a safe margin.

**Mathematical equivalence.** Crucially, this modification exploits the **shift-invariance** property of softmax: `softmax(z) = softmax(z - c)` for any scalar `c`. Standard attention uses `c = max(z)` for numerical stability. The modified version uses `c = β max(z)` when multiple maxima are detected. In exact arithmetic, the attention output is identical. In finite precision, the modification changes the bit patterns in `P̄` to prevent the `=1` entries that trigger biased rounding.

**Integration into flash attention tiling.** Algorithm 3 shows how this fits into the standard flash attention forward pass. Within the inner loop (iterating over key/value blocks), after computing `S(j)_i = Q_i K_jᵀ`, the code computes the row statistics `rm` and `rs`, applies the conditional adjustment to get `m(j)′_i`, then takes the element-wise maximum with the running maximum `m(j-1)_i` to maintain the online softmax invariant. All other operations—the exponential, the normalization factor updates, the output accumulation—proceed exactly as in standard flash attention.

**Validation results (Figure 7).** The stabilized flash attention (SFA) is tested with:
- **GPT-2S + AdamW** (same configuration as the failure case): SFA trains stably for 600K steps, while standard flash attention diverges. Validation loss curves are shown in Figure 7(a).
- **GPT-2S + Muon optimizer** (Jordan et al., 2024): SFA with Muon (learning rate `5×10⁻⁴`, weight decay `0.01`, RMS-matched variant) also trains stably for 600K steps, confirming the fix is optimizer-independent (Figure 7b).
- **GPT-2M + AdamW** (larger model, 100K steps): SFA stabilizes the larger model as well, confirming scalability (Figure 7c).

The success of this minimal, targeted modification—changing only the normalization constant in a conditional branch of the safe softmax—provides strong evidence that the diagnosed mechanism (multiple `P̄ = 1` entries → same-sign BF16 addition overflow → biased rounding → low-rank accumulation → loss explosion) is both necessary and sufficient for the failure. If there were other root causes, this fix would not completely restore stability.

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Fixing the data ordering** rather than using a random loader: This eliminates data randomness as a confounding variable, enabling precise ablation experiments where the only difference between runs is the precision of a specific operation. Without this, it would be impossible to attribute stability to a precision change versus a lucky data shuffle.

- **Ablation by precision substitution** rather than by removing operations: Replacing a BF16 operation with its FP32 equivalent tests whether that operation's precision *causes* the failure, without changing the mathematical function being computed. This is cleaner than removing operations entirely (which would change the model).

- **Focusing on a single layer and single head** after initial localization: While the failure involves multiple heads (1, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12), detailed analysis of head 8 provides a representative case study. The mechanism discovered (multiple maxima + same-sign V entries + BF16 addition overflow) applies identically to other failing heads.

- **Decomposing the gradient error as `Σ (coeff)[T] × (rank-1 matrix)[T]`**: This separation isolates the numerical factor (coefficient bias) from the representational factor (low-rank structure). It reveals that *both* must be pathological for failure to occur—biased rounding alone would cancel out if the rank-1 matrices pointed in random directions, and low-rank structure alone would be harmless if the coefficients were zero-mean.

- **Conditional dynamic maximum adjustment** rather than unconditional or fixed-offset adjustment: Applying the adjustment only when multiple maxima are detected preserves standard behavior in the vast majority of cases, avoiding new numerical issues (underflow, division by zero) that an unconditional adjustment would introduce. The dynamic `β` scaling rather than a fixed offset avoids introducing new systematic biases in `P̄`.

- **Setting `β ∈ [2, 8]`**: This range is chosen empirically based on two constraints: `β` must be large enough that `exp(-(β-1)rm)` doesn't round back to 1 in BF16, but small enough that it doesn't underflow to zero. The specific value `β = 2` is used in the main experiments.

- **Setting negative repeated maxima to `m = 0`**: Alternative approaches like `m = γ rm` with `γ ∈ (0,1)` risk `exp((1-γ)rm)` rounding to exactly 1 when `rm` is close to zero. Setting `m = 0` is a robust choice that ensures `exp(rm) < 1` for any negative `rm`.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Causal Chain Diagnosis as a Scientific Methodology for Numerical Stability

The most distinctive intellectual contribution of this paper is not the specific fix for BF16 flash attention, but the **analytical methodology** it demonstrates: tracing a high-level training failure backward through the computational graph to individual floating-point arithmetic operations at the bit level. This is a fundamental shift in how the field approaches numerical stability problems.

**What the field did before this paper.** The standard approach to training instability in low precision has been empirical patching. When GPT-2 training with BF16 flash attention explodes, the community response has been to try things—switch to FP32 attention, add QK normalization, apply gradient clipping—and observe whether the problem goes away. These fixes work (when they do) for unknown reasons. The literature on low-precision training stability (Micikevicius et al., 2017; Perez et al., 2023; Fishman et al., 2024) has focused on *preventing* known categories of error (gradient underflow from limited exponent range, activation outliers from specific nonlinearities) rather than *diagnosing* an unknown failure from first principles. The implicit assumption has been that low-precision failures are essentially stochastic—random accumulation of rounding noise that occasionally compounds catastrophically—and therefore best addressed by adding safety margins (loss scaling, clipping, normalization).

**What this paper does differently.** The authors treat the training failure as a **deterministic forensic investigation** rather than a stochastic reliability problem. The three-stage methodology they outline—(1) isolate the precise operation causing the error through targeted precision substitutions, (2) identify structural patterns in the gradient error that explain why errors accumulate rather than cancel, (3) trace the accumulation to specific bit-level arithmetic events—converts "numerical instability" from a vague diagnosis into a tractable engineering problem. Each stage eliminates a class of alternative explanations: tiling isn't the cause (Stage 1, by disabling blocks), the problem isn't distributed across the model (Stage 1, by layer-wise ablation), the error isn't random noise (Stage 2, by showing structural alignment in the rank-1 error matrices), and the bias isn't a software bug (Stage 3, by reproducing the exact IEEE 754 rounding behavior on concrete bit patterns).

**Why this is fundamental, not incremental.** This methodology is generalizable in a way that the specific BF16 fix is not. As the authors note in Section 5 and Appendix F, the same three-stage approach can be applied to FP8 training, to other architectures, and to different failure modes. The key intellectual move is reframing the problem: instead of asking "what stabilization technique prevents this failure?" (which yields empirical patches), ask "what is the complete causal chain from hardware arithmetic to training dynamics?" (which yields mechanistic understanding). This mirrors the evolution of other engineering disciplines—from trial-and-error to root-cause analysis—and represents a maturation of how the ML community approaches numerical reliability. The paper's positioning as producing a "blueprint" rather than a fix is not rhetorical; it is a genuine methodological contribution.

**Evidence anchoring this claim.** The entire structure of Sections 3.2-3.4 embodies this methodology. Figure 2 establishes the deterministic failure. The ablation sequence (Section 3.2) narrows the source from "somewhere in training" to "BF16 `O` in flash attention layer 2, head 8." Equation (2) and Figure 4 decompose the gradient error into coefficients and low-rank structure. Figure 6 and the worked bit-level example trace the bias to sticky-bit-driven round-up in BF16 addition. The stabilized flash attention (Section 4, Figure 7) closes the loop by showing that eliminating the identified mechanism eliminates the failure. This is a complete causal argument, not a correlation.

### Innovation 2: The Low-Rank Accumulation Pathway as a Necessary Condition for Numerical Failure

The paper's second major insight is the identification of **structural similarity in error matrices as the mechanism that converts small per-step rounding errors into catastrophic training failure**. This explains something that had been empirically observed but never explained: why do some low-precision training runs fail while others (with the same precision, same architecture, different random seeds) do not?

**Prior assumptions about error accumulation.** The dominant mental model of floating-point error in deep learning has been that rounding errors are essentially random noise—each operation introduces a small, unbiased perturbation, and over many operations these perturbations partially cancel. If errors are unbiased and uncorrelated, their cumulative effect grows as `~√N` (by the central limit theorem), which is slow enough that training remains stable. Under this model, training failures occur when a rare "error cascade" happens—a run where, by chance, errors happen to align in the same direction enough times to matter. This is a **stochastic model** of failure: some fraction of training runs fail because randomness occasionally produces a correlated sequence of errors.

**What this paper shows instead.** The failure is deterministic, not stochastic, because the error matrices `(PK)[T]ᵀ X[T]` are not random and uncorrelated—they are structurally similar across tokens and training steps. Figure 4 provides the direct evidence: the `PK` and `X` matrices at different batch indices and training steps (6,610 and 6,619) show nearly identical patterns of alternating positive and negative bands. The resulting rank-1 outer products `(PK)[T]ᵀ X[T]` have similar dominant directions (highlighted at input feature columns 546 and 678). This means the per-step error is not `N` independent random perturbations that partially cancel—it is approximately `N` copies of the same direction `R`, multiplied by a coefficient that is systematically biased positive.

This transforms the failure from a statistical fluke into a **structural inevitability**. The low-rank structure in `PK` and `X`—which is a consequence of the representations the model learns, not a numerical artifact—provides a coherent "channel" through which rounding errors can propagate. The biased rounding in `P̄V` provides a consistent "push" along that channel. Together, they create a positive feedback loop: biased weight updates increase the spectral norm in direction `R`, which amplifies activations, which reinforces the low-rank structure, which makes the error channel even more coherent. The failure is not a random event that happens sometimes; it is the stable fixed point of a dynamical system that, once entered, accelerates toward collapse.

**Why this resolves the "QK normalization mystery."** The paper notes in the Discussion (Section 5) that this insight explains why QK normalization (Henry et al., 2020) and Gated Attention (Qiu et al., 2025) prevent the failure. These techniques don't fix the rounding error—the biased BF16 addition still occurs. What they do is **disrupt the structural similarity** of `PK` and `X` by constraining the scale and distribution of attention scores and representations. Without coherent low-rank structure, the per-step errors point in random directions and cancel out, even though the rounding bias still exists. This is a genuinely novel explanation: stabilization techniques work not by preventing numerical errors, but by preventing errors from having a coherent direction to accumulate along.

**Evidence.** Equation (3) formalizes the approximation `dW_Q^hp - dW_Q^lp ≈ α (Σ_T (δ_lp - δ_hp)[T]) R`. Figure 4(a-f) shows the structural similarity of `PK` and `X`. Figure 5(a) shows the cumulative coefficient sum growing monotonically positive over ~100 training steps. The connection between low-rank structure and failure is not speculated—it is derived mathematically from the gradient error formula and confirmed empirically.

### Innovation 3: Bridging Architectural Phenomena and Hardware Arithmetic

A distinctive conceptual contribution of this paper is how it connects two levels of analysis that are typically studied in isolation: high-level architectural properties of transformers (attention sinks, spectral norm growth, representation structure) and low-level hardware arithmetic (BF16 rounding with sticky bits, significand overflow, IEEE 754 round-to-nearest-even). This bridge has been conspicuously absent from the literature, and its construction has explanatory power that neither level alone provides.

**The gap in prior work.** The literature on transformer training stability can be divided into two largely separate conversations. At the architectural level, researchers have documented phenomena like attention sinks (Xiao et al., 2023)—where early tokens receive disproportionately high attention scores—and spectral norm growth (Yang et al., 2023; Rybakov et al., 2024)—where weight matrices increase in magnitude during training. These are described as "training dynamics" problems, but the mechanism linking them to numerical precision is left vague. At the hardware level, work on low-precision arithmetic (Micikevicius et al., 2022; Noune et al., 2022) characterizes rounding error distributions, stochastic rounding (Ben Ali et al., 2024), and format design tradeoffs, but does not connect these to specific training failure modes in specific architectures. The two conversations have been ships passing in the night.

**What this paper accomplishes.** The paper shows that attention sinks are not merely an architectural curiosity—they are the **trigger mechanism** for the arithmetic instability. When attention concentrates on a few tokens (the sink behavior), those tokens have `P̄[T,t] = 1` at the maximum positions. When `V[t,i]` has consistent sign (negative, in the observed case), the BF16 addition of these `=1`-weighted value entries produces systematic rounding bias through the overflow-renormalize-round sequence. The attention sink is what *creates the conditions* for the biased rounding to occur and for it to occur at the same token positions repeatedly across training steps (since sinks persist). Similarly, spectral norm growth is not just a symptom of instability—it is the *mechanism* by which biased weight updates amplify themselves. The low-rank error `R` increases `W_Q`'s norm in a specific direction, which increases activations, which increases gradient magnitudes, which accelerates the error accumulation.

**Why this matters beyond this specific failure.** This bridge provides a **generative framework** for understanding numerical stability in transformers: given a proposed architecture change (e.g., a new attention variant, a new activation function, a new normalization scheme), one can analyze (a) whether it creates conditions for systematic rounding bias in low-precision arithmetic, and (b) whether it provides low-rank pathways for that bias to accumulate coherently. This is much more powerful than the current approach of training a model and observing whether it diverges. The paper's brief analysis of Llama-3.1-8B in Appendix D—showing that the same structural patterns (`PK[T]ᵀ X[T]` similarity, multiple attention maxima) exist in a different model at a different scale—suggests this framework may generalize, though the authors are appropriately cautious about claiming universality without further experiments.

**Evidence.** The Discussion (Section 5) makes this bridge explicit: "By attracting high attention scores, [attention sinks] are more likely to produce attention probabilities of 1, which triggers the biased rounding error in the `P̄V` computation. This provides a direct numerical link between the architectural behavior of sinks and the arithmetic instability that derails training." Figure 6(a) shows the negative-biased distribution of `V[:, i]` that makes the rounding bias systematic rather than symmetric. Figure 11 (Appendix) shows the spectral norm spike in layer 2 that corresponds to the error accumulation site. The analysis does not merely observe correlations between these phenomena—it traces the causal pathway connecting them through specific arithmetic operations.

### Innovation 4: Redefining the Role of Softmax Maxima from "Just Numerical Stability" to "Precision-Sensitive Structural Element"

A more focused but conceptually important insight is the paper's reframing of what the safe softmax maximum-subtraction trick actually does. In standard treatments, subtracting the row maximum before exponentiating is described as a numerical stability technique—it prevents overflow in the exponential function. This paper reveals a second, previously unrecognized role: **the safe softmax determines which entries of `P̄` become exactly 1, and these `=1` entries are precision-sensitive in a way that other entries are not**.

**The standard understanding.** Every textbook description of the softmax function includes the shift-invariance property: `softmax(z) = softmax(z - c)` for any constant `c`. The standard choice `c = max(z)` is justified solely by overflow prevention. If `z` contains large positive values (say, `z_i = 1000`), then `exp(1000)` overflows FP32. Subtracting the maximum ensures all exponentiated values are at most `exp(0) = 1`, keeping the computation in a safe range. The choice of `c` is considered arbitrary from a mathematical perspective—any `c` works in exact arithmetic, and `max(z)` is chosen for its overflow-prevention property.

**What this paper reveals.** The choice of `c` is not arbitrary in finite precision, because it determines which entries of `P̄ = exp(S - c)` evaluate to exactly `exp(0) = 1`. When `c = max(S)`, any entry where `S = max(S)` produces `P̄ = 1`. These `=1` entries have a special property in the subsequent `P̄V` multiplication: the product `1 × V[t,i] = V[t,i]` carries the full precision of `V[t,i]` with no multiplicative dithering. When `V[t,i]` entries have consistent sign and similar magnitude, adding multiple such values in BF16 produces the overflow-renormalize-round sequence with systematic bias. When `P̄ < 1` (even slightly, say `P̄ = 0.999`), the product `0.999 × V[t,i]` has a significand with mixed zero and non-zero bits at the low end, which randomizes the rounding direction and eliminates systematic bias.

This reframes the safe softmax from a simple overflow guard to a **precision-sensitivity control mechanism**. The specific value of the normalization constant `c` determines which entries of `P̄` are "hot" (exactly 1 and therefore precision-sensitive) versus "warm" (less than 1 and therefore precision-benign). The stabilized flash attention modification exploits this by conditionally adjusting `c` when the standard choice would create multiple hot entries—not to change the mathematical output (which is identical in exact arithmetic), but to change the bit-level rounding behavior of the computation.

**Why this is an innovation, not just an observation.** Prior work on numerical stability in attention (Dao et al., 2022; Dao, 2024; Shah et al., 2024) has treated precision as a uniform property—an operation is either "in high precision" or "in low precision," and the goal is to do as much as possible in low precision without causing problems. This paper shows that **precision sensitivity is non-uniform across the computation**: some arithmetic operations (adding numbers with `P̄ = 1` and same-sign `V` entries) are far more vulnerable to systematic bias than others (adding numbers with `P̄ < 1` that dither the product). This is a qualitative shift in how to think about mixed-precision design—not "which operations can be low-precision?" but "which specific numerical conditions within an operation create bias, and can we avoid those conditions while staying in low precision?"

**Evidence.** Remark 1 in Section 3.3.2 states: "When `P̄[T,t] < 1` and has a mix of zero and non-zero bits in its significand's bits, its product with `V[t,i]` will also have a non-zero value in those bits. When rounding to BF16, this will not introduce biased rounding error." The stabilization modification in Section 4 directly targets the `=1` condition. The success of this minimal change—and the failure of a naive fixed-offset approach (Appendix C) that introduces its own systematic bias—confirms that the `=1` entries, not low precision in general, are the root cause.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the **OpenWebText** corpus (Gokaslan et al., 2019), a reproduction of the WebText dataset used to train GPT-2. The model is trained on this corpus for language modeling (next-token prediction). Critically for the diagnostic methodology, the authors do not use a standard random data loader: they **record and reuse the exact sequence of data batches** from an initial failing run to ensure deterministic reproducibility across all ablation experiments. This means every experimental variant processes identical data in identical order, isolating numerical precision as the sole variable.

- **Base model(s).** The primary model is a **GPT-2 Small** architecture (Radford et al., 2019): 12 transformer layers, 12 attention heads per layer, embedding dimension 768, context length 1024 tokens. This scale is chosen because it is large enough to reproduce the community-reported failure (nanoGPT issues #303, #524, #554) while being small enough to iterate rapidly on diagnostic experiments. For a scale-up validation, the stabilized method is also tested on a **GPT-2 Medium** model (details not fully specified in the paper, but described as "larger" and trained for 100K steps). Appendix D presents structural analyses on **Llama-3.1-8B** to assess whether the identified patterns generalize to other model families, though this is observational rather than a training experiment.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **validation loss** (cross-entropy on held-out data), plotted as a function of training steps. This is the direct symptom of the failure: in the unstable configuration, loss decreases normally from ~11 to ~3-4 over several thousand steps, then spikes abruptly to infinity or NaN (Figure 2). Secondary metrics used for diagnostic purposes include:
  - **Spectral norm** of weight matrices (largest singular value), monitored across layers and training steps to identify anomalous growth preceding the loss explosion (Figure 11, Appendix).
  - **Cumulative sum of `Σ_T (δ_lp − δ_hp)[T]`** over training iterations, used as an intermediate diagnostic to demonstrate the persistent positive bias in the gradient error coefficients (Figure 5a).
  - **Frequency of multiple maxima** in pre-softmax attention scores, tracked to establish the temporal correlation between this arithmetic condition and training failure (Figure 13, Appendix).

- **Baselines.** The paper's experimental structure is unusual in that the primary comparison is **not between competing methods** but between **precision configurations of the same training pipeline**:
  - **High-precision flash attention (FP32):** The stable baseline. All attention computations performed in FP32, or specific sub-operations selectively elevated to FP32 for ablation purposes. This configuration converges normally.
  - **Low-precision flash attention (BF16):** The failing configuration. All attention computations in BF16 mixed precision (BF16 forward, FP32 backward, FP32 master weights). This configuration exhibits catastrophic loss explosion.
  - **Standard (non-flash) attention:** Used as a diagnostic substitution—when standard attention replaces flash attention in specific layers, stability is restored, confirming the failure is specific to the flash attention implementation rather than the attention mechanism in general.
  - **Alternative δ computation:** The mathematically equivalent formulation `δ = rowsum(dP ∘ P)` used in place of the efficient `δ = rowsum(dO ∘ O)`. This serves as a diagnostic baseline that avoids using the forward pass's low-precision `O`.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use "generations" or inference budgets in the sense of the reference example paper—this is a pure training analysis. Compute cost is measured indirectly through **training steps** (up to 600K for the stabilized method, with failure typically occurring at 6,500–7,000 steps). All experiments use 4 NVIDIA A100 (80GB) GPUs with Distributed Data Parallel, a micro-batch size of 32 per GPU, gradient accumulation over 4 steps, and an effective global batch size of 524,288 tokens per optimization step. The BF16 mixed-precision configuration is standard PyTorch automatic mixed precision (AMP). Hardware cross-validation (mentioned in the Discussion, Section 5) confirms the failure pattern on NVIDIA RTX 4090 and Huawei Ascend 910B, though no quantitative results from these platforms are reported.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** There is no statistical cross-validation in the traditional ML sense, because the paper conducts a deterministic mechanistic investigation rather than measuring stochastic model performance. The reproducibility mechanism—recording and reusing fixed data batches—ensures that each experimental condition is tested on identical data. The paper relies on **consilience of evidence** across independent diagnostic methods rather than statistical significance: the tiling ablation, the layer-wise isolation, the head-wise spectral norm analysis, the gradient error decomposition, the cumulative coefficient tracking, and the bit-level rounding example all converge on the same causal mechanism, and the stabilization modification (which would fail if the diagnosis were wrong) succeeds.

### Main Quantitative Results

The experimental results in this paper follow a diagnostic rather than comparative logic. Rather than benchmarking Model A against Model B at various compute budgets, the paper demonstrates a causal chain through intervention experiments. I organize the results in the order of the diagnostic cascade.

#### Reproducing the Failure (Section 3.1)

The baseline BF16 flash attention configuration exhibits **catastrophic loss explosion** after several thousand training steps. Figure 2 shows validation loss decreasing from approximately 10-11 to roughly 3-4 over the first ~6,000 steps, then abruptly spiking to approximately 10 (and ultimately to infinity/NaN shortly thereafter). The FP32 flash attention configuration converges normally throughout the training run, with validation loss declining smoothly and stabilizing. The paper does not report final validation loss values for the FP32 run beyond showing the curve shape in Figure 2.

This pattern is consistent with the community-reported failures: "This failure case, reported for over two years, manifests as a sudden loss explosion after several thousand training steps" (Section 3.1). The reproducibility trick (fixed data ordering) ensures that the failure is deterministic: every run with BF16 flash attention and this configuration will fail at the same point, enabling the precise step-by-step ablation analysis that follows.

#### Isolating the Failure Source (Section 3.2)

**Tiling is ruled out (no quantitative figure).** Setting the block size equal to the full sequence length (1024), which forces non-tiled flash attention, does not prevent the loss explosion. This is a qualitative result: the training still fails with identical characteristics, confirming that the block-wise processing strategy is not responsible.

**Failure originates specifically in layer 2 (Figure 11).** The spectral norm analysis across all layers and training steps (Figure 11 in Appendix) reveals that layer 2's attention weights develop an anomalous spectral norm spike prior to failure. Two confirmatory experiments establish causality:
- Using flash attention **only in layer 2** (standard attention elsewhere) reproduces the failure.
- Replacing flash attention with standard attention **only in layer 2** (while retaining it in all other layers) restores stability.

The paper does not report separate loss curves for these experiments; they are described qualitatively as confirming that "flash attention in layer 2 as the origin of the failure."

**The `δ` computation is the critical term.** Replacing `δ = rowsum(dO ∘ O)` with the mathematically equivalent `δ = rowsum(dP ∘ P)` in the backward pass restores training stability. This is a qualitative pass/fail result. The precise loss values or training curves for this configuration are not plotted separately; the result is reported as a diagnostic finding in the text.

**The low-precision output `O_lp` is the root cause within the module.** Two targeted experiments:
- Recomputing `O = P V` in FP32 within the backward pass (instead of reusing the forward pass's BF16 `O`) stabilizes training.
- Computing `O` in FP32 during the forward pass, while keeping all other operations in BF16, also stabilizes training.

These results converge on the finding that numerical errors introduced in the BF16 computation of `O` during the forward pass contaminate the backward pass through `δ`, producing corrupted gradients. Claim 1 ("Low-precision `δ_lp = rowsum(dO ∘ O_lp)` causes training failure") is the formal statement.

**The failure concentrates in specific attention heads (Figure 3).** Within layer 2, the spectral norms of `W_Q` (query projection) across the 12 attention heads are highly non-uniform. The values shown in Figure 3 are approximately:
- Head 1: 4.34
- Head 2: 2.68
- Head 3: 1.90
- Head 4: 2.67
- Head 5: 2.04
- Head 6: 2.66
- Head 7: 4.12
- **Head 8: 6.27** (the outlier)
- Head 9: 5.32
- Head 10: 1.74
- Head 11: 4.64
- Head 12: 5.39

Selectively computing `O` in high precision for the outlier heads (1, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12) is sufficient to restore training stability. The paper focuses subsequent detailed analysis on head 8 (highest spectral norm) as a representative case.

#### Gradient Error Decomposition Evidence (Section 3.3.1)

**The structural similarity of `(PK)[T]ᵀ X[T]` across tokens and training steps (Figure 4).** Figure 4 presents visual evidence in the form of heatmaps:
- Panels (a) and (d): `PK` matrices for head 8 at training steps 6,610 and 6,619, batch indices 190 and 209 respectively. Both show similar patterns of alternating positive (red) and negative (blue) bands along the feature dimension (horizontal axis), with the structure being visually near-identical across these different contexts.
- Panels (b) and (e): `X` (input) matrices for the same contexts, also showing strongly similar banded structure.
- Panels (c) and (f): The rank-1 outer products `(PK)[T]ᵀ X[T]` for specific tokens (token 50 at step 6,610 and token 718 at step 6,619). The resulting matrices show structural similarity, particularly at input feature columns 546 and 678 (highlighted), where both matrices exhibit similar patterns of positive and negative values.

The paper does not quantify similarity with a numerical metric (e.g., cosine similarity, correlation coefficient). The evidence is visual and qualitative: "the rows of PK and X exhibit strong structural similarity across different training steps and token positions." Appendix D extends this analysis to Llama-3.1-8B (Figure 8), where specific columns are annotated with cosine similarity values of 0.999994, 0.999994, 0.999994, and 0.999993, providing quantitative confirmation of the visual pattern in a different model.

**The cumulative coefficient sum is positively biased (Figure 5a).** Tracking `Σ_T (δ_lp − δ_hp)[T]` cumulatively over training steps 6,580 to 6,680 (the period immediately preceding the loss explosion), the sum grows from approximately 0 to roughly 0.00038 over 100 steps. The trace is consistently positive, with no significant negative excursions. This demonstrates that the scalar coefficient weighting the low-rank error direction `R` is systematically biased, meaning the weight update error does not oscillate or cancel—it accumulates monotonically in direction `R`. Claim 2 captures this: the low-rank structure provides the accumulation pathway, and the positively-biased coefficients provide the consistent push.

#### Rounding Error Analysis Evidence (Section 3.3.2)

**Large error contributors identified at specific features (Figure 5b-c).** For a specific token position `T = 718` at training step 6,619 (where `(δ_lp − δ_hp)[T]` is large and positive), Figure 5(b) shows:
- `O[T,:]` values span roughly `[−2.5, +2.0]` across features, with features 20 and 29 showing negative values around `−2.0`.
- `dO[T,:]` values span roughly `[−1.5 × 10⁻⁶, +1.5 × 10⁻⁶]`, with features 20 and 29 showing matching negative values around `−1.0 × 10⁻⁶` to `−1.5 × 10⁻⁶`.

Figure 5(c) shows `O_lp[T,:] − O_hp[T,:]` (the output error) alongside `dO[T,:]`. The output error is most negative (approximately `−1.2 × 10⁻²` to `−1.4 × 10⁻²`) at features 20 and 29—exactly where `dO` is also most negative. The sign correlation means their product is positive, contributing positively to `(δ_lp − δ_hp)[T]`.

**Cumulative error in `P̄V` jumps at `P̄ = 1` positions (Figure 6).** Figure 6(b) plots the cumulative error `Ō_error(t)` (Equation 5) as a function of token position `t` during the summation, overlaid with the attention probability `P̄[T,:]`. Large negative jumps in the cumulative error (drops of approximately `−0.006` to `−0.014`) occur precisely at token positions where `P̄ = 1`. Figure 6(c) provides a zoomed view (tokens 630-680) confirming the alignment. Figure 6(a) shows that most entries of `V[:, i]` for feature `i = 20` are negative (concentrated between approximately `−2` and `0`), establishing the same-sign condition needed for overflow and biased rounding.

**The bit-level worked example produces a quantified rounding error.** The concrete BF16 addition example in Section 3.3.2 computes:
- True FP32 sum: `−4.703990459442139`
- BF16 result after round-up: `−4.71875`
- Single-addition rounding error: `−0.014759540557861328`

This is a single addition. The paper notes that "when such rounding events occur systematically across many additions in the `P̄V` product, the errors accumulate, creating the negative bias in `O` that ultimately destabilizes training." Claim 3 formalizes this as the root cause.

#### Stabilization Validation (Section 4)

**GPT-2S with AdamW (Figure 7a).** The stabilized flash attention (SFA, with `β = 2`) is trained for 600K steps alongside the original flash attention. Validation loss is plotted on a log scale:
- Standard FP32 flash attention (not shown in this figure, but implied by the "Flash Attention" curve): diverges at approximately step 6,500–7,000 (loss spikes on the log scale).
- Stabilized flash attention: loss decreases smoothly and consistently throughout the full 600K training run. The paper does not report final validation loss values; the curves show qualitative divergence and convergence respectively.

**GPT-2S with Muon optimizer (Figure 7b).** Using the Muon optimizer (Jordan et al., 2024) with RMS-matched variant, learning rate `5 × 10⁻⁴`, weight decay `0.01`, the stabilized flash attention trains stably for 600K steps while standard flash attention diverges. The loss curves show approximately the same qualitative pattern as AdamW, with SFA converging smoothly and the baseline exploding. This result is important because it demonstrates the fix is **optimizer-independent**: the failure is in the attention computation, not in how gradients are applied.

**GPT-2M with AdamW (Figure 7c).** The larger GPT-2 Medium model is trained for 100K steps. Stabilized flash attention converges normally; standard flash attention diverges. The x-axis scale is different (shorter training duration), but the qualitative pattern is consistent, showing the mechanism scales to larger models (at least within the GPT-2 family).

**Frequency of multiple maxima as a leading indicator (Figure 13, Appendix).** The paper plots the "Number of Activated SFA Condition" (frequency of rows where multiple identical maxima are detected) alongside the validation loss over the training run. The multiple maxima count begins to increase several hundred steps before the loss explodes (rising visibly around step 6,500 while loss spikes at ~7,000). This temporal correlation suggests the multiple-maxima condition is a **leading indicator** of impending failure, consistent with the proposed causal mechanism (multiple maxima → biased rounding → error accumulation → loss explosion).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper's ablation structure is inseparable from the main diagnostic narrative (Section 3.2), since the entire investigation is built as a sequence of precision-substitution experiments. What follows are the non-obvious design choices and robustness checks that go beyond the main diagnostic path.

**Tiling disabled (full-sequence block size):** Eliminating block-wise processing does not prevent the loss explosion. This rules out tiling-specific numerical interactions (e.g., block-boundary alignment, partial softmax rescaling) as potential causes and simplifies all subsequent analysis to the non-tiled computation without loss of generality. Reported qualitatively; no separate loss curve.

**Alternative `δ` computation (`rowsum(dP ∘ P)` vs. `rowsum(dO ∘ O)`):** Using the mathematically equivalent alternative formulation restores stability. This conclusively isolates the error to the stored `O_lp` rather than implicating the backward pass logic or the gradient of the softmax. The two formulations are identical in exact arithmetic; the fact that one fails and the other doesn't is direct evidence of `O_lp` corruption.

**Per-layer substitution (flash attention only in layer 2 vs. standard attention only in layer 2):** The symmetric design—showing that flash attention in layer 2 alone is sufficient to cause failure AND that removing it from layer 2 alone prevents failure—establishes necessity and sufficiency. This is stronger than a one-directional test would be.

**Per-head precision substitution (outlier heads 1, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12):** Computing `O` in FP32 for only these six heads (out of 12 heads across 12 layers = 144 total) is sufficient to stabilize training. This demonstrates that the failure is not a distributed effect requiring all heads to contribute—a handful of heads with large spectral norms drive the instability. The paper does not test which single head is minimally sufficient; the claim is that these six collectively account for the failure.

**Naive fixed-offset approach to softmax modification (Appendix C):** The paper explicitly describes and rejects a simpler approach: subtracting a small fixed constant `ε` from the maximum to prevent `P̄ = 1`. The explanation is that this would cause all `P̄` entries to be consistently rounded in one direction during BF16 conversion (since they are all scaled by the same factor), introducing a **fixed systematic bias** that would propagate into `Ō = P̄ V` and reintroduce the failure. This negative result justifies the conditional dynamic approach over the simpler alternative. No experimental loss curves are shown for this rejected approach.

**Conditional application of dynamic maximum (Appendix C):** The paper justifies applying the `m = β rm` or `m = 0` adjustments only when multiple maxima are detected, not unconditionally. The argument is that applying the adjustment to rows with a single large positive maximum would cause `exp(−(β − 1)rm)` to underflow to zero, making the normalization factor zero and causing division-by-zero in `O`. This design choice is motivated by numerical analysis rather than backed by separate experiments—the paper does not show what happens if the adjustment is applied unconditionally, but the reasoning about underflow is sound.

**Choice of `m = 0` for negative repeated maxima (Appendix C):** The authors considered an alternative `m = γ rm` with `γ ∈ (0, 1)` for negative repeated maxima. However, if `γ` is close to 1, the adjusted maximum `(1 − γ)rm` is close to zero, and `exp((1 − γ)rm)` could round back to exactly 1 in BF16, reintroducing the `P̄ = 1` condition. Setting `m = 0` is described as a robust choice that ensures the exponent remains negative. Again, this is a design justification rather than an experimentally ablated choice.

**Choice of `β ∈ [2, 8]`:** The range for the scaling factor is empirically motivated: too small (close to 1) risks `exp(−(β − 1)rm)` rounding to 1 in BF16, defeating the fix; too large risks underflow. The main experiments use `β = 2`. The paper does not sweep `β` values to show sensitivity—this is a parameter chosen a priori based on reasoning about the numerics, not optimized empirically.

**Llama-3.1-8B structural pattern replication (Appendix D):** To assess generalizability beyond GPT-2, the paper visualizes `(PK)[T]ᵀ X[T]` and attention score distributions in Llama-3.1-8B (Figures 8 and 9). Figure 8 shows rank-1 outer products for different tokens with cosine similarities of 0.999993–0.999994 between corresponding columns, confirming the low-rank structural similarity pattern exists in a different model architecture and scale. Figure 9 shows attention probability distributions with multiple maxima (including attention sink behavior where the first token has high probability) across various layers and heads. This is observational evidence, not a training experiment—the paper does not claim that Llama-3.1-8B will fail in BF16 training, only that the structural conditions that enable the failure are present.

**Hardware independence (Discussion, Section 5, qualitative):** The paper mentions that the findings are "consistent across various hardware (NVIDIA A100, RTX 4090, Huawei Ascend 910B)" without presenting separate loss curves or quantitative comparisons. This claim should be interpreted as: the same BF16 rounding behavior (IEEE 754 compliant) produces the same failure pattern on these platforms, which is expected if they implement standard BF16 arithmetic.

**Data randomness eliminated as confounding variable:** The fixed-data-ordering reproducibility mechanism is itself a robustness check. Without it, one might attribute failure/non-failure to data-related randomness rather than precision effects. The paper does not run multiple random seeds with the failing configuration to report a failure rate, but the deterministic reproduction under fixed data establishes that the failure is not a stochastic fluke.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "Low-precision `δ_lp = rowsum(dO ∘ O_lp)` causes training failure."

**What was demonstrated:** The precision-substitution experiments in Section 3.2 show that elevating the computation of `O` to FP32 (either in the forward pass or by recomputing from `P` and `V` in the backward pass) restores training stability. The evidence is strong as far as it goes: `O_lp` is clearly *implicated* in the failure.

**What was not demonstrated:** The paper does not show that *all* failures of BF16 flash attention training reduce to this mechanism. There could be other failure modes (involving different layers, different attention heads, or different arithmetic operations) that the precision-substitution methodology didn't encounter because it was applied only to this specific deterministic reproduction. The claim is that `O_lp` causes *this* failure, not that it is the *only possible* cause of any low-precision flash attention failure. Additionally, the paper doesn't verify whether computing `O` in BF16 but `δ` in FP32 (a more targeted intervention) would also stabilize training—this would more precisely pinpoint whether it's the corruption of `δ` specifically or the forward pass `O` in general that matters.

**Conditional qualifier:** The failure is demonstrated on a specific architecture (GPT-2S), a specific dataset (OpenWebText with fixed ordering), a specific optimizer (AdamW), and a specific precision format (BF16). Generalization to other configurations is plausible given the bit-level mechanism but not experimentally verified.

#### Claim 2: "Weight update in low-precision training is biased by `(δ_lp − δ_hp)[T] R`, which arises from structurally similar matrices across tokens and training steps, and its positively-biased coefficient. This bias accumulates error, preventing cancellation, increasing weight spectral norm and activation, and leading to loss explosion."

**What was demonstrated:** The decomposition of the gradient error (Equations 1-3) is mathematically sound—it follows directly from the chain rule and the definitions of the attention mechanism. The structural similarity of `PK` and `X` is shown visually in Figure 4 (GPT-2) and Figure 8 (Llama-3.1-8B). The cumulative coefficient sum (Figure 5a) shows persistent positive bias over 100 training steps. The spectral norm spike (Figure 11) shows anomalous growth in the implicated weights.

**What was not demonstrated:** Several links in this causal chain are established by observation and reasoning rather than by direct intervention:

1. **The link from "similar low-rank structure" to "error accumulation rather than cancellation" is not experimentally intervened upon.** The paper does not, for example, artificially randomize the structure of `PK` or `X` to show that this prevents failure. The argument is that structural similarity *explains* why errors accumulate, but this is an interpretation, not a demonstrated causal necessity. It is possible that errors would accumulate even without structural similarity if the coefficient bias were large enough, or that structural similarity is a correlate rather than a cause.

2. **The approximation `≈ α Σ_T (δ_lp − δ_hp)[T] R` (Equation 3) is not quantitatively validated.** The paper does not report how well `R` approximates the individual `(PK)[T]ᵀ X[T]` matrices—no reconstruction error, no singular value decomposition, no explained variance. The visual similarity in Figure 4 is suggestive but not quantified for the GPT-2 case. The cosine similarity values (0.999993–0.999994) in Appendix D for Llama-3.1-8B are for specific columns of the outer product matrices, not for the full matrices. A more rigorous demonstration would include a Frobenius norm comparison or principal component analysis showing that a single direction `R` captures most of the variation.

3. **The claim that the error "increases weight spectral norm and activation" is supported by Figure 11 showing the correlation, but causation is not experimentally isolated.** The paper does not show that preventing the biased weight update (e.g., by using the stabilized flash attention) prevents the spectral norm growth, which would close the loop. The spectral norm data comes from the failing configuration; a comparison with the stabilized configuration would strengthen this claim.

#### Claim 3: "With more than one `P̄[T,t] = 1` and negative `V[t,i]`, the addition in `P̄V` can cause overflow of significand. This necessitates a right shift and a round-up of significand due to sticky bit, introducing negative rounding error in `O` and leading to positive `(δ_lp − δ_hp)[T]`."

**What was demonstrated:** The bit-level analysis is precise and rigorous—it specifies the exact BF16 representations, the overflow event, the right shift, the sticky bit activation, and the round-up decision for a concrete example. The mechanism is consistent with IEEE 754 behavior and is not speculative. Figure 6 shows the correlation between `P̄ = 1` positions and large error jumps. The analysis of why `P̄ < 1` doesn't cause biased rounding (Remark 1) is sound.

**What was not demonstrated:** The paper analyzes **one** rounding event in detail (the example in Section 3.3.2 with operands −2.4071154594421387 and −2.296875). It does not provide statistics on how many such events occur per training step, what fraction of the total error they account for, or whether they are the *dominant* source of bias versus other potential contributors (e.g., the subtraction in the exponent alignment, or rounding in the final normalization of `O` from `Ō`). The claim that these events cause the failure is supported by the intervention in Section 4 (preventing `P̄ = 1` prevents the failure), but the causal chain from "individual rounding event" to "dominant contributor to cumulative bias" is not quantified.

#### Does the stabilisation experiment validate all three claims simultaneously?

The stabilized flash attention (Section 4) is the capstone validation experiment. By preventing `P̄ = 1` (the condition from Claim 3), it eliminates the biased rounding and produces stable training. Since the modification targets only the `P̄ = 1` condition and changes nothing else, its success provides strong evidence that:
- The `P̄ = 1` condition is necessary for the failure (Claim 3).
- The failure propagates through `O_lp` → `δ_lp` → biased weight updates (Claim 1).
- The mechanism is deterministic, not stochastic (since a deterministic code change deterministically eliminates the failure).

However, the success of SFA does **not** independently validate Claim 2 (the low-rank accumulation pathway). The SFA fix prevents biased rounding from occurring at all, so the role of structural similarity in *accumulating* errors is not tested by this experiment. Claim 2 could be wrong in its specifics—perhaps errors would accumulate even without low-rank structure, or perhaps the low-rank structure is not the primary reason for accumulation—and the SFA fix would still work because it eliminates the error at the source. Claim 2 is an explanation of *how* the error that SFA eliminates would have caused failure; it is the most interpretive and least experimentally intervened-upon part of the argument.

#### Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design

**Single failure reproduction, single model family, single precision format.** All quantitative training experiments use GPT-2S or GPT-2M with BF16 on OpenWebText. The authors do not replicate the failure on other architectures (e.g., LLaMA-style, encoder-decoder), other tasks (e.g., fine-tuning, other datasets), or other low-precision formats (FP8, FP16). The mechanism is argued to be general based on hardware-level arithmetic properties, but neural network training involves complex interactions between architecture, data distribution, and optimization. The Llama-3.1-8B analysis (Appendix D) shows that the structural patterns exist but does not demonstrate that training would fail. A demonstration that Llama-3.1-8B with BF16 flash attention *actually fails* and is *actually stabilized by SFA* would substantially strengthen generality claims.

**The fixed-data reproducibility mechanism is a double-edged sword.** By fixing the data ordering, the paper eliminates randomness and enables precise ablation. But this means all results are from **a single training run** (or its precision-substituted variants). We don't know whether the failure would occur with other random seeds, other data orderings, or other subsets of OpenWebText. The community reports (nanoGPT issues) suggest the failure occurs across multiple independent runs, which mitigates this concern somewhat, but the paper's specific analysis is tied to one deterministic instantiation.

**No statistical characterization of the failure rate.** The paper doesn't report what fraction of BF16 flash attention training runs experience this failure, either in their own experiments or aggregated from community reports. Lee et al. (2024) reported ~10% of GPT-2 BF16 runs diverged—if this failure accounts for all of those, 10% is a practically significant but not guaranteed failure rate. Understanding the failure rate would help calibrate how urgent the fix is for practitioners.

**The spectral norm analysis (Figure 11) is presented as a heatmap without numerical values.** The color scale ranges from 10-80, but individual spectral norm values for specific layers at specific time points are not readable from the figure. This makes it difficult to assess whether the spectral norm growth is truly anomalous or within normal variation.

**No experiments on the interaction with existing stabilization techniques.** The paper argues (Section 5, Discussion) that QK normalization and Gated Attention work by "disrupting the underlying structure" that allows errors to accumulate. This is a hypothesis, not a tested claim. Training runs with QK normalization + BF16 flash attention (with and without SFA) would test whether the existing techniques prevent the failure through the claimed mechanism, or through some other pathway that SFA doesn't address.

**Limited exploration of SFA hyperparameters.** Only `β = 2` is used in the main experiments. The paper states that `β ∈ [2, 8]` is acceptable based on numerical reasoning, but doesn't show loss curves for other `β` values, doesn't test whether larger `β` impacts model quality (perplexity) even if training remains stable, and doesn't explore whether the optimal `β` varies with model scale, context length, or training duration. The claim that SFA is a "practical solution" would be stronger with evidence of robustness to `β` choice.

#### Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper

- **Training Llama-3.1-8B (or similar) with BF16 flash attention to see if the same failure occurs and is fixed by SFA.** This would directly test generality across architectures and scales.
- **Direct manipulation of the low-rank structure to test Claim 2:** For example, adding random noise to `PK` or `X` during training to disrupt structural similarity, and showing that this prevents or delays failure even without fixing the rounding bias.
- **Quantitative reconstruction analysis for the `R` approximation:** SVD of the set of `(PK)[T]ᵀ X[T]` matrices across tokens and steps to show what fraction of variance is captured by a single direction, and whether this fraction increases as training approaches the failure point.
- **FP8 replication:** The paper argues the analytical framework generalizes to lower precision. Showing that the same mechanism causes failure in FP8 (and that a similar fix works) would be a strong validation.
- **Comparison with QK normalization:** Training runs showing that QK normalization prevents the spectral norm spike in layer 2, confirming the paper's hypothesis about *how* existing fixes work.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: Difficulty Estimation Relies on a Prohibitively Expensive Pre-Computation

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework depends on knowing each prompt's difficulty bin before allocating the test-time budget. The paper's method for estimating difficulty—generating 2048 samples per question and averaging the PRM's final-answer scores—is extraordinarily expensive. The authors acknowledge this explicitly in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence.** The headline efficiency gains (4× over best-of-N) are computed *after* difficulty is already known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. At 2048 samples per question, the difficulty estimation step alone consumes more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations). In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation plus strategy execution, and the former would dominate. This means the 4× figure should be understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** under the assumption that difficulty can be estimated for free—which it currently cannot. A practitioner deploying this method would find that the upfront cost of difficulty estimation erases most or all of the reported gains unless a cheaper estimation method is available.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The cost disparity is visible simply by comparing the numbers: the difficulty estimation uses 2048 samples per question (Section 3.2), while the compute-optimal gains are demonstrated at budgets of 16–256 generations (Figures 4 and 8). The paper does not report total FLOPs or wall-clock time for difficulty estimation, nor does it include a break-even analysis showing at what inference volume the amortized estimation cost becomes negligible.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation. Section 8 flags it as a key avenue for future work: "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question." No such model is developed or evaluated. The paper also does not explore **adaptive estimation** that could amortize difficulty assessment into the solution process itself (e.g., using the first 8–16 samples to coarsely estimate difficulty, then allocating the remaining budget accordingly). Until a cheap difficulty estimator is demonstrated, the compute-optimal framework is a proof of concept rather than a deployable system.

---

### Limitation 2: All Results Come from a Single Benchmark (MATH) with a Single Model Family (PaLM 2-S*)

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses the MATH benchmark (500 test questions, high-school competition mathematics) with PaLM 2-S\* as the base model. The authors state they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this is an unverified claim.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or benchmark-specific rather than universal:

- **PRM quality and over-optimization behavior depend on PaLM 2-S\*'s output distribution.** A model with different calibration properties, different error patterns, or a different base pass@1 on MATH would yield different difficulty-dependent scaling curves. The specific thresholds where beam search outperforms best-of-N (Figure 3) would shift, possibly substantially.
- **The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples** depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary widely across model families (some models are far better at leveraging in-context corrections than others).
- **MATH is a specific reasoning domain** requiring symbolic manipulation and multi-step deduction. The difficulty-dependent patterns—beam search hurting easy problems, revisions helping easy problems, neither helping hard problems—may not generalize to code generation (where unit tests provide cleaner verification signals), factual QA (where retrieval rather than reasoning dominates), or open-ended generation (where correctness is ambiguous).

A practitioner using a different model (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, open-source models) or a different task domain (code, science, dialogue) cannot assume the same difficulty bins, the same strategy rankings, or the same 4× efficiency gains without replication.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All quantitative results (Figures 2–9, Tables 1–3) are from MATH + PaLM 2-S\*. The paper provides no replication on any other dataset or model. The FLOPs-matched comparison uses a second PaLM 2 model family member (14× larger), but both are from the same architecture and training regime.

**Mitigation status.** No mitigation is attempted. The paper acknowledges in Section 8 that extending the analysis to other models and tasks is future work. The claim that PaLM 2-S\* is "representative" is a statement of belief, not an empirical finding. A replication on at least one additional model family (e.g., an open-source model like LLaMA) and one additional reasoning benchmark (e.g., GSM8K for math, HumanEval for code) would substantially strengthen the generality argument but is absent.

---

### Limitation 3: The 14× Larger Model Baseline Is Weakened by Non-Compute-Optimal Pretraining and No Test-Time Compute

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 uses a larger PaLM 2 model trained by scaling parameters only (holding data fixed), following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023) rather than the Chinchilla-optimal approach where both parameters and data are scaled equally. Additionally, the larger model uses **greedy decoding only**—no test-time compute augmentation of any kind (no majority voting, no best-of-N, no beam search). The authors are transparent about this:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

**The consequence.** Both design choices make the pretraining baseline **weaker than it could be**, which inflates the apparent advantage of test-time compute:

- A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with the same total FLOPs would achieve better performance than a parameter-only-scaled model, narrowing or reversing the test-time compute advantage on the medium-hard problems where the current comparison shows a draw or slight disadvantage.
- Granting the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget (e.g., best-of-8 or best-of-32) would create a far more realistic comparison: large model with simple inference augmentation vs. small model with sophisticated inference augmentation. The current comparison is asymmetric—it tests whether a small model with smart inference can beat a large model with **no inference strategy at all**.

The headline result that "test-time compute with a smaller model can outperform a 14× larger model" is therefore conditional on a specific and arguably weak pretraining baseline. Against a compute-optimally trained larger model with basic best-of-N, the advantage would be smaller and might disappear on some difficulty tiers.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The FLOPs-matched results are in Figure 9 and the bar charts in Figure 1. The paper explicitly states the parameter-only scaling choice in Section 7. No ablation tests the sensitivity of the comparison to the pretraining recipe or to giving the larger model its own test-time compute budget.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation and defers compute-optimal pretraining comparisons to future work. However, it does not acknowledge or discuss the asymmetry of giving the smaller model sophisticated test-time strategies while the larger model gets none. This is a meaningful oversight: even a simple best-of-8 on the larger model would make the comparison more informative about the *marginal* value of compute-optimal test-time scaling over naive scaling.

### Limitation 4: Revisions and PRM Search Are Never Combined, Despite Complementary Strengths

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies two complementary axes for improving test-time compute—modifying the proposal distribution via sequential revisions (Section 6) and improving output selection via PRM-guided search (Section 5)—entirely independently. The two mechanisms are never deployed together in any experiment. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges this:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

**The consequence.** The paper's results represent a **lower bound** on what test-time compute can achieve. The two mechanisms have complementary strengths that the paper's own analysis reveals:

- **Revisions** are most effective on easy problems where the model's initial attempts are roughly correct and just need local refinement (Section 6, Figure 7 right, bin 1).
- **PRM search** is most effective on medium problems where the model needs to explore qualitatively different solution strategies (Section 5, Figure 3 right, bins 3–4).
- On hard problems, neither helps individually, but a combination—using the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search, or using the PRM to guide which revisions to pursue—might break through the individual performance ceilings.

The independent analysis of the two mechanisms is valuable for understanding their difficulty-dependent behavior, but the absence of any combined experiment means a practitioner cannot estimate how much additional gain would come from deploying both simultaneously. Given that the paper's central thesis is about *optimal allocation* of test-time compute, leaving the interaction between the two main allocation dimensions unexplored is a significant gap.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Sections 5 and 6 report results for search and revisions separately. The compute-optimal policies (Figures 4 and 8) are derived independently for each mechanism. No figure or table shows combined performance.

**Mitigation status.** The paper flags this as future work (Section 8). A combined experiment would require training a PRM on revision model outputs (the base model PRM doesn't transfer well, as shown in Appendix J, Figure 15a) and integrating the revision context into the search procedure, both of which are non-trivial engineering tasks. The paper does not provide even a preliminary or approximate combination to bound what might be possible.

### Limitation 5: The Revision Model Suffers from a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate with No Principled Solution

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained exclusively on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect followed by a correct target (Section 6.1). This means it never learns what to do when the current answer is already correct. At test time, the model therefore frequently "revises" correct answers into incorrect ones. The paper reports the scale of this problem:

> "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones" (Section 6.1)

**The consequence.** The revision model is **intrinsically unstable**: every revision step has a non-trivial probability of degrading a correct answer. The paper mitigates this with post-hoc selection mechanisms—majority voting or verifier-based selection across the entire revision chain, picking the best answer from any point rather than always taking the last revision. But these are patches that add computational overhead (the verifier must score every revision step) and do not address the root cause (the model doesn't know how to recognize "already correct" as a valid state).

A practitioner deploying sequential revisions must accept that correct answers will be lost during the revision process and rely on the selection mechanism to recover them. This creates a ceiling on what revisions can achieve: as the chain length grows, the probability that a correct answer gets revised into an incorrect one increases, and the selection mechanism must be increasingly reliable to compensate. The paper does not characterize how this ceiling scales with chain length or verifier quality.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% figure is reported in Section 6.1. The failure of ReST^EM-trained revisions (Appendix K, Figure 16), where sequential revisions **substantially hurt** performance, further demonstrates the fragility of the revision approach when the training data distribution shifts. The paper does not report the per-step reversion rate as a function of chain position (does it increase, decrease, or stay constant?), nor does it analyze what fraction of final answers come from intermediate revision steps versus the last step.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the problem indirectly (it's the reason for within-chain selection) but does not propose a principled solution. A training data fix—including trajectories where the correct answer appears early and is preserved unchanged through subsequent steps—is not explored. The Discussion mentions that techniques like QK normalization and Gated Attention may help by disrupting structural error patterns, but this is speculative and not tested in the context of revisions.

---

### Limitation 6: No Accounting for Latency, Only Total Generation Count

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures test-time compute exclusively in "generations"—the number of complete solutions sampled. This is a reasonable proxy for total FLOPs consumed, but it ignores **wall-clock latency**: how long the user or downstream system must wait for an answer. The paper does not discuss latency at any point.

**The consequence.** Sequential revision strategies—which the compute-optimal policy favors for easy problems (Figure 7, right, bin 1)—are **inherently serial**. A budget of 64 sequential revisions produces one answer after 64 serial forward passes. A budget of 64 parallel samples produces 64 answers simultaneously (given sufficient hardware) in the time of one forward pass. The compute-optimal policy might recommend serial strategies that achieve higher accuracy per FLOP but take 64× longer wall-clock time than a parallel strategy at the same FLOP budget.

For latency-sensitive applications—interactive chat, real-time code generation, on-device assistants—this tradeoff dominates the FLOPs-efficiency consideration. A practitioner might happily spend 4× more FLOPs for a parallel strategy that returns results 64× faster. The paper's framing of "4× more efficient" refers only to total computation, not to time-to-answer, and the compute-optimal policy does not incorporate a latency constraint or a latency-FLOPs Pareto frontier analysis.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The distinction between serial and parallel strategies is discussed in Section 6 (Figure 5 contrasts fully parallel, fully sequential, and hybrid architectures), but only in terms of accuracy per generation budget, never in terms of wall-clock time. The paper does not report inference latency for any configuration, nor does it specify whether experiments were run with sufficient hardware parallelism to make parallel sampling truly concurrent.

**Mitigation status.** None. The paper does not mention latency as a concern, does not propose latency-aware allocation policies, and does not include latency as a dimension in the compute-optimal framework. A practitioner would need to independently measure latency and manually trade off the FLOPs-efficiency gains against the time-to-answer requirements of their specific deployment context.
