# Iterative Reasoning Preference Optimization

**ArXiv:** [2404.19733](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19733)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper proposes **Iterative Reasoning Preference Optimization** (Iterative RPO), an iterative training algorithm that improves chain-of-thought reasoning by generating multiple candidate solutions, constructing preference pairs based on final-answer correctness, and training with a modified DPO loss augmented by a negative log-likelihood (NLL) term on the winning sequences.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **Iterative Reasoning Preference Optimization** (Iterative RPO), an iterative training algorithm that improves chain-of-thought reasoning by generating multiple candidate solutions, constructing preference pairs based on final-answer correctness, and training with a modified DPO loss augmented by a negative log-likelihood (NLL) term on the winning sequences. Evaluating on GSM8K, MATH, and ARC-Challenge using Llama-2-70B-Chat, the method yields large gains over baselines — increasing GSM8K accuracy from 55.6% to 81.6% and reaching 88.7% with majority voting out of 32 samples — while relying only on the training set's provided answers, establishing that iterative preference optimization can substantially improve reasoning when the NLL term prevents the probability of chosen sequences from decreasing during training.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Preference Optimization Methods Don't Work Well for Reasoning

The paper addresses a specific and puzzling gap in the current LLM training landscape: **iterative preference optimization methods — which have proven highly effective for general instruction tuning — consistently fail to deliver meaningful improvements on reasoning tasks.** This is not a hypothetical concern; it's an empirical finding that multiple research groups have independently observed. Yuan et al. (2024) and Chen et al. (2024) both report that their iterative DPO-based approaches yield "only moderate gains or even decrease the performance on standard reasoning tasks" (Section 1), despite showing substantial improvements on general instruction following.

This gap matters for several reasons:

- **Practical deployment cost**: If preference optimization can't improve reasoning, practitioners must rely on other, often more expensive, methods — such as expert human annotation of reasoning chains, large-scale data curation, or reinforcement learning with online sampling — to improve their models' reasoning capabilities. This raises the barrier to entry for organizations that want to build models that can reliably solve math problems, answer science questions, or perform multi-step logical deduction.

- **Theoretical significance**: Reasoning is fundamentally different from general instruction following. In instruction tuning, the goal is to align the model's outputs with human stylistic or factual preferences — a matter of matching a distribution of "good" responses. In reasoning, however, there is a ground-truth correct answer, and the intermediate reasoning steps (chain-of-thought) can be logically flawed even when the final answer happens to be right, or vice versa. The fact that methods successful in one regime fail in the other suggests that our understanding of preference optimization is incomplete, and that the specific structure of reasoning tasks demands different algorithmic treatment.

- **Self-improvement pipelines**: The broader vision of self-improving AI systems — where models generate their own training data and iteratively refine their capabilities — depends on algorithms that can effectively leverage model-generated reasoning attempts. STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) and ReST$^{EM}$ (Singh et al., 2024) pioneered this vision for reasoning using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on filtered correct generations. But if preference optimization could be made to work for reasoning, it would open the door to using *negative examples* (incorrect reasoning chains) as a training signal, which SFT-based methods discard entirely. The paper argues that "preference optimization allows the use of negative examples of reasoning chains and answers, which we show improves performance" (Section 2), making this a qualitatively different and potentially more data-efficient learning paradigm.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies limitations in three distinct families of prior work:

**1. Iterative alignment methods (Iterative DPO, Self-Rewarding LLMs, SPIN) don't transfer to reasoning.**

These methods share a common template: the model generates responses, those responses are scored (by a reward model, the model itself, or human labels), preference pairs are constructed, and the model is updated via DPO or a similar objective. The updated model then generates new responses for the next iteration, creating an upward spiral of improving alignment. This template works well for instruction following — Yuan et al. (2024) and Rosset et al. (2024) report significant gains — but when the same recipes are applied to reasoning benchmarks, the improvements are modest at best.

Why does this happen? The paper points to several plausible explanations without fully resolving the question, but the evidence it presents suggests that **the standard DPO objective behaves pathologically on reasoning data generated by the model itself.** Specifically:

- In the Self-Rewarding LLMs framework (Yuan et al., 2024), each iteration requires the model to act as its own reward model, scoring the quality of its own reasoning chains. But evaluating reasoning correctness is "known to be difficult" (Section 2), and LLMs are notoriously bad at self-verification on reasoning tasks — a finding documented by Huang et al. (2024).

- In SPIN (Chen et al., 2024), the winning response in each preference pair must come from human annotations, and the model's own generations are always the loser. This creates a bottleneck: "once the model generations reach human performance, they are bottlenecked" (Section 4). More subtly, it prevents the model from learning from its own correct reasoning patterns — the human-generated chain-of-thought may differ stylistically or strategically from what the model would naturally produce.

- In Iterative DPO (Xu et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2023), an external reward model scores generations. But training a reward model that can reliably judge the *correctness of reasoning steps* (as opposed to just the final answer) requires either expensive human annotation of intermediate steps (as in Lightman et al., 2023) or sophisticated Monte Carlo rollout procedures (as in the companion paper analyzed in the reference example). Without such a reward model, the preference signal is binary and based only on the final answer, which introduces noise — a correct final answer could be paired with flawed reasoning, and vice versa.

**2. SFT-based iterative reasoning methods (STaR, ReST$^{EM}$) discard useful negative signal.**

STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) pioneered the idea of bootstrapping reasoning: generate chain-of-thought rationales, filter for those that led to correct answers, and fine-tune on them. ReST$^{EM}$ (Singh et al., 2024) extends this with multiple iterations and a ground-truth verifier. These methods demonstrably improve reasoning, but they have a fundamental limitation: **they only train on positive examples.** Every incorrect reasoning chain — which contains information about what *not* to do, where the model tends to go wrong, and what patterns distinguish correct from incorrect reasoning — is thrown away.

The paper's key insight is that this discarded data is valuable. Consider a math problem where the model generates 30 candidate solutions and only 3 are correct. SFT-based methods train on only those 3, ignoring the 27 incorrect ones. But those 27 incorrect solutions contain a wealth of information: they show common error patterns, calculation mistakes, misinterpretations of the problem, and dead-end reasoning paths. A preference optimization framework can leverage this signal by explicitly teaching the model to distinguish between winning (correct) and losing (incorrect) reasoning trajectories.

However, merely applying standard DPO to such pairs doesn't work — and understanding *why* it doesn't work is central to the paper's contribution.

**3. The probability collapse problem in standard DPO on reasoning data.**

This is the paper's most important diagnostic finding, and it's worth understanding in detail because it motivates the entire technical approach. When standard DPO is applied to reasoning preference pairs — even high-quality pairs where winners have correct answers and losers have incorrect answers — the log probability of the *chosen* (winning) sequences **decreases** over training steps.

Figure 3 (left panel) shows this clearly: when training DPO initialized from Llama-2-70B-Chat on GSM8K preference pairs, the solid orange line (log probability of chosen sequences) drops from roughly -400 to below -500 over 3,000 training steps. The rejected sequence probabilities (dotted orange) drop even faster, so the *margin* between chosen and rejected increases, which is what the DPO loss optimizes for. But the absolute probability of correct reasoning chains is falling — meaning the model is becoming less likely to generate them, even as it becomes better at discriminating between correct and incorrect.

This is not a trivial implementation detail. It means that standard DPO is **making the model worse at generating correct reasoning**, even as it improves its ability to rank correct above incorrect when both are presented. At inference time, this ranking ability is irrelevant because the model must *generate* correct reasoning from scratch, not choose between pre-existing options. The DPO objective's focus on relative preferences comes at the cost of absolute generation quality — a tradeoff that is acceptable for stylistic alignment (where "good enough" outputs remain likely) but catastrophic for reasoning (where correctness is binary and any decrease in correct generation probability directly translates to lower accuracy).

The paper's diagnostic in Figure 2 adds another layer: even SFT training on only the chosen sequences has a problem. Figure 2(a) shows that when SFT is trained on chosen reasoning chains, the log probabilities of *both* chosen and rejected sequences rise together (solid and dotted green lines remain close). The model becomes more likely to generate all sequences from its own distribution, not just the correct ones. Figure 2(b) shows that SFT on the gold (human-written) chain-of-thought barely increases the probability of the model's own chosen sequences at all — there's a distribution mismatch between human reasoning patterns and model reasoning patterns.

The combined picture is: **SFT can't suppress incorrect sequences, and standard DPO suppresses everything (including correct sequences) while only preserving the relative gap.** Neither provides the right learning dynamic for reasoning.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper situates itself at the intersection of two research threads — iterative preference optimization and bootstrapped reasoning — and argues that synthesizing them requires a specific technical intervention: **augmenting the DPO loss with an NLL term on the winning sequences.**

This positioning is explicit in Section 2, where the paper contrasts Iterative RPO with three precursors:

**vs. Self-Rewarding LLMs (Yuan et al., 2024):** Three differences are highlighted. First, Iterative RPO uses a fixed set of training prompts rather than generating new prompts — this simplifies the pipeline and avoids the need for a sophisticated reward model to judge generations, since the training prompts have known gold answers. Second, because of this choice, the reward signal is binary and objective (exact match with gold answer) rather than subjective and model-generated. Third, and most critically, Iterative RPO uses the modified DPO+NLL objective, whereas Self-Rewarding LLMs used standard DPO.

**vs. STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022):** The fundamental difference is the learning objective. STaR uses SFT on filtered correct generations — positive examples only. Iterative RPO uses preference optimization, which "allows the use of negative examples of reasoning chains and answers, which we show improves performance" (Section 2). The first iteration of STaR achieves 65.2% on GSM8K versus Iterative RPO's 73.1% at iteration 1 (Table 1), and even when STaR is given twice as much data (66.9%), it still lags far behind Iterative RPO's second iteration (78.0%).

**vs. V-STaR (Hosseini et al., 2024):** V-STaR uses DPO to train a separate verifier model, which is then used to filter generations from an SFT-trained generator. In contrast, Iterative RPO "use[s] DPO to train the generator, as we do" (Section 4) — the preference optimization directly improves the reasoning model itself, not a separate scoring model.

The paper also positions itself against the broader landscape of data curation approaches (MetaMath, WizardMath, OpenMathInstruct) by emphasizing that it focuses on the *learning algorithm* rather than the data source. Those methods "curate or distill training data for reasoning tasks" (Section 4), while Iterative RPO "only rel[ies] on examples in the training set" (Abstract) — it's a method for making better use of existing data, orthogonal to and potentially combinable with data augmentation.

### Why the NLL Term Is the Key Enabler (Preview of the Mechanism)

While the detailed technical breakdown belongs in the Technical Approach section, it's worth motivating here *why* the NLL term solves the probability collapse problem, because this is the central conceptual insight.

The DPO loss optimizes the *relative* log-ratio between chosen and rejected sequences. It doesn't care about the absolute probability of either — only the gap matters. When the training data consists of sequences the model itself generated (as in iterative settings), the chosen and rejected sequences start with similar probabilities. The DPO gradient pushes them apart by decreasing the rejected probability (which is good) but can also decrease the chosen probability (which is harmful) as long as the gap widens.

The NLL term — a standard cross-entropy loss on the winning sequences — exerts an opposing force: it directly incentivizes the model to increase the probability of the chosen sequences. The combined objective L$_{\text{DPO+NLL}}$ balances these two forces: the DPO term ensures the margin between chosen and rejected grows (discrimination), while the NLL term anchors the chosen sequences at high probability (generation quality).

Figure 3 empirically validates this mechanism. With DPO alone (orange lines), chosen probabilities fall. With DPO+NLL (blue lines), chosen probabilities rise while rejected probabilities still fall — achieving both objectives simultaneously. This is not just a better tradeoff; it's a qualitatively different optimization trajectory that makes iterative preference optimization viable for reasoning for the first time.

The paper's contribution is thus not a fundamentally new algorithm, but rather an **empirical discovery that a specific modification to an existing algorithm unlocks reasoning improvements from iterative preference optimization**, when the unmodified algorithm fails. This is a practically important finding because it provides a simple, implementable recipe (DPO+NLL, iterated over model-generated pairs with binary correctness feedback) that requires no human annotation of reasoning steps, no external reward model, and no additional training data beyond what's already in standard reasoning benchmarks.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Iterative Reasoning Preference Optimization is a training recipe — not a new model architecture — that takes an existing instruction-tuned LLM and progressively improves its chain-of-thought reasoning ability through repeated cycles of self-generated practice and preference-based learning. The method solves the problem that standard preference optimization (DPO) degrades reasoning performance by causing the model to become less likely to generate correct reasoning chains, even as it learns to distinguish correct from incorrect ones; the solution is to augment the DPO loss with a negative log-likelihood term on winning sequences, which anchors the absolute probability of correct reasoning while still allowing the model to learn from the contrast between correct and incorrect reasoning trajectories.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system consists of three major components that operate in a closed loop across multiple training iterations:

1. **Chain-of-Thought Generator** — the current model `$M_t$` takes training prompts `$x_i$` as input and produces `$N$` candidate responses, each containing reasoning steps followed by a final answer. This component is responsible for creating the raw material that drives learning.

2. **Reward Assigner** — a binary correctness checker compares each generated final answer against the known gold answer `$y_i$`. Responses are partitioned into a winning set `$G^w_i$` (correct answers) and a losing set `$G^l_i$` (incorrect answers). No judgment is made about the quality of the intermediate reasoning steps — only the final answer matters.

3. **Preference Optimizer** — winning and losing responses are paired to form training data, and the model parameters are updated via a combined DPO+NLL objective that simultaneously increases the margin between chosen and rejected sequences (via DPO) and increases the absolute probability of chosen sequences (via NLL). The resulting model `$M_{t+1}$` replaces `$M_t$`, and the cycle repeats.

Information flows as follows: training prompts enter the generator → the generator produces reasoning traces with answers → the reward assigner labels each trace as correct or incorrect → pairs are constructed by matching winners with losers → the combined loss updates all model parameters → the updated model becomes the generator for the next iteration.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the iterative training loop — how models `$M_0, M_1, M_2, M_3, M_4$` are generated sequentially and what data each uses for training. This establishes the overall algorithmic structure before diving into its components.

- **Second**, the Chain-of-Thought and Answer Generation step — sampling strategy, temperature choices, number of generations per prompt, and how the reward signal is derived. This is the data engine that produces the preference pairs.

- **Third**, the preference pair construction — how winners and losers are matched, how many pairs are created per prompt, and what edge cases (no winners, no losers) are handled. This determines what the model actually learns from.

- **Fourth**, the DPO+NLL loss function — the mathematical form, what each term contributes, why the NLL term is necessary empirically, and how the hyperparameters `$\alpha$` and `$\beta$` control the balance between discrimination and generation quality.

- **Fifth**, the iterative dynamics — why multiple iterations help beyond simply having more data, what changes between iterations (temperature, data distribution), and when performance saturates.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methods paper** whose core idea is that iterative preference optimization can be made to work for reasoning tasks by adding a negative log-likelihood term to the DPO objective, preventing the probability collapse that standard DPO exhibits on self-generated reasoning data.

---

#### The Iterative Training Loop

The paper defines a sequence of models `$M_0, M_1, M_2, M_3, M_4$` produced by repeated application of a two-step procedure (Section 2, "Iterative training"). The procedure is fully specified by what data each model is trained on and how that data is generated:

**Step 0 — Initialization.** The process begins with `$M_0$`, which is the base instruction-tuned model — in all experiments, this is Llama-2-70B-Chat (Touvron et al., 2023). No training has occurred yet; this model is used only for generation.

**Step 1 — Generate data and train `$M_1$`.** The base model `$M_0$` generates `$N$` candidate solutions per training prompt. These are scored by exact match against gold answers. Preference pairs are constructed from these generations, producing a dataset `$D^{\text{pairs}}_0$`. Model `$M_1$` is initialized from `$M_0$` and trained on `$D^{\text{pairs}}_0$` using the DPO+NLL objective.

**Step 2 — Generate data and train `$M_2$`.** The newly trained model `$M_1$` generates `$N$` new candidate solutions per training prompt. These are scored, paired, and used to train `$M_2$` (initialized from `$M_1$`) on `$D^{\text{pairs}}_1$`.

**Step 3 — Generate data and train `$M_3$`.** Model `$M_2$` generates new data `$D^{\text{pairs}}_2$`, which trains `$M_3$` (initialized from `$M_2$`).

**Step 4 — Generate data and train `$M_4$`.** Model `$M_3$` generates new data `$D^{\text{pairs}}_3$`, which trains `$M_4$` (initialized from `$M_3$`).

The paper explicitly notes that the training prompts `$x_i$` are the same fixed set across all iterations. This is a deliberate simplification relative to Self-Rewarding LLMs (Yuan et al., 2024), which generates new prompts at each iteration. The rationale given is twofold: first, using a fixed training set avoids the need for a sophisticated reward model to evaluate generated prompts (since the training set has known gold answers); second, generating new reasoning prompts that are diverse and correctly answerable is itself a challenging problem that the paper chooses to sidestep.

**Why this structure matters:** Each iteration produces a model that is both (a) better at generating correct reasoning than its predecessor, and (b) different enough from its predecessor that the preference pairs it generates provide new training signal. The iterative aspect is not merely about having more data — it is about having data from an improving model that reflects the current capability frontier. As the model improves, the distribution of its errors shifts (it stops making easy mistakes and starts making subtler ones), and the preference pairs automatically capture this shifting frontier without any human intervention.

#### Chain-of-Thought and Answer Generation

Given the current model `$M_t$` and the training set `$\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_i$`, the generation step produces a dataset of candidate solutions augmented with binary rewards (Section 2, "Chain-of-thought & answer generation").

For each training prompt `$x_i$`, the model generates `$N$` independent responses:

$$(c^n_i, y^n_i) \sim M_t(x_i) \quad \text{for all } x_i \in \mathcal{D} \text{ and } n \in [N]$$

where `$c^n_i$` denotes the chain-of-thought reasoning trace (a sequence of intermediate steps), `$y^n_i$` denotes the extracted final answer, and `$[N]$` is the set `$\{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$`.

**What it computes:** For each of the training prompts, the current model is sampled `$N$` times (independently) to produce `$N$` complete reasoning trajectories. Each trajectory consists of the reasoning steps followed by a final answer. The output is a collection of `$N \times |\mathcal{D}|$` candidate solutions, each associated with its originating prompt.

**Why this form:** Independent sampling with a non-zero temperature is essential because it creates diversity in the generated solutions. If the model always produced the same reasoning chain (via greedy decoding), there would be no pairs to construct — either all answers would be correct or all would be incorrect, and the preference optimization would have no signal. The temperature controls the exploration-exploitation tradeoff: higher temperature produces more diverse (but potentially lower-quality) candidates, which is useful when the model already produces many correct answers (later iterations), while lower temperature produces more focused candidates, which is useful when the model is unreliable (early iterations).

**Reward assignment.** Each generated final answer `$y^n_i$` is compared to the gold answer `$y_i$` using exact match:

$$r^n_i = \mathbb{1}[y^n_i = y_i]$$

where `$\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$` is the indicator function returning 1 when the prediction matches the ground truth and 0 otherwise. The resulting dataset is:

$$\mathcal{G}_i = \{c^n_i, y^n_i, r^n_i\}_{n \in [N]}$$

This binary reward is the only correctness signal used in the entire pipeline. The paper emphasizes that no judgment is made about the *quality of the reasoning steps* — the model could produce correct answers with flawed reasoning, or incorrect answers with partially correct reasoning, and the reward assignment would be identical. This choice is pragmatic rather than ideal: it avoids the need for expensive step-level annotation or a learned process reward model, but it introduces noise into the preference pairs (since a "winning" sequence might contain poor reasoning that happened to yield the right answer, and a "losing" sequence might contain excellent reasoning with a minor arithmetic error at the end).

**Generation hyperparameters by task and iteration.** The paper specifies different sampling temperatures and counts per iteration, tuned to the characteristics of each benchmark:

| Task | `$N$` (samples per prompt) | Temperature (iterations 1–2) | Temperature (iteration 3) | Temperature (iteration 4) |
|------|------|------|------|------|
| GSM8K | 30 | 0.8 | 0.8 (iter 1–2), 1.3 (iter 3–4) | 1.3 |
| ARC-Challenge | 30 | 0.8 | 0.8 (iter 1–2), 1.3 (iter 3) | — |
| MATH | 20 | 0.8 | 0.8 (iter 1–2), 1.0 (iter 3) | — |

The increase in temperature at later iterations (1.3 for GSM8K iterations 3–4, 1.0 for MATH iteration 3) is motivated by the observation that as the model improves, the fraction of incorrect generations decreases. If the temperature remained low, the model might produce too few incorrect responses to construct meaningful preference pairs. Higher temperature increases diversity and ensures a sufficient supply of losing examples. The paper states this explicitly: "hoping that there is a significant number of incorrect generations in later iterations" (Section 3.1).

**Handling prompts with no correct generations.** A critical edge case occurs when a training prompt receives zero correct answers out of `$N$` samples — meaning `$G^w_i$` is empty. This can happen for genuinely hard problems that are beyond the current model's capability. The paper handles this differently for GSM8K versus ARC-Challenge:

- For GSM8K: "we include the gold human written solution `$(c_i, y_i)$` in the winning set `$G^w_i$` so it is not empty" (Section 3.1). This ensures that every prompt has at least one winning sequence, and it also exposes the model to human-quality reasoning patterns.

- For ARC-Challenge: "if there is no model-generated correct solution for a question, then that question is not included in our training" (Section 3.2). The gold chain-of-thought is not provided for ARC, so there is no fallback. This means the effective training set shrinks as the model improves — iteration 1 uses about 20k pairs, iteration 2 uses about 11k pairs, and iteration 3 uses about 5k pairs, because more and more questions have no incorrect samples.

The asymmetry between these two approaches reveals a practical design tension: providing gold reasoning chains (when available) ensures complete coverage of the training set and exposes the model to potentially higher-quality reasoning, but it also introduces a distribution shift between human-generated and model-generated reasoning patterns. Figure 2(b) shows that SFT on gold chains barely increases the probability of the model's own chosen sequences, suggesting that human reasoning patterns are different enough from model reasoning patterns that training on them has limited transfer.

**Prompt formatting.** Each task uses a specific prompt template that instructs the model to produce step-by-step reasoning and to format the final answer in an extractable way (Appendix B.1). For GSM8K, the prompt is:

> "Your task is to answer the question below. Give step by step reasoning before you answer, and when you're ready to answer, please use the format 'Final answer: ...'"

For MATH, the prompt includes four fixed in-context examples (chosen from the training set) showing the expected reasoning format and LaTeX answer formatting. The paper notes that "the language model needs these demonstrations so that the final answers can be properly formatted in LaTeX" (Section 3.3).

For ARC-Challenge, the prompt includes the multiple-choice options inline and instructs the model to output the chosen letter.

#### Preference Pair Construction

Once the generation and reward assignment step produces `$\mathcal{G}_i = \{c^n_i, y^n_i, r^n_i\}_{n \in [N]}$` for each prompt `$x_i$`, the next step constructs a training dataset of preference pairs (Section 2, "Preference optimization").

**Partitioning by correctness.** In the binary reward case, the generated responses are split into two disjoint sets:

$$G^w_i = \{c^n_i, y^n_i \mid r^n_i = 1\}$$
$$G^l_i = \{c^n_i, y^n_i \mid r^n_i = 0\}$$

where `$G^w_i$` contains all winning (correct final answer) responses and `$G^l_i$` contains all losing (incorrect final answer) responses for prompt `$x_i$`.

**Pair formation.** Preference pairs are constructed by iterating over `$G^w_i$` and `$G^l_i$` simultaneously to produce `$K$` pairs per prompt:

$$\mathcal{D}^{\text{pairs}}_t = \{(c^{w_k}_i, y^{w_k}_i), (c^{l_k}_i, y^{l_k}_i) \mid \text{for all } x_i \in \mathcal{D} \text{ and } k \in [K]\}$$

where `$(c^{w_k}_i, y^{w_k}_i)$` is a winning response and `$(c^{l_k}_i, y^{l_k}_i)$` is a losing response. The pairing procedure is simple: "we simply iterate over `$G^w_i$` and `$G^l_i$` simultaneously to produce K pairs of indices `$\{(w_k, l_k)\}$`, in order to ensure we use as much of the data as possible" (Section 2). If the iteration reaches the end of one set, it restarts from the first element. If one of the sets is empty (no winners or no losers), that prompt is excluded from training for that iteration.

**What this procedure computes:** For each training prompt, up to `$K$` pairs are created by matching each winning response with a losing response (cycling through both sets to maximize data usage). The resulting dataset consists of `$K \times |\mathcal{D}_{\text{valid}}|$` preference pairs, where `$|\mathcal{D}_{\text{valid}}|$` is the number of prompts that have at least one winner and at least one loser.

**Why this form:** The cycling approach is deliberately simple — no attempt is made to select the "most informative" pairs or to ensure that winners and losers are matched based on reasoning similarity. This simplicity is a practical strength (easy to implement, no hyperparameters to tune for pair selection) but also a potential limitation: random pairing means the model might see a winner with sophisticated reasoning paired against a loser with trivial errors (easy contrast) or a winner with lucky-guess reasoning paired against a loser with nearly-correct reasoning (misleading contrast). The paper does not ablate this pairing strategy against alternatives like edit-distance-based matching (used in the companion paper for revision model training), so it's unclear whether more sophisticated pairing would improve results.

**Pair counts per task.** The number of pairs `$K$` per prompt and the resulting dataset sizes vary by task:

| Task | `$K$` (pairs per prompt) | Pair filtering | Resulting dataset size (per iteration) |
|------|------|------|------|
| GSM8K | 10 | Remove overflow context length, excluded if no incorrect generations | ~55–60k pairs |
| ARC-Challenge | 20 | Remove overflow context length | ~20k (iter 1), ~11k (iter 2), ~5k (iter 3) |
| MATH | 15 | Filter overly long generations | ~75k pairs |

For GSM8K, the paper additionally notes: "If after filtering the number of pairs is larger than 60k, then we randomly select around 60k examples. This number is fixed because we do not want to introduce another source of variability in our experiments" (Section 3.1, footnote). This fixed cap ensures fair comparison across iterations — without it, iterations with more valid pairs would have an unfair advantage from seeing more data.

#### The DPO+NLL Loss Function

This is the paper's central technical contribution — the loss function that makes iterative preference optimization work for reasoning. The loss combines two terms (Equation 1):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO+NLL}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(c^w_i, y^w_i, c^l_i, y^l_i \mid x_i) + \alpha \mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}(c^w_i, y^w_i \mid x_i)$$

Expanding each term:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO+NLL}} = -\log \sigma\left( \beta \log \frac{M_\theta(c^w_i, y^w_i \mid x_i)}{M_t(c^w_i, y^w_i \mid x_i)} - \beta \log \frac{M_\theta(c^l_i, y^l_i \mid x_i)}{M_t(c^l_i, y^l_i \mid x_i)} \right) - \alpha \frac{\log M_\theta(c^w_i, y^w_i \mid x_i)}{|c^w_i| + |y^w_i|}$$

where `$M_\theta$` is the model being trained (parameterized by `$\theta$`), `$M_t$` is the reference model from the previous iteration (frozen during training), `$\sigma$` is the sigmoid function, `$\beta$` controls the strength of the preference signal, `$\alpha$` balances the two loss terms, and `$|c^w_i| + |y^w_i|$` is the token length of the winning response (used for normalization).

**What the DPO term computes:** The DPO loss is a binary cross-entropy loss where the "logit" is the difference between two log-ratios. For the winning response, it computes `$\log \frac{M_\theta(\text{winner})}{M_t(\text{winner})}$` — the log-ratio of how much more (or less) likely the current model is to produce the winner compared to the reference model. For the losing response, it computes the same quantity. The difference between these two log-ratios is passed through a sigmoid and treated as the probability that the winner is preferred over the loser. The loss `$-\log \sigma(\cdot)$` pushes this difference to be large and positive, meaning the current model should increase the winner's probability relative to the reference and decrease the loser's probability relative to the reference.

**What the NLL term computes:** The negative log-likelihood term is simply the standard language modeling loss (cross-entropy) on the winning response, normalized by its token length. It directly penalizes the model for assigning low probability to the correct reasoning chain. Unlike the DPO term, which only cares about the *difference* between winner and loser probabilities, the NLL term cares about the *absolute* probability of the winner.

**The combined effect:** The DPO term pushes the model to separate winners from losers (discrimination), while the NLL term anchors the winners at high probability (generation quality). The hyperparameter `$\alpha$` controls the tradeoff: `$\alpha = 0$` recovers standard DPO, `$\alpha \to \infty$` approaches pure SFT on the winners (ignoring the losers entirely), and intermediate values balance the two objectives.

**Why this form is necessary:** Standard DPO (`$\alpha = 0$`) suffers from a specific failure mode on self-generated reasoning data. As shown in Figure 3, the log probability of chosen (winning) sequences *decreases* during standard DPO training. This happens because the DPO loss only cares about the *relative gap* between chosen and rejected probabilities — it can achieve a larger gap by decreasing both probabilities while decreasing the rejected probability faster than the chosen probability. In absolute terms, the model becomes less likely to generate correct reasoning chains, which directly harms test-time accuracy (the model must generate, not just discriminate). The NLL term prevents this by adding a direct upward force on the chosen sequence probabilities. Figure 3 shows empirically that with DPO+NLL, chosen probabilities rise while rejected probabilities fall — achieving both discrimination and generation quality simultaneously.

**Normalization details.** The NLL term is normalized by the total response length `$|c^w_i| + |y^w_i|$`, which means longer responses don't receive disproportionately large gradients. Without this normalization, the model would be biased toward learning from shorter correct responses (which have higher per-token probability) and might underfit longer or more complex reasoning chains.

**The reference model choice.** The reference model in the DPO denominator is always `$M_t$` — the model from the previous iteration, which generated the training data. This is a standard DPO practice but has a specific interpretation in the iterative setting: the DPO term measures how much the current model `$M_\theta$` has shifted its probability distribution relative to the distribution that *generated the data*. This is important because the preference pairs are on-policy relative to `$M_t$` — they represent choices that `$M_t$` was capable of generating. If a different reference model were used (e.g., the original base model `$M_0$`), the DPO term would measure shifts relative to a distribution potentially far from the data-generating distribution, which could cause instability.

**Hyperparameter selection.** The paper tunes both `$\alpha$` and `$\beta$` on GSM8K and then uses the same values across all tasks:

- `$\alpha$`: Tuned in `$\{0.25, 0.5, 1, 2\}$` when training `$M_1$`. The chosen value is `$\alpha = 1$`, meaning the NLL term and DPO term are weighted equally (before normalization). This choice is used for all experiments in the paper.

- `$\beta$`: Tuned in `$\{0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0\}$`. The chosen value is `$\beta = 0.1$` for GSM8K. The paper does not explicitly state `$\beta$` values for ARC-Challenge and MATH, but the consistent methodology suggests `$\beta = 0.1$` is used throughout.

**Why `$\beta = 0.1$` rather than the more common `$\beta = 1.0$`:** A smaller `$\beta$` reduces the effective temperature of the preference signal. In the DPO loss, `$\beta$` multiplies the log-ratio difference before it enters the sigmoid. With `$\beta = 0.1$`, even large differences in log-ratios are "compressed" by the sigmoid, making the optimization more conservative — the model is less aggressively pushed to separate winners and losers. This may be important for reasoning tasks where the binary reward signal is noisy (correct answers with flawed reasoning, incorrect answers with good reasoning), because aggressive optimization could amplify the noise.

**Training hyperparameters.** The paper specifies (Section 3.1):

- Optimizer: AdamW
- Learning rate: `$7 \times 10^{-7}$` (7e-7)
- Batch size: 16
- Maximum training steps per iteration: 5000 (GSM8K, MATH), 4000 (ARC-Challenge)
- Checkpoint selection: Best checkpoint selected using a held-out 1k samples from the training set (for GSM8K; the paper does not explicitly describe validation procedures for ARC-Challenge and MATH but presumably follows a similar protocol)
- After selecting the best checkpoint, the model is retrained including the held-out validation samples for the selected number of steps ("We then retrain while including those 1k samples for the selected number of steps," Section 3.1)
- Hardware: Training uses 8 nodes each with 8 A100 GPUs (80G memory); generation uses 1 node with 8 V100 GPUs (32G memory)

#### Why the NLL Term Matters: Empirical Evidence

The paper provides extensive empirical diagnostics (Figures 2, 3, 4) that explain *why* the NLL term is necessary, not just that it improves accuracy. These diagnostics trace the log probabilities of training sequences during optimization:

**Figure 2(a) — SFT on chosen sequences.** When supervised fine-tuning is performed on only the winning (correct) sequences from `$\mathcal{D}^{\text{pairs}}_0$`, the log probabilities of *both* chosen and rejected sequences increase together — the solid and dotted green lines in Figure 2(a) rise in parallel and remain close throughout training. This means SFT doesn't discriminate: it makes the model more likely to generate all sequences from its own distribution, correct and incorrect alike. The model becomes more confident in its own outputs but does not learn to avoid errors.

**Figure 2(b) — SFT on gold CoT sequences.** When SFT is performed on the dataset-provided human-written chain-of-thought solutions, the probabilities of the model's own chosen sequences (from `$\mathcal{D}^{\text{pairs}}_0$`) barely increase — they remain around -800 log probability. The gold CoT sequences have high probability under the model (around -196), but this doesn't transfer to the model's own reasoning patterns. This is evidence of a distribution shift between human reasoning and model reasoning: learning to mimic human chain-of-thought doesn't teach the model to produce correct reasoning in its own "voice."

**Figure 3 — DPO vs. DPO+NLL.** When standard DPO is applied to the preference pairs (initialized from Llama-2-70B-Chat, Figure 3 left panel), the log probability of chosen sequences (solid orange) decreases from approximately -400 to below -500 over 3,000 training steps. The rejected sequence probability (dotted orange) decreases even faster, so the margin grows — but the absolute probability of generating correct reasoning falls. In contrast, DPO+NLL (blue lines) shows chosen probabilities rising (solid blue increases from -400 toward -200) while rejected probabilities still fall (dotted blue decreases from -400 toward -700). This is the desired dynamic: the model becomes simultaneously better at generating correct reasoning (higher absolute probability) and better at discriminating correct from incorrect (larger margin).

The same pattern appears in Figures 4(a) and 4(b) for ARC-Challenge and MATH, confirming that the NLL term's effect is consistent across tasks.

**Why this is a finding, not just a hyperparameter choice.** The NLL term in preference optimization has appeared in concurrent work (Pal et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024), but this paper's contribution is demonstrating that it is *necessary* (not just helpful) for iterative preference optimization to work on reasoning tasks, and providing a clear diagnostic mechanism (probability collapse) that explains why. Without the NLL term, each iteration of DPO would degrade the model's generation capability, making the iterative procedure harmful rather than beneficial.

---

#### Iterative Dynamics: Why Multiple Iterations Help

A natural question is whether the iterative procedure provides benefits beyond simply having more training data. The paper addresses this directly with a controlled experiment (Section 3.1, "Iterations of Iterative RPO yield improved reasoning"):

**Control experiment — doubling data in one iteration vs. two iterations.** The first iteration of Iterative RPO is run with twice the normal amount of paired data (by doubling `$K$` from 10 to 20 for GSM8K). This "twice as much data" variant achieves 74.8% accuracy, compared to 73.1% for the standard first iteration. However, two iterations (each with the standard amount of data) achieve 78.0%. Similarly, STaR with twice as much data achieves 66.9% (versus 65.2% standard), still far below Iterative RPO's second iteration.

**What this means:** The improvement from iteration 1 to iteration 2 (73.1% → 78.0%, a gain of 4.9 percentage points) is larger than the improvement from doubling the data in iteration 1 (73.1% → 74.8%, a gain of 1.7 percentage points). This demonstrates that the iterative procedure provides value beyond data quantity: the data generated by `$M_1$` is qualitatively different from the data generated by `$M_0$`, and this difference matters for learning.

**Why the data changes across iterations.** As the model improves, the distribution of its errors shifts:

- Iteration 0 (`$M_0$`): The model makes many errors, including basic arithmetic mistakes, misinterpretations of problem statements, and flawed reasoning patterns. The preference pairs contrast correct answers against these basic errors.

- Iteration 1 (`$M_1$`): After training on the first iteration's pairs, the model has largely eliminated the most basic errors. The incorrect responses it now generates reflect more subtle mistakes — partially correct reasoning with a wrong turn near the end, correct approach but calculation error, correct answer derived through flawed steps. The preference pairs now contrast correct answers against these more subtle errors.

- Iteration 2 (`$M_2$`) and beyond: The model's errors become increasingly nuanced, and the preference pairs automatically adapt to this shifting frontier. This is the key advantage of on-policy data generation: the training data always reflects the current model's actual failure modes, not a static set of errors.

**Saturation behavior.** The paper observes that gains decay across iterations: +17.5 percentage points (iteration 0→1), +4.9 (iteration 1→2), +3.1 (iteration 2→3), +0.5 (iteration 3→4) for GSM8K. The authors attribute this to the fixed training set: "indicating an upper limit on learning across iterations, especially as we are iterating across a fixed number of prompts, i.e., only from the training samples" (Section 3.1). When the model already produces correct answers for most training prompts, there are few incorrect responses to form pairs, and the learning signal diminishes. This suggests that expanding the training set (e.g., with data augmentation) could extend the benefits to more iterations.

#### Summary of Key Design Choices and Justifications

- **Fixed training prompts rather than generated prompts:** Simplifies the pipeline by avoiding the need for a reward model to judge generated prompts; leverages known gold answers for binary correctness feedback. Tradeoff: limits the diversity of training data and causes saturation as the model masters the fixed set.

- **Binary reward based on final answer only (not reasoning quality):** Avoids the expense and difficulty of step-level annotation or learned process reward models. Tradeoff: introduces noise because correct answers can have flawed reasoning and incorrect answers can have partially correct reasoning. The paper shows the method is robust to this noise across three tasks.

- **Simple cycling pair construction (not difficulty-based or edit-distance-based):** Maximizes data usage with minimal implementation complexity. Tradeoff: doesn't prioritize the most informative contrasts.

- **DPO+NLL rather than DPO or SFT alone:** The NLL term prevents the probability collapse that standard DPO exhibits on self-generated reasoning data (Figure 3), while the DPO term provides discrimination signal that pure SFT lacks (Figure 2). The combination achieves both separation and anchoring.

- **`$\alpha = 1$` and `$\beta = 0.1$`:** Equal weighting of DPO and NLL terms, with a small `$\beta$` for conservative preference optimization that avoids overfitting to noisy binary reward signal.

- **Increasing temperature at later iterations:** Ensures sufficient incorrect generations for pair construction even as the model improves, maintaining the learning signal.

- **Including gold chain-of-thought when available (GSM8K) but not when unavailable (ARC-Challenge):** A pragmatic choice that ensures every prompt has at least one winning response, but introduces a distribution shift concern (Figure 2(b) suggests limited transfer from human to model reasoning patterns).

- **Maximum training steps and early stopping:** Prevents overfitting to the preference pairs; the held-out validation set selects the checkpoint that generalizes best.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Diagnosing and Solving the Probability Collapse in Preference Optimization for Reasoning

The paper's most intellectually distinctive contribution is not the DPO+NLL loss itself — which has appeared in concurrent work (Pal et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024) — but rather the **diagnostic insight that standard DPO fails on reasoning tasks because it suppresses the absolute probability of correct sequences, not because preference optimization is fundamentally unsuited to reasoning.** This reframes the problem from "preference optimization doesn't work for reasoning" to "preference optimization has a specific, diagnosable, and fixable failure mode on self-generated reasoning data."

Prior to this work, the field had accumulated evidence that iterative preference optimization methods (Iterative DPO, Self-Rewarding LLMs, SPIN) produced disappointing results on reasoning benchmarks while excelling at general instruction following. The dominant interpretation — implicit in how these methods were applied, if not explicitly stated — was that reasoning tasks are simply different: the binary correctness signal is too coarse, the chain-of-thought structure doesn't lend itself to pairwise comparison, or the exploration space is too large for on-policy preference learning to navigate. This paper challenges that interpretation by showing that the failure is not inherent to the problem setting but is instead a **specific optimization pathology** that can be observed, diagnosed, and corrected.

The diagnostic framework in Figures 2 and 3 is the key intellectual move. Rather than treating the training process as a black box and evaluating only final accuracy, the paper traces the log probabilities of chosen and rejected sequences during training. This reveals three distinct failure modes:

- **SFT on chosen sequences (Figure 2a):** Both chosen and rejected probabilities rise together — the model learns to reproduce its own distribution indiscriminately, correct and incorrect alike. The discrimination signal is absent.

- **SFT on gold CoT sequences (Figure 2b):** The model's own chosen sequences barely budge in probability — there's a distribution mismatch between human and model reasoning patterns. The transfer is minimal.

- **Standard DPO (Figure 3, orange lines):** The chosen probability *decreases* — the model becomes less likely to generate correct reasoning, even as the margin between chosen and rejected widens. The generation capability is actively degraded.

The significance of this diagnostic approach extends beyond the specific fix proposed. It establishes that **the training dynamics of preference optimization on reasoning data can be understood by monitoring a small set of interpretable quantities** (chosen and rejected log probabilities over training steps), and that deviations from desired dynamics (chosen probability falling) are reliable indicators of test-time performance degradation. This is a conceptual tool that future work can use to evaluate new preference optimization variants on reasoning tasks without running full test-set evaluations — a practical contribution to experimental methodology.

The finding also explains *why* prior iterative preference optimization methods failed on reasoning without this modification. Self-Rewarding LLMs (Yuan et al., 2024) used standard DPO; given this paper's diagnostics, each iteration of standard DPO would have progressively suppressed the probability of correct reasoning chains, undoing any benefits from the iterative data generation. The "moderate gains or even decrease" reported in prior work (Section 1) is precisely what the probability collapse dynamic predicts.

This innovation is **fundamental rather than incremental**: it identifies a previously undescribed failure mode of a widely-used algorithm on an important class of tasks, provides a mechanistic explanation for the failure, and demonstrates that a simple modification (the NLL term) corrects it. The DPO+NLL loss is the solution; the diagnostic insight about *why* it's needed is the contribution.

---

### Innovation 2: Iterative On-Policy Preference Learning as a Reasoning Improvement Engine

The paper demonstrates that **iterative, on-policy preference optimization on self-generated data can serve as a standalone engine for improving reasoning capability, without requiring human-annotated chain-of-thought, external reward models, or expanded training sets.** This challenges the prevailing assumption in the bootstrapped reasoning literature that SFT on filtered correct generations (the STaR/ReST$^{EM}$ paradigm) is the natural and sufficient approach.

The comparison with STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) illuminates what's distinctive. STaR's training loop generates rationales, filters for those leading to correct answers, optionally uses the correct answer as a hint to generate better rationales, and fine-tunes on the filtered set — then repeats. The model only learns from positive examples. Incorrect reasoning chains, which in a typical generation run constitute the majority of outputs (often ~80-90% of samples for challenging problems), are discarded. This is wasteful in an information-theoretic sense: each incorrect chain contains signal about *where the model tends to fail*, what error patterns are common, and how correct reasoning differs from incorrect reasoning.

Iterative RPO captures this discarded signal through the preference mechanism. The DPO term explicitly teaches the model to distinguish winning from losing trajectories — a form of contrastive learning that SFT cannot provide. The empirical results quantify the value of this negative signal: on GSM8K, the first iteration of STaR achieves 65.2% while Iterative RPO achieves 73.1% on the first iteration and 78.0% on the second (Table 1). Even when STaR is given twice as much positive data (66.9%), it still substantially underperforms Iterative RPO. The gap cannot be explained by data quantity alone — it's explained by the *qualitative difference* between learning from positive examples only and learning from the contrast between positive and negative examples.

The iterative, on-policy nature of the data generation is equally important. Each iteration's preference pairs are generated by the current model `$M_t$`, meaning the training data always reflects the model's actual failure modes at its current capability level. This is fundamentally different from static preference datasets (e.g., pre-collected human preference judgments), where the distribution of errors is fixed and potentially mismatched to the model being trained. As the model improves across iterations, the nature of its errors shifts: iteration 0 produces basic arithmetic mistakes and misinterpretations, while iteration 2 produces subtler logical flaws and near-misses. The preference pairs automatically capture this shifting frontier because they are generated on-policy. This is the same insight that makes on-policy RL methods sample-efficient in sequential decision problems, applied here to language model reasoning.

The significance of this innovation is that it **establishes preference optimization as a viable alternative to SFT-based bootstrapping for reasoning**, with qualitatively different (and complementary) learning dynamics. SFT-based methods learn by imitation of correct trajectories; preference-based methods learn by contrast between correct and incorrect trajectories. The paper shows the latter is more data-efficient given the same set of training prompts, presumably because the contrastive signal provides richer gradient information per example than pure imitation.

This is an **incremental advance in method design but a fundamental reframing of the learning paradigm** for bootstrapped reasoning. The method itself (iterative training with DPO+NLL) is a straightforward combination of known components, but the demonstration that this combination substantially outperforms the SFT-only paradigm — and the conceptual argument that negative examples are valuable for reasoning — shifts how researchers should think about leveraging model-generated data.

---

### Innovation 3: The NLL Term as an "Anchor" That Prevents Degradation While Preserving the Benefits of Preference Optimization

While the diagnostic insight (Innovation 1) explains *why* DPO fails, this innovation concerns **why the NLL term specifically is the right fix, rather than alternative interventions like SFT initialization or more conservative DPO hyperparameters.** The paper provides evidence that the NLL term is not merely a regularizer or a trick to boost chosen sequence probability, but a **necessary component that enables the DPO term to do its job without collateral damage.**

The critical comparison is between DPO+NLL and DPO initialized from an SFT-trained model (Table 1, "DPO initialized from SFT trained on Iteration 1 chosen seqs"). Prior work (Rafailov et al., 2023, 2024) has argued that DPO benefits from SFT initialization — starting from a model that already assigns high probability to chosen sequences reduces the risk of probability collapse. If SFT initialization were sufficient, there would be no need for the NLL term; one could simply SFT on the winners and then apply standard DPO. But the paper shows this doesn't work: DPO initialized from SFT on chosen sequences achieves only 60.3% on GSM8K, compared to 73.1% for DPO+NLL on the first iteration — and crucially, *worse than the SFT model itself* (63.5%). This means standard DPO actively degrades the SFT model's performance, even when initialized from a strong starting point.

Figure 3(b) shows why: when DPO is initialized from SFT on chosen sequences, the chosen sequence log probabilities still decrease during training (solid orange line in the right panel drops from approximately -200 to below -400). The DPO loss's drive to increase the margin between chosen and rejected overrides the SFT initialization's anchoring effect. The NLL term in DPO+NLL is not redundant with SFT initialization — it provides a *sustained* upward force on chosen probabilities throughout training that initialization alone cannot maintain.

This finding has implications beyond reasoning. It suggests that **the probability collapse in DPO is a general phenomenon when training on self-generated preference data, not specific to any particular initialization scheme.** The NLL term solves this by making the objective directly penalize decreases in chosen sequence probability, rather than hoping that initialization or hyperparameter tuning will prevent them. The paper's ablation — showing that SFT initialization doesn't fix the problem — strengthens the case that the NLL term addresses a fundamental limitation of the DPO objective, not a superficial initialization issue.

The innovation is **conceptual rather than algorithmic**: it establishes that for on-policy preference optimization to work, the objective must contain an explicit term that anchors the absolute probability of the target distribution, not just a relative preference signal. The DPO loss alone provides only the latter; the NLL term provides the former. This framing — anchor + contrast — is a useful mental model for designing preference optimization objectives for tasks where generation quality matters as much as (or more than) discrimination ability.

This is a **moderate conceptual advance** that builds on the diagnostic work of Innovation 1. It doesn't introduce a new mathematical framework, but it clarifies the *functional role* of the NLL term in a way that guides future method design: any preference optimization objective intended for tasks requiring high absolute generation quality must include an anchoring mechanism, and SFT initialization is an insufficient substitute.

---

### Innovation 4: Empirical Evidence That Binary Final-Answer Feedback Is Sufficient for Preference-Based Reasoning Improvement

The paper demonstrates that **preference optimization for chain-of-thought reasoning can succeed using only binary feedback on final answers, without any evaluation of intermediate reasoning step quality.** This is a practically significant finding because it dramatically lowers the requirements for deploying such methods compared to alternatives that require process-level supervision.

The contrast with process reward models (PRMs) — as developed in Lightman et al. (2023) and the companion paper analyzed in the reference example — is illuminating. PRMs require training a separate model to evaluate the correctness of each reasoning step, which in turn requires either expensive human annotation of step-level correctness or sophisticated Monte Carlo rollout procedures. The companion paper's PRM training, for example, involves generating 16 rollouts per step, computing correctness fractions, and training a binary classifier — a computationally intensive pipeline that depends on the base model's output distribution.

Iterative RPO sidesteps this entirely. The only correctness signal is an exact match between the generated final answer and the gold answer — a computation that requires no model training, no human annotation, and trivial computational cost. The method does not attempt to determine whether the intermediate reasoning steps are logically valid, factually correct, or coherent with the final answer. The preference pairs may therefore contain "false positives" (correct final answer reached through flawed reasoning paired against incorrect answer from nearly-correct reasoning) and "false negatives" (incorrect final answer from mostly-correct reasoning with a minor arithmetic slip).

The surprising result is that the method is robust to this noise. On GSM8K, where gold chain-of-thought exists and could theoretically provide cleaner supervision, Iterative RPO with binary final-answer feedback achieves 81.6% — matching or exceeding methods that use more sophisticated supervision. On ARC-Challenge, where correct answers can be achieved by random guessing (25% baseline), the noise level is even higher: a "winning" sequence may contain completely unrelated reasoning that happened to select the right multiple-choice option. Yet the method still improves from 77.8% (base model) to 86.7% (iteration 3), demonstrating robustness to substantial label noise.

This finding has significant practical implications. It means that **any dataset with ground-truth answers can be used for preference-based reasoning improvement without additional annotation.** This includes not only standard benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH, but also any domain where correctness can be automatically verified — code execution (unit tests), formal theorem proving (proof checkers), scientific calculations (numerical verification), and many others. The method's requirements are minimal: a set of input prompts, a way to check if the final output is correct, and the ability to sample multiple generations per prompt. This makes it applicable to a much wider range of problems than methods requiring step-level supervision or learned verifiers.

The innovation is **primarily practical rather than theoretical**: it's a demonstration that a simple, cheap feedback signal can drive substantial reasoning improvements through iterative preference optimization, contrary to what one might expect given the coarseness of the signal. The paper does not explain *why* binary feedback works despite the noise — this is left as an open question — but the empirical robustness across three diverse tasks (math word problems, competition mathematics, multiple-choice science) provides strong evidence that it's a general phenomenon rather than a task-specific fluke.

This is an **incremental but practically impactful finding**. It doesn't introduce new theory about why binary feedback suffices, but it establishes an empirical fact that lowers the barrier to entry for preference-based reasoning improvement and opens the door to applications beyond curated benchmarks.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on three standard reasoning benchmarks. GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) consists of ~7.5k training and ~1.3k test grade-school math word problems, each with a gold chain-of-thought solution and a numerical final answer. MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021) contains 12,500 competition-level math problems (7,500 training, 5,000 test) spanning algebra, geometry, probability, and other topics; gold chain-of-thought solutions are provided, and answers are formatted in LaTeX requiring special normalization for exact match. ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018) is a multiple-choice science reasoning benchmark with 7.7k training questions (split into easy and challenge sets) and 1,172 test questions; no gold chain-of-thought is provided, and answers are single letters corresponding to 3–5 options.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use the chat version of Llama-2-70B (Touvron et al., 2023) as the seed model `$M_0$`. The paper chose this model because it is a widely-used open-weight instruction-tuned LLM at the 70B scale, making results reproducible and comparable to other work in the Llama-2 ecosystem. The instruction tuning ensures the model can follow the zero-shot or few-shot prompt formats required for chain-of-thought generation without additional SFT prior to the iterative procedure.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric across all tasks is **exact match accuracy on the test set**: the fraction of test questions for which the model's final answer exactly matches the ground-truth answer (after normalization for MATH). For GSM8K, this is a direct numerical comparison. For MATH, answers are normalized using the dataset's provided grading utilities to handle LaTeX formatting variations. For ARC-Challenge, the extracted answer letter is compared to the correct option letter. The paper also reports **majority voting accuracy over 32 samples** (sampling with temperature 0.8), where the most common final answer among 32 generations is selected; this is a standard technique to improve reasoning performance and tests whether the model's improvements transfer to ensemble settings.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five categories of baselines, all using the same base model and training data:
  - **Zero-shot / few-shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT):** The base model `$M_0$` with no additional training, prompted to produce reasoning steps before answering. This is the performance floor before any iterative training.
  - **Supervised Fine-Tuning on gold CoT examples (SFT on gold):** Standard fine-tuning on the dataset-provided human-written chain-of-thought solutions for each training prompt (available for GSM8K and MATH). This tests whether training on high-quality human reasoning transfers to the model's own generation.
  - **Supervised Fine-Tuning on chosen sequences (SFT on chosen):** Fine-tuning on only the winning (correct final answer) sequences generated by the base model in the first iteration of Iterative RPO. This corresponds to the first iteration of the STaR method (Zelikman et al., 2022) and tests whether SFT on model-generated correct reasoning alone can match preference optimization.
  - **Standard DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023):** Applied to the same preference pairs `$D^{\text{pairs}}_0$` used in the first iteration of Iterative RPO. Two initialization variants are tested: DPO initialized directly from Llama-2-70B-Chat, and DPO initialized from an SFT model trained on the chosen sequences (following the recommendation of Rafailov et al., 2024 that SFT initialization improves DPO).
  - **STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022):** The first iteration of Self-Taught Reasoning, which generates rationales, filters for those leading to correct answers, and fine-tunes on the filtered set. The paper also tests STaR with twice the filtered data to disentangle data quantity effects from iterative effects.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a unified compute metric (like FLOPs or generation count) across methods because the comparison is primarily about *what can be achieved with the same base model and training data*, not about matching inference-time budgets. Instead, the paper controls for data quantity: Iterative RPO's first iteration uses `$K$` preference pairs per training prompt, and the STaR and "doubled data" baselines are given equivalent or expanded data. Within iterative RPO, each iteration uses the same number of training prompts and the same generation budget per prompt (`$N$` samples), so improvements across iterations cannot be attributed to more data. For majority voting, the standard 32-sample budget is used across all methods.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For each iteration of training, the paper selects the best checkpoint using a held-out validation set: for GSM8K, 1,000 samples from the training set are held out during training, and the checkpoint with the best performance on this held-out set is selected. After selection, the model is retrained including those 1,000 samples for the selected number of steps. The paper does not report standard deviations, confidence intervals, or multiple random seeds, so the statistical reliability of individual accuracy numbers cannot be assessed. For ARC-Challenge, the paper "relies on the provided development set" for hyperparameter tuning (Section 3.2), suggesting similar validation-based checkpoint selection.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### GSM8K Results: Iterative RPO Achieves Large Gains Over All Baselines

The headline result for GSM8K is given in Table 1: Iterative RPO improves zero-shot accuracy from 55.6% (base model) to 81.6% at iteration 4, a gain of 26.0 percentage points. With majority voting over 32 samples, performance reaches 88.7% at iteration 4, compared to 70.7% for the zero-shot CoT baseline — a gain of 18.0 percentage points in the ensemble setting.

**Iteration-by-iteration improvements.** The paper reports performance at each iteration (Table 1): iteration 1 achieves 73.1%, iteration 2 achieves 78.0%, iteration 3 achieves 81.1%, and iteration 4 achieves 81.6%. The gains across iterations are +17.5, +4.9, +3.1, and +0.5 percentage points respectively, showing a clear decay pattern. Majority voting follows a similar trajectory: 88.2% at iteration 3 and 88.7% at iteration 4 (the paper only reports majority voting for iterations 3 and 4, not for earlier iterations).

**Comparison with SFT-based methods.** SFT on the dataset-provided gold chain-of-thought solutions achieves 63.5% — an 8.0 percentage point improvement over zero-shot but 17.6 percentage points below Iterative RPO at iteration 3. SFT on model-generated chosen sequences (the first iteration of STaR) achieves 65.2%, slightly better than SFT on gold but still 15.9 points below Iterative RPO. The gap is not primarily a data quantity issue: STaR with twice as much data achieves only 66.9%, still far below Iterative RPO's 78.0% at two iterations.

**Comparison with DPO-based methods.** Standard DPO applied to the same preference pairs `$D^{\text{pairs}}_0$` as iteration 1 of Iterative RPO achieves only 61.8% when initialized from Llama-2-70B-Chat — worse than SFT on gold (63.5%) and dramatically worse than Iterative RPO's 73.1% at iteration 1. DPO initialized from SFT on chosen sequences performs even worse at 60.3%, confirming that SFT initialization does not rescue standard DPO. The 11.3 percentage point gap between DPO (61.8%) and DPO+NLL (73.1%) on the first iteration is the direct empirical evidence for the NLL term's necessity.

**Iterations vs. more data.** The paper runs a controlled experiment to test whether iterative updates provide benefits beyond simply having more data. Iterative RPO with twice the paired data in iteration 1 (`$K = 20$` instead of `$K = 10$`) achieves 74.8%, compared to 73.1% with the standard amount of data — a gain of 1.7 points from doubling the data. However, two iterations (each with standard data) achieve 78.0%, a gain of 4.9 points over the single iteration. The improvement from iteration 1 to iteration 2 is larger than the improvement from doubling data in iteration 1, demonstrating that the iterative procedure provides value beyond data quantity: the on-policy data from `$M_1$` is qualitatively different from and more informative than additional data from `$M_0$`.

**Comparison with literature.** The paper contextualizes its GSM8K results against published numbers: Llama-2-70B 8-shot CoT achieves 56.8% (Touvron et al., 2023), close to the 55.6% zero-shot baseline. MetaMath (Yu et al., 2024) reports 82.3% and WizardMath (Luo et al., 2023) reports 81.6%, both using additional augmented training data. Iterative RPO achieves 81.6% without using any additional prompts — only the standard GSM8K training set. The paper emphasizes that data augmentation approaches are "orthogonal to ours, and both can provide benefits" (Section 3.1). Among proprietary models, GPT-4 achieves 92.0% (5-shot CoT), Claude 2 achieves 88.0%, PaLM 2 achieves 80.7%, and GPT-3.5 achieves 57.1% (5-shot).

#### ARC-Challenge Results: Robust Improvement Despite Multiple-Choice Noise

Table 2 reports ARC-Challenge results. Iterative RPO improves zero-shot accuracy from 77.8% to 86.7% at iteration 3, a gain of 8.9 percentage points over three iterations. Majority voting over 32 samples yields a further boost to 87.9% at iteration 3.

**Iteration-by-iteration improvements.** Iteration 1 achieves 84.8%, iteration 2 achieves 86.2%, and iteration 3 achieves 86.7%. The gains are +7.0, +1.4, and +0.5 percentage points, again showing decay across iterations. The paper notes that the number of usable training pairs decreases across iterations (from ~20k in iteration 1 to ~5k in iteration 3) because "more and more questions have no incorrect samples" as the model improves (Section 3.2).

**Comparison with baselines.** SFT on chosen sequences achieves 79.8%, only 2.0 points above the zero-shot baseline — substantially less improvement than on GSM8K (where SFT on chosen achieved 65.2% vs. 55.6% zero-shot). Standard DPO achieves 82.8%, and DPO initialized from SFT on chosen achieves 83.5%. While DPO does improve over the zero-shot baseline (unlike on GSM8K where it performed worse than SFT), Iterative RPO at iteration 1 (84.8%) still outperforms standard DPO (82.8%) by 2.0 points, and the iterative procedure adds another 1.9 points over subsequent iterations.

**Robustness to noisy labels.** The paper highlights that ARC-Challenge's multiple-choice format introduces significant noise into the preference pairs because "a correct final answer may be reached by luck (as random guessing is correct 25% of the time)" (Section 3.2). Despite this, Iterative RPO improves performance, and the DPO+NLL loss (Figure 4a) shows the same favorable dynamics (chosen probabilities rise, rejected probabilities fall) as observed on GSM8K.

#### MATH Results: Modest But Consistent Gains on Harder Problems

Table 2 reports MATH results using a 4-shot prompt. Iterative RPO improves few-shot accuracy from 12.5% to 20.8% at iteration 3, a gain of 8.3 percentage points. Majority voting over 32 samples yields 29.1%, compared to 18.8% for the few-shot baseline with majority voting — a gain of 10.3 points.

**Iteration-by-iteration improvements.** Iteration 1 achieves 17.7%, iteration 2 achieves 19.9%, and iteration 3 achieves 20.8%. The gains are +5.2, +2.2, and +0.9 percentage points — a similar decay pattern to the other tasks but starting from a much lower baseline.

**Comparison with baselines.** SFT on chosen sequences achieves 16.8%, a 4.3 point improvement over the baseline but 3.1 points below Iterative RPO's iteration 1. Standard DPO degrades performance: 12.4% when initialized from Llama-2-70B-Chat (worse than the 12.5% baseline) and 10.5% when initialized from SFT on chosen sequences. This is the starkest example of DPO's failure on reasoning: on the hardest task, standard DPO actively harms performance, even when initialized from a model that already improved over the baseline via SFT. Iterative RPO's DPO+NLL loss not only avoids this degradation but improves performance by 5.2 points in the first iteration.

**The difficulty gradient across tasks.** The three tasks form a clear difficulty spectrum: GSM8K (grade-school math) is the easiest, with a 55.6% zero-shot baseline; ARC-Challenge (multiple-choice science) is intermediate at 77.8% (note the 25% random baseline makes raw accuracy less comparable); MATH (competition math) is the hardest at 12.5% few-shot. The relative gains from Iterative RPO follow this gradient: GSM8K improves by 47% relative (55.6 → 81.6), ARC-Challenge improves by 11% relative (77.8 → 86.7), and MATH improves by 66% relative (12.5 → 20.8). However, the absolute gains are largest on GSM8K (+26.0 points) and smallest on ARC-Challenge (+8.9 points). The pattern suggests that Iterative RPO provides substantial relative improvements across difficulty levels, but the absolute ceiling is bounded by the base model's capability — MATH starts so low that even large relative gains leave substantial room for improvement.

**Comparison with literature on MATH.** The paper does not provide extensive literature comparisons for MATH, but the 20.8% accuracy places Iterative RPO's Llama-2-70B-Chat-based model in a moderate performance range compared to methods that use augmented data or larger models. The key comparison is internal: the method improves over all baselines that use the same base model and training data.

#### Cross-Task Consistency of Training Dynamics

The paper presents training dynamics plots for all three tasks (Figures 3 for GSM8K, 4a for ARC-Challenge, 4b for MATH) showing a consistent pattern:

**DPO without NLL (orange lines):** The log probability of chosen sequences either decreases (GSM8K, Figure 3) or barely increases (ARC-Challenge, Figure 4a; MATH, Figure 4b). The log probability of rejected sequences always decreases, so the margin grows — but the absolute generation probability of correct sequences is not improving.

**DPO with NLL (blue lines):** The log probability of chosen sequences consistently increases across all three tasks. The rejected probabilities consistently decrease. The margin between chosen and rejected grows more slowly than with standard DPO, but the absolute probability of chosen sequences rises — which is what matters for test-time generation.

This cross-task consistency is important evidence that the NLL term addresses a general optimization pathology, not a task-specific quirk. The three tasks differ substantially in format (open-ended vs. multiple-choice), difficulty, and domain (math vs. science), yet the same training dynamics are observed in all cases.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**NLL loss weight `$\alpha$`:** The paper tunes `$\alpha$` in `$\{0.25, 0.5, 1, 2\}$` on GSM8K iteration 1 and selects `$\alpha = 1$` for all experiments. No ablation table is provided showing performance at different `$\alpha$` values, so the sensitivity of results to this hyperparameter cannot be assessed. The choice of `$\alpha = 1$` implies equal weighting of the DPO and NLL terms in the loss, but the paper does not discuss whether performance degrades smoothly or sharply as `$\alpha$` moves away from 1.

**DPO temperature `$\beta$`:** The paper tunes `$\beta$` in `$\{0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0\}$` on GSM8K and selects `$\beta = 0.1$`. Again, no ablation table is provided. The choice of a small `$\beta$` is consistent with the idea that conservative preference optimization (compressing log-ratio differences before the sigmoid) is beneficial when the preference signal is noisy, but the paper does not explicitly test this hypothesis or show results at other `$\beta$` values.

**SFT initialization vs. DPO+NLL for iteration 1:** Table 1 reports that Iterative RPO iteration 1 achieves 73.1% regardless of whether the model is initialized from Llama-2-70B-Chat or from SFT trained on chosen sequences. The paper states: "whether we initialize with Llama-2-70b-chat or SFT on chosen for Iterative RPO, accuracy results of first iteration training do not seem to deviate (both obtain the same score 73.1%). This is another advantage of our method as the training process is simpler without the SFT step" (Section 3.1). This is a robustness check demonstrating that the DPO+NLL loss is not sensitive to the quality of the initialization — unlike standard DPO, which performs worse when initialized from SFT (60.3% vs. 61.8% from Llama-2-70B-Chat).

**Number of pairs `$K$` per prompt:** The paper runs a single ablation on GSM8K doubling `$K$` from 10 to 20 in iteration 1, achieving 74.8% (vs. 73.1% with `$K = 10$`). This is a modest 1.7 percentage point gain from doubling the training data. The paper does not explore `$K$` values systematically, nor does it report whether optimal `$K$` varies by task or iteration. The choice of `$K = 10$` for GSM8K, `$K = 20$` for ARC-Challenge, and `$K = 15$` for MATH appears to be based on practical data quantity management (e.g., the 60k pair cap for GSM8K) rather than systematic optimization.

**Generation temperature across iterations:** The paper increases the sampling temperature at later iterations for GSM8K (0.8 → 1.3 at iteration 3) and adjusts it for ARC-Challenge (0.8 for iterations 1–2, 1.3 for iteration 3) and MATH (0.8 for iterations 1–2, 1.0 for iteration 3). No ablation is provided showing the effect of maintaining constant temperature vs. increasing it. The stated rationale — "hoping that there is a significant number of incorrect generations in later iterations" (Section 3.1) — is plausible but untested: it's unknown whether the increased temperature is necessary for the observed gains, or whether performance would plateau earlier without it.

**Inclusion of gold chain-of-thought in winning set (GSM8K only):** For GSM8K, the paper includes the human-written gold solution in `$G^w_i$` when no model-generated solution is correct. This is not done for ARC-Challenge (no gold CoT available) or MATH (not mentioned). No ablation is provided showing GSM8K performance without this fallback. This means it's impossible to determine how much of the GSM8K improvement depends on having access to human-quality reasoning examples for the hardest training prompts.

**Cross-task hyperparameter transfer:** The paper tunes `$\alpha$` and `$\beta$` on GSM8K and uses the same values for ARC-Challenge and MATH. The implicit claim is that these hyperparameters transfer across tasks. The results on ARC-Challenge and MATH support this (both show improvements with DPO+NLL), but no systematic study of hyperparameter sensitivity on the other tasks is provided.

**Negative result: DPO degrades MATH performance.** Standard DPO on MATH achieves 12.4% (initialized from Llama-2-70B-Chat), which is worse than the 12.5% few-shot baseline, and 10.5% (initialized from SFT on chosen), which is worse than the 16.8% SFT baseline. This is the most severe instance of the probability collapse phenomenon in the paper's results, and it underscores the importance of the NLL term: on sufficiently hard tasks where the model's correct generation probability is already low, standard DPO's suppression of chosen sequence probability can push performance below the untrained baseline.

**Negative result: Iteration 4 on GSM8K shows negligible improvement.** From iteration 3 (81.1%) to iteration 4 (81.6%), the gain is only 0.5 percentage points, and majority voting improves only from 88.2% to 88.7%. The paper interprets this as saturation due to the fixed training set: as the model masters the training problems, it produces fewer incorrect responses, reducing the training signal. This is a practical limitation rather than a failure of the method, but it suggests that further iterations on the same training set will not yield additional gains.

### Critical Assessment

**Central Claim 1: "Iterative preference optimization with DPO+NLL substantially improves chain-of-thought reasoning."** The experiments strongly support this claim for the three tasks tested. On GSM8K, the improvement is large and sustained across four iterations (55.6% → 81.6%). On ARC-Challenge, the improvement is more modest but consistent (77.8% → 86.7%). On MATH, the improvement is smaller in absolute terms but large in relative terms (12.5% → 20.8%, a 66% relative gain). The claim holds across task types (open-ended math, multiple-choice science, competition math) and across difficulty levels. However, the claim has been demonstrated on only three benchmarks, all in the math/science reasoning domain. The paper does not test on code generation, logical reasoning, or other forms of multi-step deduction, so the generality of "chain-of-thought reasoning" as a category is asserted rather than demonstrated. Additionally, all experiments use a single model family (Llama-2-70B-Chat); the method may interact with model scale, pretraining data, or instruction-tuning recipe in ways not explored here.

**Central Claim 2: "The NLL term is necessary for iterative preference optimization to work on reasoning."** The evidence for necessity is strong within the experimental scope. On GSM8K, standard DPO (61.8%) underperforms DPO+NLL (73.1%) by a wide margin, and DPO initialized from a superior SFT checkpoint (60.3%) performs even worse. On MATH, standard DPO degrades performance below the baseline (12.4% vs. 12.5%), while DPO+NLL improves it (17.7%). The diagnostic evidence in Figures 3 and 4 — showing that standard DPO causes chosen sequence probabilities to fall while DPO+NLL causes them to rise — provides a mechanistic explanation. The paper also shows that SFT initialization alone does not prevent the collapse (Figure 3b), ruling out the most obvious alternative fix. However, "necessary" should be qualified: the paper tests only one specific recipe (iterative on-policy preference optimization with binary feedback on Llama-2-70B-Chat). It does not rule out that other modifications to standard DPO — different `$\beta$` scheduling, gradient clipping, different reference model selection, or alternative preference objectives — could achieve similar effects without an explicit NLL term. The NLL term is shown to be *sufficient* and its removal is shown to be *harmful*, but the space of possible fixes to DPO's probability collapse is not exhaustively explored.

**Central Claim 3: "Multiple iterations yield improvements beyond simply having more data."** This claim is supported by a single well-designed control experiment (Section 3.1): doubling the training data in iteration 1 improves accuracy from 73.1% to 74.8%, while two iterations (each with standard data) improve it to 78.0%. The 3.2 percentage point gap between the doubled-data single iteration and the standard two-iteration setup is the specific evidence that iteration provides value beyond data quantity. However, this experiment only tests the transition from iteration 1 to iteration 2. It does not test whether iteration 3 provides value beyond having three times the data in iteration 1, or whether the benefit of iteration diminishes as the model improves. The decay in iteration gains (+17.5, +4.9, +3.1, +0.5) suggests that the marginal value of additional iterations decreases, and by iteration 4, the benefit is essentially zero. This pattern is consistent with the paper's own saturation hypothesis but limits the practical significance of the iterative claim beyond 2–3 iterations.

**Central Claim 4: "Binary final-answer feedback is sufficient — no process-level supervision is needed."** The paper demonstrates this empirically across three tasks but does not provide analysis of *why* it works or *when* it might fail. The robustness to noisy labels is particularly evident on ARC-Challenge, where random guessing produces a 25% false positive rate in the preference pairs, yet performance still improves. However, the paper does not measure the actual noise rate in the preference pairs (what fraction of "winning" sequences contain flawed reasoning, and what fraction of "losing" sequences contain mostly-correct reasoning). Without this measurement, the claim that binary feedback is "sufficient" lacks a characterization of its limits. It is possible — even likely — that on tasks where correct answers are frequently reached through incorrect reasoning (e.g., problems with obvious answers but deceptive intermediate steps), the noise level would overwhelm the preference signal. The paper's positive results on MATH (where such deceptive problems are more common) provide partial reassurance, but a systematic noise analysis would strengthen the claim.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Comparison with process reward model (PRM) training.** The paper's method uses only final-answer correctness, while the companion paper (Iterative Reasoning Preference Optimization reference example) uses a trained PRM to score intermediate steps. A direct comparison — or even a discussion of expected tradeoffs — would help readers understand when the simpler binary-feedback approach suffices and when PRM-level supervision becomes necessary.

- **Ablation of the NLL term's normalization.** The NLL loss is normalized by response length (`$|c^w_i| + |y^w_i|$`). The paper does not test whether this normalization matters, or whether unnormalized NLL would work as well. Given that reasoning chains vary substantially in length (some solutions are 50 tokens, others 500), normalization could have a significant effect on which examples dominate the gradient.

- **Sensitivity to `$\alpha$` and `$\beta$` across tasks.** While the paper states that `$\alpha = 1$` and `$\beta = 0.1$` were selected via tuning on GSM8K and used for all tasks, no sensitivity curves are shown. It's unknown whether ARC-Challenge and MATH would benefit from different hyperparameters, or whether the GSM8K-chosen values are near-optimal for the other tasks.

- **Effect of model scale.** All experiments use Llama-2-70B-Chat. The paper does not test whether the method's effectiveness scales with model size (does a 13B model benefit more or less?), which would inform whether this approach is primarily useful for large models or generalizes to smaller ones.

- **Generalization to out-of-distribution reasoning.** The paper tests on the same dataset distributions as training (GSM8K test set, MATH test set, ARC-Challenge test set). There is no evaluation on transfer to related but distinct reasoning tasks (e.g., training on GSM8K and evaluating on SVAMP or ASDiv), which would test whether the method improves general reasoning capability or overfits to the training distribution.

- **Wall-clock time and computational cost reporting.** The paper reports hardware used (8 nodes × 8 A100 GPUs for training, 1 node × 8 V100 GPUs for generation) but does not provide training time, total FLOPs, or cost estimates. The four-iteration GSM8K procedure involves generating 30 samples for each of ~7.5k prompts (225k generations) per iteration, training for 5,000 steps, and repeating four times — a substantial computational investment that is not quantified.

**Conditions under which claims hold:**

The paper's claims are most strongly supported for: (1) instruction-tuned models at the 70B scale, (2) math and science reasoning tasks with objective correctness criteria, (3) tasks where the base model already has non-trivial performance (the method cannot create capability from zero — MATH starts at 12.5% and reaches 20.8%, a large relative gain but still modest absolute performance), and (4) settings where a fixed training set with gold answers is available. The claims are untested for: models at other scales, non-reasoning tasks (general instruction following), reasoning tasks without clear correctness criteria (open-ended analysis, debate, essay writing), and out-of-distribution generalization. The iterative procedure shows diminishing returns after 2–3 iterations on a fixed training set, suggesting that the method's ceiling is bounded by the diversity and difficulty coverage of the available training prompts.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Performance Saturates on Fixed Training Sets, Limiting Scalability

**The assumption or constraint.** Iterative RPO reuses the same fixed set of training prompts across all iterations. The model generates new preference pairs on each iteration, but those pairs are always drawn from the same underlying problem distribution — the GSM8K training questions, the ARC training questions, or the MATH training questions. The paper acknowledges this explicitly: "we are iterating across a fixed number of prompts, i.e., only from the training samples" (Section 3.1). It also flags the data re-use limitation in Appendix A: "When training iteration t using iterative RPO, we do not make use of the collected data in previous iterations. Utilizing those data could potentially boost the performance even more."

**The consequence.** The improvement from successive iterations decays sharply: on GSM8K, the gains are +17.5, +4.9, +3.1, and +0.5 percentage points across iterations 1 through 4 (Table 1). By iteration 4, the marginal gain is effectively zero (81.1% → 81.6%). The same pattern appears on ARC-Challenge (+7.0, +1.4, +0.5 over three iterations; Table 2) and MATH (+5.2, +2.2, +0.9 over three iterations; Table 2). The root cause is that as the model masters the training problems, it produces fewer incorrect responses, which shrinks the preference pair dataset: on ARC-Challenge, usable pairs drop from ~20k in iteration 1 to ~5k in iteration 3 (Section 3.2). At saturation, the model generates correct answers for nearly all training prompts, leaving few losing sequences to construct preference pairs. The training signal thus vanishes, and further iterations provide no benefit. This means the method's ceiling is **bounded by the diversity and difficulty coverage of the available training prompts**, not by the iterative procedure itself. For practitioners, this implies that the method cannot improve indefinitely on a fixed dataset — scaling to higher performance requires either expanding the training set (e.g., with data augmentation like MetaMath or WizardMath, which the paper identifies as orthogonal) or generating new training prompts (which the paper explicitly avoids to maintain simplicity).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 (GSM8K iteration-wise accuracies), Table 2 (ARC-Challenge and MATH iteration-wise accuracies), and Section 3.2 (ARC-Challenge pair count decline) provide direct evidence. The paper interprets the decay explicitly: "the gain decays across the iterations... indicating an upper limit on learning across iterations" (Section 3.1).

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation transparently but does not address it. Appendix A suggests that utilizing data from all previous iterations (rather than only the most recent iteration's data) "could potentially boost the performance even more" and leaves this to future work. The paper also notes that data augmentation approaches (MetaMath, WizardMath) are "orthogonal to ours, and both can provide benefits" (Section 3.1), implying that combining Iterative RPO with expanded training data could push past the saturation point. However, no such combination is tested.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for in the Absence of Gold Answers

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's method requires a binary reward signal — exact match between the generated final answer and the gold answer — for every training prompt. This is available in the experimental benchmarks because they are curated datasets with provided correct answers. In a realistic deployment where a practitioner wants to apply Iterative RPO to their own domain-specific reasoning problems, **gold answers must either already exist or be generated through some external verification mechanism.** The paper does not discuss this cost or propose a method for handling settings without pre-existing answer labels. Furthermore, the paper's method provides no mechanism for handling prompts where the model never generates a correct answer — for GSM8K, the human-written gold chain-of-thought is inserted as a fallback winning sequence (Section 3.1), but for ARC-Challenge, such prompts are simply dropped from training (Section 3.2). The paper acknowledges this dependency in Appendix A: "The current recipe requires correct answers, and a clear metric for comparing a generated response with this correct answer."

**The consequence.** This assumption creates two related problems for deployment. First, **the cost of obtaining gold answers is externalized from the paper's reported metrics.** The headline numbers (81.6% on GSM8K, 86.7% on ARC-Challenge, 20.8% on MATH) reflect performance after iterative training on datasets that already have gold answers — the cost of creating those datasets is not amortized into the method's efficiency. For a practitioner starting from scratch with a new domain, this cost could be substantial: human annotation of correct answers for thousands of reasoning problems, or development of an automatic verifier (e.g., unit tests for code, proof checkers for formal mathematics, numerical simulation for science problems). Second, **prompts that are too hard for the model to ever answer correctly provide no training signal** under the binary reward framework. On ARC-Challenge, the number of usable prompts shrinks from the full training set to ~5k pairs by iteration 3 (Section 3.2), meaning a substantial fraction of the training data is discarded. The method therefore cannot help the model learn to solve problems that are completely beyond its current capability — it can only refine performance on problems where the model already has at least some chance of success. This is a fundamental capability ceiling: Iterative RPO amplifies existing reasoning ability but does not create it from nothing, similar to the companion paper's finding that test-time compute cannot help on difficulty-bin-5 problems where the base model's pass@1 is near zero.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 3.2 documents the ARC-Challenge training data shrinkage. Appendix A explicitly states the requirement for correct answers. The paper does not measure the cost of obtaining gold answers, nor does it test the method on a domain where answers must be generated from scratch. The experimental setup in Section 3.1-3.3 makes clear that all three benchmarks provide pre-existing gold answers.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It acknowledges the requirement (Appendix A) but treats it as a given rather than a problem to solve. The companion paper (Iterative Reasoning Preference Optimization — the reference example) develops a process reward model trained via Monte Carlo rollouts to provide step-level correctness signals without human annotation; a similar approach could potentially provide final-answer verification for Iterative RPO, but this is not explored. The paper also does not discuss whether the method could work with noisy or approximate answer verification (e.g., using a weaker LLM to judge correctness, or using consistency across multiple samples as a proxy for correctness), which would reduce the dependency on ground-truth labels.

---

### No Combination of Preference Optimization with Process-Level Supervision

**The assumption or constraint.** The binary reward signal in Iterative RPO is based solely on final-answer correctness — exact match between the generated answer and the gold answer. The paper explicitly states: "We assume that we have access to a correctness measure for the final answer, and not for the correctness of the reasoning steps used to reach that answer" (Section 2). No process-level supervision, step-level verifier, or reasoning-quality assessment is used. The method treats a correct final answer reached through flawed reasoning identically to a correct final answer reached through perfect reasoning; similarly, an incorrect final answer from nearly-correct reasoning (e.g., a single arithmetic slip at the end) is treated identically to an incorrect answer from completely nonsensical reasoning.

**The consequence.** This assumption introduces **label noise into the preference pairs that the paper does not quantify.** On tasks where correct final answers are frequently reached through incorrect reasoning, the "winning" sequences may contain poor chain-of-thought, and the model learns to imitate those reasoning patterns. On tasks where incorrect final answers frequently result from minor errors in otherwise-correct reasoning, the "losing" sequences contain high-quality reasoning that is penalized. The paper's results on ARC-Challenge demonstrate robustness to substantial noise (random guessing produces 25% false positives), but the actual noise rate in the preference pairs — what fraction of winning sequences contain logically flawed reasoning, and what fraction of losing sequences contain mostly-correct reasoning — is never measured. On MATH, where problems are more complex and the gap between reasoning quality and answer correctness may be larger (e.g., a problem requiring 10 steps where an error at step 2 yields the wrong answer, but steps 2–10 are all logically sound given step 2's error), the noise could be particularly severe. The method's relative underperformance on MATH (20.8% vs. 81.6% on GSM8K) may be partially attributable to this noise rather than purely to problem difficulty — more complex reasoning chains create more opportunities for the binary reward signal to misalign with reasoning quality. The paper cannot distinguish between "the model fails to learn harder reasoning" and "the binary reward signal becomes too noisy to learn from" because it provides no analysis of preference pair quality.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no direct evidence on this limitation — no measurement of preference pair noise rates, no comparison with a method that uses process-level supervision, and no analysis of whether winning sequences with flawed reasoning correlate with reduced test-time performance. The closest proxy is the ARC-Challenge result (Section 3.2), where the paper notes that random guessing introduces noise and concludes that "the method seems robust to this issue." However, robustness on a 4-option multiple-choice task does not necessarily extend to open-ended generation where the noise structure is different (the ratio of false positive reasoning to true positive reasoning is not bounded by a fixed probability as it is in multiple choice). The paper's diagnostic plots (Figures 3, 4) show only aggregate log probabilities of chosen and rejected sequences, not the distribution of reasoning quality within each set.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It treats the robustness to binary feedback as a feature rather than a limitation to be solved, and it does not discuss when this robustness might break down. The companion paper's process reward model training pipeline (which scores every intermediate reasoning step) represents a potential solution, but integrating PRM-based verification with Iterative RPO's preference optimization is not explored. The paper also does not discuss whether training a lightweight verifier (e.g., an outcome reward model trained on the model's own generations) could provide a richer reward signal than binary exact match while remaining cheaper than full process supervision.

### The $14\times$ Larger Model Baseline Is Not Tested; Comparison Scope Is Limited to Llama-2-70B-Chat

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use a single base model: Llama-2-70B-Chat. The paper compares Iterative RPO against other training methods applied to the same base model (SFT, DPO, STaR) and against published results from other Llama-2-70B-derived models (MetaMath, WizardMath). It does **not** test Iterative RPO on models of other scales (e.g., Llama-2-7B-Chat, Llama-2-13B-Chat), other model families (e.g., Mistral, Qwen, Gemma), or base models without instruction tuning. The paper also does not compare against the alternative strategy of simply using a larger pretrained model — for instance, does Iterative RPO on Llama-2-70B-Chat match or exceed the performance of a much larger model (e.g., Llama-2-405B if it existed, or a proprietary model like GPT-4) with zero-shot CoT? The prior sections' reference example companion paper conducts a systematic FLOPs-matched comparison between test-time compute scaling and pretraining scaling; this paper provides no analogous analysis.

**The consequence.** The paper's central claim — that Iterative RPO is an effective method for improving chain-of-thought reasoning — has been validated on **exactly one model at exactly one scale.** This creates several uncertainties for practitioners. First, **does the method's effectiveness scale with model size?** At smaller scales (7B, 13B), the base model's pass@1 rate on training prompts will be lower, meaning fewer winning sequences per prompt and potentially insufficient data to construct informative preference pairs. At the same time, smaller models may benefit more from the contrastive signal because their reasoning is more error-prone. The direction of this effect is unknown. Second, **does the method depend on instruction tuning?** Llama-2-70B-Chat has been fine-tuned for instruction following, which may be critical for the model to produce coherent chain-of-thought in the zero-shot or few-shot formats required. A base pretrained model without instruction tuning might generate degenerate or off-format responses that cannot be reliably parsed for final answers, breaking the reward assignment step. Third, **how does Iterative RPO on a smaller model compare to simply using a larger model?** If Iterative RPO on Llama-2-70B-Chat achieves 81.6% on GSM8K, but GPT-4 (which is much larger) achieves 92.0% with 5-shot CoT alone (Achiam et al., 2023), then practitioners must decide whether the 81.6% figure is "good enough" given their compute budget, or whether investing in a larger base model with simpler inference is a better use of resources. The paper provides no guidance on this tradeoff because it doesn't measure performance at other model scales.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no experimental evidence on model scale or model family effects. Section 3.1 states that all experiments use Llama-2-70B-Chat and provides comparisons to published results from other Llama-2-70B variants (MetaMath, WizardMath). The comparison with GPT-4, Claude 2, PaLM 2, and GPT-3.5 (Section 3.1, "Other results in the literature") is purely contextual — these models differ in architecture, pretraining data, instruction tuning recipe, and scale, making any direct attribution to Iterative RPO's method impossible. The paper does not claim that these comparisons demonstrate superiority of Iterative RPO over scaling; it merely positions its results in the broader landscape.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It acknowledges in the broader literature discussion that proprietary models have "superior" results (Section 3.1) but does not analyze whether those results stem from model scale, training data, or training methodology. The absence of multi-scale experiments is noted as a limitation in Appendix A only indirectly ("We have experimented on three tasks. It is unclear how the approach would perform on general instruction tuning tasks"), which concerns task generalization rather than model generalization. The companion paper's FLOPs-matched analysis framework — comparing test-time compute on a smaller model to pretraining a larger model — is not replicated here, leaving open the question of whether Iterative RPO on a smaller model could be competitive with a proportionally larger model, as the companion paper demonstrated for its compute-optimal inference strategies.

### Sequential Generation Cost and Latency Are Not Accounted for in the Efficiency Picture

**The assumption or constraint.** Each iteration of Iterative RPO requires generating `$N$` samples per training prompt (where `$N = 30$` for GSM8K and ARC-Challenge, `$N = 20$` for MATH), scoring them via exact match, constructing `$K$` preference pairs, and training for up to 5,000 steps. The generation step is **entirely sequential with respect to the training loop**: you cannot begin training `$M_{t+1}$` until `$M_t$` has finished training and generated its samples. The generation itself can be parallelized across prompts (all prompts are independent), but the `$N$` samples per prompt are independent only if sufficient hardware is available to run them simultaneously. The paper reports hardware usage (8 nodes × 8 A100 GPUs for training, 1 node × 8 V100 GPUs for generation; Section 3.1) but does not report wall-clock time, total FLOPs, or the fraction of total compute spent on generation versus training. In a resource-constrained setting, the generation step may become a bottleneck: generating 30 samples for each of ~7.5k GSM8K prompts (225,000 total generations) at the 70B scale is computationally expensive, and this must be repeated at every iteration.

**The consequence.** The paper's reported accuracy numbers (81.6% GSM8K, 86.7% ARC-Challenge, 20.8% MATH) are the result of a **computationally intensive multi-iteration pipeline whose total cost is not quantified.** For a practitioner deciding whether to adopt this method, the efficiency question is not "does Iterative RPO improve accuracy?" (it does) but rather "does the accuracy improvement justify the computational cost compared to alternatives?" The alternatives include: (1) SFT on gold chain-of-thought, which requires only one training run and no generation step (63.5% on GSM8K — 18.1 points lower than Iterative RPO but vastly cheaper); (2) STaR with one iteration of generation and SFT (65.2% on GSM8K — 16.4 points lower but much cheaper than four iterations of Iterative RPO); (3) simply deploying a larger model (GPT-4 achieves 92.0% on GSM8K, likely at higher per-query inference cost but with zero iterative training cost). Without cost quantification, practitioners cannot evaluate which point on the accuracy-per-dollar Pareto frontier each method occupies. The paper's comparison with MetaMath (82.3%) and WizardMath (81.6%) is informative for accuracy but silent on cost: those methods use augmented training data (which has its own curation cost), while Iterative RPO uses iterative generation (which has its own compute cost). The relative cost-effectiveness is unknown.

Additionally, the sequential dependency between iterations means that the **wall-clock time to deploy an Iterative RPO-trained model is at least the sum of (generation time + training time) across all iterations.** If each iteration takes, for example, 12 hours of generation plus 24 hours of training, a 4-iteration run takes 6 days of continuous computation. This may be impractical for rapid iteration cycles or for applications where the model must be updated frequently.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides hardware specifications (Section 3.1: "Throughout this paper, all generation is done using one node containing eight V100 GPUs (32G memory). All training is done using eight nodes each containing eight A100 GPUs (80G memory).") but no timing, FLOP count, or cost estimates. The number of generations per iteration is calculable from the reported `$N$` and training set sizes: GSM8K iteration 1 generates ~225k responses (7.5k prompts × 30 samples), ARC-Challenge generates ~231k (7.7k × 30), and MATH generates ~150k (7.5k × 20). Training runs for up to 5,000 steps per iteration with batch size 16. The total computational cost across all iterations is thus substantial but unquantified.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It does not discuss the cost of generation relative to training, the latency implications of the iterative pipeline, or potential strategies to reduce generation cost (e.g., reusing generations across iterations, reducing `$N$` at later iterations when the model produces fewer incorrect responses, or using speculative decoding to accelerate generation). Appendix A mentions that "utilizing those data [from previous iterations] could potentially boost the performance even more" but frames this as a potential accuracy improvement rather than a cost-reduction strategy. The paper does not provide guidance for practitioners on how to choose `$N$`, `$K$`, and the number of iterations to balance cost and accuracy.

### Generalisation to Non-Reasoning Tasks and Open-Ended Generation Is Untested

**The assumption or constraint.** All three benchmarks evaluated in the paper — GSM8K, MATH, and ARC-Challenge — share a common structure: there is a single objectively correct answer, and correctness can be determined by exact match (after normalization). The paper's method relies on this structure for the binary reward signal. The paper does not evaluate on tasks where correctness is subjective, multi-dimensional, or continuous (e.g., summarization quality, dialogue helpfulness, creative writing, code quality beyond unit test pass/fail), nor on tasks where chain-of-thought reasoning is not the primary mode of problem-solving (e.g., factual QA requiring knowledge retrieval, translation, structured data extraction). The paper acknowledges this explicitly in Appendix A: "It is unclear how the approach would perform on general instruction tuning tasks without a clear best answer, but we argue that positive results on the three tasks in this paper can already prove the method useful."

**The consequence.** The method's applicability is **currently restricted to domains with verifiable correct answers** — a subset of reasoning tasks that excludes many practically important applications. This matters for two reasons. First, many reasoning tasks that practitioners care about do not have a single correct answer or cannot be automatically verified. For example, generating a business strategy recommendation, writing a legal analysis, or debugging a complex software system all require reasoning but have open-ended or multi-faceted correctness criteria. The paper's binary reward framework cannot be directly applied to these tasks. Second, even within the domain of math and science reasoning, the method has only been tested on three specific benchmarks. The paper does not evaluate on other standard reasoning benchmarks (e.g., SVAMP, ASDiv, AQuA for math; OpenBookQA, PIQA for science; BigBench-Hard for general reasoning), leaving open the question of whether the method's effectiveness generalizes across reasoning subdomains or is specific to the GSM8K/MATH/ARC-Challenge problem distributions. The paper's claim that the method "has the potential to improve the reasoning ability of LLMs over a wide range of tasks" (Section 1) is aspirational rather than empirically supported by the current evaluation scope.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's experimental results are limited to the three reported benchmarks (Tables 1 and 2). Section 4 mentions that prior iterative DPO methods showed "only modest gains on reasoning tasks" in the context of general instruction tuning benchmarks, but the paper does not test Iterative RPO on those general instruction tuning tasks to see whether the DPO+NLL modification helps there as well. The loss dynamics in Figures 3 and 4 are shown only for the three reasoning benchmarks, not for any non-reasoning task. Appendix A directly acknowledges the limitation: "It is unclear how the approach would perform on general instruction tuning tasks without a clear best answer."

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges this limitation in Appendix A but offers no empirical investigation or proposed extension to handle it. The limitation is presented as a scope boundary rather than a problem to solve. The paper argues that "positive results on the three tasks in this paper can already prove the method useful" — which is true for the specific domain of math and science reasoning with verifiable answers, but does not constitute evidence for broader applicability. Future work would need to address: (1) how to define and compute rewards for open-ended reasoning tasks (e.g., using LLM-as-judge, learned reward models, or multi-dimensional rubrics), (2) whether the DPO+NLL dynamics observed on math/science reasoning transfer to subjective preference settings, and (3) whether the probability collapse phenomenon (Section 4, Innovation 1) is specific to binary-correctness preferences or generalizes to other preference structures. The paper does not propose any of these investigations.
