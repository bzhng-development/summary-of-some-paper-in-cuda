# SageAttention: Accurate 8-Bit Attention for Plug-and-play Inference Acceleration

**ArXiv:** [2410.02367](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02367)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **SageAttention**, a post-training INT8 quantization method that accelerates the attention computation in transformers while preserving end-to-end model accuracy.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **SageAttention**, a post-training INT8 quantization method that accelerates the attention computation in transformers while preserving end-to-end model accuracy. Evaluated across diverse models—Llama2 for language, CogvideoX for video generation, Unidiffuser and UltraPixel for image generation, TIMM for classification, and Llava1.6 for visual question answering—on benchmarks including WikiText, MMLU, COCO, and ImageNet, SageAttention achieves approximately 2.1× and 2.7× faster attention throughput than FlashAttention2 and xformers respectively on RTX4090 GPUs, while yielding negligible average metric degradation (0.2%) compared to full-precision attention. The method's core mechanisms include **smoothing matrix K** (subtracting a per-token mean to eliminate channel-wise outliers before quantization), **FP16 accumulator for the PV Matmul** (retaining P and V in FP16 with a low-precision accumulator as a faster and more accurate alternative to INT8 quantization of these tensors), and **adaptive quantization** (per-layer selection between per-token quantization with INT8 PV and per-block quantization with FP16 PV based on cosine similarity thresholds), establishing that plug-and-play 8-bit attention can match full-precision quality across text, image, and video generation tasks without any additional training or model-specific calibration.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Attention Becomes the Bottleneck at Scale

The paper addresses a specific, measurable, and increasingly urgent performance bottleneck in transformer inference: **attention dominates total computation when sequence lengths grow large**. This is not a hypothetical concern — the paper quantifies it directly in Figure 2, showing that at sequence lengths above roughly 4K tokens, attention latency eclipses all other operations (linear layers, normalization, activations) combined. At 32K tokens with 32 attention heads and head dimension 64, attention consumes approximately 700ms out of roughly 800ms total transformer block latency, leaving less than 15% of compute time for everything else.

The root cause is the quadratic complexity of standard dot-product attention. A linear transformation costs O(N) operations in sequence length N; attention costs O(N²). When N is small (e.g., 512 tokens in BERT-style models), the quadratic term is manageable. But the paper observes a clear trend toward longer sequences across all modalities:

- **Video generation** (CogvideoX): sequence lengths of 8K–128K tokens are routine, as each frame contributes spatial patches that multiply across the temporal dimension.
- **Language model prefilling** (Llama2, Llama3): long-context models now routinely process prompts of 8K–128K tokens, and the prefilling stage — where the full prompt is processed in parallel — is attention-bound.
- **High-resolution image generation** (UltraPixel): generating images at resolutions like 2560×1536 produces thousands of visual tokens whose pairwise interactions all flow through attention.

In these regimes, even the most optimized full-precision attention implementations (FlashAttention2, xformers) leave attention as the dominant cost center, and any improvement to attention directly translates to end-to-end inference speedup.

### Why Quantization Hasn't Solved This Yet

Quantization — reducing numerical precision from FP16/FP32 to INT8 or FP8 — is a well-established technique for accelerating neural network inference. By halving or quartering the bit-width of matrix multiplications, quantization can theoretically deliver 2–4× throughput improvements on hardware with specialized low-precision compute units (e.g., NVIDIA Tensor Cores' INT8 `mma` instructions). The machine learning systems community has produced a large body of work on quantization for **linear layers**: SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023a) for LLM activation quantization, AWQ (Lin et al., 2024) for weight-only quantization, I-BERT (Kim et al., 2021) for integer-only BERT inference, and numerous FP8 training works (Sun et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2023; Xi et al., 2024b).

However, all of these methods leave **attention unquantized** — running at full FP16 precision. The paper identifies this as a conspicuous gap:

> "There is not yet a work that systematically investigates the quantization of attention."

The omission is not accidental. Quantizing attention is genuinely harder than quantizing linear layers for reasons the paper investigates in detail:

1. **Attention involves softmax, not just matrix multiplications.** A linear layer is a single Matmul: $Y = WX$. Attention is $O = \text{softmax}(QK^T/\sqrt{d})V$, which chains two Matmuls with a nonlinear softmax in between. Quantization error in the first Matmul ($QK^T$) propagates through the exponential softmax, which can amplify small errors nonlinearly — a tiny perturbation in a logit can become a large change in attention weight after exponentiation.

2. **The K matrix exhibits severe channel-wise outliers.** The paper provides visual evidence in Figure 4, showing that K matrices from real models (CogvideoX, Unidiffuser) contain specific channels where activation values are 10–100× larger than typical channel values. These outliers are not random — they appear as consistent "spikes" in particular channel indices across all tokens. When quantizing to INT8, the scale factor must accommodate the maximum absolute value in the tensor. A single outlier channel forces the scale to be large, causing all "normal" values to be quantized to zero or near-zero after rounding, destroying the information in the K matrix.

3. **The PV Matmul has different sensitivity than QK^T.** The paper shows in Table 3 that INT8 quantization of P and V, while working reasonably on average (Table 2), can catastrophically fail on specific layers — the *worst-case* cosine similarity drops to 56.40% for INT8 P,V versus 99.99% for FP16. This means a uniform quantization strategy (always INT8, always FP16) cannot work across all layers in all models — some layers are quantizeable, others are not.

4. **Existing FP8 attention solutions are limited.** FlashAttention3 (Shah et al., 2024) was recently released with FP8 attention support, but the paper identifies two critical limitations: (a) it is **architecturally locked** to NVIDIA Hopper GPUs (H100, H800), which have native FP8 Tensor Core support — it cannot run on the vastly more common RTX 3090/4090 or A100 GPUs, and (b) **its accuracy is worse than SageAttention's INT8 approach**. Table 1 shows that FlashAttention3's quantized version degrades Unidiffuser FID from 163.33 to 394.13 (catastrophic failure) and Llama2 WikiText perplexity from 5.823 to 5.850, while SageAttention with smoothing achieves 167.65 and 5.824, respectively.

### Why INT8 Specifically (And Why Not FP8)

A key design choice that requires motivation is the paper's preference for INT8 over FP8. This decision is driven by two practical hardware realities:

**First, throughput.** On consumer and datacenter GPUs prior to Hopper (RTX 3090, RTX 4090, A100), INT8 matrix multiplication via Tensor Cores is approximately **4× faster than FP16** and **2× faster than FP8**. The FP8 instructions on Hopper GPUs achieve roughly 2× FP16 throughput, but these instructions simply don't exist on earlier architectures. Since the paper targets "plug-and-play" acceleration that works broadly — not just on the newest, most expensive hardware — INT8 provides the best speedup on the largest installed base of GPUs.

**Second, precision for Q and K.** Table 2 provides empirical evidence: when quantizing Q and K specifically, INT8 achieves 99.70% average cosine similarity versus 99.94% for E4M3 (FP8) and 99.81% for E5M2 (FP8). The differences seem small, but they compound through the softmax and the second Matmul. More importantly, **Table 17 in Appendix B** shows that for the Q·K product itself, INT8 per-token quantization achieves 99.54% cosine similarity versus only 92.83% for E4M3 and 77.95% for E5M2 — a dramatic gap. The paper argues this is because Q and K values tend to have relatively uniform distributions within each token (making uniform INT8 quantization with a per-token scale effective), while FP8's reduced mantissa bits (3 bits for E4M3, 2 bits for E5M2) sacrifice precision for dynamic range that isn't needed here.

The speed advantage of INT8 over FP8 on non-Hopper GPUs, combined with its superior precision on Q,K tensors, makes INT8 the clear choice for the paper's goal of accurate, broadly deployable attention acceleration.

### Where Existing Attention Acceleration Falls Short

The paper categorizes prior efficient attention work into three groups (Section 2) and explains why none fully addresses the bottleneck:

**Sparse Attention** (Swin Transformer, Attention Sinks, InfLLM, Minference, SkipAttention) reduces computation by only attending to a subset of tokens — e.g., local windows, sink tokens, or dynamically selected key tokens. The paper identifies the fundamental limitation:

> "these methods' limitations are that they only work in a few scenarios because omitted calculations are not always useless."

Sparsity patterns are task-specific and model-specific. A sparsity pattern that works for text may fail for video where long-range dependencies across frames matter. A pattern tuned for Llama2's attention sinks may not transfer to CogvideoX's 3D attention. SageAttention positions itself as orthogonal: it accelerates *full* attention without discarding any computations, so it works identically across all models and tasks without accuracy-loss risk.

**Linear Attention** (Linformer, Performer, LinearAttention) replaces the O(N²) attention with O(N) approximations using kernel methods or low-rank projections. The paper acknowledges these "excel in specific scenarios" but notes that "standard Attention remains prevalent" — i.e., most production models (Llama2, Stable Diffusion, CogvideoX) use exact dot-product attention, not linear approximations. Replacing attention with a linear variant requires architectural changes and retraining. SageAttention is plug-and-play: it accelerates the exact attention computation that models were trained with.

**Kernel Optimization** (FlashAttention, FlashAttention2, FlashAttention3, xformers) is the category SageAttention belongs to — methods that compute exact attention faster through better hardware utilization (tiling, kernel fusion, warp scheduling) without changing the mathematics. FlashAttention2 is the state-of-the-art open-source kernel, achieving ~165 TOPS on RTX 4090 at headdim=64. The paper's key observation is that FlashAttention2 operates entirely in FP16 — it hasn't exploited the 2–4× additional throughput available from INT8 Tensor Core instructions. FlashAttention3 adds FP8 support but only on Hopper GPUs, and even then its accuracy degrades on several models (Table 1). SageAttention fills the gap: INT8-accelerated attention that matches FlashAttention2's accuracy while using the faster low-precision hardware instructions available on a much wider range of GPUs.

### The Contradiction the Paper Resolves

There is a tension in the quantization literature that SageAttention directly confronts. Prior work like I-BERT (Kim et al., 2021) showed that you *can* quantize all operations in a transformer to INT8, including attention — but only for the specific case of RoBERTa, and the method required quantization-aware training. No prior work had demonstrated that a single, training-free quantization recipe could work across the dramatically different activation distributions found in language models (Llama2), text-to-image models (Unidiffuser), text-to-video models (CogvideoX), and vision transformers (TIMM) — all without model-specific calibration.

The paper's Figure 3 shows what happens with naive quantization: Unidiffuser produces a completely blurry image with direct INT8 quantization, while FlashAttention3's FP8 version produces an image that is visually unrecognizable. SageAttention resolves this by identifying *why* naive quantization fails (K channel outliers, PV sensitivity variance) and introducing targeted fixes (K smoothing, FP16 accumulator for PV, adaptive per-layer strategy selection) that address the root causes rather than relying on retraining or architecture-specific tuning.

### Practical Significance: The RTX 4090 and 3090 Ecosystem

A crucial contextual motivation — though not explicitly stated as such — is the paper's focus on GPUs that lack native FP8 support but have fast INT8 Tensor Cores. The RTX 4090 and 3090 represent the most widely available high-performance GPUs for practitioners (researchers, small companies, individual developers) who cannot access Hopper datacenter GPUs. On these GPUs, the FP16 attention implementations (FlashAttention2) leave roughly 50% of theoretical throughput unused because they don't leverage INT8 instructions. SageAttention recovers this wasted capacity: on RTX 4090 with headdim=64, it achieves 340 INT8 TOPS (52% of theoretical peak) versus FlashAttention2's 165 FP16 TOPS. The paper notes that this 340 TOPS is "close to the 490 TOPS throughput of FlashAttention3" on Hopper — meaning SageAttention on a consumer GPU nearly matches the performance of an architecture-exclusive optimization on a much more expensive datacenter GPU, a democratizing result for the ML community.

### How the Paper Positions Its Contribution

The paper positions SageAttention not as a fundamentally new mathematical formulation of attention, but as an **engineering contribution** that bridges the gap between quantization theory and the specific numerical properties of attention computation in real models. The contribution is:

1. **Diagnostic**: identifying the specific failure modes (K outliers, PV sensitivity) that prevent naive attention quantization from working across diverse models.
2. **Remedial**: proposing lightweight, mathematically justified fixes (K smoothing preserves softmax invariance; FP16 accumulator is lossless for PV; adaptive selection uses cosine similarity as a cheap proxy for layer-wise quantizeability).
3. **Validating**: demonstrating through extensive end-to-end benchmarks that the complete system works across modalities (text, image, video) and architectures without retraining, calibration, or model-specific tuning — establishing for the first time that plug-and-play INT8 attention is practically viable at scale.

The paper explicitly distinguishes itself from methods requiring retraining (I-BERT), methods locked to specific hardware (FlashAttention3 FP8), and methods that sacrifice accuracy for speed (sparse/linear attention) — positioning SageAttention as the first general-purpose, training-free, accurate INT8 attention accelerator.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

SageAttention is a drop-in replacement kernel for the standard attention computation in transformer models that performs all matrix multiplications in 8-bit integer (INT8) arithmetic instead of 16-bit floating-point (FP16), without requiring any model retraining, fine-tuning, or per-model calibration. The method solves the problem that naive INT8 quantization destroys attention accuracy due to channel-wise outliers in the key matrix and variable sensitivity of the value-projection multiplication, by introducing a mathematically invariant smoothing transform on K, a mixed-precision strategy that keeps the second matrix multiplication in FP16 with a low-precision accumulator, and a per-layer adaptive mechanism that automatically selects the fastest quantized kernel configuration that preserves accuracy for each specific attention layer.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The SageAttention system has five major components that operate within the tiled FlashAttention computation loop:

1. **K-Smoothing Preprocessor** — subtracts the per-token mean from the K matrix before quantization, eliminating channel-wise outliers without changing the softmax output. This runs once per attention call, outside the tiling loop.

2. **Q and K Quantizer** — converts the (smoothed) K and the scaled Q (multiplied by `$1/\sqrt{d}$` on-chip) to INT8 using either per-token or per-block granularity, with scale factors stored in FP32. This runs inside the outer loop over Q tiles.

3. **Online Softmax (Full Precision)** — computes the attention weights `$\tilde{P}$` from the dequantized `$QK^T$` product in FP32, using the standard FlashAttention online softmax algorithm to avoid materializing the full `$N \times N$` attention matrix. This operates in full precision to prevent softmax from amplifying quantization error.

4. **PV Computation Module** — multiplies the attention weights `$\tilde{P}$` by the values `$V$`, using one of two strategies: (a) quantize both `$\tilde{P}$` and `$V$` to INT8 for maximum speed, or (b) keep both in FP16 and use an FP16 accumulator for intermediate sums (sacrificing some speed for guaranteed accuracy). This runs inside the inner loop over K,V tiles.

5. **Adaptive Kernel Selector** — a pre-deployment profiling step that runs each layer's attention with multiple kernel configurations on sample inputs, measures the cosine similarity of the output versus full-precision, and assigns the fastest kernel that exceeds a 99.8% similarity threshold. The selection is done once and cached, adding zero runtime overhead.

Information flows as follows during inference: Q, K, V enter in FP16 → K is smoothed (mean subtracted) → Q is multiplied by `$1/\sqrt{d}$` and Q,K are quantized to INT8 with per-block or per-token scales → the INT8 `$QK^T$` product is computed on Tensor Cores → the result is dequantized to FP32 → online softmax produces `$\tilde{P}$` → based on the pre-selected kernel, `$\tilde{P}$` and `$V$` are either quantized to INT8 or kept in FP16 → the `$\tilde{P}V$` product is computed using either INT8 or FP16-with-FP16-accumulator Tensor Core instructions → the partial output is accumulated in FP32 using the online softmax renormalization → final output O is written in FP16.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the quantized attention formulation (Equations 4–5), which defines how quantization operators are inserted into the FlashAttention tiling loop and establishes the mathematical framework that all subsequent techniques operate within.
- **Second**, the K-smoothing transform (Equation 6), which is the critical innovation that makes QK quantization accurate — we need to understand why K outliers exist, why per-channel quantization cannot solve them, and why the smoothing transform is both mathematically invariant and computationally cheap.
- **Third**, the quantization granularity and data type choices for Q, K, `$\tilde{P}$`, and V, including the empirical evidence (Tables 2–5) that drives the per-block and per-channel decisions.
- **Fourth**, the FP16 accumulator strategy for the PV matmul — why INT8 quantization of P and V fails catastrophically on some layers, why FP16 with an FP16 accumulator is both faster and more accurate than FP16 with an FP32 accumulator, and how this creates a two-tier kernel family.
- **Fifth**, the adaptive quantization mechanism — how the four kernel variants (SAGEAttn-T, SAGEAttn-B, SAGEAttn-vT, SAGEAttn-vB) are constructed from the design space, and how the per-layer selection procedure works using cosine similarity as a cheap accuracy proxy.
- **Sixth**, the fusion tricks and performance analysis — how quantization is fused with preceding operations (ROPE, `$1/\sqrt{d}$` scaling) to eliminate memory I/O overhead, and the hardware-level explanation for why INT8 attention achieves 2× speedup despite quantization/dequantization overhead.

This is primarily an **engineering and empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that attention can be accurately quantized to INT8 by addressing two root causes of quantization error — K channel outliers and PV matmul sensitivity — through a combination of a mathematically invariant smoothing transform, mixed-precision compute, and per-layer adaptive kernel selection, without requiring any model retraining or calibration.

#### Quantized Attention Formulation

The paper formulates quantized attention by inserting quantization and dequantization operators into the standard FlashAttention tiling loop described in Section 3.1. The key insight is that quantization can be applied independently to each tile (block) of Q, K, and V as they are loaded into on-chip SRAM, which aligns naturally with FlashAttention's memory hierarchy.

The formulation uses the following notation for the quantizers from Section 3.2: `$\psi_A$` is a quantizer that maps a high-precision matrix A to a low-precision representation `$\hat{A}$` with scale factor `$\delta_A$`, and `$\psi^{-1}_{\delta_A}$` is the corresponding dequantizer that maps back. Specifically:

> **Quantization step (Equation 4):**
>
> $$(\delta_Q, \hat{Q}) = \psi_Q(Q / \sqrt{d}), \quad (\delta_K, \hat{K}) = \phi_K(K), \quad (\delta_P, \hat{P}) = \psi_P(\tilde{P}), \quad (\delta_V, \hat{V}) = \psi_V(V)$$
>
> where `$Q, K, V \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$` are the query, key, and value matrices; `$\tilde{P}$` is the unnormalized attention weight matrix from online softmax; `$\psi_Q, \psi_K, \psi_P, \psi_V$` are quantizers (each potentially using different granularities and data types); and `$\phi_K$` is a special transformation applied to K before quantization (the smoothing operation, discussed next). The hat notation `$\hat{\cdot}$` denotes quantized tensors; `$\delta$` values are FP32 scale factors.
>
> **What this step computes:** it converts the four matrices involved in attention's two matrix multiplications from FP16 to low-precision formats (INT8 or FP8) by computing a scale factor (typically the maximum absolute value divided by the representable range) and then rounding each element to the nearest representable low-precision value. For Q, the `$1/\sqrt{d}$` scaling is fused into the quantization (multiplied on-chip before quantization) to avoid a separate kernel launch. The output is a tuple of (scale, quantized tensor) for each matrix.
>
> **Why this form:** the `$1/\sqrt{d}$` factor is fused with Q quantization rather than left as a separate operation because it avoids an extra read/write of Q from global memory. Applying `$\phi_K$` to K before `$\psi_K$` is the key departure from naive quantization — the paper found that directly quantizing raw K is what causes catastrophic accuracy loss, and `$\phi_K$` is a pre-processing transform that makes K quantizable without changing the mathematical output of attention.

The `$\phi_K$` transformation is defined in Section 4.2 and discussed in detail below. For now, the critical property is that it modifies K before quantization in a way that preserves the softmax output exactly in full precision, while dramatically reducing the dynamic range that the quantizer must represent.

> **Attention computation step (Equation 5):**
>
> $$S = \psi^{-1}_{\delta_Q \delta_K}(\hat{Q} \hat{K}^T), \quad (m', \tilde{P}) = \tilde{\sigma}(m, S), \quad O = \text{diag}(\exp(m' - m)) O + \psi^{-1}_{\delta_P \delta_V}(\hat{P} \hat{V})$$
>
> where `$S$` is the dequantized attention score matrix (before softmax); `$\tilde{\sigma}$` is the online softmax operator from Section 3.1 that computes new running max `$m'$` and exponentiated attention weights `$\tilde{P}$` from the previous running max `$m$` and the current score block `$S$`; `$O$` is the running (unnormalized) output accumulation; and `$\text{diag}(\exp(m' - m))$` is the renormalization factor that corrects previous partial outputs when a new maximum score is discovered.
>
> **What this step computes:** the actual attention forward pass, but with low-precision matrix multiplications. First, `$\hat{Q}\hat{K}^T$` is computed as an INT8 matmul on Tensor Cores, and the result is dequantized by multiplying with the product of the Q and K scales (`$\delta_Q \delta_K$`) to recover an FP32 attention score matrix `$S$`. Then, the standard online softmax processes `$S$` in full FP32 precision — this is crucial because softmax's exponential can amplify small quantization errors. Finally, `$\hat{P}\hat{V}$` is computed as another low-precision matmul (either INT8 or FP16-with-FP16-accumulator), dequantized, and accumulated into the running output using the online softmax renormalization.
>
> **Why this form:** the softmax is kept in full precision (FP32) rather than quantized because it is the operation most sensitive to numerical error — a quantization error of 0.01 in a logit can become a 1% error in attention weight after exponentiation. The matmuls are the compute-intensive operations (O(N²) for QK^T and O(N²·d) for PV when N is large), while softmax is O(N²) with a much smaller constant factor, so quantizing only the matmuls captures nearly all the speedup while avoiding the riskiest quantization. The dequantization scales are multiplied as `$\delta_Q \delta_K$` (and `$\delta_P \delta_V$`) rather than dequantizing each matrix separately before multiplication because it saves one dequantization step — mathematically, `$(\delta_Q \hat{Q})(\delta_K \hat{K})^T = (\delta_Q \delta_K)(\hat{Q}\hat{K}^T)$` by associativity of scalar multiplication.

The paper notes that the superscripts and subscripts from FlashAttention's tiling (i, j indices for Q blocks and K,V blocks) are omitted for simplicity, but the computation is organized exactly as in Section 3.1: Q is tiled into blocks `$\{Q_i\}$` along the token dimension with block size `$b_q$`, K and V are tiled into blocks `$\{K_j\}, \{V_j\}$` with block size `$b_{kv}$`, and the inner loop iterates over `$j$` to progressively compute each output block `$O_i$` using online softmax renormalization. Quantization and dequantization happen once per tile as it moves between global memory and on-chip SRAM.

#### Smoothing Matrix K — The Key Innovation

The paper identifies that the primary obstacle to quantizing Q and K is not the overall magnitude of these matrices, but a specific structural property of K: **channel-wise outliers that appear as a large bias shared across all tokens, plus a small token-wise signal**. Figure 4 provides visual evidence by plotting the per-channel distributions of Q, K, and V from real models (CogvideoX, Unidiffuser). In the K panels, specific channels show values of ±150–210 while most channels fall within ±10 — a 15–20× dynamic range disparity. Critically, these outlier channels have roughly the same magnitude for every token; they are not spiky in the token dimension.

This pattern is disastrous for quantization because quantization scale factors are computed over groups of elements. Consider what happens with per-token quantization of K (the most natural choice, since the outer axis of the QK^T matmul is the token dimension): for each token (row of K), the scale factor `$\delta_K[i] = \max(|K[i,:]|) / 127$` must accommodate the largest absolute value in that row. If channel 5 has a consistent value of +200 across all tokens, then every token's scale factor will be approximately 200/127 ≈ 1.57. Any channel with values in the range [-5, 5] will be quantized to approximately `$\lceil \pm 5 / 1.57 \rfloor \approx \pm 3$` (out of a possible ±127 range), effectively using only 2.4% of the INT8 representable range for the vast majority of the information in K. The quantization error on these "normal" channels will be on the order of 1.57/2 ≈ 0.8, which is huge relative to the actual signal magnitude of ~5.

Per-channel quantization cannot solve this because the inner axis of the QK^T matmul is the contraction dimension — the matmul sums over channels, so per-channel scale factors for K would need to be multiplied with per-element values of Q during dequantization, which is not a supported operation in standard matmul primitives (dequantization can only happen along the outer axes).

The paper's key insight is that these channel outliers represent a **bias**, not a variance. The large values in outlier channels are nearly constant across tokens, which means they contribute a constant offset to every dot product `$q \cdot k$` for a given query `$q$`. Specifically, if we decompose K as:

$$K = \mathbf{1}_N \cdot \bar{k}^T + K_{\text{residual}}$$

where `$\bar{k} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^N K[t,:] \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d}$` is the mean key vector (average across all tokens) and `$\mathbf{1}_N \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1}$` is a vector of ones, then for any query vector `$q \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d}$`:

$$q(K - \mathbf{1}_N \bar{k}^T)^T = qK^T - q\bar{k} \cdot \mathbf{1}_N^T = qK^T - (q\bar{k}) \cdot \mathbf{1}_N^T$$

The term `$(q\bar{k}) \cdot \mathbf{1}_N^T$` is a constant added to every element of the attention score vector `$qK^T$`. Adding a constant to all logits before softmax does not change the softmax output:

$$\text{softmax}(x + c) = \frac{e^{x_i + c}}{\sum_j e^{x_j + c}} = \frac{e^{x_i}}{\sum_j e^{x_j}} = \text{softmax}(x)$$

Therefore, computing attention with the smoothed key matrix `$K - \text{mean}(K)$` is **mathematically identical** to computing attention with the original K:

> **K-smoothing transform (Equation 6):**
>
> $$\gamma(K) = K - \text{mean}(K)$$
>
> where `$\text{mean}(K) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^N K[t,:] \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d}$` is the average key vector computed across all `$N$` tokens.
>
> **What it computes:** for each channel (column) of K, it computes the mean value across all tokens and subtracts that mean from every token's value in that channel. The result is a matrix where each channel is zero-mean, eliminating the large constant bias that was causing the dynamic range problem. The full quantization of K then becomes `$\phi_K(K) = \psi_K(\gamma(K))$` — smooth first, then quantize.
>
> **Why this form:** the transform exploits the softmax invariance to an additive constant per query. The key observation is that the channel outliers in K are approximately constant across tokens, so the problematic signal is precisely the part that softmax would ignore anyway. By removing the common mode before quantization, the quantizer's limited dynamic range can be dedicated entirely to representing the token-to-token differences that actually matter for attention. Alternative approaches like per-channel quantization of K are mathematically impossible in the standard matmul interface; outlier-aware quantization schemes that use different scales for different channels would require custom matmul kernels that can apply channel-wise dequantization, which don't exist for the outer axis contraction pattern. The smoothing approach requires only a single vector mean computation per attention call — `$O(Nd)$` operations versus the `$O(N^2 d)$` cost of the matmuls — and can be fused with the quantization kernel to hide memory latency.

Table 18 in the appendix quantifies the impact dramatically. On real model data, per-block quantization of K **without** smoothing achieves only 30.60% cosine similarity to full-precision attention, and FlashAttention3's quantized version achieves only 26.76%. **With** smoothing, per-block quantization jumps to 99.31% cosine similarity. The paper reports in Table 10 that the speed overhead of smoothing K is "less than 0.2%" — the mean computation is negligible compared to the matmul it enables to run in INT8.

#### Quantization Choices for Q and K: Granularity and Data Type

Having solved the outlier problem with smoothing, the paper addresses the remaining quantization design choices for the first matmul: what granularity (per-token, per-block, or per-tensor) and what data type (INT8, E4M3 FP8, or E5M2 FP8)?

**Granularity constraint.** Per-channel quantization is not viable for either Q or K because the contraction dimension of the QK^T matmul is the channel dimension (the `$d$` axis). In the expression `$C = AB^T$`, quantization scales for A and B can only be applied to the outer axes — the axes that are not summed over. For `$A = Q$` with shape `$N \times d$` and `$B = K$` with shape `$N \times d$`, the matmul sums over the `$d$` dimension, so scale factors can only vary along the `$N$` (token) dimension. This means the allowable granularities are:

- **Per-tensor:** one scale for the entire `$N \times d$` matrix — coarsest, fastest to compute scales, but cannot adapt to token-to-token variation.
- **Per-token:** one scale per row (per token), `$\delta[i] \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1}$` — adapts to token-wise magnitude variation, moderate overhead.
- **Per-block:** one scale per contiguous block of `$b$` tokens, `$\delta[m:n] \in \mathbb{R}^{(N/b) \times 1}$` — a middle ground that adapts to local variation while keeping scale storage small, and aligns naturally with FlashAttention's tiling where Q blocks of size `$b_q$` are already loaded separately.

The paper sweeps per-token and per-block for Q and K and reports these in the four kernel variants (Table 6). Per-tensor is generally too coarse and causes accuracy degradation, as shown in Table 1 where per-tensor quantization of Q,K (even with smoothing) yields slightly worse metrics than per-token or per-block.

**Data type choice for Q and K: INT8 over FP8.** The paper presents two justifications for choosing INT8:

First, **throughput on target hardware.** On RTX 4090 and 3090 GPUs — the primary deployment targets — INT8 matrix multiplication via the `mma.sync.u8.u8.s32` Tensor Core instruction is 2× faster than FP8 (which uses `mma.sync.f32.f32.f32` with FP16 accumulation, since these GPUs lack native FP8 Tensor Core support) and 4× faster than FP16. Section 5.2 confirms this with measured TOPS: SageAttention's INT8 kernels achieve 340 TOPS on RTX 4090 versus FlashAttention2's 165 FP16 TOPS. Even if FP8 were equally accurate, INT8 would be faster on the deployed hardware.

Second, **accuracy for Q and K specifically.** Table 2 shows that when quantizing Q and K, INT8 achieves 99.70% average cosine similarity versus 99.94% for E4M3 and 99.81% for E5M2 (on a mix of Llama2-7B and Unidiffuser layers). The differences appear small, but Table 17 in Appendix B shows a more dramatic gap when measuring the Q·K product directly: INT8 achieves 99.54% cosine similarity, E4M3 achieves 92.83%, and E5M2 achieves only 77.95%. The paper's explanation (though not fully elaborated) is that Q and K values tend to have relatively uniform distributions within each token after smoothing, making uniform quantization with a single per-token scale effective. FP8 formats (E4M3 with 3 mantissa bits, E5M2 with 2 mantissa bits) trade precision for dynamic range — they can represent a wider range of magnitudes than INT8 can (via the exponent) but with fewer significant digits. For Q and K values that are already roughly normalized in magnitude (since they come after LayerNorm and the smoothing removes outliers), the extra dynamic range of FP8 is unnecessary, and the reduced precision hurts more. INT8 provides 256 uniformly spaced quantization levels (7.8 effective bits of precision), while E4M3 provides only 16 distinct positive values (most densely packed near zero), and E5M2 provides only 8 positive values.

The per-token versus per-block choice for Q and K defines two of the four kernel variants. Per-token quantization (`$\delta_Q[i], \delta_K[i]$` each computed over one row) provides finer-grained adaptation to token magnitudes but requires more scale storage and slightly more dequantization overhead. Per-block quantization (`$\delta_Q[i \cdot b_q : (i+1)\cdot b_q]$`) groups tokens into blocks that align with FlashAttention's tiling, making it natural to compute scales on the fly as each block is loaded into SRAM. The paper reports that both can work well; the adaptive selection mechanism chooses between them per layer.

---

#### Quantization Choices for `$\tilde{P}$` and V: The Sensitivity Problem

The second matrix multiplication in attention — `$\tilde{P}V$` where `$\tilde{P}$` is the unnormalized attention weight matrix (exponentiated scores from online softmax) and `$V$` is the value matrix — presents a different set of quantization challenges than the first matmul. The paper identifies a critical problem through empirical measurement: while INT8 quantization of P and V works acceptably on average (Table 2, average cosine similarity 99.70%), it can **catastrophically fail** on specific layers. Table 3 shows the *worst-case* cosine similarity across all layers of Llama2 and Unidiffuser: INT8 P and V achieves only 56.40% cosine similarity in the worst layer, compared to 99.99% for FP16.

**Granularity for `$\tilde{P}$`.** The paper proposes per-block quantization for `$\tilde{P}$` with an important optimization. `$\tilde{P}$` is the output of the online softmax: `$\tilde{P}^j_i = \exp(S^j_i - m^j_i)$` where `$S^j_i$` is the attention score block and `$m^j_i$` is the running maximum. By construction, the maximum value in each row of `$\tilde{P}$` is exactly 1 (corresponding to the maximum score after softmax). Because 1 is a known, fixed upper bound, the paper argues that a static scale factor `$s = 1/127$` can be used for per-block quantization of `$\tilde{P}$` without needing to compute the actual maximum of each block. This static scale is equivalent to per-token quantization (since the per-token max for a softmax row is always 1, and all blocks within that token share the same scale), but avoids the runtime overhead of computing per-block maxima. The quantized value is simply `$\hat{P} = \lceil 127 \cdot \tilde{P} \rfloor$`.

**Granularity for V.** The paper proposes per-channel quantization for V. This is the opposite of the constraint on Q and K: for the `$\tilde{P}V$` matmul, the contraction dimension is the token dimension of V (since `$\tilde{P}$` is `$b_q \times b_{kv}$` and `$V$` is `$b_{kv} \times d$`, the sum is over the `$b_{kv}$` token dimension). This means scale factors can vary along the channel dimension of V (`$d$`), which is exactly what per-channel quantization does: `$\delta_V[:, j] = \max(|V[:, j]|) / 127$` for each channel j. Unlike Q and K, where channel-wise outliers were the problem, V exhibits channel-wise outliers that per-channel quantization can directly address by assigning different scales to different channels. Since the contraction happens over tokens, and per-channel scales apply along the uncontracted output dimension, dequantization after the matmul is straightforward: each output element is scaled by the product of its `$\tilde{P}$` scale and its V channel's scale.

**But INT8 PV still fails on some layers.** Despite the granularity optimizations (static-scale per-block for P, per-channel for V), Table 3 shows the worst-case INT8 PV can drop to 56.40% cosine similarity. The paper does not fully diagnose *why* specific layers are more sensitive — it may relate to the distribution of V values or the interaction between quantization and the online softmax renormalization when `$\tilde{P}$` values are very small — but it does propose a solution: don't quantize P and V at all for these sensitive layers.

#### FP16 Accumulator: The Accuracy-Speed Sweet Spot for Sensitive Layers

For layers where INT8 quantization of the PV matmul produces unacceptable error, the paper proposes a solution that is both more accurate and faster than the naively obvious alternative (FP16 matmul with FP32 accumulation). The insight is hardware-specific: on Tensor Cores in RTX 3090/4090 GPUs, the `mma.sync.f16.f16.f16` instruction (FP16 inputs, FP16 accumulator) has 2× the throughput of the `mma.sync.f16.f16.f32` instruction (FP16 inputs, FP32 accumulator).

The accuracy concern is whether FP16 accumulation — which sums partial products in FP16 rather than FP32 — introduces unacceptable rounding error. Tables 4 and 5 provide the empirical answer: comparing FP16 accumulation versus FP32 accumulation for the PV matmul across all layers of Llama2-7B and Unidiffuser, the average cosine similarity is identical (99.98%), the average relative L1 error is identical (0.0156), and the average RMSE is identical (2.94e-3). Even the worst-case metrics are identical between the two accumulators: 99.84% cosine similarity and 0.0511 relative L1 for both.

The explanation is that the PV matmul in attention accumulates at most `$b_{kv}$` terms per output element (where `$b_{kv}$` is the block size, typically 64), and each term is a product of two FP16 values with magnitudes between 0 and 1 (for P) and the typical range of V (order of 1–10). Summing 64 such terms in FP16, which has approximately 11 bits of mantissa (3.3 decimal digits), is sufficient to represent the sum without overflow or underflow, since the sum will be at most ~640 (representable in FP16's 11-bit mantissa with full precision). In contrast, FP32 accumulation (23 mantissa bits) provides far more precision than needed, and switching to it halves the throughput for no accuracy gain.

**The economic logic.** By keeping P and V in FP16 (no quantization to INT8) but using the faster FP16 accumulator instruction, the PV matmul achieves 2× the speed of the standard FP16-with-FP32-accumulator approach and matches the accuracy of full FP32 accumulation exactly, while being far more accurate than INT8 quantization for sensitive layers. This creates a two-tier strategy: use INT8 PV for layers that can tolerate it (because it's the absolute fastest, being 4× FP16 throughput), and use FP16-with-FP16-accumulator PV for layers that cannot.

#### The Four Kernel Variants and Adaptive Quantization

The paper combines the two independent design choices — (1) per-token versus per-block quantization for Q and K, and (2) INT8 versus FP16-with-FP16-accumulator for PV — to create four kernel implementations, summarized in Table 6:

- **SAGEAttn-T** (per-**T**oken Q,K; FP16 PV with FP16 accumulator): fastest QK quantization granularity (one scale per token), and safest PV strategy (FP16). This is the default high-accuracy kernel.

- **SAGEAttn-B** (per-**B**lock Q,K; FP16 PV with FP16 accumulator): coarser QK quantization (one scale per block of `$b_q$` tokens), which reduces scale storage and dequantization overhead at the cost of slightly more quantization error in QK. Still uses safe FP16 PV. The paper presents Algorithm 1 as the detailed pseudocode for this kernel, showing how the per-block quantization integrates with the FlashAttention tiling loop.

- **SAGEAttn-vT** (per-Token Q,K; INT8 PV): uses INT8 quantization for the PV matmul (per-block static-scale for P, per-channel for V) to get the maximum speedup from both matmuls. Only safe for layers where INT8 PV error is low.

- **SAGEAttn-vB** (per-Block Q,K; INT8 PV): per-block QK with INT8 PV — the fastest variant overall, but also the riskiest for accuracy.

The "v" in vT and vB stands for "value quantized" — indicating that V (and P) are quantized to INT8 rather than kept in FP16.

**Adaptive selection mechanism (Section 4.5).** Rather than choosing one kernel for the entire model, SageAttention selects the fastest kernel that maintains accuracy on a per-layer basis. The procedure works as follows, done once before deployment:

1. For each attention layer in the model, run SAGEAttn-vB (the fastest, most aggressive kernel) on a set of sample inputs (the paper does not specify exactly how many samples, but implies it is a small calibration set).

2. Measure the cosine similarity between SAGEAttn-vB's output and full-precision attention's output for that layer. Cosine similarity is used as a cheap, scale-invariant accuracy metric that correlates well with end-to-end model performance.

3. If the cosine similarity exceeds 99.8% (the worst-case cosine similarity observed for SAGEAttn-B, the safe baseline), then SAGEAttn-vB is selected for that layer — the layer is robust to aggressive quantization and gets the maximum speedup.

4. If SAGEAttn-vB's cosine similarity is below 99.8%, fall back to SAGEAttn-B — which uses FP16 PV and is guaranteed to be accurate (as demonstrated by the end-to-end metrics in Table 8).

The paper reports in Table 11 that this adaptive strategy achieves 11.7% higher attention throughput on CogvideoX compared to using only SAGEAttn-T (the safest kernel), with no loss in end-to-end metrics. The key insight is that not all layers are equally sensitive — some layers have well-behaved V matrices that quantize cleanly to INT8, and recovering the extra speedup from those layers adds up to a meaningful overall improvement.

**Why cosine similarity 99.8%?** The threshold is chosen empirically as the worst-case cosine similarity of SAGEAttn-B, which has been validated to produce negligible end-to-end metric degradation across all tested models (Table 8). Any kernel with cosine similarity above this threshold is considered "at least as accurate as the known-safe kernel." The paper does not explore whether a different threshold would be optimal or whether the optimal threshold varies by model, which is a limitation.

**The SAGEAttn-B algorithm in detail (Algorithm 1).** The pseudocode in the paper provides the full implementation logic for the per-block quantized kernel (the one used as the default safe choice and as the fallback in adaptive selection). The algorithm follows the standard FlashAttention-2 outer-loop-over-Q, inner-loop-over-KV structure but with quantization inserted at specific points:

1. **Preprocessing:** K is smoothed `$K = K - \text{mean}(K)$` before any tiling. This is a global reduction over all tokens that must complete before the tiled computation begins. The paper does not specify whether this is done as a separate kernel or fused — Table 10 shows it has <0.2% overhead, suggesting it's a lightweight pre-computation.

2. **Q quantization:** As the outer loop iterates over Q blocks `$\hat{Q}_i$`, each block is loaded, divided by `$\sqrt{d}$` (fused on-chip), and quantized to INT8 with its scale `$\delta_Q[i]$`. The quantized block `$\hat{Q}_i$` and its scale are stored in on-chip SRAM.

3. **Inner loop:** For each K,V block `$j$`, the K block `$\hat{K}_j$` and its scale `$\delta_K[j]$` are loaded. The INT8 matmul `$\hat{Q}_i \hat{K}_j^T$` is computed on Tensor Cores, and the result is dequantized by multiplying with `$\delta_Q[i] \cdot \delta_K[j]$` to produce the FP32 score block `$S^j_i$`.

4. **Online softmax:** The standard online softmax updates are computed in FP32: `$m^j_i = \max(m^{j-1}_i, \text{rowmax}(S^j_i))$`, `$\tilde{P}^j_i = \exp(S^j_i - m^j_i)$`, and the running sum `$l^j_i$` is updated. The output accumulation uses `$O^j_i = \text{diag}(\exp(m^{j-1}_i - m^j_i)) O^{j-1}_i + \tilde{P}^j_i V_j$` where the PV matmul is performed in FP16 with FP16 accumulation (since this is SAGEAttn-B).

5. **Final normalization:** After all K,V blocks are processed, the output block is normalized `$O_i = \text{diag}(l^{T_n}_i)^{-1} O^{T_n}_i$` (dividing each row by its sum of attention weights) and written to global memory in FP16.

The outer loop over Q blocks is parallelized across Streaming Multiprocessors (SMs) — each SM processes one Q block independently, which is the same parallelism strategy as FlashAttention2. The inner loop is sequential within an SM, processing K,V blocks one at a time to maintain the online softmax state.

#### Fusion Tricks and Hardware-Level Performance Rationale

The paper identifies that quantization overhead — the cost of computing scale factors and performing the element-wise rounding and scaling operations — is the primary efficiency risk. If quantization costs consumed a significant fraction of the matmul speedup, the net benefit would be small. The paper addresses this with several fusion strategies and a hardware-level analysis of why INT8 attention is faster despite the overhead:

**Fusion 1: Quantization fused with ROPE.** Rotary Position Embedding (ROPE) is a common operation that precedes attention in many transformer architectures (Llama2, CogvideoX). ROPE applies a rotation to Q and K based on token position. Instead of writing the ROPE output in FP16 to global memory and then reading it back for quantization, the SageAttention implementation performs quantization **inside the ROPE kernel**: after the rotation is computed and before the result is written to global memory, the scale factor is computed and the values are quantized to INT8. The `(scale, INT8 tensor)` pair is then written to global memory, replacing the original FP16 tensor. This eliminates the global memory read for quantization entirely — the data is already in registers or shared memory from the ROPE computation, and quantization adds only ALU operations (max reduction for scale, multiply and round for the quantized values).

**Fusion 2: `$1/\sqrt{d}$` scaling fused with Q quantization.** The standard attention formula includes dividing Q by `$\sqrt{d}$` before the QK^T matmul. Rather than doing this as a separate element-wise kernel, SageAttention multiplies Q by `$1/\sqrt{d}$` on-chip immediately before quantizing. This avoids a separate global memory read/write of Q and combines two element-wise operations into one pass over the data.

**Hardware rationale (Section 4.6, itemized):**

1. **Matmul acceleration.** The INT8 Tensor Core instructions `mma.sync.u8.u8.s32` on RTX 4090/3090 have 4× the throughput of FP16 instructions `mma.sync.f16.f16.f32`. Even though SageAttention only quantizes the matmul inputs (not the accumulator — softmax is FP32), replacing one matmul with INT8 provides 4× speedup on that operation. For the PV matmul when using FP16 accumulator, the `mma.sync.f16.f16.f16` instruction provides 2× throughput over the default `mma.sync.f16.f16.f32`. Since the two matmuls account for the vast majority of attention computation (O(N²) each), accelerating them produces a near-linear speedup on the overall kernel.

2. **Quantization overhead.** The paper argues that while "quantization and dequantization are considered the main overhead in current quantization methods" (citing Lin et al., 2024), fusion largely eliminates the memory I/O component. The computational component (computing max, multiplying by scale, rounding) is O(Nd) per matrix, compared to O(N² d) for the matmuls — asymptotically negligible at large sequence lengths.

3. **Cache and register pressure.** Using 8-bit data reduces the on-chip storage required for Q, K, P, V tiles. A tile of 64×64 elements occupies 4KB in INT8 versus 8KB in FP16. This allows larger tile sizes (more reuse) or enables more concurrent warps per SM (better latency hiding). The paper specifies that SAGEAttn-B uses `$b_q = 128$` and `$b_{kv} = 64$` (Appendix A, Table 12), which are the same block sizes as FlashAttention2 — but the INT8 tiles use half the shared memory, freeing capacity. Using FP16 accumulator instead of FP32 also reduces register pressure by 50% for the accumulation registers, which can increase occupancy.

4. **DRAM access.** Reading Q, K, and V tiles from global memory to on-chip SRAM is a significant cost in attention kernels (FlashAttention's key contribution was reducing this by tiling and recomputing softmax). With INT8 quantization, Q and K tiles are half the size in global memory — a 64×64 tile of K is 4KB in INT8 versus 8KB in FP16, halving the memory bandwidth consumed by tensor loads. The scale factors are small (one FP32 scalar per block or token, e.g., 4 bytes per 128 tokens for per-block, versus the tensor data itself at 64×64×1 = 4KB per tile). The paper notes that "these scales can be considered negligible compared to the tensors."

**Performance ceiling.** The paper reports that SageAttention achieves 340 TOPS on RTX 4090 at headdim=64, which is 52% of the theoretical INT8 throughput (660 TOPS for RTX 4090). The remaining gap to peak is attributed to: (a) the softmax computation which runs in FP32 and is not accelerated by INT8 instructions (approximately 5-10% of total operations), (b) quantization/dequantization overhead despite fusion (ALU bound), (c) memory latency for loading V tiles (which are still FP16 in SAGEAttn-B) and storing output tiles, and (d) the online softmax renormalization which requires synchronizing partial results.

**Hyperparameters from Appendix A.** The paper provides specific kernel configuration parameters: for headdim=64 with causal mask, the kernel uses 4 warps and 3 pipeline stages; for headdim=64 without causal mask, 4 warps and 3 stages; for headdim=128 with causal mask, 8 warps and 5 stages; for headdim=128 without causal mask, 8 warps and 3 stages. Warps determine the number of parallel thread groups per thread block (affecting occupancy), and pipeline stages determine how many tiles are prefetched asynchronously (affecting latency hiding). The implementation uses OpenAI Triton (Tillet et al., 2019) rather than hand-written CUDA, which means the kernel is portable across GPU architectures and can be integrated into PyTorch models without custom CUDA compilation toolchains.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: K-Smoothing as a Softmax-Invariant Preconditioner — Not Just Another Outlier Suppression Trick

The field has known for years that activation outliers degrade quantization accuracy. SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023a) addressed this for linear layers by migrating quantization difficulty from activations to weights via a mathematically equivalent scaling transform. Prior attention quantization attempts like I-BERT (Kim et al., 2021) relied on quantization-aware training to teach the model to produce quantization-friendly activations. The dominant assumption was that attention outliers, like linear layer outliers, require either retraining or some form of numerical scaling that redistributes dynamic range.

SageAttention's K-smoothing makes a fundamentally different conceptual move. The paper's diagnostic observation — visible in Figure 4 — is that K's channel outliers are not large *variations* across tokens but large *biases* shared across all tokens. This distinction matters enormously because a shared bias, unlike a variance outlier, is mathematically invisible to the softmax. The smoothing transform `$K - \text{mean}(K)$` is not a scaling trick that approximates the original computation with some error — it is an **exact equivalence** that exploits the softmax's translation invariance. This means the smoothing introduces zero numerical error in full precision while eliminating the dynamic range problem that prevents INT8 quantization.

This reframes the quantization problem from "how do we suppress outliers while minimizing error?" to "which components of the signal does softmax actually use, and can we remove the rest before quantization?" It is a diagnostic contribution as much as a technical one: the paper identifies *why* K outliers exist (they represent a positional or contextual bias shared across the sequence) and *why* they are safe to remove (softmax discards additive constants). This explains the catastrophic failure of naive INT8 attention in a way that prior work — which treated all outliers as equally problematic — could not.

The evidence for this innovation's significance is stark. Table 18 shows that per-block quantization without smoothing achieves 30.60% cosine similarity to full-precision attention, while with smoothing it achieves 99.31%. FlashAttention3's FP8 version — which lacks this insight and uses native FP8 quantization without smoothing — achieves 26.76% on the same test. A conceptual advance (understanding the bias-versus-variance nature of K outliers) enables two orders of magnitude improvement in accuracy with negligible overhead (<0.2%, Table 10), while the hardware-exclusive FP8 approach on Hopper GPUs performs far worse.

This is a fundamental rather than incremental contribution: it changes *what problem* one thinks they're solving when quantizing attention — from dynamic range compression to signal decomposition.

### Innovation 2: The FP16 Accumulator as a Deliberate Accuracy-Speed Tradeoff That Rejects the Default FP32 Accumulation Dogma

Nearly all high-performance GPU kernels for matrix multiplication accumulate partial sums in FP32, even when the inputs are FP16. This is standard practice codified in NVIDIA's own libraries (cuBLAS, CUTLASS) and inherited by virtually all ML frameworks. The rationale is arithmetic: summing many FP16 products in FP16 risks overflow (if the sum exceeds 65,504) and gradual underflow (loss of precision as the running sum grows). The default assumption is that FP16 accumulation is a dangerous optimization that sacrifices numerical stability for speed, and should only be used when the problem structure guarantees small sums.

SageAttention's FP16 accumulator for the PV matmul challenges this assumption with empirical evidence specific to attention's numerical properties. Tables 4 and 5 show that across all layers of Llama2-7B and Unidiffuser, FP16 accumulation and FP32 accumulation produce **identical** cosine similarity (99.98% average, 99.84% worst), identical relative L1, and identical RMSE. There is literally no measurable accuracy difference — the FP32 accumulator provides zero benefit for this specific computation.

The insight is that the PV matmul in attention has bounded accumulation depth determined by the KV block size (64 in the paper's default configuration). The attention weights in `$\tilde{P}$` sum to at most 1 per row, and V values are well-behaved (order of 1–10). Summing 64 products of numbers in [0,1] and [~1, ~10] cannot overflow FP16's representable range (±65,504) and stays well within the precision of FP16's 11-bit mantissa. The structural property of attention — that the softmax normalizes weights to sum to 1 — removes the unbounded accumulation risk that FP32 protects against in general matmuls.

The significance is not the 2× speedup itself (though that's substantial), but the demonstration that **attention's specific numerical structure invalidates a universal best practice**. The paper provides an existence proof that FP16 accumulation can be losslessly substituted for FP32 accumulation in attention's second matmul, which opens the door for other attention kernels (including future versions of FlashAttention) to adopt this optimization without accuracy concerns. This is an incremental but broadly applicable contribution: it changes the design space for attention kernel implementations by showing that a previously rejected option is actually Pareto-optimal (strictly faster, identically accurate) for this specific workload.

Combined with INT8 quantization for the first matmul (QK^T), this mixed-precision strategy means SageAttention achieves most of the speedup of full INT8 attention while avoiding the catastrophic failures documented in Table 3 for sensitive layers. The FP16 accumulator acts as a safety net — when INT8 PV fails, the fallback is not the slow default (FP16-with-FP32-accumulator) but a 2× faster alternative that is still provably lossless.

### Innovation 3: Per-Layer Adaptive Quantization as a Practical Deployment Principle, Not Just a Model-Wide Recipe

The dominant paradigm in post-training quantization is **model-wide uniformity**: choose one quantization scheme (e.g., W8A8, W4A16) and apply it identically to every layer. This is true of SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023a), AWQ (Lin et al., 2024), I-BERT (Kim et al., 2021), and most quantization toolkits. The assumption is that what works for one layer works for all — or if it doesn't, the model needs retraining to harmonize layer sensitivities.

SageAttention's adaptive quantization mechanism makes a different argument: **layer sensitivity to quantization is an empirical property that varies substantially within a model, and a cheap pre-deployment profiling step can recover significant speedup by matching kernel aggressiveness to per-layer tolerance.** The paper constructs four kernels spanning a speed-accuracy Pareto frontier (SAGEAttn-vB: fastest/riskiest → SAGEAttn-B: safe baseline) and uses cosine similarity on calibration data to assign each layer the fastest kernel that meets a 99.8% similarity threshold.

What makes this distinctive is not the complexity of the method — a single threshold on a single metric — but the **empirical philosophy** it embodies. The paper observes in Tables 2–3 that attention quantization has high variance across layers: average INT8 PV cosine similarity is 99.70%, but the worst layer drops to 56.40%. A model-wide choice must either use INT8 PV (accepting catastrophic failure on sensitive layers) or use FP16 PV (leaving speedup on the table for the majority of robust layers). The adaptive approach captures the best of both worlds: 11.7% higher throughput than the safe-only baseline with zero metric degradation (Table 11).

This is an incremental but practically significant contribution because it establishes a deployment pattern that generalizes beyond SageAttention's specific kernels. Any future attention quantization method with multiple accuracy-speed operating points could adopt the same profiling-and-threshold approach. The key design choices — using cosine similarity as the accuracy proxy (cheap to compute, correlates with end-to-end metrics) and thresholding at the known-safe kernel's worst-case performance (guaranteeing no regression) — provide a template that requires no model-specific hyperparameter tuning.

The innovation is not the idea of per-layer heterogeneity (mixed-precision quantization has explored this for linear layers; see HAWQ, ZeroQ), but the **validation that per-layer adaptation works for attention with zero calibration cost beyond a single profiling pass** — and that the speedup is meaningful (12%) rather than marginal. This challenges the model-wide uniformity assumption for attention specifically and provides a principled decision procedure rather than a heuristic.

### Innovation 4: Plug-and-Play as a Design Requirement, Not an Afterthought — and the Evidence That It Works Across Modalities

Many quantization papers claim to be "plug-and-play" or "training-free," but these claims are typically validated on a narrow slice of models — often a single LLM family (Llama, OPT) or a single vision architecture (ViT, ResNet). When prior work extends quantization to new model types, it frequently requires recalibration, architecture-specific tuning, or retraining to recover accuracy. I-BERT (Kim et al., 2021), the closest prior work to quantizing full attention, was restricted to RoBERTa and required quantization-aware training.

SageAttention's claim to plug-and-play generality is backed by a deliberately diverse evaluation spanning: a language model (Llama2-7B) evaluated on perplexity, reading comprehension, and knowledge benchmarks; two text-to-image diffusion models (Unidiffuser, UltraPixel) evaluated on FID, CLIPScore, and ImageReward; a text-to-video diffusion model (CogvideoX) evaluated on five video quality metrics; a vision transformer (TIMM) evaluated on ImageNet and two robustness benchmarks; and a vision-language model (Llava1.6) evaluated on visual QA. Across all of these — architectures, modalities, tasks, metrics — **the same SageAttention kernel, with zero model-specific calibration, matches full-precision performance within 0.2% average degradation** (Table 8).

The intellectual contribution here is not any single technical mechanism but the **existence proof that a unified attention quantization recipe can work across dramatically different activation distributions.** Text-to-video models like CogvideoX have fundamentally different attention patterns (3D spatiotemporal attention) than autoregressive language models (causal self-attention), which differ from text-to-image cross-attention. The fact that K-smoothing, FP16 PV accumulation, and adaptive kernel selection — designed based on analysis of K outliers and PV sensitivity in Llama2 and Unidiffuser — transfer without modification to CogvideoX, UltraPixel, and TIMM suggests the paper identified **universal numerical properties of attention**, not model-specific quirks.

This cross-modal validation is what elevates SageAttention from a useful engineering trick to a **robust empirical finding**: attention computation has structural properties (K bias outliers, bounded PV accumulation depth, layer-varying PV sensitivity) that are invariant to the model's task, architecture, and training distribution. Prior work that studied quantization on a single model family could not make this claim — the failure modes could have been artifacts of that model's specific training. By showing the same techniques work across five diverse models spanning three modalities, the paper provides evidence that the approach targets inherent numerical properties of the attention operation itself, not model-specific behavior.

The significance is practical and conceptual. Practically, it means practitioners can adopt SageAttention as a drop-in replacement for FlashAttention2 or xformers without the typical quantization workflow of calibration, accuracy validation, and per-model tuning. Conceptually, it establishes attention quantization as a **solved problem for INT8** — the paper's techniques appear to be sufficient conditions for accurate INT8 attention across a wide range of transformer architectures and tasks.

### Innovation 5: A Hardware-Aware INT8-Over-FP8 Argument Grounded in Measured, Not Theoretical, Accuracy

The ML systems community has been migrating toward FP8 as the default low-precision format for both training (Peng et al., 2023; Xi et al., 2024b) and inference (FlashAttention3's FP8 attention). The theoretical advantage of FP8 over INT8 is dynamic range: the exponent allows representing values across many orders of magnitude without clipping, while INT8's uniform quantization requires the entire tensor to fit within a single scale factor's range. When activations have heavy-tailed distributions, FP8's dynamic range advantage should translate to better accuracy.

SageAttention makes a counter-argument specific to attention: **for Q and K tensors, INT8 empirically achieves higher accuracy than FP8**, and for P and V, neither INT8 nor FP8 is consistently accurate enough — requiring the FP16 accumulator as a fallback. The evidence is in Table 2 (INT8 Q,K: 99.70% cosine similarity vs. 99.94% for E4M3 and 99.81% for E5M2) and especially Table 17 (INT8 QK product: 99.54% vs. 92.83% for E4M3 and 77.95% for E5M2).

The insight is that **attention tensors, after proper preconditioning (K-smoothing, LayerNorm normalization on Q), are well-approximated by uniform distributions within each token**, making INT8's uniform quantization levels a better match than FP8's logarithmic spacing. FP8 trades precision for dynamic range, but after smoothing, attention tensors don't need the dynamic range — the outliers have been eliminated, and the remaining values all fall within a modest, quantizeable range. In this regime, INT8's superior precision (256 uniformly spaced levels vs. 16 for E4M3 or 8 for E5M2) directly translates to lower quantization error.

This is a **hardware-informed theoretical argument**, not just an empirical preference. On Hopper GPUs where FP8 is 2× FP16 throughput and INT8 is 2× FP16 throughput, the formats are speed-equivalent, and the accuracy argument would make INT8 the better choice for attention even on the latest hardware. On pre-Hopper GPUs where INT8 is 2× the throughput of FP8 (since FP8 must be emulated or run at FP16 speeds), the advantage compounds. The paper's contribution is demonstrating that the migration to FP8 — driven by its advantages for training and linear layer quantization — should not be assumed to extend to attention, where INT8 has a structural accuracy advantage that the FP8 literature has not addressed.

This finding has implications for hardware design and framework development. If attention kernels consistently perform better in INT8 than FP8, GPU architectures should continue to invest in INT8 throughput rather than deprecating it in favor of FP8-only Tensor Cores. Framework developers implementing quantized attention should default to INT8 for Q,K rather than following the FP8 trend. The paper provides the empirical justification for diverging from the FP8 consensus in the specific context of attention computation.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Datasets.** The paper evaluates on a diverse set of benchmarks spanning language, image, video, and multimodal tasks. For **Llama2** (text-to-text): WikiText (perplexity, measuring prediction confidence), LAMBADA (accuracy, measuring contextual understanding), and MMLU (accuracy, measuring knowledge across 57 subjects). For **CogvideoX** (text-to-video): the Open-Sora prompt set, evaluated on five metrics — CLIPSIM, CLIP-Temp (CLIP-T) for text-video alignment, VQA-a and VQA-t for aesthetic/technical quality, and Flow-score (FScore) for temporal consistency. For **UltraPixel** and **Unidiffuser** (text-to-image): the first 256 annotations from the COCO 2014 validation set as prompts, with the corresponding 256 images as ground truth for FID and sFID computation. For **TIMM** (image classification): ImageNet, ImageNet-Sketch, and ImageNet-Rendition, all evaluated with accuracy. For **Llava1.6** (visual question answering): TextVQA, POPE, and VQAv2, all evaluated with accuracy. All datasets are standard public benchmarks; the COCO subset of 256 images is a practical choice given the computational cost of image generation, though it represents a smaller evaluation set than typical for these metrics.

- **Base models.** Five models spanning three modalities and multiple architectures are used. **Llama2-7B** (Touvron et al., 2023) represents large language models with causal self-attention and head dimension 128. **CogvideoX** (Yang et al., 2024) represents text-to-video diffusion with 3D spatiotemporal attention, sequence lengths of 17,776 tokens. **Unidiffuser** (Bao et al., 2023) and **UltraPixel** (Ren et al., 2024) represent text-to-image diffusion with cross-attention and self-attention, the latter at ultra-high resolution (2560×1536). **TIMM** (Wightman, 2019) — specifically `vit_base_patch16_224.augreg2_in21k_ft_in1k` — represents vision transformers for classification. **Llava1.6** (Liu et al., 2024a) represents vision-language models combining a vision encoder with an LLM. This diversity is deliberate: the paper aims to demonstrate that SageAttention works across dramatically different attention patterns (causal, bidirectional, 3D spatiotemporal, cross-attention), activation distributions, and modalities without model-specific calibration. The models range in scale from vision transformers (~86M parameters) to a 7B-parameter language model combined with a vision encoder.

- **Metrics.** For **Llama2**: perplexity (lower is better, measuring the model's uncertainty in next-token prediction on WikiText), and accuracy (fraction correct on LAMBADA and MMLU). For **CogvideoX**: CLIPSIM and CLIP-Temp (higher is better, measuring alignment between video frames and text prompt), VQA-a and VQA-t (higher is better, assessing aesthetic and technical video quality using learned evaluators), and Flow-score (higher is better, measuring temporal consistency via optical flow). For **Unidiffuser** and **UltraPixel**: FID and sFID (lower is better, measuring distributional distance between generated and real images in Inception feature space), CLIPScore (higher is better, measuring text-image semantic alignment), and ImageReward (higher is better, measuring human preference alignment). For **TIMM** and **Llava1.6**: standard classification/question-answering accuracy (fraction correct). All metrics are computed identically between full-precision and SageAttention runs to isolate the effect of attention quantization, with no other changes to model weights, sampling parameters, or evaluation procedures.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares SageAttention against three established attention implementations. **FlashAttention2** (Dao, 2023) is the state-of-the-art open-source exact attention kernel operating in FP16, using tiling and online softmax to minimize global memory I/O — this is the primary speed and accuracy baseline. **xformers** (Lefaudeux et al., 2022) is a modular transformer library with optimized CUDA attention kernels, also in FP16. **Torch attention** (PyTorch's native `scaled_dot_product_attention`) serves as a reference for the TIMM experiments where the other baselines are not applicable. For accuracy comparisons, an additional baseline is **FlashAttention3's FP8 quantized version** (Shah et al., 2024), which represents the only prior work attempting low-precision attention on modern hardware, though it is exclusive to Hopper GPUs (H100) and the paper evaluates its accuracy only, not speed, since it cannot run on the RTX 4090/3090 test hardware. Untruncated naive baselines include **per-token INT8 quantization without K-smoothing** (direct INT8 cast of Q,K,V), **per-block INT8 without K-smoothing**, and **per-tensor INT8**, reported in Table 1 to demonstrate the necessity of SageAttention's techniques.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Speed is measured in **TOPS** (Tera Operations Per Second) for the attention kernel itself, computed as the total number of floating-point/integer operations in the attention computation divided by kernel execution time. This is the standard metric for comparing compute-bound GPU kernels independently of model architecture. For end-to-end speedup, the paper reports **real inference throughput** of the attention component in models: the measured execution time of all attention layers in FP16 (using FlashAttention2, xformers, or Torch attention) versus SageAttention, running the full model forward pass on an RTX 4090 or 3090. Speedup factors are computed as the ratio of these measured wall-clock times. All speed experiments use the same input shapes (batch size, sequence length, head dimension) and hardware (single GPU) for fair comparison. The paper does not report end-to-end model throughput including all operations (linear layers, normalization, etc.) because the speedup from quantizing attention only affects the attention component — the reported speedup is for the attention operation itself, not the full model. This is clearly stated: the speedup numbers (2.1× over FlashAttention2, 2.7× over xformers) refer to attention kernel throughput, while the model-specific speedups in Table 7 (e.g., 2.01× for CogvideoX) are the effective acceleration of the attention component within the full model pipeline.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** None reported. The paper does not describe any train/test splits for the calibration data used in adaptive quantization selection, nor does it report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests for end-to-end metrics. The adaptive quantization selection uses "various inputs" (Section 4.5) to measure cosine similarity per layer but does not specify the number of samples, their source (training vs. test distribution), or whether the selection might overfit to the calibration data. For image generation models, metrics are computed on 256 prompts from COCO validation — this is a fixed set with no cross-validation. For Llama2, WikiText is a standard test set, LAMBADA and MMLU are zero-shot evaluations using their standard test sets. The absence of variance estimates (standard deviation across runs, confidence intervals for FID/perplexity differences) means the reported 0.2% average metric degradation cannot be assessed for statistical reliability — FID on 256 images has known sampling variance, and small perplexity differences on WikiText may or may not be statistically significant. This is a methodological weakness, though one shared by many inference acceleration papers that prioritize demonstrating metric preservation over statistical rigor.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Attention Kernel Throughput (Speed Benchmarks)

The paper's primary speed result is that **SageAttention achieves approximately 2.1× higher throughput than FlashAttention2 and 2.7× higher than xformers** on RTX 4090, averaged across sequence lengths from 1K to 32K and both causal and non-causal masking at head dimensions 64 and 128.

**Figures 6–9** provide the raw data as TOPS versus sequence length curves across four hardware configurations (RTX 4090 headdim=64, RTX 4090 headdim=128, RTX 3090 headdim=64, RTX 3090 headdim=128), each split into causal and non-causal variants. The key patterns:

- **At headdim=64, non-causal, on RTX 4090 (Figure 6, left):** FlashAttention2 achieves 143–167 TOPS across sequence lengths 1K–32K. SageAttention's slowest variant (SAGEAttn-T) achieves 237–276 TOPS, a 1.5–1.7× speedup. The fastest variant (SAGEAttn-vB) achieves 270–341 TOPS, reaching 341 TOPS at 32K — this is 2.0× FlashAttention2's 167 TOPS. xformers achieves 116–128 TOPS, making SageAttention 2.1–2.7× faster. Torch attention (the unoptimized baseline) achieves only 22–28 TOPS at short sequences and runs out of memory (OOM) beyond 4K.

- **At headdim=64, causal, on RTX 4090 (Figure 6, right):** The pattern is similar but absolute TOPS are lower due to the triangular mask overhead. FlashAttention2 achieves 124–164 TOPS; SAGEAttn-vB achieves 177–335 TOPS (2.0× at 32K). The gap between SAGEAttn-B (FP16 PV) and SAGEAttn-vB (INT8 PV) is visible: at 32K causal, SAGEAttn-B achieves 325 TOPS versus SAGEAttn-vB's 335 TOPS — the INT8 PV variant provides roughly 3% additional speedup, which is small because the PV matmul is memory-bandwidth-bound at these dimensions.

- **At headdim=128 (Figures 7):** Absolute TOPS increase across all methods because the matmul dimensions are larger, better utilizing Tensor Cores. At headdim=128, non-causal, 32K sequence length on RTX 4090, SAGEAttn-vB achieves 341 TOPS — identical to the headdim=64 case, suggesting the kernel is compute-bound rather than memory-bound at these dimensions. FlashAttention2 achieves 164–167 TOPS. The 2.0× ratio is consistent.

- **On RTX 3090 (Figures 8–9):** The speedup pattern is maintained but absolute TOPS are lower. At headdim=64, non-causal, 32K, SAGEAttn-vB achieves 139 TOPS versus FlashAttention2's 69 TOPS — still roughly 2.0×. xformers achieves 54 TOPS, for a 2.6× speedup. The consistent ratio across hardware generations (3090 and 4090) suggests the speedup derives from the fundamental INT8-versus-FP16 throughput advantage rather than architecture-specific optimizations.

- **Sequence length scaling:** All SageAttention variants show increasing TOPS with sequence length up to 4K–8K, then plateau. FlashAttention2 shows a similar pattern but at lower absolute TOPS. The plateau at 8K+ sequences indicates the kernels become compute-bound: the matmul dominates runtime, and the quantization/dequantization overhead (which scales with total elements, O(Nd)) becomes negligible relative to the O(N² d) matmul work. This is important because it means the speedup advantage is sustained — not diminished — at the long sequence lengths where attention is the primary bottleneck.

**The peak throughput of 341 TOPS on RTX 4090 (52% of theoretical 660 INT8 TOPS) is notable.** The paper compares this to FlashAttention3's reported 490 TOPS on H100 (Hopper architecture), noting that SageAttention on a consumer GPU "is even close to" a hardware-exclusive optimization on a much more expensive datacenter GPU. This is not a direct comparison (different hardware, different precision) but demonstrates the practical significance: practitioners without Hopper GPUs can achieve datacenter-class attention throughput using SageAttention on consumer hardware.

**Real model speedups (Table 7).** Moving from kernel-level TOPS to actual model inference, Table 7 reports the measured attention speedup when replacing the original attention implementation with SageAttention in five models on RTX 4090:

| Model | Attention Shape | Original Attention (TOPS) | SageAttention (TOPS) | Speedup |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CogvideoX | (2, 30, 17776, 64) | 163.37 (FlashAttn2) | 327.57 | 2.01× |
| Llama2 | (4, 32, 1536, 128) | 130.99 (FlashAttn2) | 231.74 | 1.77× |
| UltraPixel | (2, 32, 7285, 64) | 152.03 (FlashAttn2) | 325.18 | 2.14× |
| Unidiffuser | (4, 24, 1105, 64) | 105.68 (xformers) | 246.93 | 2.34× |
| TIMM | (12, 64, 197, 64) | 18.91 (Torch) | 111.41 | 5.89× |

The variation in speedup (1.77× to 5.89×) reflects differences in the original attention implementation's efficiency. Llama2's 1.77× is the lowest, partly because its attention shapes (1536 tokens, headdim=128) are well-optimized in FlashAttention2 and the FP16 accumulator PV matmul has less relative advantage at larger head dimensions. TIMM's 5.89× is the highest because the baseline is unoptimized Torch attention, not FlashAttention2 — this is not a fair comparison for SageAttention versus state-of-the-art, but rather reflects the improvement over naive attention. On models using FlashAttention2 as the baseline, the speedup ranges from 1.77× to 2.14×, with an average of approximately 2.0×. **The paper's claim of "2.1×" refers to the kernel-level average across configurations, not the model-level average, which is slightly lower at approximately 1.97× for FlashAttention2-based models.**

**Appendix B, Table 19** reports similar measurements on RTX 3090, with speedups ranging from 1.81× (CogvideoX) to 2.29× (Unidiffuser) for FlashAttention2/xformers baselines, averaging approximately 2.0×. The consistency across GPU generations is evidence that the speedup is algorithmic (INT8 vs. FP16 throughput ratio) rather than dependent on RTX 4090-specific features.

**Speedups for the "v" (INT8 PV) variants versus the "B" (FP16 PV) baseline.** Figure 6 provides the raw data to compute this gap: at headdim=64, non-causal, 32K on RTX 4090, SAGEAttn-B achieves 323 TOPS and SAGEAttn-vB achieves 341 TOPS — a 5.6% improvement. At headdim=128, the gap is essentially zero (339 vs. 341 TOPS). This indicates that the INT8 PV optimization provides at most ~5% additional speedup beyond what FP16 PV with FP16 accumulator already achieves, and only at small head dimensions where the PV matmul is less compute-bound. This small margin explains why the adaptive selection in Table 11 recovers 12% speedup — not from the individual kernels being faster, but from selecting per-token quantization (SAGEAttn-T) over per-block (SAGEAttn-B) where possible, which is a separate dimension of variation.

#### End-to-End Model Accuracy (Metric Preservation)

The paper's central accuracy claim is that **SageAttention incurs "almost no end-to-end metrics loss" across all tested models, with an average degradation of approximately 0.2%** compared to full-precision attention.

**Table 8** provides the raw data across all models, which we reproduce conceptually:

- **Llama2-7B:** WikiText perplexity 5.824 (SageAttention) vs. 5.823 (full-precision) — a degradation of 0.001, which is within sampling noise. LAMBADA accuracy 0.887 vs. 0.886 — a 0.1 percentage point improvement. MMLU accuracy 0.46 vs. 0.46 — identical. These differences are negligible and well within the expected variance of zero-shot evaluation.

- **CogvideoX:** CLIPSIM 0.1836 vs. 0.1837; CLIP-T 0.9976 vs. 0.9976; VQA-a 68.839 vs. 68.962 (0.12 point degradation); VQA-t 75.037 vs. 75.925 (0.89 point degradation); FScore 3.8339 vs. 3.7684 (0.066 improvement). The VQA-t degradation of 0.89 points on a 76-point scale (~1.2%) is the largest relative change across all models and metrics, though the paper presents it as negligible. Whether a 1.2% change in a video quality metric is practically significant depends on the application; for video generation, visual quality differences may be more apparent than numeric metrics suggest.

- **Unidiffuser:** FID 166.49 vs. 163.33 (3.16 point increase, ~1.9% degradation); sFID 143.18 vs. 145.08 (1.9 point improvement); CLIPScore 0.3154 vs. 0.3152; ImageReward 0.1521 vs. 0.1609 (0.0088 point decrease). The FID increase of 3.16 points is the largest metric degradation in the table. FID is known to have high variance when computed on small sample sets — with only 256 images, the standard deviation of FID can easily be 5–10 points, making this difference statistically indistinguishable from noise. The paper does not report variance, so we cannot confirm this.

- **UltraPixel:** FID 179.79 vs. 179.78 (0.01 point increase); sFID 141.63 vs. 141.35 (0.28 point increase); CLIPScore 0.3131 vs. 0.3132; ImageReward 0.6110 vs. 0.6169. All differences are minimal.

- **TIMM:** ImageNet accuracy 84.74% vs. 84.79% (0.05 point decrease); ImageNet-Sketch 45.78% vs. 45.32% (0.46 point improvement); ImageNet-R 60.32% vs. 59.55% (0.77 point improvement). SageAttention slightly *outperforms* full-precision on two robustness benchmarks, which the paper does not explain — it may be a benign noise effect where quantization slightly regularizes attention weights, though this is speculative.

- **Llava1.6:** TextVQA 60.09% vs. 60.25% (0.16 point decrease); POPE 86.44% vs. 86.45% (0.01 point decrease); VQAv2 77.47% vs. 77.55% (0.08 point decrease). All within expected evaluation noise.

**The "0.2% average degradation" claim** appears to be derived by averaging the relative changes across all metrics. For instance, for Unidiffuser: FID increases by ~1.9% relative (3.16/163.33), but CLIPScore changes by ~0.06% and ImageReward changes by ~5.5% (0.0088/0.1609). Without knowing the exact averaging formula, the overall claim is qualitatively supported by the data: no single metric shows a dramatic, clearly significant degradation, and some metrics even improve (likely due to noise).

**Visual results (Figures 10–14 in Appendix B).** The paper provides qualitative evidence through generated images and videos. Figure 10 shows UltraPixel generations at 2560×1536 resolution where SageAttention and full-precision produce visually indistinguishable images. Figure 11 shows video frames from Open-Sora where the two methods produce identical-looking frames. Figures 12–14 provide multiple additional examples. While qualitative evidence does not replace quantitative metrics, these examples serve an important rhetorical purpose: for image and video generation, metric degradation can be "invisible" to human viewers even when metrics show small changes. The fact that 2560×1536 images — which push attention sequence lengths to thousands of tokens — can be generated with quantized attention without visible artifacts is strong evidence that the method preserves practical quality.

#### FlashAttention3 Accuracy Comparison (Table 1)

A key competitive claim is that SageAttention is **more accurate than FlashAttention3's FP8 quantized version**. Table 1 provides the evidence:

| Method | Llama2 WikiText↓ | CogVideoX FScore↑ | Unidiffuser FID↓ | UltraPixel FID↓ | TIMM ImageNet↑ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full-Precision | 5.823 | 3.768 | 163.33 | 179.78 | 84.79% |
| SageAttention (per-block, smoothed K) | 5.824 | 3.718 | 166.93 | 179.98 | 84.76% |
| FlashAttention3 (quantized) | 5.850 | 3.394 | 394.13 | 383.61 | 84.70% |

FlashAttention3's FP8 attention produces catastrophic degradation on image generation: Unidiffuser FID jumps from 163.33 to 394.13 (completely unusable), and UltraPixel FID from 179.78 to 383.61. Video generation also degrades significantly (FScore drops from 3.768 to 3.394). Language model perplexity is only mildly affected (5.823 → 5.850). The paper attributes FlashAttention3's poor accuracy to the lack of K-smoothing — the FP8 quantization directly quantizes raw K, including the channel outliers. This comparison is slightly unfair: FlashAttention3 is designed for and tested on Hopper GPUs, and the paper is evaluating its numerical accuracy independent of hardware — but the accuracy of a quantization scheme should be hardware-independent, so the comparison is valid as a measure of which approach better handles attention's numerical properties.

Note that FlashAttention3 achieves 84.70% on TIMM ImageNet, comparable to full-precision (84.79%) and SageAttention (84.74%). This suggests FlashAttention3's accuracy problems are most severe when attention has large channel-wise outliers (as in diffusion models), and less severe in vision transformers where activations may be better behaved. The paper does not investigate this model-type dependence.

#### Naive Quantization Baselines (Table 1)

To demonstrate that SageAttention's components are individually necessary, Table 1 compares multiple quantization strategies on five metrics:

- **Per-token INT8 without K-smoothing:** CogvideoX FScore drops from 3.768 to 1.924 (nearly halved). Unidiffuser FID increases from 163.33 to 221.18 (~35% degradation). UltraPixel FID increases from 179.78 to 193.36. TIMM ImageNet drops from 84.79% to 84.21% (0.58 points). Llama2 WikiText shows almost no change (5.823 → 5.824).

- **Per-block INT8 without smoothing:** Similar pattern, with CogvideoX FScore 2.014 and Unidiffuser FID 229.08.

- **Per-tensor INT8 without smoothing:** Worst results across the board — Unidiffuser FID 267.06, CogvideoX FScore 1.902. This confirms that coarser granularity amplifies the outlier problem.

- **With K-smoothing:** All granularities (per-token, per-block, per-tensor) recover to near full-precision levels across all models — e.g., Unidiffuser FID 166.52 (per-token + smoothing), 166.93 (per-block + smoothing), 167.65 (per-tensor + smoothing). **The smoothing transformation is the dominant factor in accuracy recovery.** The granularity (per-token vs. per-block) has minimal impact on accuracy once smoothing is applied, which explains why the adaptive selection can choose the faster per-token quantization for some layers without degrading quality.

The most striking finding in this table is the model-type dependency: **Llama2-7B is barely affected by quantization even without smoothing** (WikiText 5.824 without smoothing vs. 5.823 full-precision). The paper notes in Appendix Section A.6 that "the distribution of Q, K, and V in the attention of Llama2-7B is relatively uniform," which explains this robustness. Diffusion models (CogvideoX, Unidiffuser, UltraPixel) are the ones that suffer catastrophic degradation without smoothing. This heterogeneity across model types is what makes the paper's plug-and-play claim nontrivial — the method works for the sensitive models (diffusion) without breaking the already-robust models (language).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Overhead of K-smoothing (Table 10):** On CogvideoX, the attention kernel achieves 327.57 TOPS without K-smoothing and 327.52 TOPS with it — a 0.015% overhead. On UltraPixel: 325.18 vs. 324.56 TOPS, a 0.19% overhead. The paper claims "<0.2% overhead," which is supported. The smoothing operation computes a mean across N tokens for each of d channels — this is a reduction of N elements per channel, totalling O(Nd) operations, which is negligible compared to the O(N² d) QK^T matmul at large N. The mean computation can be done once for the entire K matrix (since it's the same mean for all tiles), and the subtraction is fused with quantization.

- **Benefit of adaptive quantization (Table 11):** Using only SAGEAttn-T (per-token Q,K; FP16 PV) as the universal kernel yields: CogvideoX CLIPSIM 0.1827 and attention TOPS 292.17; Llama2 MMLU 0.46 and attention TOPS 208.59. The adaptive strategy (selecting per-layer between kernels) yields: CogvideoX CLIPSIM 0.1835 (improvement of 0.0008, within noise) and TOPS 327.57 (12.1% speedup); Llama2 MMLU 0.46 (identical) and TOPS 231.74 (11.1% speedup). The adaptive strategy recovers approximately 11–12% additional throughput with no metric degradation, by applying per-token quantization (faster) to some layers and falling back to per-block (safer) for others, and potentially applying INT8 PV to the most robust layers. The paper does not report what fraction of layers use each kernel variant, which would be informative for understanding where the speedup comes from.

- **INT8 data type accuracy for Q and K (Tables 2, 17):** Table 2 (average across all layers of Llama2-7B and Unidiffuser) shows INT8 Q,K achieves 99.70% cosine similarity vs. 99.94% for E4M3 FP8 and 99.81% for E5M2 FP8 — a small but consistent advantage for INT8. Table 17 (Appendix B) measures the Q·K product specifically on one layer of Unidiffuser: INT8 achieves 99.54% cosine similarity, E4M3 92.83%, E5M2 77.95%. The dramatic gap in the product (vs. the modest gap in the individual tensors) suggests that FP8's reduced precision interacts poorly with the matmul accumulation — the quantization errors in E4M3/E5M2 are correlated across elements in a way that compounds in the dot product, while INT8's uniform quantization produces more independent errors. This is a non-obvious finding: the formal precision advantage of INT8 (256 levels) over E4M3 (16 positive levels) matters far more once tensors are multiplied than the raw tensor-level cosine similarity would suggest.

- **PV quantization accuracy (Tables 2–5):** The average-case vs. worst-case distinction is the critical finding. Average cosine similarity for INT8 P,V is 99.70% (Table 2), which might suggest INT8 PV is safe. But Table 3 reveals worst-case cosine similarity of 56.40% for INT8 P,V versus 99.99% for FP16 P,V — a catastrophic outlier on specific layers. This worst-case behavior is what motivates the FP16 accumulator fallback: a method that works "on average" but fails on some layers is not acceptable for plug-and-play deployment. The FP16 accumulator achieves identical average and worst-case metrics to FP32 accumulation (Tables 4–5: 99.98% average, 99.84% worst), confirming it is lossless for this computation. The fact that E4M3 P,V achieves worst-case cosine similarity of 76.36% (better than INT8's 56.40% but still unacceptable) suggests that FP8 would not fully solve the PV sensitivity problem either — the safe choice is to keep PV in FP16 and accept the 2× throughput limit for this matmul.

- **FlashAttention3 accuracy comparison (Table 1, Table 18):** Table 18 extends the comparison: per-block quantization with smoothed K achieves 99.31% cosine similarity; FlashAttention3 achieves 26.76%. This is a dramatic gap that the paper attributes to K-smoothing (absent in FlashAttention3) and possibly to FP8's reduced precision for Q,K (Table 17). However, the paper does not test whether adding K-smoothing to FlashAttention3's FP8 quantization would close the gap — this would require modifying FlashAttention3's kernel to preprocess K before quantization, which is a non-trivial experiment given FlashAttention3's hardware-specific implementation. The comparison as presented demonstrates that SageAttention's approach (smoothing + INT8) is more accurate than FlashAttention3's approach (unsmoothed FP8), but does not isolate the contribution of smoothing versus the data type choice since both differ simultaneously.

- **SAGEAttn kernel accuracy on synthetic data (Table 9):** On (Q,K,V) drawn from a normal distribution, SAGEAttn-T achieves 100% cosine similarity and RMSE 6.8e-4; SAGEAttn-B achieves 100% cosine similarity and RMSE 7.3e-4; SAGEAttn-vT achieves 99.9% cosine similarity and RMSE 0.065; SAGEAttn-vB achieves 98.9% cosine similarity and RMSE 0.067. The two FP16 PV variants (T, B) are near-perfect; the two INT8 PV variants (vT, vB) show 1–2 orders of magnitude higher error. This synthetic data experiment confirms that the accuracy bottleneck is PV quantization, not QK quantization — the INT8 PV variants have error roughly 100× larger than FP16 PV variants. The paper uses this as justification for defaulting to FP16 PV and only using INT8 PV when per-layer profiling confirms it's safe for that specific layer's activation distribution.

- **Real speedup on RTX 3090 (Table 19, Appendix B):** Speedups range from 1.81× (CogvideoX) to 2.29× (Unidiffuser) for FlashAttention2/xformers baselines, averaging ~2.0×. This demonstrates the speedup is not specific to RTX 4090's Ada Lovelace architecture but generalizes to the previous Ampere generation (RTX 3090), consistent with both architectures having INT8 Tensor Cores with the same relative throughput advantage over FP16.

- **Speed comparison including Torch-based SageAttention (Table 16, Appendix B):** The paper implements SageAttention on top of Torch's native attention to demonstrate the speedup is not solely dependent on the custom Triton kernel. At sequence length 4096, Torch attention achieves 55 TOPS vs. SageAttention's 87 TOPS — a 1.58× speedup, modest but still present. The gap is smaller than with the custom kernel (2.1× over FlashAttention2) because Torch attention is already less optimized, leaving less room for INT8 to improve. This experiment shows the INT8 advantage is fundamental, not an artifact of kernel engineering.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "SageAttention outperforms FlashAttention2 by about 2.1× and xformers by about 2.7×"

**This claim is supported for the specific hardware tested, but the numbers require qualification.** The "2.1×" refers to kernel-level TOPS averaged across configurations, not end-to-end model speedup. When measured in real models (Table 7), the speedup over FlashAttention2 ranges from 1.77× (Llama2) to 2.14× (UltraPixel), with an unweighted average of 1.97× — slightly below 2.1×. The gap arises because real model attention shapes (batch size, sequence length distribution, causal masking) may differ from the uniform sweep used for kernel benchmarking. The TIMM 5.89× speedup inflates the average if included, but this is over an unoptimized Torch baseline, not FlashAttention2. **The fair headline number for practitioners replacing FlashAttention2 is approximately 2.0×, not 2.1×.**

More importantly, **the paper does not measure end-to-end model throughput.** The speedups are for the attention operation only. If attention accounts for 60% of total model latency (as Figure 2 suggests at 32K sequence length), a 2× attention speedup translates to approximately 1.4× end-to-end speedup. If attention is only 30% of latency (at shorter sequences), the end-to-end gain is ~1.15×. The paper leaves this calculation to the reader, which is a practical limitation: the attention speedup is most valuable when sequences are long, which is precisely when the paper's method is most relevant, but the paper does not quantify the sequence-length-dependent end-to-end benefit.

**Missing baseline: FlashAttention3 on H100.** While the paper compares accuracy against FlashAttention3 (Table 1), it does not provide an apples-to-apples speed comparison. FlashAttention3 achieves 490 TOPS on H100; SageAttention achieves 340 TOPS on RTX 4090. These are incomparable numbers (different hardware, different TOPS measurement methodology). A proper comparison would require running both on the same hardware or normalizing by theoretical peak throughput. The paper's statement that SageAttention's throughput "is even close to" FlashAttention3's should not be interpreted as equivalent performance — 340 vs. 490 is a 44% gap.

#### Claim 2: "SageAttention incurs almost no end-to-end metrics loss across diverse models, with about 0.2% average degradation"

**This claim is supported by the data presented, but confidence intervals are absent, making "0.2% average degradation" unverifiable.** The specific issue:

- **Sample sizes are small for some metrics.** FID is computed on 256 images (COCO validation subset). The standard deviation of FID on 256 images is typically 5–10 FID points. The observed FID changes (Unidiffuser: +3.16; UltraPixel: +0.01) fall within or below this expected noise level, meaning we cannot statistically distinguish "no change" from "small degradation." The paper does not report multiple runs or confidence intervals.

- **Perplexity differences of 0.001 (5.823 vs. 5.824) on WikiText** are almost certainly not statistically significant, but no variance is reported. LAMBADA accuracy improving by 0.001 (0.886 vs. 0.887) is equally likely to be noise.

- **VQA-t for CogvideoX decreases by 0.89 points** (75.925 → 75.037 on a ~76-point scale). This is a 1.2% relative degradation — the largest across all metrics. Whether this is "almost no loss" depends on the application. For academic benchmarks, it's negligible. For production video generation where quality is competitively differentiated, small metric changes may correspond to perceptible differences.

**The visual evidence (Figures 10–14) partially compensates for the lack of statistical rigor.** Generated images and videos that are indistinguishable to human viewers despite small metric changes suggest the quantization error is perceptually invisible. However, the paper tests a small number of prompts (256 for images, unspecified for videos) and does not perform human evaluation. The qualitative evidence is suggestive but not conclusive for perceptual quality preservation.

**The claim of cross-modal generality is well-supported by the diversity of models tested.** The same SageAttention recipe (K-smoothing, FP16 PV accumulator, adaptive selection) works on autoregressive language models, diffusion-based image generators, diffusion-based video generators, vision transformers, and vision-language models without per-model tuning. This is genuinely impressive and the strongest evidence in the paper. **No other attention quantization work has demonstrated this breadth of zero-shot transfer.**

#### Claim 3: "The K-smoothing transform is critical for accuracy — without it, quantization fails catastrophically"

**Strongly supported, but with an important model-type qualification.** Table 1 shows that Llama2-7B is barely affected by quantization even *without* K-smoothing (WikiText 5.824 vs. 5.823 full-precision). The catastrophic failures occur in diffusion models (CogvideoX, Unidiffuser, UltraPixel) — FScore dropping from 3.768 to 1.924, FID increasing from 163 to 221. The paper's diagnostic (Figure 4) shows that diffusion model K matrices have severe channel-wise outliers, while Llama2's are more uniform (acknowledged in Appendix A.6). This means:

- **K-smoothing is essential for diffusion models.** Without it, INT8 attention produces unusable output.
- **K-smoothing is harmless for language models.** It costs <0.2% overhead (Table 10) and doesn't degrade already-good accuracy.
- **The plug-and-play claim works because K-smoothing is a safe default** — it helps where needed and doesn't hurt where not needed.

**The paper would be stronger if it explained *why* diffusion models exhibit K outliers while Llama2 doesn't**, rather than just noting the empirical difference. Possible explanations (cross-attention vs. self-attention, training dynamics of diffusion vs. autoregressive models, the effect of LayerNorm placement) are not explored.

#### Claim 4: "FP16 accumulator for PV is both faster and perfectly accurate"

**Supported but only tested on the specific hardware (RTX 4090/3090) and block size (64).** The theoretical justification — that summing 64 FP16 products of bounded magnitude cannot overflow — is correct for the paper's chosen block size. However:

- **Larger block sizes would increase accumulation risk.** If FlashAttention's block sizes were increased (e.g., to 128 or 256 to improve data reuse), the accumulation depth would double or quadruple, and FP16 might overflow or lose precision. The paper does not discuss this scalability limit.

- **The empirical evidence (Tables 4–5) covers Llama2-7B and Unidiffuser only.** It's possible that other models have V matrices with larger magnitudes (e.g., after different normalization schemes) that could cause FP16 accumulation to overflow. The paper does not test on models with known activation magnitude issues (e.g., models without LayerNorm before attention).

- **The 2× speedup of FP16 accumulator over FP32 is specific to RTX 3090/4090 Tensor Cores.** On datacenter GPUs (A100, H100), FP16 accumulation may not provide the same throughput advantage — A100's FP16 Tensor Cores use FP32 accumulation by default and may not support FP16 accumulation at higher throughput. The paper does not discuss this hardware dependency.

#### Claim 5: "Adaptive quantization improves throughput by 12% without accuracy loss"

**Supported by Table 11, but the mechanism is underspecified.** The paper reports that the adaptive strategy achieves 327.57 TOPS vs. 292.17 TOPS for SAGEAttn-T on CogvideoX (12.1% gain) and 231.74 vs. 208.59 on Llama2 (11.1% gain). The accuracy metrics are identical or within noise. However:

- **The paper does not report what fraction of layers use each kernel variant.** If 12% of layers use SAGEAttn-vB (the fastest) and the rest use SAGEAttn-T, that's a different picture than if 50% of layers use each. The distribution matters for understanding the method's behavior.

- **The cosine similarity threshold of 99.8% is unablated.** The paper chooses this as the worst-case similarity of SAGEAttn-B (the "safe" kernel), but does not test whether 99.5% or 99.0% would also produce negligible metric degradation while enabling faster kernels on more layers. The threshold may be unnecessarily conservative, or it may be optimal — we cannot tell from the reported experiments.

- **The calibration data is not described.** How many samples are used to compute per-layer cosine similarity? Are they from the training or test distribution? If test-distribution data is used for calibration, the adaptive selection may be slightly optimistic (overfitting to the calibration set). If training-distribution data is used, the selection may not generalize to out-of-distribution inference inputs. **The absence of any cross-validation or held-out calibration set is a notable gap.**

#### Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper

1. **End-to-end model throughput, not just attention kernel throughput.** The paper measures attention speedup but never reports total model inference time. For practical deployment, the end-to-end speedup (including linear layers, normalization, etc.) is what matters. At short sequence lengths where attention is not the bottleneck, SageAttention's speedup might be invisible. At long sequences, it could be substantial. Quantifying this relationship would help practitioners decide when to adopt the method.

2. **Accuracy at higher quantization levels (INT4).** The paper focuses on INT8 but acknowledges in the related work section that 4-bit quantization exists for linear layers. The natural extension — INT4 attention — is not explored. Given that the FP16 PV accumulator already provides 2× FP16 throughput, further reducing Q,K to INT4 might provide additional speedup on hardware with INT4 Tensor Core support (Hopper). The paper's techniques (K-smoothing, per-block quantization) might enable this, but it's untested.

3. **Ablation of the 99.8% threshold.** Testing adaptive selection at multiple thresholds (99.5%, 99.0%, 99.9%) and measuring the speed-accuracy tradeoff would characterize how sensitive the method is to this hyperparameter and whether it requires per-model tuning.

4. **Variance estimates for all metrics.** Reporting standard deviations for FID, perplexity, and accuracy across multiple runs or bootstrap resamples would allow statistical assessment of whether observed differences are significant or noise. This is standard practice in the generative modeling literature but absent here.

5. **Comparison with FlashAttention3 on the same hardware.** Running FlashAttention3 on H100 and SageAttention on H100 (or both on a GPU both support) would provide a clean speed-accuracy comparison. The current comparison mixes hardware and algorithm effects.

6. **Stability under sequence length variation.** All metric evaluations are at the models' default sequence lengths (e.g., Llama2 at 1536 tokens). Since the paper's motivation is long-sequence inference, testing whether SageAttention's accuracy degrades at longer sequences (8K, 16K, 32K) would validate the method for its intended use case. The precision of FP16 accumulation in PV depends on the block size, but the *number* of blocks increases with sequence length, which could compound errors.

7. **Integration with weight quantization methods.** Table 13 (Appendix Section A.5) shows preliminary results combining SageAttention with AWQ (W4A16 weight quantization) on Llama2, with a small perplexity increase (5.4729 → 5.5998). A more systematic study combining SageAttention with weight and KV-cache quantization would demonstrate the method's compatibility with broader quantization pipelines.

#### Overall assessment

The experiments substantiate the paper's core practical contribution: **a training-free INT8 attention method that reliably matches full-precision accuracy across diverse models while providing approximately 2× speedup on consumer GPUs.** The evidence for speed is thorough (multiple GPUs, multiple sequence lengths, multiple head dimensions, real models). The evidence for accuracy is broad (five models, multiple modalities, diverse metrics) but lacks statistical rigor (no variance estimates, no significance tests). The adaptive quantization mechanism is demonstrated to provide additional speedup but is not thoroughly characterized (threshold sensitivity, layer-wise kernel distribution, calibration data requirements are not reported).

The paper's strongest empirical finding — that K-smoothing combined with INT8 quantization recovers accuracy across all tested models — is robust and well-supported. The weaker aspects are: (1) the missing end-to-end throughput numbers, which would translate kernel speedup into practical impact; (2) the absence of statistical characterization for accuracy metrics, limiting confidence in the "negligible degradation" claim for borderline cases like CogvideoX VQA-t; and (3) the unablated calibration procedure for adaptive selection, which leaves the method's sensitivity to hyperparameters unknown.

The experiments successfully demonstrate that the proposed techniques solve the specific problem identified (attention quantization failures due to K outliers and PV sensitivity), and do so without introducing new failure modes or model-specific dependencies. However, they leave open how the method scales to longer sequences, larger models, INT4 precision, and combined weight-activation quantization — all natural extensions that practitioners would want to understand before adopting the method in production.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for in Headline Speedup Numbers

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework depends on estimating prompt difficulty *before* allocating the inference budget. The paper's method for doing so — generating 2048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness (oracle) or PRM scores (predicted) — is extraordinarily expensive. Section 3.2 explicitly acknowledges this without resolving it:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence.** The reported 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation plus strategy execution, and the former could dominate the latter. For a batch of 500 test questions, generating 2048 samples each to estimate difficulty consumes ~1,024,000 generations — far exceeding any of the test-time budgets studied (which max out at 256–512 generations per question). If difficulty estimation costs 2048 generations per prompt and the compute-optimal strategy saves a few hundred generations versus best-of-N, the net compute may be *higher*, not lower. The 4× figure should be understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** — what is possible if difficulty were known for free — rather than a realized deployment gain.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports the 4× improvement in Figures 4 and 8 without including difficulty estimation cost in the budget, so the limitation is visible in the experimental methodology itself. The fact that both oracle and predicted difficulty bins (both based on 2048 samples) produce similar curves does not address this — both methods are equally expensive. The paper provides no experiments that reduce the number of difficulty-estimation samples (e.g., 16, 64, 256) to characterize the accuracy-cost tradeoff of the difficulty estimate.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly flags this as future work in Section 8: "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question." No such model is developed or evaluated. An alternative — adaptive difficulty estimation that starts with a few samples and allocates budget dynamically — is mentioned but not explored. Until this gap is closed, the compute-optimal framework is a proof of concept for adaptive allocation, not a deployable system.

### Hard Problems Remain Unsolved — Test-Time Compute Amplifies Existing Capability but Cannot Create It

**The constraint.** The paper's framework assumes the base model's proposal distribution contains at least *some* correct solutions for the problem in question — i.e., pass@1 is non-trivially above zero. Section 7 explicitly states:

> "For problems that are fundamentally beyond the model's capabilities (hard problems), scaling test-time compute provides minimal improvement."

**The consequence.** Across all methods — search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations — the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5) show **near-zero improvement regardless of compute budget**. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all methods and all budgets. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%, falling far below the `~14×` larger model's performance. This creates a sharp boundary: **test-time compute is not a substitute for pretraining on genuinely novel or out-of-distribution reasoning problems.** If the base model's pass@1 is near zero, no amount of search or revision will help — there are no correct solutions in the proposal distribution to find or refine. For such problems, only pretraining (larger models, more data, better training) provides a path forward.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The per-bin accuracy breakdowns in Figures 3, 7, and 9 all demonstrate the flat-lining of bin 5 performance. The FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9, Section 7) quantitatively shows the pretraining-compute advantage for hard problems: at moderate-to-high `R` values, test-time compute with the smaller model is substantially worse than simply using the larger model (e.g., −52.9% relative disadvantage for PRM search on hard problems at `R ≫ 1`). The paper is transparent about this boundary in the Section 7 takeaway, characterizing it as a clear finding rather than a shortcoming to be fixed.

**Mitigation status.** None. The paper frames this as an inherent limitation of test-time compute — it can amplify existing capability but cannot create capability from nothing. It is presented as a feature of the framework (understanding *where* test-time compute helps is as important as understanding *how much* it helps) rather than a bug. No mechanism for expanding the base model's capability frontier at inference time is proposed, and it's unclear whether one could exist — this may be a fundamental limitation.

### The 14× Larger Model Baseline Is Deliberately Weakened, Making the Pretraining-vs-Inference Comparison Asymmetric

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023) rather than compute-optimal pretraining (Hoffmann et al., 2022), where both data and parameters are scaled equally. The paper acknowledges this explicitly:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

Additionally, the `~14×` larger model uses only **greedy decoding** — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search. The smaller model, in contrast, is allowed extensive test-time compute optimization.

**The consequence.** A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with `14×` more total FLOPs (scaling both parameters and data) would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model, making the pretraining baseline **weaker than it needs to be.** This inflates the apparent advantage of test-time compute. Similarly, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget (say, best-of-8 or best-of-32 with simple majority voting) would create a much stronger baseline that is never tested. The paper's conclusion that "a smaller model with additional test-time compute can outperform a ~14× larger model" is true under the specific baseline conditions tested, but may not hold against: (a) a compute-optimally trained larger model, or (b) a larger model that also uses some test-time compute. The asymmetry matters for practical resource allocation decisions — organizations deciding between training bigger models versus investing in smarter inference need to know the fair tradeoff, not the one where the larger model is handicapped.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The baseline description is in Section 7: scaling only parameters, using greedy decoding for the larger model. No ablation tests stronger baselines (e.g., the larger model with best-of-N). The paper does not report how much the larger model would improve with even modest test-time compute, so we cannot estimate how much of the reported advantage would persist in a symmetric comparison.

**Mitigation status.** The paper flags the compute-optimal pretraining limitation as future work but does not address the greedy-decoding asymmetry. A fairer experiment — giving the larger model the same test-time compute budget per dollar of total FLOPs — is not proposed. The current results are best interpreted as evidence that test-time compute *can* compete with pretraining under specific conditions, not as a universal prescription.

### Revisions and Search Are Studied Independently — Their Combination, Which Should Be Synergistic, Is Never Tested

**The constraint.** Section 2 frames revisions (modifying the proposal distribution) and PRM search (improving candidate selection) as complementary, independent scaling axes. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges the gap:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

**The consequence.** This is a significant gap because the two mechanisms have complementary difficulty-dependent strengths documented in the paper itself: revisions help most on easy problems (local refinement of roughly correct answers), while PRM search helps most on medium problems (global exploration of solution strategies). Combining them — using the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search, or using the PRM to guide which revision branches to pursue — could yield gains beyond either method alone. The paper demonstrates that both mechanisms individually achieve ~4× efficiency improvements over best-of-N (Figures 4, 8), but the reader cannot know whether combining them would be additive, multiplicative, or antagonistic. The current results therefore represent a **lower bound** on what an integrated system could achieve, leaving open whether the full potential of test-time compute is significantly higher than what is reported.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The experiments in Sections 5 and 6 are conducted entirely separately — PRM search uses the base few-shot prompted model, revisions use the fine-tuned revision model, and the two pipelines never intersect. The difficulty-bin analyses show that the optimal strategy types differ across bins (search better on medium, revisions better on easy), which *suggests* combination could be beneficial, but no direct evidence exists. No ablation or pilot experiment tests even a simple combination (e.g., beam search over revision model outputs).

**Mitigation status.** The paper flags this as future work in Section 8 but provides no preliminary results or design sketch for how combination would work. Given that each component individually required substantial engineering (PRM training, revision model fine-tuning), a combined system is non-trivial and the paper offers no guidance on integration challenges.

### The Single Benchmark, Single Model Family Limits Generalizability of Difficulty-Dependent Findings

**The constraint.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions, competition-level math problems) with PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The paper argues this model is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this is asserted, not demonstrated. The difficulty-dependent patterns — beam search hurting easy problems, revisions dominating easy problems, parallel search helping medium problems — are derived entirely from one model on one task type.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or task-specific in ways that limit transfer. First, the PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend on PaLM 2-S\*'s output distribution — a model with different calibration properties might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves, changing which strategies are optimal for which bins. Second, the revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families — models with weaker in-context learning might not benefit from revisions at all. Third, MATH consists exclusively of symbolic math reasoning problems with clean ground-truth answers. It is unclear whether the difficulty-dependent patterns generalize to other reasoning domains (code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA) or to tasks requiring factual knowledge rather than inference. The paper's finding that "beam search outperforms best-of-N on medium problems" may be a property of math reasoning where intermediate steps can be verified, rather than a general property of LLM reasoning.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All quantitative results (Figures 3–9, Tables throughout) are on MATH with PaLM 2-S\*. No experiments on other benchmarks (GSM8K, HumanEval, ARC, MMLU reasoning subsets) or other model families (Llama, GPT, Gemini) exist. The 500-question test set, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 questions each, then further split by two-fold cross-validation, means the compute-optimal policy is selected based on ~50 questions per fold per bin. This is a small sample for strategy selection, and the selected strategies may be brittle.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation. The author's belief that PaLM 2-S\* is "representative" is stated without evidence. Replication on other models and tasks is left entirely to future work. This is a standard limitation for a single-paper study, and the paper is transparent about its scope, but the strong prescriptive claims about optimal strategy selection (Figures 4, 8) should be understood as potentially specific to the MATH + PaLM 2-S\* setting until replicated.

### The Revision Model Has a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate, Patched by Post-Hoc Selection Rather Than Solved

**The constraint.** Section 6.1 reports that approximately 38% of correct answers produced during a sequential revision chain get "revised" back to incorrect answers in the subsequent step:

> "since the model was trained only on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect (followed by a correct target), at test time the model may encounter correct answers in its context (produced during earlier revisions) and incorrectly 'revise' them into wrong answers."

**The consequence.** This is a direct consequence of the training data construction: the revision model only sees incorrect-to-correct trajectories (0–4 incorrect answers followed by a correct answer), so it has no training signal for what to do when the current answer is *already* correct. At test time, when the chain produces a correct answer, the model may treat it as if it were incorrect and produce a wrong revision. The paper mitigates this by using majority voting or verifier-based selection *across the entire chain* to pick the best answer, rather than always taking the final revision. This works — chain-level selection recovers the correct answer lost to reversion — but it is a patch, not a solution. It means the model is generating work (revisions) that is actively harmful ~38% of the time when starting from a correct answer, wasting compute and potentially confusing downstream selection mechanisms. Moreover, the selection mechanism (majority or verifier) must be robust enough to distinguish the correct earlier answer from the incorrect later revision, which adds dependency on the verifier's quality.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% reversion rate is reported in Section 6.1. Figure 6 (left) shows that while per-step pass@1 gradually improves, it oscillates and does not monotonically increase — likely due to this reversion phenomenon. The ReST^EM experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) shows that an alternative training approach makes the problem *worse*, with sequential revisions substantially degrading performance, suggesting the reversion issue is sensitive to training methodology.

**Mitigation status.** The paper mitigates but does not solve the reversion problem. The chain-level selection (majority voting or verifier) is a post-hoc correction that adds overhead and depends on having a reliable selection mechanism. A more principled solution — training the model to recognize when the current answer is already correct and output a "no revision needed" token, or training on mixed trajectories that include correct-to-correct transitions — is not explored. The paper does not discuss this as a limitation, but the 38% reversion rate represents a substantial efficiency cost: ~38% of revision steps after a correct answer are wasted computation, and the chain must be long enough that selection can recover the correct answer despite these regressions.
