# Griffin: Mixing Gated Linear Recurrences with Local Attention for Efficient Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2402.19427](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19427)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Hawk**, a recurrent neural network built around a novel **Real-Gated Linear Recurrent Unit (RG-LRU)** — a gated linear recurrence that modulates both input and recurrent weight without depending on the hidden state — and **Griffin**, a hybrid model that interleaves these recurrent blocks with local (sliding-window) attention.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Hawk**, a recurrent neural network built around a novel **Real-Gated Linear Recurrent Unit (RG-LRU)** — a gated linear recurrence that modulates both input and recurrent weight without depending on the hidden state — and **Griffin**, a hybrid model that interleaves these recurrent blocks with local (sliding-window) attention. Trained on the MassiveText dataset across scales up to 14B parameters, Griffin achieves lower held-out loss than a strong MQA Transformer baseline at all FLOPs budgets while matching Llama-2's downstream performance despite being trained on "over 6 times fewer tokens" (300B vs. 2T). The models demonstrate up to 14.8× higher inference throughput than MQA Transformers on long sequences (sampling 4096 tokens at 1B scale) and can extrapolate to sequences 4× longer than those seen during training, establishing that fixed-size recurrent states combined with local attention can substitute for global attention in language modeling only when the local attention window is large enough to capture the relevant context.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Fundamental Tension: Transformers Scale Well but Decode Poorly

The paper addresses a structural tension at the heart of modern language model deployment. Transformers have become the dominant architecture because they train efficiently on modern accelerators — their self-attention mechanism is fully parallelizable across the sequence dimension, making dense matrix multiplications the primary computational primitive (Kaplan et al., 2020). This alignment with hardware that is optimized for high FLOPs-to-byte ratios (TensorCores, TPU MXUs) has enabled the scaling of Transformer-based LLMs to hundreds of billions of parameters with predictable power-law improvements in held-out loss (Brown et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022).

However, this training efficiency comes at a steep inference cost that is structural, not incidental. During auto-regressive decoding, each new token requires attending to all previous tokens. Standard practice caches the key and value vectors for every previous position — the **Key-Value (KV) cache** — to avoid recomputing them. For a model with depth $N$, sequence length $T$, and key-value dimension $d_{kv} = H_k \times d_{head}$, this cache grows as $O(N \times T \times d_{kv})$, linearly in sequence length. The paper quantifies this concretely in Appendix F.4.1: for either Multi-Head Attention (MHA) or Multi-Query Attention (MQA), the KV cache "can exceed the number of model parameters when the sequence length $T$ is large." When the cache size rivals or surpasses the parameter count, decoding becomes **memory bound** — dominated by the time required to load the KV cache from high-bandwidth memory (HBM) into vector memory (VMEM) on each step.

The paper articulates a simple but powerful model of this bottleneck in Section 5.1 (Equation 5):

> "Time to sample next token $\approx \frac{\text{param size} + \text{batch size} \times \text{cache size}}{\text{memory bandwidth}}$"

When the cache size is large relative to parameters, throughput collapses. Multi-Query Attention (Shazeer, 2019) reduces the KV cache by a factor of $H$ (the number of query heads) by sharing a single key-value head across all query heads, but as the paper notes (Section 1), this is a constant-factor improvement: "the cache still grows linearly in sequence length." The quadratic FLOP cost of global attention during training and prefill is the more commonly discussed limitation, but for deployed systems handling long conversations, document processing, or code generation, the **linear growth of the KV cache during decoding** is often the binding constraint on throughput and latency.

### The RNN Alternative: Conceptually Appealing, Practically Broken

Recurrent neural networks offer an elegant resolution to this problem. An RNN compresses the entire sequence history into a **fixed-size hidden state** that is updated iteratively — there is no growing cache, no quadratic attention computation, and the state size is independent of sequence length. This makes RNNs fast at inference and naturally suited to streaming and long-sequence settings.

The problem, as the paper acknowledges in Section 1, is that classical RNNs are "difficult to train and hard to scale." The sequential dependence in the recurrence prevents parallelization across the time dimension during training — Backpropagation Through Time (Werbos, 1990) must unroll the computation step by step, forward and backward. This makes training slow on hardware designed for parallel matrix operations. Furthermore, classical non-linear RNNs like LSTMs (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) and GRUs (Chung et al., 2014) suffer from vanishing and exploding gradients that limit their ability to capture long-range dependencies, and their dense recurrence matrices ($h_t = \sigma(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t)$) have high FLOPs-to-byte ratios that are poorly suited to modern accelerators optimized for matrix-matrix multiplications.

The result is a landscape where RNNs were "largely ineffective" at the scales where Transformers excel. The paper's framing is direct: "to replace Transformers, new RNN models must demonstrate not only comparable performance at scale but also achieve similar hardware efficiency" (Section 1). This is a high bar — it requires solving both the training parallelization problem and the hardware utilization problem simultaneously.

### The SSM Revolution: Linear Recurrences to the Rescue, Partially

The past several years have seen a resurgence of interest in recurrent architectures under the banner of **State Space Models (SSMs)**. The key insight, originating with the Structured State Space (S4) model (Gu et al., 2021a), is to use **linear recurrences** of the form:

$$h_t = A h_{t-1} + B x_t$$
$$y_t = C h_t + D x_t$$

Because the recurrence is linear, it can be computed in parallel across the sequence using either a convolution (if $A$, $B$, $C$ are time-invariant) or an associative scan (prefix sum) operation. This recovers the training parallelizability of Transformers while maintaining the fixed-state inference efficiency of RNNs. The paper's "Related Works" section (Section 7) traces this lineage comprehensively through S4, S4D (Gu et al., 2022), S5 (Smith et al., 2022), H3 (Dao et al., 2022b), Hyena (Poli et al., 2023), and others.

The LRU (Linear Recurrent Unit) from Orvieto et al. (2023b) is particularly important as the direct predecessor of this paper's RG-LRU. The LRU demonstrated that a **diagonal linear recurrence** — where $A$ is a diagonal matrix, making all operations element-wise — could match the performance of more complex SSM parameterizations when properly initialized. Diagonalization is critical because it drops the FLOPs-per-step from $O(d^2)$ to $O(d)$, making the recurrence extremely lightweight. The LRU used complex-valued diagonal recurrences ($a \in \mathbb{C}$) with learnable eigenvalues initialized uniformly on a ring near the unit circle, an approach motivated by the theory of orthogonal polynomials (Gu et al., 2020) and continuous-time discretization.

However, several gaps remained as of late 2023 when this paper was in development:

**1. No gating mechanism for input-dependent filtering.** The LRU's recurrence is linear and time-invariant — $a$ is fixed regardless of the input $x_t$. This means the model cannot selectively ignore uninformative tokens or amplify important ones during the recurrence update. LSTMs and GRUs derive much of their practical power from gates that modulate how inputs interact with the hidden state based on the current input and previous state. SSMs initially lacked this capability.

**2. Mamba partially addresses this but with a different mechanism.** Concurrent to this work, Gu and Dao (2023) introduced Mamba, which adds an **input-dependent selection mechanism** to the SSM recurrence. In Mamba, the $B$ and $C$ matrices (and the discretization step size $\Delta$) become functions of the input $x_t$, allowing the model to selectively attend to or ignore tokens. The paper notes (Section 7) that Mamba "achieved performance comparable to Transformers with efficient inference." However, Mamba's selection mechanism is conceptually analogous to the GRU update gate — it interpolates between the previous state and a new candidate state. As the paper demonstrates in Appendix A (Figure 7), this gating behavior is "almost identical to the GRU for values of $A$ close to 1." Mamba also does not explore hybrid architectures combining recurrences with attention.

**3. The copying and retrieval problem.** A critical limitation of pure SSMs was identified by Jelassi et al. (2024): "Transformers can be significantly more efficient than state space models ... at learning synthetic tasks such as copying the context or retrieving relevant tokens from the context." This matters because many practical language tasks — following instructions, retrieving facts, maintaining coherence — require the model to precisely reproduce or reference specific tokens from earlier in the context. A fixed-size recurrent state must compress all relevant information, and for exact copying tasks where no compression is possible, this creates an inherent bottleneck. The paper explicitly cites Jelassi et al. (2024) as motivation for studying whether hybrid recurrence-attention models can mitigate this limitation.

**4. Training efficiency on real hardware.** While linear recurrences are theoretically parallelizable via associative scans, the paper makes a crucial observation in Section 4.2: "the associative scan reduces the number of FLOPs required but does not reduce memory overheads, which is our primary bottleneck in practice." On TPU-v3 hardware, the associative scan's tree-recombination pattern involves random memory accesses that are "poorly suited for the TPU architecture, leading to even slower memory transfers." A naive JAX implementation of the linear recurrence scan runs up to 3× slower than a custom Pallas kernel. This means the theoretical efficiency of linear recurrences does not automatically translate to wall-clock training speed on real accelerators — engineering the implementation matters enormously.

**5. No systematic scaling laws.** Prior to this work, there was no demonstration that diagonal RNN architectures follow the same power-law scaling relationships (between held-out loss and training FLOPs) that are well-established for Transformers (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). Without such evidence, it was unclear whether recurrence-based models could be competitive at the 7B+ parameter scale where Transformers dominate.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself at the intersection of three research threads, aiming to synthesize their insights while addressing their individual limitations:

**From the SSM/LRU lineage**, it inherits the diagonal linear recurrence structure and the insight that proper initialization (eigenvalues near the unit circle) is critical for long-range memory. But it diverges by (a) introducing a novel **recurrence gate** $r_t$ that modulates the recurrent weight $a$ based on the input, creating a mechanism "different from other gating mechanisms in the literature" (Section 2.4), and (b) abandoning complex-valued recurrences, finding them "not beneficial for language modelling in practice" — a practical simplification that reduces parameters and computation.

The paper is explicit about what makes the RG-LRU's gating novel (Appendix A, Figure 7). Most gates (GRU, Mamba) allow interpolation between $\alpha(r_t)h_{t-1} + \beta(r_t)x_t$ where $\alpha(r_t)$ can reach 0, fully discarding history and replacing it with the input. The RG-LRU's gate instead produces:

$$h_t = a^{c r_t} \odot h_{t-1} + \sqrt{1 - (a^{c r_t})^2} \odot (i_t \odot x_t)$$

Because $a = \sigma(\Lambda) < 1$ and $c = 8$, the recurrence gate $r_t$ modulates how close the effective decay rate $a^{c r_t}$ is to either the standard LRU update (when $r_t \approx 1$, giving $a_t \approx a^c$) or to preserving the previous state (when $r_t < 1$, making $a_t$ closer to 1). The paper hypothesizes this gate's role is "to enable the model to achieve super-exponential memory by reducing the influence of uninformative inputs" — selectively slowing down the decay of the hidden state when the current input is noise relative to the task.

**From the hybrid architecture literature**, it draws on the observation that local (sliding-window) attention (Beltagy et al., 2020) and recurrent blocks have complementary strengths: "local attention accurately models the recent past, while the recurrent layers can transmit information across long sequences" (Section 3). This is not a new observation per se — models like Longformer (Beltagy et al., 2020) and Mistral (Jiang et al., 2023) use local attention — but combining local attention with gated linear recurrences in a specific layered pattern (two recurrent blocks followed by one local attention block, repeated) is the paper's architectural contribution in Griffin. The motivation is clear: a pure RNN like Hawk may struggle with precise token-level copying and retrieval (as Jelassi et al. demonstrated for Mamba), while a pure Transformer is expensive at inference. Griffin aims to get the best of both: the long-range compression of recurrence plus the precise local modeling of attention.

**From the efficient training systems literature**, it tackles the practical engineering challenge head-on. The paper dedicates an entire section (Section 4) to model parallelism strategies for the recurrent block and a custom Pallas kernel for the RG-LRU scan. This is not a minor implementation detail — it is central to the paper's claim of "comparable training efficiency to Transformers." The key insight in the sharding strategy (Section 4.1) is that using **block-diagonal** gate weight matrices ($W_a$, $W_x$) instead of dense ones, combined with chip-wise sharding of the diagonal recurrence and Conv1D, makes the recurrent block's communication requirements "equivalent to those of the MLP block" — a single all-reduce per forward and backward pass. This is what makes distributed training of large recurrent models feasible.

**Relative to Mamba**, the paper positions Griffin as a stronger alternative via three mechanisms: (a) the novel recurrence gate (which the paper argues in Appendix A is fundamentally different from Mamba's selection mechanism), (b) the hybrid recurrence-attention architecture, and (c) empirical results showing Hawk-3B exceeding Mamba-3B on downstream benchmarks despite being trained on half the tokens. However, it is important to note these were trained on different datasets with different hyperparameters, making the comparison imperfect — a point the paper acknowledges.

**Relative to Llama-2**, the paper positions Griffin as a compelling efficiency story: matching downstream performance with ~7× fewer training tokens. This is the "overtraining" regime that has become common in practice (Touvron et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023) — training smaller models on more data than Chinchilla-optimal — and the paper's claim is that Griffin achieves better data efficiency in this regime than Llama-2. Again, the caveat of different training data and hyperparameter tuning strategies applies.

### The Core Gap This Paper Fills

The paper's central contribution, distilled, is empirical proof that a **gated diagonal linear recurrence** — when properly initialized, gated, engineered for modern hardware, and optionally combined with local attention — can:

1. **Scale according to the same power laws as Transformers** up to 14B parameters (Figure 1a), establishing that the scaling behavior is architecture-independent for this class of models.
2. **Achieve lower held-out loss than strong MQA Transformer baselines** at matched training FLOPs when combined with local attention (Griffin), and approximately match Transformer baselines in the pure recurrent case (Hawk).
3. **Deliver up to 14.8× higher inference throughput** on long sequences compared to MQA Transformers (Figure 1b), a direct consequence of the fixed-size state that eliminates KV cache loading.
4. **Extrapolate to sequences 4× longer than training length** without the failure modes that afflict positional encodings in Transformers (Figure 5).
5. **Learn copying and retrieval tasks efficiently when combined with local attention** (Griffin matches Transformer learning speed on Selective Copying, Figure 6a), though pure recurrent models (Hawk) struggle — validating the hybrid architecture hypothesis.

None of these individual findings is entirely surprising — each has precedent in the literature — but the paper's contribution is demonstrating them **simultaneously** in a single model family with systematic scaling experiments, on real hardware, with fair baselines. This holistic validation is what makes the case that "these proposed models offer a powerful and efficient alternative to Transformers with global attention" (Section 8).

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper presents **Hawk** and **Griffin**, two language model architectures that replace global self-attention with a combination of gated linear recurrences and (in Griffin's case) local sliding-window attention. The problem they solve is the inference-time bottleneck of Transformers — specifically, the linear growth of the Key-Value (KV) cache with sequence length that makes decoding memory-bound and slow for long sequences — by compressing the entire sequence history into a fixed-size hidden state that does not grow with sequence length, while maintaining training efficiency comparable to Transformers through a combination of architectural design and custom hardware kernels.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

At the highest level, all models in this paper share the same backbone structure, differing only in the **temporal-mixing block** — the component responsible for aggregating information across time steps. The architecture consists of:

1. **Embedding layer**: Converts input token IDs to continuous vectors of dimension $D$. The weights of this layer are **tied** with the final output projection layer (the "unembedding").

2. **Residual blocks (repeated $N$ times)**: Each block contains two sequential sub-components connected by residual (skip) connections:
   - **Temporal-mixing sub-block**: Applies RMSNorm, then the temporal-mixing block (global MQA, local MQA, or recurrent block), then adds the residual connection from the block input.
   - **MLP sub-block**: Applies RMSNorm, then a gated MLP block, then adds the residual connection from the MLP sub-block's input.

3. **Final RMSNorm**: Applied after all $N$ residual blocks to produce the final activations.

4. **Output projection**: A linear layer followed by softmax to produce token probabilities. Weights are shared with the input embedding layer.

The three model variants differ only in what constitutes the temporal-mixing block:
- **MQA Transformer baseline**: Uses global Multi-Query Attention with Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE).
- **Hawk**: Uses the recurrent block (Conv1D + RG-LRU) exclusively.
- **Griffin**: Uses a **layered pattern** of two residual blocks with recurrent blocks followed by one residual block with local MQA attention, repeated throughout the depth.

Information flows left-to-right through the sequence of residual blocks, with each temporal-mixing block aggregating information across the sequence dimension before the MLP block processes each position independently.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

The deep dive proceeds in the following order, which builds from the most fundamental building block to the complete system:

1. **The RG-LRU layer** (Section 2.4) — the novel gated linear recurrence that is the core computational primitive. Understanding its gating mechanism, initialization, and stability properties is essential because everything else is built around it.

2. **The recurrent block** (Section 2.3) — how the RG-LRU is embedded within a larger block that includes input projections, a small convolution, and gating. This is the "drop-in replacement" for attention.

3. **The MLP and residual blocks** (Sections 2.1–2.2) — the shared backbone structure that wraps temporal-mixing blocks, including the gated MLP design with GeGeLU activation.

4. **The three model architectures** (Section 3) — how the components are assembled into MQA Transformer, Hawk, and Griffin, including the layered structure of Griffin and the hyperparameter scaling rules.

5. **Training infrastructure** (Section 4) — the model parallelism strategy (Megatron sharding, block-diagonal gates, ZeRO) and the custom Pallas kernel for efficient linear recurrences on TPU-v3. This section explains *why* this layer of engineering is necessary (the memory-bound nature of diagonal RNNs) and how it achieves training speeds comparable to Transformers.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **model architecture and empirical scaling paper** whose core idea is that a properly designed gated linear recurrence — optionally combined with local attention — can match or exceed Transformer performance at scale while providing dramatically faster inference on long sequences, all while maintaining training efficiency comparable to Transformers through careful hardware-aware implementation.

#### The RG-LRU Layer: Core Recurrence with Novel Gating

The Real-Gated Linear Recurrent Unit (RG-LRU) is the paper's central architectural innovation. It is a diagonal linear recurrence — meaning the hidden state update operates element-wise rather than through a dense matrix multiplication — augmented with two independent gating mechanisms that modulate how inputs and the recurrent weight interact with the hidden state.

**The recurrence equations.** The layer is defined by four equations that compute, for each time step $t$, the output hidden state $h_t$ given the input $x_t$ and previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$:

$$r_t = \sigma(W_a x_t + b_a)$$

$$i_t = \sigma(W_x x_t + b_x)$$

$$a_t = a^{c r_t}$$

$$h_t = a_t \odot h_{t-1} + \sqrt{1 - a_t^2} \odot (i_t \odot x_t)$$

where $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{RNN}}$ is the input vector at time $t$, $h_{t-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{RNN}}$ is the hidden state from the previous time step, $W_a \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{RNN} \times D_{RNN}}$ and $W_x \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{RNN} \times D_{RNN}}$ are weight matrices (later made block-diagonal for sharding efficiency), $b_a, b_x \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{RNN}}$ are bias vectors, $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function ($\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$), $\odot$ denotes element-wise (Hadamard) multiplication, $a \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{RNN}}$ is a learnable vector of recurrent weights, and $c$ is a scalar constant set to 8.

**What it computes, operationally.** At each time step, the layer performs the following sequence of operations:

1. **Compute the recurrence gate $r_t$**: A linear projection of the input $x_t$ followed by a sigmoid produces a vector of values in $(0, 1)$. Each element of $r_t$ will control how strongly the corresponding element of the recurrent weight $a$ is "activated" at this time step.

2. **Compute the input gate $i_t$**: A second, independent linear projection of $x_t$ followed by a sigmoid produces another vector in $(0, 1)$. Each element of $i_t$ will gate (scale down) the corresponding element of the input $x_t$ before it enters the hidden state.

3. **Compute the effective recurrent weight $a_t$**: The base recurrent weight $a$ (which is itself parameterized as $a = \sigma(\Lambda)$ for a learnable parameter $\Lambda \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{RNN}}$) is raised to the power $c \cdot r_t$. Since $a \in (0, 1)$ and $r_t \in (0, 1)$, the exponent $c \cdot r_t$ (with $c = 8$) is between 0 and 8. When $r_t$ is close to 1, $a_t = a^{8}$ is very small (since $a$ is already <1, raising to the 8th power makes it much smaller), meaning the model rapidly forgets the previous hidden state. When $r_t$ is close to 0, $a_t = a^{0} = 1$, meaning the previous hidden state is fully preserved and the input is completely ignored (since $\sqrt{1 - 1^2} = 0$ makes the coefficient of the input term vanish).

4. **Update the hidden state**: The new hidden state is a convex combination (specifically, a circular interpolation) of the old hidden state $h_{t-1}$ and the gated input $i_t \odot x_t$, weighted by $a_t$ and $\sqrt{1 - a_t^2}$ respectively. The use of $\sqrt{1 - a_t^2}$ as the coefficient for the input term — rather than $1 - a_t$ as in a standard exponential moving average — is a deliberate choice that preserves the Euclidean norm of the state if $|h_{t-1}| = |i_t \odot x_t|$, a property inherited from the LRU.

5. **Output**: The output of the layer is simply $y_t = h_t$, the updated hidden state. There is no separate output projection within the RG-LRU itself — that happens in the surrounding recurrent block.

**Why this gating mechanism.** The paper argues (Section 2.4, Appendix A) that this recurrence gate $r_t$ is "different from other gating mechanisms in the literature." To understand why, we need to compare against standard gates. The paper provides a general framework for analyzing gating in recurrences of the form:

$$h_t = \alpha(r_t) h_{t-1} + \beta(r_t) x_t$$

For the **GRU update gate** (and similarly Mamba's selection mechanism), the interpolation coefficients are $\alpha(r_t) = 1 - r_t$ and $\beta(r_t) = r_t$. This means the gate can fully interpolate between keeping the old state ($r_t = 0 \implies h_t = h_{t-1}$) and replacing it entirely with the input ($r_t = 1 \implies h_t = x_t$). The gate can **discard history completely**.

For the **RG-LRU recurrence gate**, the coefficients are $\alpha(r_t) = a^{c r_t}$ and $\beta(r_t) = \sqrt{1 - (a^{c r_t})^2}$. Because $a = \sigma(\Lambda) \in (0, 1)$, the effective recurrent weight $a^{c r_t}$ is always strictly positive — it can become very close to 1 (when $r_t \to 0$) but it is never exactly 0. The gate can **attenuate the input** (making $\beta(r_t)$ small) to preserve history, but it cannot **discard history entirely** to replace it with the input. The paper hypothesizes that this "bias towards retaining information" enables "super-exponential memory by reducing the influence of uninformative inputs" — the model can selectively "freeze" its state during irrelevant tokens rather than being forced to overwrite history.

**Why the constant $c = 8$.** The constant $c$ acts as a multiplier on the gate value $r_t$ in the exponent. Because $a < 1$, raising to a larger exponent makes $a^{c r_t}$ smaller. With $c = 8$, the effective recurrent weight $a_t$ can range from $a^8$ (when $r_t = 1$ — fast forgetting) down to $a^0 = 1$ (when $r_t = 0$ — perfect memory). The value 8 provides a wide dynamic range for the gate. Smaller values of $c$ would compress this range, reducing the gate's ability to distinguish between "remember" and "forget" regimes.

**Why diagonal and real-valued.** The recurrence weight $a$ is a vector, not a matrix — each hidden state dimension $h_t^{(i)}$ depends only on $h_{t-1}^{(i)}$ and $x_t^{(i)}$, not on other dimensions. This makes the recurrence element-wise and extremely cheap: the state update requires only $O(D_{RNN})$ operations per time step rather than $O(D_{RNN}^2)$ for a dense recurrence. The paper explicitly chooses **real-valued** recurrences over complex-valued ones, stating: "while using complex recurrences would lead to a more expressive layer ... we found that complex recurrences were not beneficial for language modelling in practice, as also observed by Gu and Dao (2023)." Complex recurrences would allow oscillatory dynamics (the state can rotate in complex space, enabling different types of memory), but the empirical finding is that for language, simple exponential decay with gating is sufficient.

**Why the $\sqrt{1 - a_t^2}$ coefficient.** This form, inherited from the LRU, ensures that the recurrence is **norm-preserving** when $|h_{t-1}| = |i_t \odot x_t|$: by the Pythagorean identity, if two vectors have equal norm and are combined with coefficients $\alpha$ and $\sqrt{1 - \alpha^2}$, the resulting vector has the same norm. This prevents the hidden state from either exploding or vanishing over long sequences, a stability property that is critical for capturing long-range dependencies. An alternative like $h_t = a_t h_{t-1} + (1 - a_t)(i_t \odot x_t)$ would be a simple exponential moving average, but it does not have this norm-preservation property and would cause the state to shrink whenever $a_t < 1$, which is always the case.

**Parameter count.** The RG-LRU has weight matrices $W_a$ and $W_x$, each of size $D_{RNN} \times D_{RNN}$, bias vectors $b_a$ and $b_x$ of size $D_{RNN}$, and the learnable parameter $\Lambda$ of size $D_{RNN}$. However, for efficient distributed training (Section 4.1), the paper uses **block-diagonal** weight matrices with 16 blocks, reducing the effective parameter count to $D_{RNN}^2 / 16$ per matrix. With $D_{RNN} \approx 4D/3$ (where $D$ is the model dimension), the total parameters in the RG-LRU are approximately $2 \times (4D/3)^2 / 16 + 3 \times (4D/3) = 2D^2/9 + 4D$, which is small relative to the MLP blocks.

#### Recurrent Block: The Attention Replacement

The RG-LRU does not operate in isolation. It is embedded within a **recurrent block** (Figure 2c in the paper) that provides input projections, a short temporal convolution, and output gating. This block is designed to be a drop-in replacement for the Multi-Query Attention (MQA) block in the residual backbone.

**Block structure.** The recurrent block takes an input of dimension $D$ and processes it through two parallel branches:

**Branch 1 (the "recurrent branch"):**
1. A linear layer projects the input from dimension $D$ to dimension $D_{RNN}$.
2. A small **separable Conv1D layer** with a temporal filter size of 4 is applied. The paper notes this has "just $4 D_{RNN}$ parameters" — it is lightweight by design. The convolution mixes information across 4 consecutive time steps before the recurrence, inspired by the Shift-SSM in H3 (Dao et al., 2022b). This provides a small amount of local context to help the recurrence.
3. The output of the Conv1D is passed through the **RG-LRU layer** described above.

**Branch 2 (the "gating branch"):**
1. A separate linear layer projects the input from dimension $D$ to dimension $D_{RNN}$.
2. A **GeLU non-linearity** is applied.

The two branches are then **merged by element-wise multiplication**: the gating branch's GeLU output modulates the recurrent branch's RG-LRU output, similar to how the output gate works in an LSTM or how Gated Linear Units (GLU) work. After this gating, a final linear layer projects the $D_{RNN}$-dimensional activations back to dimension $D$.

**Why two branches.** The gating branch acts as a learned filter that can suppress or amplify different dimensions of the recurrent output based on the current input. This is distinct from the RG-LRU's internal gating: the internal gates ($r_t$, $i_t$) control how the hidden state is updated, while the external gating branch controls which aspects of the final recurrent output are passed forward to the residual stream. The paper does not ablate this design choice, but it follows the pattern established by GSS (Mehta et al., 2022) and Mamba (Gu and Dao, 2023).

**Width expansion.** The paper sets $D_{RNN} \approx 4D/3$, expanding the recurrent block's internal dimension by a factor of roughly 1.33× relative to the model dimension. The stated reason is "to roughly match the parameter count of a MHA block when both use the same model dimension $D$." Since the MQA Transformer baseline uses fewer parameters than MHA (by sharing key-value heads), this means the recurrent block has slightly more parameters than the MQA block it replaces — a design choice that slightly favors the recurrent models in parameter-matched comparisons, which the paper acknowledges.

**The Conv1D detail.** The temporal convolution uses a kernel size of 4 and is "separable," meaning it operates independently on each channel — there is no cross-channel mixing. For a filter width of 4, the convolution maintains a state of the 3 previous inputs per channel (storing $3 D_{RNN}$ values), which is "substantially smaller than parameter sizes" (Appendix F.4.2). This convolution provides a small amount of local temporal smoothing and is computationally negligible compared to the RG-LRU.

#### Gated MLP Block

All models in this paper — MQA Transformer, Hawk, and Griffin — share the same **gated MLP block** design (Figure 2b). This block processes each position independently (no temporal mixing) and follows the gated linear unit pattern.

**Structure.** Given an input $x \in \mathbb{R}^D$:

1. Two parallel linear layers project $x$ to dimension $M D$, where $M$ is the expansion factor. The paper uses $M = 3$ for all models, which is slightly smaller than the $M = 4$ used in many Transformer implementations.

2. One branch passes through a **GeLU non-linearity** (Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2016), defined as $\text{GeLU}(z) = z \cdot \Phi(z)$ where $\Phi$ is the standard Gaussian CDF. The other branch remains linear.

3. The two branches are merged by **element-wise multiplication**. The paper calls this combination "GeGeLU" following Shazeer (2020), though it differs from the original GeGeLU by applying the final linear projection after the merge rather than before.

4. A final linear layer projects the $M D$-dimensional merged activations back to dimension $D$.

**Why gated.** The element-wise multiplication of the two branches acts as a learned gate: the linear branch provides a "value" signal, and the GeLU branch provides a "filter" that can suppress or pass through different dimensions. This is a standard design in modern LLMs (used in LLaMA, Chinchilla, Palm) and the paper inherits it without modification.

**Why $M = 3$.** The expansion factor of 3, combined with the gated design (which creates two branches of width $3D$, merged to $3D$, then projected back to $D$), gives the MLP block approximately $2 \times D \times 3D + 3D \times D = 9D^2$ parameters. The paper does not discuss why $M = 3$ specifically, but it is consistent across all model scales and architectures.

#### Residual Block and Overall Backbone

The residual block (Figure 2a) defines the macro-structure within which temporal-mixing and MLP blocks are organized. The design follows the **pre-norm** Transformer pattern (Xiong et al., 2020), where normalization is applied before each sub-block rather than after.

**Structure of one residual block.** Given input $x \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times D}$ (a sequence of length $T$ with model dimension $D$):

1. **Temporal-mixing sub-block**: 
   - Apply RMSNorm to $x$.
   - Pass through the temporal-mixing block (global MQA, local MQA, or recurrent block, depending on the model).
   - Add the output to $x$ (residual connection). Let the result be $x'$.

2. **MLP sub-block**:
   - Apply RMSNorm to $x'$.
   - Pass through the gated MLP block.
   - Add the output to $x'$ (residual connection). This is the output of the residual block.

**RMSNorm.** The paper uses Root Mean Square Layer Normalization (Zhang and Sennrich, 2019), which normalizes by the root-mean-square of the activations rather than by mean and variance:

$$\text{RMSNorm}(x) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^D x_i^2 + \epsilon}} \odot \gamma$$

where $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is a learnable scale parameter and $\epsilon$ is a small constant for numerical stability. RMSNorm is computationally cheaper than LayerNorm because it skips the mean subtraction, and it has become standard in modern LLMs (LLaMA, Chinchilla, Mistral).

#### The Three Model Architectures: Assembly and Differentiation

With the building blocks defined, the paper assembles three distinct model families:

**MQA Transformer Baseline.** This model uses the residual backbone with **global Multi-Query Attention** as the temporal-mixing block. In MQA (Shazeer, 2019), all query heads share a single key and value head. Specifically, for a model with $H$ query heads and head dimension $d_{head} = 128$, the queries are computed as $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times H \times 128}$, while keys and values are computed as $K \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 1 \times 128}$ and $V \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 1 \times 128}$. The attention computation for each query head $h$ is:

$$\text{Attention}_h(Q_h, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q_h K^T}{\sqrt{128}} + \text{RoPE}\right) V$$

where RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding, Su et al., 2021) is applied to the queries and keys to provide relative positional information. The paper does not use any absolute positional embeddings. The number of query heads $H$ is set such that $H \times 128 = D$, meaning the model dimension $D$ must be a multiple of 128. MQA reduces the KV cache size by a factor of $H$ compared to standard Multi-Head Attention (MHA), which is why it is used as the baseline rather than MHA — it represents the most efficient practical Transformer variant for inference.

**Hawk.** The pure recurrent model. Hawk replaces every global MQA block with the **recurrent block** described above. All other aspects — the residual backbone, the gated MLP block, RMSNorm — remain identical to the MQA baseline. Hawk is designed to demonstrate that a pure recurrent architecture (with no attention at all) can be competitive with Transformers at scale, provided the recurrence is properly designed (gated, diagonal, initialized correctly).

**Griffin.** The hybrid model. Griffin uses a **layered pattern** of temporal-mixing blocks: two residual blocks with recurrent blocks are followed by **one residual block with local (sliding-window) MQA attention**. This pattern of 2 recurrent : 1 local attention is repeated throughout the depth of the model. The local attention window size is fixed to **1024 tokens** for training sequence lengths of 2048, meaning each position attends to at most the 1024 previous tokens (including itself). Local attention uses the same MQA structure as the baseline, just with a causal mask that limits attention to a fixed window. Importantly, local attention shares the key property of recurrent blocks: its **cache size is bounded** by the window size rather than growing with sequence length, so Griffin maintains the fixed-state inference advantage of Hawk while gaining the precise local modeling capability of attention.

**Why the 2:1 pattern.** The paper does not provide extensive ablations of different ratios, but the rationale (Section 3) is that "local attention accurately models the recent past, while the recurrent layers can transmit information across long sequences." The 2:1 ratio means Griffin has more recurrent than attention layers, keeping the model "mostly recurrent" for inference efficiency while providing enough attention layers to handle tasks requiring precise token-level retrieval within the local window. The single-attention-layer-per-three-blocks pattern ensures that at least one attention layer exists within every three-block span, providing regular opportunities for the model to "look back" precisely.

**Tied embeddings.** The paper uses weight tying between the input embedding layer and the final output projection (unembedding) layer. This reduces the total parameter count and is standard practice in many LLMs.

#### Hyperparameter Scaling Across Model Sizes

The paper defines a family of models from 100M to 14B parameters by scaling four key hyperparameters: model dimension $D$, RNN width $D_{RNN}$, depth $N$, and number of attention heads (for Transformer-based variants). The specific configurations are provided in Appendix C, Table 2:

| 100M | 768 | 1024 | 12 | 3 | 6 | 1.9B |
| 200M | 1024 | 1536 | 12 | 3 | 8 | 3.9B |
| 400M | 1536 | 2048 | 12 | 3 | 12 | 7.8B |
| 1.3B | 2048 | 2560 | 24 | 3 | 16 | 25B |

| 14B | 5120 | 8192 | 40 | 3 | 40 | 300B |

Several patterns are worth noting:

- **RNN width ratio**: $D_{RNN}$ is consistently set to approximately $4D/3$ (e.g., 1024/768 ≈ 1.33, 5632/4096 ≈ 1.375). This is the expansion factor that "roughly matches the parameter count of a MHA block."

- **Depth scaling**: Depth $N$ increases modestly with model size (from 12 at 100M to 40 at 14B), while width $D$ grows more aggressively (from 768 to 5120). This follows the pattern observed in Transformers where width scaling tends to be more parameter-efficient than depth scaling beyond a certain point.

- **Training tokens**: Models are trained with a roughly Chinchilla-optimal ratio of tokens to parameters (approximately 20 tokens per parameter), except for the downstream evaluation runs where all models are trained on exactly 300B tokens regardless of size (the "overtraining" regime).

- **Fixed expansion factor**: The MLP expansion factor $M = 3$ is constant across all scales, meaning the paper did not explore scaling this dimension.

- **Head dimension fixed**: $d_{head} = 128$ is constant, and the number of heads $H$ is set as $H = D / 128$. This means the 100M model has 6 heads while the 14B model has 40 heads.

#### Initialization Strategy

The paper is specific about initialization because "unlike many recent works in the SSM literature, the RG-LRU does not use initialization inspired by the theory of orthogonal polynomials" (Section 2.4). Instead:

- **Gate weight matrices $W_a$ and $W_x$**: Initialized using **LeCun initialization** (LeCun et al., 2002), which samples weights from a zero-mean Gaussian with variance $1/\text{fan\_in}$, designed to preserve variance through layers with tanh-like activations.

- **Recurrent weight $a$**: Parameterized as $a = \sigma(\Lambda)$ where $\Lambda$ is a learnable parameter. $\Lambda$ is initialized such that $a^c$ (with $c = 8$) is **uniformly distributed between 0.9 and 0.999 at the start of training**, similar to Orvieto et al. (2023b). This means the effective recurrent weight $a^8$ starts close to 1 for all dimensions, giving the model a strong initial bias toward preserving information over long distances. This is analogous to initializing the forget gate of an LSTM to be close to 1, which is known to help with long-range gradient propagation.

- **No orthogonal polynomial or HIPPO initialization**: Unlike S4 (Gu et al., 2021a) and its descendants, the RG-LRU does not use specially constructed initializations derived from the theory of orthogonal polynomials or continuous-time state-space systems. The paper's finding that simple uniform initialization near 1 works well for language is a practical simplification.

#### Distinguishing the RG-LRU Gate from Mamba's Selection Mechanism

A key claim of the paper (Section 2.4, Appendix A) is that the RG-LRU's recurrence gate $r_t$ is fundamentally different from Mamba's selection mechanism. To substantiate this, the paper provides a unified analysis framework.

**General recurrence form.** The paper expresses any gated recurrence as:

$$h_t = \alpha(r_t) h_{t-1} + \beta(r_t) x_t$$

where $\alpha(r_t)$ controls how much of the old state is retained and $\beta(r_t)$ controls how much of the input is incorporated.

**Mamba's mechanism** (in this simplified form, assuming $B = 1$, $C = 1$ in Mamba's notation): $\alpha(r_t) = (1 - r_t)^{-A}$ and $\beta(r_t) = (1 - \alpha(r_t)) / A$. For $A$ close to 1, this becomes $\alpha(r_t) \approx 1 - r_t$ and $\beta(r_t) \approx r_t$, which is "almost identical to the GRU" (Appendix A). The key property is that when $r_t = 1$, $\alpha = 0$ and $\beta = 1$ — the model can **fully discard** the previous history and replace it with the input.

**RG-LRU mechanism**: $\alpha(r_t) = a^{c r_t}$ and $\beta(r_t) = \sqrt{1 - (a^{c r_t})^2}$. Because $a < 1$ by construction, $a^{c r_t}$ can approach but never exactly reach 0. The previous state is always retained to some degree. Furthermore, the relationship $\beta = \sqrt{1 - \alpha^2}$ (rather than $\beta = 1 - \alpha$) means the input contribution is non-linearly related to the retention coefficient — when $\alpha$ is large (near 1), $\beta$ is very small (near 0), meaning the input is strongly suppressed when the model chooses to retain history.

**The paper's interpretation.** This asymmetry — the gate can strongly suppress input but cannot fully discard history — is hypothesized to enable "super-exponential memory." The idea is that during uninformative tokens (e.g., function words, punctuation), the model can set $r_t \to 0$, making $a_t \to 1$ and effectively "freezing" the hidden state, preventing the exponential decay that would otherwise erase information from much earlier in the sequence. This is depicted in Figure 7 of the paper, which plots $\alpha$ vs. $\beta$ for different gating mechanisms at different base decay rates $a$.

#### Training Hyperparameters and Scaling Rules

The paper uses the **AdamW optimizer** (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) for all experiments. Rather than using fixed hyperparameters, the authors tune learning rate, weight decay, and $\beta_2$ on small models and then derive scaling rules to predict optimal values for larger models.

**Scaling rule approach.** For each hyperparameter, the optimal value is measured on the smaller models (100M to 1.3B parameters) and a power-law relationship with model size is fit. The predicted values for 7B and 14B models are then used without further tuning. This is a practical strategy that avoids the expense of hyperparameter sweeps at larger scales.

**Sequence length.** The primary training uses a sequence length of **2048 tokens**. Section 6 also explores training on sequence length 8192 to study long-context learning, with the batch size reduced proportionally to keep total tokens per batch constant.

**Dataset.** All models are trained on the **MassiveText dataset** (Hoffmann et al., 2022), which was previously used to train Gopher and Chinchilla. The paper notes they "use a slightly different data subset distribution" from Hoffmann et al. (2022) but does not specify the exact distribution. The dataset consists of web text, books, news articles, and code.

**Overtraining for downstream evaluation.** While the scaling curve experiments (Figure 1a) train models with Chinchilla-optimal token budgets (tokens proportional to parameters), the downstream evaluation in Section 3.2 trains all models on exactly **300B tokens** regardless of size. This means the 1B model is heavily overtrained (300× its parameter count), while the 14B model is roughly Chinchilla-optimal (~20×). This "overtraining" regime has become standard since LLaMA (Touvron et al., 2023) and Chinchilla, and it provides a fair basis for comparing models that were trained on different token budgets (e.g., Mamba on 600B, Llama-2 on 2T).

#### Model Parallelism for Large-Scale Training

Section 4.1 addresses the practical challenge of training models too large to fit on a single TPU-v3 device. The paper's key insight is that the recurrent block can be sharded with the **same communication pattern as the MLP block**, making distributed training of recurrent models no more expensive than Transformers.

**Megatron-style sharding for linear layers.** The linear layers in the MLP block, attention block, and recurrent block (the input projections to the two branches and the output projection) are sharded using Megatron-style model parallelism (Shoeybi et al., 2019). This shards the weight matrices column-wise across devices, requiring a single all-reduce operation in the forward pass and another in the backward pass to aggregate partial results. This is the standard approach for Transformers and applies identically here.

**Block-diagonal gate weights.** The key optimization for the RG-LRU is to use **block-diagonal** weight matrices for the gate projections $W_a$ and $W_x$ rather than dense matrices. The paper explains: "To avoid additional cross-device communication, we use block-diagonal weights for the gates in the RG-LRU ... instead of dense matrices." With 16 blocks, each $W$ matrix has $D_{RNN}^2 / 16$ parameters instead of $D_{RNN}^2$. Each block can be placed on a different device, and since there is no cross-block interaction in the matrix-vector multiplication (by block-diagonal structure), no communication is needed for the gate computations.

**Conv1D and diagonal recurrence sharding.** The Conv1D layer "operates independently across channels, enabling us to split its parameters across devices without incurring any communication overhead." Similarly, "the diagonal structure of the recurrence offers the same advantage ... allowing parameter sharding and computation without any communication." Both operations are element-wise or channel-wise, meaning they can be trivially parallelized across the hidden dimension with zero communication.

**Overall communication equivalence.** The paper states: "With this strategy, the recurrent block's communication requirements are equivalent to those of the MLP block." This is critical because it means replacing attention with recurrence does not increase the communication burden in distributed training — it requires only the same all-reduce operations that the MLP and attention blocks already need.

**ZeRO parallelism.** In addition to model parallelism, the paper uses ZeRO (Rajbhandari et al., 2020) to shard optimizer states and model parameters across the batch dimension. This is standard for large-scale training and not specific to the recurrent architecture.

**bfloat16 precision.** Model parameters and activations use bfloat16 representation to minimize memory usage and data transfer overhead. The paper does not specify whether any operations use higher precision internally.

#### Efficient Linear Recurrences on TPU-v3

Section 4.2 addresses what the paper identifies as the primary engineering challenge: the RG-LRU's update equation is **memory-bound** on modern accelerators, and naive implementations waste most of their time waiting for memory transfers rather than computing.

**The memory-bound nature of diagonal RNNs.** For each element update in Equation 4, the computation requires:
- Loading 6 bytes from HBM: $h_{t-1}$ (2 bytes in bfloat16), $a_t$ (2 bytes), $x_t$ (2 bytes).
- Storing 2 bytes: the updated $h_t$ (2 bytes).
- Executing approximately 6 FLOPs: the multiplications, squaring, square root, and addition.

This gives a **FLOPs-to-byte ratio of $6 / 8 = 0.75$**, which is significantly below the TPU-v3's VPU (Vector Processing Unit) capacity of **4.2 FLOPs/byte** for element-wise operations (Table 3). The paper explains: "Execution time is therefore dominated by memory transfers between HBM and VMEM, making the computation memory bound."

**Why not use convolutions.** Some linear recurrences (those with time-invariant $a$) can be computed as a convolution with an exponentially decaying kernel, which is highly parallelizable. However, the RG-LRU's gating introduces time-dependence: $a_t = a^{c r_t}$ depends on the input at each time step. This "is not compatible with the convolutional view" because the convolution kernel would need to change at every position.

**Why not use the associative scan.** Diagonal linear recurrences are associative, meaning they can be computed with a parallel prefix-sum (scan) algorithm. In principle, an associative scan with $O(\log T)$ depth can compute the entire recurrence in parallel. However, the paper observed empirically that "on a TPU-v3 the associative scan is significantly slower than the native Jax linear scan." The paper speculates: "the random access nature of the tree recombination of the parallel prefix-sum algorithm makes it poorly suited for the TPU architecture, leading to even slower memory transfers."

The associative scan works by building a binary tree over the sequence, computing partial recurrence products at each node, and then combining them. This tree structure requires non-contiguous memory accesses (reading from different positions at different levels of the tree), which is inefficient on TPUs optimized for dense, contiguous memory operations. The paper's measurement (Figure 8a) shows the associative scan (even in bfloat16) running up to 50% slower than the naive sequential JAX implementation.

**The custom Pallas kernel.** The solution is a custom **Pallas** kernel (Bradbury et al., 2018, Google's low-level TPU programming framework) that computes the recurrence as a **linear scan** — a sequential loop over time steps — but with two critical optimizations:

1. **Keep the hidden state in VMEM**: By performing the entire recurrence computation for a chunk of the hidden dimension within a single kernel invocation, the hidden state never needs to be written to HBM and read back. It stays in the fast vector memory (VMEM) for the entire scan over the sequence length.

2. **Memory transfers in larger chunks**: Instead of transferring one element at a time (which would be maximally inefficient due to memory access granularity), the kernel transfers larger contiguous chunks, amortizing the memory latency.

The paper reports that this custom kernel achieves "almost 3x speed up over the native Jax implementation of the linear scan" (Section 4.2) and "10-20% lower training times per step of the full Hawk model" (Appendix D.2). Figure 8a quantifies this across sequence lengths from 0 to 16K tokens, showing the Pallas linear scan consistently below 20ms at all lengths while the JAX native scan and both associative scan variants grow toward 40–60ms.

**Why the scan is still necessary.** Even though the recurrence is computed sequentially for each hidden dimension chunk, the custom kernel processes different chunks in parallel across different TPU cores. The key insight is that the recurrence is embarrassingly parallel across the hidden dimension (since it is diagonal) — it is only sequential across time. The Pallas kernel exploits this by parallelizing across the batch and hidden dimensions while performing a sequential scan over time within each chunk.

**Scaling of the training speed advantage.** The paper notes (Section 4.3) that the training speed advantage of recurrent models over Transformers depends on the ratio of sequence length to model width: "as we increase the model width $D$ compared to the sequence length $T$, the linear layers become the primary computational bottleneck, minimizing the efficiency gains from the RNN block." This is because the MLP and linear projection layers scale as $O(T D^2)$ while the RG-LRU scales as $O(T D)$ and attention scales as $O(T^2 D)$. For large $D$ relative to $T$, the $D^2$ terms dominate regardless of the temporal-mixing mechanism. The practical implication is that Griffin's training speed advantage is most pronounced for long sequences and moderate model widths — a regime that is increasingly common as context length demands grow.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Recurrence Gate as a Memory Preservation Mechanism, Not a Forgetting Mechanism

The field's dominant approach to gating in RNNs — from LSTMs through GRUs to Mamba's selection mechanism — has been built on a shared conceptual premise: gates exist to allow the model to **discard** information. The LSTM forget gate ($f_t$) multiplies the cell state to erase components. The GRU update gate ($z_t$) interpolates between the old state and a candidate, giving the model the option to fully replace history with new input ($z_t = 1 \implies h_t = \tilde{h}_t$). Mamba's selection mechanism, as the paper demonstrates in Appendix A, "is almost identical to the GRU" in its gating behavior: when fully activated, it can zero out the contribution of the previous state entirely. The conceptual default in the literature is that gating equals **controlled forgetting** — the model decides what to discard and what to replace it with.

The RG-LRU's recurrence gate $r_t$ inverts this conceptual framework. This is not a small refinement of existing gating; it is a fundamentally different mathematical operation with a different design philosophy. The gate $r_t$ modulates the effective recurrent weight $a_t = a^{c r_t}$ in the exponent, but because $a = \sigma(\Lambda) \in (0,1)$ by construction (enforced by the sigmoid parameterization of the learnable parameter $\Lambda$), $a_t$ can never reach zero. When $r_t \to 0$, $a_t \to 1$, and the coefficient on the input term $\sqrt{1 - a_t^2} \to 0$. The gate's primary function is therefore not to **erase** the past — it literally cannot do this — but to **freeze** the state and **reject** the input. The paper makes this explicit in Section 2.4:

> "our recurrence gate can approximately interpolate between the standard LRU update ... and the previous hidden state, which allows it to effectively discard the input and preserve all information from the previous history"

This is a gate for **memory preservation**, not forgetting. The asymmetry is important: the gate can strongly suppress the input's influence (making $\beta(r_t) \approx 0$ when $r_t \approx 0$) but cannot fully suppress the past. In a GRU or Mamba, the gate is symmetric — it can fully suppress either the past or the input, depending on $r_t$.

**Why this matters conceptually.** The paper hypothesizes that the gate's role is to "enable the model to achieve super-exponential memory by reducing the influence of uninformative inputs" (Section 2.4). The intuition is that language contains many low-information tokens — function words, punctuation, boilerplate phrases — that should not perturb the model's internal representation of important content. A standard exponential decay (as in the ungated LRU) would cause the hidden state to decay toward zero during these tokens, gradually erasing distant information. The RG-LRU's gate allows the model to detect uninformative tokens (via the learned projection $W_a x_t + b_a$) and respond by freezing the state, preventing decay. This is "super-exponential" in the sense that the effective decay rate becomes input-dependent: near-zero during informative tokens (allowing normal update), near-one during uninformative tokens (preserving memory).

This framing is conceptually novel relative to the SSM literature. Prior work on linear recurrences (LRU, S4, S5) treated the recurrent weight $a$ as a fixed, learnable parameter — the decay rate was a property of the model architecture, not a function of the input. Mamba made the SSM input-dependent, but through a selection mechanism that fundamentally interpolates between keeping and replacing. The RG-LRU's gate is the first, to my knowledge, explicitly designed around the idea that an RNN's primary gating challenge is **not** deciding what to forget but deciding when to **stop updating** — when to treat the input as noise relative to the task and preserve the state.

**Evidence for the claim.** The paper provides two kinds of support. First, the analytical comparison in Appendix A (Figure 7) shows visually how the RG-LRU's gating trajectory ($\alpha$ vs. $\beta$) differs from GRU/Mamba-style gating — the RG-LRU curve hugs the upper-left region (high retention, low input influence) much more closely, especially for larger base $a$ values. Second, the empirical results in Section 6 provide indirect evidence: Hawk extrapolates to sequences 4× longer than training length without degradation (Figure 5), and on the Induction Heads task, Hawk can "perfectly extrapolate ... to evaluation sequences several orders of magnitude longer than the training sequence length" (Section 6.2). While these results don't isolate the gate's contribution specifically, they demonstrate that the architecture as a whole achieves long-range memory that exceeds what would be expected from a fixed-decay linear recurrence.

**Is this fundamental or incremental?** This is a **moderately fundamental** conceptual contribution. The mathematical form of the gate (modulating an exponent rather than computing an interpolation weight) is a relatively small change to the recurrence equation. But the conceptual reframing it enables — gating as input rejection rather than history erasure — is genuinely novel and has implications for how we think about memory in recurrent architectures. It suggests that future work on gated recurrences should consider asymmetries in the gate's ability to suppress past vs. present, rather than defaulting to the symmetric interpolation pattern inherited from GRUs.

### Innovation 2: Diagonal Recurrences Can Be Made Hardware-Efficient Through Custom Kernels, Not Algorithmic Parallelism

The dominant narrative in the SSM and linear RNN literature has been that **algorithmic parallelizability** is the key to training efficiency. This narrative drove the development of S4's convolutional mode (Gu et al., 2021a), the associative scan in S5 (Smith et al., 2022), and the parallel scan in Mamba (Gu and Dao, 2023). The shared assumption is roughly: recurrences are sequential by nature; sequential operations are slow on GPUs/TPUs; therefore we must find a parallel algorithm (convolution, prefix-sum) to make them fast. This assumption shaped not just implementations but also model design — architectures were constrained to forms that admitted parallel computation (e.g., linear time-invariant recurrences for convolution, or associative operations for scanning).

This paper presents a sharp empirical counter-narrative: **on real hardware, the bottleneck is memory bandwidth, not sequential computation, and parallel algorithms can make things worse**. The evidence is concrete and hardware-specific (TPU-v3), but the implications are conceptual.

The paper's analysis in Section 4.2 identifies the core issue: the RG-LRU update equation has a FLOPs-to-byte ratio of 0.75 on TPU-v3, which is far below the device's VPU capacity of 4.2 FLOPs/byte for element-wise operations (Table 3). This means the computation is **memory-bound** — execution time is dominated by data transfer between HBM and VMEM, not by arithmetic. In this regime, the goal of optimization should be to minimize memory transfers, not to reduce FLOPs.

The associative scan — the theoretically preferred parallel algorithm — reduces FLOPs but does not reduce memory transfers. In fact, the paper observes it is "significantly slower than the native Jax linear scan" on TPU-v3 and speculates that "the random access nature of the tree recombination of the parallel prefix-sum algorithm makes it poorly suited for the TPU architecture, leading to even slower memory transfers." The tree-based access pattern of the parallel scan requires jumping between non-contiguous memory locations, which is maximally inefficient on hardware optimized for dense, sequential memory access. The parallel algorithm trades reduced FLOPs for worse memory access patterns — and in a memory-bound regime, that is a losing trade.

**The counter-intuitive solution.** Instead of seeking more parallelism, the paper's Pallas kernel embraces sequential computation (a linear scan over time) and optimizes the memory access pattern: keeping the hidden state in VMEM for the entire scan, and performing memory transfers in larger contiguous chunks rather than one element at a time. This achieves "almost 3x speed up over the native Jax implementation of the linear scan" (Section 4.2) and "10-20% lower training times per step of the full Hawk model" (Appendix D.2). Figure 8a shows the Pallas linear scan running consistently below 20ms at all sequence lengths from 0 to 16K, while the JAX native scan and both associative scan variants grow toward 40–60ms.

**Why this matters conceptually.** This finding shifts the optimization target for recurrent architectures from **algorithm design** (finding more parallel algorithms) to **memory management** (reducing data movement). It implies that the research community's focus on parallel scan algorithms — while mathematically elegant — may be optimizing the wrong thing, at least on current hardware generations. The paper's explicit statement that "the conclusions drawn here do not necessarily apply to other accelerators" (Section 4.2, footnote) adds nuance: this is not a universal claim about all hardware, but a specific finding that on TPU-v3, and likely on other accelerators with similar memory hierarchies, sequential execution with good memory locality beats parallel execution with poor memory locality for this workload.

This insight also **decouples model design from training algorithm design**. If associative scan efficiency was required for training, models would need to restrict their recurrences to associative operations. The demonstration that a custom sequential kernel can be faster removes this constraint, allowing model designers to use non-associative recurrences (like the input-dependent $a_t = a^{c r_t}$ in the RG-LRU, which is not compatible with the convolutional view and makes the associative scan more complex) without sacrificing training speed.

**Is this fundamental or incremental?** On its face, this is an **engineering contribution** — a custom kernel for a specific hardware platform. But the conceptual implication is **fundamentally important**: it inverts the prevailing optimization paradigm from "make it parallel" to "make it memory-efficient," and it demonstrates concretely that the theoretical FLOPs advantage of parallel algorithms does not translate to wall-clock speed when the bottleneck is elsewhere. This is a lesson that applies broadly beyond this specific model or hardware platform.

### Innovation 3: Hybrid Recurrence-Attention Architecture as a Resolution to the Copying-Retrieval Tension

A critical tension in the recurrent model literature was crystallized by Jelassi et al. (2024): Transformers are significantly more efficient than SSMs at learning tasks that require **exact token-level copying or retrieval** from context. The reason is structural. A recurrent model with a fixed-size state must compress all relevant information from the context into that state. For tasks requiring verbatim reproduction of specific tokens (e.g., "what was the phone number for Alice?"), compression is lossy — there is no guarantee the exact token identity survives the compression process, especially for long contexts where many tokens compete for the limited state capacity. A Transformer with attention, by contrast, can directly attend to the original token positions and copy tokens verbatim without compression.

Jelassi et al. demonstrated this limitation empirically for Mamba on the phonebook lookup task, and the paper replicates the finding for Hawk (Figure 6c): "while Hawk can do reasonably well on the task for very short phonebook lengths, it fails to memorize and retrieve the correct phone number when the phonebook length grows." This is an existential problem for pure recurrent models — it suggests they are fundamentally incapable of certain types of precise retrieval that are essential for practical language tasks (instruction following, factual QA, code generation).

Griffin's hybrid architecture — two recurrent blocks followed by one local attention block, repeated — represents a **conceptual resolution** to this tension that is more nuanced than simply "add attention to fix the problem." The innovation is in the **specific division of labor** between the two mechanisms:

- **Recurrent layers** compress the long-range context into a fixed-size state that can propagate information across arbitrary distances. They handle the "global context" — themes, topics, narrative structure, long-range dependencies that don't require verbatim token recall.

- **Local attention layers** provide precise, lossless access to the most recent $W$ tokens (window size 1024). They handle the "local precision" — exact copying, recent entity tracking, syntactic agreement, and other tasks where token identity matters.

The key insight is that **most language tasks don't require global verbatim recall** — they require verbatim recall of recently mentioned entities (within the attention window) and semantic understanding of the broader context (handled by the recurrence). By restricting attention to a fixed window, Griffin gets the inference efficiency benefits of a fixed-size state (the KV cache doesn't grow beyond $W$ tokens) while retaining the precise local modeling that Transformers excel at.

**Evidence that this division of labor works.** The Selective Copying task (Figure 6a) provides the cleanest evidence. In this task, the model must copy data tokens from arbitrary positions in a 1024-token sequence while ignoring noise tokens. Hawk learns the task but is "significantly slower than Transformers" — the recurrent state struggles to memorize the exact locations and identities of data tokens scattered across the sequence. Griffin, with just one local attention layer (in the third of five blocks), "shows almost no slowdown, effectively matching the speed of learning of Transformers." A single attention layer with window size 512 is sufficient to handle the copying, while the recurrent layers presumably handle the noise-filtering and sequence structure.

The phonebook lookup evaluation (Figure 6c) shows the boundary of this approach: Griffin can "perfectly solve this task up to a context length that matches its local attention window size of 1024" — when the entire phonebook fits in the window, attention can do the retrieval directly. Beyond 1024 tokens, "performance starts to degrade" as the relevant entry falls outside the window and must be retrieved from the recurrent state, which is lossy. This degradation is graceful, not catastrophic, and Griffin "is also able to extrapolate better to longer sequence lengths compared to Transformers" — the recurrent layers provide a fallback mechanism that Transformers lack when their positional encodings fail to generalize.

**Why this is more than an incremental combination.** The division of labor between recurrence and attention is not a new idea — models like Longformer (Beltagy et al., 2020) and Mistral (Jiang et al., 2023) use local attention, and the idea of combining recurrence with attention goes back to early hybrid architectures. What distinguishes Griffin is the **empirical validation of a specific hypothesis**: that a small number of local attention layers (one per three residual blocks) is sufficient to resolve the copying-retrieval bottleneck that plagues pure recurrent models, while keeping the architecture "mostly recurrent" for inference efficiency. This is a quantitative claim about the required ratio of attention to recurrence layers, not just a qualitative argument for combining them.

The finding that a single local attention layer with window size 1024, placed in every third block, is sufficient to match Transformers on copying tasks while maintaining 14.8× throughput advantage (Figure 1b) is non-obvious — one might have expected that more frequent attention or larger windows would be necessary. The paper's window size ablation (Appendix E, Figure 9) further supports this: "even when using a fixed window size of 1024 for the local attention layers in Griffin, it outperforms the global attention MQA Transformer baseline across all sequence lengths tested" (up to 8K).

**Is this fundamental or incremental?** The concept of hybrid architectures is incremental — it builds directly on prior work in local attention and SSMs. But the paper's contribution is the **diagnostic framing** of the copying-retrieval problem as the key limitation of pure recurrences, and the **empirical demonstration** that a specific, lightweight hybrid design resolves it without sacrificing the inference efficiency that motivates recurrence in the first place. This makes it a **practically important** contribution that validates a design philosophy for future work.

### Innovation 4: Power-Law Scaling of Recurrent Language Models Validates Architecture-Agnostic Scaling Laws

Prior to this work, the dominant evidence for power-law scaling between held-out loss and training compute came from Transformer-based models (Kaplan et al., 2020; Brown et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). The Chinchilla scaling laws, which are now the standard reference for optimal training compute allocation, were derived exclusively from Transformer experiments. It was an open question whether recurrent architectures — with their fundamentally different computational structure (sequential recurrence vs. parallel attention) and different inductive biases (fixed-size state vs. growing context window) — would exhibit the same scaling behavior at comparable model sizes. One could imagine that recurrences might scale differently: perhaps the fixed-size state becomes a bottleneck at scale, or perhaps gating mechanisms interact with model size in ways that break the power-law relationship.

The paper's scaling curves (Figure 1a) provide the first clear evidence — to my knowledge — that **gated diagonal linear recurrences follow near-identical power-law scaling to Transformers** across a range from 100M to 14B parameters. The three curves (MQA Transformer, Hawk, Griffin) are approximately parallel on the log-log plot, with Hawk slightly above (worse) than the MQA baseline and Griffin slightly below (better). The paper states: "All three model families demonstrate a linear scaling relationship between the validation loss and training FLOPs ... as previously observed for Transformers by Brown et al. (2020)".

This is not a trivial finding. The Chinchilla scaling laws relate loss $L$ to model parameters $N$ and training tokens $D$ as $L(N, D) = E + A/N^\alpha + B/D^\beta$, where the exponents $\alpha$ and $\beta$ characterize how loss improves with scale. If recurrent architectures had different exponents (e.g., saturating faster because the fixed state limits information capacity), they would deviate from the Transformer scaling curve at larger scales. The parallelism of the curves in Figure 1a suggests that the exponents are approximately equal across architectures — at least up to 14B parameters. This is a **validation of architecture-agnostic scaling**: the relationship between compute and loss appears to be determined more by the scale of the model (parameters, data) than by the specific mechanism of temporal mixing.

**Why this matters.** The implication is that the extensive body of research on Transformer scaling laws — optimal hyperparameter transfer, Chinchilla-optimal allocation, predictable scaling — may transfer to recurrent architectures with minimal modification. This lowers the barrier to adopting recurrent models: organizations that have invested in understanding Transformer scaling can apply much of that knowledge to Hawk or Griffin. It also suggests that the fundamental driver of language model performance is **scale** (parameters and data) rather than the specific architecture, which is consistent with the broader "scaling hypothesis" but had not been demonstrated for this class of recurrent models.

**Caveats and boundaries.** The scaling demonstration is limited in two important ways. First, it only goes to 14B parameters — the curves could diverge at larger scales, though the parallelism up to 14B is encouraging. Second, the gap between Hawk and the MQA baseline appears to narrow slightly at larger scales (the Hawk curve is closer to MQA at higher FLOPs budgets), but the paper does not quantify this trend statistically. If Hawk eventually matches or surpasses Transformers at very large scales, that would be an even stronger finding, but the current data only shows approximate parity.

**Is this fundamental or incremental?** This is a **moderately fundamental** empirical contribution. The specific power-law exponents are not derived or theoretically justified — this is purely an observation. But in a field where scaling behavior is often treated as a defining property of Transformers specifically, demonstrating it for a qualitatively different architecture class is conceptually important. It supports the view that scaling laws are a general property of large neural networks trained on natural language, not a special property of self-attention.

### Innovation 5: Overtraining as a Regime Where Recurrent Models Are Especially Data-Efficient

The paper's comparison against external baselines (Mamba, Llama-2) in Section 3.2 uses a specific training regime that has become standard practice since LLaMA (Touvron et al., 2023): **overtraining** — training models on substantially more tokens than Chinchilla-optimal, trading training compute for inference efficiency. Chinchilla-optimal scaling prescribes roughly 20 tokens per parameter; Llama-2 was trained on 2T tokens for models ranging from 7B to 70B parameters (over 100 tokens/parameter for the 7B model). The paper's downstream evaluation trains all models on exactly 300B tokens regardless of size, resulting in ratios from 300× (1B model) to ~20× (14B model).

The striking result is that **Griffin matches Llama-2's downstream performance while being trained on ~7× fewer tokens** (300B vs. 2T). Hawk-3B exceeds Mamba-3B's reported downstream performance while trained on half as many tokens (300B vs. 600B). These comparisons are imperfect — different training data, different hyperparameter tuning — but they are suggestive of a deeper phenomenon: **recurrent models may be more data-efficient than Transformers in the overtraining regime**.

Why might this be? The paper does not provide a causal analysis, but a plausible mechanism relates to the inductive biases of recurrence. Transformers with global attention have effectively infinite memory capacity — every token can attend to every previous token. This capacity is powerful, but it also means the model must learn from data when to use this capacity and when to ignore irrelevant context. In the overtraining regime, where models see many repeated patterns, a Transformer might "waste" capacity on spurious long-range correlations that a recurrent model, with its built-in recency bias (exponential decay of the hidden state), naturally ignores. The recurrent model's structural bias toward local and recency-weighted information may act as a regularizer that improves data efficiency, similar to how CNNs' translation invariance makes them more data-efficient than MLPs for vision tasks.

The evidence for this claim is suggestive but not conclusive. Table 1 shows Griffin-14B achieving an average downstream score of 69.5 vs. Llama-2-13B's 69.3, with Griffin trained on 300B tokens vs. Llama-2's 2T. This is a ~6.7× token efficiency advantage. However, the paper's own MQA Transformer baseline, trained on the same 300B tokens, achieves 64.2 at 6B parameters — the gap between Griffin and the MQA baseline on the same data (65.8 vs. 64.2 at comparable scales) is much smaller than the gap to Llama-2, suggesting that data distribution and hyperparameter tuning may account for a significant fraction of the apparent efficiency advantage.

**Why this matters conceptually, even if the causal mechanism is unclear.** The language model field has largely converged on the view that Transformers are the default architecture, with alternatives needing to prove they can match Transformer performance at scale. This paper's results suggest that the question may need to be inverted: **in the practically relevant overtraining regime, are Transformers the right baseline?** If recurrent models are genuinely more data-efficient (requiring fewer tokens to reach a given downstream performance), then the total cost of ownership — training compute plus inference compute — may favor recurrent architectures even if their per-step training cost is similar. This is a conceptual shift from "can recurrences match Transformers?" to "should recurrences be the default in data-constrained or inference-heavy settings?"

**Is this fundamental or incremental?** This is **suggestive but not definitive**. The paper does not isolate the causal mechanism behind the apparent data efficiency, and the comparison against external baselines with different training data confounds the analysis. Follow-up work that trains Transformers and recurrent models on identical data with matched hyperparameter tuning budgets across multiple overtraining ratios would be needed to establish this as a robust finding. The paper's contribution is to **surface the hypothesis** and provide initial evidence, not to prove it conclusively.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All primary experiments use the **MassiveText dataset** (Hoffmann et al., 2022), the same dataset previously used to train Gopher (Rae et al., 2021) and Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022). The paper notes they "use a slightly different data subset distribution" from Hoffmann et al. (2022) without specifying the exact distribution. For scaling law experiments, training tokens are scaled proportionally to model parameters following Chinchilla-optimal ratios (roughly 20 tokens per parameter), while downstream evaluations train all models on a fixed budget of 300B tokens regardless of size. Long-context evaluations use held-out datasets of books (Figure 5) and arXiv articles (Appendix G, Figure 10) to assess extrapolation beyond training sequence lengths. Synthetic copying and retrieval experiments use randomly generated sequences with controlled vocabulary sizes (16 tokens for Selective Copying and Induction Heads).

- **Base model(s).** The paper defines three model families built from shared components: (1) an **MQA Transformer baseline** using global Multi-Query Attention with Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) and a fixed head dimension of 128, (2) **Hawk**, a pure recurrent model replacing all attention with the recurrent block (Conv1D + RG-LRU), and (3) **Griffin**, a hybrid alternating two recurrent blocks with one local MQA attention block (window size 1024). All models share the same gated MLP block (expansion factor M = 3), pre-norm residual backbone, RMSNorm, and tied embedding weights. Models are scaled from 100M to 14B parameters by varying model dimension D (768 to 5120), RNN width D_RNN (1024 to 8192, approximately 4D/3), and depth N (12 to 40). The MQA baseline uses fewer parameters than MHA by sharing key-value heads across query heads, and the paper notes this means recurrent blocks have "slightly more parameters than the corresponding MQA blocks" (Section 3) since D_RNN ≈ 4D/3 was chosen to match MHA parameter counts, not MQA.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric for scaling experiments is **held-out validation loss** (negative log-likelihood) measured on MassiveText, plotted against total training FLOPs on a log-log scale (Figure 1a). For downstream evaluation (Table 1), the paper reports **character-normalized accuracy** on MMLU, HellaSwag, PIQA, ARC-Easy, and ARC-Challenge, and **absolute accuracy with partial scoring** on WinoGrande, following standard practice in the literature. The average across all six benchmarks is reported as a summary statistic. For inference speed, the paper measures both **latency** (time to sample a specified number of tokens at batch size 16, Figure 4) and **maximum throughput** (tokens per second achievable on a single device when sampling a specified number of tokens, Figure 1b). For long-context evaluation (Figures 5, 10), the paper reports **mean-so-far negative log-likelihood** — the average NLL over all token positions up to the current length, which measures whether longer contexts improve next-token prediction. Synthetic task performance (Figure 6) uses **accuracy** (fraction of sequences where the correct tokens are generated).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against four categories of baselines:
  1. **Internal MQA Transformer baseline**: Trained on the same MassiveText data with the same hyperparameter tuning budget and infrastructure as Hawk and Griffin. This is the fairest comparison and controls for data distribution, tokenizer, optimizer, and training regime.
  2. **Mamba-3B** (Gu and Dao, 2023): The strongest reported small recurrent model in the literature at the time of writing, trained on 600B tokens (twice the 300B used for downstream evaluation). Compared against Hawk-3B and Griffin-3B in Table 1.
  3. **Llama-2-13B** (Touvron et al., 2023): A widely-used open Transformer model trained on 2T tokens (nearly 7× the 300B used for downstream evaluation). Compared against Griffin-7B and Griffin-14B in Table 1.
  4. **Global MQA Transformer** and **local MQA Transformer** variants (Appendix E, Figure 9): Used to assess whether Griffin's performance comes from local attention alone or the hybrid recurrence-attention combination.

  The paper explicitly acknowledges that Mamba and Llama-2 were "trained on different datasets and with different hyper-parameter tuning strategies, which may partially explain our strong performance" (Section 3.2).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For training, compute is measured in **total training FLOPs**, following standard practice from Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022). Training tokens are scaled proportionally to model parameters for scaling law experiments (Chinchilla-optimal ratios), with the largest Griffin-14B model trained on 300B tokens. For inference comparisons, compute is measured in several complementary ways: training step time (Figure 3, relative to MQA baseline at 2K sequence length), maximum throughput in tokens/second (Figure 1b), and latency in seconds to sample a specified number of tokens (Figure 4). For fair comparison during training speed benchmarks, "as we increase the sequence length, we proportionally decrease the number of sequences" to keep total tokens per batch fixed (Section 4.3). For the long-context training experiments (Figure 5, right), models trained at sequence length 8192 use 4× smaller batch size to maintain the same total tokens. For downstream evaluation, all models are trained on exactly 300B tokens, meaning smaller models are significantly overtrained (1B model sees 300× its parameter count in tokens) while larger models are closer to Chinchilla-optimal (14B model sees ~21×).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ formal cross-validation for its primary results — scaling curves (Figure 1a), downstream evaluations (Table 1), inference speed measurements (Figures 1b, 4), and long-context evaluations (Figures 5, 10) are all reported as single-run results. Hyperparameters for large models (7B, 14B) are not directly tuned; instead, the paper "tune[s] the learning rate, weight decay and β₂ parameters for small models, and use[s] these runs to identify scaling rules for these hyper-parameters which predict their optimal values for the 7B and 14B models" (Section 3.1). For the window size ablation (Appendix E, Figure 9), 400M parameter models are trained at three sequence lengths with multiple window sizes and compared to MQA baselines, but no error bars or multiple seeds are reported. The paper provides a single training run per configuration in all presented results.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Scaling Laws: Griffin Achieves Lower Held-Out Loss Than Transformers at Matched FLOPs

The central scaling result appears in Figure 1(a), which plots validation loss against training FLOPs on a log-log scale for MQA Transformer, Hawk, and Griffin models ranging from small-scale (approximately 10^18 FLOPs) up to Griffin-14B (exceeding 10^21 FLOPs).

**Headline finding**: All three model families exhibit power-law scaling — approximately linear on the log-log plot — with **Griffin achieving lower held-out loss than the MQA Transformer baseline at all FLOPs budgets**. Hawk achieves slightly higher validation loss than the MQA baseline, but "this gap appears to close as the training budget increases" (Section 3.1). The paper states the relationship explicitly: "All three model families demonstrate a linear scaling relationship between the validation loss and training FLOPs ... as previously observed for Transformers by Brown et al. (2020)" (Section 3.1).

At the largest scale shown (Griffin-14B at approximately 10^21.5 FLOPs), the validation loss reaches approximately 2.0–2.1 for Griffin, compared to approximately 2.15 for Hawk and around 2.1 for the MQA baseline (estimated from Figure 1a — the paper does not tabulate exact loss values). The key comparative claims are:

- **Griffin vs. MQA Transformer**: Griffin's curve is consistently below the MQA Transformer curve, with a gap that appears roughly constant in log-loss space across scales. This means Griffin achieves equivalent loss with fewer FLOPs, or lower loss at matched FLOPs.

- **Hawk vs. MQA Transformer**: Hawk is slightly above the MQA curve at smaller scales but the gap narrows. The paper interprets this as evidence that pure recurrences become competitive at scale.

- **Hawk vs. Griffin**: Griffin outperforms Hawk across all scales, confirming that the addition of local attention provides consistent benefit beyond what pure recurrence can achieve.

**Why power-law scaling matters**: The parallelism of these curves on log-log axes implies that the exponent relating loss to FLOPs is approximately equal across architectures. If recurrent models saturated earlier (e.g., the fixed-size state becoming a bottleneck), the Hawk and Griffin curves would flatten relative to the MQA baseline at larger scales. The fact that they remain approximately parallel up to 14B parameters suggests that, at least through this scale, the scaling behavior is architecture-agnostic.

#### Downstream Task Performance: Griffin Matches Llama-2 with ~7× Fewer Tokens

Table 1 presents the primary downstream evaluation, comparing Hawk and Griffin against the internal MQA Transformer baseline and two external baselines (Mamba, Llama-2). All internal models are trained on 300B tokens; Mamba-3B was trained on 600B tokens; Llama-2-13B was trained on 2T tokens.

**Comparison against Mamba-3B**:
- Hawk-3B achieves an average downstream score of **59.5** across the six benchmarks, exceeding Mamba-3B's reported **58.5** despite being trained on half as many tokens (300B vs. 600B). Individual scores: MMLU 31.3 vs. 26.2, HellaSwag 71.7 vs. 71.0, PIQA 78.8 vs. 78.1, WinoGrande 66.5 vs. 65.9, ARC-E 68.4 vs. 68.2, ARC-C 40.2 vs. 41.7.
- Griffin-3B achieves **60.7**, significantly outperforming Mamba-3B by 2.2 points on average, with notable advantages on HellaSwag (73.5 vs. 71.0), ARC-E (71.5 vs. 68.2), and MMLU (32.6 vs. 26.2).

**Comparison against Llama-2-13B**:
- Griffin-7B achieves an average score of **65.8** compared to Llama-2-13B's reported average of **69.3** — a gap of 3.5 points. Griffin is trained on 300B tokens vs. Llama-2's 2T (~6.7× fewer).
- Griffin-14B achieves **69.5**, slightly exceeding Llama-2-13B's **69.3** by 0.2 points. The paper states Griffin-14B was trained on "roughly 7 times fewer tokens" (300B vs. 2T). Individual comparisons: MMLU 49.5 vs. 54.8 (Llama-2 leads), HellaSwag 81.4 vs. 80.7 (Griffin leads), PIQA 81.8 vs. 80.5 (Griffin leads), WinoGrande 74.1 vs. 72.8 (Griffin leads), ARC-E 79.1 vs. 77.3 (Griffin leads), ARC-C 50.8 vs. 49.4 (Griffin leads).

**Comparison against internal MQA Transformer baseline** (trained on same 300B tokens):
- Hawk is "competitive with our MQA Transformer baseline" (Section 3.2): at 3B, Hawk achieves 59.5 vs. MQA's 59.0; at 7B, Hawk achieves 63.8 vs. the 6B MQA baseline's 64.2. The 7B Hawk and 6B MQA are not parameter-matched, complicating direct comparison.
- Griffin "outperforms this baseline" (Section 3.2): at 3B, Griffin achieves 60.7 vs. MQA's 59.0 (+1.7); at 7B, Griffin achieves 65.8 vs. the 6B MQA's 64.2; at 14B, Griffin reaches 69.5 (no 14B MQA baseline is reported).

**Key patterns in the benchmark breakdown**: Griffin shows the largest advantages over the MQA baseline on ARC-E (79.1 vs. 74.1 at 14B/6B comparison — a 5-point gap) and ARC-C (50.8 vs. 45.2 — a 5.6-point gap), suggesting particular strength on reasoning tasks. Performance on MMLU shows more modest gains (Hawk-3B: 31.3, Griffin-3B: 32.6, MQA-3B: 31.7), indicating that knowledge-intensive tasks benefit less from the architectural change.

**Interpretation caveats**: The paper acknowledges that Mamba and Llama-2 comparisons are confounded by different training data distributions and hyperparameter optimization procedures. The internal MQA baseline — trained on identical data with identical tuning budgets — provides the cleanest comparison, and there Griffin shows a modest but consistent advantage (1.7 points at 3B, scaling to larger advantages at 7B/14B, though parameter matching is imperfect at larger scales). The claim of matching Llama-2 with ~7× fewer tokens should be understood as partially attributable to data and hyperparameter differences, not purely to architectural efficiency.

#### Training Efficiency: Comparable to Transformers at Short Sequences, Better at Long Sequences

Figure 3 presents training step durations for Griffin relative to the MQA Transformer baseline at sequence length 2048, across model sizes (400M, 1B, 7B) and sequence lengths (2K, 4K, 8K). The key findings:

**At 2K sequence length**: Griffin and MQA train at comparable speeds. At 400M parameters, Griffin trains at 0.93× the MQA baseline speed (7% slower). At 1B parameters, Griffin trains at 0.97× (3% slower). At 7B parameters, Griffin trains at 1.05× (5% faster). The crossover from slower to faster as model size increases is attributed to the fact that the MQA baseline has "slightly fewer parameters than Griffin at the same model scale (and performs fewer FLOPs)," which disproportionately affects small models where the FLOP difference is a larger fraction of total compute.

**At longer sequence lengths**: Griffin's advantage grows. At 8K sequence length, Griffin trains at 0.98× MQA speed for the 400M model (essentially even), 1.01× for 1B, and 1.07× for 7B. The MQA baseline, by contrast, slows down substantially: at 400M parameters, the MQA Transformer at 8K runs at 1.00× / 1.17× / 1.46× its 2K speed across the three sequence lengths (2K / 4K / 8K) — a 46% slowdown at 8K — while Griffin remains nearly constant at 0.93× / 0.96× / 0.98×. The paper explains: "as we increase the sequence length the Transformer becomes slower, while Griffin's runtime remains the same" (Section 4.3). This is because the RG-LRU computation scales as O(TD) while global attention scales as O(T²D), so as T grows, attention becomes an increasingly dominant fraction of total compute.

**Scaling behavior with model size**: The advantage at long sequences "is more pronounced at smaller model sizes and decreases at larger model sizes" because "as we increase the model width D compared to the sequence length T, the linear layers become the primary computational bottleneck" (Section 4.3). The linear layers scale as O(TD²), so for wide models, the MLP and projection layers dominate regardless of whether attention or recurrence is used for temporal mixing.

#### Inference Speed: Up to 14.8× Higher Throughput for Recurrent Models

Figures 1(b) and 4 present inference speed comparisons at the 1B parameter scale.

**Throughput (Figure 1b)**: Maximum tokens per second decoded on a single device when sampling from an empty prompt, across decode lengths from 512 to 4096 tokens:
- At 512 tokens decoded: MQA baseline achieves 1.0× (reference throughput), Griffin achieves 1.1× (10% higher), Hawk achieves 2.5× (150% higher).
- At 4096 tokens decoded: MQA achieves 1.0×, Griffin achieves **6.6×** (560% higher), Hawk achieves **14.8×** (1380% higher).

The rapid growth in throughput advantage with sequence length is explained by the paper's latency model (Section 5.1, Equation 5): as sequence length grows, the KV cache size grows linearly and eventually dominates the parameter loading time. The recurrent state, by contrast, remains fixed-size. Hawk achieves higher throughput than Griffin because Griffin's local attention cache eventually becomes significant at large batch sizes: "the size of the local attention cache eventually becomes comparable to the size of the parameters when the batch size is large" (Section 5.2).

**Latency (Figure 4)**: Time taken to sample tokens at batch size 16:
- **Empty prefill (Figure 4a)**: At short decode lengths (128 tokens), all three models have similar latency (~20ms). As decode length increases to 4096 tokens, the MQA Transformer latency grows to approximately 140ms, while Hawk and Griffin remain at approximately 50–60ms. The gap widens progressively: MQA latency grows roughly linearly with sequence length (due to KV cache loading), while recurrent latency stays approximately constant.
- **4K prefill (Figure 4b)**: With a 4096-token prefill (meaning the KV cache starts large), the MQA Transformer at 128 tokens decoded already takes approximately 90ms vs. 30ms for Hawk and Griffin. At 4096 tokens decoded, MQA latency reaches approximately 150ms, while Hawk and Griffin remain around 70–80ms.

**The prefill length matters**: Comparing Figure 4a and 4b, the MQA Transformer's latency disadvantage is present even at short decode lengths when the prefill is long — the KV cache from the prefill must be loaded regardless of how many new tokens are generated. Recurrent models are unaffected by prefill length in the decode stage since they only maintain a fixed-size state.

**Throughput vs. latency relationship**: The paper notes that throughput gains come from two sources: "reducing the latency" and "reducing memory usage to enable the use of larger batch sizes on device" (Section 5). Since the recurrent state is much smaller than the KV cache, more sequences can fit in device memory simultaneously, increasing the maximum batch size and thus throughput beyond what latency reduction alone would predict.

#### Long-Context Extrapolation: Recurrent Models Improve with Longer Contexts and Extrapolate Past Training Length

Figure 5 evaluates how 1B parameter models trained on sequence length 2048 perform when evaluated on longer sequences (up to 32K tokens on held-out books, up to 131K on arXiv in Appendix G).

**Extrapolation beyond training length (Figure 5, left)**: All models were trained on sequences of length 2048 and evaluated up to 32K tokens (books) or 32K–131K tokens (arXiv, Figure 10).

- **Hawk**: Mean-so-far NLL improves (decreases) as context length increases from 128 to approximately 16K–32K tokens, substantially beyond the 2048 training length. At 2048 tokens, Hawk achieves approximately 2.65 NLL on the books dataset; at 16K tokens, NLL drops to approximately 2.55. The improvement continues beyond training length, indicating the model can productively use longer contexts.
- **Griffin**: Shows similar or slightly better extrapolation than Hawk. At 2048 tokens on books, Griffin achieves approximately 2.55 NLL; at 16K, approximately 2.45–2.50. Griffin "extrapolates remarkably well even when using RoPE for the local attention layers" (Section 6.1), which is notable because RoPE-based Transformers typically struggle to extrapolate.
- **MQA Transformer with RoPE**: Performs comparably up to the training length (2048) but degrades sharply beyond it. On books, NLL rises from approximately 2.55 at 2048 to approximately 3.2 at 8K and 3.4 at 32K — worse than at sequence length 128. This is the well-known positional encoding extrapolation failure.
- **MQA Transformer without positional encoding (NoPE)**: Extrapolates better than RoPE but worse than recurrent models. NLL remains approximately flat or slightly improves to 8K, then degrades. This confirms that positional encodings are a key bottleneck for Transformer extrapolation, but even without them, the fixed-state recurrences extrapolate more gracefully.

**Learning from longer contexts (Figure 5, right)**: Models trained at sequence length 8192 (8K) are compared to those trained at 2048 (2K), with the same total training tokens (batch size reduced 4× for 8K to compensate).

- **Hawk-8K** and **Griffin-8K** both achieve lower evaluation loss than their 2K-trained counterparts at sequence lengths of 8192 and above. On the arXiv dataset (Figure 10), Griffin-8K reaches approximately 1.70 NLL at 8K vs. Griffin-2K at approximately 1.80 — a clear improvement from training on longer sequences.
- **At short sequence lengths**, the 2K-trained models slightly outperform the 8K-trained models. The paper notes: "Hawk-2K and Griffin-2K perform slightly better than Hawk-8K and Griffin-8K" at short evaluation lengths (Section 6.1). This suggests a tradeoff: training on longer sequences improves long-context performance at a small cost to short-context performance, implying "the training sequence length should be carefully chosen according to the intended downstream use of the model."

**Extrapolation limits**: Even the 8K-trained models continue to improve beyond their training length — in Figure 5 (right), the mean-so-far NLL continues to decrease past 8K for both Hawk-8K and Griffin-8K, though the rate of improvement slows. On the arXiv dataset (Figure 10, right), 8K-trained models evaluated at 65K–131K show NLL below the 2K-trained models, indicating sustained benefit from the longer training context at extreme extrapolation lengths.

#### Synthetic Copying and Retrieval Tasks: Griffin Overcomes the Recurrent Copying Bottleneck

Figure 6 evaluates copying and retrieval capabilities on three tasks using either small trained models (5-layer, ~250K parameters) or pre-trained large models (7B/6B).

**Selective Copying (Figure 6a)**: Models must output data tokens from a sequence while ignoring noise tokens. Vocabulary size 16, sequence length 1024, 16 data tokens at random positions. Griffin uses local attention window 512.

- All three models eventually solve the task (reach ~1.0 accuracy).
- **Hawk learns significantly slower**: At 25K training steps, MQA and Griffin both achieve approximately 0.8–0.9 accuracy while Hawk is at approximately 0.15–0.20. Hawk requires roughly 75K steps to match MQA's performance at 25K steps — a ~3× slowdown.
- **Griffin matches Transformer learning speed**: Griffin's learning curve is "almost no slowdown, effectively matching the speed of learning of Transformers, despite using only a single local attention layer" (Section 6.2). Griffin reaches near-perfect accuracy at approximately the same number of steps as the MQA Transformer.

This result directly addresses the concern raised by Jelassi et al. (2024): pure recurrent models (Hawk, and by implication Mamba) are slow to learn copying tasks, but adding even a single local attention layer (Griffin) resolves this limitation entirely.

**Induction Heads (Figure 6b)**: Models must recall, given a special token, the token that immediately followed it in the context. Vocabulary size 16, training sequence length 256, Griffin uses window 128.

- All three models achieve perfect accuracy (~1.0) on sequences up to the training length.
- **MQA Transformer fails to extrapolate**: At evaluation sequence length 2⁹ (512), MQA accuracy drops to approximately 0.5. At 2¹³ (8192), MQA is near 0.0. The paper notes "our Transformer baseline is not able to extrapolate to longer sequences during evaluation" and cites Gu and Dao (2023) for similar observations with various positional encodings.
- **Hawk extrapolates perfectly**: Accuracy remains at ~1.0 for evaluation sequences "several orders of magnitude longer than the training sequence length" (extending to 2¹³ = 8192 in the plot). The fixed-size state can maintain the induction head pattern indefinitely.
- **Griffin also extrapolates perfectly**: Despite using RoPE-based local attention, Griffin maintains ~1.0 accuracy out to sequence length 8192. The paper highlights this as "exceptional ability to extrapolate on this task" (Section 6.2).

**Phonebook Lookup with Pre-Trained Models (Figure 6c)**: Evaluates whether pre-trained 7B Hawk, 7B Griffin, and 6B MQA Transformer models can retrieve phone numbers from a synthetic phonebook of varying length. The prompt includes a phonebook, two examples of the retrieval task, and a query name.

- **MQA Transformer (6B)**: Achieves ~1.0 accuracy for phonebook lengths up to the training sequence length (2048), then drops sharply to ~0.0 for longer contexts. This is the expected Transformer extrapolation failure.
- **Hawk (7B)**: "Can do reasonably well on the task for very short phonebook lengths" but "fails to memorize and retrieve the correct phone number when the phonebook length grows" (Section 6.2). Even at length 256, Hawk's accuracy is below 0.5, and it drops to near 0.0 by length 1500. The fixed-size state cannot store exact token identities for retrieval.
- **Griffin (7B)**: "Perfectly solve[s] this task up to a context length that matches its local attention window size of 1024" — accuracy is ~1.0 from length 0 through 1024. Beyond 1024 tokens, "performance starts to degrade" as relevant entries fall outside the attention window, but the degradation is gradual: at 2048 (2× window size), accuracy is still approximately 0.6, and at 3000, approximately 0.3. Griffin "is also able to extrapolate better to longer sequence lengths compared to Transformers" — while the MQA baseline drops to ~0.0 past training length, Griffin retains partial retrieval capability through its recurrent layers.

**Implication**: The phonebook lookup perfectly illustrates the paper's division-of-labor hypothesis. Within the attention window, Griffin matches the Transformer's perfect retrieval (both use attention to look up exact tokens). Beyond the window, Griffin degrades gracefully because the recurrent layers provide a compressed representation, while the Transformer fails catastrophically because its positional encodings don't generalize. Hawk fails at all lengths because it has no attention mechanism for exact token lookup.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Local attention window size in Griffin (Appendix E, Figure 9)**: Trains 400M parameter models at sequence lengths 2048, 4096, and 8192 with varying local attention window sizes. The key finding: "even when using a fixed window size of 1024 for the local attention layers in Griffin, it outperforms the global attention MQA Transformer baseline across all sequence lengths tested." At training length 2048, Griffin with window 1024 achieves validation loss ~2.53 vs. MQA Transformer with global attention at ~2.58. At training length 4096, Griffin with window 1024 achieves ~2.50 vs. MQA global at ~2.54. At training length 8192, the gap narrows: Griffin window 1024 achieves ~2.48 vs. MQA global at ~2.50. The paper notes that "if the sequence length grows further, it is likely important to slowly also grow the local attention window size" since the gap shrinks with sequence length. MQA Transformers using purely local attention (window sizes less than training length, shown as bars where window < training length) "perform significantly worse than both global attention MQA Transformers, as well as Griffin" — confirming that Griffin's hybrid design (recurrence + local attention) is what drives the performance, not local attention alone.

**Training on longer sequences (Figure 5, right and Figure 10, right)**: Ablation of training sequence length (2048 vs. 8192) for 1B models on the same total tokens. Hawk-8K and Griffin-8K achieve lower NLL than their 2K counterparts at long evaluation lengths, but slightly higher NLL at short evaluation lengths. This demonstrates that (a) recurrent models can effectively learn from longer training contexts, and (b) there is a tradeoff — training on longer sequences provides long-context benefits at a small short-context cost, even when total tokens are held constant.

**Positional encoding for Transformers (Figure 5, left; Figure 10, left)**: Compares MQA Transformers with RoPE vs. no positional encoding (NoPE) for extrapolation. RoPE Transformers degrade sharply beyond training length (NLL rises from ~2.55 to ~3.4 on books when going from 2K to 32K). NoPE Transformers extrapolate substantially better (NLL stays approximately flat through 8K–16K before degrading), confirming that positional encodings are a primary bottleneck for Transformer length generalization. However, both Transformer variants are outperformed by Hawk and Griffin at long extrapolation lengths.

**Model scale effect on training speed (Figure 3)**: Ablation across model sizes (400M, 1B, 7B) and sequence lengths (2K, 4K, 8K). At 400M, Griffin is 7% slower than MQA at 2K but matches MQA speed at 8K. At 1B, Griffin is 3% slower at 2K but 1% faster at 8K. At 7B, Griffin is 5% faster at 2K and 7% faster at 8K. The paper's interpretation: "replacing Transformers with Hawk or Griffin offers the most significant wall-clock time improvement when sequence length is sufficiently large relative to model width" (Section 4.3). This is an important practical constraint — the training speed advantage only materializes at long sequence lengths, and for small, wide models at short sequences, the recurrent models may actually train slower.

**Complex vs. real recurrences (Section 2.4, Appendix B)**: The paper states it "ablates the use of complex numbers" and "found that complex recurrences were not beneficial for language modelling in practice, as also observed by Gu and Dao (2023)." The Complex-Gated Linear Recurrent Unit (CG-LRU) is defined in Appendix B but no experiments with it are reported in the main text. This is a negative result that informed the architectural choice but is not empirically validated in the paper itself.

**Recurrent block width expansion (Section 3)**: The paper sets D_RNN ≈ 4D/3 "in order to roughly match the parameter count of a MHA block when both use the same model dimension D." However, the baseline uses MQA (which has fewer parameters than MHA), meaning "our recurrent blocks have slightly more parameters than the corresponding MQA blocks." This design choice slightly favors the recurrent models in parameter-matched comparisons, though the paper is transparent about it.

**Associative scan vs. linear scan (Appendix D.2, Figure 8)**: Compares four implementations of the recurrence computation on TPU-v3: Pallas linear scan, native JAX linear scan, associative scan in bfloat16, and associative scan in float32. At sequence length 16K, the Pallas linear scan runs at approximately 18ms, the native JAX scan at approximately 52ms (~2.9× slower), the bfloat16 associative scan at approximately 42ms (~2.3× slower), and the float32 associative scan at approximately 58ms (~3.2× slower). For the full Hawk model, the Pallas kernel provides "10-20% lower training times per step" relative to the native JAX implementation. This ablation directly validates the paper's claim that associative scans are not the optimal implementation on TPU-v3 due to memory access patterns.

**Pre-trained vs. from-scratch evaluation on copying/retrieval (Figure 6)**: The paper distinguishes between models explicitly trained on synthetic tasks (Figures 6a, 6b, using small 5-layer models) and pre-trained models evaluated zero-shot (Figure 6c, using 7B/6B models). The synthetic training results show what the architecture can learn; the zero-shot evaluation shows what capabilities naturally emerge from language modeling pretraining. The key insight: Hawk can learn copying and induction tasks when explicitly trained on them (Figures 6a, 6b), but does not develop these capabilities from language modeling pretraining alone (Figure 6c). Griffin's local attention provides these capabilities in both settings. This is a non-obvious finding: architectural capacity for a capability does not guarantee that the capability will emerge from language modeling pretraining.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "Griffin achieves slightly lower held-out loss than strong Transformer baselines at all model scales" (Section 1)

**What the experiments actually demonstrate**: Figure 1(a) shows that across a range of FLOPs budgets up to ~10^21.5 (14B parameters), the Griffin curve lies below the MQA Transformer curve. The gap appears roughly constant in log-loss space, indicating a consistent advantage. The downstream evaluation (Table 1) corroborates this: Griffin-3B at 60.7 vs. MQA-3B at 59.0; Griffin-7B at 65.8 vs. MQA-6B at 64.2.

**Limitations**: 
- The MQA baseline uses head dimension 128 fixed, with the number of heads determined as D/128. The paper does not tune this hyperparameter for the baseline, nor does it explore whether MHA (with its larger parameter count at matched D) would close the gap. The MQA baseline has fewer parameters than both Hawk and Griffin at the same model dimension D.
- The training hyperparameters for large models (7B, 14B) are predicted from scaling rules fit on small models, not directly tuned. If the scaling rules are more accurate for one architecture than another, this could bias the comparison.
- Only one training run per configuration is reported. Without multiple seeds, it is unclear whether the gap between Griffin and MQA (which appears to be roughly 0.05–0.1 in validation loss at larger scales) is statistically significant or within run-to-run variance.

**What would strengthen this claim**: Training a 14B MQA Transformer on identical data for direct parameter-matched comparison, multiple training runs with different seeds to quantify variance, and hyperparameter sweeps for the MQA baseline at larger scales (rather than using scaling rules derived from recurrent models).

#### Claim 2: "Griffin matches the performance of Llama-2 despite being trained on over 6 times fewer tokens" (Abstract, Section 1)

**What the experiments actually demonstrate**: Table 1 shows Griffin-14B (trained on 300B tokens) achieving an average downstream score of 69.5 vs. Llama-2-13B's 69.3 (trained on 2T tokens). This is a striking headline comparison. However:

**Limitations**:
- **Different training data**: Griffin is trained on MassiveText; Llama-2 is trained on a proprietary dataset with a different composition. The paper acknowledges this: "both Mamba and Llama-2 were trained on different datasets and with different hyper-parameter tuning strategies, which may partially explain our strong performance." This is not a minor caveat — data quality and distribution can dramatically affect downstream performance.
- **Different hyperparameter tuning**: Llama-2's hyperparameters were extensively tuned for that specific model and training setup. Griffin's hyperparameters were tuned on small models and scaled up.
- **The internal baseline tells a more modest story**: The internal MQA Transformer baseline, trained on the same 300B tokens as Griffin, achieves 64.2 at 6B parameters. The jump from MQA-6B (64.2) to Llama-2-13B (69.3) is 5.1 points; Griffin-14B at 69.5 recovers this gap, but we cannot attribute the full 7.7-point gap between MQA-6B and Griffin-14B to architecture alone — roughly 2× parameter scaling and the specific MassiveText data distribution contribute.
- **Parameter mismatch**: Griffin-14B (14B parameters) is compared to Llama-2-13B (13B parameters) — close but not identical. The paper's own MQA baseline at 14B is not reported, making it impossible to separate the effect of scale from the effect of architecture in the Llama-2 comparison.

**What would strengthen this claim**: A Llama-2-equivalent Transformer trained on MassiveText with matched hyperparameter tuning, and a Griffin model trained on Llama-2's data, to isolate architectural efficiency from data effects. Alternatively, reporting where Griffin-14B falls relative to Chinchilla-optimal scaling laws for Transformers trained on the same data.

#### Claim 3: "Hawk exceeds the reported performance of Mamba on downstream tasks" (Abstract, Section 1)

**What the experiments actually demonstrate**: Table 1 shows Hawk-3B at 59.5 average vs. Mamba-3B at 58.5 average, with Hawk trained on 300B tokens vs. Mamba's 600B tokens. This is a narrow margin (1.0 point) with a 2× token efficiency advantage.

**Limitations**:
- Same confounds as the Llama-2 comparison (different training data, different hyperparameter strategies).
- The performance difference is small (1.0 point on a six-benchmark average) and may not be robust to different evaluation protocols or benchmark selections.
- Mamba is a concurrently developed model (Gu and Dao, 2023), and the paper's own analysis of gating mechanisms (Appendix A) shows that Mamba's selection mechanism is fundamentally different from the RG-LRU's recurrence gate, making this a comparison between two different architectural philosophies rather than a direct ablation of a specific design choice.
- Mamba-3B's downstream scores are taken from Gu and Dao (2023) and may use a different evaluation protocol (though the paper states it follows the same character-normalized accuracy conventions). Subtle differences in prompt formatting or answer extraction can shift benchmark scores by several points.

**What would strengthen this claim**: Training both Hawk and Mamba on identical data with matched hyperparameter tuning budgets, or reporting results for a Hawk variant that uses Mamba-style gating to isolate the contribution of the recurrence gate specifically.

#### Claim 4: "Hawk and Griffin achieve significantly higher throughput than MQA Transformers" (Section 1, Section 5.2)

**What the experiments actually demonstrate**: Figures 1(b) and 4 show clear and substantial throughput and latency advantages for recurrent models at the 1B parameter scale, especially for long sequences. At 4096 tokens decoded, Hawk achieves 14.8× and Griffin achieves 6.6× the throughput of the MQA baseline. Latency measurements (Figure 4) show Hawk and Griffin at roughly 50–60ms for 4096 tokens vs. MQA at ~140ms.

**Limitations**:
- **Scale specificity**: All inference measurements are at the 1B parameter scale. The paper's own analysis (Appendix F.4.1) notes that "as the model size grows the sequence length at which we see latency benefits (where the KV cache size is comparable to parameter size) also increases." At 14B parameters with a short sequence, the KV cache may be small relative to parameters, and the throughput advantage would diminish. No inference measurements are reported for larger models.
- **Hardware specificity**: All measurements are on TPU-v3. The paper explicitly notes that "the conclusions drawn here do not necessarily apply to other accelerators" (Section 4.2, footnote). GPUs with different memory hierarchies, or TPU-v4/v5 with different HBM bandwidth and MXU configurations, may show different relative speeds.
- **Batch size effects**: Throughput gains come partially from reduced latency and partially from the ability to use larger batch sizes. The latter advantage depends on whether the application can batch requests — for interactive single-query settings, only the latency advantage matters. For batch processing, the throughput advantage is more relevant.
- **Prefill cost**: The paper's inference analysis focuses on the decode stage. For short prompts with long generation, decode dominates. For long prompts with short generation, prefill cost (which is comparable between architectures since it involves a full forward pass) may dominate, and the recurrent advantage would be smaller.

**What would strengthen this claim**: Inference benchmarks at 7B and 14B scales, throughput and latency measurements at a wider range of prefill lengths (not just 0 and 4096), and comparisons on different hardware (GPU, TPU-v4/v5) to establish generality.

#### Claim 5: "Griffin can extrapolate on sequences significantly longer than those seen during training" (Abstract, Section 6.1)

**What the experiments actually demonstrate**: Figure 5 shows that 1B Griffin models trained at sequence length 2048 continue to show improving mean-so-far NLL up to at least 16K–32K tokens on books and arXiv datasets, substantially beyond the training length. Griffin-8K (trained on 8192 tokens) extends this further. The synthetic Induction Heads task (Figure 6b) shows perfect extrapolation to 32× the training length.

**Limitations**:
- **Domain specificity**: Extrapolation is evaluated on books and arXiv — relatively structured, coherent long-form text. Performance on other long-context tasks (multi-turn dialogue, long-document QA, needle-in-haystack retrieval) is not evaluated.
- **Metric**: Mean-so-far NLL measures average prediction quality across all positions. It can improve with longer context even if the model fails to use specific long-range dependencies, because it also averages over positions where context is helpful. Task-specific evaluations (like the phonebook lookup) show more nuanced results: Griffin fails to retrieve exact information beyond its attention window (Figure 6c), even though NLL continues to improve.
- **No direct Transformer comparison at matched context lengths**: The MQA Transformer with RoPE fails to extrapolate at all. The NoPE Transformer extrapolates better but is still outperformed by Hawk and Griffin. However, more sophisticated Transformer length-extension methods (ALiBi, xPos, YaRN, etc.) are not compared, making the comparison somewhat unfair to the Transformer baseline.
- **Scale**: Extrapolation experiments are at 1B parameters (Figure 5). It is unclear whether extrapolation behavior changes at larger scales — the paper does not report long-context evaluation for the 7B or 14B models.

**What would strengthen this claim**: Evaluating 7B and 14B models on long-context benchmarks, comparing against Transformers with modern length-extension techniques (not just vanilla RoPE), and including task-specific long-context evaluations (not just perplexity-based metrics) to verify that improved NLL translates to improved downstream task performance.

#### Claim 6: "Our models match the hardware efficiency of Transformers during training" (Abstract)

**What the experiments actually demonstrate**: Figure 3 shows that Griffin trains at 0.93×–1.07× the speed of the MQA baseline across different model sizes and sequence lengths, with the recurrent models becoming relatively faster at longer sequences. The custom Pallas kernel (Section 4.2, Appendix D.2) provides 10–20% improvement over a naive JAX implementation.

**Limitations**:
- **The MQA baseline may not be optimally efficient**: The paper does not report whether FlashAttention or other optimized attention kernels are used for the MQA baseline. If a naive attention implementation is used, the baseline's training speed is artificially slow, making recurrent models look better by comparison.
- **Parameter mismatches**: "Our MQA baseline has slightly fewer parameters than Griffin at the same model scale (and performs fewer FLOPs). This explains why Griffin trains slightly slower than our MQA baseline at 7B for short sequences" (Section 4.3). Griffin at 7B training 5% faster than the MQA baseline at 2K sequence length (Figure 3c) is actually outperforming a model with fewer parameters — a positive result for Griffin, but one driven partially by the parameter accounting.
- **Hardware specificity**: The custom Pallas kernel is specific to TPU-v3. The paper does not provide a GPU-equivalent implementation, and the memory-bound analysis may differ on GPUs with different memory hierarchies and TensorCore capabilities.
- **The kernel contribution**: The 10–20% improvement from the Pallas kernel (Appendix D.2) suggests that a significant fraction of the training efficiency claim depends on custom engineering, not just architectural properties. Without this kernel, the training speed story would be less favorable.

**What would strengthen this claim**: Comparisons against an MQA baseline using FlashAttention or other optimized attention implementations, reporting training speed on GPU hardware, and ablating the effect of the custom kernel vs. using the JAX implementation for a fairer comparison of architectural efficiency alone.

#### Missing Experiments and Analyses

**No combination of MQA Transformer baseline training speed with optimized attention (FlashAttention)**: The paper's training speed comparison would be substantially stronger if it demonstrated that the recurrent models' efficiency advantage persists even when the Transformer baseline uses state-of-the-art attention kernels.

**No 14B inference benchmarks**: All inference speed measurements (throughput, latency) are at 1B parameters. The paper's own analysis suggests the advantage decreases with model scale for a given sequence length, making the 14.8× throughput figure potentially misleading for practical large-model deployments. Inference benchmarks at 7B and 14B would clarify the practical significance.

**No controlled comparison with Mamba**: Mamba is the closest prior work, but the paper only compares against Mamba's reported downstream numbers (Table 1), not against a Mamba model trained on the same data. Training a Mamba-equivalent on MassiveText with matched hyperparameter tuning would isolate whether Griffin's improvements come from the recurrence gate, the hybrid architecture, the training data, or hyperparameter choices.

**Limited diversity of downstream benchmarks**: The six benchmarks (MMLU, HellaSwag, PIQA, WinoGrande, ARC-E, ARC-C) are standard but heavily weighted toward multiple-choice knowledge and reasoning tasks. Generation-based evaluations (e.g., summarization, translation, code generation) and long-context benchmarks (e.g., SCROLLS, Zero-SCROLLS, LongBench) are not included. This matters because the paper's key claims about long-context capabilities and inference efficiency are most relevant for tasks that actually require long contexts.

**No scaling of the 2:1 recurrent-to-attention ratio**: Griffin's layered pattern (two recurrent blocks, one local attention block) is a specific design choice. The paper provides no ablation of different ratios (1:1, 3:1, 4:1) to validate that 2:1 is optimal or near-optimal. A single attention layer every three blocks might be too few for some tasks and more than needed for others.

**No comparison against sparse attention Transformers**: The paper uses global MQA as the baseline, but the natural alternative for efficient long-context modeling is not global attention but sparse attention patterns (sliding window + global tokens, as in Mistral or Longformer). Comparing Griffin against a local+global attention Transformer (rather than purely global attention) would test whether the recurrent layers provide benefits beyond what attention sparsity can achieve.

**Single seed for all experiments**: The paper reports no error bars, confidence intervals, or multiple training runs for any result. Given the relatively narrow gaps in some comparisons (e.g., Hawk-3B at 59.5 vs. Mamba-3B at 58.5; Griffin-14B at 69.5 vs. Llama-2-13B at 69.3), run-to-run variance could meaningfully affect the conclusions. Language model training is known to have non-trivial seed sensitivity.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Training Efficiency Advantage Only Materializes at Sufficiently Long Sequences Relative to Model Width

**The assumption.** The paper's training speed comparisons in Figure 3 rely on a specific relationship between sequence length and model width. For wide models at short sequence lengths, the MLP and linear projection layers — which scale as $O(T D^2)$ — dominate the total FLOPs regardless of whether attention or recurrence is used for temporal mixing. The paper acknowledges this explicitly in Section 4.3: "as we increase the model width D compared to the sequence length T, the linear layers become the primary computational bottleneck, minimizing the efficiency gains from the RNN block."

**The consequence.** At the 7B parameter scale with sequence length 2K tokens, Griffin trains only 5% faster than the MQA baseline (Figure 3c). At 1B parameters and 2K tokens, Griffin is actually 3% slower. The paper's statement that models "match the hardware efficiency of Transformers during training" (Abstract) is technically true but obscures the fact that the efficiency parity at common training configurations (2K–4K sequence lengths at moderate model widths) is roughly neutral. The training speed advantage only becomes substantial at sequence lengths (8K+) that are not yet standard in most LLM training pipelines, and the advantage shrinks as model width grows. For practitioners training models at common configurations — say, 7B parameters at 4K sequence length — the expected training speed difference is minimal.

**Evidence in the paper.** Figure 3 provides the direct comparison. At 400M parameters and 2K sequence length, Griffin trains at 0.93× the MQA baseline speed (7% slower). At 1B and 2K, it is 0.97× (3% slower). Only at 7B and 2K does Griffin become 1.05× faster. At 8K sequence length, the advantage is clearer: 0.98×, 1.01×, and 1.07× for 400M, 1B, and 7B respectively. The paper also notes that the MQA baseline has "slightly fewer parameters than Griffin at the same model scale (and performs fewer FLOPs)," meaning the parameter mismatch confounds the training speed comparison — Griffin is performing more total computation per step even when wall-clock time is equal. The custom Pallas kernel contributes 10–20% of the training speed improvement (Appendix D.2, Figure 8b), meaning without custom engineering, the architectural efficiency advantage alone would be negative or negligible at most tested configurations.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about this limitation in Section 4.3, stating: "replacing Transformers with Hawk or Griffin offers the most significant wall-clock time improvement when sequence length is sufficiently large relative to model width." However, it does not provide guidance on what "sufficiently large" means quantitatively, and the headline claim of "comparable training efficiency" does not distinguish between the short-sequence (neutral/slightly worse) and long-sequence (modestly better) regimes. No attempt is made to predict at what model size and sequence length combination recurrent models become clearly faster, which is the information a practitioner would need to decide whether to adopt the architecture for training efficiency reasons alone.

---

### The 14.8× Inference Throughput Advantage Is Measured Only at 1B Parameters, and the Analysis Predicts It Diminishes with Model Scale

**The assumption.** All inference throughput and latency measurements (Figures 1b, 4) are conducted at the 1B parameter scale. The paper's own latency model (Section 5.1, Equation 5) predicts that the throughput advantage depends on the ratio of KV cache size to parameter count: when parameters dominate, the recurrent advantage is small; when the KV cache rivals or exceeds parameters, the advantage is large.

**The consequence.** At larger model scales (7B, 14B, or beyond), the parameter count grows as $O(12 D^2 N)$ (roughly, accounting for all linear layers and embeddings), while the KV cache for a single sequence of length $T$ grows as $O(2 N T d_{head})$ for MQA. As the model dimension $D$ increases, the parameter count grows quadratically while the KV cache grows linearly in $D$ (since $d_{head} = 128$ is fixed). This means the sequence length at which the KV cache size becomes comparable to the parameter count increases with model scale. The paper notes this explicitly in Section 5.1: "as the model size grows the sequence length at which we see latency benefits (where the KV cache size is comparable to parameter size) also increases." At 14B parameters with a 4K sequence length, it is unclear whether the KV cache is large enough relative to the parameters for the decode stage to be cache-bound rather than parameter-bound. If it is parameter-bound, the recurrent latency advantage narrows substantially.

**Evidence in the paper.** Appendix F.4.1 provides the cache size analysis: the KV cache for MQA at batch size 1 has size $2 N T d_{head}$. For the 1B model (D = 2048, N = 24, $d_{head}$ = 128), at T = 4096, the cache size is $2 × 24 × 4096 × 128 ≈ 25$M elements ≈ 50MB in bfloat16. The model has roughly 1B parameters ≈ 2GB. The cache is ~2.5% of the parameter memory. For a 14B model (D = 5120, N = 40), the parameters are ~28GB and the cache at T = 4096 is $2 × 40 × 4096 × 128 ≈ 42$M elements ≈ 84MB — still only ~0.3% of the parameter memory. At T = 32K, the 14B cache reaches ~670MB, which is still only ~2.4% of parameters. To reach cache sizes comparable to parameters (the regime where the 14.8× throughput advantage was observed at 1B scale), the 14B model would need sequence lengths in the hundreds of thousands of tokens. No inference measurements at 7B or 14B are reported in the paper.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not directly address this scale-dependent diminishment. The inference speed claims are presented without qualification about model scale, and Figure 1(b) prominently displays the 14.8× figure without noting that it applies to 1B models. The latency model in Section 5.1 provides the analytical tools for a practitioner to estimate the advantage at their target scale, but the paper does not perform this analysis for the models it actually trained (7B, 14B), which would be the most practically relevant demonstration. This is a significant omission for a paper whose primary motivation is improving inference efficiency.

### Recurrent Models Do Not Develop Copying and Retrieval Capabilities from Language Modeling Pretraining, Limiting Their Applicability to Tasks Requiring Exact Token Recall

**The assumption.** The paper's motivation for Griffin's hybrid architecture is that pure recurrent models struggle with exact token-level copying and retrieval — a limitation inherited from the fixed-size state that must lossily compress all historical information. The paper demonstrates this for Hawk on the phonebook lookup task (Figure 6c), but the deeper issue is that language modeling pretraining alone does not cause these capabilities to emerge in recurrent architectures, even when the architectural capacity for them exists (as shown by Hawk's ability to learn them when explicitly trained on synthetic tasks, Figures 6a, 6b).

**The consequence.** For deployment scenarios where exact retrieval is important — factual question answering, instruction following with specific constraints, code generation requiring precise reproduction of earlier definitions, long-document summarization with quoted excerpts — Hawk is likely to underperform Transformers or Griffin even at large scales. The paper states that "more work is needed to improve these capabilities for such models" (Section 6.2). For Griffin, the copying capability is bounded by the local attention window size (1024 tokens in the paper's configuration). If a task requires retrieving information from more than 1024 tokens in the past, Griffin degrades to its recurrent state's lossy compression, as shown by the gradual accuracy decline in Figure 6c beyond window size. The practical implication is that Griffin's effective retrieval range is limited to its attention window, which may need to grow with sequence length for retrieval-heavy applications, partially eroding the inference efficiency advantage. The paper's window size ablation (Appendix E, Figure 9) confirms this: "if the sequence length grows further, it is likely important to slowly also grow the local attention window size."

**Evidence in the paper.** The phonebook lookup task (Figure 6c) is the primary evidence. Hawk's accuracy drops below 0.5 even at phonebook lengths of 256 and falls to near 0.0 by length 1500 — it cannot retrieve specific tokens at any meaningful scale. Griffin achieves perfect accuracy only within its 1024-token attention window; beyond that, accuracy degrades to approximately 0.6 at 2048 tokens and 0.3 at 3000 tokens. The Selective Copying task (Figure 6a) shows that Hawk can learn to copy when explicitly trained on synthetic data but requires approximately 3× more training steps than the Transformer baseline or Griffin to reach comparable accuracy, suggesting the inductive bias is unfavorable for this capability. The Induction Heads task (Figure 6b) is the exception where recurrence excels — the pattern involves recognizing a special token and retrieving the following token, which is representable as a simple state transition and benefits from the recurrent state's ability to maintain information indefinitely.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses this through Griffin's hybrid architecture, which provides exact retrieval within the attention window. The authors acknowledge the limitation explicitly for pure recurrent models: "more work is needed to improve these capabilities" (Section 6.2). However, they do not provide guidance on how to determine the necessary window size for a given application, nor do they explore architectural modifications that might allow the recurrent state itself to better preserve token identity (e.g., larger state dimension, different gating mechanisms, or learned retrieval from the state). The window size of 1024 was chosen for the training sequence length of 2048, but the relationship between optimal window size, sequence length, and task requirements is not characterized.

### All Experiments Use a Single Dataset (MassiveText) and a Single Model Family (PaLM 2-Derived Infrastructure), Leaving Cross-Domain and Cross-Infrastructure Generalization Unverified

**The constraint.** Every result in the paper — scaling laws, downstream evaluations, long-context extrapolation, copying and retrieval capabilities — is obtained using models trained on MassiveText (with a "slightly different data subset distribution" from Hoffmann et al., 2022) and evaluated on a standard set of six multiple-choice benchmarks plus perplexity-based long-context metrics. The training infrastructure (TPU-v3, custom Pallas kernel, JAX framework, Megatron-style sharding) is specific to Google's internal ecosystem.

**The consequence.** Three forms of generalization failure are possible but unmeasured. First, **data generalization**: MassiveText has a particular mixture of web text, books, news, and code. The paper does not evaluate on datasets with substantially different distributions (e.g., multilingual text, scientific papers, legal documents, dialogue), so it is unknown whether Griffin's advantage over Transformers — or even Hawk's parity with Transformers — persists across domains. Second, **task generalization**: All downstream evaluations are multiple-choice benchmarks. Performance on generation-based tasks (summarization, translation, open-ended QA, code generation) is not measured. The synthetic copying and retrieval experiments provide mechanistic insight but do not substitute for real-world task evaluation. Third, **infrastructure generalization**: The training efficiency results depend on a custom Pallas kernel that provides 10–20% speedup over a naive JAX implementation (Appendix D.2). On GPU hardware with different memory hierarchies and different attention kernel optimizations (e.g., FlashAttention-2 on A100/H100 GPUs), the relative training speed of recurrent vs. Transformer models may differ substantially. The paper explicitly notes that "the conclusions drawn here do not necessarily apply to other accelerators" (Section 4.2, footnote), but this caveat applies broadly — the paper's training efficiency claims are fundamentally hardware-dependent.

**Evidence in the paper.** The paper's own comparisons against Mamba and Llama-2 (Table 1) demonstrate how data and hyperparameter differences confound cross-model comparisons — the authors acknowledge that these baselines were "trained on different datasets and with different hyper-parameter tuning strategies, which may partially explain our strong performance" (Section 3.2). This same logic applies in reverse: Griffin's apparent advantage might not transfer to other data distributions. The long-context evaluations (Figures 5, 10) use books and arXiv articles — both are coherent, well-structured long-form text. Performance on less structured long contexts (meeting transcripts, multi-turn dialogues, code repositories) is unmeasured. The paper also provides no evaluation on standard long-context benchmarks (e.g., SCROLLS, LongBench, L-Eval) that test specific long-range capabilities beyond next-token prediction.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address domain generalization. The authors justify the choice of MassiveText by its prior use in training Gopher and Chinchilla, making it a well-characterized dataset for scaling law studies, but this does not address the question of whether the findings transfer. The paper suggests future work on "other modalities" (Section 2.4, footnote about complex recurrences) but does not commit to multi-domain or multi-lingual evaluation. The lack of GPU benchmarks is noted but not mitigated — the paper is transparent that its implementation is TPU-specific.

### The Recurrent Block's Width Expansion and the MQA Baseline's Parameter Disadvantage Create a Systematic Bias Favoring Recurrent Models in Fairness-Critical Comparisons

**The design choice.** The paper sets the recurrent block's internal width to $D_{RNN} \approx 4D/3$, explicitly stating this is "in order to roughly match the parameter count of a MHA block when both use the same model dimension D" (Section 3). However, the baseline uses Multi-Query Attention (MQA), not MHA. MQA reduces parameters by sharing key-value heads across all query heads. The consequence is that "our recurrent blocks have slightly more parameters than the corresponding MQA blocks" (Section 3) — an asymmetry the paper acknowledges but does not correct.

**The consequence.** This parameter asymmetry means that at a given nominal model dimension $D$, Hawk and Griffin have more total parameters and perform more FLOPs than the MQA Transformer baseline. The paper compares models at matched model sizes (e.g., "1B," "3B," "7B"), but the internal parameter allocation differs between architectures. A practitioner choosing between architectures at a fixed parameter budget would need to adjust model dimensions to achieve exact parameter matching, which is not what the paper's comparisons represent. The effect of this asymmetry on the scaling curve (Figure 1a) is difficult to isolate: Griffin achieves lower loss than MQA at matched model sizes, but part of this advantage may come from having more parameters. The downstream comparisons in Table 1 are not parameter-matched — Griffin-7B (with the 4D/3 expansion) is compared to a 6B MQA baseline (no 7B MQA is reported), and Griffin-14B is compared only to external baselines (Llama-2-13B), not to a 14B MQA Transformer.

The magnitude of the parameter discrepancy can be estimated. For the 7B configuration (D = 4096, D_RNN = 5632, N = 32): each recurrent block has two input linear layers (D → D_RNN, D_RNN × D parameters each, but with block-diagonal gates at 1/16 the parameters for W_a and W_x), an RG-LRU (negligible parameters), and an output linear layer (D_RNN → D). Each MQA block has query, key, value, and output projections. For MQA with head dimension 128, the KV projection parameters are smaller than the recurrent block's corresponding projections by roughly the factor by which D_RNN > D and by the difference between MHA and MQA parameter counts. The paper does not quantify this discrepancy in total parameters, FLOPs per forward pass, or memory usage, leaving the practitioner unable to correct for it when making architecture decisions.

**Evidence in the paper.** The parameter tables (Appendix C, Table 2) show model dimension D and RNN width D_RNN but do not report total parameter counts for each architecture at each scale. The paper states the asymmetry qualitatively (Section 3) but never quantifies it. The training speed comparisons (Figure 3) are affected by this: "our MQA baseline has slightly fewer parameters than Griffin at the same model scale (and performs fewer FLOPs). This explains why Griffin trains slightly slower than our MQA baseline at 7B for short sequences" (Section 4.3). The inference throughput advantage (Figure 1b) is also affected — the recurrent models have more parameters to load from memory, partially offsetting the cache size advantage.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not provide a parameter-matched comparison or a FLOP-matched comparison that would isolate the architectural contribution from the parameter count contribution. The authors are transparent about the asymmetry but treat it as a minor implementation detail rather than a confounding factor in their primary claims. A practitioner wanting to make a fair comparison would need to construct parameter-matched or FLOP-matched configurations themselves — the paper does not provide the data to do this (e.g., exact parameter counts per architecture at each scale).

### The Recurrence Gate's Behavior and the Constant c = 8 Are Not Ablated, Leaving the Mechanism's Importance Unquantified

**The design choice.** The RG-LRU's recurrence gate $r_t$ and the constant $c = 8$ are the paper's key architectural innovations relative to prior linear recurrences. The gate modulates the effective recurrent weight as $a_t = a^{c r_t}$, creating an input-dependent decay rate that the paper hypothesizes "enable[s] the model to achieve super-exponential memory by reducing the influence of uninformative inputs" (Section 2.4). The constant $c = 8$ controls the dynamic range of this modulation — with $c = 8$ and $a$ initialized such that $a^8 \in [0.9, 0.999]$, the effective recurrent weight $a_t$ can vary from near 1 (when $r_t \to 0$, perfect memory) to near $a^8 \approx 0.9$ (when $r_t \to 1$, fastest decay).

**The consequence.** Without ablation of the gate, the constant $c$, or the gating mechanism more broadly, the paper cannot distinguish between several hypotheses about why the architecture works: (1) the specific mathematical form of the gate (modulating the exponent rather than interpolating) is critical, (2) any form of input-dependent recurrence would work equally well, (3) the gate is largely irrelevant and the performance comes from other factors (wider recurrent dimension, the Conv1D layer, careful initialization, the gated MLP block). The paper's claim that the recurrence gate is "different from other gating mechanisms in the literature" (Section 2.4) and Appendix A's analysis of gating trajectories provide motivation but not empirical evidence that the specific gate design matters. A practitioner implementing a gated linear recurrence has no guidance on whether to replicate the RG-LRU's gate exactly, use a simpler alternative (e.g., GRU-style interpolation, or Mamba-style selection), or drop gating entirely in favor of a fixed learned decay.

**Evidence in the paper.** The paper provides exactly one ablation relevant to the recurrence design: complex vs. real recurrences (Section 2.4), which found that "complex recurrences were not beneficial for language modelling in practice." No ablation compares the RG-LRU gate against: (a) no gating (LRU-style fixed decay), (b) GRU-style gating ($h_t = (1 - r_t) h_{t-1} + r_t x_t$), (c) Mamba-style selection, (d) different values of $c$ (e.g., $c = 1, 2, 4, 16$), or (e) different initialization ranges for $a^c$. The paper's analysis of gating behavior (Appendix A, Figure 7) is purely mathematical — plotting $\alpha(r_t)$ vs. $\beta(r_t)$ for different mechanisms — and does not demonstrate that the observed differences in gating trajectories produce observable differences in model performance. The Conv1D layer (temporal filter width 4) is also not ablated — it is not obvious whether removing it would harm performance, or whether a larger filter would help.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The authors state: "We believe the key role of this gate is to enable the model to achieve super-exponential memory by reducing the influence of uninformative inputs" (Section 2.4), but this belief is not tested empirically. The claim that the gate is novel and important is supported by analytical argument and indirect performance evidence (Hawk's long-context extrapolation), but not by controlled ablation. For a paper whose primary architectural contribution is the RG-LRU and its gating mechanism, the absence of gate ablations is a significant gap — it is the difference between presenting a working system and explaining why the system works.
