Here are the takeaways from this Mike Stonebraker interview (Turing Award winner, pioneer of Ingres and Postgres, now building the DBOS platform):

**How he got into databases**

When Mike Stonebraker joined UC Berkeley as an assistant professor in 1971, his PhD work was a dead end. A senior colleague, Gene Wong, took him under his wing and suggested they look at databases—the year after Ted Codd published his pioneering relational model paper. The landscape at the time had two contenders: the CODASYL proposal, a low-level pointer-chasing spaghetti network where "if anything changed in your schema, you basically had to throw away everything and do it all again because it was absolutely rooted at the physical level," and IBM’s IMS, a hierarchical tree-structured system IBM themselves had hacked into a limited network because trees weren't general enough. Both were terrible. Wong said "well let's build one of these puppies," and they started building Ingres in 1972. For Stonebraker, "Ingres was my ticket to getting tenure"—which he earned in 1976.

A crucial decision separated Ingres from most academic prototypes. "A lot of people would build prototypes which were sort of studenty code—you could get it to run, but if you gave it to anybody else, they couldn't." The Berkeley team put in the first 90% to get it running, then "put in the next 90% to get it to where it really worked." The result was a legitimate, working database system that ran on Unix, and about 100 universities adopted it. However, when Arizona State considered running Ingres on their 40,000-student records, the project collapsed because there was no COBOL on Unix. "Unsupported operating system, unsupported database system, no COBOL doomed us to irrelevance." The only way out was to start a company. In 1980, they raised venture capital and founded Ingres Corporation to port it to DEC VMS—a real operating system with real support.

**Competing with Oracle**

Stonebraker's view of Larry Ellison is blunt and specific. "Larry Ellison is a fabulous salesman and he made present tense and future tense indistinguishable. He basically lied to customers." Ellison would ship software that didn't work and have his initial customers help debug it—"shady business practices" Stonebraker considers unconscionable. He gives a concrete example: referential integrity. Ingres Corporation actually implemented it. "Oracle Corporation wrote two manual pages that said here's the definition of referential integrity, and then down at the bottom it said 'not yet implemented.'" He notes that when Oracle later acquired MySQL, fear of Oracle's stewardship drove the community to Postgres—"that was the genesis of Postgres replacing MySQL as the preferred open source relational database system."

**What made Postgres special**

The original academic Ingres was designed to support a geographic information system for a neighboring professor. That required points, lines, polygons, and line groups—data types Ingres couldn't efficiently support with its hard-coded integers, floats, and strings. "As a GIS, the academic version of Ingres was a complete failure." Then, around 1985, a commercial Ingres customer called about date/time handling. The man dealt with bond financial instruments where every month was treated as 30 days regardless of actual length. He needed to subtract two dates, multiply by a coupon rate, and get interest paid—but the subtraction had to use *his* definition, not the Gregorian calendar. Retrieving dates to user code and doing the math externally cost him a factor of two or three in performance. "He said, why can't I just overload your definition of subtraction with what I want? And of course with Ingres it was hard coded."

That was the insight behind Postgres. "Postgres was engineered to have an extendable type system so you could have whatever data types you wanted, and they were very efficient." While business data processing users were mostly happy with standard types, relational databases were spreading into domains where abstract data types had great applicability. Postgres also supported inheritance (what the AI community wanted at the time) and time travel, "but the implementation absolutely sucked and it got taken out after a while." On hiring extraordinary engineers, Stonebraker is characteristically direct: "I have a good feel for how difficult stuff is. If they get 3x the amount done in school that I think is reasonable, then they're incredible." And on the flip side: "I can't stand people who aren't really smart. It's challenging to talk to them." You surface it by asking deep technical questions about their work—how exactly did it work, how did you handle error conditions, why threads and not processes.

**One size fits none**

Stonebraker's 2004 paper argued that "one size fits none"—architecting a database for a specific workload yields an order of magnitude improvement over general-purpose systems. The evidence at the time was three wildly different systems, each an order of magnitude faster than the competition: a stream processing engine (became StreamBase), a column store for data warehouses (became Vertica), and a traditional row store. "Here were three wildly different implementations that had no resemblance to each other." He believes this still holds today—ClickHouse is a column store, Pinecone beats user-defined types on text-based vector processing, and Postgres doesn't implement a column store or multi-node support, so it's "not competitive on sizable data warehouses."

He offers a nuanced take on Postgres's role: "If you want to get going, you have a database problem, the answer is choose Postgres." It's free, has a huge community, all kinds of data type implementations, and you can find talent easily. "At the low end it's absolutely the right one size fits all. At the low end, it's Postgres. At the high end, that's just not true." Until you're doing a million transactions per second or supporting a petabyte data warehouse, Postgres works fine. On GPUs: "the big challenge is that GPUs are SIMD, single instruction multiple data, and that's the anathema of indexing." Indexing—walking a B-tree with several pointer-chasing memory accesses—doesn't parallelize well. Plus the GPU-to-CPU bus often becomes the bottleneck. When Ryan asks about the hardest part of implementing Ingres from scratch, the answer is immediate: "Query optimizer. It's algorithmically difficult. If you ask most any senior database programmer what's the hardest part, they'll still say the optimizer."

**Why he disagreed with Google**

When MapReduce emerged in the early 2000s, "there were a lot of not very enlightened people who said Google is really smart, they must know what they're doing, so we'll do whatever they say." Stonebraker and collaborators including Dave DeWitt understood distributed databases and knew you could "beat the heck out of Hadoop with a distributed database system"—which is what their 2011 paper demonstrated. But MapReduce wasn't the only thing Google was "stupid about." Google also pushed eventual consistency as the right way to do concurrency control, "and all the database people said you're out of your friggin' mind."

He walks through why eventual consistency fails for most real-world applications. In a replicated east-coast/west-coast database, if you decrement inventory asynchronously, two users on opposite coasts can simultaneously sell the last widget. "Eventually the state of the warehouse will be minus one and somebody won't get their widget." If you're Amazon and can say "usually ships in 24 hours," maybe you can oversell. "But most enterprises can't do that." Referential integrity constraints like "stock is greater than minus one" fail with eventual consistency. "It's performance versus data integrity. If you don't care about your data then you're willing to deal with bad things happening." Stonebraker's group approached Google before the 2011 paper to propose partnering; Google declined. Eventually, "Jeff Dean finally figured that out and when they did Spanner, Spanner had a conventional transactional system. So Google completely abandoned eventual consistency and completely abandoned MapReduce."

On Amazon: he gave a talk there about three years ago and told them they're supporting about 15 different database systems, "and that's about 12 too many." He argues most of those systems are outperformed by some other system Amazon already offers—graph databases, for instance, are "almost never the performant option." If you want a graph interface, "put a layer on top of a relational database system that gives you that user model." Any system that isn't performant in a big enough market to justify maintenance should be retired.

**Why he chose academia over big tech**

Taking a job at AWS or a similar company "gives you a boss and gives you company rules. Limits your ability to publish, limits your ability to go talk at conferences, limits your ability to go poke at what various competitors are doing." After the commercial version of Postgres was acquired by Informix, a 2,000-person company, he worked part-time there and "didn't feel like I could make a difference because it was bureaucratic and whatever the president wanted he got." He's direct about his temperament: "I'm just not cut out for politicking. I don't do that very well and I have a hard time interacting with people I think are dumb." Startups and academia have been the right fit.

**Replacing state in an OS with a database**

Matei Zaharia, the Stanford professor and Databricks co-founder who created Spark, told Stonebraker that Databricks was orchestrating up to a million Spark jobs at a time and "we tried all the schedulers written by the OS folks and they didn't scale. So we put all the scheduling data in a Postgres database." That clicked: most of what an operating system does is manage data at scale, so why not use database technology? The DBOS academic project at Berkeley and Stanford in the early 2020s set out to "replace at least the upper half of Linux with a database system." It worked. A file system on top of a DBMS was faster than the Linux file system; the scheduling engine was competitive; high availability came for free through failover. "The answer is there's really no downside."

The Stanford collaborators also built extensions to JavaScript so that every program's state lived in the database. When they pitched VCs on replacing Linux, "to a person, they said you're dreaming." But the programming language model was compelling—a way to give any program database features like durability, transactions, and failover. So DBOS Inc. was founded in 2023, now supporting versions of TypeScript, Java, Go, and Python. Programs are structured as workflows where individual steps are transactional and durable. "It's a great deal faster and a great deal easier to use than the competition." About two-thirds of early customers are doing agentic AI—large language models surrounded by tools that add signal. Currently most of it is read-only, but Stonebraker believes "the whole world is going to move to using agents to do read-write applications, and that's going to make them very, very databasey." When two agents need to debit one account and increment another, the workflow must be atomic—it all happens or looks like it never happened. DBOS handles that natively. On resistance from OS and programming-language communities: "they would get very, very threatened. It's the database guys trying to take over their turf." He's philosophical: "It took Java 10 years to become widely accepted. The time constant is substantial."

**Future problems in databases**

Stonebraker identifies two frontiers. The first is text-to-SQL with large language models. Existing benchmarks like Spider and Bird show LLMs achieving ~85% accuracy—"not quite ready for prime time but it certainly looks pretty good." But on four production data warehouse workloads his team has reverse-engineered, LLMs get "0% and if you enhance them with RAG and all the tricks, goes to 10%. If you give as a prompt the FROM clause—all the actual tables and join clauses—accuracy goes to about 35%."

The gap has specific causes. First, "data warehouse LLMs are trained on the Pile. Data warehouse data is not in the Pile." Second, real warehouse queries are often hundreds of lines of SQL, not the 10-20 lines in academic benchmarks. Third, real schemas are messy—column names like "underscore Z underscore blah" instead of mnemonics, materialized views creating redundancy, idiosyncratic data and terminology like J-term that never appeared in training data. "Those are true of every data warehouse I know of." His team published **Beaver**, an anonymized benchmark from these four warehouses, so anyone claiming good text-to-SQL can test on real data. Their current approach: turn everything into tables and join in SQL. For the city of Munich's Department of Transportation, that means joining SQL data (trolley schedules, light sequencing), CAD data (intersections), and text (federal and city regulations) all as tables with a query optimizer. A knowledgeable SQL programmer with the schema gets "very high accuracy." LLMs, on the Beaver benchmark, are at essentially zero.

The second frontier is agentic AI going read-write: "The minute this becomes read-write, it's a distributed database problem and you want atomicity, consistency, all that stuff." That's fertile ground he expects to grow rapidly.

**Technical book recommendations**

He recommends the **Red Book** (*Readings in Database Systems*), co-edited with Joe Hellerstein, though it's now eight years old. Beyond that: "papers, popular papers from the literature."

**Advice for younger self**

"Starting off doing something that was that crazy was really pretty crazy. You effort and you make stuff work and you learn along the way. I think the answer is think outside the box, think crazy thoughts and try and do them." For someone starting out today, he's not sure he'd recommend computer science—"I think healthcare and the building trades are safe bets and everything else looks much riskier." For a new PhD: take the most prestigious job you can, find a mentor, and pick an area that "isn't going with the flow and try and make it work." His current project, codenamed Rubicon, is exactly that. On passion versus pragmatism, he and his wife both say "follow your passion, somehow the money will work out"—and he adds "I don't believe that for a minute, but I think that's what you have to tell your kids." His wife, who had a CS degree but wanted to be a K-12 teacher, was told by her parents it didn't pay enough. "I don't think she has ever regretted that decision. She wasn't passionate about doing computer science, it was simply a trade." His own calculus: "I really like what I do. Wouldn't matter whether I made a lot of money or didn't."

---

Stonebraker's career arcs back to the same core conviction: **one size fits none**—whether in database engines, operating systems, or career paths. His **Beaver benchmark** exposes that LLMs, like the general-purpose databases he critiqued in 2004, collapse on real workloads architected for humans who understand schemas. DBOS itself is a bet that the **extendable type system** philosophy he baked into Postgres—let users define what they need, then make it efficient—applies just as well to application state and OS services. And his bluntness about Google's "stupid" eventual-consistency era and Amazon's 12-too-many database systems reflects a consistent stance: correctness and fitness-to-purpose are non-negotiable, and politicking around them is a waste of time he's never been willing to spend.
