Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman post on onboarding as a software engineer (distilling methods from Meta’s CTO and Ryan’s own experience mentoring hires):

**Andrew Bosworth’s “Career Cold Start Algorithm”**

Ryan anchors his onboarding philosophy in a process he credits to Meta CTO Andrew Bosworth, which he calls the **Career Cold Start Algorithm**. The core is a relentless, structured set of 30-minute meetings with anyone who will talk to you. In those meetings, you spend the first 25 minutes asking the person to tell you everything they think you should know, taking copious notes and only interrupting to clarify what you don’t understand—and you *always* stop them when you don’t understand. “Always stop them to ask about things you don’t understand.” The next 3 minutes are dedicated to asking about the biggest challenges the team faces right now, and the final 2 minutes are for asking who else you should talk to, writing down every name. The algorithm terminates only when you stop hearing new names; you repeat the process for every person on your list until the graph closes.

Ryan proposes two pragmatic tweaks for software engineers that make this more efficient. First, **ask your manager or tech lead for a directed initial list of names**—treating the search as a guided graph traversal rather than an undirected random walk. Second, **replace the generic opener with targeted questions** designed to surface the specific knowledge an engineer needs. For understanding team direction, he suggests questions like “What is the motivation behind your work?”, “How does this fit into the team’s plan?”, and “What wins are we expecting from this?” For system design, he recommends “Where does your work fit into the overall system?” and “What pieces does our team own?” And to build a map of expertise, he advises asking “Who knows the most about <tech area>?” and “If <tech area> breaks, who knows how to fix it?” This turns Bosworth’s broad curiosity prompt into a deliberate reconnaissance mission that simultaneously builds relationships, technical understanding, and a mental model of who owns what.

**Contribute Code Fast**

The immediate priority after (or alongside) the relationship crawl is to start landing code. Ryan emphasizes that writing code ties high-level understanding to the actual codebase, and it’s “the fastest way to start having an impact.” He recalls a Staff Engineer who joined his team and became a solid contributor in just a week or two by landing a high volume of low-risk changes—roughly one to two per day—that improved the codebase. This tactic let him learn the system quickly and get comfortable making changes without the pressure of high-stakes deliverables. “You don’t need to write that much code but you get the idea.”

To enable this, a manager should pair the new hire with an **onboarding buddy** for hands-on support, but the team’s process maturity matters. Ryan outlines three signs of a great onboarding process: an up-to-date wiki for setting up the dev environment, async support channels like Q&A groups or engineering chat threads for small technical questions, and a backlog of unambiguous, low-risk tasks ready to be picked up. He also frames the newcomer’s naivety as a unique asset: a fresh perspective that spots gaps in existing processes. His advice is to not merely unblock yourself when you encounter friction, but to fix the underlying documentation or tooling for the next person who will hit the same wall—“don’t just unblock yourself. Take the opportunity to also fix it for future hires.”

As a rule of thumb, Ryan sets the expectation that a solid contributor contribution level should be reachable after about three months, and for Senior+ roles that includes starting to contribute to the team’s direction by that same point. He closes by noting that even if you never switch companies, you’ll onboard many times across your career—big wins often come from underinvested areas—so the ability to ramp up quickly is a skill worth deliberately practicing.

---

Ryan’s **Career Cold Start Algorithm** is fundamentally a structured information-gathering protocol, and his engineer-specific tweaks—treating the name list as a directed search and replacing the open prompt with targeted questions—convert it from a generic networking exercise into a **deliberate reconnaissance mission**. This mirrors his “fix it for the next person” stance: both are instances of turning a personal friction (not knowing who does what, hitting bad docs) into a systematic, reusable asset. The Staff Engineer’s tactic of landing ~1-2 low-risk changes per day operationalizes the same principle—using volume and low stakes as a learning accelerant rather than waiting for permission or perfect understanding. Together, these patterns form a coherent onboarding playbook: crawl the knowledge graph aggressively, ship code immediately to ground your mental model, and leave the path smoother than you found it.
