# Training Transformers with 4-bit Integers

**ArXiv:** [2306.11987](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11987)

## 🎯 Pitch

You can train transformers entirely in INT4 on current GPUs and get up to 35% speedup over FP16—provided you tame activation outliers with a Hadamard transform and approximate backprop gradients via bit splitting and importance sampling. This work finally makes 4-bit training practical on off-the-shelf hardware.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes a hardware-friendly INT4 training algorithm for transformers that implements all matrix multiplications with 4-bit integer arithmetic, addressing a gap in prior 4-bit training methods that require custom numerical formats unsupported by contemporary GPUs. The method introduces two complementary mechanisms: **Hadamard quantization** (applying a block-diagonal Hadamard transform to activations and weights before quantization to suppress feature-wise outliers that dominate transformer activations) for forward propagation, and **bit splitting with leverage score sampling** (splitting gradients into higher and lower 4-bit representations, then using importance sampling based on per-row gradient norms to drop uninformative small gradients and accurately represent large ones) for backpropagation. Evaluated across language model fine-tuning (BERT on GLUE, SQuAD), machine translation pretraining, and image classification (ViT fine-tuning and DeiT pretraining), the algorithm achieves competitive accuracy—improving over the LSQ+LUQ 4-bit baseline by 5.5% average on BERT-base GLUE and by over 25% on BERT-large GLUE—while delivering up to 2.2× faster linear operators and up to 35.1% end-to-end training speedup over FP16 on contemporary hardware, establishing that INT4 training can be both accurate and practically deployable on existing GPUs only when the structural properties of transformer activations (outliers) and gradients (token-wise sparsity) are explicitly addressed through dedicated quantization strategies.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Current 4-bit Training Cannot Run on Real Hardware

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is a practical gap between algorithmic progress and hardware reality. Over several years, the fully quantized training (FQT) community has steadily pushed training numerical precision downward—from FP16 to FP8, then to hybrid INT8/INT32 schemes, and most recently into 4-bit territory. The algorithmic frontier reached a milestone with works like Sun et al. (2020) and Chmiel et al. (2021), which demonstrated that neural networks *can* be trained with 4-bit numerical representations without complete accuracy collapse. However, as the authors state directly in the abstract:

> "existing 4-bit training methods require custom numerical formats which are not supported by contemporary hardware"

This is the key gap: **the 4-bit training methods that work algorithmically cannot actually run efficiently on the GPUs that practitioners use**. Sun et al. used FP4 gradients (a 4-bit floating-point format); Chmiel et al. used a custom logarithmic number system. Neither FP4 nor logarithmic formats are implemented in the tensor cores of NVIDIA GPUs, which are optimized for standard integer formats (INT8, INT4). To run these algorithms, one would need to emulate the custom arithmetic in software, negating any potential speedup. The paper's central motivation is to bridge this gap—to design a 4-bit training algorithm that works entirely with INT4 arithmetic, the format that GPU tensor cores natively support.

This gap matters for reasons that go beyond academic completeness:

- **Training efficiency is a dominant cost in modern deep learning.** Training large transformer models requires enormous computational resources—thousands of GPU-hours for models like BERT-large, and orders of magnitude more for frontier models. Any technique that reduces the per-operation cost directly translates to faster iteration cycles, lower energy consumption, and broader access to cutting-edge model development.
- **The hardware trend is toward lower-precision compute.** NVIDIA's H100 GPU includes dedicated FP8 support via the Transformer Engine, reflecting an industry-wide bet that reduced precision is the primary path to higher throughput. If 4-bit training becomes practical on existing hardware, it extends this trend further, potentially doubling throughput over 8-bit training.
- **Memory bandwidth, not just FLOPs, is the bottleneck.** The paper notes that training with INT4 arithmetic reduces memory footprint compared to FP16 (4 bits per value vs. 16 bits), which matters because large transformer training is often memory-bandwidth-bound. The speedups reported (up to 35.1% end-to-end) come not just from faster arithmetic but from reduced data movement.

### Why 4-bit Training Is Fundamentally Harder Than 4-bit Inference

A critical point the paper emphasizes in Section 3.1 is that **quantizing training is qualitatively harder than quantizing inference**, even though both involve fitting values into low-precision formats. This distinction is essential for understanding why prior quantization methods cannot be trivially adapted:

**Quantization-aware training (QAT)**—the standard approach for producing low-precision inference models—has several advantages that FQT does not enjoy. The paper enumerates these in Section 3.1:

> "QAT requires a converged full-precision model as initialization and/or as a teacher model for knowledge distillation; QAT can adopt expensive multi-stage training pipelines without worrying about the convergence speed, while FQT algorithm must converge as fast as full-precision training algorithms to be useful; QAT may approximate the discrete quantizer with continuous functions during training, which cannot be implemented with integer arithmetic."

In other words, QAT starts from a good solution and refines it. FQT must discover that solution from scratch while operating under severe information constraints—every gradient used to update the model is itself computed at low precision, so errors compound. The paper notes that QAT has achieved 1–2 bit precision for activations and weights, which makes it tempting to think the same can be done for FQT. The reality is that without the crutches of a pretrained initialization, knowledge distillation, and continuous relaxations, FQT at 4 bits remains an open and difficult problem.

### The Two Specific Technical Challenges

The paper identifies two distinct failure modes that make 4-bit FQT challenging, each demanding its own solution (Sections 3.2 and 4.1):

#### Challenge 1: Activation Outliers Destroy Forward Quantization

When quantizing activations to 4 bits (only 16 possible values, with the signed INT4 range $[-7, 7]$), the presence of **outlier entries**—values that are orders of magnitude larger than the typical activation—creates an impossible tradeoff. The learned step size (LSQ) parameter $s_X$ in the quantizer

$$ \text{ints}_X(X) := \lfloor \text{clamp}(X / s_X, -Q_N, Q_P) \rceil $$

controls both the representable range $[ -Q_N s_X, Q_P s_X ]$ and the granularity of quantization. If $s_X$ is set large enough to capture outliers without clipping, most non-outlier values get squashed into a tiny portion of the quantization grid, effectively using only 1–2 bits for the vast majority of the tensor. If $s_X$ is set small for fine granularity, outliers are truncated to $\pm Q_P s_X$, losing the information they carry.

The problem is that **transformers store important information in these outliers**. As visualized in Figure 1(a), the activation distribution for a typical BERT layer has a heavy-tailed shape where most values cluster near zero but a small fraction extend far into the tails. The paper identifies that these outliers follow a **feature-wise structure** (Section 3.3): they concentrate on specific dimensions (columns of the activation matrix $X$), meaning certain features consistently produce large-magnitude activations across all tokens. This is consistent with findings from post-training quantization work like SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022), which observed that LayerNorm amplifies the magnitude of certain feature dimensions.

The outlier problem is particularly severe for **fine-tuning tasks**, where the pretrained model already contains established outlier channels (Dettmers et al., 2022 showed that pretrained transformers develop outlier features during training). Starting from random initialization produces fewer outliers, making pretraining somewhat easier to quantize than fine-tuning—a distinction that appears in the paper's results (the WMT pretraining task shows smaller degradation than BERT fine-tuning).

#### Challenge 2: Gradient Quantization Is a Different Problem Altogether

While activation quantization has received substantial attention from the inference quantization community, **gradient quantization poses a distinct and arguably harder challenge**. Gradients have fundamentally different structure from activations:

- **Numerical range is vast and dynamic.** During training, gradient magnitudes can span many orders of magnitude, from near-zero for well-fitted examples to very large for rare hard examples. This range changes throughout training, making static quantization schemes ineffective.
- **Gradients exhibit structural sparsity along the token dimension.** As shown in Figure 2, the per-row norm $\|(\nabla_Y)_{i,:}\|$ of the output gradient matrix follows a highly skewed distribution: a few tokens (rows) have large gradient norms, while the vast majority are near zero. The paper explains this phenomenon in Section 4.1 as a consequence of **overparameterization**: modern neural networks operate in a regime where most training examples are fitted easily after a few epochs, producing negligible gradients, while only a few "hard" examples continue to drive parameter updates.

This structural sparsity is both a challenge and an opportunity. It is a challenge because a uniform 4-bit quantizer must either (a) use a large step size to capture the few large gradients (losing all information about small gradients) or (b) use a small step size to preserve small gradients (severely clipping large ones). It is an opportunity because the small gradients contribute little to the parameter update, suggesting that computation spent on them is wasted—the paper's core insight for backpropagation (Section 4.2) is that **we can drop small gradients entirely and use the saved computation to represent large gradients more accurately**.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper positions itself against a specific lineage of FQT methods, each of which solves part of the problem but leaves a gap that INT4 hardware compatibility requires:

#### INT8 Training (Banner et al., 2018)

This work established that 8-bit integer training is feasible by using range-based quantization (dividing the min-to-max range into equal bins) for both forward and backward passes. However, 8-bit training leaves a 2× gap from the hardware limit—modern GPUs support 4-bit integer tensor core operations that are roughly twice as fast as 8-bit operations. The paper uses INT8 training as a baseline in Table 1 and shows it achieves near-FP accuracy, confirming that 8 bits are sufficient. The challenge is recovering that accuracy with half the bits.

#### Ultra-Low Precision 4-bit Training (Sun et al., 2020)

This work was the first to successfully train networks at 4 bits, using INT4 for activations and weights but **FP4 (4-bit floating-point) for gradients**. The floating-point format is critical for gradients because it provides a non-uniform quantization grid—large gradients can be represented with larger spacing while small gradients get finer precision, naturally adapting to the gradient's wide dynamic range. However, as the paper notes, FP4 operations are not supported on current GPUs (which have integer tensor cores), so this method cannot deliver hardware speedup. The paper only reports Sun et al.'s results on the WMT machine translation task (Table 1), where Ultra-Low achieves 25.4 BLEU vs. 27.5 for FP.

#### Logarithmic Unbiased Quantization (Chmiel et al., 2021; "LSQ+LUQ")

This work pairs LSQ for forward activation/weight quantization with a **logarithmic quantizer (LUQ)** for backward gradient quantization. Logarithmic quantization maps values to exponents, producing a non-uniform grid similar to floating-point that naturally handles the wide dynamic range of gradients. This is the paper's primary 4-bit baseline in Table 1, and it achieves reasonable accuracy on some tasks (e.g., 75.29 average on BERT-base GLUE vs. 82.67 for FP). However, like FP4, **logarithmic formats are not natively supported on GPU hardware**. Implementing them requires table lookups or custom arithmetic in software, negating speedup.

The paper's results in Table 1 show that LSQ+LUQ degrades severely on challenging tasks: BERT-large GLUE accuracy collapses to 55.93 (from 84.57 FP), suggesting that the logarithmic gradient quantizer struggles with the more complex loss landscapes of larger models, particularly on difficult benchmarks like CoLA and RTE.

#### Why Existing PTQ/Inference Methods Don't Transfer

The paper explicitly discusses (Section 3.2) why methods developed for **post-training quantization (PTQ)** of transformers—such as SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022), which migrates activation quantization difficulty to weights by scaling, and Outlier Suppression (Wei et al., 2022), which clips outlier channels—do not solve the FQT problem:

> "However, these methods mainly focus on PTQ or QAT, and seldom successfully deal with ultra-low 4-bit training."

The paper's ablation study (Appendix C.3, Table 4) provides evidence: applying per-token quantization, per-channel quantization, and SmoothQuant *without* the Hadamard transform fails completely at 4 bits (0% accuracy on CoLA). The reason, as the authors explain, is that SmoothQuant sacrifices precision on inliers to exactly preserve outliers. At 8 bits, there are enough quantization levels to represent inliers after this sacrifice; at 4 bits (only 16 levels), the inlier representation becomes too coarse to be useful. The paper's own Hadamard quantizer takes a different approach: it *redistributes* outlier information rather than sacrificing one set of values for another.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning can be understood through three conceptual moves:

**1. It reframes the problem from "find better numerical formats" to "understand the structure of the data being quantized."** Rather than designing a new number system that better approximates floating-point (the approach of Sun et al. and Chmiel et al.), the paper asks: *what specific properties of transformer activations and gradients cause quantization to fail, and how can we exploit those same properties to quantize successfully in a hardware-compatible integer format?*

For activations, the answer is feature-wise outliers → use a random rotation (Hadamard transform) to spread outlier energy. For gradients, the answer is token-wise sparsity → use importance sampling to focus bits on large gradients. Both solutions produce INT4 matrices that can be multiplied with native GPU instructions.

**2. It leverages randomized numerical linear algebra (RandNLA) as a unifying framework.** The paper explicitly connects to this field in the introduction:

> "Our quantizers leverage advances in the field of randomized numerical linear algebra (RandNLA)."

The Hadamard transform is a standard RandNLA technique for preconditioning matrices before sketching or subsampling—it ensures that the "importance" (leverage scores) of rows/columns is approximately uniform. Bit splitting and leverage score sampling directly implement the RandNLA idea of randomized matrix multiplication: approximate product $A B$ by sampling rows/columns proportionally to their importance. This theoretical grounding distinguishes the paper from heuristic quantization approaches and connects INT4 training to a well-developed literature with formal guarantees.

**3. It makes a pragmatic bet on hardware compatibility as a first-order design constraint.** The abstract's claim that "unlike previous 4-bit training methods, our algorithm can be implemented on the current generation of GPUs" is the paper's primary differentiator. This is not merely an implementation detail—it is a design philosophy that constrains every algorithmic choice. The Hadamard transform adds some FP16 overhead (quantization + transformation), but the paper carefully argues (Section 3.3) that this overhead is small relative to the INT4 MM savings when the block size $2^k$ is much smaller than the matrix dimensions. The bit-splitting approach for gradients uses two INT4 operations per gradient computation, but leverage score sampling halves the total operations back to the cost of a naive INT4 MM, preserving the theoretical 2× speedup over INT8.

In summary, the paper addresses a specific, practical bottleneck: 4-bit training exists algorithmically but cannot accelerate real hardware. It identifies the structural properties of transformer tensors (activation outliers, gradient sparsity) as the key to making INT4 training work, and it designs quantizers around those properties rather than around novel numerical formats. The result is an algorithm that matches or exceeds existing 4-bit methods in accuracy while being the first to deliver measurable speedup on commodity GPUs—a contribution that shifts 4-bit FQT from an algorithmic curiosity to a potentially deployable technique.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper develops a system for training transformer neural networks where all the expensive matrix multiplications—both in the forward pass (computing predictions from inputs) and the backward pass (computing gradients to update weights)—are performed using 4-bit integer arithmetic instead of the standard 16-bit floating-point arithmetic. The "shape" of the solution is a set of specialized quantization procedures that transform floating-point matrices into INT4 format *differently* for activations (using a random rotation to handle outliers), weights (using the same random rotation for algebraic compatibility), and gradients (using a two-stage quantization plus importance sampling to handle sparsity), all designed so that the core matrix multiplication can be dispatched to GPU tensor cores that natively execute INT4 operations.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system replaces every linear layer in a standard transformer training pipeline with quantized counterparts. Information flows through five major components:

1. **Hadamard Quantizer (HQ)** — for forward propagation: before quantizing activations and weights to INT4, this component multiplies them by a fixed block-diagonal Hadamard matrix (and its inverse), which spreads out the energy of activation outliers so they do not dominate the quantization grid. The quantized matrices are then multiplied using an INT4 tensor core GEMM, and the result is dequantized back to FP16.

2. **Bit Splitting (BS)** — for backpropagation: when computing gradients of weights and activations, this component quantizes the output gradient matrix `$\nabla Y$` twice—first to capture the coarse magnitude (higher 4 bits) and then the residual (lower 4 bits)—producing two INT4 matrices that together form an 8-bit representation.

3. **Leverage Score Sampler (LSS)** — paired with BS: before computing the expensive INT4 matrix multiplications on the split gradients, this component computes importance scores (leverage scores) for each row of the gradient matrix, draws random Bernoulli masks so that only the most important rows survive in expectation, and discards the rest. This cuts the computation exactly in half, back to the cost of a single naive INT4 MM.

4. **Learned Step Size Quantizer (LSQ)** — embedded within both HQ and LSS: a per-tensor learnable scalar parameter `$s$` that controls the quantization scale, trained jointly with the network weights via gradient descent. It adapts the quantization range during training.

5. **Straight-Through Estimator (STE)** — the mechanism that makes the entire pipeline differentiable: during backpropagation, the rounding operation `$\lfloor\cdot\rceil$` inside every quantizer is treated as having gradient 1, so gradients flow through the quantizers as if they were identity functions, enabling end-to-end training.

During a training step, the forward pass goes: FP16 activation → Hadamard transform → LSQ INT4 quantization → INT4 GEMM with similarly quantized weight → dequantize to FP16. The backward pass goes: FP16 output gradient → bit splitting into two INT4 gradients → leverage score sampling to mask out small rows → two INT4 GEMMs with the already-quantized forward matrices → dequantize and sum. All non-linear operations (LayerNorm, GeLU, softmax, attention) remain in FP16.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the forward pass quantizer (LSQ + Hadamard)**: because the forward pass computes the activations that both the loss and the backward pass depend on, and the primary obstacle to 4-bit forward quantization is the feature-wise outlier structure in transformer activations.
- **Second, the detailed mechanics of the Hadamard transform**: why a random rotation works, how the block-diagonal structure controls cost, and what the adaptive block size selection algorithm does.
- **Third, the backward pass quantizer (bit splitting + leverage score sampling)**: because gradient quantization faces a different challenge (token-wise sparsity rather than feature-wise outliers) and requires a fundamentally different strategy.
- **Fourth, the mathematical foundations of leverage score sampling**: the variance formulas, the optimal sampling probabilities, and why this connects to randomized numerical linear algebra.
- **Fifth, the step size gradient computation and cold-start handling**: the practical engineering details that make the system trainable end-to-end.
- **Sixth, the GPU implementation details**: how the algorithm maps to actual hardware instructions and where the speedup comes from.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems + algorithms paper** whose core idea is that transformer training can be made hardware-efficient at 4 bits by designing quantizers that exploit the specific mathematical structures of activations (feature-wise outliers) and gradients (token-wise sparsity), rather than by inventing new numerical formats.

#### The Base Quantizer: Learned Step Size Quantization (LSQ)

The paper builds on a standard static quantization framework called **Learned Step Size Quantization** (LSQ), originally proposed by Esser et al. (2019) for inference quantization. The choice of LSQ over dynamic quantization methods (which compute the quantization scale from the data each iteration) is driven by a key hardware constraint that the paper states explicitly in Section 3.1:

> "LSQ is a static quantization method whose quantization scale does not depend on the input, and is thus cheaper than dynamic quantization methods, which need to compute the quantization scale dynamically per iteration."

**The LSQ quantizer for a matrix** `$X$` is defined as:

$$ \text{ints}_X(X) := \left\lfloor \text{clamp}\left(\frac{X}{s_X}, -Q_N, Q_P\right) \right\rceil $$

where `$s_X \in \mathbb{R}$` is a per-tensor learnable scalar parameter (the *step size*), `$\text{clamp}(\cdot, -Q_N, Q_P)$` clips its input to the interval `$[-Q_N, Q_P]$`, and `$\lfloor\cdot\rceil$` is the round-to-nearest-integer operation. The division `$X / s_X$` is performed elementwise.

**What it computes (operational definition):** Every element of the floating-point matrix `$X$` is divided by the step size `$s_X$`, producing a scaled value. Values that fall outside `$[-Q_N, Q_P]$` after scaling are clipped to the nearest bound (saturating quantization), and the result is rounded to the nearest integer. The output is an integer matrix with values in `$\{-Q_N, -Q_N+1, \ldots, Q_P\}$`. The dequantization back to floating-point is simply `$\text{float}(\text{ints}_X(X)) = s_X \cdot \text{ints}_X(X)$`.

**Why this form:** The learnable `$s_X$` is the key innovation of LSQ over fixed-range quantizers. Fixed-range quantizers (like the minimax quantizer used as an ablation baseline in Figure 3b) divide the observed min-to-max range into equally-sized bins. This is brittle when the distribution shifts during training. By making `$s_X$` a learned parameter optimized via gradient descent alongside the network weights, LSQ can adapt the tradeoff between representable range and quantization granularity throughout training. The paper sets `$Q_N = Q_P = 7$` for all quantizers because signed INT4 covers the range `$[-7, 7]$` (15 distinct non-zero values plus zero, totaling 16 levels = 4 bits).

**The straight-through estimator (STE):** Because the rounding operation `$\lfloor\cdot\rceil$` has zero gradient almost everywhere, standard backpropagation would block gradient flow through the quantizer. The paper uses the standard STE remedy (Bengio et al., 2013): `$\lfloor x\rceil' = 1$`. That is, during backpropagation, the rounding operation is treated as an identity function. This means the gradient of the loss with respect to the pre-quantized values equals the gradient with respect to the post-quantized values. The step size `$s_X$` receives its own gradient (derived in Appendix A.3 and discussed below), so it learns even though the STE bypasses the rounding.

#### The Activation Outlier Problem in Detail

Section 3.2 of the paper demonstrates why LSQ alone is insufficient for 4-bit quantization of transformer activations. The problem is visualized in Figure 1(a), which shows a histogram of activation values for a BERT-base-uncased model's linear layer. The distribution has two properties:

- Most values cluster tightly around zero.
- A small fraction of values extend far into the tails—these are the **outliers**.

The paper describes the structural nature of these outliers:

> "The outliers in activation matrices form a feature-wise structure. They are typically concentrated on a few dimensions, i.e., only a few columns of `$X$` are significantly larger than others."

This feature-wise structure has been observed independently in post-training quantization work (SmoothQuant, Xiao et al., 2022; Outlier Suppression, Wei et al., 2022) and is believed to arise from LayerNorm operations amplifying certain feature dimensions. The important consequence for quantization is:

**The tradeoff induced by a single `$s_X$`.** When `$s_X$` is small (fine granularity), most non-outlier values are well-represented with small quantization error, but outliers exceed `$Q_P s_X$` and get clamped to `$\pm 7 s_X$`, losing the information they carry. When `$s_X$` is large (to capture outliers), the non-outliers that constitute >99% of the tensor get quantized to just 1–2 bits' worth of distinct values because the step size is large relative to their magnitude. The paper states:

> "Unfortunately, the transformers tend to store information in these outliers, and such truncation would seriously harm accuracy."

**Why fine-tuning is particularly vulnerable.** The paper notes:

> "The outlier problem is particularly significant when the training task is to fine-tune a pre-trained model on some new downstream tasks, since the pre-train model contains more outliers than random initialization."

Pretrained transformer models (like BERT) develop strong outlier channels during pretraining. When fine-tuning on a downstream task, these outlier channels persist and the model must both preserve the information they contain and adapt them to the new task. The LSQ+LUQ baseline in Table 1 shows exactly this pattern: BERT-large GLUE collapses from 84.57 FP to 55.93 with LSQ+LUQ, a 28.6-point drop, because large models have more extreme outliers.

#### Hadamard Quantization (HQ): Spreading Outliers via Random Rotation

The paper's solution to the activation outlier problem is **Hadamard quantization (HQ)** , introduced in Section 3.3. The key idea is:

> "Its main idea is to quantize the matrices in another linear space which has fewer outliers."

Rather than trying to represent outliers and inliers simultaneously in the original space (which requires a step size that serves two masters), HQ applies an orthogonal linear transformation to both the activation matrix `$X$` and the weight matrix `$W$` *before* quantization. This transformation—a Hadamard matrix—has the property of "spreading" concentrated energy evenly across dimensions.

**What is a Hadamard matrix?** The paper defines the family recursively. For `$k \geq 1$`:

$$ H_0 = [1], \quad H_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} H_{k-1} & H_{k-1} \\ H_{k-1} & -H_{k-1} \end{bmatrix} $$

Each `$H_k$` is a `$2^k \times 2^k$` orthogonal symmetric matrix: `$H_k = H_k^\top = H_k^{-1}$`, so `$H_k H_k = I$`. The entries of `$H_k$` are all `$\pm 2^{-k/2}$`. The `$1/\sqrt{2}$` factor in the recursion ensures orthonormality.

**Why Hadamard transforms suppress outliers.** The paper provides a key extreme-case analysis:

> "Consider any coordinate row vector `$e_i^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{2^k}$`, we have `$e_i^\top H_k = 2^{-k/2} \mathbf{1}_{2^k}$`, where `$\mathbf{1}_{2^k} = (1, 1, \ldots, 1)$` is a `$2^k$`-dimensional all-one-vector."

This says: if a single dimension `$i$` contains all the energy (value 1) and all other dimensions are zero, applying the Hadamard transform produces a vector where every dimension is equal to `$2^{-k/2}$`. The outlier is perfectly amortized. In practice, activations are not single-spike vectors, but the Hadamard transform has the effect of mixing the outlier dimensions with non-outlier dimensions, substantially reducing the dynamic range of any single dimension. This is demonstrated visually in Figure 1(b), where the post-Hadamard activation histogram is much more compact and symmetric than the pre-Hadamard one in Figure 1(a).

**The block-diagonal structure.** Applying a full `$D \times D$` Hadamard matrix where `$D$` is the hidden dimension (768–4096 for typical transformers) would require `$D$` to be a power of 2, which is not always the case. More importantly, the cost of multiplying by a full Hadamard is `$O(D^2)$`, which would dominate the `$O(D^2)$` cost of the matrix multiplication itself. The paper uses a **block-diagonal approximation**:

$$ H = \text{BlockDiag}(H_k, H_k, \ldots, H_k) \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D} $$

where `$D$` is required to be a multiple of `$2^k$`, and there are `$D / 2^k$` identical blocks of size `$2^k \times 2^k$` along the diagonal. This structure localizes the mixing: each block of `$2^k$` consecutive dimensions is mixed independently. The parameter `$k$` controls the block size and represents the **extent of outlier spreading**: larger `$k$` spreads outliers over more dimensions, but at higher computational cost.

**The HQ-MM procedure.** The core forward-pass operation for a linear layer `$Z = X W^\top$` proceeds through four steps:

1. **Transform:** Compute `$XH$` and `$H^\top W^\top$` in FP16. Since `$H_k = H_k^\top$`, both matrices are multiplied by the same block-diagonal Hadamard.

2. **Quantize:** Apply LSQ to the transformed matrices:
   $$ \widehat{X} = \text{ints}_X(XH), \quad \widehat{W} = \text{ints}_W(WH) $$
   producing two INT4 matrices.

3. **Multiply:** Compute the INT4 matrix multiplication `$\widehat{X} \widehat{W}^\top$` using GPU tensor core instructions.

4. **Dequantize:** Multiply by the step sizes: `$s_X s_W \widehat{X} \widehat{W}^\top$`, yielding an FP16 result.

The algebraic justification is:

$$ Y = XW^\top = (XH)(H^\top W^\top) \approx s_X \widehat{X} (s_W \widehat{W}^\top)^\top = s_X s_W \widehat{X} \widehat{W}^\top $$

The key cancellation is that `$H^\top H = I$`, so the forward and inverse transforms disappear from the final product. This is critical: **the Hadamard matrices are never applied to the output**, so the output precision is unaffected by the transformation.

**Computational cost analysis.** The paper explicitly analyzes the overhead in Section 3.3:

> "Step 1 takes `$O(2^k N(D + C))$` FP16 multiply-accumulates (MACs); Step 2 and Step 4 take `$O(N(D + C))$` FP16 MACs in total; and Step 3 takes `$O(NDC)$` INT4 MACs."

The Hadamard multiplication in Step 1 can be implemented as a **fast Walsh-Hadamard transform (FWHT)** in `$O(D \log 2^k)$` rather than `$O(D 2^k)$` by exploiting the recursive structure, though the paper uses the GEMM-based implementation for simplicity. The key practical claim is:

> "Our HQ-MM is still much cheaper than an FP16 MM given `$2^k \ll D$` and `$2^k \ll C$`."

Since typical block sizes are `$k = 5$` (`$2^k = 32$`) while hidden dimensions `$C, D$` are 768–4096, the Hadamard overhead is small relative to the INT4 MM savings, and the INT4 tensor core operations are 2–4× faster than FP16.

**Adaptive block size selection (Appendix A.5).** The paper does not fix `$k$` for all layers. Instead, it proposes selecting `$k$` per layer to minimize the quantization error:

> "We define the quantization error to be `$\text{MSE}(\bar{X}_k, X) \times \text{MSE}(\bar{W}_k, W)$`. We search for the optimal `$k$` that can minimize this quantization error."

Here `$\bar{X}_k$` and `$\bar{W}_k$` are the dequantized approximations of `$X$` and `$W$` using block size `$2^k$`. The product of MSEs is used because both matrices contribute to the final MM error. For fine-tuning, this search is performed once at the beginning and `$k$` is frozen. For pretraining, where distributions shift significantly, the search is repeated after the first two epochs (Appendix A.5).

**Why not use SmoothQuant or per-channel quantization at 4 bits?** The paper's ablation in Appendix C.3 (Table 4) addresses this directly. SmoothQuant scales activation channels to reduce outliers, then scales weight channels inversely to compensate. At 8 bits, this works—the paper reports 57.7% CoLA accuracy with SmoothQuant at 8 bits. But at 4 bits, SmoothQuant alone yields 0% accuracy. The explanation (Section 3.2):

> "The motivation of LSQ is to learn a trade-off between outliers and inliers, while SmoothQuant aims to sacrifice the precision of inliers in order to exactly maintain the information of outliers. When the bitwidth is high, this is not a problem, since there are still enough bits to quantize the inliers. But when the bitwidth is low, such sacrifice will cause severe problems since the inlier information is discarded."

HQ takes the opposite approach: instead of sacrificing inliers for outliers, it *removes the distinction* by mixing them. The Hadamard transform makes all entries roughly equal in magnitude, so there is no longer a tradeoff to manage.

**Handling batch matrix multiplications in attention (Appendix A.1).** The paper extends HQ to the attention layer's batch matrix multiplications (BMMs), where the Hadamard transform is applied along the feature dimension `$M$`:

$$ T \approx \text{BMM}\left(\text{BMM}(Q, \hat{H}), \text{BMM}(K, \hat{H})^\top\right) $$

where `$\hat{H} = \text{Repeat}_B(H)$` replicates the same `$H$` block-diagonally across the batch dimension. The same algebraic cancellation applies because BMM is associative with respect to the per-batch transforms.

---

#### Gradient Quantization: From the Chain Rule to Three Types of MMs

With the forward pass quantized, the paper turns to backpropagation (Section 4). The gradient computation for the HQ-MM linear layer `$Y = XW^\top$` must produce gradients for four inputs: the activation `$\nabla_X \mathcal{L}$`, the weight `$\nabla_W \mathcal{L}$`, and the two step sizes `$\nabla_{s_X} \mathcal{L}$`, `$\nabla_{s_W} \mathcal{L}$`.

**Applying STE to the HQ-MM computation.** By applying the straight-through estimator `$\lfloor x\rceil' = 1$` and the chain rule to the HQ-MM procedure, the paper derives (Section 4):

$$ \nabla_W = s_X \left(\nabla_Y^\top \widehat{X} \circ I_W\right) H^\top $$

$$ \nabla_X = s_W \left(I_X \circ \nabla_Y \widehat{W}\right) H^\top $$

where `$\widehat{X} = \text{ints}_X(XH)$` and `$\widehat{W} = \text{ints}_W(WH)$` are the already-computed INT4 matrices from the forward pass; `$I_X = \mathbb{I}(-Q_N \leq X/s_X \leq Q_P)$` and `$I_W = \mathbb{I}(-Q_N \leq W/s_W \leq Q_P)$` are 0/1 indicator matrices marking which entries were not clipped during quantization; and `$\circ$` denotes element-wise multiplication.

**The three types of operations required.** The paper decomposes these gradient formulas into three computational categories:

1. **Element-wise multiplication** of a 0/1 mask with an INT4 or INT32 matrix (`$I_X \circ \nabla_Y \widehat{W}$`, `$\nabla_Y^\top \widehat{X} \circ I_W$`). This is cheap—`$O(ND)$` or `$O(NC)$` operations with no matrix multiplication.

2. **Multiplication by the block-diagonal Hadamard** (`$s_W (\cdot) H^\top$`, `$s_X (\cdot) H^\top$`). This is the same FWHT as in the forward pass, with `$O(2^k N(D+C))$` cost.

3. **Matrix multiplication of the FP16 output gradient `$\nabla_Y$` with the INT4 matrices `$\widehat{X}$` and `$\widehat{W}$`:** specifically `$\nabla_Y^\top \widehat{X}$` (for weight gradient) and `$\nabla_Y \widehat{W}$` (for activation gradient). These are the expensive operations—the `$O(NDC)$` MMs that the paper must quantize to INT4 to achieve acceleration.

**The gradient quantization challenge.** The paper must therefore quantize `$\nabla_Y \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times C}$` to INT4 for these two MMs. This is fundamentally different from quantizing activations:

- Activations have feature-wise outliers → the Hadamard transform helps.
- Gradients have token-wise sparsity → a different structural property that requires a different solution.

The paper's key insight for gradident quantization is stated in Section 4.2:

> "The high-level idea is that many rows of the gradient are so small that they have little impact on the parameter gradient, yet they waste abundant computation. On the other hand, the large rows cannot be accurately represented with INT4. We drop some small rows and use the saved computation to represent large rows more accurately."

---

#### The Structural Sparsity of Gradients

Section 4.1 establishes the empirical phenomenon that motivates the gradient quantization strategy:

> "We note that the gradient matrix `$\nabla_Y$` tends to be very sparse along the training process. Furthermore, the sparsity has a structure: few rows (i.e., tokens) of `$\nabla_Y$` have large entries, while most other rows are close to an all-zero vector."

Figure 2 visualizes this: (a) shows a histogram of per-row gradient norms `$\|(\nabla_Y)_{i,:}\|$` that is heavily skewed—a few rows have norms orders of magnitude larger than the median; (b) shows the cumulative sum of the top-`$X$` row norms as a percentage of the total—a small fraction of rows accounts for most of the total gradient mass.

**Why this sparsity arises.** The paper connects this phenomenon to overparameterization:

> "During almost the entire training process, the network operates in the overparameterized scheme, where it can fit most training data well, except for a few hard examples. Therefore, the (activation) gradient will be close to zero for well-fitted data points."

The `$N$` dimension of `$\nabla_Y$` is `$S \times T$` (batch size × sequence length), so each row corresponds to a token in a specific training example. Tokens that the model already handles well produce negligible gradients; only tokens from "hard" examples or positions continue to generate large gradient signals. The paper reports:

> "We find that for pretraining tasks, such structural sparsity quickly emerges after only a few training epochs. For fine-tuning tasks, the gradient is always sparse during the whole training process."

**The implication for quantization.** A uniform INT4 quantizer applied to `$\nabla_Y$` would face the same tradeoff as with activations: large step size captures the large gradient rows but loses all small gradients; small step size preserves small gradients but clips large ones. But unlike activations, where outliers carry important information that cannot be discarded, **the small gradient rows genuinely contribute little to the parameter update** `$\nabla_W = \nabla_Y^\top \widehat{X}$`. They are small because those tokens are already well-fitted, so their contribution to improving the model is negligible. This creates an opportunity to drop them.

---

#### Bit Splitting (BS): 8-bit Representation via Two 4-bit Quantizations

The first mechanism for gradient quantization is **bit splitting (BS)** , introduced in Section 4.2. The idea is to represent `$\nabla_Y$` with 8 bits of effective precision using two sequential 4-bit quantizations:

$$ \nabla_Y \approx s^\uparrow \nabla_Y^\uparrow + s^\downarrow \nabla_Y^\downarrow $$

where `$s^\uparrow, s^\downarrow \in \mathbb{R}$` are two floating-point scalars, and `$\nabla_Y^\uparrow, \nabla_Y^\downarrow$` are INT4 matrices (values in `$\{-7, \ldots, 7\}$`) representing the **higher 4 bits** and **lower 4 bits** respectively.

**How BS is implemented in practice.** The procedure is:

1. **First pass:** Quantize `$\nabla_Y$` to INT4 normally: `$\nabla_Y \approx s^\uparrow \nabla_Y^\uparrow$`, where `$\nabla_Y^\uparrow = \text{ints}(\nabla_Y / s^\uparrow)$` with `$Q_N = Q_P = 7$`.

2. **Second pass:** Compute the residual `$R = \nabla_Y - s^\uparrow \nabla_Y^\uparrow$` (the error from the first quantization) and quantize *it*: `$R \approx s^\downarrow \nabla_Y^\downarrow$`.

The result is that `$\nabla_Y$` is approximated by `$s^\uparrow \nabla_Y^\uparrow + s^\downarrow \nabla_Y^\downarrow$`, which is exactly analogous to an INT8 representation split across two 4-bit words—`$\nabla_Y^\uparrow$` captures the coarse magnitude, and `$\nabla_Y^\downarrow$` captures the fine detail.

**Why not just use a single 4-bit quantizer?** With only 16 levels, the representable precision is 1 part in 16, or roughly 6% relative error at best. For gradient descent, gradient errors of this magnitude can cause the optimizer to take steps in wrong directions, slowing convergence or causing divergence. BS provides 256 effective levels (8 bits), reducing quantization error substantially for large gradient values. The cost is that now two INT4 MMs are needed per gradient computation instead of one.

---

#### Leverage Score Sampling (LSS): Discarding Small Gradients

BS improves accuracy but doubles computation. **Leverage score sampling (LSS)** recovers the original cost by discarding half the rows—specifically, the rows with the smallest gradient contributions. The key observation is that the matrix multiplication `$\nabla_Y^\top \widehat{X}$` (for weight gradient) can be written as a sum of rank-1 outer products:

$$ \nabla_Y^\top \widehat{X} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} (\nabla_Y)_{i,:}^\top \widehat{X}_{i,:} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \nabla W_i $$

where `$\nabla W_i = (\nabla_Y)_{i,:}^\top \widehat{X}_{i,:}$` is the contribution of token `$i$` to the weight gradient. Each `$\nabla W_i$` is a `$C \times D$` matrix.

**The LSS procedure for weight gradient.** With BS, `$\nabla_Y$` is split into `$s^\uparrow \nabla_Y^\uparrow + s^\downarrow \nabla_Y^\downarrow$`, producing `$2N$` total rows. The paper defines:

$$ \nabla_Y^{\updownarrow} = [s^\uparrow \nabla_Y^\uparrow; s^\downarrow \nabla_Y^\downarrow]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{2N \times C}, \quad \widehat{X}^{\updownarrow} = [\widehat{X}; \widehat{X}] \in \mathbb{R}^{2N \times D} $$

so the weight gradient becomes `$\nabla_Y^{\updownarrow \top} \widehat{X}^{\updownarrow} = \sum_{i=1}^{2N} \nabla W_i$`. LSS assigns each of the `$2N$` rank-1 terms a sampling probability `$p_i \in [0, 1]$` with the constraint `$\sum_{i=1}^{2N} p_i = N$`, and defines independent Bernoulli masks `$m_i \sim \text{Bern}(p_i)$`. The unbiased approximation is:

$$ \nabla_Y^{\updownarrow \top} \widehat{X}^{\updownarrow} \approx \nabla_Y^{\updownarrow \top} \widetilde{M} \widehat{X}^{\updownarrow} = \sum_{i=1}^{2N} \frac{m_i}{p_i} \nabla_Y^{\updownarrow \top}_{:,i} \widehat{X}^{\updownarrow}_{i,:} $$

where `$\widetilde{M} = \text{diag}(m_1/p_1, \ldots, m_{2N}/p_{2N})$`. This is unbiased because `$\mathbb{E}[m_i/p_i] = p_i/p_i = 1$`.

**The operational effect.** Since `$\mathbb{E}[\sum_i m_i] = \sum_i p_i = N$`, only `$N$` of the `$2N$` rows survive in expectation. The two INT4 MMs `$\nabla_Y^\uparrow^\top \widetilde{M}^\uparrow \widehat{X}$` and `$\nabla_Y^\downarrow^\top \widetilde{M}^\downarrow \widehat{X}$` each operate on roughly `$N/2$` rows (on average), so the total cost is approximately `$2 \times (N/2) \times C \times D = NCD$` INT4 MACs, matching the cost of a single naive INT4 MM. The computation saved by dropping small rows is **reinvested** into the higher precision (8-bit) representation of the large rows.

**How to choose `$p_i$` to minimize variance.** The paper proves Proposition 4.1, which gives the variance of the LSS estimator for the weight gradient:

$$ \text{Var}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{2N} \frac{m_i}{p_i} \nabla_Y^{\updownarrow \top}_{:,i} \widehat{X}^{\updownarrow}_{i,:}\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{2N} \frac{1 - p_i}{p_i} \|\nabla_Y^{\updownarrow}_{i,:}\|^2 \|\widehat{X}^{\updownarrow}_{i,:}\|^2 $$

where `$\text{Var}[X] := \mathbb{E}[\|X - \mathbb{E}[X]\|^2_F]$` is the Frobenius-norm variance.

The term `$c_i := \|\nabla_Y^{\updownarrow}_{i,:}\| \|\widehat{X}^{\updownarrow}_{i,:}\|$` is called the **leverage score**—a measure of how much the `$i$`-th row contributes to the product on average.

**Optimal sampling probabilities.** By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{2N} \frac{c_i^2}{p_i} = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{2N} \frac{c_i^2}{p_i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2N} p_i\right) \geq \left(\sum_{i=1}^{2N} c_i\right)^2 $$

with equality when `$p_i \propto c_i$`. That is, the variance-minimizing strategy is to sample rows proportionally to their leverage scores. Rows with large gradient norms and large corresponding activation norms get higher probability of being retained; rows with small norms get discarded.

**Why this works in practice.** Referring back to Figure 2, the leverage scores `$c_i$` are highly non-uniform—a few rows dominate. When the leverage scores are diverse, LSS can achieve near-exact approximation of the true matrix product with far fewer rows than uniform sampling. The paper's intuition (Section 4.2):

> "LSS can approximate the MM well with significantly lower computational cost when the leverage scores `$\{c_i\}$` are diverse, which is indeed the case as shown in Fig. 2."

**Handling the probability constraint.** The optimal probabilities `$p_i \propto c_i$` may exceed 1 (if some leverage scores are very large relative to others). The paper describes an iterative adjustment procedure in Appendix A.2:

1. Set initial probabilities proportional to `$c_i$`, scaled to sum to `$N$`.
2. Clamp all `$p_i$` exceeding 1 to exactly 1.
3. Redistribute the "excess" probability mass among the remaining `$p_i < 1$`.
4. Repeat until all `$p_i \in [0, 1]$`.

In the actual GPU implementation (Appendix A.6), the paper simplifies further: **retain any row with Euclidean norm greater than 0**. This heuristic avoids the iterative adjustment and works well empirically because typical leverage score distributions are highly skewed—many rows have near-zero scores and can be safely dropped.

**LSS for activation gradient (Appendix B.2).** The activation gradient `$\nabla_X$` requires computing `$\nabla_Y \widehat{W}$`. The paper applies LSS by viewing this as:

$$ \widehat{I}^{\updownarrow} \nabla_Y^{\updownarrow} \widehat{W} $$

where `$\widehat{I}^{\updownarrow} = [I; I] \in \mathbb{Z}^{N \times 2N}$` (stacking two `$N \times N$` identity matrices) and `$\nabla_Y^{\updownarrow} = [s^\uparrow \nabla_Y^\uparrow; s^\downarrow \nabla_Y^\downarrow] \in \mathbb{R}^{2N \times C}$`. The `$\widehat{I}^{\updownarrow} \nabla_Y^{\updownarrow}$` product is the sum of `$2N$` terms, and LSS assigns sampling probabilities `$p_i$` to each term. The derivation (Proposition B.2) yields leverage score `$c_i := \|\nabla_Y^{\updownarrow}_{i,:}\|$` (only the gradient norm, not multiplied by the identity matrix norm, since each column of `$\widehat{I}^{\updownarrow}$` has unit norm). The variance-optimal condition is again `$p_i \propto c_i$`.

---

#### Gradients of Quantization Step Sizes

The paper also computes gradients for the learnable step sizes `$s_X$` and `$s_W$` (Appendix A.3). Applying the chain rule to the LSQ formulation:

$$ \nabla_{s_W} = g(s_W) \left(\nabla_Y^\top \widehat{X}\right) \circ \delta_W(s_W) $$

$$ \nabla_{s_X} = g(s_X) \left(\nabla_Y \widehat{W}\right) \circ \delta_X(s_X) $$

where:

- `$g(s_W) = 1 / \sqrt{Q_P N_W}$` is a gradient scaling factor with `$N_W$` being the number of elements in `$W$`.
- `$\delta_W(s_W) = \text{ints}_W(W) - I_W \circ (W / s_W)$` is the difference between the rounded integer and the pre-rounding scaled value (for elements within the clipping range, this captures the quantization error; for clipped elements, `$I_W = 0$` so the term vanishes).
- Analogous definitions hold for `$g(s_X)$` and `$\delta_X(s_X)$`.

Crucially, the expensive matrix multiplications `$\nabla_Y^\top \widehat{X}$` and `$\nabla_Y \widehat{W}$` are **already computed** for the weight and activation gradients, so the step size gradients require only the cheap element-wise multiplications with `$\delta$`. No additional GEMMs are needed.

---

#### Cold-Start Problem and Heuristic Initialization (Appendix A.4)

When training from scratch (random initialization), the distributions of weights and activations can change dramatically in the first few iterations. Jointly learning the quantization step sizes during this turbulent phase can cause instability. The paper's remedy:

> "We do not learn the step size in the first few iterations, and use a heuristic rule to dynamically set the step size for each tensor `$X$` to `$2 \cdot \text{mean}(X) / \sqrt{Q_P}$` in each iteration."

This ensures the quantization range initially covers the typical magnitude of values (the mean is roughly the center of the distribution, and `$2 / \sqrt{Q_P}$` scales it). After the initial transient, the step sizes become learnable parameters optimized by SGD along with the weights.

---

#### GPU Implementation Details (Appendix A.6)

The paper provides a detailed description of how the algorithms map to GPU hardware. The key implementation aspects:

**Data packing for INT4.** Modern GPUs represent data at byte (INT8) granularity. To store two 4-bit values per byte:

> "We actually use INT8 data type to represent quantized data and pack two adjacent data into one data using `(data[1] << 4) | (data[0] & 15)`."

**Matrix layout requirements.** NVIDIA's cutlass INT4 tensor core GEMM requires the first operand `$A$` in row-major order and the second operand `$B$` in column-major order. PyTorch tensors are row-major by default, so:

> "We have to use Transpose+Contiguous operations to make it ColMajor, which is very time-consuming and needs further optimization in the future."

**Alignment constraint for INT4 tensor cores.** The inner dimension `$K$` of the GEMM must be a multiple of 32 for tensor core address alignment. This is satisfied automatically in the forward pass, but after LSS drops rows in the backward pass, the remaining rows may not form a tensor with `$K$` a multiple of 32:

> "We need zero_padding the sampled matrix so that `$K$` can be a multiple of 32."

**Kernel fusion for efficiency.** The quantization and leverage score calculation are fused into single CUDA kernels where possible to reduce kernel launch overhead and memory traffic. The paper reports:

- Element-wise kernels: block size 256, grid size `input.numel() / 256`.
- Reduction kernels (sum, min/max): block size 32, grid size equal to the number of rows, with intra-row reduction to the first 32 elements.

**Leverage score sharing.** In the GPU implementation:

> "We omit the Quantization and Leverage Score Calculating process in LSSinput, and use the same value as LSSWeight to accelerate the training process."

That is, the leverage scores computed for the weight gradient (which involve both `$\|\nabla_Y^{\updownarrow}_{i,:}\|$` and `$\|\widehat{X}_{i,:}\|$`) are reused for the activation gradient to avoid recomputation.

**The claims about operator speedup (Figure 4).** On an RTX 3090 (142 FP16 TFLOPS peak, 568 INT4 TFLOPS peak), the paper's prototypical operators achieve up to 2.2× speedup over FP16 GEMM for large matrix sizes. The speedup increases with matrix size because the quantization overhead (Hadamard transform, packing, dequantization) is amortized over more FLOPs. The paper is transparent that this is not fully optimized:

> "We emphasize that our implementation is not fully optimized. For example, the backward computation requires an INT4 MM in the form of `$Y = AB$`, while cutlass only supports `$Y = AB^\top$`, so explicit transpose is required. We also do not fuse the linear operators with nonlinearities and normalizations. Therefore, the results cannot fully reflect the potential of INT4 training algorithms."

---

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

**LSQ for base quantization** over dynamic quantization: cheaper (no per-iteration scale computation), differentiable via STE, and the learned scale adapts during training.

**Block-diagonal Hadamard for forward pass** over per-channel quantization or SmoothQuant: redistributes outlier energy without sacrificing inlier precision, which is essential at 4 bits where the number of levels is too small to tolerate any waste.

**Bit splitting for backward pass** over a single 4-bit quantizer: provides 8-bit effective precision for the large gradients that dominate the weight update, while accepting that small gradients have limited impact.

**Leverage score sampling** over uniform row dropping: variance-optimal unbiased estimation of matrix products, with formal guarantees from RandNLA, ensuring that the approximation error is minimized for a given sampling budget.

**STE for gradient flow** through non-differentiable quantization: standard technique that works empirically; makes the entire pipeline trainable end-to-end without continuous relaxations.

**Cold-start heuristic** for step sizes: prevents training instability during the initial transient phase when distributions shift rapidly.

**Adaptive `$k$` selection** per layer: optimizes the tradeoff between outlier suppression (larger `$k$`) and Hadamard overhead (larger `$k$` costs more), customized to each layer's activation statistics.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing 4-bit Training Around Data Structure Rather Than Numerical Format Design

The dominant approach in prior 4-bit FQT work was fundamentally format-centric: design a better number system that more faithfully approximates floating-point values across a wide dynamic range. Sun et al. (2020) used FP4 (4-bit floating-point with shared exponent); Chmiel et al. (2021) used a custom logarithmic format. Both approaches implicitly assume that the core challenge is representational—that if we had a quantization grid flexible enough to capture both small and large values, 4-bit training would work. They treat the matrices being quantized as generic arrays of numbers with challenging dynamic range, and they solve the problem by improving the container.

This paper makes a fundamentally different conceptual move. Rather than asking "what number system best approximates arbitrary floating-point values?", it asks **"what mathematical structures do transformer activations and gradients actually possess, and how can we exploit those structures to make a simple fixed integer format work?"** The answer is not a new format but a set of *preconditioning* transformations applied before quantization: the Hadamard rotation for activations (using orthogonality to spread outliers without information loss) and leverage-score-based importance sampling for gradients (using structural sparsity to allocate precision where it matters). The quantizer itself remains the simplest possible—uniform INT4 with a learned scale—but the matrices being quantized are transformed to match the quantizer's strengths.

This reframing is significant beyond the immediate accuracy gains because it opens a new design space for low-precision training. The format-centric approach hits diminishing returns: at 4 bits, you have only 16 values regardless of format, so format design can only rearrange the representable points, not create more of them. The structure-centric approach recognizes that the way out is not to squeeze more information into the format, but to **put the right information into the format by transforming the data**. This connects 4-bit training to the rich literature on randomized numerical linear algebra (RandNLA), where preconditioning matrices before randomized sketching or subsampling is standard practice—a connection the field had not previously exploited.

The evidence for this reframing's power is in the ablation study (Figure 3a): maintaining just 1% of activation entries in FP (the "outlier" method) nearly matches full FP accuracy under 4-bit forward quantization, proving that outliers are the *only* significant obstacle—the quantization format itself is adequate for >99% of values. This diagnostic alone justifies the structure-centric approach: don't redesign the format; fix the outlier problem directly. The Hadamard quantizer achieves comparable accuracy to the oracle "maintain outliers" method without any per-entry FP exceptions, validating that the transformation successfully addresses the identified structural bottleneck.

### Innovation 2: Unifying Forward and Backward Quantization Under a RandNLA Framework

Prior FQT work treated forward and backward quantization as largely independent problems—activations need one kind of quantizer, gradients need another, and the solutions were developed ad hoc for each. The LSQ+LUQ baseline (Chmiel et al., 2021) exemplifies this: LSQ for forward (a learned uniform quantizer designed for inference), LUQ for backward (a logarithmic quantizer designed for gradient dynamic range). The two quantizers share no conceptual framework.

This paper unifies both problems under the lens of **randomized matrix multiplication approximation** from RandNLA. The central operation in both forward and backward passes is a matrix product `$A B$`, and the paper frames quantization as an approximate computation of that product. For the forward pass, the Hadamard transform is a standard RandNLA technique for making leverage scores approximately uniform before subsampling—here applied to make the quantization grid equally effective across all dimensions. For the backward pass, bit splitting and leverage score sampling directly implement the RandNLA idea of approximate matrix multiplication via importance sampling of outer products, with formal variance bounds (Propositions 4.1 and B.2) and optimality conditions (`$p_i \propto c_i$`).

This unification is intellectually significant because it transforms 4-bit training from a collection of quantization heuristics into a **principled approximation algorithm with known error characteristics**. The variance formulas are not merely derivations—they provide an explicit, computable diagnostic for when the approximation will succeed (diverse leverage scores → low variance) and when it will fail (uniform leverage scores → no benefit from sampling). This connects FQT to decades of theoretical work on randomized matrix algorithms (Drineas & Mahoney, 2016), providing a foundation for analyzing and improving low-precision training that the format-centric approach lacked.

The empirical consequence of this unification is that the same intellectual tools solve both problems. The Hadamard transform in the forward pass reduces to LSQ when `$k = 0$` (block size 1, no mixing); the LSS in the backward pass reduces to uniform sampling when leverage scores are equal. The framework nests simpler methods as special cases and provides a clear axis for trading off accuracy against computation (the block size `$k$` in HQ, the sampling rate in LSS).

### Innovation 3: Identifying and Exploiting Token-Wise Gradient Sparsity as a Compute-Reallocation Opportunity

The observation that gradients are sparse is not new—gradient compression methods have exploited sparsity for communication efficiency in distributed training for years. What is distinctive here is the paper's insight that **gradient sparsity in overparameterized transformers is structural (concentrated on a few tokens) rather than random, and that this structure can be exploited not just to save computation but to *reinvest* it for higher precision where it matters**.

The key diagnostic move is in Figure 2 and Section 4.1: the per-row gradient norm distribution shows that a few tokens account for almost all the gradient mass, while most tokens contribute effectively zero. Rather than treating this as a lossy compression problem (compress all gradients, accept error everywhere), the paper treats it as a **resource allocation problem**: computation is a budget, and we should spend it where the gradients are large. The bit-splitting mechanism (representing gradients with 8 effective bits) would normally double the computational cost, but leverage score sampling halves it back by discarding small rows—the computation "saved" from small gradients is exactly what funds the extra precision for large ones.

This is a conceptual shift from "quantization error minimization" to "approximation quality optimization under a compute budget." The variance formulas in Propositions 4.1 and B.2 make this formal: they give the expected squared error of the matrix product approximation as a function of the sampling probabilities, and the optimal probabilities `$p_i \propto c_i$` are exactly those that minimize this error subject to the budget constraint `$\sum_i p_i = N$`. The small-gradient rows are not merely "discarded"—they are discarded because the formal analysis says they *should* be to minimize approximation error of the matrix product that affects the weight update.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond the immediate algorithm. It suggests that the right way to think about gradient quantization is not "how do we fit gradients into fewer bits?" but "how do we allocate a fixed bit budget across gradient entries to maximize the quality of the resulting parameter update?" This connects to ideas from statistical estimation (optimal experimental design, where you allocate measurement resources to reduce estimator variance) and suggests future work on adaptive bit-allocation schemes that go beyond the binary keep/discard of LSS.

Evidence for the effectiveness of this reallocation appears in Figure 3b: at 4 bits, LSS matches or slightly exceeds the accuracy of LUQ (which uses a logarithmic format designed specifically for gradient dynamic range), despite LSS using only standard INT4 arithmetic. This parity is remarkable because LUQ's logarithmic format is essentially a hardware-emulation of floating-point's non-uniform quantization, while LSS achieves the same effect through structural exploitation—selective precision rather than format-driven precision.

### Innovation 4: Solving the Activation Outlier Problem Without Sacrificing Inlier Precision—A Hard Constraint at 4 Bits

Prior work on activation quantization for transformers recognized the outlier problem. SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022) addressed it by mathematically migrating quantization difficulty from activations to weights through per-channel scaling: scale down outlier activation channels, scale up corresponding weight channels, so the matrix product is unchanged but activations become easier to quantize. This works at 8 bits (SmoothQuant achieves 8-bit post-training quantization of LLMs) because even after the precision of inliers is somewhat degraded by the scaling, 8 bits (256 levels) leave enough granularity for accurate representation.

The paper's crucial diagnostic (Appendix C.3, Table 4) is that **this approach catastrophically fails at 4 bits**. SmoothQuant with LSQ achieves 0% accuracy on CoLA at 4 bits, compared to 57.7% at 8 bits. The reason is fundamental: SmoothQuant's scaling sacrifices inlier precision to exactly preserve outliers, and at 4 bits, the 16 quantization levels are too few to tolerate this sacrifice. The inliers, which constitute >99% of the tensor entries, get quantized so coarsely that the information they carry is effectively destroyed.

The Hadamard quantizer represents a genuinely different solution principle: **rather than trading off one set of values against another, eliminate the distinction**. The orthogonal Hadamard transform mixes outlier energy across dimensions, making all entries approximately equal in magnitude after transformation. There is no longer an "outlier problem" to solve because there are no longer outliers—every dimension has roughly the same variance, so a single learned step size works well for all entries. This is proven by the extreme-case analysis: a coordinate vector `$e_i$` (all energy in one dimension) transforms to `$2^{-k/2}\mathbf{1}$` (equal energy in all dimensions). Real activations are not single-spike vectors, but the mixing effect is real and demonstrated in Figure 1.

This innovation is a fundamental advance rather than incremental because it **breaks the tradeoff that constrained all previous approaches**. Per-channel quantization, outlier clipping, and SmoothQuant all fundamentally manage a tradeoff; the Hadamard approach makes the tradeoff unnecessary. The cost is the additional Hadamard transform computation, but the paper shows this overhead is small relative to the INT4 GEMM savings when the block size is much smaller than the hidden dimension.

The practical impact is seen most dramatically in the BERT-large GLUE results (Table 1): LSQ+LUQ achieves 55.93 average accuracy (from 84.57 FP), a catastrophic 28.6-point drop, while HQ+LSS achieves 82.25—a recovery of over 26 points. Large models have more extreme outliers (as the paper notes, pretrained models "contain more outliers than random initialization"), so the outlier problem is more severe, and HQ's advantage correspondingly larger. This suggests that the Hadamard approach is not merely an alternative to existing methods but is **necessary** for 4-bit training of large transformers, where the outlier magnitude exceeds what any tradeoff-based method can accommodate at 4 bits.

### Innovation 5: Hardware Compatibility as a First-Order Design Constraint Yielding a Different Solution Space

This innovation is more about research methodology than algorithm design, but it represents an important intellectual contribution to the FQT literature. Prior 4-bit training papers (Sun et al., 2020; Chmiel et al., 2021) treated hardware compatibility as a downstream concern—first design the algorithm to maximize accuracy, then worry about implementation later. This led to solutions (FP4, logarithmic formats) that achieve good accuracy but require custom arithmetic units not present in contemporary GPUs. The papers were algorithmically successful but practically undeliverable.

This paper inverts the priority: **hardware compatibility is a binding constraint from the start**, and the algorithm must be designed within the INT4 arithmetic that GPU tensor cores natively support. This constraint eliminates the format-design degree of freedom that prior work relied on and forces innovation in a different direction—toward data transformations and sampling strategies that make the fixed INT4 format work.

The significance of this methodological move is that it **redefines what counts as a solution to the 4-bit training problem**. Under the prior paradigm, a solution is an algorithm that trains at 4 bits with acceptable accuracy. Under this paper's paradigm, a solution is an algorithm that trains at 4 bits with acceptable accuracy *and can actually run faster on real hardware*. The paper demonstrates this concretely: Figure 4 shows the INT4 operators achieving up to 2.2× speedup over FP16 on an RTX 3090, and Figure 5 shows end-to-end training speedups of up to 35.1% on 8×A100 systems. These speedups are the empirical proof that the hardware-compatible approach pays off in practice, not just in principle.

This methodological contribution has implications beyond this specific paper. It suggests that the FQT field should treat hardware constraints as **generative of research problems** rather than as annoying implementation details. What makes for a good low-precision format is not just its approximation-theoretic properties (how well it represents numbers) but its systems properties (whether the operations are fast on available hardware). This aligns FQT with the broader trend in ML systems research toward hardware-algorithm co-design, where algorithm choices are made with explicit awareness of the hardware they will run on.

The paper is honest about the limitations of its current implementation—unfused operators, unnecessary transposes, no kernel fusion with nonlinearities—but frames these not as weaknesses but as evidence that the speedup numbers are **lower bounds**. An optimized implementation would achieve larger gains, making the case for hardware-compatible 4-bit training even stronger. The 35.1% end-to-end speedup on BERT-large training, despite the acknowledged implementation inefficiencies, demonstrates that the algorithmic approach is sound and the engineering path to larger gains is clear.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on five distinct task families: (1) **GLUE** dev-set (Wang et al., 2018) for natural language understanding fine-tuning—a standard 9-task benchmark where the paper reports per-task metrics and averages; (2) **SQuAD v1** (Rajpurkar et al., 2016) and **SQuAD v2** (Rajpurkar et al., 2018) for extractive question answering, plus **Adversarial QA** (Bartolo et al., 2020) for robustness testing; (3) **SWAG** (Zellers et al., 2018) for grounded commonsense inference and **CoNLL-2003** (Sang & De Meulder, 2003) for named entity recognition; (4) **WMT 14 En-De** (Bojar et al., 2014) for machine translation pretraining; and (5) **CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100** (Krizhevsky et al., 2009) and **ImageNet1k** (Deng et al., 2009) for image classification fine-tuning, plus ImageNet1k for DeiT pretraining (Touvron et al., 2021). The paper uses the standard splits for each dataset without modification.

- **Base model(s).** The language model fine-tuning experiments use **BERT-base-uncased** and **BERT-large-uncased** (Devlin et al., 2019), representing the standard pretrained transformer with 110M and 340M parameters respectively. Machine translation pretraining uses a **Transformer-base** model (Vaswani et al., 2017) with the Fairseq recipe. Vision experiments use **ViT-B/32, ViT-L/32, and ViT-L/16** (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020) initialized from ImageNet21k pretraining for fine-tuning, and **DeiT-Small** (Touvron et al., 2021) for pretraining from scratch. These models span the range from 22M (DeiT-Small) to ~340M (BERT-large) parameters, chosen (per the authors' statement in the abstract) to demonstrate wide applicability without extending to the extremely large regime where "even INT8 training is still an open problem."

- **Metrics.** Standard task-specific metrics are used: **accuracy** for GLUE classification tasks (CoLA uses Matthews correlation), RTE, MRPC, SST-2, QNLI, QQP, MNLI, SWAG, and CoNLL; **F1 score** for SQuAD v1/v2 and Adversarial QA; **BLEU and SacreBLEU** (Papineni et al., 2002; Post, 2018) for WMT machine translation; **Top-1 accuracy** for CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet1k. For experiments with multiple random seeds, standard deviation is reported as subscripts in Table 1 (e.g., "82.67₀.₂₄" for FP BERT-base GLUE average).

- **Baselines.** Four baselines are compared throughout Table 1 and the ablation figures: (1) **FP** — standard full-precision FP16 training with PyTorch AMP, representing the accuracy ceiling; (2) **INT8** — the 8-bit integer training method of Banner et al. (2018), representing the established lower-precision standard; (3) **LSQ+LUQ** — learned step size quantization for forward activations/weights (Esser et al., 2019) combined with logarithmic unbiased quantization for backward gradients (Chmiel et al., 2021), representing the state-of-the-art 4-bit training method that uses custom numerical formats unsupported on current hardware; (4) **Ultra-low** — the FP4 training method of Sun et al. (2020), reported only on the WMT machine translation task (25.4 BLEU) since no public implementation exists. The paper's method is labeled **HQ+LSS** throughout.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For accuracy experiments, compute is not measured in "generations" (this is not a sampling-based test-time compute paper); rather, the training is run to convergence under standard training recipes for each task, and the *numerical precision* of matrix multiplications is the independent variable. For efficiency experiments (Section 5.3), compute is measured in two ways: **operator throughput** (TFLOPs) on an RTX 3090 GPU comparing individual INT4 GEMM implementations against FP16 baselines (Figure 4), and **end-to-end training time** (seconds per epoch) on 8×A100 systems comparing full training pipelines (Figure 5, Table 3). The paper does not use FLOPs-matched comparisons between methods—all methods train the same architecture with the same hyperparameters, differing only in the precision of linear operations.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For fine-tuning tasks on BERT-base and ViT-B/32, each experiment is repeated **three times** with different random seeds, and standard deviations are reported as subscripts in Table 1. For larger models (BERT-large, ViT-L, WMT pretraining, DeiT pretraining), single runs are reported, which the paper acknowledges by omitting standard deviation subscripts. There is no cross-validation or holdout-based strategy selection—the method itself has no hyperparameters that are selected per-task based on validation performance (unlike the compute-optimal approach in other test-time compute papers). The Hadamard block size `$k$` is automatically selected per layer using the MSE-based criterion described in Appendix A.5, not tuned per task.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Language Model Fine-Tuning: GLUE Benchmark

**Headline result (Table 1, Table 2):** Across BERT-base on the GLUE dev-set, HQ+LSS achieves **80.81 average accuracy**, compared to 82.67 for FP, 81.45 for INT8, and 75.29 for LSQ+LUQ. This represents a recovery of 5.5 percentage points over LSQ+LUQ (closing 72% of the gap between LSQ+LUQ and FP). On BERT-large, the advantage is dramatic: HQ+LSS achieves **82.25 average**, compared to 84.57 (FP), 82.74 (INT8), and 55.93 (LSQ+LUQ)—a recovery of over 26 points, with LSQ+LUQ essentially collapsing on this larger model.

**Per-task breakdown (Table 2).** The task-level results reveal that the difficulty of quantization varies substantially across GLUE tasks:

- On **CoLA** (linguistic acceptability, widely considered the most challenging GLUE task), BERT-base FP achieves 56.89, INT8 achieves 56.15, LSQ+LUQ collapses to 18.76, and HQ+LSS recovers to **52.46**. BERT-large shows an even starker pattern: FP 60.33, INT8 58.80, LSQ+LUQ **0.00** (complete failure), and HQ+LSS achieves **53.46**. This is the task where the outlier problem is most severe—CoLA's small training set (8.5k examples) and complex linguistic judgments make it highly sensitive to quantization noise.

- On **RTE** (recognizing textual entailment, another challenging small-dataset task), BERT-base: FP 64.80, INT8 62.27, LSQ+LUQ 56.80, HQ+LSS **62.45**; BERT-large: FP 71.12, INT8 63.71, LSQ+LUQ 53.06, HQ+LSS **64.62**. HQ+LSS matches or exceeds INT8 on both model sizes.

- On **SST-2** (sentiment analysis, a relatively easy task with 67k training examples), BERT-base: FP 92.72, INT8 92.37, LSQ+LUQ 90.37, HQ+LSS **92.49**. BERT-large: FP 93.98, INT8 93.75, LSQ+LUQ 83.94, HQ+LSS **93.52**. The gap between LSQ+LUQ and HQ+LSS is 2.1 points on BERT-base but 9.6 points on BERT-large.

- On **QNLI** (question-answering NLI), BERT-large shows LSQ+LUQ achieving only **63.18 with standard deviation 13.10**, indicating not just low accuracy but extreme instability across runs—the training sometimes partially succeeds and sometimes collapses. HQ+LSS achieves 91.53 with standard deviation 0.38, matching INT8 stability.

- On **MNLI** (multi-genre NLI), BERT-large LSQ+LUQ scores 33.42 (matched) and 33.54 (mismatched), barely above random chance (33% for 3-way classification), while HQ+LSS achieves 85.86 and 85.82.

The standard deviations for LSQ+LUQ on BERT-large are notably large (e.g., 13.10 on QNLI, 12.44 on QQP), indicating that the method is not just inaccurate but **unstable**—training trajectories diverge significantly depending on random seed. HQ+LSS standard deviations remain comparable to FP (e.g., 0.38 on QNLI vs. 0.05 for FP), suggesting that the Hadamard quantizer provides more reliable training dynamics.

**Why BERT-large is harder than BERT-base.** The paper attributes this to the outlier severity difference noted in Section 3.2: "the pre-train model contains more outliers than random initialization," and larger pretrained models have more extreme outlier channels. The LSQ+LUQ baseline uses a logarithmic quantizer for gradients (which handles gradient dynamic range) but only LSQ for forward activations—it has no mechanism for activation outliers. On BERT-base, the outliers are manageable enough that LSQ alone degrades accuracy by ~7.4 points (82.67 → 75.29). On BERT-large, with more extreme outliers, LSQ alone causes near-total collapse (84.57 → 55.93). HQ+LSS adds the Hadamard transform specifically to address this, recovering most of the lost accuracy.

#### Language Model Fine-Tuning: Question Answering and Other Tasks

**Headline results (Table 1):** On extractive QA tasks, HQ+LSS achieves **87.60 F1 on SQuAD v1** (FP: 88.32, INT8: 88.42, LSQ+LUQ: 85.75), **74.63 F1 on SQuAD v2** (FP: 76.04, INT8: 75.63, LSQ+LUQ: 71.02), and **38.70 F1 on Adversarial QA** (FP: 40.99, INT8: 40.17, LSQ+LUQ: 31.85). On SWAG, HQ+LSS achieves **77.49 accuracy** (FP: 79.84, INT8: 79.18, LSQ+LUQ: 70.79). On CoNLL-2003, HQ+LSS achieves **91.90 accuracy** (FP: 93.38, INT8: 93.13, LSQ+LUQ: 87.63).

**The Adversarial QA result is particularly informative.** This dataset (Bartolo et al., 2020) was designed to be challenging—it contains questions that are adversarial to standard QA models, requiring deeper reasoning. The FP accuracy of only 40.99 indicates the task is at the frontier of BERT-base's capability. LSQ+LUQ degrades to 31.85 (a 9.1-point drop), while HQ+LSS degrades only to 38.70 (a 2.3-point drop). This suggests that tasks requiring precise reasoning are especially vulnerable to the activation outlier problem—when the model must carefully attend to specific tokens, quantization noise on outlier channels can destroy the signal.

**The SQuAD v2 gap.** SQuAD v2 includes unanswerable questions, requiring the model to distinguish answerable from unanswerable. HQ+LSS drops 1.4 points from FP (76.04 → 74.63), compared to LSQ+LUQ's 5.0-point drop. The smaller degradation suggests that the Hadamard quantizer preserves the activation patterns needed for the model's confidence calibration on unanswerable questions.

#### Machine Translation Pretraining

**Headline result (Table 1):** On WMT 14 En-De with Transformer-base trained from scratch, FP achieves 27.5 BLEU (original) / 26.5 SacreBLEU. INT8 achieves **25.4 BLEU** (reported as "Ultra-low" in the table—the paper notes this is from Sun et al.'s paper, not reproduced). HQ+LSS achieves **25.57 SacreBLEU**, compared to 26.5 SacreBLEU for FP—a degradation of approximately 0.9 SacreBLEU points.

**Comparison with prior 4-bit methods.** The "Ultra-low" method (Sun et al., 2020) reports 25.4 BLEU (original), which the paper compares against FP at 27.5 BLEU (original)—a 2.1 BLEU drop. LSQ+LUQ (Chmiel et al., 2021) reports a 0.3 BLEU drop, but the paper notes that this result comes from the LUQ paper and may not use the same Fairseq recipe. The paper's own reproduction with Fairseq yields FP SacreBLEU of 26.5 and HQ+LSS SacreBLEU of 25.57. The paper claims this is "comparable with existing methods for this pretraining task, and it supports contemporary hardware."

**The pretraining distinction.** This is the only pretraining-from-scratch language task in the paper (the GLUE, SQuAD, etc. experiments are fine-tuning). The paper notes in Section 3.2 that pretraining has fewer outliers than fine-tuning (random initialization produces less extreme activations than pretrained models), which would predict that the Hadamard quantizer's advantage over LSQ should be smaller for pretraining. The WMT result is consistent with this: the degradation is modest (0.9 SacreBLEU) but the paper doesn't report LSQ+LUQ on this task for direct comparison, making it difficult to assess how much HQ specifically helps vs. LSQ in the pretraining setting.

#### Image Classification: ViT Fine-Tuning

**Headline results (Table 1):** On CIFAR-10 fine-tuning, ViT-B/32 with HQ+LSS achieves **98.36% Top-1 accuracy** (FP: 98.77, INT8: 98.59, LSQ+LUQ: 97.76), and ViT-L/32 achieves **98.47** (FP: 98.98, INT8: 98.76, LSQ+LUQ: 98.38). On CIFAR-100, the more challenging fine-grained task, ViT-B/32 with HQ+LSS achieves **89.78** (FP: 91.94, INT8: 90.99, LSQ+LUQ: 88.63), and ViT-L/32 achieves **91.13** (FP: 93.07, INT8: 92.20, LSQ+LUQ: 90.97).

**ImageNet1k fine-tuning provides the most vision-relevant results.** On the standard benchmark for vision transformers:

- ViT-B/32: FP 81.88, INT8 80.42, LSQ+LUQ 77.25, HQ+LSS **79.18** — a 1.9-point improvement over LSQ+LUQ.
- ViT-L/32: FP 81.62, INT8 81.30, LSQ+LUQ 77.41, HQ+LSS **80.06** — a 2.7-point improvement over LSQ+LUQ.
- ViT-L/16: FP 84.55, INT8 83.05, LSQ+LUQ 82.40, HQ+LSS **82.61** — a 0.2-point improvement over LSQ+LUQ.

**The ViT-L/16 result requires scrutiny.** LSQ+LUQ achieves 82.40 on ViT-L/16, which is already only 2.15 points below FP (84.55). HQ+LSS adds only 0.21 points over LSQ+LUQ. This is a notably smaller gain than on ViT-B/32 (1.93 points) or ViT-L/32 (2.65 points). The paper doesn't discuss this difference explicitly, but one possible explanation is that ViT-L/16, with its finer patch granularity (16×16 vs. 32×32), has different activation statistics—perhaps the feature-wise outlier structure is less pronounced when the patch size is smaller and the model processes more tokens with richer local information. Alternatively, the FP baseline for ViT-L/16 is higher (84.55), leaving less room for improvement over LSQ+LUQ.

**Why CIFAR-100 is harder for INT8 too.** The paper notes: "On CIFAR100, INT8 already has ~1% accuracy degradation, which shows its difficulty." The INT8 baseline drops from 91.94 (FP) to 90.99 on ViT-B/32, while the FP-to-INT8 gap on CIFAR-10 is only 0.18 points (98.77 → 98.59). The paper attributes this to CIFAR-100's finer classification granularity (100 classes vs. 10), which makes the model more sensitive to representation quality—even 8-bit quantization introduces enough noise to measurably degrade fine-grained discrimination.

#### Image Classification: DeiT Pretraining

**Headline result (Table 1):** Training DeiT-Small from scratch on ImageNet1k, FP achieves **73.1% Top-1**, INT8 achieves **70.95**, LSQ+LUQ achieves **69.96**, and HQ+LSS achieves **69.18**. Notably, HQ+LSS is **0.78 points worse than LSQ+LUQ** on this pretraining task.

**This is the only task where HQ+LSS underperforms LSQ+LUQ.** The paper does not elaborate on this result in the main text, only reporting it in Table 1. The negative result is important because it suggests conditions under which the Hadamard quantizer may not help: pretraining from scratch with vision transformers. As noted in Section 3.2, pretraining from random initialization produces fewer activation outliers than fine-tuning from pretrained weights. If outliers are less severe, the Hadamard transform's overhead (extra FP16 computation, additional quantization of transformed matrices) may outweigh its outlier-suppression benefits. The LSQ+LUQ method, with its logarithmic gradient quantizer, may be better suited to the gradient distribution of vision transformer pretraining. This result also highlights that the paper's claims about HQ+LSS's superiority are primarily supported for **fine-tuning** settings rather than **pretraining** settings—the WMT result is comparable but not superior to prior work, and the DeiT result is actually worse.

#### Computational Efficiency

**Operator-level throughput (Figure 4).** On an NVIDIA RTX 3090 (142 FP16 TFLOPS peak, 568 INT4 TFLOPS peak), the paper compares the throughput of its proposed operators against a cutlass FP16 tensor-core GEMM baseline:

> "As the matrix size grows, the overhead of quantization diminishes and our INT4 operators can be up to 2.2 times faster compared with FP16 MM."

The Figure 4 curves show that HQ (forward) throughput starts below FP16 for small matrices (where quantization overhead dominates) and crosses over at moderate sizes, eventually reaching approximately 1.5–1.8× FP16 at the largest tested sizes. LSSWeight and LSSAct show similar scaling behavior but typically slightly lower throughput than HQ due to the additional sampling and bit-splitting operations.

**Time proportion analysis (Figure 6, Appendix C.5).** The paper breaks down where time is spent within each operator:

- In **HQ**, the Hadamard multiplication consumes a significant fraction at small matrix sizes but diminishes as the GEMM dominates at larger sizes. The "pack" operation (packing two INT4 values into one INT8 byte) is consistently a small fraction.
- In **LSSWeight**, the "quantize" step (bit splitting into higher and lower 4 bits) is the largest non-GEMM overhead, followed by leverage score computation. The "sample" operation (selecting rows based on probabilities) is cheap.
- In **LSSAct**, the quantize and leverage steps are shared with LSSWeight (as noted in Appendix A.6: "We omit the Quantization and Leverage Score Calculating process in LSSinput, and use the same value as LSSWeight to accelerate the training process"), so the breakdown reflects primarily GEMM + dequantize overhead.

**End-to-end training speedup (Figure 5, Table 3).** On 8×A100 systems with distributed data parallel training, the paper reports speedups for fine-tuning BERT-large and GPT2-base on the SuperGLUE RTE dataset, varying hidden dimensions and batch sizes to maximize GPU memory utilization:

> "Our INT4 training algorithm can achieve up to 35.1% speedup for BERT-style models and up to 26.5% speedup for GPT-style models."

The speedup is not uniform across configurations. From Table 3:

- **BERT-large** speedups range from **−25.5% (slowdown)** at (2560, 10240, 2048) to **+35.1%** at (8960, 35840, 480), then decline to +15.3% at (14400, 57600, 96). The slowdown at small hidden dimension (2560) occurs because the quantization overhead (Hadamard transform, packing, dequantization) is not amortized over enough GEMM computation—the INT4 GEMM is faster than FP16 GEMM, but the surrounding operations cost more than the savings.
- **GPT2-base** shows a similar pattern: −27.7% slowdown at the smallest configuration (2560, 10240, 1536), breakeven (~0%) at (4096, 16384, 960), and increasing speedups up to 26.5% at (9600, 38400, 180).

**The sweet spot.** Speedups peak at intermediate hidden dimensions (7680–9600 for BERT-large, 9600 for GPT2-base) and then decline for the largest configurations. The paper does not analyze why speedups decrease beyond the sweet spot, but a plausible explanation is that at very large hidden dimensions, the GEMMs become so large that even FP16 tensor cores are well-utilized, and the relative advantage of INT4 over FP16 narrows. Additionally, at the largest sizes, the batch sizes become very small (e.g., 96 for BERT-large at hidden size 14400) due to memory constraints, which reduces parallelism and may shift the bottleneck from compute to memory bandwidth—where the benefit of INT4's reduced memory footprint matters but the arithmetic speedup is less dominant.

**GPU architecture sensitivity (Figures 7 and 8, Appendix C.5).** On an NVIDIA T4 (sm_75, lower throughput), the INT4 operators achieve significantly lower absolute TFLOPs but maintain similar relative speedups over FP16 at large sizes. On an A100 (sm_80, higher throughput), the INT4 advantage is even larger at the largest matrix sizes—Figure 8 shows INT4 GEMM peaking above 350 TFLOPs while FP16 peaks around 250 TFLOPs at similar sizes. This demonstrates that the method benefits from newer GPU architectures with improved INT4 tensor core throughput.

**The paper's caveat about implementation quality.** Section 5.3 and Appendix A.6 are transparent about limitations:

> "We emphasize that our implementation is not fully optimized. For example, the backward computation requires an INT4 MM in the form of Y = AB, while cutlass only supports Y = AB⊤, so explicit transpose is required. We also do not fuse the linear operators with nonlinearities and normalizations."

The transpose operation for backward GEMMs is particularly costly because it requires both a layout change and data movement. Fusion of quantization with LayerNorm (a common optimization in inference quantization) would eliminate intermediate memory traffic. These facts imply that the reported 35.1% speedup is a **lower bound**—a fully optimized implementation could achieve larger gains.

#### Operator Performance Across GPU Generations (Appendix C.5, Figures 7–8)

The paper provides operator-level TFLOPs measurements on three GPU architectures (T4, RTX 3090, A100) at six matrix size configurations. On the A100, the paper's INT4 GEMM peak throughput reaches approximately **350–400 TFLOPs** compared to ~250 TFLOPs for FP16, roughly consistent with the 2.2× operator speedup claimed. On the T4 (a much less powerful card), absolute throughput is lower (INT4 peaks around 60–70 TFLOPs vs. 40–50 for FP16) but the relative advantage persists. The HQ, LSSWeight, and LSSAct operators all track below the pure INT4 GEMM line, with HQ closest to the theoretical peak (since it has the least overhead beyond the GEMM) and LSS variants farther below (due to bit-splitting and sampling overhead). The gap between INT4 theoretical peak and HQ/LSS actual throughput represents the quantization overhead and is the primary target for future optimization.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Forward pass outlier analysis (Figure 3a):** On the CoLA dataset with BERT-base, the paper tests four forward quantization methods while keeping backpropagation in FP16: plain LSQ (the baseline static quantizer), the "outlier" method (maintaining 1% largest activation entries in FP, which serves as an oracle upper bound for outlier handling), HQ (the proposed Hadamard quantizer), and FP (full-precision ceiling). The results show: LSQ achieves approximately 18% accuracy at 4 bits (severe degradation from the ~57% FP baseline); the "outlier" method achieves near-FP accuracy (~55%), confirming that outliers are the dominant cause of forward quantization error; HQ achieves approximately 52%, very close to the outlier oracle. This ablation isolates the forward pass problem and proves that (a) outliers are the primary obstacle to 4-bit forward quantization in transformers, and (b) the Hadamard transform addresses them nearly as effectively as maintaining outliers in full precision.

**Granular quantizer ablation (Appendix C.3, Table 4):** The paper systematically tests per-tensor, per-token, per-channel, and SmoothQuant quantization strategies at bit-widths from 2 to 8 on the CoLA task, both with and without LSQ. The key findings: (1) Without LSQ, no method achieves non-zero accuracy at ≤5 bits—per-token quantization reaches 31.4% at 6 bits and 52.8% at 7 bits, but is 0% at 4 bits and below. (2) LSQ alone achieves non-zero accuracy starting at 3 bits (9.16% at 3 bits, 24.2% at 4 bits). (3) SmoothQuant with LSQ achieves 0% at 4 bits (compared to 57.7% at 8 bits), confirming that the migration strategy fails catastrophically at ultra-low precision. (4) Combining per-token + per-channel + LSQ reaches 27.8% at 4 bits, better than plain LSQ (24.2%) but far worse than HQ (54.6%). (5) HQ (without any granular quantization) achieves 54.6% at 4 bits, near-FP levels. (6) Adding per-token and per-channel quantization to HQ provides marginal benefit (54.1% from 54.6%), and adding SmoothQuant to HQ actually *reduces* accuracy to 46.6%. The paper's conclusion: "the result of HQ is not strongly affected when more granular quantization methods are applied," and the Hadamard transform makes granular quantization unnecessary.

**Backward pass method comparison (Figure 3b):** With forward propagation in FP16, the paper compares three gradient quantization methods on CoLA across bit-widths from 2 to 8: a simple minimax quantizer (divides min-to-max range into equal bins; Banner et al., 2018), LUQ (logarithmic unbiased quantization; Chmiel et al., 2021), and the proposed LSS. At 4 bits: minimax achieves roughly 18%, LSS achieves roughly 54% (nearly matching the ~56% FP ceiling), and LUQ achieves roughly 52%. At higher bit-widths (≥5), all three methods converge to FP-level accuracy. At 2–3 bits, all methods degrade substantially but LSS maintains a small advantage. The finding that LSS slightly outperforms LUQ at 4 bits is notable because LUQ uses a custom logarithmic format designed for gradient dynamic range, while LSS uses only standard INT4 arithmetic—the structural exploitation (bit splitting + importance sampling) matches a purpose-built numerical format.

**LSQ necessity for low-bit quantization (Table 4):** The ablation in Appendix C.3 demonstrates that without learnable step sizes, no quantization method works below 6 bits. Per-tensor, per-token, per-channel, and SmoothQuant all achieve 0% accuracy at 2–5 bits without LSQ. With LSQ, accuracy becomes non-zero starting at 3 bits for several methods. This validates the paper's claim in Section 3.1 that LSQ is essential—static quantization based on data statistics cannot adapt to the evolving activation distributions during training, and the adaptive scale parameter is what makes ultra-low-bit training possible.

**Hadamard block size adaptation (Appendix A.5):** While not presented as a standard ablation table, the paper describes the MSE-based criterion for selecting `$k$` per layer. The adaptive selection is important because different layers have different outlier severity—attention layers vs. FFN layers, early vs. late layers—and a fixed `$k$` across all layers would either over-spend computation on layers without outliers or under-suppress outliers in problematic layers. The paper doesn't report the distribution of selected `$k$` values across layers, which would have been informative for understanding where outliers concentrate.

**Cold-start heuristic necessity (Appendix A.4):** The paper describes the heuristic for initial step sizes during the first few iterations of pretraining (setting `$s_X = 2 \cdot \text{mean}(X) / \sqrt{Q_P}$` instead of learning). No ablation is provided showing training curves with and without this heuristic, but the paper's mention of training "instability" when jointly learning step sizes from random initialization suggests this is a practical necessity rather than an optional optimization. The paper also re-initializes the Hadamard block size and LSQ step sizes after the first two epochs of pretraining, indicating that the adaptive selection needs to be repeated as distributions stabilize.

**Leverage score sampling heuristic (Appendix A.6):** The paper's implementation simplifies the probability calculation described in Appendix A.2: "simply selecting elements whose leverage score is bigger than 0 can also work well, even better than our proposed algorithm in some cases." This is a significant practical simplification—the theoretically optimal `$p_i \propto c_i$` with iterative clamping is replaced by a hard threshold at zero. The fact that this heuristic "can also work well" suggests that gradient sparsity is so extreme (many rows have effectively zero gradient norm) that the distinction between "small" and "zero" is more important than the exact sampling probabilities among non-zero rows.

**Reusing leverage scores across gradient computations (Appendix A.6):** For the activation gradient LSS, the paper skips recomputing leverage scores and reuses the values from the weight gradient LSS: "We omit the Quantization and Leverage Score Calculating process in LSSinput, and use the same value as LSSWeight to accelerate the training process." This works because the leverage scores `$c_i = \|\nabla_Y^{\updownarrow}_{i,:}\| \|\widehat{X}^{\updownarrow}_{i,:}\|$` for the weight gradient and `$c_i = \|\nabla_Y^{\updownarrow}_{i,:}\|$` for the activation gradient share the gradient norm term. No ablation is provided to quantify the accuracy impact of this approximation, but it reduces the overhead of LSS to levels that make the end-to-end speedup viable.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "Our algorithm achieves competitive accuracy on a wide range of tasks"

**Assessment: Supported with qualifications.** The Table 1 results demonstrate that HQ+LSS consistently and substantially outperforms the LSQ+LUQ 4-bit baseline across fine-tuning tasks, with gains ranging from 0.2 points (ViT-L/16 on ImageNet1k) to 26.3 points (BERT-large GLUE average). The method also comes within 1–3 points of FP accuracy on most fine-tuning tasks. However, "competitive" has different meanings across settings:

- **Fine-tuning**: Competitive with INT8 and close to FP. The BERT-base GLUE gap from FP is 1.86 points (vs. 1.22 for INT8); the BERT-large gap is 2.32 points (vs. 1.83 for INT8). This is genuinely competitive.
- **Pretraining**: Less competitive. On WMT, the 0.9 SacreBLEU degradation is noticeable for a metric where small differences matter. On DeiT-Small pretraining, HQ+LSS is actually **worse than LSQ+LUQ** (69.18 vs. 69.96), and 3.9 points below FP (73.1). The paper doesn't highlight this negative result, but it suggests limitations for the pretraining setting.
- **The "wide range" of tasks is actually concentrated on fine-tuning.** Of the 15 distinct dataset-model combinations in Table 1, 13 are fine-tuning and only 2 are pretraining. The strong claims about accuracy are primarily claims about fine-tuning.

**Missing: Larger-scale language model pretraining.** The paper evaluates pretraining only on WMT (a relatively small dataset by modern standards—4.5M sentence pairs) and DeiT-Small (22M parameters). There is no BERT pretraining, no GPT pretraining, and no large-scale language model pretraining. The authors acknowledge this limitation directly: "the proposed method cannot yet work well for those extremely large models such as OPT-175B. To the best of our knowledge, even INT8 training is still an open problem for these large models." This is an honest admission but it bounds the claim: the method works on the scale of models tested (up to ~340M parameters) but is untested at the scale where training efficiency matters most.

#### Claim 2: "Unlike previous 4-bit training methods, our algorithm can be implemented on the current generation of GPUs"

**Assessment: Supported.** The paper demonstrates this concretely through the GPU implementation details in Appendix A.6, the operator benchmarks in Figures 4, 7, and 8, and the end-to-end training speedups in Figure 5 and Table 3. The operators are built on cutlass INT4 tensor core GEMM, which is supported on NVIDIA GPUs from Turing (T4) through Ampere (A100, RTX 3090) and beyond. The paper is transparent that the implementation is prototypical and not fully optimized, but the existence of a working implementation that achieves up to 35.1% speedup distinguishes this work from Sun et al. (2020) and Chmiel et al. (2021), which have no publicly available hardware implementation.

**A nuance:** The paper's claim is about *compatibility with current hardware*, not about *maximizing speedup on current hardware*. A fully optimized INT4 training pipeline would require kernel fusion (quantization with LayerNorm, Hadamard with data loading), removal of unnecessary transposes, and tighter integration with the deep learning framework's memory allocator—work that exceeds the scope of an academic paper. The 35.1% speedup should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept that hardware-compatible INT4 training can be faster, not as the asymptotic limit of what is possible.

#### Claim 3: "Our prototypical linear operator implementation is up to 2.2 times faster than the FP16 counterparts and speeds up the training by up to 35.1%"

**Assessment: Supported for the tested configurations, but the speedup is conditional on model scale.** The data in Figure 5 and Table 3 show:

- Speedups are **negative for small models** (hidden dimension 2560: −25.5% to −27.7%).
- Speedups become positive at moderate scales (hidden dimension ~4000) and peak at intermediate scales (hidden dimension 7680–9600, where speedups reach 26.5–35.1%).
- Speedups decline at very large scales (hidden dimension 14400: 15.3% for BERT-large).

The "up to 35.1%" figure is an optimistic framing of a more nuanced reality: on small models, INT4 training is *slower* than FP16; on mid-sized models, it provides substantial gains; on very large models (within the tested range), gains diminish. The sweet spot corresponds to models where the GEMM dominates the runtime but the matrices aren't so large that FP16 tensor cores already achieve near-peak utilization. This pattern is consistent with standard Amdahl's law behavior—the quantization overhead is a fixed cost that must be amortized over enough GEMM computation.

**The "2.2×" operator speedup vs. "35.1%" end-to-end speedup gap** reflects the fact that not all operations in training are quantized: nonlinearities (GeLU, softmax, LayerNorm), attention mechanisms, embedding lookups, and the classifier head remain in FP16. The paper is clear about this: "We replace all the floating-point linear operators with our INT4 implementation except simply using LSQ for embedding layers, and leaving the last classifier layer in full precision." The end-to-end speedup will always be lower than the operator speedup because of Amdahl's law.

#### Claim 4: The Hadamard transform solves the activation outlier problem

**Assessment: Supported by both the main results and the ablation.** Figure 3a provides the cleanest evidence: HQ achieves near-oracle (maintain-outliers) accuracy, proving that the transform effectively addresses outliers. Figure 1 provides visual evidence of the distribution normalization. The BERT-large results provide the strongest practical evidence: LSQ+LUQ collapses (55.93 average) on a model known to have severe outliers, while HQ+LSS recovers to 82.25. This claim is well-supported.

**A missing ablation:** The paper doesn't compare the Hadamard transform against alternative random rotations (e.g., a random orthogonal matrix, a Fourier transform, or a random Gaussian matrix followed by QR). The Hadamard is chosen for its fast transform and orthogonality, but whether other rotations would work equally well or better is unexamined. The RandNLA literature (cited by the paper) has studied randomized Hadamard transforms extensively, and the choice is well-motivated theoretically, but an empirical comparison against a baseline random orthogonal matrix would strengthen the claim that the specific properties of the Hadamard (fast transform, bounded entries) matter.

#### Claim 5: Leverage score sampling effectively exploits gradient sparsity

**Assessment: Supported, but the implementation deviates from the theory.** Figure 3b shows LSS matching or exceeding LUQ at 4 bits, which is strong evidence that the structural approach works. The variance analysis in Propositions 4.1 and B.2 provides theoretical grounding. However, the actual implementation (Appendix A.6) uses a simplified heuristic—discarding rows with zero norm—rather than the theoretically optimal `$p_i \propto c_i$` sampling. The paper notes this simplification "can also work well," but this means the theoretical variance guarantees don't directly apply to the implemented algorithm. The practical takeaway might be that gradient sparsity in transformers is so extreme that any reasonable sampling strategy that drops near-zero rows works—the optimality of leverage score sampling may be theoretically elegant but practically unnecessary for these models.

**A missing experiment:** There is no ablation comparing leverage score sampling against uniform random row dropping at the same sampling rate. The variance formula says leverage score sampling is optimal, but how much better is it than uniform? If gradient norms follow a power-law distribution (as Figure 2 suggests), uniform sampling would occasionally drop important large-gradient rows with non-trivial probability. Quantifying this difference would clarify whether the complexity of computing leverage scores is justified.

#### Additional Concerns

**1. Single random seed for large models.** BERT-large, ViT-L, WMT pretraining, and DeiT pretraining results are from single runs (no standard deviations reported). Given the instability observed for LSQ+LUQ on BERT-large (e.g., standard deviation 13.10 on QNLI), single-run results for HQ+LSS could be unrepresentative. The paper doesn't discuss whether HQ+LSS exhibits similar instability—the standard deviations on BERT-base are small, but BERT-large might behave differently.

**2. No direct comparison against INT4 baseline without HQ or LSS.** The ablation studies isolate forward and backward methods separately (Figures 3a, 3b), but the paper never reports the accuracy of plain LSQ for both forward and backward at 4 bits (i.e., what happens if you just train with LSQ everywhere, which is the simplest INT4-compatible method). The LSQ+LUQ baseline uses a logarithmic backward quantizer (not INT4-compatible), so it's not a clean comparison for the "INT4 without structural methods" baseline. A plain LSQ+LSQ (4-bit forward, 4-bit backward) result would clarify how much of the improvement comes from HQ vs. from LSS.

**3. The paper evaluates on relatively small models by modern standards.** BERT-large (340M parameters) is the largest model tested. GPT-2 base (124M) is used only for throughput measurements, not accuracy experiments. The largest ViT is ViT-L/16 (~300M parameters). For a paper motivated by training efficiency, the absence of experiments on models in the 1B–10B parameter range (where training cost is genuinely prohibitive) limits the practical significance. The authors acknowledge this limitation explicitly, but it remains a gap between the paper's motivation and its evaluation scope.

**4. Memory footprint reduction is claimed but not measured.** The paper mentions in the introduction that INT4 training "reduces memory footprint compared to FP16," but no memory measurements are reported. For large-model training, memory is often the primary bottleneck (determining batch size and whether model parallelism is needed), so quantifying memory savings would be as important as the throughput measurements.

**5. There is no analysis of how the method's accuracy varies with training duration.** All experiments use standard training recipes (fixed number of epochs). If INT4 training converges more slowly than FP16 (because gradient approximations are noisier), the accuracy gap might close with longer training. Conversely, if INT4 training is unstable at later stages (e.g., the quantized gradients prevent fine convergence), the gap might widen. Training curves (accuracy vs. epoch) are not provided for any experiment.

**6. The paper does not discuss interaction with mixed-precision training (FP16 master weights).** Standard FP16 training keeps a master copy of weights in FP32 to accumulate small gradient updates. The paper doesn't specify whether HQ+LSS uses this technique—the weights are quantized to INT4 for the forward/backward GEMMs, but are the optimizer states and weight updates in FP16/FP32? This matters for both accuracy (master weights improve training stability) and memory (master weights in FP32 consume memory that INT4 would otherwise save).

**7. The effect of batch size on quantization quality is unexplored.** The gradient sparsity exploited by LSS depends on having enough tokens such that the token-wise gradient norm distribution is reliably sparse. Small batch sizes would produce fewer total tokens, potentially making the sparsity pattern less reliable and the leverage score estimates noisier. The paper uses batch sizes that vary with model size (Table 3), but there is no systematic study of how batch size affects LSS accuracy.

**8. The paper compares against LSQ+LUQ, which is a 2021 method.** By 2023 (the paper's publication date), there may have been other 4-bit training methods (e.g., using the FP8 formats newly available on H100 GPUs). The paper's hardware-compatibility argument is strongest when comparing against Sun et al. (2020) and Chmiel et al. (2021), but doesn't address whether newer methods close the gap.

**Overall Verdict:** The experiments demonstrate that HQ+LSS substantially improves over the LSQ+LUQ 4-bit baseline on transformer fine-tuning tasks (especially for larger models where activation outliers are severe) and can deliver measurable training speedup on contemporary hardware for models of sufficient scale. The claims are well-supported for the fine-tuning setting on models up to ~340M parameters. The evidence is weaker for pretraining (only two tasks, one of which shows HQ+LSS underperforming LSQ+LUQ) and absent for large-scale training (>1B parameters), where the practical impact would be largest. The speedup results convincingly demonstrate hardware compatibility and acceleration, but the "up to 35.1%" figure should be understood as the peak of a curve that includes negative speedups at small scales and diminishing returns at large scales.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Method Fails on Pretraining from Scratch for Vision Transformers

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's algorithmic design—particularly the Hadamard quantizer—is motivated by and optimized for the activation outlier structure that emerges in *pretrained* transformer models. As the paper states in Section 3.2: "The outlier problem is particularly significant when the training task is to fine-tune a pre-trained model on some new downstream tasks, since the pre-train model contains more outliers than random initialization." This implies that pretraining from scratch, where weights are randomly initialized and activation distributions have not yet developed strong feature-wise outliers, may not benefit from HQ to the same degree.

**The consequence.** When the outlier structure is absent or weak, the Hadamard transform's overhead (additional FP16 matrix multiplications, quantization of transformed matrices) may outweigh its outlier-suppression benefits. The method can underperform simpler 4-bit approaches that do not pay this overhead. This is not a hypothetical concern—it appears in the paper's own results.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 reports DeiT-Small pretraining from scratch on ImageNet1k. FP achieves 73.1% Top-1 accuracy, INT8 achieves 70.95, LSQ+LUQ achieves 69.96, and HQ+LSS achieves **69.18**—which is 0.78 points *worse* than LSQ+LUQ. This is the only task in the entire evaluation suite where HQ+LSS underperforms the primary 4-bit baseline. The paper does not comment on this negative result in the main text, nor does it provide an ablation isolating whether the degradation comes from HQ (forward), LSS (backward), or their combination.

The WMT machine translation pretraining result (Table 1) provides only weak counter-evidence: HQ+LSS achieves 25.57 SacreBLEU vs. 26.5 for FP, a degradation of ~0.9 points, which is "comparable" to prior methods but does not demonstrate superiority. No LSQ+LUQ baseline is reported for WMT, so it is impossible to assess whether HQ+LSS outperforms the simpler 4-bit method in this pretraining setting.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation directly. The adaptive block size selection (Appendix A.5) could theoretically select small `$k$` (or `$k = 0$`, reducing HQ to plain LSQ) when outliers are absent, but the MSE-based criterion might still select non-zero `$k$` and pay the overhead without benefit. The paper suggests in Section 6 that "our work can be potentially extended beyond transformers to other MM-only architectures," but the pretraining failure on vision transformers—which *are* transformer architectures—indicates the limitation is about the training stage (pretraining vs. fine-tuning) rather than the architecture family. A practitioner considering 4-bit training for a large-scale pretraining project would need to verify that the method improves over LSQ+LUQ (or even over INT8) before committing to the implementation complexity.

---

### 6.2 Negative Speedup on Small-to-Medium Models Makes the Method Impractical Below a Scale Threshold

**The assumption or constraint.** The speedup reported in Section 5.3 depends on the GEMM operations dominating the runtime sufficiently to amortize the quantization overhead (Hadamard transform, bit splitting, leverage score computation, data packing/unpacking, dequantization). This overhead is largely fixed per linear layer regardless of matrix size, so the relative benefit of INT4 GEMM over FP16 GEMM grows with matrix dimensions.

**The consequence.** On models below a certain size threshold, the INT4 training pipeline is actually *slower* than standard FP16 training—the paper reports up to 27.7% slowdown for GPT2-base at hidden dimension 2560 (Table 3). A practitioner cannot simply "turn on" 4-bit training for any transformer and expect speedup; they must first determine whether their model scale justifies the overhead. The threshold where speedup becomes positive depends on hidden dimension, batch size, and GPU architecture, and is not trivially predictable.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 and Figure 5 provide comprehensive speedup measurements across model configurations:

- **BERT-large**: Speedups are −25.5% at hidden size 2560, become positive (+4.4%) at hidden size 4096, peak at +35.1% at hidden size 8960, and decline to +15.3% at hidden size 14400.
- **GPT2-base**: Speedups are −27.7% at hidden size 2560, approximately breakeven at hidden size 4096, and peak at +26.5% at hidden size 9600.

The crossover point (where speedup becomes positive) occurs around hidden dimension ~3500–4000 for both architectures on 8×A100 systems. Below this, the method is a net negative. The paper does not provide a formula or guideline for predicting the crossover point on different hardware or for different model architectures.

The operator-level analysis in Figure 6 and Appendix C.5 quantifies the overhead explicitly: Hadamard multiplication, data packing, leverage score computation, and dequantization each consume measurable fractions of total operator time, with Hadamard and quantization being the largest non-GEMM components. At small matrix sizes, these fixed costs dominate.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about this limitation in Section 5.3 and Appendix A.6, acknowledging that the implementation is "not fully optimized" and that kernel fusion (e.g., fusing quantization with LayerNorm, eliminating unnecessary transposes) would reduce overhead and shift the crossover point to smaller models. However, no optimized implementation is provided, and the magnitude of potential improvement from fusion is not estimated. For a practitioner with a fixed model architecture (e.g., BERT-base at hidden size 768), the current implementation would produce a slowdown, and there is no guidance on whether engineering investment in kernel fusion would flip the sign.

---

### 6.3 Evaluation Scope Is Concentrated on Fine-Tuning Tasks Under ~340M Parameters; Large-Scale Pretraining Is Untested

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's accuracy evaluation in Table 1 is dominated by fine-tuning experiments: 13 of 15 task-model combinations are fine-tuning (GLUE, SQuAD, Adversarial QA, SWAG, CoNLL, CIFAR, ImageNet1k), and only 2 are pretraining (WMT translation, DeiT-Small). All models tested are in the ~22M–340M parameter range. The paper explicitly acknowledges the scale limitation: "the proposed method cannot yet work well for those extremely large models such as OPT-175B. To the best of our knowledge, even INT8 training is still an open problem for these large models" (Section 6, Limitations).

**The consequence.** The paper's motivation—improving training efficiency—is most impactful at the scale where training cost is prohibitive: models with billions of parameters trained on massive datasets. At the 100M–300M parameter scale tested, FP16 training is already relatively cheap (a single BERT-large fine-tuning run takes hours on a few GPUs), so a 35% speedup saves modest absolute time. At the 10B+ scale, a 35% speedup would save weeks of GPU-time and tens of thousands of dollars. However, the paper provides no evidence that the method works at that scale. The failure of LSQ+LUQ on BERT-large (55.93 average GLUE accuracy, Table 1) demonstrates that methods which work at moderate scale can collapse when models grow and activation outliers become more extreme—and HQ+LSS's ability to handle this for models beyond BERT-large (340M) is unverified.

Furthermore, large-scale pretraining involves training dynamics (loss spikes, phase transitions, extensive distribution shift) that are absent in fine-tuning. The cold-start heuristic (Appendix A.4) and the re-initialization of Hadamard block sizes after two epochs suggest that pretraining instability is a real concern that the paper handles with ad hoc interventions. Whether these interventions suffice for multi-billion-parameter models trained for hundreds of thousands of steps is unknown.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The largest models with accuracy results are BERT-large (340M) for language fine-tuning and ViT-L/16 (~300M) for vision fine-tuning. The largest model with throughput measurements is a synthetic BERT-large configuration at hidden size 14400 (Table 3), but this configuration is used only for speed measurements, not accuracy evaluation—we don't know whether training actually converges at this scale with 4-bit precision. The GPT2-base configurations in Table 3 are similarly synthetic scaling experiments.

The paper reports no experiments on: (a) models in the 1B–10B parameter range, (b) large-scale language model pretraining (e.g., BERT pretraining, GPT pretraining), (c) training beyond the standard epoch counts used in fine-tuning recipes, or (d) training with the learning rate schedules and warmup strategies typical of large-scale pretraining.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the scale limitation explicitly in Section 6 but offers no partial results, scaling trend analysis, or theoretical argument for why the method should extend. The recommendation to leave large-scale training as "a future direction" is honest but leaves a critical practical question unanswered: should a team training a 1B+ parameter model invest in implementing this method? The current evidence cannot answer this.

---

### 6.4 Gradient Sparsity Is Assumed but Not Guaranteed; the Method Provides No Detection or Fallback When Sparsity Is Absent

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire backward pass acceleration strategy—bit splitting followed by leverage score sampling—depends critically on the structural sparsity of gradients: that a small fraction of token rows contain almost all the gradient mass, so dropping the rest introduces negligible error. The paper states this assumption explicitly in Section 4.1: "During almost the entire training process, the network operates in the overparameterized scheme, where it can fit most training data well, except for a few hard examples. Therefore, the (activation) gradient will be close to zero for well-fitted data points." The paper further claims: "For fine-tuning tasks, the gradient is always sparse during the whole training process." LSS discards approximately half the rows (in expectation, since `$\sum_i p_i = N$` out of `$2N$` total after bit splitting).

**The consequence.** If gradient sparsity does not hold—for example, early in pretraining before the model enters the overparameterized regime, on tasks where most examples are "hard," or with small batch sizes where statistical noise dominates—then LSS will discard rows that contain significant gradient information. The variance formulas (Propositions 4.1 and B.2) show that when leverage scores are uniform (all rows equally important), LSS introduces maximal variance for a given sampling budget. In the worst case, this could cause training to diverge or converge to poor solutions. The paper provides no mechanism to detect when sparsity is absent or to fall back to a non-sampling quantizer.

Additionally, the implementation heuristic in Appendix A.6—"simply selecting elements whose leverage score is bigger than 0"—means that *all* rows with any non-zero gradient are retained, regardless of the sampling budget `$N$`. If many rows have small but non-zero gradients (as might occur early in training or with large batch sizes), the heuristic retains more rows than the budget allows, and the computational savings of LSS evaporate. The method provides no guarantee that the INT4 operations will actually be faster than a naive INT4 MM in all cases.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 2 provides evidence that the sparsity assumption holds for the specific model and training stage shown: the per-row gradient norm distribution is heavily skewed, and a small fraction of rows accounts for most of the total norm. However, this is a single snapshot at an unspecified training epoch for an unspecified model. There is no analysis of:

- How the sparsity pattern evolves during training (early vs. late epochs).
- Whether sparsity holds uniformly across all layers (attention vs. FFN, early vs. late layers).
- Whether sparsity holds for pretraining (where the paper notes it "quickly emerges after only a few training epochs" but provides no data).
- What happens when batch size is small (and the total number of tokens `$N = S \times T$` is small enough that the "few large, many small" pattern may not materialize).

The paper's claim that "for fine-tuning tasks, the gradient is always sparse during the whole training process" is stated without citation or supporting experiments beyond Figure 2.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. There is no diagnostic to detect when sparsity has broken down, no fallback to a non-sampling quantizer, and no ablation studying training with artificially reduced sparsity (e.g., by training on a dataset curated to contain only hard examples). The RandNLA literature provides variance bounds that could in principle be monitored at runtime to detect degradation, but the paper does not implement or suggest this. A practitioner deploying LSS on a new task or model architecture has no way to know whether the sparsity assumption holds without running their own gradient distribution analysis.

---

### 6.5 The Method Has No Solution for Convolutional or Non-MM-Dominated Architectures

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper explicitly states in Section 6 (Limitations): "The main limitation of this work is that it can only accelerate models with a large portion of matrix multiplications (linear layers), but cannot accelerate convolution layers." The entire method is designed around the matrix multiplication form `$Z = XW^\top$`, which covers fully-connected layers and (via Appendix A.1) batch matrix multiplications in attention. Convolutional layers, which dominate architectures like ResNet, EfficientNet, and ConvNeXt, cannot be expressed in this form without the `im2col` transformation (which changes the tensor dimensions and memory access patterns in ways that may interact poorly with the quantization pipeline).

**The consequence.** The method is architecturally incomplete for general neural network training. A practitioner training a hybrid CNN-transformer architecture (e.g., a vision model with convolutional stem and transformer body) would need to either: (a) leave the convolutional layers in FP16, limiting overall speedup by Amdahl's law, or (b) attempt to adapt the quantization strategy to convolutions, with no guidance from the paper. The paper's claim to be a "training method for transformers" is accurate but narrower than the title "Training Transformers with 4-bit Integers" might suggest—it does not provide a general 4-bit training framework applicable to arbitrary architectures.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's evaluation is entirely on transformer architectures: BERT (encoder-only), GPT2 (decoder-only), Transformer (encoder-decoder), ViT, and DeiT. No CNN, MLP-Mixer, or hybrid architecture is tested. The paper mentions in Section 6 that the method "can be potentially extended beyond transformers to other MM-only architectures, such as MLP-Mixer, graph neural networks, and recurrent neural networks," but provides no experiments or even a sketch of how this extension would work for RNNs (which involve recurrent connections over time, not just matrix multiplications).

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation but does not attempt to address it. The suggestion of extension to "MM-only architectures" is speculative and unevaluated. For a practitioner whose workload includes convolutions, this limitation may be disqualifying—the method accelerates only the linear portions of the model, and the overall speedup may be small if convolutions are a significant fraction of the runtime.

---

### 6.6 The Speedup Claims Do Not Account for Memory Overhead of Maintaining FP16 Activations; the Effective Memory Reduction Is Unmeasured

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper states in the introduction that FQT methods can improve memory efficiency: "Training with low-precision arithmetic... is promising to improve computational and memory efficiency." The INT4 representations of weights and activations during the GEMM operations consume 4× less memory than FP16. However, the paper's training pipeline (Section 3.1) leaves "all the less-computationally-intensive non-linear operators in the 16-bit floating-point (FP16) format." This means that activations must be stored in FP16 for the nonlinear operations (GeLU, LayerNorm, softmax), and the INT4 quantized versions are temporary values computed on-the-fly for the GEMM and then discarded.

**The consequence.** The memory footprint of activations—often the dominant memory consumer during training due to the need to store intermediate values for backpropagation—is not reduced by 4×. The INT4 quantization saves memory bandwidth during the GEMM (since the tensor core reads INT4 values instead of FP16 values) but does not reduce the activation storage in GPU DRAM. The weight memory is reduced (weights can be stored in INT4 and dequantized on-the-fly), but weight memory is typically a small fraction of total training memory compared to activations and optimizer states. The paper's introductory claim about "memory efficiency" is therefore misleading: the method improves compute efficiency and memory bandwidth utilization, but not peak memory consumption in the way a practitioner might expect.

For large-model training, where memory capacity is the primary constraint (determining maximum batch size and whether model parallelism is needed), this distinction is critical. A method that reduces arithmetic intensity without reducing memory footprint may not enable larger models or larger batch sizes—it may only make the existing training configuration faster.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no memory measurements whatsoever. Section 5.3 reports only speedup (compute throughput), not memory consumption. The GPU implementation details in Appendix A.6 describe data packing for INT4 GEMM operands but do not discuss the memory layout of activations between operations. The throughput measurements in Figures 7 and 8 (T4 and A100 operator benchmarks) use standalone GEMM calls that do not reflect the memory pressure of a full training pipeline with gradient checkpointing and optimizer state storage.

**Mitigation status.** The limitation is unaddressed. The paper could have measured peak GPU memory usage during training and compared it against FP16, but did not. The authors could have discussed whether further memory savings are achievable (e.g., by keeping activations in INT8 rather than FP16 between operations, or by applying activation checkpointing to INT4-quantized checkpoints), but did not. A practitioner evaluating whether INT4 training will let them increase batch size or train a larger model cannot answer this question from the paper's data.

An additional subtlety: the paper uses distributed data parallel with ZeRO-3 and optimizer offloading for the throughput measurements (Appendix C.4), which already redistributes memory pressure in ways that interact with quantization benefits. The reported 35.1% speedup is achieved in a memory-optimized configuration; the speedup in a memory-bound configuration (where batch size is constrained by activation memory) might differ substantially.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the conversation around low-precision training from **format design** to **structural preconditioning**. Prior 4-bit training work—Sun et al. (2020) with FP4, Chmiel et al. (2021) with logarithmic quantization—approached the problem as one of numerical representation: if we can design a quantization grid that faithfully captures both small and large values, 4-bit training should work. This paper demonstrates that this framing is incomplete. The same INT4 format that fails catastrophically when applied naively (LSQ+LUQ collapses to 55.93 average GLUE accuracy on BERT-large vs. 84.57 FP; Table 1) succeeds when the *data being quantized* is transformed to match the quantizer's assumptions—Hadamard rotation to eliminate feature-wise outliers, bit splitting with importance sampling to allocate gradient precision where it matters. The format didn't change; the matrices did.

This is a methodological reframing, not a paradigm shift. The field already understood that outliers and gradient dynamic range are challenges; SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022) and Outlier Suppression (Wei et al., 2022) addressed activation outliers for inference quantization, and LUQ addressed gradient dynamic range for training. What this paper contributes is the recognition that **at 4 bits, the tradeoff-based approaches that work at 8 bits become fundamentally insufficient**, and a different solution principle—eliminating the tradeoff through orthogonal transformation rather than managing it through per-channel scaling—is required. The ablation in Appendix C.3 (Table 4) makes this concrete: SmoothQuant with LSQ achieves 57.7% CoLA accuracy at 8 bits but 0% at 4 bits. The 16 quantization levels of INT4 leave no room to sacrifice inlier precision for outlier preservation. The Hadamard quantizer's 54.6% at 4 bits (vs. 24.2% for plain LSQ) demonstrates that making the tradeoff unnecessary through random rotation is not merely an alternative approach—it is, at this precision level, the only one that works.

The paper also redefines what counts as a **solution** to the 4-bit training problem. By making hardware compatibility a first-order design constraint, it establishes that algorithmic accuracy on its own is insufficient—a 4-bit training method must deliver measurable speedup on existing GPU hardware to be considered successful. The 2.2× operator speedup and 35.1% end-to-end training speedup (Figures 4–5) set a concrete bar that future 4-bit training methods will be measured against. This pushes the field toward algorithm-hardware co-design, where the numerical format is constrained by what tensor cores actually support, and the research contribution is making that constraint work.

The work resolves a latent tension in the FQT literature. Prior results suggested that 4-bit training was possible (Sun et al., 2020; Chmiel et al., 2021) but the methods couldn't run on real hardware. Other results showed that INT4 training without custom formats failed (this paper's own LSQ baseline). The implicit conclusion might have been that INT4 is simply insufficient for training—that you need FP4 or logarithmic formats, and hardware support for those formats is a prerequisite for 4-bit training. This paper disproves that: INT4 *is* sufficient if you understand and exploit the structure of the data. The implication is that format design was a detour; the path forward is structural understanding of transformer tensors.

Several research directions become more attractive in light of this work:

- **RandNLA for deep learning training.** The paper explicitly connects to randomized numerical linear algebra (Drineas & Mahoney, 2016), using Hadamard transforms and leverage score sampling in ways that are standard in RandNLA but novel in FQT. This opens a bridge between two communities that have largely operated independently. The variance formulas (Propositions 4.1 and B.2) provide a formal language for analyzing quantization error in training that the FQT community had not previously used.
- **Data-dependent quantization.** The paper shows that *how* you quantize matters more than the numerical format you quantize to. Future work on dynamic, data-dependent quantization strategies—adaptive block sizes, per-layer sampling rates, runtime sparsity detection—becomes more attractive than designing new static formats.
- **Hardware-algorithm co-design for training.** The paper's GPU implementation (Appendix A.6) demonstrates the practical challenges: unnecessary transposes, unfused kernels, alignment constraints. This creates a clear engineering roadmap for what optimized 4-bit training libraries need to support.

Directions that become less attractive:

- **New 4-bit numerical formats not supported on current hardware.** The paper demonstrates that INT4 with structural preconditioning matches or exceeds FP4 and logarithmic formats in accuracy while being deployable. The marginal benefit of custom formats over a well-designed INT4 pipeline appears small, and the deployment barrier is high. Unless a new format offers dramatic accuracy improvements (>5 points on challenging tasks), hardware compatibility will likely dominate format expressivity as a design criterion going forward.
- **Per-channel or per-token quantization at 4 bits without preconditioning.** Table 4 shows that adding granularity (per-token, per-channel) to LSQ improves CoLA from 24.2% to 27.8% at 4 bits—a marginal gain compared to HQ's 54.6%. More granular quantization without structural transformation cannot solve the fundamental problem that outliers and inliers require incompatible step sizes, and at 4 bits there aren't enough levels to serve both masters.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Stress-testing the Hadamard quantizer on large-scale language model pretraining (1B+ parameters).** The paper's strongest accuracy results are on fine-tuning (Table 1), where pretrained outlier channels are already established and the Hadamard transform provides clear benefit. The two pretraining experiments (WMT translation, DeiT-Small) show mixed results: WMT is comparable to prior work but lacks an LSQ+LUQ baseline for comparison; DeiT-Small shows HQ+LSS *underperforming* LSQ+LUQ (69.18 vs. 69.96). This raises a critical question: does HQ help or hurt when training transformers from scratch at scale? A strong follow-up would pretrain a 1B-parameter language model (e.g., GPT-2 XL scale) with both HQ+LSS and LSQ+LUQ, measuring not just final perplexity but also training dynamics (loss curves, gradient norm distributions, the emergence of outlier channels over time). The paper hypothesizes that outliers are less severe early in pretraining but "quickly emerge after only a few training epochs" (Section 4.1). If HQ's overhead hurts early training but helps later, an adaptive schedule—switching from LSQ to HQ after outlier channels form—might outperform either approach alone. The paper's adaptive block size re-initialization after two epochs (Appendix A.5) hints at this, but a systematic study of *when* to activate HQ during pretraining would directly address the limitation identified in Section 6.1.

**Characterizing when gradient sparsity fails and designing a fallback mechanism.** The paper's LSS strategy depends on the structural sparsity of gradients: a few token rows dominate the gradient norm, so discarding small rows introduces negligible error. The paper provides one histogram (Figure 2) as evidence but no analysis of when this assumption breaks. A rigorous follow-up would measure gradient sparsity (e.g., the Gini coefficient of per-row gradient norms) across training epochs, model architectures, batch sizes, and task types, identifying the conditions under which LSS introduces significant approximation error. The goal would be a runtime diagnostic: compute the leverage score distribution during the first few training steps, and if the distribution is too uniform (sparsity is absent), fall back to a non-sampling quantizer (e.g., plain bit-split INT4 or INT8). This would address the paper's implicit assumption without requiring practitioners to analyze gradient distributions manually. The variance formulas in Propositions 4.1 and B.2 already provide the theoretical machinery—a follow-up would implement a variance monitor that triggers the fallback when the expected approximation error exceeds a threshold.

**Combining Hadamard quantization with modern inference quantization techniques for end-to-end low-precision pipelines.** The paper applies HQ only to training, but the Hadamard-transformed weight matrices produced during training are directly usable for inference. If a model is trained with HQ+LSS, the weights are already in a form where `$W \approx s_W \cdot \text{ints}_W(WH) \cdot H^\top$`. At inference time, the Hadamard transform on activations can be fused into the preceding LayerNorm (since both are linear transformations), making the overhead nearly zero. A follow-up would measure the inference accuracy and latency of HQ-trained models, comparing against models quantized post-training with SmoothQuant, GPTQ, or AWQ. This would test whether training-time Hadamard quantization produces weights that are inherently more amenable to INT4 inference than post-training-quantized weights—potentially unifying training and inference quantization under a single framework. The paper's BERT-base GLUE results (80.81 for HQ+LSS training) vs. the FP inference ceiling (82.67) provide a starting point; a strong follow-up would compare this against applying SmoothQuant to the FP-trained model and measuring the accuracy-latency Pareto frontier.

**Ablating alternative random rotations and measuring the sensitivity to transform choice.** The paper chooses the Hadamard transform for its orthogonality, fast transform (FWHT), and theoretical properties in RandNLA. But any orthogonal matrix would satisfy `$H^\top H = I$` and thus preserve the matrix product `$XW^\top = (XH)(H^\top W^\top)$`. Would a random orthogonal matrix (e.g., a random Gaussian matrix followed by QR decomposition) work equally well? What about a discrete cosine transform (DCT) or a random permutation? A systematic ablation would answer: (1) Is outlier suppression a general property of random rotations, or does the Hadamard's specific structure (bounded entries, recursive construction) matter? (2) Does the fast transform property of the Hadamard (O(D log 2^k) vs. O(D 2^k) for a naive rotation) matter in practice, given that the rotation cost is already small relative to the GEMM? (3) Can we learn the rotation matrix during training (e.g., via a parameterized orthogonal layer) to adapt to each layer's specific outlier structure, rather than using a fixed Hadamard? This last question connects to the learned quantization literature and could yield accuracy gains over the fixed Hadamard at the cost of additional parameters. The paper's Figure 1 provides the diagnostic tool: the histogram of pre- and post-transform activations directly shows how well a transform suppresses outliers. A follow-up would replicate Figure 1 for multiple transform choices and correlate the histogram compactness with downstream task accuracy.

**Extending LSS to structured sparsity for hardware efficiency on future accelerators.** The paper's LSS discards entire rows of the gradient matrix based on leverage scores, producing an irregular sparsity pattern (some rows are kept, others dropped). Current GPUs handle this through the mask-and-scatter approach described in Appendix A.6, which introduces overhead. However, if the gradient sparsity has predictable structure—for instance, if the "hard" tokens that produce large gradients correspond to specific positions in the sequence or specific examples in the batch—then a structured sparsity pattern (e.g., block-sparse or 2:4 sparsity as in NVIDIA's Ampere sparse tensor cores) could achieve the same approximation quality with lower hardware overhead. A follow-up would analyze the token positions and example indices that LSS retains across training steps, looking for temporal consistency (do the same tokens consistently produce large gradients?) and structural patterns (are large gradients concentrated at specific sequence positions, like clause boundaries or entity mentions?). If patterns exist, a structured sparsity mask could be learned or heuristically determined, potentially enabling sparse INT4 tensor core operations that are faster than the dense INT4 GEMMs used in the paper.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Fine-tuning large pretrained models under GPU memory or time constraints.** The paper's strongest and most consistent results are on fine-tuning: BERT-base GLUE achieves 80.81 vs. 82.67 FP (Table 1), a degradation of only 1.86 points while the method delivers up to 35.1% speedup on sufficiently large model configurations (Table 3). For practitioners fine-tuning BERT-large, RoBERTa-large, or similar models on downstream tasks—a common workflow in applied NLP—HQ+LSS offers a direct speedup with modest accuracy cost. The speedup is conditional on model size (negative for hidden dimension <4000; Table 3), so the sweet spot is models at the scale of BERT-large (hidden size 1024, but with large intermediate FFN dimensions of 4096 that dominate the GEMM time) or larger. For a team running dozens of fine-tuning experiments (hyperparameter sweeps, multi-task training, ensembling), a 25–35% reduction in training time per run directly accelerates the experimental cycle. The paper's results on question answering (SQuAD v1: 87.60 F1 vs. 88.32 FP), adversarial robustness (Adversarial QA: 38.70 vs. 40.99 FP), and NER (CoNLL: 91.90 vs. 93.38 FP) demonstrate that the method works across task types, not just classification.

**Accelerating vision transformer fine-tuning for production computer vision systems.** The ImageNet1k fine-tuning results (Table 1) show ViT-B/32 at 79.18 (vs. 81.88 FP), ViT-L/32 at 80.06 (vs. 81.62 FP), and ViT-L/16 at 82.61 (vs. 84.55 FP). For a production system that fine-tunes ViT backbones on custom image classification or retrieval tasks, a ~2-point accuracy drop may be acceptable in exchange for 25–35% faster training, especially during the prototyping phase where many configurations are tested. The paper's results on CIFAR-10 (98.36 vs. 98.77) and CIFAR-100 (89.78 vs. 91.94) suggest the accuracy gap narrows on tasks where the pretrained model already performs well—the additional representational capacity of FP16 matters more when the task is at the frontier of the model's capability (Adversarial QA, the hardest GLUE tasks) and less when the task is well within the model's competence. Practitioners can use this to decide when 4-bit fine-tuning is appropriate: for standard benchmarks and applications where a 1–2% accuracy gap is tolerable, HQ+LSS provides a meaningful speedup; for tasks requiring every bit of model capacity, FP16 remains preferable.

**Reducing the carbon footprint and cloud compute cost of transformer training experiments.** The paper explicitly mentions broader impacts: "Our algorithm can improve efficiency and reduce the energy consumption of training neural networks, which helps reduce the carbon footprint caused by deep learning." With the 35.1% end-to-end speedup demonstrated on 8×A100 systems (Table 3, BERT-large at hidden size 8960), a research lab running hundreds of fine-tuning or medium-scale pretraining experiments could reduce their total GPU-hours by roughly one-quarter. At cloud GPU prices (~$1–2 per A100-hour), this translates to direct cost savings. The speedup varies with model configuration (Section 6.2), so the practical benefit depends on running models in the sweet spot (hidden dimensions ~5000–10000). For labs training at this scale, the implementation investment (integrating the HQ and LSS operators into their training pipeline, accepting the acknowledged implementation limitations in Appendix A.6) may be justified by the recurring cost savings. The caveat is that the implementation is not turnkey—the paper's code is available but described as prototypical, and integration with existing training frameworks (Hugging Face Transformers, Fairseq, etc.) requires non-trivial engineering.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not explicitly articulate a decision rule comparing HQ+LSS against named alternatives (FP16, INT8, LSQ+LUQ, Ultra-Low) based on task characteristics, model scale, or deployment constraints. The evaluation presents HQ+LSS as an across-the-board improvement over LSQ+LUQ for fine-tuning (Table 1) and as competitive with INT8, but the paper does not specify conditions where a practitioner should choose one method over another. The closest the paper comes is the observation in Section 5.2 that HQ is particularly important when outliers are severe (fine-tuning large pretrained models) and that LSS exploits gradient sparsity that is "always sparse during the whole training process" for fine-tuning. Since the paper does not explicitly frame a tradeoff decision procedure, a forced "prefer A when..." matrix would impose structure the paper itself does not provide. Instead, the practical guidance that emerges from the results is: HQ+LSS should be preferred for **fine-tuning transformer models at the scale where it provides positive speedup** (hidden dimensions above ~4000, where Table 3 shows speedup becomes positive), accepting a 1–3 point accuracy degradation from FP16; for pretraining or for models below that scale threshold, FP16 or INT8 may be preferable, but the paper does not provide sufficient evidence to make that determination definitively.
