# Golden Goose: A Simple Trick to Synthesize Unlimited RLVR Tasks from Unverifiable Internet Text

**ArXiv:** [2601.22975](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22975)

## 🎯 Pitch

Training on just 25% of available RLVR data actually improves the model while the rest causes degradation—revealing that effective problem-solving hinges on encountering genuinely novel reasoning patterns rather than more data. Golden Goose unlocks unlimited such patterns by turning any text’s missing reasoning steps into multiple-choice questions, generating over 700K fresh tasks that revive saturated models and boost 4B models past 7B domain-specialized ones in zero-data cybersecurity.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Golden Goose**, a data synthesis pipeline that converts reasoning-rich but unverifiable internet text into unlimited RLVR tasks by constructing a multiple-choice version of the fill-in-the-middle task—given a source text, an LLM identifies and masks a contiguous span of crucial reasoning steps, treats the removed content as the ground-truth answer, and generates diverse, plausible distractors to form a verifiable MCQ. Using source corpora spanning Olympiad math proofs (AoPS-Instruct), coding problems lacking test cases (rStar-Coder), and free-form textbook QA (MegaScience), the authors construct **GooseReason-0.7M**, a dataset of over 0.7 million tasks that provides a 13× increase in effective RLVR examples over the 136K ProRL dataset for a saturated model (ProRL-1.5B-v2), reviving models that plateaued on existing data and yielding robust, sustained gains—including a 2.27% absolute improvement on Qwen-4B-Instruct after the original ProRL recipe produced a 0.79% degradation at merely 300 steps—while establishing new state-of-the-art results for 1.5B and 4B-Instruct models across 15 benchmarks. In a real-world deployment synthesizing **GooseReason-Cyber** (180K tasks) from raw FineWeb scrapes for cybersecurity—a domain with no prior open-source RLVR data—training Qwen3-4B-Instruct for only 100 RL steps achieves a 4.44% absolute gain across three benchmarks and surpasses a 7B domain-specialized model (Llama-Primus-Instruct) that underwent extensive domain-specific pre-training and post-training, establishing that abundant unverifiable internet text can fuel RLVR scaling only when transformed through this MCQ fill-in-the-middle construction that converts open-ended reasoning into automatically verifiable tasks.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RLVR Data Is the Bottleneck for Scaling Reasoning

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has emerged as one of the most effective techniques for teaching LLMs to reason. The formula is straightforward: give a model a problem with a ground-truth answer, let it generate multiple solution attempts (rollouts), and reward it when the verifier confirms correctness—a math expression evaluator for math, unit tests for code. This recipe underlies the recent breakthroughs of DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI-o3, and Gemini-3, and has spawned a cottage industry of RL training recipes (ProRL, BroRL, ScaleRL) focused on algorithmic improvements to sustain training for longer steps or larger rollout budgets.

However, these algorithmic scaling efforts share a common assumption: that there is enough diverse training data to keep feeding the model novel problems. **This assumption breaks down in practice.** As the paper demonstrates in Figure 3, for a model that has already undergone extensive RL training (ProRL-1.5B-v2, trained for over 20,000 H100 GPU hours), only about 25% of the 136K samples in the ProRL data blend remain "effective"—meaning the model doesn't either consistently succeed or consistently fail on them, both of which provide zero learning signal. The rest are stale. You cannot improve a model on problems it has already mastered, nor on problems it fundamentally cannot solve; the gradient vanishes in both cases.

This is data saturation: the point where additional RL training on the same task distribution yields diminishing returns, regardless of algorithmic sophistication. The paper's key observation is that data saturation is not merely a nuisance—it is **the primary bottleneck** for continued RL scaling, and it becomes more severe, not less, as models become more capable. While the ProRL recipe sustained continuous gains for DeepSeek-R1-1.5B over two thousand steps, applying the identical recipe to the stronger Qwen-4B-Instruct caused performance to plateau or regress after merely 300 steps (Figure 2, Table 1). A stronger model saturates faster because it more quickly reduces the fraction of problems on which it exhibits mixed success and failure.

This is the gap the paper addresses: how do you generate fresh, diverse, verifiable training tasks when the model has exhausted the signal in existing RLVR datasets, and when the construction of new verifiable tasks is inherently constrained by the requirement for automatic answer checking?

### Why This Problem Matters (Beyond Academic Interest)

The practical stakes are substantial and operate at multiple levels of the LLM ecosystem:

**The ceiling on reasoning capabilities is a data ceiling, not an algorithmic one.** The recent flurry of RL recipe improvements (GRPO variants, decoupled advantage normalization, dynamic sampling ratios) implicitly assumes that better optimization can extract more from a fixed dataset. The paper provides evidence that this is false: when the fraction of effective examples drops to ~25%, algorithmic improvements alone cannot compensate (Figure 5—continued ProRL data yields 0.13% STEM gain vs. 3.48% with GooseReason). This means the field's research effort on RL algorithms, while valuable, is hitting diminishing returns without complementary data scaling. The paper reframes the conversation: **data synthesis, not algorithmic engineering, is the critical path for the next generation of reasoning models.**

**The scarcity is domain-asymmetric and worsens for real-world applications.** Math and code have relatively abundant verifiable data because correctness can be checked by expression evaluators and unit tests. But as you move into STEM, medicine, law, economics, and cybersecurity, the problems become open-ended, multi-paragraph, or require specialized domain knowledge to verify. These domains are precisely where reasoning-capable LLMs would be most valuable (medical diagnosis, legal analysis, threat intelligence), yet they are systematically excluded from RLVR pipelines. The paper's experiments on cybersecurity (Section 3.2) make this concrete: before Golden Goose, the domain had **zero** open-source RLVR data, and the previous state-of-the-art (Llama-Primus-Instruct) achieved only a 1.44% gain over its base model despite extensive domain-specific pre-training and post-training—the exact scenario where RLVR should help most but cannot because there is nothing to RL on.

**The economics of data creation fundamentally favor synthesis over human curation.** Human-authored verifiable problems (Big-Math, AceReason-Nemotron, Omni-Math) are high-quality but expensive, slow to produce, and limited to domains where human experts can be recruited and paid. Procedural generators (RLVE, Reasoning Gym, ScaleR) can produce infinite examples but require hand-engineered verifiable environments—logical puzzles, grid navigation, formal games—that are difficult to scale beyond a few hundred distinct environments and whose reasoning patterns bear limited resemblance to the open-ended scientific and professional reasoning that LLMs are ultimately deployed to perform. Golden Goose proposes a third path: exploit the vast corpus of **already-existing reasoning text** on the internet—textbook explanations, forum solutions, security analyses, code walkthroughs—and mechanically transform it into verifiable tasks without requiring the original text to be verifiable. This is what the "unverifiable internet text" in the title refers to: text that contains reasoning but has no naturally associated correctness check.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies limitations across three established paradigms for constructing RLVR training data:

**1. Human-authored and human-verified datasets.** These are the gold standard but are fundamentally bottlenecked by human effort. The ProRL dataset (Liu et al., 2025a) blends 136K examples from multiple human-curated sources spanning math, code, logic, and instruction-following. It is diverse, high-quality, and effective—until the model saturates on it. At that point, the only way to get more data is to hire more domain experts, which is expensive and slow. This approach also inherently skews toward domains where verification is easy (math, code) and away from domains where human experts are scarce or where problems are inherently open-ended (e.g., medical differential diagnosis, where there may be multiple defensible answers rather than a single ground truth).

**2. Procedural environment generators.** RLVE (Zeng et al., 2025a), Reasoning Gym (Stojanovski et al., 2025), and ScaleR (Xu et al., 2026) take a different approach: hand-code verifiable environments that procedurally generate problems with tunable difficulty. This solves the scalability problem in principle—you can generate infinite examples—but introduces a new bottleneck: **the environments themselves must be manually designed.** Reasoning Gym, for instance, provides environments for algebraic manipulation, arithmetic, geometry, graphs, algorithmic puzzles, games, and logic. These are all formal, symbolic domains where a human programmer can write a verifier. But how do you write a procedural generator for "explain the chemical reactions when NH₃ is added to CuSO₄"? Or for "analyze this cybersecurity vulnerability report and identify the attack vector"? These require domain knowledge that cannot be reduced to a few hundred lines of Python. The paper's empirical comparison with RLVE (Figure 5) bears this out: RLVE is highly effective on math but yields only a 0.62% gain on STEM—because its procedurally generated environments are fundamentally algorithmic, not knowledge-intensive.

**3. Mining existing verifiable data.** Some efforts (rStar-Coder, DeepScaler) attempt to augment existing verifiable datasets by synthesizing test cases for coding problems or filtering clean examples from noisy sources. However, these approaches only work where verification is already near-at-hand. rStar-Coder, for example, successfully generated test cases for only 380K out of 1,656K synthesized problems—a 77% failure rate—because the pipeline requires problems to be amenable to input-output testing. The remaining 1.28M problems, which include algorithmic discussions and multi-step solution walkthroughs without clear I/O pairs, were discarded. Golden Goose specifically targets these discarded examples.

### How Golden Goose Positions Itself

The paper's contribution is best understood not as a new RL algorithm or a new model architecture, but as a **data transformation pipeline** that converts an abundant, currently wasted resource (reasoning-rich but unverifiable text) into a scarce, bottlenecked resource (RLVR-compatible training tasks). The insight is that the barrier to using internet text for RLVR is not that the text lacks reasoning—it often contains rich, multi-step reasoning (theorem proofs, scientific explanations, code walkthroughs)—but that there is no automatic way to check whether the model's reproduction of that reasoning is correct.

Golden Goose solves this by changing the verification paradigm from "check the model's generated answer" to "check the model's multiple-choice selection." In the standard RLVR setup, the model generates free-form text, and a verifier checks whether it matches the ground truth—which works for exact-match domains (math answers, code outputs) but fails for open-ended reasoning. In Golden Goose's MCQ fill-in-the-middle setup, the model selects from a fixed set of options, and verification is a trivial string match against the ground-truth option index. The verifiability comes from **constraining the output space**, not from making the underlying reasoning simpler. The key design choice—and the reason the approach is non-trivial—is that the multiple-choice task preserves the reasoning demand: the model must understand the masked context well enough to distinguish the correct reasoning continuation from distractors that are "plausible, similar in style and length to the removed content, but incorrect" (Section 2.1).

This positions Golden Goose as **complementary** to, rather than competitive with, existing data pipelines. It does not replace human-authored math datasets (which remain valuable for exact-solution training), nor does it replace procedural environments (which provide clean, controllable difficulty for formal reasoning). Instead, it fills a specific gap that neither approach addresses: open-ended, knowledge-intensive, multi-step reasoning in domains where verification is hard. The paper's experiments on cybersecurity (Section 3.2) are the cleanest demonstration of this complementarity—the domain has no RLVR data, no procedural generators, and no human-curated datasets, yet Golden Goose produces 180K verifiable tasks from raw web scrapes.

The paper also positions itself as **algorithm-agnostic**. Golden Goose is a data pipeline, not an RL recipe. It is compatible with GRPO, REINFORCE++, ProRL's clipped objective, or any other RL algorithm that accepts verifiable training examples. The experiments use ProRLv2 (a GRPO variant with decoupled advantage normalization), but the authors emphasize that the choice is incidental—the gains come from the data, not the optimizer.

A subtle but important positioning decision: the paper frames Golden Goose as **"a simple trick"** (reflected in the title). This is not false humility—it is making a methodological claim. Unlike procedural generators that require hand-designed environments, or human curation pipelines that require expert annotators, Golden Goose requires only a capable LLM (GPT-5 in the experiments) and a prompt template. The simplicity is the point: if the approach works, it democratizes RLVR data creation, enabling researchers and practitioners to construct domain-specific training sets from any text corpus without specialized infrastructure. The cybersecurity case study is the proof of concept for this claim—a domain with no prior infrastructure for RLVR, bootstrapped from raw internet scrapes in a matter of days.

### The Conceptual Shift: From Verifiable Sources to Verifiable Formats

The deepest conceptual contribution of the paper is a shift in how to think about verifiability for RL training. Prior work implicitly equates "verifiable" with "derivable from a ground-truth source that is itself automatically checkable" (a math expression equals 42, a code output passes unit tests). Golden Goose decouples these: the **source text** remains unverifiable (there is no automatic way to confirm the chemistry textbook's explanation is correct), but the **task format** is verifiable (the model's answer is either option A or it isn't). The correctness signal comes from the original author's reasoning, which is trusted as ground truth, not from an independent verification mechanism.

This is a trade-off: Golden Goose replaces verification rigor with coverage. The paper does not claim that the masked content is the uniquely correct continuation—it acknowledges that some distractors might be alternative reasonable continuations—but argues that for RL training, this is acceptable because (a) the distractors are explicitly designed to be incorrect in context, and (b) the MCQ format makes the verification objective unambiguous even when the underlying content admits multiple interpretations. Whether this trade-off introduces systematic biases or ceiling effects in the learned reasoning is an open question the paper does not fully explore, but the empirical results suggest the training signal is genuine—the model improves on non-MCQ benchmarks (AIME, MATH, LiveCodeBench) despite training only on MCQ tasks, indicating transfer of generalizable reasoning skills rather than format-specific strategies.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Golden Goose is a data synthesis pipeline—not a model, not an RL algorithm—that mechanically converts passages of explanatory text from the internet into automatically-graded multiple-choice questions suitable for RLVR training, without requiring the original text to be verifiable in any way. The system solves the RLVR data bottleneck problem with a "format transformation" approach: rather than trying to find or generate new text that passes an external correctness check (which is hard for open-ended reasoning), it takes existing reasoning-rich text, masks out a critical span, generates plausible-but-wrong alternatives as distractors, and uses exact-match verification against the known ground-truth option—trading the hardness of verifying free-form reasoning for the constraint of a multiple-choice interface.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The Golden Goose pipeline has four stages:

1. **Source Text Ingestion:** A reasoning-rich passage enters the system—this could be a math proof from AoPS-Instruct, a code walkthrough from rStar-Coder, a textbook answer from MegaScience, or a raw web scrape from FineWeb (for cybersecurity). The text may contain multi-step reasoning, specialized notation, and open-ended explanations, but none of it needs to be machine-verifiable.

2. **MCQ Task Construction (the core transformation):** A strong LLM (GPT-5 in the experiments) reads the source text and performs two sequential operations: (a) it identifies a contiguous span of "important reasoning steps" and replaces it with a `[MASK]` token, treating the removed content as the ground-truth answer, and (b) it generates a set of diverse distractors—plausible continuations that are similar in style and length to the masked span but incorrect in the surrounding context. For noisy sources, an initial extraction step first summarizes the source into a coherent, educationally valuable passage.

3. **Difficulty Filtering (quality control):** The synthesized MCQ is scored against the target student model (the one being RL-trained). If the model consistently succeeds across all rollouts (indicating the task is too easy), or consistently fails (indicating it is too hard or malformed), the example may be filtered out for being stale. The paper keeps examples where the model exhibits mixed success and failure—these provide the gradient signal for RL.

4. **RL Training Consumption:** The MCQ task—consisting of the masked passage as context, the candidate options in randomized order, and the ground-truth option label—is fed into any standard RLVR pipeline. The student model reads the masked passage and options, outputs an answer selection, and receives a binary reward from exact-match verification against the ground truth. The paper uses the ProRLv2 recipe (GRPO with decoupled advantage normalization), but the pipeline is algorithm-agnostic.

Information flows linearly: raw text → GPT-5 synthesis → MCQ in JSON format → optional difficulty filter → RL training step → model updates → repeated with fresh batches.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First:** the core MCQ construction algorithm (the "fill-in-the-middle → multiple-choice" transformation), because it is the central intellectual contribution—everything else depends on understanding what kind of tasks Golden Goose produces and why they are verifiable when the source text was not.

- **Second:** the prompt templates and output format specification, because the reliability of the synthesis depends on precise prompt engineering that constrains GPT-5 to produce well-formed tasks with the required properties (contiguous masked span, diverse distractors, correct JSON structure).

- **Third:** the design choices around distractor count and task difficulty, because Figure 4 reveals a non-obvious calibration problem—too few distractors make tasks trivially solvable by elimination, too many may make them impossible—and getting this right is critical for generating effective RL training signal.

- **Fourth:** the source corpora selection and preprocessing, because the pipeline’s generality claim rests on applying the same core algorithm to qualitatively different text types (theorem proofs, code walkthroughs, textbook explanations, cybersecurity analyses) with minimal per-domain adaptation.

- **Fifth:** the difficulty filtering mechanism, because the fraction of "effective" examples (those providing learning signal) is the key metric for evaluating whether GooseReason-0.7M actually solves the data saturation problem.

**Sixth:** the integration with RL training—how the MCQ tasks plug into the ProRLv2 recipe and what verification looks like at training time—to connect the data synthesis pipeline to the empirical results in Section 3 of the paper.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **data synthesis paper** whose core idea is that the barrier between unverifiable reasoning text and RLVR training is a *format mismatch*, not a *content deficit*: reasoning-rich text already contains correct multi-step reasoning (written by humans or strong models), and the challenge is repackaging that reasoning into tasks with automatic verification—which Golden Goose achieves by constraining the model's output to a fixed set of discrete options and using the original text's reasoning as the ground truth, sidestepping the need to verify free-form generation.

---

#### The Core Transformation: Fill-in-the-Middle as Multiple-Choice

The fundamental algorithmic step in Golden Goose is deceptively simple: take a passage of explanatory text, cut out a contiguous chunk, and ask the model to select which of several alternatives correctly fills the gap. What makes this non-trivial—and what distinguishes it from generic MCQ generation—is the set of constraints the paper imposes on *which* chunk gets cut and *what* the alternatives look like.

**Step 1: Identify a critical reasoning span.** Given source text $S$, the synthesis LLM must locate "a consecutive multi-sentence span of text that contains several important reasoning steps" (Appendix A, prompts). This is not a random deletion—it is a targeted excision of content whose removal creates a genuine reasoning challenge. If you mask a trivial transition word ("therefore," "however"), the task becomes a vocabulary test. If you mask the entire argument, the task becomes impossible because there is insufficient context to discriminate alternatives. The paper implicitly relies on GPT-5's ability to identify spans that are *central but not definitional*: steps where the surrounding text provides enough constraint to make exactly one continuation correct and the others wrong, but where that discrimination requires understanding the reasoning.

**Step 2: Replace with `[MASK]` and treat the removed content as the ground-truth answer.** This is the verification trick. Let $t$ be the removed span (the ground truth). The synthesis LLM produces a masked passage $S_{\text{mask}}$, which is $S$ with $t$ replaced by the literal token `[MASK]`. During RL training, the student model's task is to select which option corresponds to $t$ from the candidate set. Verification is a string equality check: does the model's chosen option match the ground-truth option? There is no need for an external verifier, no need for unit tests, no need for expression evaluation. The correctness signal is derived from the *author of the source text*, whose reasoning is trusted as authoritative.

**Step 3: Generate diverse, plausible distractors.** The synthesis LLM produces a set of distractors $D = \{d_1, d_2, ..., d_k\}$ that must satisfy three criteria simultaneously: (a) **plausible**—each distractor must, when read in isolation, appear to be a reasonable continuation of the masked passage (otherwise the task reduces to spotting obviously nonsensical options); (b) **incorrect in context**—despite surface plausibility, each distractor must be wrong specifically given the logical, mathematical, or factual constraints established by the surrounding text; and (c) **similar in style and length** to the ground truth—so that the model cannot use superficial features (option A is always shorter, option B uses a different notation) to short-circuit the reasoning.

This three-part constraint is what makes the MCQ task a test of genuine reasoning rather than pattern matching. The paper's Figure 4 implicitly validates this: with only 3 distractors, the majority of problems are "overly easy" because the model can use an elimination strategy—identifying superficial flaws in two options and selecting the third—without performing the intended reasoning about the masked content. With 9 distractors, the elimination strategy becomes computationally infeasible under the model's output length budget, and over 70% of tasks fall into the medium-difficulty regime where the model exhibits both successes and failures.

The final MCQ task $Q$ is then:

$$Q = (S_{\text{mask}}, \{t\} \cup D)$$

where $S_{\text{mask}}$ is the masked passage, $\{t\} \cup D$ is the set of $k+1$ candidate options (ground truth plus $k$ distractors), and the options are presented to the student model in randomized order to prevent positional bias.

**What the complete MCQ looks like in practice (from Figure 1):**

For a coding example, the source text is a forum post explaining how to properly allocate a 2D array in C. The synthesis LLM identifies the critical line `data[r]=(int*)malloc(sizeof(**data)*5);`, masks it, and generates distractors including `data[r]=(int*)malloc(sizeof(int));`, `data[r]=(int*)malloc(sizeof(**data)*5);` (note: this is the correct one—the distractors are similar in syntax but differ in the sizeof argument, allocation pattern, or cast type). The student model sees the surrounding explanatory text, the code skeleton with a `[MASK]` placeholder, and the candidate options. Selecting the correct option requires understanding both the C memory model (why `sizeof(**data)` is the right element size, why the cast to `int**` is needed) and the context of the forum answer (the explanation preceding the code indicates this is about allocating pointers for each row).

For a cybersecurity example (from FineWeb scrapes), the source text discusses directory hash collisions as a denial-of-service vector. The synthesis LLM first extracts a coherent passage (since the raw scrape is noisy), then masks the paragraph that explains the attack mechanism, and generates distractors that describe other plausible but incorrect attack vectors or mischaracterize the vulnerability's scope. The student model must distinguish the correct technical description from alternatives that sound plausible to a non-expert but are wrong in the specific context of CRC32C hash collisions in shared directories.

#### Prompt Templates and Output Format Specification

The synthesis pipeline is driven by three prompt templates (Appendix A), each tailored to a source text format. The prompts serve as both task specification and quality control—they constrain GPT-5's output to the precise format needed for downstream RL training and encode domain-specific calibration.

**Math and STEM Prompt (for AoPS-Instruct and MegaScience):**
The prompt receives a question and its reference solution and instructs the LLM to "identify several consecutive steps that are important in the reference solution and replace them with [MASK]" and to "generate at least ten diverse distractors that are plausible, similar in style and length to the removed steps, but incorrect." The output must be a JSON object with keys `masked_reference_solution`, `removed_steps`, and `distractors` (an array).

Why "at least ten"? The paper's distractor ablation (Figure 4) shows that 9 distractors (10 total options including the ground truth) places the majority of tasks in the effective difficulty regime. The prompt asks for "at least ten" to ensure sufficient diversity even after potential quality filtering—some generated distractors may be too obviously wrong or too similar to the ground truth, and having extras allows for post-hoc curation.

**Code Prompt (for rStar-Coder):**
Structurally identical to the math prompt but specialized for code: it instructs the LLM to "identify several consecutive lines of code that are important in the reference solution and replace them with [MASK]" and to generate distractors that are "plausible, similar in style and length to the removed lines, but incorrect." The key difference from the math prompt is that distractors for code must be syntactically valid—a distractor that doesn't parse would be trivially distinguishable. This requires GPT-5 to generate alternatives that preserve the surface structure of the correct code (same APIs, similar variable names, valid syntax) while introducing semantic errors (wrong memory allocation, incorrect loop bounds, off-by-one indexing).

**Cybersecurity Prompt (for FineWeb and Primus-Seed):**
This prompt has an additional preprocessing stage. Because raw web scrapes are noisy—they may contain navigation elements, user comments, incomplete sentences, or entirely irrelevant content—the LLM is first instructed to "extract or summarize a coherent passage (around 100-600 words) that is educationally valuable for learning Cybersecurity." Only after this extraction does the standard MCQ construction proceed. If no educationally valuable passage exists, the LLM returns an empty string, effectively filtering the source text.

The cybersecurity prompt also specifies the distractor constraints differently: distractors must be "plausible, similar in style and length to the removed content, but incorrect." There is no "at least ten" specification; the prompt simply says "generate at least ten diverse distractors." The cybersecurity tasks tend to involve longer masked spans (multi-sentence explanations of attack mechanisms or defensive strategies) compared to math tasks (where the masked span is typically a few equations or derivation steps), and the distractor generation must account for the higher-level semantic content.

**JSON output constraint:** All prompts require output within `<answer></answer>` tags in JSON format. This is a parsing reliability measure—the JSON structure enforces field separation between the masked passage, the removed content, and the distractors, making the pipeline's downstream consumption deterministic rather than requiring heuristic parsing of free-text LLM responses. The ground-truth option label (A, B, C, etc.) is implicitly defined by the position of the ground truth in the shuffled candidate list at training time, not by the synthesis LLM—this prevents the synthesis LLM from accidentally encoding the correct answer position in the raw output.

**Prompt design rationale:** Why GPT-5 rather than a smaller, cheaper model? The paper states they used "the strongest LLM available at the time of the experiment" for synthesis and found the resulting questions were of sufficient quality to require no further post-processing for reasoning-dense source text (Section 2.1). This is a pragmatic rather than principled choice—the quality of the MCQ generation depends on the synthesis model's ability to (a) understand the source reasoning well enough to identify what is critical versus incidental, (b) generate distractors that are close enough to the ground truth to be challenging without being correct, and (c) produce syntactically valid JSON consistently. Weaker models may fail on (b) or (c), introducing noise into the RL training data. The paper does not ablate the synthesis model choice, so the sensitivity of the pipeline to the quality of the synthesis LLM remains an open question.

#### Design Choice: Multiple-Choice vs. Open-Ended Fill-in-the-Middle

The paper explicitly considers an alternative task formulation (Section 2.3) that would preserve more of the generative challenge: instead of MCQ selection, present the masked passage and ask the model to generate the infill text, then use an LLM-as-judge to compare the generation against the ground truth. This open-ended formulation is closer to the original reasoning task—the model must produce the reasoning, not just recognize it among options.

The paper rejects this alternative based on an empirical finding that "over 83% of examples in the open-ended version of GooseReason-Math result in consistent zero accuracy for ProRL-1.5B-v2" (Figure 4). The cause is poor instruction following: models that have been heavily tuned with RL for math problem-solving develop a strong behavioral prior to "solve the problem from scratch and completely ignore the task requirement of generating the infill." Even when the prompt explicitly asks for the masked content, the model defaults to its training distribution—which is to produce full solutions, not completions.

This is a revealing result about RL-trained models: their instruction-following behavior on atypical task formats degrades even as their reasoning capabilities improve, because the RL optimization pressure has no signal about format adherence (it only rewards final-answer correctness). The MCQ formulation avoids this problem because the output space is structurally constrained: the model must output an option label or the option text, not a full solution. The verification signal for correct format is immediate (if the model doesn't output a recognizable option, it gets zero reward), and the task format is unambiguous.

The paper also identifies a secondary cost argument: LLM-as-judge verification during RL training requires hosting a powerful judge model alongside the training pipeline, which multiplies the computational overhead. This is not a theoretical concern—ProRL-style training already spans thousands of GPU hours; adding judge-model inference to every rollout would dramatically increase the cost per training step.

**Design choice summary:** MCQ format over open-ended because (a) RL-trained models exhibit format adherence failures on fill-in-the-middle tasks, (b) LLM-as-judge introduces computational overhead and potential judge bias, and (c) MCQ verification is a deterministic exact match, providing a clean, noise-free reward signal.

#### Design Choice: Number of Distractors and Task Difficulty Calibration

The paper's distractor count ablation (Figure 4) is central to validating the approach:

**With 3 choices (2 distractors + ground truth):** The majority of tasks in GooseReason-Math become "overly easy" for ProRL-1.5B-v2. The model's accuracy distribution is heavily skewed toward high success rates. Why? With only two wrong options, an elimination strategy is highly effective: the model can identify that option A uses the wrong variable, option B has the wrong sign, and therefore option C must be correct—without ever reasoning about what the masked content should contain. This is a well-documented behavior in MCQ-answering LLMs; reducing the distractor count makes the task a process-of-elimination exercise rather than a genuine reasoning test.

**With 6 choices (5 distractors + ground truth):** The distribution shifts toward medium difficulty but still has a substantial fraction of easy tasks. The elimination strategy becomes more computationally expensive (the model must evaluate 5 alternatives), but for a 1.5B-parameter reasoning model, this remains within its effective context-processing capacity.

**With 9 choices (8 distractors + ground truth):** Over 70% of problems fall into the medium-difficulty regime, where the model exhibits both successful and failed rollouts. The elimination strategy is no longer reliable because (a) the model's context window and attention mechanism must track 8 candidate alternatives while simultaneously understanding the masked passage, and (b) the distractors are explicitly designed to be similar in style and length to the ground truth, making superficial differentiation difficult. At this scale, the model is forced to actually reason about the masked content—to infer what the missing steps should be and then match against the options—rather than reverse-engineering the answer from flaws in the distractors.

**Why 9 specifically?** The paper does not justify the exact number theoretically, but it can be understood as an empirical calibration: 9 distractors is the point where the difficulty distribution maximizes the fraction of tasks in the "effective" regime for ProRL-1.5B-v2. For weaker models, fewer distractors might be optimal; for stronger models, more might be needed. The distractor count is a difficulty-tuning knob: increasing it raises task difficulty by making elimination harder, decreasing it lowers difficulty. This is analogous to adjusting the temperature in sampling—it's a hyperparameter that controls the signal-to-noise ratio of the RL training data.

**The underlying metric:** The paper defines task effectiveness operationally: a task is "effective" if the model's rollouts exhibit both successes and failures (Section 2.2.1, Figure 3 caption). If the model always succeeds, the RL gradient is zero because there is no contrast between good and bad outcomes to learn from. If the model always fails, the gradient is also zero because there is no positive signal to reinforce. The goal of the distractor count calibration is to shift the task distribution into the "mixed outcome" regime, which is the only regime where RL training produces a learning signal. This is a standard insight from RL theory (the policy gradient is proportional to the advantage, which is zero when all actions have the same expected return), but applied to the meta-problem of training data curation rather than the training algorithm itself.

---

#### Source Corpora and Preprocessing

Golden Goose targets three qualitatively distinct types of unverifiable reasoning text, each excluded from prior RLVR pipelines for different reasons:

**AoPS-Instruct (Olympiad math proofs):** The Art of Problem Solving forum contains approximately 600K question-answer pairs, predominantly Olympiad-level math problems with community-driven solutions. These solutions are rich in multi-step reasoning but systematically excluded from RLVR for two reasons: (a) many problems are theorem-proving tasks whose "answers" are entire proofs, not evaluable by expression-matchers (you cannot check whether a proof of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality is correct by comparing it to a ground-truth string); and (b) solutions vary widely in format, notation, and completeness due to the unstructured forum setting—some may be partial sketches, others may contain non-standard notation, and many mix explanatory prose with mathematical expressions in ways that resist automatic parsing. Golden Goose handles both issues: the MCQ format doesn't require verifying the proof, only verifying that the model selects the option matching the original author's reasoning; and GPT-5's synthesis step can normalize format inconsistencies by extracting the core reasoning steps into a clean masked passage.

**rStar-Coder (coding problems without test cases):** Of the 1,656K problems synthesized in rStar-Coder, only 380K (23%) successfully obtained test cases through the input-output synthesis pipeline. The remaining 1,276K problems—including the entire `sft` split, which contains questions and teacher model solutions but no test cases—were discarded for RL purposes. These problems represent exactly the type of reasoning that Golden Goose targets: code walkthroughs where the solution contains explanatory steps, algorithm descriptions, and code snippets, but where automatic I/O testing is either impossible (because the problem doesn't have well-defined input-output pairs) or the synthesis pipeline failed. The paper uses the `sft` split specifically, treating the teacher model's solution as the ground truth and generating distractors for critical code segments.

**MegaScience (free-form textbook QA):** This corpus contains 650K question-answer pairs extracted from 12K university-level scientific textbooks spanning physics, biology, chemistry, medicine, computer science, mathematics, and economics. Many answers involve specialized scientific formulas (chemistry stoichiometry, physics derivations), multi-paragraph explanations (medical diagnosis rationales, economic analyses), or open-ended discussions. These are excluded from standard RLVR for the same reason as proofs: there is no automatic way to verify whether a generated explanation of "why NH₃ forms a blue solution when added to CuSO₄" is correct. The answer is not a number you can evaluate, nor a code you can run. Golden Goose's MCQ format sidesteps this by asking the model to select the correct continuation of the textbook's explanation, not to generate the explanation itself.

**FineWeb for Cybersecurity:** Unlike the reasoning-domain corpora, FineWeb is a general web crawl—it contains HTML artifacts, navigation elements, duplicate content, and topic-irrelevant text alongside valuable cybersecurity discussions. The paper applies two preprocessing stages: (a) The `Primus-Seed` and `Primus-FineWeb` filtering that Yu et al. (2025) used to isolate cybersecurity-related content from the broader FineWeb corpus (using Primus-Seed, a curated set of known cybersecurity sources, as positive samples for a text classifier); (b) Golden Goose's own extraction step, where GPT-5 either identifies educationally valuable cybersecurity passages within a document or returns an empty string if none exist. This two-stage filtering is necessary because the raw FineWeb documents are far noisier than the reasoning corpora—they are web scrapes, not curated educational content. The cybersecurity prompt's instruction to extract "a coherent passage (around 100-600 words) that is educationally valuable for learning Cybersecurity" serves as a quality gate: passages that are too short lack sufficient context for the masked span to be discriminable; passages that are too long may contain multiple distinct topics, making it unclear what the task is testing.

**Corpus overlap considerations:** The paper uses source corpora that were *already excluded* from prior RLVR pipelines—the AoPS proofs that math verifiers couldn't handle, the rStar-Coder problems that test-case synthesis failed on, the MegaScience explanations that expression evaluation couldn't check. This is a deliberate choice: it ensures GooseReason-0.7M is *complementary* to existing RLVR datasets rather than redundant with them. The 13× increase in effective examples shown in Figure 3 comes from targeting text that prior work explicitly discarded, not from repackaging already-verifiable problems.

#### Difficulty Filtering

The pipeline includes an optional filtering step that removes tasks the student model finds trivially easy or impossibly hard. This is specified in Section 2.1:

> "we found some masked spans could be easily inferred from context rather than requiring reasoning; thus, we additionally employ difficulty-based filtering to remove easy problems on which the student model consistently succeeds across all 16 rollouts"

The filtering criterion is operationalized as: for each synthesized MCQ, sample 16 rollouts from the current student model (before or during RL training). Compute the accuracy—the fraction of rollouts where the model selects the correct option. If accuracy is 100% (consistently succeeds) or 0% (consistently fails), the task provides no RL learning signal and may be filtered. This is applied primarily to the cybersecurity data, where noisy source text produces some tasks whose masked spans are recoverable from local context alone (e.g., a masked term that is restated in the next sentence).

**Why 16 rollouts?** The paper does not justify this number, but it is consistent with standard practice in RLVR experiments (the ProRL recipe uses 16 rollouts per prompt for advantage estimation), and it provides a reasonable statistical sample for estimating whether a task is truly trivial/impossible versus just having high/low but non-degenerate success rates. Sixteen samples give you a binomial proportion with standard error approximately $\sqrt{p(1-p)/16}$; if the true success rate is 95%, you'd observe 16/16 successes with probability $0.95^{16} \approx 44\%$, meaning the filter has a non-trivial false-positive rate for tasks that are merely very easy rather than perfectly predictable. This is likely acceptable because (a) the filtering is conservative—it only definitively removes tasks the model has already mastered, and (b) the cost of erroneously keeping a mastered task is low (zero gradient, but no harm), while the cost of erroneously removing a task with genuine learning potential is higher (lost training signal).

**Filtering as staleness detection:** This filtering mechanism is conceptually identical to the "effective example" metric used in Figure 3—both identify tasks where the model's rollouts are all-success or all-failure. The difference is that Figure 3 uses the metric for analysis (measuring how much of the existing ProRL dataset has gone stale), while the difficulty filter uses it operationally (preventing already-stale tasks from entering the training loop at all). The insight is that difficulty filtering at data creation time prevents the RL training from wasting compute on tasks that provide no gradient signal, effectively increasing the density of "effective" examples in the training batch.

#### Integration with RL Training (ProRLv2 Recipe)

Golden Goose produces MCQ tasks in a format that plugs directly into any RLVR training loop. The paper uses ProRLv2 (Hu et al., 2025b), a GRPO variant, but emphasizes that the data pipeline is algorithm-agnostic.

**What happens at training time:**
1. A batch of MCQ tasks is sampled from GooseReason-0.7M (or mixed with existing ProRL data in the experiments).
2. For each task, the student model receives the masked passage $S_{\text{mask}}$ as part of the prompt, along with the candidate options presented in randomized order. The prompt format specifies the task (e.g., "You are given a science problem and its solution, with some steps of the solution replaced by [MASK]. Select the option that best fills in the [MASK].").
3. The model generates an answer selection. The paper does not specify the exact output format, but based on standard MCQ RL practices, the model is trained to output either the option label (A, B, C, ...) or the option text. During evaluation, the output is parsed and matched to the candidate options.
4. Verification: the selected option is compared to the ground-truth option (the removed content $t$) via exact string match. If they match, reward = 1; otherwise, reward = 0.

**The GRPO training objective:** The paper uses the ProRLv2 variant of GRPO, which employs the clipped GRPO objective with decoupled advantage normalization (from REINFORCE++). For a batch of rollouts, the advantage for each rollout is computed as:

$$A_i = \frac{r_i - \mu_{\text{group}}}{\sigma_{\text{batch}}}$$

where $r_i$ is the binary reward for rollout $i$, $\mu_{\text{group}}$ is the mean reward within the rollout group (the 16 rollouts for a given prompt), and $\sigma_{\text{batch}}$ is the standard deviation of rewards across the entire training batch.

**Interpretation:** The advantage normalizes rewards at two levels: first within each prompt group (so the model learns relative to its own typical performance on that prompt), then across the batch (so the scale of advantages is consistent across training steps). For a binary reward, this means a correct answer on a hard prompt (where most rollouts fail) receives a higher advantage than a correct answer on an easy prompt (where most rollouts succeed), which naturally weights the learning signal toward tasks at the frontier of the model's capability—exactly the "effective" regime the data pipeline targets.

**Why this objective form matters for Golden Goose:** The MCQ tasks produce binary rewards—the model either selects the correct option or it doesn't. There is no partial credit, no continuous score. The advantage normalization ensures that the training signal is proportional to how surprising the outcome is given the model's current policy, not to the absolute reward magnitude. This prevents the RL process from devoting most of its update to reinforcing already-easy tasks (where reward = 1 is common and therefore low-advantage) and instead focuses the update on tasks where the model sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails (high-advantage correct answers, high-magnitude negative-advantage incorrect answers).

**Data mixing strategy:** In the continued training experiments (Figures 2, 5), GooseReason-0.7M is mixed with the original ProRL data blend. The paper does not specify the exact mixing ratio, but the results show that the addition of GooseReason data "revives" the saturated model—the performance gains come from the fresh tasks, not from changes to the training algorithm. In the compute-constrained experiments (Figure 6), joint training with GooseReason and ProRL data from scratch consistently outperforms ProRL data alone at the same number of steps.

**A subtle implication:** The binary, exact-match reward structure means that the RL training signal for Golden Goose tasks is *cleaner* than for open-ended generation tasks, where verifier noise (false positives or false negatives from the math expression evaluator or unit test executor) introduces stochasticity into the reward. For MCQ tasks, the reward is perfectly deterministic given the model's output: either the selected option matches the ground-truth string, or it doesn't. This reduces the variance of the policy gradient estimate and may contribute to the stability of training—the paper does not make this argument explicitly, but it follows from the standard analysis of RL with stochastic vs. deterministic rewards.

#### The Overall Pipeline as a Data Scaling Engine

Stepping back, the Golden Goose pipeline can be understood as a **data scaling engine** that converts a resource that is currently abundant and unused (unverifiable reasoning text on the internet) into a resource that is scarce and bottlenecked (verifiable training tasks for RL). The key properties that make this work:

1. **The verification burden is shifted from content to format.** The original text doesn't need to be checkable; only the MCQ selection needs to be checkable, which is trivial.

2. **The synthesis model (GPT-5) serves as a domain-knowledge bridge.** It translates raw text into a structured task format without requiring the pipeline designers to understand the domain—the cybersecurity case study is the proof of this, since the authors are not cybersecurity experts.

3. **The distractor count provides a tunable difficulty knob** that can be calibrated to the student model's current capability, ensuring that generated tasks fall in the "effective" regime where RL produces learning signal.

4. **The approach is additive rather than substitutive.** It produces tasks for domains and text formats that prior pipelines explicitly excluded, meaning it expands the RLVR data frontier rather than competing for space within existing domains.

The pipeline's output—GooseReason-0.7M—is geometrically larger than the effective examples in the saturated ProRL dataset (a 13× increase, per Figure 3), because it targets the long tail of reasoning text that prior work could not operationalize. This quantitative claim is the basis for the paper's central argument: data synthesis at this scale is what enables RL training to continue beyond the saturation point, not algorithmic improvements to the training recipe.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Decoupling Verifiability from Source Content—The Format-Level Verification Trick

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is to break the implicit equation that has governed RLVR data construction: "verifiable training data = source text whose correctness can be automatically checked." Under this assumption, the universe of RLVR-compatible problems is limited to domains with machine-checkable ground truth—math expressions that evaluate to a number, code that passes unit tests, game states with deterministic win conditions. Everything else—theorem proofs, medical explanations, security analyses, textbook derivations—is walled off, not because it lacks reasoning, but because reasoning isn't machine-verifiable.

Golden Goose rejects this equation entirely and substitutes a different one: **verifiability is a property of the task format, not the source content.** The source text remains unverifiable—there is no automated way to confirm the chemistry textbook's explanation of ammonia-copper complexation is correct. But the task format is verifiable: given a masked passage and a set of candidate options, the model either selects the option that matches the ground-truth author's text, or it doesn't. The verification is a trivial exact-match check against a known string, completely independent of whether the underlying content is mathematically or scientifically provable.

This is a **fundamental reframing**, not an incremental improvement. Prior data synthesis approaches—whether human-curated (Big-Math, AceReason-Nemotron), procedurally generated (RLVE, Reasoning Gym, ScaleR), or test-case-augmented (rStar-Coder, DeepScaler)—all operate within the "verifiable source content" paradigm. Human curators select problems with known answers. Procedural generators create environments where answers are checkable by construction. Test-case synthesis extends verifiability to new problems but fails when the problem format resists I/O testing (rStar-Coder's 77% failure rate). Golden Goose is the first approach to say: the source content doesn't need to be verifiable at all; we can borrow the original author's reasoning as authoritative ground truth and make the *task* verifiable by constraining the model's output space.

The significance of this shift extends beyond the immediate empirical gains. It opens a **qualitatively new category of data** for RLVR—any text that contains reasoning, regardless of domain or format, becomes a candidate for transformation into training tasks. The cybersecurity case study (Section 3.2) is the cleanest proof of concept: a domain with zero prior RLVR infrastructure, bootstrapped from raw web scrapes, produces a 4.44% absolute gain in 100 RL steps. This would be impossible under any prior paradigm. Human curation is infeasible for every specialized domain; procedural generation can't encode cybersecurity expertise in a few hundred lines of Python; test-case synthesis has no purchase on threat intelligence analysis. Only format-level verification makes this feasible, because it doesn't require understanding the domain—it only requires a synthesis model strong enough to identify critical reasoning spans and generate plausible distractors.

The trade-off is acknowledged but under-analyzed: Golden Goose trusts the original text's author as ground truth, which means the training signal inherits whatever errors, biases, or incompleteness exist in the source. If a forum solution contains a subtle mathematical error, the MCQ will train the model to reproduce that error—the format is verifiable but the content is not validated. The paper implicitly accepts this as the cost of scale, and the strong downstream results suggest the noise is manageable, but the boundary conditions of this trade-off (how much source error is tolerable before RL training degrades?) remain unexplored.

### Innovation 2: The "Effective Example" as a Diagnostic Metric for Data Saturation

The paper introduces a specific, operationalized metric—the **effective example**—that crystallizes a problem the field knew about vaguely but had not measured. An RLVR training example is "effective" if, for the current model policy, sampling multiple rollouts produces both successes and failures (Figure 3, Section 2.2.1). Examples where the model always succeeds are stale because they provide no positive gradient; examples where it always fails are stale because they provide no contrastive signal. The fraction of effective examples in a dataset is therefore a direct measure of how much learning potential remains.

This metric is not mathematically novel—it follows directly from the policy gradient theorem, where the advantage is zero when all actions have the same expected return. But its **diagnostic power** is the real contribution. By measuring effectiveness for ProRL-1.5B-v2 against both the original ProRL dataset and GooseReason-0.7M, the paper quantifies exactly _why_ continued RL training saturates: only ~25% of the 136K ProRL samples remain effective for this heavily trained model. The dataset hasn't changed; the model has simply solved or exhausted the learnable signal in three-quarters of the examples. GooseReason-0.7M retains ~70% effectiveness because its tasks are drawn from text distributions the model hasn't already optimized against—the Olympiad proofs in AoPS-Instruct, the untestable code walkthroughs in rStar-Coder, the textbook derivations in MegaScience.

Prior work on RL scaling (ProRL, BroRL, ScaleRL) focused on algorithmic dimensions—longer training, more rollouts per example, better advantage normalization—implicitly assuming the data contained sufficient unexploited signal. The "effective example" metric reframes the problem: **data saturation is the primary bottleneck, not algorithmic efficiency.** This is reinforced by the observation that Qwen-4B-Instruct saturates much faster than R1-1.5B (300 steps vs. 2,000+ steps) on the same ProRL recipe. A stronger model doesn't need a better optimizer—it needs fresh data, because it exhausts existing data faster. The metric provides a principled way to measure when data augmentation, rather than training continuation, is the appropriate intervention.

The metric also serves as a **curation and calibration tool** within the Golden Goose pipeline itself. The distractor count ablation (Figure 4) uses effectiveness as the optimization target: the optimal number of distractors is the one that maximizes the fraction of tasks falling into the mixed-outcome regime. The difficulty filtering step (Section 2.1) uses the same logic operationally, removing tasks the model always gets right or always gets wrong before they enter the training loop. This creates a feedback loop where the data pipeline adapts to the model's current capability—as the model improves, tasks that were previously medium-difficulty become easy and can be filtered, while new tasks synthesized from harder source material replenish the effective pool.

### Innovation 3: Showing That MCQ-Based RL Training Transfers to Open-Ended Reasoning Benchmarks

A natural objection to Golden Goose is that training on multiple-choice fill-in-the-middle tasks might produce a model that is good at MCQ fill-in-the-middle tasks—and nothing else. The format is artificial: real-world reasoning rarely presents itself as "select option C from this curated list of plausible continuations." If the RL training signal is entirely about discriminating pre-written alternatives rather than generating reasoning from scratch, the acquired skill might be format-specific pattern matching rather than generalizable reasoning capability.

The paper addresses this objection not through argument but through **benchmark selection.** Every evaluation benchmark in Tables 1-3 tests open-ended generation: AIME, MATH, Minerva, and Olympiad Bench require producing final numerical answers or expressions, not selecting among options; HumanEvalPlus, LiveCodeBench, and CodeContests require generating executable code from scratch; GPQA Diamond asks free-form scientific questions; Reasoning Gym involves logical puzzles with generated solutions. If Golden Goose training merely taught MCQ test-taking strategies, the model's performance on these benchmarks would be flat or negative—you can't elimination-strategy your way through an AIME problem that asks for a three-digit integer answer.

The results show the opposite. Continued RL with GooseReason-0.7M on Qwen-4B-Instruct yields absolute gains of 2.18% in math, 2.24% in coding, and 2.40% in STEM (Figure 2, Table 1-3). Gains appear even on Reasoning Gym logical puzzles—a domain not directly covered by GooseReason's source corpora—indicating that the reasoning skills acquired through MCQ fill-in-the-middle training are genuinely generalizable, not format-locked.

This finding is significant because it validates the **implicit hypothesis** of the format-transformation approach: that the reasoning required to discriminate among plausible distractors in a masked context is structurally similar to the reasoning required to generate correct solutions from scratch. When the model learns to identify why distractor A is wrong (wrong variable scope in C memory allocation) and why option H is correct (proper `sizeof` argument for row pointers), it is learning the underlying domain principles, not just the surface features of the distractor set. The MCQ format constrains the output space to make verification possible, but the **computational problem**—understanding the context, identifying the logical or mathematical constraints, and selecting the continuation that satisfies them—remains the same reasoning task.

The paper does not provide mechanistic evidence for _how_ this transfer occurs (e.g., analyzing whether the model's internal representations for MCQ tasks and open-ended tasks converge during training), which leaves open the possibility that the gains are partly attributable to general RL optimization benefits (more training steps, more diverse gradient signals) rather than specific skill transfer. However, the differential gains by domain—largest in STEM, where GooseReason provides genuinely novel data, smallest in coding, where existing RLVR data was already abundant—suggest the effect is data-driven rather than a generic "more training is better" phenomenon.

### Innovation 4: Identifying Instruction-Following Degradation as a Barrier to Open-Ended Data Synthesis

The paper's comparison of MCQ versus open-ended fill-in-the-middle formats (Section 2.3, Figure 4) produces a **diagnostic negative result** that is as illuminating as the positive findings. When presented with open-ended fill-in-the-middle tasks—"generate the missing text" rather than "select the correct option"—ProRL-1.5B-v2 fails on over 83% of examples, producing consistent zero accuracy. The cause is not that the model lacks the reasoning capability to fill the gap; it's that the model has developed a strong behavioral prior from RL training to "solve the problem from scratch" and ignores the task instruction to generate only the infill.

This is a revealing finding about a **hidden cost of RLVR training**: as models are optimized for final-answer correctness on standard problem formats (math problems with numerical answers, coding problems with executable solutions), their instruction-following fidelity on atypical task formats degrades. The RL objective provides no signal about format adherence—it only rewards whether the final answer matches the ground truth—so the model's behavior collapses toward its training distribution. When asked to do something unusual (fill in the middle rather than solve from scratch), the model defaults to what it was rewarded for.

This finding has implications beyond Golden Goose's design choice. It suggests that **the scaling of RL-trained models may be non-monotonic in capabilities**: as a model gets better at reasoning, it may simultaneously get worse at following instructions that deviate from its RL training distribution. This is a form of capability specialization analogous to the "alignment tax" observed in RLHF, but operating on task format rather than helpfulness/harmlessness. It also explains why prior work on self-correction and iterative refinement (Huang et al., 2023) found limited success—the models had been optimized for single-pass solution generation, not for the meta-cognitive task of revising their own outputs.

The paper treats this finding pragmatically—as justification for the MCQ format choice—but it has broader significance for the RLVR research program. If continued RL training on standard formats systematically erodes format-general instruction following, then the community needs to either (a) include diverse task formats in the training mix to preserve behavioral flexibility, (b) develop RL objectives that explicitly reward format adherence alongside correctness, or (c) accept that RL-trained models will be specialized tools that require careful prompt engineering to elicit their capabilities. The paper doesn't pursue these implications, but the diagnostic is clear and actionable.

### Innovation 5: Demonstrating That Data Diversity, Not Just Data Volume, Is the Active Ingredient in RL Scaling

A superficial reading of the paper's results might conclude that "more data = better RL training"—GooseReason-0.7M is larger than the effective ProRL subset, and performance improves. But the paper's evidence supports a sharper claim: **the gains come from data diversity, specifically from targeting domains and reasoning patterns that are absent from existing RLVR datasets, not from adding volume to already-represented domains.**

The clearest evidence is the domain-differential pattern in Figure 5 and Tables 1-3. When GooseReason-0.7M is added to continued ProRL training:

- **STEM shows the largest gain** (3.48% with GooseReason vs. 0.13% with continued ProRL data, Figure 5). This is the domain where existing RLVR data is scarcest—math and code have abundant verifiable data, but general science (chemistry, biology, medicine, economics) has almost none because verification is hard. GooseReason bridges this gap by converting MegaScience textbook QA into verifiable tasks.
- **Math and coding show moderate gains** (2.71% and 2.12% respectively). These domains already have substantial existing RLVR data, so GooseReason's contribution is incremental—adding Olympiad proof tasks and untestable code walkthroughs provides some novel reasoning patterns, but the model is already strong in these domains from prior training.
- **Domains not directly covered by GooseReason corpora** (logical puzzles in Reasoning Gym) still show improvement, suggesting that the reasoning skills acquired from diverse STEM/code/math text transfer across domains.

This pattern distinguishes Golden Goose from approaches that scale volume within existing domains. RLVE's procedural generators can produce infinite math problems, but they produce math problems—and Figure 5 shows RLVE achieves only a 0.62% STEM gain because procedurally generated environments don't encode domain-specific scientific knowledge. The active ingredient in GooseReason-0.7M is not "0.7 million tasks" in the abstract; it's that those 0.7 million tasks cover reasoning patterns (theorem-proving, multi-step scientific explanations, algorithmic code analysis without executable tests) that are structurally different from the math-expression-evaluation and code-unit-test patterns that dominate existing RLVR data.

This has direct implications for how the field should think about RLVR data scaling. The standard scaling paradigm—analogous to pretraining scaling laws—assumes that more data from the same distribution yields predictable, smooth improvements. The paper's results suggest RLVR scaling is better understood as a **coverage problem**: performance improves not by adding more examples from already-covered reasoning patterns, but by expanding into qualitatively different types of reasoning that the model hasn't yet learned to handle. If this interpretation is correct, then the limiting factor for future RLVR scaling is not the total volume of verifiable data but the diversity of reasoning formats that can be operationalized as verifiable tasks—precisely the bottleneck that Golden Goose's format-transformation approach addresses by unlocking text types (proofs, explanations, analyses) that were previously inaccessible.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary evaluation spans 15 benchmarks across four domains. Math performance is tested on AIME 2024 and 2025 (MAA, 2024; 2025), AMC (MAA), MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021b), Minerva (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), and Olympiad Bench (He et al., 2024). Coding is assessed using the PRIME validation set (Cui et al., 2025), covering APPS (Hendrycks et al., 2021a), CodeContests (Li et al., 2022), CodeForces, and TACO (Li et al., 2023), alongside HumanEvalPlus (Liu et al., 2023) and LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2024). STEM reasoning is measured via GPQA Diamond (Rein et al., 2023), logical reasoning via Reasoning Gym (Stojanovski et al., 2025), and instruction following via IFEval (Bae et al., 2025). Cybersecurity evaluation uses CTI-Bench (Alam et al., 2024), CyberMetric (Tihanyi et al., 2024), and SecEval (Busch et al., 2014). All reasoning benchmarks test open-ended generation, not MCQ selection—the model must produce answers from scratch.

- **Base model(s).** Three model families are used across experiments. ProRL-1.5B-v2 (Hu et al., 2025b) is the starting point for data-saturation experiments—it was originally trained from R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (Guo et al., 2025b) using the ProRL recipe for over 20,000 H100 GPU hours and had reached performance saturation. Qwen3-4B-Instruct (Team, 2025) is used for the stronger-model saturation experiments and as the base for GooseReason-4B-Instruct. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct serves as the base for the cybersecurity domain comparison (Llama-Primus-Instruct and Llama-Primus-Merged baselines from Yu et al., 2025). The paper also reports Qwen3-30B-Instruct as a reference point for a ~7.5× larger model.

- **Metrics.** All reasoning benchmarks report pass@1 accuracy—the fraction of test instances where the model's single generated answer matches the ground truth. For coding benchmarks, this means the generated code passes all test cases. For math, answers are parsed and compared numerically or via the standard MATH grading protocol. For cybersecurity benchmarks, answers are matched against the ground-truth option or explanation. The primary aggregate is the arithmetic mean across benchmarks within each domain (math average across 6 benchmarks, coding across 6, etc.).

- **Baselines.** The core baselines across all experiments are continued RL training with the original ProRL data blend (Liu et al., 2025a)—a 136K-example dataset spanning math, coding, logical reasoning, STEM, and instruction-following—versus joint training with GooseReason-0.7M added. For the smaller-model experiments (Section 3.1.1, ProRL-1.5B-v2 starting point), RLVE (Zeng et al., 2025a) is included as a procedural-generation baseline, using the publicly released checkpoint trained under equivalent computational budget. For cybersecurity (Section 3.2), baselines include the base Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama-Primus-Instruct (domain-specialized via extensive pre-training and post-training), and Llama-Primus-Merged (a merged variant). Qwen3-30B-Instruct is provided as an upper-bound reference in reasoning tables but is not directly compared—it is grayed out to indicate it serves as context, not as a target.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper uses two distinct accounting frameworks. For continued training experiments (Figures 2, 5; Tables 1-3), the budget is measured in RL training steps, where one step corresponds to one GRPO update using 16 rollouts per sampled prompt. Computational cost is reported in H100 GPU hours: ProRL-1.5B-v2 continued training runs for 1,100 H100 GPU hours (Figure 5), and Qwen-4B-Instruct experiments run for 270 additional steps beyond the 333-step ProRL saturation point (Tables 1-3). For compute-constrained experiments (Figure 6), training from scratch is capped at 200 steps to evaluate data efficiency under a fixed budget. There is no explicit measurement of synthesis cost for GooseReason-0.7M—the GPT-5 API calls used to generate the dataset are treated as a one-time upfront cost rather than amortized into training FLOPs.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No explicit cross-validation or statistical significance testing is reported. The paper evaluates on standard benchmark test sets (e.g., MATH test split, AIME 2024/2025 competition sets, LiveCodeBench's held-out problems) and reports aggregate averages. For the cybersecurity domain, evaluation follows the protocol of Yu et al. (2025) on CTI-Bench, CyberMetric, and SecEval. The absence of confidence intervals, error bars, or multiple-training-run averages means that small differences between methods (e.g., 0.43% vs. 2.24% gains in Table 1 coding averages) should be interpreted cautiously—they represent single-run outcomes whose variance across random seeds is unknown.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Continued RL Beyond Data Saturation: GooseReason-0.7M Revives Saturated Models

The central empirical claim is that GooseReason-0.7M enables continued RL training beyond the saturation point where existing RLVR data provides negligible or negative returns. This is tested across two model scales and starting points.

**Headline result for ProRL-1.5B-v2 (Figure 5):** Continued RL training for 1,100 H100 GPU hours on the original ProRL data blend yields marginal domain-average gains of 0.63% in math, 0.95% in coding, and 0.13% in STEM. Adding GooseReason-0.7M to the training mix produces gains of 2.71% in math, 2.12% in coding, and 3.48% in STEM. The STEM gain is the largest absolute improvement, and it is where the gap between GooseReason and continued ProRL data is widest (3.48% vs. 0.13%—a factor of ~27× difference in improvement rate). The breakdown figures (Figures 11-12 in Appendix B) show per-benchmark results: on AIME 2024, GooseReason improves from approximately 58% to beyond 63%, while continued ProRL data barely moves. On LiveCodeBench, GooseReason pushes from roughly 31% to beyond 35%, while continued ProRL data is essentially flat.

**Comparison to RLVE (Figure 5, rightmost bars):** RLVE achieves strong math gains (comparable to or exceeding GooseReason on some math benchmarks, per Figure 11 breakdown) but limited STEM gains (0.62% vs. GooseReason's 3.48%). This directly demonstrates the domain-coverage argument: RLVE's procedurally generated environments produce math and logic problems but cannot generate knowledge-intensive STEM tasks. On coding benchmarks (Figure 12), RLVE underperforms both continued ProRL data and GooseReason, suggesting its formal environments don't transfer well to code generation.

**Headline result for Qwen3-4B-Instruct (Figure 2, Tables 1-3):** This is the stronger-model experiment where data saturation is more severe. After 333 RL steps on ProRL data (which already produced substantial initial gains over the base Instruct model—e.g., math average improved from 68.21% to 71.65%), an additional 156 steps of continued ProRL training cause regression in math (70.36%, down from 71.65%), a marginal 0.43% coding gain, and a 1.52% STEM loss. In contrast, adding GooseReason-0.7M for 270 additional steps reverses all domains: math improves to 73.83% (+2.18% over the 333-step point), coding to 55.70% (+2.24%), and STEM to 66.79% (+2.40%).

The domain breakdown (Figures 8-10 in Appendix B) reveals the pattern:
- **Math (Figure 8):** The ProRL-only model degrades on AIME24 (from 66.46% to 62.29%), AIME25 (57.29% to 55.21%), and Olympiad Bench (68.24% to 67.19%). GooseReason reverses all three: AIME24 reaches 70.00%, AIME25 reaches 63.96%, Olympiad reaches 68.79%. MATH and AMC show small improvements in both conditions, but GooseReason's improvements are larger.
- **Coding (Figure 9):** ProRL-only shows mixed results—improvements on APPS (57.92% to 58.45%), CodeContests (52.55% to 52.88%), and CodeForces (51.67% to 54.47%), but regression on TACO (33.13% to 32.80%) and LiveCodeBench (41.28% to 40.56%). GooseReason improves all six coding benchmarks, with the largest deltas on APPS (60.48%, +2.56% over the ProRL 333-step point) and HumanEvalPlus (86.46%, +2.22%).
- **STEM and other (Figure 10):** GPQA Diamond degrades from 64.39% to 62.87% with ProRL-only, then recovers to 66.79% with GooseReason. IFEval is essentially flat across conditions (~76%), suggesting instruction-following is not sensitive to the choice of RLVR data. Reasoning Gym categories (Table 3) show small but consistent improvements with GooseReason: 81.28% average vs. 81.06% with ProRL-only, with the gains concentrated in Algorithmic (83.91% vs. 83.24%) and Logic (87.80% vs. 87.71%).

**Comparison to Qwen3-30B-Instruct (Tables 1-3, gray rows):** GooseReason-4B-Instruct achieves math average of 73.83% vs. Qwen3-30B-Instruct's 75.20%—a gap of only 1.37 percentage points despite being 7.5× smaller. On coding, GooseReason-4B-Instruct scores 55.70% vs. 30B's 50.94%, actually outperforming the larger model by 4.76 points. On Reasoning Gym, the 4B model scores 81.28% vs. 30B's 43.56%. The 30B model is not RL-trained, so this comparison is between RLVR-trained smaller models and instruction-tuned larger models—it demonstrates that RLVR can close or reverse the scale gap, but does not isolate the contribution of Golden Goose specifically (the 333-step ProRL-only model also outperforms the 30B on several benchmarks).

#### Scaling Behavior Analysis: Diverge, Outpace, Align Categories (Figure 7)

The paper introduces a qualitative taxonomy of how adding GooseReason affects scaling curves relative to ProRL-only continued training:

- **Diverge (regression vs. gain):** GPQA Diamond and most math benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, Minerva, Olympiad Bench) fall into this category. ProRL-only training causes performance to decrease, while GooseReason causes it to increase. This is the most impactful category—GooseReason is not just accelerating improvement, it is preventing degradation.
- **Outpace (faster gains):** Coding benchmarks primarily fall here (LiveCodeBench, HumanEvalPlus, APPS, CodeContests). Both conditions improve, but GooseReason improves faster.
- **Align (similar trends):** A few benchmarks show similar trajectories regardless of data source. IFEval is an example from Figure 10. MATH from Figure 8 also shows similar small improvements in both conditions.

This taxonomy is descriptive rather than explanatory—the paper does not analyze *why* particular benchmarks diverge versus outpace—but it provides a useful diagnostic for understanding where GooseReason's contribution is most essential (domains where the model would otherwise regress) versus merely accelerative.

#### Compute-Efficient Scaling from Scratch (Figure 6)

When training Qwen3-4B-Instruct from scratch for a fixed 200 RL steps, joint training with GooseReason-0.7M consistently achieves higher performance at every step count compared to ProRL data alone. The per-benchmark breakdowns (Figures 13-14):
- **Math (Figure 13):** At 200 steps, GooseReason reaches roughly 64% on AIME24 vs. ~59% with ProRL-only; ~46% on AIME25 vs. ~38%; ~94% on MATH vs. ~93%.
- **Coding (Figure 14):** At 200 steps, GooseReason reaches ~58% on APPS vs. ~55%; ~48% on CodeContests vs. ~44%; ~77% on HumanEvalPlus vs. ~75%.

The key takeaway is that GooseReason enables more *data-efficient* RL training: under a fixed compute budget, the model reaches higher performance because the training data contains more effective examples (higher fraction of tasks with mixed success/failure) from the start, rather than requiring the model to first saturate on the existing data before fresh tasks are needed.

#### Cybersecurity Domain: GooseReason-Cyber (Table 4)

Training Qwen3-4B-Instruct on 180K GooseReason-Cyber tasks for 100 RL steps yields the following across three cybersecurity benchmarks:

| Benchmark | Qwen3-4B-Instruct (base) | GooseReason-Cyber-4B | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| CTI-MCQ | 63.44% | 73.79% | +10.35 |
| CyberMetric | 89.78% | 92.05% | +2.27 |
| SecEval | 70.44% | 71.14% | +0.70 |
| **Average** | **74.55%** | **78.99%** | **+4.44** |

The previous state-of-the-art, Llama-Primus-Instruct (7B parameters, domain-specific pre-training and post-training), achieves only a 1.44% average gain over its base model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct). GooseReason-Cyber-4B surpasses it by 10.51 points in average (78.99% vs. 67.48% for Llama-Primus-Instruct and 67.93% for Llama-Primus-Merged), despite being roughly half the parameter count and not undergoing any domain-specific pre-training.

The improvement is largest on CTI-MCQ (+10.35 points), which tests threat-intelligence reasoning and vulnerability analysis—exactly the type of knowledge-intensive domain reasoning that traditional RLVR pipelines exclude. CyberMetric (+2.27 points) and SecEval (+0.70 points) show smaller gains, which may reflect ceiling effects (CyberMetric base performance is already 89.78%) or the distribution of GooseReason-Cyber's source material (FineWeb cybersecurity discussions may emphasize threat analysis over compliance checking or foundational security concepts).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Multiple-choice vs. open-ended fill-in-the-middle (Figure 4):** The open-ended formulation—where the model must generate the masked content rather than select from options—fails catastrophically for ProRL-1.5B-v2, with "over 83% of examples in the open-ended version of GooseReason-Math result in consistent zero accuracy." The paper attributes this to instruction-following degradation: RL-trained models ignore the infill instruction and "solve the problem from scratch," producing full solutions rather than the requested completions. This is a critical negative result that justifies the MCQ design choice and reveals a broader issue with RLVR-trained models' format adherence.

**Number of distractors (Figure 4):** The difficulty distribution of GooseReason-Math tasks shifts dramatically with the number of options:
- With 3 choices: the majority of tasks are easy (model consistently succeeds), making them ineffective for RL training because the elimination strategy—identifying flaws in the two distractors—requires no genuine reasoning about the masked content.
- With 6 choices: the distribution shifts toward medium difficulty but still retains a substantial easy fraction.
- With 9 choices: over 70% of problems fall into the medium-difficulty regime with both successful and failed rollouts. This is the calibration used for GooseReason-0.7M. The mechanism is that 8 distractors make the elimination strategy computationally expensive given the model's output length budget, forcing the model to reason about the masked content directly.

This ablation functions as a difficulty-tuning validation: the optimal distractor count is the one that maximizes the density of training examples in the "effective" regime for the target model.

**Data saturation measurement (Figure 3):** The paper quantifies the staleness of existing RLVR data by measuring the fraction of "effective" examples relative to ProRL-1.5B-v2. Only ~25% of the 136K ProRL dataset samples remain effective for this model—they have both successful and failed rollouts. The remaining ~75% are stale: the model either always succeeds (~30% of the dataset) or always fails (~45%). GooseReason-0.7M retains ~70% effectiveness, providing a 13× increase in total effective examples over the effective subset of ProRL data (roughly 450K effective GooseReason examples vs. ~34K effective ProRL examples).

This is not an ablation in the traditional sense—it's a measurement rather than a controlled manipulation—but it serves the same diagnostic function: it quantifies why continued ProRL training fails (75% of the data provides zero gradient) and why GooseReason succeeds (70% of the data provides gradient).

**RLVE comparison (Figure 5, Figures 11-12):** The comparison against RLVE functions as a procedural-generation baseline ablation. RLVE is highly effective on math (matching or exceeding GooseReason on some math benchmarks) but limited on STEM (0.62% vs. 3.48%) and coding (underperforms continued ProRL data). This isolates the contribution of Golden Goose's specific data source strategy—leveraging unverifiable reasoning text—from the general benefit of adding any new training data.

**Model scale interaction with saturation (Figure 2 vs. Figure 5):** The paper implicitly ablates model scale by testing the same ProRL recipe on two model sizes. ProRL-1.5B-v2 (starting from R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B) sustains gains for over 2,000 steps on the ProRL recipe, while Qwen3-4B-Instruct plateaus or regresses after 300 steps. This demonstrates that data saturation is not a fixed property of the dataset but an interaction between dataset difficulty and model capability—stronger models exhaust training signal faster. GooseReason's effectiveness on both model scales (1.5B and 4B) provides evidence that the approach generalizes across capability levels.

**Cybersecurity domain transfer (Table 4):** The cybersecurity experiment is effectively an out-of-domain robustness check. GooseReason-Cyber is synthesized from raw FineWeb scrapes using a modified pipeline with an additional extraction step (to handle noisy source text) and difficulty filtering (to remove easy problems). The 4.44% absolute gain across three benchmarks validates that the Golden Goose pipeline generalizes to specialized domains with no prior RLVR infrastructure and noisy, uncurated source text. The fact that a 100-step RL training run produces a model that surpasses a 7B domain-specialized model with extensive pre-training and post-training (Llama-Primus-Instruct) is the strongest evidence for the pipeline's practical value.

**Benchmark domain coverage (Tables 1-3 vs. training data sources):** The evaluation benchmarks intentionally target open-ended generation rather than MCQ fill-in-the-middle, testing whether the skills acquired through Golden Goose training transfer beyond the training format. The consistent improvements across math, coding, STEM, and logical reasoning—all evaluated with open-ended answer formats—serve as an implicit ablation of format specificity: if the gains were merely teaching MCQ test-taking strategies, open-ended benchmark performance would not improve. The fact that Reasoning Gym (logical puzzles not directly covered by GooseReason source corpora) also improves (Table 3: 81.28% vs. 81.06%) suggests genuine reasoning transfer rather than format-specific learning.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: GooseReason-0.7M provides a 13× increase in effective RLVR examples over the saturated ProRL dataset.

The 13× figure comes from Figure 3: GooseReason retains ~70% effectiveness on ~500K+ examples (~350K effective examples, though the paper says "over 450,000"), while the ProRL dataset retains ~25% effectiveness on 136K examples (~34K effective). The math internally checks out. However, the "effective example" metric is defined relative to a specific model (ProRL-1.5B-v2) sampled with 16 rollouts per task, and the paper does not report what fraction of GooseReason tasks would be effective for a stronger model (e.g., Qwen3-4B-Instruct). Given the paper's own finding that stronger models saturate faster, it is likely that GooseReason's effectiveness ratio would drop for Qwen3-4B-Instruct—perhaps substantially. The 13× figure is therefore a point estimate at a specific capability level, not a general property of the dataset.

Additionally, the comparison is between a dataset that has already been used for training (ProRL data, on which ProRL-1.5B-v2 has already trained extensively) and a dataset that has not (GooseReason-0.7M, freshly synthesized). This is exactly the point—GooseReason provides fresh data—but it means the 13× figure conflates two effects: the inherent "effectiveness density" of the synthesis method, and the fact that the ProRL dataset is stale specifically because the model has already trained on it. A fairer comparison would measure effectiveness of both datasets on a model that has trained on neither (a base instruct model), which would tell us whether GooseReason tasks are inherently more "effective-dense" or simply novel. The paper does not report this.

#### Claim 2: GooseReason-0.7M "revives saturated models" and produces "robust, sustained gains under continuous RL."

The evidence for revival is strong but qualified. On ProRL-1.5B-v2 (Figure 5), GooseReason produces clear improvements across all three domain aggregates versus continued ProRL data, which yields near-zero gains. On Qwen3-4B-Instruct (Figure 2, Tables 1-3), the revival pattern is even starker: ProRL-only continued training causes *regression* in math (-1.29%) and STEM (-1.52%), while GooseReason produces gains (+2.18% and +2.40%, respectively). The coding domain is a partial exception: ProRL-only training still produces a small coding gain (+0.43% from the 333-step point to the 489-step point), so the "revival" is more about acceleration than reversal on coding benchmarks.

The claim of "sustained gains" is based on training curves that extend for 270 additional steps beyond the 333-step saturation point (Figure 2, Tables 1-3). The paper shows training curves that are still rising at the endpoint, suggesting further gains are possible, but does not demonstrate what happens at 500+ additional steps. It is possible that GooseReason data, too, would eventually saturate—the paper provides no evidence about the asymptotic behavior. The "sustained" qualifier is supported for the training budget studied but not established as a general property.

A critical nuance: the Qwen3-4B-Instruct experiments compare "ProRL data for 333 steps, then 156 more steps of ProRL data" against "ProRL data for 333 steps, then 270 more steps of joint ProRL+GooseReason data" (Tables 1-3). The GooseReason condition receives more total training steps (333 + 270 = 603) than the ProRL-only condition (333 + 156 = 489), which means part of the gain could be attributable to longer training rather than better data. The paper does not equalize total steps between conditions. The Figure 6 experiment (training from scratch for exactly 200 steps in both conditions) addresses this for the compute-constrained setting and shows GooseReason advantage, but the continued-training experiments lack this control.

#### Claim 3: Data saturation occurs earlier and is more severe with stronger LLMs.

The evidence compares ProRL-1.5B-v2 (saturates after 2,000+ steps) with Qwen3-4B-Instruct (saturates after 300 steps). This is a single comparison between two models that differ in scale, architecture (R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B vs. Qwen3-4B-Instruct), and training history. The claim would be stronger with at least one intermediate model scale (e.g., a 3B variant) to demonstrate a monotonic relationship between capability and saturation speed. As reported, the finding is suggestive—stronger models do appear to saturate faster—but could be confounded by architectural differences or the specific data mixture used. The paper's explanation ("a stronger model more quickly reduces the fraction of problems on which it exhibits mixed success and failure") is plausible but not rigorously isolated from alternative explanations (e.g., maybe the ProRL data blend is better calibrated to 1.5B-scale models, or Qwen3-4B-Instruct's instruction tuning creates different learning dynamics).

#### Claim 4: GooseReason enables more compute-efficient RL scaling (Figure 6).

This claim is supported for the specific setting tested: training Qwen3-4B-Instruct from scratch for 200 steps. At every step count, joint training with GooseReason produces higher benchmark performance than ProRL data alone. However, the experiment does not control for total data volume—GooseReason adds 0.7M tasks to whatever the ProRL blend contains, which means the joint training condition simply has more total training data. The "efficiency" claim would be stronger if the comparison equalized total examples (e.g., 200 steps of ProRL-only with a larger per-step batch vs. 200 steps of joint training with the same total number of training examples). As reported, the result shows that GooseReason data is *useful*, but does not isolate whether the benefit comes from data diversity (GooseReason tasks are qualitatively different) or from data volume (more total tasks leads to more training signal).

#### Claim 5: GooseReason-Cyber establishes new state-of-the-art for cybersecurity LLMs.

The comparison in Table 4 is between a 4B model (GooseReason-Cyber-4B, based on Qwen3-4B-Instruct) and 8B models (Llama-Primus-Instruct, based on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct). This is a cross-family, cross-scale comparison: Qwen3 vs. Llama 3.1, 4B vs. 8B, RLVR-only post-training vs. domain-specific pre-training + post-training. The paper cannot disentangle whether the SOTA results come from Golden Goose's data, from Qwen3 being a stronger base model than Llama 3.1, or from RLVR being inherently more effective than the continued pre-training + SFT approach used by Primus. The base model baselines are partially informative: Qwen3-4B-Instruct scores 74.55% average vs. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct's 66.49%, meaning the Qwen3 base already has a 8.06-point advantage before any cybersecurity training. GooseReason-Cyber adds 4.44 points on top of this, while Primus training adds 1.44 points on top of the Llama base. The SOTA claim is technically correct, but the contribution of Golden Goose specifically—versus the contribution of starting from a stronger base model—cannot be isolated from these numbers.

A fairer comparison would be GooseReason-Cyber applied to Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (the same base as Primus), or Primus training applied to Qwen3-4B-Instruct. Neither experiment is reported.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Strengthen the Paper

**Synthesis model ablation:** All GooseReason tasks are synthesized using GPT-5. The paper does not report what happens if a weaker model (GPT-4, Claude, Llama-70B) is used for synthesis, or if open-weight models could be used. Given that the pipeline's scalability argument depends on not requiring expensive human expertise, the dependence on a frontier proprietary model for synthesis is a practical limitation. An ablation showing that the approach degrades gracefully with weaker synthesis models, or that the tasks remain useful even with higher noise, would substantially strengthen the scalability claim.

**Distractor count sweep beyond 9:** Figure 4 shows a trend toward higher difficulty with more distractors. It is possible that 12 or 15 distractors would be even better for stronger models (like Qwen3-4B-Instruct), or that diminishing returns set in. The paper only tests 3, 6, and 9, leaving the optimal count for the 4B model uncharacterized.

**Effectiveness ratio for different model scales:** The paper reports effectiveness of GooseReason-0.7M relative to ProRL-1.5B-v2 (~70%). It does not report effectiveness for Qwen3-4B-Instruct at any point during training. This is a gap because the core argument—that Golden Goose provides fresh training signal beyond saturation—depends on the dataset remaining partially effective for the target model. If Qwen3-4B-Instruct quickly saturates on GooseReason tasks too (e.g., effectiveness drops to 30% after 500 steps), the gains would not be sustainable.

**Training curve endpoints:** The Qwen3-4B-Instruct continued training in Figure 2 and Tables 1-3 stops at 270 additional steps. The paper shows increasing trends but does not demonstrate what happens at 500 or 1000 additional steps. It is unknown whether GooseReason data itself eventually saturates, or whether the model would continue improving if given more GooseReason data.

**Domain-specific data contamination analysis:** GooseReason-0.7M is synthesized from AoPS-Instruct, rStar-Coder, and MegaScience—source corpora that may overlap with the training data of the base models. If Qwen3-4B-Instruct was trained on AoPS content or similar Olympiad math problems, the "freshness" of GooseReason tasks could be overstated. The paper does not discuss or measure potential contamination between the source corpora and the base models' pretraining data.

**Direct human evaluation of synthesis quality:** The paper relies entirely on downstream RL training performance as a quality signal for the synthesized tasks. There is no human evaluation of whether the distractors are genuinely plausible, whether the masked spans are genuinely reasoning-critical (rather than definitional or trivially inferable), or whether some synthesized tasks contain errors (incorrect ground-truth spans, distractors that are accidentally correct). A human quality audit on even a small sample (e.g., 100 tasks) would substantially strengthen confidence in the synthesis pipeline.

**Statistical significance:** None of the benchmark comparisons report confidence intervals, standard errors, or multiple-run averages. With test sets of varying sizes (MATH: 5000 problems, AIME: 30 problems per year, GPQA Diamond: 198 problems), the variance of the point estimates differs substantially. The 0.43% vs. 2.24% coding gain comparison on Qwen3-4B-Instruct (Tables 1-3) could be meaningfully different or could be within the noise of the evaluation protocol—the paper provides no basis for distinguishing these cases.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Dependence on a Frontier Proprietary Model for Synthesis

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire Golden Goose pipeline depends on a strong LLM to perform the MCQ construction—identifying reasoning-critical spans, generating plausible distractors, and producing structured JSON output. The paper states explicitly that it "used the strongest LLM available at the time of the experiment, GPT-5, for the synthesis pipeline" and that for reasoning-dense source text, "the questions constructed by GPT-5 were of sufficient quality and difficulty to require no further post-processing" (Section 2.1). No ablation is performed with weaker or open-weight synthesis models.

**The consequence.** This creates a practical dependency on a proprietary, closed-source model whose availability, cost, and behavior may change unpredictably. If GPT-5 (or equivalent) becomes unavailable, expensive, or is updated in ways that alter synthesis quality, Golden Goose's pipeline cannot be replicated or sustained by the broader research community. More fundamentally, the paper's framing—"a simple trick to synthesize unlimited RLVR tasks from unverifiable internet text"—implies accessibility, but the synthesis step outsources the actual intelligence required to a model that costs money to query, has unknown failure modes, and cannot be inspected. The approach is not self-bootstrapping: you need a model at least as capable as the target student model to synthesize useful training data for it, which may create a chicken-and-egg problem for domains where no sufficiently capable synthesis model exists.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides zero evidence about how synthesis quality degrades with weaker models. There is no ablation comparing GPT-5 to GPT-4, Claude, Gemma, or any open-weight model. The cybersecurity domain uses the same GPT-5 pipeline, so there is no evidence that the extraction-plus-synthesis approach works with cheaper or open models either. The paper's only quality diagnostic is downstream RL performance—there is no human evaluation of distractor plausibility, masked-span appropriateness, or task correctness on even a sample of synthesized examples.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the synthesis model dependency as a limitation or propose alternatives for researchers without GPT-5 access. This is a notable omission given that the cybersecurity case study is presented as evidence that Golden Goose can democratize RLVR data creation for arbitrary specialized domains—yet domain experts without access to frontier proprietary models would be unable to replicate the pipeline.

---

### Synthesis Cost Is Unaccounted for in Efficiency Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures RL training efficiency in terms of H100 GPU hours for the RL step (e.g., "1,100 H100 GPU hours" for continued ProRL-1.5B-v2 training, "100 RL steps" for cybersecurity), but the cost of generating GooseReason-0.7M—querying GPT-5 to synthesize 0.7 million MCQs from three source corpora, plus an additional 180K from FineWeb for cybersecurity—is treated as a one-time upfront investment that is not amortized into any of the reported compute budgets. Section 2.1 mentions this only implicitly: "we used the strongest LLM available at the time of the experiment, GPT-5, for the synthesis pipeline," with no discussion of API cost, token consumption, or synthesis time.

**The consequence.** The headline efficiency numbers—4× improvement over best-of-N baselines, 4.44% gain in 100 RL steps for cybersecurity—are understated in terms of total resource consumption. A practitioner evaluating whether to adopt Golden Goose needs to know: how many GPT-5 API calls does it take to synthesize 0.7M tasks? What is the dollar cost? How does this compare to alternative data acquisition strategies (hiring domain experts, licensing existing datasets, using procedural generators)? If synthesis costs dominate the total budget, the claimed efficiency gains over existing RLVR data pipelines may disappear when total cost of ownership is considered. The cybersecurity case study is particularly vulnerable to this critique: the paper presents 100 RL steps as remarkably efficient, but the 180K cybersecurity MCQs were synthesized from noisy FineWeb scrapes requiring an additional extraction step, making the per-task synthesis cost potentially higher than for clean reasoning corpora.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper provides no measurements of synthesis cost, API call counts, token consumption, or total wall-clock time for data generation. The absence is acknowledged only through silence—there is no section discussing synthesis overhead, no table comparing data acquisition costs across methods, and no amortized efficiency metric.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's framing (Golden Goose is "a simple trick") and the emphasis on scalability ("unlimited RLVR tasks") implicitly suggest the synthesis step is cheap or at least cheaper than alternatives, but no evidence supports this. A footnote or appendix section estimating the synthesis cost for GooseReason-0.7M and GooseReason-Cyber would substantially strengthen the practical deployment argument.

---

### The MCQ Training Format May Teach Recognition Rather Than Generation

**The assumption or constraint.** Golden Goose trains models exclusively on multiple-choice fill-in-the-middle tasks where the model selects the correct continuation from a fixed set of options. The core assumption is that the reasoning skills acquired through this discrimination task will transfer to open-ended generation—the model will learn to *produce* correct reasoning, not merely to *recognize* it among alternatives. The paper acknowledges this concern implicitly by evaluating entirely on open-ended benchmarks (AIME, MATH, HumanEvalPlus, GPQA Diamond, etc.), but does not test for format-specific artifacts or analyze *how* transfer occurs.

**The consequence.** A model trained extensively on MCQ discrimination may develop strategies that exploit the format rather than the underlying reasoning. The paper's own distractor-count ablation (Figure 4) demonstrates one such strategy: with 3 choices, the model uses an elimination approach—identifying superficial flaws in two distractors and selecting the third—rather than reasoning about the masked content from first principles. With 9 choices, the elimination strategy becomes computationally expensive and the model is forced toward genuine reasoning, but this only shifts the threshold, not the underlying concern. There may be subtler format-specific heuristics—option length patterns, keyword matching between the masked passage and distractors, syntactic cues in distractor construction—that the model could exploit to achieve high MCQ accuracy without developing transferable reasoning capabilities. The paper's positive open-ended benchmark results argue against severe exploitation, but the mechanism of transfer is unanalyzed: we do not know whether the model is genuinely learning the domain principles tested by the masked spans or learning a more general "reason about missing information in context" capability.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The open-ended benchmark results (Tables 1-3, Figure 5) are the primary evidence for transfer: GooseReason-trained models improve on AIME, MATH, HumanEvalPlus, and GPQA Diamond, all of which require open-ended generation. The Reasoning Gym improvements (Table 3) on logical puzzles not directly covered by GooseReason source corpora provide additional circumstantial evidence for generalizable skill acquisition. However, the paper provides no mechanistic analysis—no probing of internal representations, no comparison of attention patterns between MCQ and open-ended tasks, no analysis of whether the model's generated solutions on open-ended benchmarks qualitatively resemble the reasoning patterns found in the MCQ distractors. The claim of transfer rests entirely on benchmark scores, not on understanding of how or why transfer occurs.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through benchmark selection. By evaluating exclusively on open-ended benchmarks, the paper demonstrates that the trained model's capabilities extend beyond the MCQ format. However, the paper does not establish *how far* this transfer extends or whether it has an asymptotic limit. Could a model reach 90% MCQ accuracy on a domain while plateauing at 50% open-ended accuracy because the remaining gap requires generative reasoning that MCQ training cannot provide? The paper's training curves (Figures 2, 5, 6) end before any obvious plateau in open-ended performance, so this question remains open.

---

### The Method Produces Zero Gains on Problems Beyond the Base Model's Capability Range

**The assumption or constraint.** Golden Goose synthesizes verifiable tasks from existing reasoning text. This means the training tasks can only test reasoning that is already present in the source corpora—the pipeline extracts and reformats existing knowledge, it does not create new problems or reasoning patterns de novo. More critically, the MCQ format requires that the source text contain a *correct* reasoning span to serve as the ground-truth option. The paper implicitly assumes that the source corpora contain reasoning that is within the capability range of the target model—challenging enough to provide learning signal, but not so advanced that the model cannot engage with it at all.

**The consequence.** For genuinely novel or out-of-distribution reasoning—the kind that would require new conceptual breakthroughs or the integration of knowledge from disparate domains—Golden Goose provides no training signal because such reasoning is not present in existing internet text in a form that can be masked and verified. More practically, for problems the base model fundamentally cannot solve (the equivalent of difficulty bin 5 in the MATH benchmark analysis of Snell et al.), Golden Goose's effectiveness ratio would drop to near zero: if the model's rollouts are all failures regardless of distractor count, the tasks provide no learning signal. The paper does not measure this effect, but it follows from the same logic that drives the "effective example" metric—a task where the model always fails is as useless for RL as one where it always succeeds.

This limitation is partially masked by the paper's experimental design: all experiments start from already-capable models (ProRL-1.5B-v2, Qwen3-4B-Instruct) that have non-trivial performance on the target benchmarks. Golden Goose amplifies existing capability by providing fresh training signal in domains where the model has partial competence, but there is no evidence it can *create* capability from scratch. If you applied Golden Goose to a domain where the base model has near-zero competency—say, generating proofs in a specialized area of algebraic topology where no training text exists—the synthesized MCQs would likely have near-zero effectiveness and produce no improvement.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not directly test this boundary condition, but the data saturation measurements (Figure 3) provide indirect evidence. GooseReason-0.7M retains ~70% effectiveness for ProRL-1.5B-v2, meaning ~30% of synthesized tasks are already stale (either too easy or too hard) even for a model that has never trained on them. Some fraction of that 30% is likely in the "always fails" category—problems whose reasoning is genuinely beyond the model's current capability. For a weaker base model, the stale fraction would be higher. The paper does not report effectiveness ratios for Qwen3-4B-Instruct on GooseReason data, so we do not know how the stale fraction scales with model capability in the other direction either.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the lower bound of base model capability required for Golden Goose to be effective, nor does it provide guidance on when a practitioner should expect the approach to fail. The difficulty filtering mechanism (Section 2.1) removes trivially easy tasks but does not address the problem of tasks that are fundamentally too hard—those would be filtered too, but replacing them requires source text at an appropriate difficulty level, which may not exist for cutting-edge or highly specialized domains.

---

### Single Model Family and Single Synthesis Model Limit Generalization Evidence

**The assumption or constraint.** All RL training experiments use models from the Qwen family (R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B for the 1.5B experiments, Qwen3-4B-Instruct for the 4B experiments). All MCQ synthesis uses GPT-5. The cybersecurity baselines use Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and its domain-specialized variants, but Golden Goose itself is not applied to a Llama base model in that domain—the cybersecurity experiment trains only Qwen3-4B-Instruct and compares against Llama-based baselines from prior work. The paper contains no cross-family experiments where Golden Goose is applied to, say, a Llama, Gemma, or DeepSeek base model to verify that the gains are not specific to Qwen architectures, Qwen tokenizers, or Qwen pre-training data distributions.

**The consequence.** The paper's central claim—that Golden Goose is a general-purpose data synthesis pipeline—rests on evidence from a single model family for RL training and a single model for synthesis. This creates several uncertainty vectors:

1. **Base model sensitivity**: Qwen models may have particular properties—instruction-following behavior, in-context learning capability, pre-training data distribution—that make them especially amenable to MCQ fill-in-the-middle training. The instruction-following degradation finding (Figure 4, where ProRL-1.5B-v2 fails on 83% of open-ended fill-in-the-middle tasks) might be Qwen-specific: a model family with different instruction-tuning characteristics might not exhibit the same collapse, potentially making the open-ended format viable and rendering the MCQ format design choice less consequential.

2. **Synthesis model sensitivity**: GPT-5's distractor generation might be calibrated to produce distractors that are discriminable by GPT-5 but not by other model families, or conversely, might produce distractors that are inadvertently easier for Qwen models due to shared pre-training biases. Without testing synthesis quality across model families, the pipeline's robustness to synthesis-model choice is unknown.

3. **Pre-training data overlap**: If Qwen models were pre-trained on AoPS-Instruct, rStar-Coder, or MegaScience data, the "freshness" of GooseReason tasks could be overstated—the model might benefit from recognition of familiar content patterns rather than from genuinely novel reasoning challenges. The paper does not discuss or measure contamination between the source corpora and the base models' pre-training data.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The cybersecurity domain results (Table 4) provide partial cross-family evidence: the evaluation is on Llama-based baselines, and the Golden Goose-trained Qwen model outperforms them. However, this is a cross-family *comparison*, not a cross-family *ablation*—it shows that a Qwen model with Golden Goose training beats Llama models without it, but does not show that applying Golden Goose to a Llama model would produce similar relative gains over the Llama base. The paper also notes that the Qwen3-4B-Instruct base already outperforms Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct by 8.06 points on cybersecurity benchmarks before any Golden Goose training, so the SOTA claim conflates base model quality with Golden Goose effectiveness.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the single-family limitation, propose cross-family experiments, or discuss the implications for practitioners who may need to apply Golden Goose to non-Qwen models. Given the paper's emphasis on generality ("any RL algorithm applicable to RLVR," "any text corpus"), the absence of model-family diversity in the experiments is a significant gap.

### No Evidence That Gains Are Sustained Beyond the Reported Training Horizon

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper demonstrates that adding GooseReason-0.7M to RL training produces gains over continued training with the original ProRL data for the specific training budgets studied: +1,100 H100 GPU hours for ProRL-1.5B-v2 (Figure 5), +270 additional RL steps for Qwen3-4B-Instruct (Tables 1-3), and 200 steps from scratch (Figure 6). The training curves in Figure 2 and Figure 6 are still rising at their endpoints, which the paper interprets as evidence of "sustained gains." However, the paper does not show what happens at longer training horizons: 500 additional steps, 1,000 steps, or until a new saturation point is reached.

**The consequence.** The "unlimited RLVR data" framing implies that Golden Goose can fuel indefinite RL scaling—synthesize more data, train longer, get more gains. But if GooseReason data itself saturates (the model eventually masters the discrimination patterns in AoPS, rStar-Coder, and MegaScience tasks, causing the effectiveness ratio to drop), then the gains will plateau, and the pipeline will require new source corpora to continue scaling. The paper provides no evidence about the *saturation rate* of GooseReason data—whether the ~70% effectiveness for ProRL-1.5B-v2 drops to 50%, 30%, or 10% after extended training, and how quickly. Without this information, a practitioner cannot estimate how much GooseReason data they need to synthesize to achieve a target performance level, or whether they will need to continuously source new unverifiable text to prevent stagnation.

The Qwen3-4B-Instruct experiment is particularly vulnerable to this critique: the paper shows that this stronger model saturates on ProRL data in 300 steps, but then trains it on GooseReason data for only 270 additional steps without measuring whether a second saturation point is approaching. Given the paper's own finding that stronger models saturate faster, it is plausible that Qwen3-4B-Instruct would saturate on GooseReason data in, say, 500-600 steps—meaning the reported gains represent a one-time boost from fresh data rather than a sustainable scaling trajectory.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The training curves end at the reported budgets. The effectiveness measurement (Figure 3) is a point estimate for ProRL-1.5B-v2 *before* extended GooseReason training—it measures data freshness relative to a model that has not yet trained on GooseReason data. There is no measurement of how effectiveness evolves during GooseReason training. The paper does not discuss the expected saturation rate of synthetic MCQ data, propose methods to estimate it, or provide guidance on how much source text is needed to sustain RL training for a target number of steps.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's conclusion states that Golden Goose "highlights the potential of automatically re-utilizing reasoning-rich unverifiable internet text to enable RL scaling" and envisions extending the paradigm to "other high-value disciplines such as law and medicine," but does not discuss whether those extensions would require continuously sourcing *new* unverifiable text as the model improves or whether the existing source corpora can support extended training. This is a practical concern for anyone planning to deploy Golden Goose at scale: is 0.7M tasks from three source corpora sufficient for indefinite scaling, or does the pipeline require a growing corpus of unverifiable text to prevent a second saturation event?

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

Golden Goose introduces a **format-level verification paradigm** that fundamentally reframes how the field should think about RLVR data construction. Prior to this work, the implicit assumption governing RLVR data pipelines was that training tasks must be derived from content whose correctness is independently machine-checkable—math problems that evaluate to a number, code that passes unit tests, game states with deterministic win conditions. This assumption created a hard boundary around which domains and reasoning types could benefit from RL training, systematically excluding the vast majority of reasoning-rich text on the internet: proofs, scientific explanations, code walkthroughs without test cases, security analyses, medical diagnoses, and every other form of multi-step reasoning that cannot be reduced to expression evaluation or unit testing.

Golden Goose breaks this boundary not by improving verification technology—not by building better math verifiers or more sophisticated test-case synthesizers—but by **changing where verification happens in the pipeline.** The verification burden shifts from the content layer (is this explanation correct?) to the format layer (did the model select the option matching the original author's text?). The source text remains unverifiable in the traditional sense, but the training task becomes trivially verifiable because the output space is constrained to a fixed set of discrete options. This is a genuine methodological shift, not merely a new dataset: it redefines what counts as "verifiable data" from a property of the underlying reasoning to a property of the task interface.

The magnitude of this shift becomes apparent when considering what it unlocks. The cybersecurity case study (Section 3.2) is the cleanest proof of concept: a domain with zero prior open-source RLVR data, no procedural generators, and no human-curated training sets, bootstrapped from raw web scrapes, producing a 4.44% absolute gain in 100 RL steps and surpassing a 7B domain-specialized model that underwent extensive domain-specific pre-training and post-training. Under any prior RLVR paradigm, this experiment would be impossible—you cannot write a math verifier for threat intelligence analysis, cannot hand-code a procedural environment for vulnerability assessment, and cannot hire domain experts to author 180K verifiable cybersecurity problems. Only format-level verification makes this feasible, because it does not require the data engineer to understand the domain at all—only to have a synthesis model capable of identifying reasoning-critical spans and generating plausible distractors.

This paper also **reconciles a growing tension in the RL scaling literature.** Multiple recent efforts (ProRL, BroRL, ScaleRL) have focused on algorithmic improvements to sustain RL training for longer steps or larger rollout budgets, operating under the implicit assumption that the bottleneck is optimization efficiency—that better advantage normalization, broader exploration, or dynamic sampling ratios can extract more signal from a fixed dataset. Golden Goose provides direct evidence that this assumption is false in the regime where models have saturated on their training data. Figure 5 shows that continued ProRL-1.5B-v2 training on the original ProRL data yields near-zero STEM gain (0.13%) over 1,100 H100 GPU hours, while adding GooseReason-0.7M produces 3.48%—a factor of ~27× difference in improvement rate. The algorithmic recipe is identical in both conditions; the difference is entirely in the data. This reframes the RL scaling conversation from "how do we optimize better?" to "how do we generate fresh training signal?" and positions data synthesis, not algorithmic engineering, as the critical path for the next generation of reasoning models.

The paper's "effective example" diagnostic (Figure 3) provides a **quantitative language for data saturation** that the field previously lacked. By operationalizing staleness as the fraction of training tasks where the model's rollouts are either all-success or all-failure (providing zero policy gradient), the paper gives researchers a concrete metric for diagnosing when continued training on a fixed dataset has reached diminishing returns, and for measuring how much learning potential a new dataset adds. The finding that only ~25% of the 136K ProRL samples remain effective for ProRL-1.5B-v2—while GooseReason-0.7M retains ~70% effectiveness—explains the performance gap between the two conditions more directly than any algorithmic analysis could. The paper also demonstrates that this metric tracks model capability: stronger models saturate faster because they more quickly reduce the fraction of tasks on which they exhibit mixed success and failure. This creates a feedback dynamic where data needs grow with model capability, and the effective-example metric provides the diagnostic tool for measuring whether a given data pipeline can keep pace.

The paper's comparison of open-ended versus MCQ fill-in-the-middle tasks (Figure 4) reveals a **hidden cost of RLVR training** that has significant implications for how the field designs training pipelines. The finding that ProRL-1.5B-v2 fails on 83% of open-ended fill-in-the-middle tasks—not because the model lacks reasoning capability, but because RL training has caused instruction-following to collapse toward the model's training distribution of full-solution generation—suggests that RLVR optimization can produce non-monotonic capability changes: reasoning improves while format adherence degrades. This has implications beyond Golden Goose's design choices. It suggests that RLVR-trained models may require careful prompt engineering to elicit their capabilities on atypical task formats, and that the community should either include diverse task formats in the training mix to preserve behavioral flexibility or develop RL objectives that explicitly reward format adherence. It also explains why prior work on self-correction and iterative refinement found limited success—the models had been optimized for single-pass solution generation, not for the metacognitive task of revising their own outputs.

Finally, Golden Goose shifts research priorities by demonstrating that **data diversity, not just data volume, is the active ingredient in RL scaling.** The domain-differential pattern in Figure 5—with STEM showing the largest gain (3.48%), where existing RLVR data is scarcest, and math and coding showing smaller gains, where existing data is already abundant—indicates that the benefit comes from expanding into qualitatively different reasoning patterns (theorem-proving, multi-step scientific explanations, algorithmic code analysis without unit tests) rather than from adding more examples to already-covered domains. The comparison with RLVE reinforces this: RLVE's procedural generators can produce infinite math problems but achieve only 0.62% STEM gain because procedurally generated environments don't encode domain-specific scientific knowledge. This shifts the RLVR data scaling paradigm from "more data from known distributions" to "coverage of previously excluded reasoning types," where the limiting factor is not total data volume but the diversity of text formats that can be operationalized as verifiable tasks.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Synthesis model quality ablation and open-weight pipeline construction.** The paper's complete dependence on GPT-5 for MCQ synthesis is the single largest barrier to reproducibility and community adoption. A concrete follow-up would systematically evaluate synthesis quality as a function of the synthesis model's capability: generate GooseReason-style MCQs from a fixed set of source passages using GPT-5, GPT-4, Claude Opus, Llama-3-70B, and Qwen-72B, then measure (a) human-evaluated distractor plausibility and masked-span appropriateness on a sample of ~500 tasks per model, and (b) downstream RL training effectiveness when each synthesized dataset is used to continue training a saturated model. The key question is whether there is a sharp capability threshold below which synthesis quality collapses (producing distractors that are trivially distinguishable or masked spans that don't require reasoning), or whether quality degrades gracefully with synthesis model capability. If open-weight models can produce usable tasks, Golden Goose becomes genuinely accessible; if only frontier proprietary models work, the approach is effectively limited to well-resourced industry labs. A companion negative-result study would test whether models can synthesize training data for themselves—can Qwen-4B-Instruct synthesize GooseReason-style MCQs that are effective for training Qwen-4B-Instruct? The "instruction-following degradation" finding (Figure 4) suggests this might be challenging, but the result would be informative either way: success implies self-bootstrapping is possible, failure identifies a fundamental limitation of the format-transformation approach.

**Measuring and predicting the saturation rate of synthesized data.** The paper shows that GooseReason-0.7M is ~70% effective for ProRL-1.5B-v2 before training, but provides no information about how quickly this effectiveness decays during continued RL training. A critical follow-up would track the effective-example ratio of GooseReason data at regular intervals (every 50 RL steps) throughout extended training of Qwen-4B-Instruct, continuing until the ratio drops below a threshold (say, 30%) and performance plateaus. This would answer three practical questions: (1) How many RL steps can 0.7M synthesized tasks sustain before a second saturation event? (2) Does the decay rate depend on the source corpus—do AoPS-derived math tasks saturate faster than MegaScience-derived STEM tasks because math reasoning patterns are more uniform? (3) Can you predict the saturation rate in advance from the distractor count and source text properties, enabling practitioners to estimate how much synthesis is needed for a target training budget? If saturation is rapid (e.g., effectiveness drops to 30% in 200 steps for the 4B model), then Golden Goose is a one-time boost rather than a sustainable scaling solution, and the field needs methods for continuously sourcing *new* unverifiable text. If saturation is slow, the existing source corpora can support extended training, and the approach genuinely scales.

**Cross-model-family RL training with identical synthesized data.** All RL experiments use Qwen-family models (R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, Qwen3-4B-Instruct). A rigorous generalization test would apply the identical GooseReason-0.7M dataset to RL training of non-Qwen base models—specifically, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Gemma-2-9B, and DeepSeek-Coder-7B—using the same ProRLv2 recipe and measuring domain-aggregate performance gains over continued training with the original ProRL data only. This would answer: (a) whether the gains are Qwen-specific (due to pre-training data overlap with AoPS/MegaScience, architecture properties, or instruction-tuning format compatibility), (b) whether the optimal distractor count shifts across model families (the 9-distractor calibration in Figure 4 was done for ProRL-1.5B-v2 only), and (c) whether the instruction-following degradation that kills the open-ended format (83% failure rate) is universal or Qwen-specific. A negative result—GooseReason helps Qwen models but not Llama or Gemma models—would substantially narrow the claimed generality. A positive result would validate the format-transformation approach as model-agnostic.

**Mechanistic analysis of MCQ-to-open-ended transfer.** The paper's central implicit claim is that learning to discriminate correct reasoning continuations from plausible distractors transfers to the ability to generate correct reasoning from scratch. The open-ended benchmark results (Tables 1-3) are consistent with this claim, but provide no evidence about mechanism. A targeted probing experiment would compare the internal representations of a model before and after GooseReason training on matched MCQ and open-ended versions of the same underlying problems. Specifically: take a set of MATH problems that have both (a) a standard open-ended version and (b) a Golden Goose MCQ version synthesized from the same solution text (mask a critical derivation step, generate distractors for it). For each problem, record the model's hidden-state activations at the final token of the context (just before answer generation) for both versions, before and after GooseReason training. If transfer is occurring, the MCQ-version activations should become more predictive of open-ended-version correctness over training, and the representations for the two formats should converge in a shared reasoning subspace. If the representations remain format-separated, the benchmark gains might reflect separate skill acquisition for MCQ and open-ended tasks rather than genuine transfer, and the ceiling for open-ended improvement might be lower than the MCQ training curves suggest.

**Scaling the pipeline to genuinely novel domains: law, medicine, finance.** The cybersecurity case study demonstrates that Golden Goose works for a specialized domain with no prior RLVR data, but cybersecurity reasoning (vulnerability analysis, threat assessment) is more similar to STEM reasoning (systematic, causal, constraint-based) than to the interpretive and precedent-based reasoning in law, the differential-diagnostic and probabilistic reasoning in medicine, or the forecasting and risk-assessment reasoning in finance. A domain-stress-test experiment would synthesize RLVR tasks from domain-specific unverifiable corpora in each of these three fields—legal case briefs and judicial opinions (from CourtListener or similar), medical case reports and clinical reasoning textbooks (from PubMed Central), and financial analysis reports (from SEC filings and analyst reports)—then measure both synthesis quality (human evaluation of distractor plausibility and masked-span appropriateness) and downstream RL training effectiveness on domain-specific benchmarks (legal bar exam questions, USMLE-style medical reasoning, CFA-style financial analysis). The key questions: does the MCQ format-transfer approach break when the "correct" reasoning is a matter of interpretive judgment rather than mathematical or factual truth? can a synthesis model without legal or medical expertise produce distractors that capture the genuine ambiguity of these domains (where multiple continuations might be defensible) without creating noise that corrupts the RL signal? A negative result in these domains would establish the boundary of Golden Goose's applicability: it works for reasoning with determinate answers (math, code, cybersecurity) but not for reasoning under genuine uncertainty.

**Combining Golden Goose with procedural generation for hybrid data pipelines.** The comparison with RLVE (Figure 5) shows that procedural generators are highly effective for math but weak for knowledge-intensive STEM, while Golden Goose is strong for STEM but depends on the availability of high-quality source text. A hybrid pipeline would use procedural generators for formal reasoning domains where verifiable environments can be hand-coded (math, logic, algorithmic puzzles, basic code) and Golden Goose for knowledge-intensive domains where environment design is infeasible (specialized science, cybersecurity, law, medicine). The research question is whether these data sources are complementary or redundant: does adding GooseReason-derived tasks provide additional gains over RLVE-derived tasks in a model that already trains on RLVE's procedural math and logic environments, or do the reasoning skills overlap enough that the marginal benefit is small? A concrete experiment would train Qwen-4B-Instruct from scratch for a fixed compute budget with (a) RLVE-only data, (b) GooseReason-STEM-only data (the MegaScience-derived subset), (c) a 50/50 mix, and (d) a 50/50 mix with the mix ratio adjusted per difficulty bin (more RLVE for easy math, more GooseReason for hard STEM). The result would inform whether future RLVR data pipelines should be designed as monolithic formats (one approach to rule all domains) or as modular, domain-specific combinations.

**Long-horizon RL training with fresh synthesis from growing corpora.** The paper demonstrates that GooseReason revives a saturated model, but does not test whether the model can be revived *again* after it saturates on GooseReason data. A sustained-scaling experiment would establish a pipeline that continuously ingests new unverifiable source text as the model trains, synthesizes fresh MCQs, and adds them to the training mix—simulating the "unlimited RLVR data" scenario the title promises. Starting from Qwen-4B-Instruct, train for 1,000+ RL steps while dynamically adding new GooseReason tasks synthesized from newly crawled sources (up-to-date arXiv papers for STEM, new AoPS posts for math, new GitHub discussions for code, new security disclosures for cybersecurity). Measure whether performance continues to improve over the full training horizon or whether a fundamental ceiling emerges from the MCQ format itself—perhaps because all MCQ tasks ultimately test the same kind of context-completion reasoning, and the model eventually learns a general "fill the mask" capability that transfers across domains but saturates once that meta-skill is acquired. If gains continue throughout, Golden Goose genuinely enables indefinite RL scaling; if a ceiling emerges, the approach is limited to a one-time boost per domain, and the field needs fundamentally different methods for continued scaling beyond that.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Domain-specialized reasoning models for high-value verticals.** The cybersecurity case study (Section 3.2) is the template. For any specialized domain where (a) reasoning quality matters, (b) verifiable RL training data does not exist, and (c) high-quality explanatory text is available (textbooks, research papers, professional forums, technical documentation), Golden Goose can bootstrap an RLVR pipeline that produces a domain-specialized reasoning model with a fraction of the cost of human data curation or domain-specific pre-training. Concrete candidates include: medical diagnosis (synthesize MCQs from clinical case reports in PubMed Central, train on USMLE and MedQA benchmarks), legal reasoning (synthesize from judicial opinions and law review articles, evaluate on LSAT and bar exam questions), financial analysis (synthesize from analyst reports and SEC filings, evaluate on CFA exam items), and software engineering (synthesize from architecture decision records and postmortem analyses, evaluate on system design benchmarks). In each case, the key metric is the improvement per RL training hour relative to the cost of GPT-5 API calls for synthesis—a calculation the paper enables by providing the cybersecurity numbers (180K tasks from raw web scrapes, 4.44% gain in 100 RL steps) as a baseline for estimating synthesis requirements in other domains.

**Data augmentation for existing RLVR pipelines at the saturation point.** The paper shows that even well-constructed RLVR datasets like ProRL's 136K-example blend eventually saturate—only 25% of examples remain effective for a heavily trained model (Figure 3). Organizations that have invested in RLVR training infrastructure (model families, training recipes, evaluation suites) can use Golden Goose as a drop-in data augmentation step when their models plateau. Rather than redesigning the training algorithm or sourcing expensive human-authored problems, they can run Golden Goose on unverifiable text corpora relevant to their target domains—the STEM textbook explanations, coding walkthroughs, and theorem proofs that their existing verifiable datasets don't cover—and directly mix the synthesized MCQs into their training batches. The paper's Qwen-4B-Instruct experiment (Tables 1-3) provides the template: the first 333 steps use the existing ProRL recipe, and only when performance plateaus (math degrades from 71.65% to 70.36%) does GooseReason data enter the mix, producing a recovery to 73.83%. An organization following this template would monitor the effective-example ratio of their training data (using the paper's 16-rollout diagnostic), and trigger Golden Goose synthesis when the ratio drops below a threshold.

**Cheap, scalable domain adaptation for open-weight models in low-resource settings.** The cybersecurity experiment demonstrates that Golden Goose can produce state-of-the-art domain-specific performance with a base model that had no prior domain exposure—Qwen3-4B-Instruct was not pre-trained or fine-tuned on cybersecurity data before the 100-step RLVR run. This has direct implications for organizations with limited compute budgets and no access to domain-specific training data: rather than paying for domain-specific pre-training (which requires millions of dollars of GPU compute and careful data curation) or hiring domain experts to write training examples, they can prompt GPT-5 (or an equivalent API) to synthesize RLVR tasks from publicly available domain text, then run a lightweight RL fine-tuning on an open-weight base model. The 4.44% gain in 100 RL steps represents a compute budget trivially achievable on consumer hardware (a few GPU-hours for the RL step, plus API costs for synthesis), yet it surpasses a 7B model (Llama-Primus-Instruct) that required extensive domain-specific pre-training and post-training. For a cybersecurity startup, legal tech company, or medical AI lab, this reduces the barrier to entry for producing competitive domain-specialized models from "hire a team of ML engineers and spend millions on compute" to "query an API and run a weekend fine-tuning job."

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions Golden Goose as complementary to, rather than competitive with, existing RLVR data pipelines—filling a specific gap that neither human-curated datasets nor procedural generators address. Explicit conditions for preferring Golden Goose emerge from the paper's experimental design and limitations:

- **Prefer Golden Goose when the target domain has abundant unverifiable reasoning text but no machine-checkable ground truth.** This is the core use case: theorem-proving, scientific explanations, code walkthroughs without test cases, threat intelligence analysis, medical diagnostics, legal reasoning—any domain where the reasoning is complex and multi-step, but correctness cannot be evaluated by expression matching, unit tests, or rule-based verifiers. The cybersecurity case study (Section 3.2) is the canonical example: no prior RLVR data exists, no procedural generators exist, yet 180K verifiable tasks are synthesized from raw web scrapes and produce a 4.44% gain.

- **Prefer Golden Goose when the model has saturated on existing RLVR data and algorithmic changes (longer training, more rollouts) produce diminishing returns.** The effective-example diagnostic (Figure 3) provides the trigger condition: if continued training on the existing data blend yields near-zero gains (0.13% STEM gain with ProRL data in Figure 5), switching to Golden Goose-augmented data can recover the training trajectory (3.48% STEM gain). The paper's specific finding that stronger models saturate faster (Qwen-4B-Instruct plateaus at 300 steps vs. 2,000+ for R1-1.5B) means this condition becomes more relevant, not less, as base model capability improves.

- **Prefer Golden Goose over procedural generation (RLVE, Reasoning Gym) when the target domain is knowledge-intensive rather than formal-symbolic.** RLVE is highly effective on math (Figure 5) but achieves only 0.62% STEM gain because procedurally generated algorithmic environments cannot encode domain-specific scientific knowledge. For domains like chemistry, biology, medicine, economics, and cybersecurity—where reasoning depends on domain facts, not just logical structure—Golden Goose's approach of mining existing explanatory text is inherently more scalable than designing hundreds of domain-specific verifiable environments.

- **Prefer existing human-curated verifiable datasets (ProRL, Big-Math, AceReason-Nemotron) over Golden Goose for core math and code domains where machine verification is available.** The paper does not claim that Golden Goose replaces these datasets; it uses them as the starting point for all experiments and adds GooseReason to supplement them. For domains where expression evaluation or unit testing provides a clean verification signal, training directly on open-ended generation tasks is likely more efficient than the MCQ fill-in-the-middle format, because it avoids any potential format-transfer loss and trains the model on the exact task it will be evaluated on.

- **Prefer alternative approaches when the target domain has no high-quality explanatory text to serve as source material.** Golden Goose's verification trick depends on trusting the original author's reasoning as ground truth. If the available source text is unreliable—forum posts with incorrect solutions, outdated textbooks, hallucination-prone LLM-generated content—the synthesized MCQs will encode those errors as training signal. The paper does not test this failure mode, but it follows directly from the pipeline design: garbage in, garbage out, with the MCQ format providing no guardrail against content errors in the source. In such domains, investment in human verification or procedural generation with guaranteed correctness may be necessary despite the higher cost.
