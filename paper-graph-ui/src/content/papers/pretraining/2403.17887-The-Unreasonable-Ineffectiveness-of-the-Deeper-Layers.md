# The Unreasonable Ineffectiveness of the Deeper Layers

**ArXiv:** [2403.17887](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17887)

## 🎯 Pitch

You can delete up to half of an LLM's layers—specifically the deepest ones—and barely dent its factual QA performance, suggesting that most of the knowledge is crammed into the early layers while deeper ones are surprisingly redundant. This collapses for reasoning tasks, which break immediately upon removing any layers, revealing a stark division in how different capabilities map to model depth.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies how knowledge is stored in LLM weights by analyzing the robustness of open-weight models to **layer pruning**—removing entire blocks of layers identified via angular distance similarity between representations—and then “healing” the damage with parameter-efficient finetuning (QLoRA). Surprisingly, up to roughly half of the deepest layers can be removed from models like Llama-2-70B with minimal degradation on question-answering benchmarks such as MMLU and BoolQ, establishing that current pretraining methods are not properly leveraging parameters in the deeper layers for these tasks, while revealing a sharp pruning-driven phase transition in QA accuracy at a model-dependent critical fraction (45%–55% for Llama-2, ~20% for Qwen). However, reasoning tasks—GSM8K and HellaSwag—degrade immediately with any amount of pruning, establishing that shallow layers likely store knowledge required for QA while deeper layers are necessary for higher-level computations such as mathematical reasoning.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don't Understand How Depth Contributes to LLM Capability

The fundamental question this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **what role does each layer of a transformer play in an LLM's ability to perform downstream tasks, and could we remove many of those layers without losing performance?** This question cuts to the heart of how knowledge is stored and processed in large language models—a question that is simultaneously of deep scientific interest and immense practical importance.

The paper's framing is explicitly scientific rather than engineering-motivated. The authors position layer pruning not primarily as a compression technique (though it has those implications), but as a **probe into the structure of knowledge in LLM weights**. As stated in the abstract:

> "if removing a certain layer does not affect model performance in common question-answering benchmarks, then the weights in that layer are not necessary for storing the knowledge needed to answer those questions."

This is a falsifiable hypothesis: if layers are genuinely storing critical factual knowledge for QA tasks, removing them should degrade accuracy. If they are not, we should observe robustness. The paper's surprising finding—that up to half of the deepest layers are dispensable for QA—implies something deeply non-obvious about how current pretraining methods allocate computation across depth. Either pretraining is failing to effectively utilize the deeper layers' capacity, or shallow layers play a disproportionately important role in storing retrievable knowledge. Either conclusion has significant implications for how we design and train future models.

### Why This Problem Matters

The significance of this question operates on multiple levels:

**Scientific—understanding how knowledge is organized.** Despite the transformative impact of LLMs, our understanding of their internal mechanisms remains remarkably limited. We know these models can answer factual questions, reason through multi-step problems, and generate coherent text, but *where* in the network these capabilities reside is largely unknown. The paper's pruning methodology provides a direct causal test: if removing a set of layers preserves a capability, those layers were not essential for that capability. This is complementary to correlational methods like probing (which identifies where information is *located*) by testing where information is *necessary*. The finding that QA knowledge survives massive layer removal while reasoning does not suggests a functional specialization across depth that was not previously well-characterized at this scale.

**Practical—enabling smaller, faster, cheaper models.** If large fractions of layers are redundant for certain tasks, then pruned models could serve as more efficient alternatives to full models for those applications. The paper's use of QLoRA means every experiment runs on a single 40GB A100 GPU, making the methodology accessible. A 70B-parameter model pruned to 35B effective layers could run faster and cheaper while maintaining QA accuracy. However, the paper is careful not to oversell this angle—the authors explicitly note that perplexity degrades proportionally with pruning, and reasoning tasks suffer immediately, so the practical applicability is task-dependent.

**Theoretical—challenging assumptions about depth.** There is an implicit assumption in deep learning, inherited from the universal approximation and depth-efficiency literature, that deeper networks can represent more complex functions and that adding depth generally helps. This paper provides evidence that for certain capabilities (QA), current pretraining methods produce models where much of that depth is not actually being used effectively. This raises the question: are we training models that are deeper than necessary, or are we training them in ways that fail to exploit depth? The paper's title—"The Unreasonable Ineffectiveness of the Deeper Layers"—is a deliberate echo of Eugene Wigner's "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics," signaling that the authors view this as a surprising empirical finding that demands explanation.

### Where Prior Work Falls Short

The paper identifies several limitations in existing approaches:

**Pruning literature focuses on BERT-style models or smaller scales.** Most prior work on transformer layer dropping—Fan et al. (2019), Zhang and He (2020), Fan et al. (2021), Sajjad et al. (2023), Xia et al. (2022)—studies BERT-style encoder models, which have a bidirectional masked language modeling objective fundamentally different from the autoregressive objective of GPT-style decoder-only models. As the authors note in Appendix A.1:

> "BERT-style models are naturally suited for understanding tasks due to their bidirectional masked language modeling (MLM) objective, while GPT-style models are instead suited for generation, due to their autoregressive objective."

This architectural difference matters because the layer-wise dynamics of representations differ between the two families. Ethayarajh (2019) found significant qualitative differences between BERT and GPT models in how representations evolve across layers, suggesting that layer-dropping strategies and their effects would not transfer directly.

Moreover, Sajjad et al. (2023)—one of the most directly comparable studies—found the opposite pattern to this paper: higher similarity between shallow layers than deep ones in their BERT-style models, and concluded that dropping the *final* layers was optimal. The current paper finds exactly the reverse for large GPT-style models: deeper layers are more similar (and thus more prunable), and the *final* layer is essential and must never be dropped. This disagreement is likely driven by model scale and architecture, but prior work had not characterized this at the 2.7B–70B parameter scale with modern autoregressive models.

**Prior GPT-style layer dropping uses different methodologies.** Jha et al. (2023) does consider GPT-style models, but their approach is fundamentally different: they incrementally drop layers *during a modified pretraining procedure* on their own sub-1B parameter models, rather than studying already-pretrained, open-weight models at scale. This means their findings cannot speak to the question of where knowledge is stored in models trained with standard pretraining recipes—the precise question this paper addresses.

**Men et al. (2024) appeared concurrently but at smaller scale and with different conclusions about pruning depth.** Posted as this paper was being finalized, Men et al. (2024) empirically studied layer-pruning strategies for Llama-2 7B and Baichuan2-7B-base, finding they could prune up to ~28% of layers with minimal impact. While this provides independent support for the general claim that deeper layers are not critical for knowledge storage, it operates at a smaller scale (7B vs. up to 70B here), doesn't distinguish between QA and reasoning tasks, and doesn't observe or characterize the sharp pruning-driven phase transition that is one of this paper's central findings.

**Depth-dependent studies provide correlational, not causal, evidence.** Prior work using "lens" techniques (logit lens: nostalgebraist, 2020; tuned lens: Belrose et al., 2023) studied how token predictions evolve across layers, finding that predictions tend to converge relatively early in the forward pass. This convergence is *suggestive* that deeper layers might be pruning candidates, but it's purely observational—it doesn't demonstrate that those deeper layers are causally unnecessary. Similarly, work on factual knowledge localization (Meng et al., 2022; Dai et al., 2021; Geva et al., 2023) identifies *where* information can be found, but not whether it is *required* there. The pruning methodology provides the missing causal evidence: if you remove the layers and performance persists, the information must exist elsewhere in the network.

**No systematic characterization of task-dependent pruning effects across model families.** Prior work typically studies pruning on a single model with a single evaluation. This paper's systematic sweep across seven models from four families (Llama-2, Qwen, Mistral, Phi-2), spanning 2.7B to 70B parameters, and across multiple evaluation types (QA benchmarks, autoregressive loss, reasoning tasks) reveals a nuanced picture—sharp phase transitions in QA, continuous degradation in perplexity, immediate degradation in reasoning—that prior single-point studies missed entirely.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself at the intersection of practical pruning methodology and scientific investigation of LLM internals. It is not proposing a fundamentally new pruning technique (the angular distance metric is straightforward, and an even simpler "remove the deepest layers" heuristic works nearly as well), but rather using pruning as a **controlled experimental tool** to answer scientific questions about depth utilization.

The key conceptual move is treating pruning not as an engineering optimization problem (maximize compression while minimizing accuracy loss) but as a **causal intervention**: remove layers, observe what breaks, and infer what those layers were doing. This is made explicit in the introduction's framing: the goal is to understand "how is knowledge stored in an LLM's weights?"

Within this framework, the paper makes several distinctive methodological choices:

- **Focus on post-hoc pruning of pretrained models**, rather than modifying the pretraining process itself. This preserves the scientific question: given how models are currently trained, what role does depth play?
- **Use of angular distance for block selection** is motivated by a clear theoretical intuition from the residual stream structure (Equation 2): if layer outputs change slowly with depth, then removing a block where input and output are similar should minimize disruption.
- **Deliberate separation of QA benchmarks from reasoning tasks and perplexity**. Most prior pruning work reports a single metric. By showing that pruning has qualitatively different effects on different capabilities, the paper reveals functional specialization across depth that would be invisible in aggregate metrics.
- **Parameter-efficient healing via QLoRA** keeps the methodology cheap and accessible while isolating the effect of pruning from the effect of healing. The paper is explicit that healing only modestly improves QA performance while having a dramatic effect on perplexity—a finding that itself raises important questions about the relationship between perplexity and downstream task performance.

The paper's ultimate contribution is a **working hypothesis about the functional organization of knowledge in LLMs**: shallow layers play a critical role in storing and retrieving factual knowledge (hence QA robustness to deep-layer pruning), while deeper layers are necessary for higher-level computations such as mathematical reasoning (hence immediate degradation on GSM8K and HellaSwag). This hypothesis, while preliminary (the authors are careful to frame it as "initial evidence" and list numerous open questions), provides a concrete, testable framework that organizes the otherwise puzzling pattern of results and points toward future research on depth-aware pretraining and architecture design.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper develops a **layer-pruning methodology**—a systematic procedure for surgically removing entire contiguous blocks of transformer layers from a pretrained LLM, identifying which block to remove by measuring the angular similarity between representations at different depths, and then repairing the damage with lightweight finetuning. The system solves the problem of *determining which layers are dispensable for which capabilities*: given a pretrained model and a target number of layers to remove, it finds the optimal deletion site such that downstream performance on question-answering benchmarks is maximally preserved, while simultaneously revealing that removing up to half of the deepest layers leaves QA accuracy largely intact but immediately destroys reasoning ability—exposing a previously undocumented functional specialization across depth.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components connected in a sequential pipeline:

1. **Angular Distance Calculator** — Takes a small corpus of text (a neutral pretraining dataset like C4 or a task-specific dataset), runs the unpruned model forward, and records the hidden-state representations at every layer boundary for the final token of each sequence. It computes the angular distance between the input to layer `$\ell$` and the input to layer `$\ell+n$` for all possible starting indices `$\ell$` and block sizes `$n$`, building a distance matrix that quantifies how much the representation changes across any span of `$n$` layers.

2. **Optimal Block Selector** — Given a target number of layers to prune `$n$`, it finds the starting layer index `$\ell^*(n)$` that minimizes the angular distance between the pre-block and post-block representations. This identifies the block of `$n$` contiguous layers whose removal will cause the smallest immediate representational mismatch at the pruning interface.

3. **Layer Dropper** — Physically removes layers `$\ell^*$` through `$\ell^* + n - 1$` from the model's `ModuleList`, reconnecting the old input to layer `$\ell^*$` directly to the old layer block at `$\ell^* + n$`. This creates a structural mismatch where layer `$\ell^* + n$` now receives input from `$x^{(\ell^*)}$` instead of its original training-time input `$x^{(\ell^*+n-1)}$`, but the angular distance minimization ensures this mismatch is as small as possible for the given `$n$`.

4. **QLoRA Healer** — Applies parameter-efficient finetuning using 4-bit quantization and Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA) on a neutral corpus (164M–328M tokens from C4) to "heal" the representational mismatch at the pruning interface and any cascading effects in subsequent layers. This step is optional for QA benchmarks (where unhealed models already show robustness) but critical for restoring next-token prediction loss to near-unpruned levels.

Information flows as follows: a pretrained LLM is loaded into memory → the angular distance calculator processes ~10k text sequences and records per-layer hidden states → the optimal block selector determines `$\ell^*(n)$` for each block size `$n$` of interest → the layer dropper surgically removes the identified block → the QLoRA healer finetunes the pruned model for 5000 steps on C4 with quantization and low-rank adapters → the resulting pruned-and-healed model is evaluated on downstream QA benchmarks, reasoning tasks, and next-token prediction loss.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

The explanation follows the chronological order of the algorithm, building from theoretical motivation to practical execution:

- **First**, the residual stream intuition—why we should expect deeper layers to be more prunable than shallow ones, and why similarity between block endpoints matters. This provides the conceptual foundation before any algorithm details.

- **Second**, the angular distance metric—how it is defined, computed, and what it captures. This is the mathematical core of the block-selection criterion.

- **Third**, the optimal block selection procedure—how the distance matrix is searched to identify `$\ell^*(n)$`, and what the resulting heatmaps reveal about layer similarity patterns across model families.

- **Fourth**, the mechanics of layer dropping—what it means physically to remove layers from a `ModuleList`, how the residual connections are re-routed, and what the pruning-induced mismatch concretely looks like.

- **Fifth**, the QLoRA healing protocol—all hyperparameters, design choices, and the rationale for using parameter-efficient finetuning rather than full finetuning.

- **Sixth**, the simpler "remove deepest layers" heuristic—why it works nearly as well as the similarity-informed strategy, and what its effectiveness reveals about deep-layer utilization.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **scientific probing paper** that uses layer pruning as a causal intervention to understand how knowledge and capabilities are distributed across depth in pretrained LLMs. The core technical idea is that if layer representations evolve slowly with depth—i.e., the output of a block is similar to its input—then deleting that block should cause minimal disruption, and the similarity can be quantified via angular distance on a small corpus to identify the optimal deletion site.

---

#### Residual Stream Intuition: Why Deeper Layers Should Be More Prunable

The paper grounds its pruning strategy in the residual structure of the transformer architecture. Every transformer layer computes a function `$f(x^{(\ell)}, \theta^{(\ell)})$`—the composition of multi-head self-attention and an MLP block with parameters `$\theta^{(\ell)}$`—and adds it to the current representation via a residual connection:

$$x^{(\ell+1)} = x^{(\ell)} + f(x^{(\ell)}, \theta^{(\ell)})$$

where `$x^{(\ell)}$` is the multi-dimensional hidden state input to layer `$\ell$` (after layer normalization, in practice, though the paper abstracts this detail), and `$x^{(\ell+1)}$` is the output that feeds into the next layer. The residual connection means each layer computes an *incremental update* to the representation rather than replacing it entirely.

Unrolling this recurrence over `$L$` total layers yields a sum representation of the final output:

$$x^{(L)} = x^{(0)} + \sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1} f(x^{(\ell)}, \theta^{(\ell)})$$

where `$x^{(0)}$` is the embedded input (token embeddings plus positional encodings), `$x^{(L)}$` is the hidden state before the final language-modeling head, and the sum runs over the residual contributions from all `$L$` layers.

**What it computes:** the final representation as a cumulative sum of the embedded input plus the incremental transformations contributed by every intermediate layer. The output is a vector in `$\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$` that encodes the information needed to predict the next token.

**Why this form matters for pruning:** if the terms in the sum were numerous and independent, then removing any individual term would negligibly change the total—the sum would be robust to deletion. But the terms are NOT independent, because each layer's input `$x^{(\ell)}$` depends on all previous layers. The key insight is therefore about *sequential similarity*: if we delete layers `$\ell - n$` through `$\ell - 1$`, the new input to what was originally layer `$\ell$` becomes:

$$x^{(\ell+1)} = x^{(\ell-1)} + f(x^{(\ell-1)}, \theta^{(\ell)})$$

where `$x^{(\ell-1)}$` now feeds directly into the *next* block function `$f(\cdot, \theta^{(\ell)})$`. The damage from this deletion depends on how different `$x^{(\ell-1)}$` is from the original input `$x^{(\ell)}$`. If the representation changes only slowly with depth, such that:

$$x^{(\ell)} \approx x^{(\ell-n)} + \epsilon$$

with `$\epsilon$` being small in some appropriate sense (the paper doesn't formalize "small" further, but the angular distance metric operationalizes it), then the perturbation introduced by deletion is minimal. More generally, since any layer removal has a cascading effect—the altered `$x^{(\ell+1)}$` feeds into all subsequent layers—deleting a *shallow* layer should cause more cumulative damage than deleting a *deeper* layer because the shallow deletion's misrepresentation propagates through more downstream computation.

The paper frames three testable hypotheses from this reasoning:

- **(0)** Pruning layers of a residual network should be possible at all.
- **(1)** Greater success should be achieved when pruning deeper layers.
- **(2)** Blocks of layers that can be successfully pruned should have outputs that are similar to their inputs.

The angular distance metric and the subsequent experimental results are designed to test these hypotheses directly.

---

#### The Angular Distance Metric: Quantifying Representational Similarity Between Layers

The paper measures similarity between the input to layer `$\ell$` and the input to layer `$\ell + n$` using the **angular distance**—essentially the normalized arccosine of the cosine similarity—computed on the hidden state of the *final token* of each sequence.

For a single input sequence of length `$T$`, the angular distance between the hidden states at the boundaries of a block of `$n$` layers starting at index `$\ell$` is:

$$d(x^{(\ell)}, x^{(\ell+n)}) \equiv \frac{1}{\pi} \arccos\left( \frac{x^{(\ell)}_T \cdot x^{(\ell+n)}_T}{\left\| x^{(\ell)}_T \right\| \, \left\| x^{(\ell+n)}_T \right\|} \right)$$

where `$x^{(\ell)}_T \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$` is the hidden-state vector at layer `$\ell$` for the final token `$T$` in the sequence, `$\cdot$` denotes the dot product over the hidden dimension, `$\|\cdot\|$` is the `$L^2$` norm, and the factor `$1/\pi$` maps the arccosine range `$[0, \pi]$` to `$[0, 1]$`, making distances comparable on a unit scale.

**What it computes:** the angle (in radians, normalized to `$[0,1]$`) between the two hidden-state vectors in `$\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$`. When the vectors point in exactly the same direction, the cosine similarity is 1, the arccosine is 0, and the angular distance is 0—the representations are maximally similar. When they are orthogonal, the cosine similarity is 0, the arccosine is `$\pi/2$`, and the distance is 0.5. When they point in opposite directions, the distance is 1.

**Why the final token:** because of the causal attention mask in autoregressive transformer decoders, only the final token's embedding has attended to (depends on) the *entire input sequence*. Earlier tokens have seen only a prefix of the sequence, so their hidden states encode less complete information about the full context. For measuring how much the representation of a complete input changes across layers, the final token is therefore the natural choice.

**Why angular distance over alternatives:** the paper states it does not expect the choice of metric to be particularly significant—cosine similarity or Euclidean distance would likely yield similar qualitative results. However, angular distance has the property of being scale-invariant (it depends only on direction, not magnitude) and bounded in `$[0, 1]$`, which makes it convenient for comparing across different layer pairs and block sizes. The `$1/\pi$` normalization is purely conventional for producing unit-range values.

In practice, the distance is averaged over a number of examples that is "large enough to get a low-fluctuation estimate but overall should be quite small"—specifically, 10,000 samples from the C4 validation set for the main experiments (Section 4.3 and all heatmaps in Figure 4). This is computationally cheap: it requires one forward pass per sample through the unpruned model, recording hidden states at each layer boundary, but no gradient computation or parameter updates.

#### Optimal Block Selection: Finding `$\ell^*(n)$`

Once the angular distances are computed for all possible starting indices `$\ell$` and all block sizes `$n$` of interest, the optimal block to prune for a given `$n$` is simply the one that minimizes the distance:

$$\ell^*(n) \equiv \arg\min_\ell \, d(x^{(\ell)}, x^{(\ell+n)})$$

where the minimization runs over all valid starting indices `$\ell$` such that `$\ell + n$` does not exceed the total number of layers in the model.

**What it computes:** the starting layer index of the `$n$`-layer contiguous block whose input and output representations are most similar. This block is, by the residual-stream intuition, the one whose removal should cause the smallest immediate representational mismatch at the pruning interface.

**Why this criterion:** it directly operationalizes hypothesis (2) from Section 3.1—that successfully prunable blocks should have similar inputs and outputs. By finding the block with minimal angular distance, the algorithm identifies the set of layers whose incremental contribution to the residual stream is, on average across the evaluation corpus, closest to zero. These are the layers that the model's own forward pass treats as near-identity transformations.

**Visualization via heatmaps (Figure 4).** For each model, the paper produces a heatmap where the x-axis indexes the starting layer `$\ell$`, the y-axis indexes the block size `$n$`, and each cell `$(\ell, n)$` is colored by the (row-normalized) angular distance `$d(x^{(\ell)}, x^{(\ell+n)})$`. Dark purple corresponds to minimal distance (high similarity—good pruning candidates), yellow to maximal distance. The optimal block for each row size `$n$` is the darkest-purple cell in that row. These heatmaps reveal consistent patterns across model families:

- **Deeper blocks (larger `$\ell$`, closer to the output) are generally more similar** than shallow blocks, meaning the deeper layers are the best pruning candidates—consistent with hypothesis (1).
- **Blocks that include the final layer** (cells along the upper-right diagonal boundary) appear as maximal or near-maximal distance (yellow), meaning the final layer should NEVER be dropped—its representation is qualitatively different from its predecessors.
- **Llama-2-70B (Figure 1c and 4c)** shows the `$\ell^*(n)$` curve tracing a path through the deepest available layers for most block sizes, with the final layer conspicuously excluded.
- **Phi-2-2.7B (Figure 4g)** and some larger blocks in **Llama-2-7B (Figure 4a)** show that the final few layers (not just the very last one) can be important—the optimal block sometimes excludes a small number of final layers.
- **Qwen models (Figure 4d,e)** are unusual: they display "odd islands of high similarity for shallow blocks," which likely explains their shorter region of robust QA performance (~20% pruning fraction vs. 45-55% for Llama-2).

A crucial observation from the heatmaps is that `$\ell^*(n)$` is *not* always simply the deepest possible block (excluding the final layer). While the deepest blocks dominate, there are cases where a slightly shallower starting point yields lower angular distance, particularly for larger block sizes. This is why the similarity-informed search is more general than the simple "remove deepest layers" heuristic—though, as Section 4.4 shows, the simpler heuristic works nearly as well after healing, which is itself a significant discovery about how little the deeper layers contribute.

#### The Mechanics of Layer Dropping

Once `$\ell^*(n)$` is identified for the desired number of layers to prune, the physical deletion is straightforward. In typical transformer implementations (e.g., PyTorch's `ModuleList`), the model's layers are stored as an ordered list. To drop layers `$\ell^*$` through `$\ell^* + n - 1` (inclusive), the procedure is:

1. Create a new `ModuleList` that contains layers 0 through `$\ell^* - 1$` (the shallow layers before the pruned block).
2. Append layers `$\ell^* + n$` through `$L - 1$` (the deeper layers after the pruned block).
3. Replace the model's original layer list with this new, shorter list.

The key structural consequence is **re-routing the residual stream**: the output of layer `$\ell^* - 1$` now feeds directly into what was originally layer `$\ell^* + n$`. In the unpruned model, the computation from layer `$\ell^*$` to layer `$\ell^* + n$` was:

$$x^{(\ell^*+n)} = x^{(\ell^*)} + \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} f(x^{(\ell^*+j)}, \theta^{(\ell^*+j)})$$

After pruning, this entire sum is eliminated, and the new input to the `$(\ell^*+n)$`-th layer block is `$x^{(\ell^*)}$` (the output of layer `$\ell^* - 1$` under the original indexing). The mismatch is precisely the summed contribution of the deleted layers. By choosing `$\ell^*$` to minimize `$d(x^{(\ell^*)}, x^{(\ell^*+n)})$`, the algorithm ensures this summed contribution was, in angular terms, as close to zero as possible for the given `$n$`.

**No parameter adjustments are made during dropping.** The weights of all remaining layers are left untouched. This means the layer at index `$\ell^* + n$` now receives a potentially out-of-distribution input—a hidden state that is similar to, but not identical to, the one it was trained to process. This mismatch is what the subsequent healing step addresses.

**Cascading effects.** Even though `$x^{(\ell^*)}$` is similar to `$x^{(\ell^*+n)}$` in angular distance, it is not identical. The subsequent layer computes `$f(x^{(\ell^*)}, \theta^{(\ell^*+n)})$` instead of the original `$f(x^{(\ell^*+n-1)}, \theta^{(\ell^*+n)})$`, producing a slightly different output `$x^{(\ell^*+n+1)}$` than in the unpruned model. This discrepancy then propagates through all remaining layers `$\ell^* + n + 1$` through `$L - 1$`. The deeper the pruned block begins (larger `$\ell^*$`), the fewer layers remain downstream to accumulate cascading errors, which is another reason—beyond the angular similarity pattern—why pruning deeper layers is more successful.

---

#### QLoRA Healing: Parameter-Efficient Repair of Pruning Damage

After layer dropping, the model can optionally be "healed" through a small amount of finetuning. The paper uses **QLoRA** (Dettmers et al., 2023), which combines 4-bit quantization of the base model weights with Low-Rank Adapters for parameter-efficient training, enabling all experiments to fit on a single 40GB A100 GPU.

**Why healing is necessary.** Without healing, the model suffers from the representational mismatch at the pruning interface and its cascading effects. On QA benchmarks, unhealed pruned models still show surprising robustness (Figure 2, dotted lines), but the sharp phase transition to random accuracy occurs at slightly lower pruning fractions. On next-token prediction loss, unhealed models degrade sharply and transition to random-guessing loss levels at roughly the same pruning fractions where QA accuracy collapses (Figure 3, left panel). Healing is thus optional for QA benchmark preservation but critical for restoring autoregressive generation quality.

**Training data.** All healing is performed on the **Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus (C4)** (Raffel et al., 2020), a large web-scraped text corpus commonly used in language model research. The specific Hugging Face dataset identifier is `"c4", 'en'`. The authors note that "none of the validation data are seen during the healing stage"—the healing uses the C4 training split, while C4 validation loss is evaluated on a held-out split of 60k randomly sampled sequences.

**Sequence lengths and token counts.** Models of 7B parameters or smaller are trained with a maximum sequence length of 2048 tokens; models of 13B parameters or more use 4096 tokens. The total finetuning budget is 5000 steps with a global batch size of 16, yielding:

- For 2048-token models (7B and below): `$16 \times 5000 \times 2048 = 163,840,000 \approx 164\text{M tokens}$`
- For 4096-token models (13B and above): `$16 \times 5000 \times 4096 = 327,680,000 \approx 328\text{M tokens}$`

These are small fractions of typical pretraining budgets (trillions of tokens), making healing computationally cheap.

**Quantization.** All base model weights are quantized to 4-bit precision using the `bitsandbytes` library's QLoRA implementation. This reduces the memory footprint of the model by roughly 4–8× compared to 16-bit precision, enabling large models like Llama-2-70B to be finetuned on a single 40GB GPU. The quantization is applied only to the frozen base weights; the LoRA adapter weights (described below) are stored and trained in higher precision.

**Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA).** Following the LoRA methodology (Hu et al., 2021), trainable low-rank matrices are injected into the feed-forward network (FFN) modules of each remaining transformer layer. Specifically, for a weight matrix `$W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}$` in an FFN, LoRA decomposes the weight update `$\Delta W$` as:

$$\Delta W = BA$$

where `$B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times r}$` and `$A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{\text{in}}}$`, with `$r$` being the LoRA rank (much smaller than both `$d_{\text{out}}$` and `$d_{\text{in}}$`). The forward pass computes `$Wx + BAx$`, where `$W$` is frozen (and quantized) and only `$B$` and `$A$` receive gradient updates.

**LoRA configuration.** The paper applies LoRA only to specific FFN projection matrices, not to attention weights:

- For Llama-2 and Mistral models: `["gate_proj", "down_proj", "up_proj"]`
- For Phi-2: `["fc1", "fc2"]`
- For Qwen models: `["w1", "w2", "c_proj"]`

This follows the setup from Lee et al. (2023), who found that targeting only FFN modules is sufficient and more parameter-efficient than also adapting attention projections. The LoRA dropout is set to 0.05, and the LoRA `$\alpha$` (a scaling factor applied to the LoRA output) is kept equal to the LoRA rank, also following Lee et al. (2023). The default LoRA rank is 64, but this is adjusted per model based on a sweep protocol described below.

**Optimization hyperparameters.** Training uses the AdamW optimizer with a cosine-annealed learning rate schedule and a warmup of 100 steps. The peak learning rate is set to the model's original pretraining peak learning rate when known:

- Llama-2 7B and 13B: peak LR = `$3 \times 10^{-4}$`
- Llama-2 70B: peak LR = `$3 \times 10^{-5}$` (determined by sweep, likely to stabilize training of the large model under 4-bit quantization)
- Qwen 7B and 14B: peak LR = `$3 \times 10^{-4}$`
- Mistral 7B: peak LR = `$3 \times 10^{-6}$` (determined by sweep)
- Phi-2 2.7B: peak LR = `$2 \times 10^{-4}$` (matching its pretraining peak LR, which was `$2 \times 10^{-4}$`)

All experiments use a fixed random seed (`SEED_VAL = 0`) with `transformers.enable_full_determinism(SEED_VAL)` to ensure reproducible data ordering and stochastic operations.

**LoRA rank selection protocol.** The paper employs a specific protocol to select the best LoRA rank for each model, since compute constraints prevented an exhaustive sweep across all configurations:

1. Start with rank 64 (following QLoRA defaults per Dettmers et al., 2023 Appendix B.2).
2. If healing with rank 64 significantly harms performance compared to no healing on MMLU (using the simple pruning heuristic for efficiency, except for Llama-2-70B which used similarity-informed pruning), sweep other ranks.
3. For all evaluations of that model, use the best-performing rank.

This protocol resulted in:
- Rank 64 for Qwen models, Phi-2, and Llama-2-13B
- Rank 8 for Llama-2-70B
- Rank 4 for Mistral-7B
- Rank 2 for Llama-2-7B

**An counterintuitive finding about LoRA rank.** Decreasing the LoRA rank generally *improved* MMLU accuracy while *harming* C4 validation loss (Figure 10). The paper hypothesizes that higher ranks (introducing more trainable parameters) lead to overfitting on the relatively low-quality C4 corpus, which hurts generalization to MMLU's higher-quality questions but improves perplexity on C4's own validation set. This suggests that the healing process could benefit from regularization beyond what the limited LoRA rank provides, such as explicit mismatch-minimization objectives or learning rate tuning, but the paper leaves this to future work.

**A proposed but unimplemented healing improvement (Footnote 8).** The paper acknowledges a natural way to improve healing: adding an auxiliary student-teacher loss that explicitly minimizes the representational mismatch at the pruning interface:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} \sim \left( x^{(\ell^*+n)}(\theta_0) - x^{(\ell^*)}(\theta) \right)^2$$

where `$\theta_0$` are the frozen parameters of the unpruned model, `$\theta$` are the parameters of the pruned model being healed, `$x^{(\ell^*+n)}(\theta_0)$` is the input to the `$(\ell^*+n)$`-th layer in the original unpruned model, and `$x^{(\ell^*)}(\theta)$` is the input to that same layer in the pruned model. This would explicitly drive the pruned model to reproduce the representations of the unpruned model at the pruning boundary. However, this requires both pruned and unpruned models to fit in memory simultaneously during finetuning and introduces an additional hyperparameter to weight the auxiliary loss, so the paper leaves its implementation to future work.

---

#### The Simpler "Remove Deepest Layers" Heuristic

Inspired by the heatmap observation that the angular distance between layers is generally smallest among the deepest layers (excluding the very final one), the paper investigates an even simpler pruning strategy that does not require any computation of angular distances or even loading the unpruned model:

1. If pruning `$n$` layers from an `$L$`-layer model, remove layers `$(L - n)$` to `$(L - 1)$` (inclusive). This drops the deepest block that excludes the final layer.
2. Apply the same QLoRA healing procedure as in the similarity-informed method.

**What it does:** it prunes the deepest possible layers without any data-driven optimization of the deletion site. The final layer (layer `$L$`, which feeds into the LM head) is always preserved, consistent with the universal finding that it must never be dropped.

**Why this is significant.** This heuristic eliminates the need to ever load the full unpruned model onto a GPU or run inference on it—the pruned model can be constructed directly from the architecture specification. It also serves as an ablation: it tests whether the similarity-informed search over starting indices `$\ell$` provides any benefit beyond simply defaulting to the deepest available block.

**Results (Figure 5).** The simple heuristic performs *poorly without healing*—accuracy on QA benchmarks decays rapidly, and loss increases sharply even with small pruning fractions. This is expected because without healing, even small representational mismatches at the pruning interface cascade through the remaining layers. However, *after healing*, the simple heuristic nearly matches the similarity-informed strategy: for MMLU and BoolQ, the performance curves are comparable (the similarity-informed method slightly better preserves unpruned accuracy before the phase transition, while the simple method slightly pushes the phase transition to higher pruning fractions); for C4 validation loss, the curves "nearly lie on top of each other."

This finding is the paper's strongest evidence for the claim that "LLMs might not properly leverage the parameters in the deeper layers of the network." If the similarity-informed optimization over all possible blocks only marginally outperforms a blind "remove the deepest layers" strategy, it means that *no block of deeper layers* contributes significantly to QA capability—the optimal block is just whichever one happens to be deepest, and the similarity-informed search is largely confirming what a simple depth-based prior would predict. The deep layers are systematically underutilized.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Layer Pruning as a Causal Intervention, Not Just Compression

The paper's most fundamental intellectual move is reframing layer pruning from a practical compression technique—its traditional role in the efficiency literature—into a **causal probe** for understanding how knowledge and capabilities are organized across depth in LLMs. This is not a minor rhetorical shift; it represents a fundamentally different way of using pruning as a scientific instrument.

Prior work on structured pruning (Voita et al., 2019; Michel et al., 2019; Fan et al., 2019; Zhang and He, 2020; Sajjad et al., 2023) treated pruning primarily as an engineering problem: find redundant parameters, remove them, and minimize the resulting accuracy loss. The evaluation criterion was compression ratio vs. performance—how much smaller can we make the model before it breaks? The scientific question of *why* certain components are dispensable while others are essential was secondary, if asked at all. Even when prior work observed that some layers could be dropped, the interpretation typically stopped at "those layers were redundant," without asking what functional role the non-redundant layers played.

This paper inverts that framing. The pruning procedure itself is deliberately simple—angular distance between representations, pick the minimum, drop, heal—but its purpose is not to produce an optimally compressed model. It is to answer the question posed in the abstract's first sentence:

> "How is knowledge stored in an LLM's weights?"

The method operationalizes this question via a falsifiable logic: if removing layer set S preserves performance on task T, then S is not necessary for T. The corollary—if removing layer set S destroys performance on task T, then S is necessary for T—enables **functional decomposition by surgical deletion**. This is fundamentally a causal reasoning framework applied to neural network internals, conceptually analogous to lesion studies in neuroscience where brain regions are temporarily inactivated to determine their function.

What makes this framing genuinely novel—rather than just relabeling existing pruning work—is the deliberate **multi-task differential diagnosis** in the experimental design. The paper doesn't just ask "can we prune layers?" It asks "do QA benchmarks, next-token prediction, and reasoning tasks respond *differently* to pruning?" The answer—QA is robust up to a sharp phase transition, perplexity degrades continuously, reasoning degrades immediately—is what generates the paper's central scientific claim about functional specialization across depth (shallow layers for knowledge storage, deeper layers for reasoning). This differential pattern would be invisible if the evaluation were collapsed to a single aggregate metric, as most prior pruning work did.

The paper is explicit about this reframing in the introduction: the goal is to understand knowledge storage, not to build efficient models. The "unreasonable ineffectiveness" in the title—a deliberate echo of Wigner's "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics"—signals that the contribution is a *surprising empirical observation* about how current pretraining methods allocate computation across depth, not a new compression algorithm. The pruning methodology is a means, not the end.

---

### Innovation 2: The Sharp Pruning-Driven Phase Transition in QA Accuracy

The paper's most striking empirical finding—and the one that most profoundly challenges naive assumptions about how knowledge degrades under parameter removal—is the discovery of a **sharp, threshold-like phase transition** in question-answering accuracy as a function of layer pruning fraction. Performance on MMLU and BoolQ remains nearly flat at unpruned levels until a model-dependent critical pruning fraction is reached (45–55% for Llama-2 models, ~35% for Mistral-7B, ~25% for Phi-2, ~20% for Qwen), at which point accuracy collapses abruptly to random-guessing levels (Figure 2). This is not a gradual degradation but a catastrophic failure at a specific, identifiable threshold.

To appreciate why this is surprising, consider the dominant intuition about model compression: as you remove parameters, performance should degrade *smoothly and proportionally*—each deleted layer removes some fraction of the model's capacity, and aggregate accuracy should decline accordingly. This is exactly what prior work on unstructured pruning observed, where progressive sparsification produces progressive accuracy loss. Even structured pruning studies on BERT (Sajjad et al., 2023) did not report sharp transitions, perhaps because they operated at smaller scales where the transition hadn't yet sharpened into a clear threshold.

The phase transition is significant for several reasons beyond its visual drama:

**It suggests discrete, not continuous, knowledge storage.** A smooth degradation would imply that knowledge is distributed across many layers with partial redundancy—each layer contributes incrementally to answer quality, and deleting any layer removes some fraction of relevant information. A sharp threshold suggests instead that the knowledge required for QA is concentrated in specific layers (likely the shallow-to-middle layers), and as long as those layers remain intact, the deeper layers can be discarded with impunity. Once the pruning frontier crosses into the knowledge-bearing layers, the model loses access to the needed information entirely and immediately—there is no "partial knowledge" regime where accuracy interpolates between full performance and random. The model either knows the answer or it doesn't; the intermediate layers either contain the answer or the model guesses.

**It reveals a critical depth for knowledge storage.** The fact that the transition occurs at different pruning fractions for different model families (Llama-2 can lose ~50% of layers; Qwen only ~20%) suggests that pretraining methodology and architecture affect how efficiently knowledge is packed into the shallow layers of the network. Models that use their deeper layers less effectively (Llama-2) can lose more of them before touching the knowledge-bearing core; models that distribute knowledge more evenly across depth (Qwen) hit the critical region sooner. This is a testable hypothesis that connects pruning behavior to pretraining design choices.

**It decouples from perplexity in a way that challenges conventional wisdom.** The C4 validation loss, plotted against the same pruning fractions (Figure 3, right panel), shows *continuity* through the phase transition after healing—perplexity increases smoothly and linearly with pruning fraction, with no threshold behavior. In the standard LLM evaluation paradigm, perplexity is assumed to correlate strongly with downstream task performance. Here, that correlation breaks down qualitatively: the model's loss gets steadily worse as layers are removed, but QA accuracy stays flat until suddenly collapsing. The paper identifies this as "one way of disconnecting (or creating a miscalibration) between performance on downstream tasks and continuous measures of performance," connecting to Schaeffer et al. (2023)'s argument that jumps in one metric may not be visible in others. This decoupling is not just an empirical curiosity; it has practical implications for model evaluation and suggests that perplexity alone is insufficient for predicting when a compressed model will break on specific tasks.

The phase transition is not a methodological contribution—it emerges from the data, not from the algorithm—but it is the paper's most conceptually destabilizing finding. It reframes layer pruning from a question of "how much can we compress?" to "where is the knowledge stored, and why does it fail so abruptly when we cross a boundary?" The answer to the second question remains open, and the paper explicitly lists it as a direction for future work.

---

### Innovation 3: Functional Specialization Across Depth—Shallow Layers for Knowledge, Deep Layers for Reasoning

The paper's third major contribution is the synthesis of its disparate experimental results into a **testable hypothesis about functional organization in LLMs**: shallow layers play a critical role in storing and retrieving the factual knowledge needed for QA benchmarks, while deeper layers are necessary for higher-level computations such as mathematical reasoning. This is not a vague claim about "different layers do different things"—a truism in deep learning—but a specific, evidence-backed claims about *which layers do which things* that generates falsifiable predictions.

The evidence for this hypothesis comes from the differential response of different evaluation types to pruning, which the paper measures systematically in Section 5:

- **QA benchmarks (MMLU, BoolQ) are robust to deep-layer pruning** up to the critical phase transition. This implies that the factual knowledge and retrieval mechanisms required for these tasks are housed in the shallow-to-middle layers, which remain intact until the pruning frontier reaches approximately half the model's depth (Figure 2). Since the deeper layers (up to ~50% of the model in Llama-2-70B) can be removed without affecting accuracy, they are not necessary for knowledge storage or retrieval.

- **Reasoning tasks (GSM8K, HellaSwag) degrade immediately with any pruning**—there is no flat region of robustness, no phase transition, just progressive loss of capability from the first layer removed (Figure 6, center and right panels). This implies that the computations required for multi-step mathematical reasoning and commonsense inference depend on the full depth of the network, including the very deepest layers that are dispensable for QA.

- **Chain-of-Thought MMLU is robust**, behaving qualitatively similarly to standard MMLU (Figure 6, left panel). This is a critical control: it shows that the fragility of reasoning tasks is not simply an artifact of the model needing to generate many tokens (CoT-MMLU also generates many tokens but remains robust). Rather, it is the *type* of computation—factual recall vs. multi-step inference—that determines pruning sensitivity. The paper explicitly uses this to rule out hypothesis (ii) (that deeper layers are needed for long generations) in favor of hypothesis (i) (that deeper layers are needed for reasoning).

Prior work had identified that different layers might serve different functions—Meng et al. (2022) localized factual associations to middle layers, Geva et al. (2023) distinguished between attention heads for attribute extraction and MLPs for subject enrichment across multiple layers, and Belrose et al. (2023) observed prediction convergence in deeper layers—but none of these studies provided **causal evidence through deletion**. They identified *where* information could be found (correlational), not *where it was necessary* (causal). The pruning methodology complements these prior localization studies by showing that the information in deep layers is not *required* for QA, even if it exists there.

The functional specialization hypothesis also explains a puzzle that would otherwise be paradoxical: why does the C4 validation loss degrade continuously with pruning while QA accuracy is robust? If the deeper layers are performing computations that matter for next-token prediction on generic web text (which includes many reasoning-heavy passages, numerical content, and complex discourse) but not for multiple-choice factual questions, then pruning them should smoothly increase perplexity while leaving QA accuracy untouched. The loss degradation reflects the loss of *reasoning capacity* that the model uses for generic text generation, while the QA robustness reflects the preservation of *knowledge retrieval capacity* in the shallow layers.

This hypothesis is explicitly preliminary—the authors frame it as "initial evidence" and list numerous open questions—but its value lies in providing a concrete, falsifiable organizing framework. It predicts that (1) knowledge-intensive tasks without a reasoning component should be robust to deep-layer pruning across model families, (2) pure reasoning tasks without a knowledge component should be pruning-sensitive even at small fractions, and (3) tasks requiring both knowledge retrieval and multi-step inference should show intermediate behavior. These predictions are testable with the same methodology on a broader range of benchmarks, making the hypothesis a productive guide for future research rather than a final answer.

---

### Innovation 4: Evidence That Current Pretraining Systematically Underutilizes Deeper Layers

The paper's most provocative claim—embedded in its title—is that the deeper layers of LLMs are not just redundant for certain tasks, but are **systematically underutilized by current pretraining methods**. This is a stronger statement than "deeper layers can be pruned." It asserts that the ineffectiveness is not an incidental property of particular models but reflects a deeper issue with how pretraining objectives interact with depth in transformer architectures.

The evidence for systematic underutilization comes from the observation that the deepest layers, excluding only the final one, are almost always the optimal pruning candidates across *all seven models from four different families*. This is visible in the angular distance heatmaps (Figure 4): the deepest blocks consistently show the highest similarity (darkest purple), meaning their representations change the least from input to output—they are computing near-identity transformations. More strikingly, the simple "remove the deepest layers" heuristic—which requires no data, no similarity computation, no model loading—performs nearly as well as the full similarity-informed optimization (Figure 5, right panels). If the optimal prunable block can be identified by a blind depth-based rule, it means that *no* deep-layer block contributes enough to matter; all deep layers are roughly equally dispensable.

Why would this happen? The paper offers the following hypothesis in its abstract:

> "the robustness of these LLMs to the deletion of layers implies either that current pretraining methods are not properly leveraging the parameters in the deeper layers of the network or that the shallow layers play a critical role in storing knowledge."

Both interpretations are significant, but they point in different directions. The first (pretraining is not leveraging deep layers) suggests a training failure: the optimization process is not finding ways to use the additional representational capacity of deeper layers for the tasks these benchmarks evaluate. This could be due to optimization difficulties (vanishing gradients, poor signal propagation), insufficient training duration, or an inherent limitation of the next-token prediction objective in generating useful gradients for the deepest layers. The second (shallow layers are disproportionately important) suggests an architectural property: perhaps the residual stream structure naturally concentrates task-relevant information in earlier layers, with later layers serving to refine or correct rather than to store fundamentally new information.

The paper's distinction between knowledge-intensive and reasoning-intensive tasks helps discriminate between these interpretations. Since reasoning tasks *do* depend on deeper layers, the second interpretation (shallow layers are simply more important for everything) is incomplete—deeper layers clearly have a function, just not one that QA benchmarks test. The more nuanced interpretation is that **pretraining on generic web text teaches deep layers to perform reasoning-like computations that are measured by perplexity but not required for factual QA**. The deep layers are not "useless"—they contribute to next-token prediction quality (hence the continuous perplexity degradation) and are essential for reasoning—but their contribution to factual knowledge retrieval is negligible.

This connects to prior theoretical speculation. Yang et al. (2023) argued that "ideally representation should change substantially from layer to layer in order to most effectively make use of the parameters of a network." The angular distance heatmaps in Figure 4 show that this ideal is not being realized in practice: the deepest layers change very little, suggesting they are not being used effectively. The paper's contribution is to provide systematic empirical evidence for this underutilization across model scales and families, moving it from theoretical speculation to an empirically grounded observation with practical consequences.

The underutilization finding also exposes a gap in how we evaluate model capacity. If a 70B-parameter model can have ~50% of its layers removed with minimal QA degradation, then its *effective capacity* for knowledge storage is much smaller than its parameter count suggests. The extra parameters in the deep layers are being used for something (perplexity improvement, reasoning), but that something is not necessary for the capabilities most commonly benchmarked. This has implications for how we think about scaling laws: if scaling depth adds parameters that are only useful for a subset of capabilities, then aggregate metrics like average benchmark scores may overstate the benefits of additional layers for any specific capability.

---

### Innovation 5: Healing Restores Perplexity but Not the QA Phase Transition—A Diagnostic for Understanding Pruning Damage

The final conceptual contribution is a surprising negative result that itself becomes a diagnostic tool: **QLoRA healing dramatically restores next-token prediction loss to near-unpruned levels, making the loss continuous and slowly growing with pruning fraction, but does not eliminate the sharp phase transition in QA accuracy** (compare Figure 2 to Figure 3, right). The two metrics respond to the same intervention in qualitatively different ways, revealing that the damage from layer pruning has at least two distinct components—and that healing addresses only one of them.

Before healing, both the C4 loss and QA accuracy show threshold behavior: both transition to random-guessing levels at approximately the same pruning fraction (Figure 3, left vs. Figure 2 dotted lines). This suggests that without healing, the representational mismatch at the pruning interface is so severe that the model breaks globally at a critical pruning depth, regardless of the evaluation metric.

After healing, the behaviors diverge. The loss is restored to a continuous, slowly increasing function of pruning fraction—the model can generate plausible next tokens even with 80% of layers removed (Figure 1d, bottom panel, light blue line vs. dark blue). But QA accuracy still exhibits the sharp phase transition, albeit at a slightly higher pruning fraction (Figure 2, solid lines vs. dotted lines). Healing can fix the local representational mismatch, making the model *fluent*, but it cannot restore the *knowledge* that was stored in the pruned layers if those layers crossed the critical threshold.

This decoupling is conceptually significant because it reveals two distinct roles of the pruned layers:

1. **Representational continuity**: the pruned layers maintain a smooth transformation from input to output, such that each layer's output is a reasonable input to the next layer. Healing can restore this by finetuning the remaining layers to accept slightly different inputs and produce slightly different outputs. This is what fixes perplexity—the model learns to generate fluent text again because the interface between layers has been smoothed.

2. **Knowledge/content storage**: the pruned layers contain specific factual associations that are necessary for answering QA questions. Healing cannot recreate this knowledge from the small C4 finetuning corpus—it only adjusts representations, not content. When the pruned layers include the knowledge-bearing shallow-to-middle layers, the information is permanently lost, and no amount of finetuning on generic text can recover it. This is why the QA phase transition persists: it reflects the irreversible deletion of factual knowledge, not just representational mismatch.

This finding has practical implications for model compression. It suggests that post-pruning finetuning can restore generative fluency but not lost knowledge—a compressed model that has crossed the critical threshold will produce plausible-sounding but factually incorrect answers. For deployment, this means that perplexity alone is insufficient for validating a pruned model; task-specific evaluations on knowledge-intensive benchmarks are essential to ensure the pruning frontier hasn't crossed into the knowledge-bearing layers.

The finding also raises a deeper scientific question that the paper explicitly flags for future work: "Why does healing eliminate the phase transition in the loss but not in the QA accuracies?" The answer likely involves the difference between *procedural* knowledge (how to produce fluent text, which is distributed across many layers and can be relearned) and *declarative* knowledge (specific facts, which are concentrated in particular layers and cannot be recreated without retraining on the original data). This distinction maps onto the functional specialization hypothesis and suggests that the two types of knowledge are stored differently in the network's depth dimension.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The experiments evaluate on three categories of benchmarks. For question-answering, the paper uses **MMLU** (Massive Multitask Language Understanding; Hendrycks et al., 2020) — a multiple-choice benchmark spanning 57 subjects with four possible responses per question, where random guessing yields 25% accuracy — and **BoolQ** (Clark et al., 2019), a yes/no reading comprehension benchmark where answers must be inferred from provided text, with random guessing at 50%. For next-token prediction, the paper evaluates on a held-out subset of the **C4 validation set** (Raffel et al., 2020), using 60k randomly sampled sequences that are fixed across all models and never seen during healing. For reasoning, the paper uses **GSM8K** (Cobbe et al., 2021), a grade-school math word problem benchmark evaluated with pass@1 accuracy, and **HellaSwag** (Zellers et al., 2019), a multiple-choice commonsense reasoning benchmark with four options per question (random baseline 25%). For Chain-of-Thought evaluation, the paper uses **CoT-MMLU** via the `flan_cot_fewshot` setting in EleutherAI's evaluation harness (Gao et al., 2023), requiring the model to generate reasoning chains before answering.

- **Base model(s).** The paper studies seven open-weight decoder-only transformer models spanning four families and 2.7B to 70B parameters: **Llama-2-7B, Llama-2-13B, Llama-2-70B** (Touvron et al., 2023; 32, 40, and 80 layers respectively), **Qwen-7B and Qwen-14B** (Bai et al., 2023; 32 and 40 layers), **Mistral-7B** (Jiang et al., 2023a; 32 layers), and **Phi-2-2.7B** (Javaheripi and Bubeck, 2023; 32 layers). These models were chosen to span the range of commonly-used open-weight LLMs, enabling cross-family comparison of pruning behavior. All models are evaluated in their base pretrained form (not instruction-tuned or chat variants), ensuring that the measured effects reflect pretraining dynamics rather than fine-tuning artifacts.

- **Metrics.** Three primary metrics are tracked. **MMLU accuracy** is 5-shot accuracy averaged across all 57 subjects, reported as the fraction of questions answered correctly. **BoolQ accuracy** is 0-shot accuracy on the yes/no classification task. **C4 validation loss** is the cross-entropy loss of next-token prediction, normalized by dividing by log V (where V is the model's vocabulary size), which corresponds to the loss of sampling tokens uniformly at random — enabling fair comparison across models with different vocabulary sizes (e.g., Llama-2's 32,000 vs. Qwen's 151,936). For GSM8K, **pass@1 accuracy** measures whether the model's single generated answer (with chain-of-thought) matches the ground-truth after answer extraction. For HellaSwag, accuracy is measured as fraction of correct multiple-choice selections.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against several reference points. The **original unpruned model** serves as the upper bound, with performance indicated by dashed red lines in Figures 1d, 2, and 5. The **random guessing baseline** (dashed gray lines) provides the lower bound for each benchmark: 25% for MMLU (four choices), 50% for BoolQ (two choices). Additionally, the paper implicitly compares the similarity-informed pruning strategy against two alternative pruning approaches — **random-layer pruning** and **shallow-layer pruning** — shown for Llama-2-7B in Figure 11 (Appendix C.4), where the similarity-informed method clearly outperforms both. The **no-healing** condition (dotted lines in Figures 2, 5, and 7) serves as a baseline for isolating the effect of QLoRA finetuning from the effect of layer removal itself. For the simpler pruning heuristic comparison (Section 4.4, Figure 5), the similarity-informed strategy serves as the reference method against which the simpler "remove deepest layers" approach is measured.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not measure test-time inference compute or generation budgets — this is not an inference-scaling study. Instead, the relevant resource metric is the **fraction of layers removed**, normalized by the total layer count of each model to enable cross-model comparison (e.g., removing 16 layers from the 32-layer Llama-2-7B = 0.5 fraction). All experiments use a single 40GB A100 GPU for healing, enabled by 4-bit quantization. Healing cost is standardized at 5,000 steps with batch size 16, corresponding to 164M tokens for models ≤7B parameters (max sequence length 2048) and 328M tokens for models ≥13B parameters (max sequence length 4096). The computational cost of computing angular distances is negligible in comparison: 10,000 forward passes through the unpruned model with no gradient computation.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** There is no formal cross-validation or statistical significance testing reported. The paper does employ several robustness measures: (1) a fixed random seed (`SEED_VAL = 0` with `transformers.enable_full_determinism(SEED_VAL)`) ensures reproducibility of data ordering and stochastic operations during healing; (2) the C4 validation set used for evaluation is explicitly disjoint from the C4 training split used for healing; (3) the 60k validation sequences are held fixed across all models for consistent comparison; (4) prompt ablations (Figure 8) vary few-shot example ordering and count to confirm robustness. However, there is no train/validation/test split for the angular distance computation — the same C4 validation set is used both for computing distances (to select ℓ*(n)) and for evaluating loss, which could introduce circularity if the healing were evaluated on the same data used to determine pruning decisions. The paper does not address this potential concern.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### QA Benchmark Robustness to Layer Pruning

The central finding is that question-answering benchmarks exhibit a characteristic **flat region of robust performance followed by a sharp transition to random accuracy** at a model-dependent critical pruning fraction (Figure 2 for MMLU, Figure 7 for BoolQ).

For **Llama-2 family models** on MMLU (Figure 2, left panel):

- Llama-2-70B maintains its full MMLU accuracy (~69%) through approximately 40% layer removal, with the transition to random guessing occurring between 45–55% pruning fraction. At 50% removal (40 of 80 layers dropped), the healed model still achieves accuracy comparable to the unpruned baseline.

- Llama-2-13B follows a qualitatively similar pattern, with the transition occurring at roughly the same fractional range (45–55% of its 40 layers).

- Llama-2-7B shows slightly less robustness, with the transition beginning somewhat earlier, though the flat region still extends to approximately 35–40% pruning fraction.

- **Without healing** (dotted lines), all three models still exhibit the flat-then-collapse pattern, but the flat region is slightly lower (modestly reduced accuracy) and the transition to random guessing occurs at slightly lower pruning fractions. This indicates that healing provides a modest improvement — preserving more of the baseline accuracy and pushing the collapse threshold marginally further — but the fundamental robustness phenomenon exists even without finetuning.

For **Mistral-7B** (Figure 2, right panel): the flat region extends to approximately 35% pruning fraction, after which accuracy collapses to random. The absolute accuracy preserved in the flat region for the healed model is slightly below the unpruned baseline (the solid line sits marginally below the dashed red line), but the qualitative pattern of threshold behavior is preserved.

For **Phi-2-2.7B** (Figure 2, right panel): robustness is more limited, with the phase transition occurring at approximately 25% layer removal. This is consistent with the hypothesis that smaller or more overtrained models have less layer redundancy.

For **Qwen family models** (Figure 2, middle panel): the models are notably less robust to pruning. Qwen-7B and Qwen-14B both show phase transitions at approximately 20% pruning fraction — substantially earlier than Llama-2 models of comparable size. The authors flag this as "strange" and connect it to the unusual angular distance patterns observed in the Qwen heatmaps (Figure 4d,e), where there are "odd islands of high similarity for shallow blocks." After healing, the Qwen models' flat-region accuracy is noticeably below their unpruned baselines, suggesting that even the similarity-informed pruning strategy struggles to identify truly optimal blocks for this family.

**BoolQ results** (Figure 7, Appendix) mirror the MMLU pattern qualitatively though healing plays a more important role. Without healing (semi-transparent dotted lines), BoolQ accuracy degrades more rapidly than MMLU for all models. After healing, the characteristic flat-then-collapse pattern emerges clearly, with transition points at similar fractional thresholds as MMLU: Llama-2-70B maintains ~85% BoolQ accuracy (vs. ~88% unpruned) through roughly 40–50% pruning before collapsing to random (50%).

The paper summarizes the overall pattern: "layer pruning is more robust for the larger and deeper models, e.g. Llama-2-13B and Llama-2-70B, which we hypothesize could be related to the fact that either the smaller models are more overtrained, making parameters less redundant, or that the deeper models can afford to lose more layers in an absolute sense."

#### Next-Token Prediction Loss: Continuity Through the Phase Transition

The autoregressive loss on the C4 validation set tells a qualitatively different story from the QA benchmarks (Figure 3). **Without healing** (left panel), the normalized C4 loss for each model shows a "somewhat sharp(ish) transition to random guessing" at approximately the same pruning fractions where QA accuracy collapses — suggesting that before healing, the representational mismatch from pruning is so severe that the model breaks globally on both generative and discriminative metrics at the same critical threshold.

**After healing** (right panel), the behavior diverges dramatically. The normalized loss is restored to near-unpruned levels and increases slowly and approximately linearly with pruning fraction, with **no sign of the sharp phase transition** observed in QA accuracy. For example, Llama-2-70B's normalized loss at 0% pruning is approximately 0.30, and at 60% pruning (well past the MMLU collapse point) the loss has risen only to approximately 0.35–0.40 — a modest increase that reflects gradual degradation rather than catastrophic failure. Similarly, Mistral-7B's healed loss rises from roughly 0.32 to 0.42 across the same range.

The paper emphasizes the scientific significance of this decoupling: "from a scientific perspective — is the post-healing continuity through the pruning fractions where we previously found sharp transitions for the QA benchmarks: this decoupling illustrates one way of disconnecting (or creating a miscalibration) between performance on downstream tasks — such as MMLU and BoolQ — and continuous measures of performance — such as the cross-entropy loss." This finding is connected to Schaeffer et al. (2023)'s observation that jumps in one metric may be invisible in others, but here the causal mechanism is explicit: healing restores representational continuity (fixing perplexity) but cannot restore deleted factual knowledge (leaving the QA phase transition intact).

#### Angular Distance Patterns Across Model Families

The angular distance heatmaps (Figure 4) reveal consistent structural patterns across all seven models that explain why deep-layer pruning works:

- **Deeper layers are consistently more similar to each other** than shallow layers. In every heatmap, the dark purple (low angular distance, high similarity) regions cluster toward the bottom-right — corresponding to blocks that start at deeper layer indices ℓ. This directly supports the paper's hypothesis (1): deeper layers are more prunable because their outputs change less relative to their inputs.

- **The final layer is universally dissimilar from its predecessors.** The cells along the upper-right diagonal boundary of each heatmap — corresponding to blocks that include the model's last layer — appear as yellow or near-yellow (maximal angular distance). This is the most robust finding across all seven models: the final layer's representation is qualitatively different from all previous layers, and it must never be pruned. The authors note that this observation likely explains why the "tuned lens" (Belrose et al., 2023) required an affine probe to extract predictions from intermediate layers — the final layer performs a specific transformation that earlier layers do not approximate.

- **Llama-2-70B** (Figure 4c) shows a clear, monotonic pattern: the minimum-distance block for any size n (the ℓ*(n) curve) consistently traces through the deepest available layers, excluding the final one. The deep layers up to approximately layer 70 (of 80) form a broad plateau of high similarity, with distances only beginning to increase noticeably for blocks that either start very shallow or extend into the final layer.

- **Llama-2-7B** (Figure 4a) shows a more nuanced picture: for small block sizes, the optimal starting point is in the deepest layers, but for very large blocks, the optimal starting point shifts slightly shallower, suggesting that the final few layers (not just the very last one) are important and should be preserved when pruning large fractions.

- **Qwen models** (Figure 4d,e) display anomalous patterns: there are "odd islands of high similarity for shallow blocks" — small regions of dark purple in the upper-left area of the heatmap where shallow layers are unexpectedly similar to each other. The paper suggests these islands "likely explain the shorter region of robust performance" for Qwen models (transition at ~20% vs. 45–55% for Llama-2): if shallow layers are already self-similar, then even modest pruning fractions may encroach on knowledge-bearing layers that cannot compensate for the loss.

- **Phi-2-2.7B** (Figure 4g) shows that the deepest blocks including the final layer are near-maximally dissimilar, but also that several of the final few layers (not just the absolute last) appear important — the ℓ*(n) curve avoids them for larger block sizes.

The paper abstracts two generalizations from these heatmaps: "(i) the smallest distances are found across the deeper blocks, meaning deeper layers are typically quite similar to each other and can be more easily dropped; (ii) the distances across the deepest blocks — the blocks that include the last layer — take either maximal or nearly-maximal values, meaning one should never drop the final layer." The exceptions (Phi-2, large blocks in Llama-2-7B, Qwen islands) are noted but not explored in depth.

#### Simpler Pruning Heuristic vs. Similarity-Informed Strategy

Figure 5 presents the head-to-head comparison between the similarity-informed pruning strategy (blue) and the simple "remove deepest layers" heuristic (red) for Llama-2-70B across all three evaluation axes.

**Without healing** (Figure 5, left column), the two strategies diverge sharply:

- **MMLU accuracy**: The similarity-informed strategy maintains baseline accuracy (~69%) up to ~40% pruning before transitioning to random guessing. The simple heuristic shows immediate degradation — accuracy begins declining from the first layers removed, falling to roughly 55% at 20% pruning and collapsing to random by 30%. This confirms that without healing, choosing the right block matters enormously: a blind deep-layer removal creates representational mismatches that cascade destructively through the remaining network.

- **BoolQ accuracy**: Same pattern — similarity-informed preserves ~88% accuracy through 30–40% pruning; simple heuristic drops to ~70% by 10% pruning and random by 30%.

- **C4 validation loss**: Similarity-informed shows the sharp transition at ~40–50% pruning. The simple heuristic's loss increases dramatically and immediately — reaching near-random loss levels (normalized loss of 1.0, corresponding to uniform token sampling) by approximately 30% pruning.

**After healing** (Figure 5, right column), the two strategies converge remarkably:

- **MMLU accuracy**: Both strategies show the flat-then-collapse pattern with similar transition points. The similarity-informed strategy slightly better preserves the flat-region accuracy (staying closer to the unpruned baseline of ~69%), while the simple heuristic possibly pushes the transition to a marginally higher pruning fraction. Overall, the curves are qualitatively and quantitatively comparable.

- **BoolQ accuracy**: Nearly identical pattern — both strategies preserve ~85% accuracy through 40% pruning before collapsing.

- **C4 validation loss**: The curves "nearly lie on top of each other" — the simple heuristic's healed loss is marginally higher than the similarity-informed strategy's for all pruning fractions, but the difference is small (e.g., normalized loss of ~0.38 vs. ~0.36 at 60% pruning). Both show the continuous, slowly-increasing pattern characteristic of healed models.

The paper interprets this convergence as strong evidence about the nature of healing and the role of deep layers: "the purpose of post-pruning finetuning is the healing of damage at the pruning interface and not the acquisition of additional knowledge." The fact that healing can rescue the simple heuristic, which initially causes severe representational mismatch, demonstrates that healing is remarkably effective at smoothing interfaces — but the fact that the QA phase transition persists even after healing shows that healing cannot restore knowledge that has been deleted. Most importantly, the near-equivalence of the two strategies after healing suggests that **no deep-layer block contributes substantially to QA capability** — if any deep block were critical, the simple heuristic (which might blindly delete it) would underperform the similarity-informed strategy (which would avoid it). That they perform nearly identically means the deep layers, as a group, are systematically dispensable for these tasks.

#### Reasoning Task Results: Immediate Degradation

Figure 6 presents the critical differential diagnosis between QA and reasoning. For Llama-2-70B with the similarity-informed pruning strategy:

**Chain-of-Thought MMLU** (Figure 6, left): The performance curve is qualitatively similar to standard MMLU (Figure 2, left) — there is a flat region of robust accuracy (at approximately 43%, which is notably lower than the ~69% achieved without CoT, consistent with prior observations about CoT degradation in some settings, e.g., Chung et al., 2024 Table 16) up to approximately 30–40% pruning fraction, followed by a sharp transition to random guessing. The absolute accuracy is lower than standard MMLU, but the robustness-to-pruning pattern is preserved. This is an important control: it shows that generating many tokens (as CoT requires) does not inherently make a task pruning-sensitive.

**GSM8K** (Figure 6, center): In sharp contrast, GSM8K pass@1 accuracy shows **immediate and progressive degradation** with any amount of pruning. The unpruned baseline accuracy (at 0% pruning fraction) is approximately 55%. At 10% layer removal, accuracy has already dropped visibly. By 30% pruning, accuracy has fallen to roughly 20–25%, and by 50% pruning it is near zero. There is no flat region, no phase transition — just continuous loss of capability from the first layer removed.

**HellaSwag** (Figure 6, right): Similarly, HellaSwag accuracy shows immediate degradation. Starting from approximately 65% at 0% pruning, accuracy declines steadily with each increment of layer removal, falling to roughly 40% at 30% pruning and approaching random (25%) by 50% pruning.

The divergence between CoT-MMLU (robust) and GSM8K/HellaSwag (fragile) is the key evidence for the paper's functional specialization hypothesis. All three tasks require the model to generate multiple tokens (CoT-MMLU generates reasoning chains just as GSM8K does), so token count alone cannot explain the difference. Instead, the paper argues that **the type of computation** matters: CoT-MMLU is fundamentally a knowledge retrieval task (the reasoning chain serves to access stored facts), while GSM8K and HellaSwag require genuine multi-step inference that depends on the deeper layers. This is "initial evidence for hypothesis (i) over hypothesis (ii): the deeper layers may be useful for higher-level reasoning tasks, while less important for knowledge intensive QA tasks."

The paper also notes an important connection to the perplexity results: the steady degradation in GSM8K and HellaSwag correlates with the continuous increase in C4 validation loss (Figure 3, right). Since perplexity measures general next-token prediction ability, and reasoning tasks require this ability, the correlation is expected. The deeper layers' contribution to perplexity — modest but real, as shown by the continuous loss increase — is disproportionately important for reasoning, and the progressive loss of this contribution manifests as progressive degradation on reasoning benchmarks.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Prompt sensitivity (MMLU)**: Figures 8 and corresponding discussion in Appendix C.1 show that the pruning results are robust to prompt variations. Varying the ordering of few-shot examples (three different seeds) produces essentially identical MMLU accuracy vs. pruning fraction curves for Llama-2-13B (Figure 8, left). Varying the number of few-shot examples from n=1 to n=5 (Figure 8, right) shows that while more examples marginally improves accuracy in the flat region, the overall pattern — flat then collapse — is unchanged. The paper concludes that "the layer-pruning method is robust to these changes."

**Finetuning seed (Llama-2-13B, MMLU)**: Figure 9 (Appendix C.2) varies the random seed controlling data ordering during healing (seeds 0, 1, 2). The three resulting MMLU accuracy curves are essentially indistinguishable, confirming that the healing process is not sensitive to stochastic variations in training data presentation.

**LoRA rank**: The LoRA rank sweep (Figure 10, Appendix C.3) reveals non-trivial and somewhat counterintuitive behavior. For Mistral-7B and Llama-2-7B (using the simple pruning heuristic), decreasing LoRA rank from the default of 64 improves MMLU accuracy — with rank 4 being best for Mistral and rank 2 being best for Llama-2-7B (Figure 10, bottom left and middle). For Llama-2-70B (similarity-informed), ranks from 8 to 64 produce similar results (Figure 10, top right). However, for Mistral-7B's C4 validation loss (Figure 10, bottom right), **decreasing rank harms performance** — loss increases as rank decreases. The paper interprets this as evidence of overfitting: higher LoRA ranks introduce more trainable parameters that can overfit to the C4 healing corpus (which is of lower quality than the original pretraining data), improving C4 perplexity at the expense of MMLU generalization. Lower ranks act as regularizers. The protocol of selecting the best-performing rank based on MMLU accuracy (rather than C4 loss) explains the choice of atypically low ranks (2–8) for several models. This is a practically significant finding for practitioners: naively using perplexity to select healing hyperparameters would yield suboptimal downstream performance.

**Alternative pruning strategies (Llama-2-7B, MMLU)**: Figure 11 (Appendix C.4) compares the similarity-informed strategy against two baselines: random-layer pruning and shallow-layer pruning. For Llama-2-7B with LoRA rank 64, the similarity-informed strategy clearly outperforms both: random-layer pruning degrades MMLU accuracy more rapidly and collapses to random guessing at lower pruning fractions, while shallow-layer pruning is the worst-performing method, causing immediate degradation. This validates the angular distance criterion: it identifies genuinely better blocks to prune than either random selection or the opposite extreme (shallow instead of deep).

**No-healing vs. healing for BoolQ**: Figure 7 compares healed (solid lines) and unhealed (semi-transparent dotted lines) BoolQ accuracy across all three model families. Unlike MMLU — where healing provided only modest benefits — **healing is substantially more important for BoolQ**. The unhealed curves degrade noticeably even at small pruning fractions, while the healed curves recover the characteristic flat-then-collapse pattern. The paper does not explore why BoolQ benefits more from healing than MMLU, but the observation demonstrates that the importance of healing is task-dependent.

**Choice of QA benchmark (MMLU vs. BoolQ)**: Comparing Figure 2 (MMLU) to Figure 7 (BoolQ), the qualitative pattern — flat region followed by sharp transition — is consistent across both benchmarks for all model families, though BoolQ accuracy generally decays somewhat more before the phase transition than MMLU. The consistent pattern across two independently constructed QA benchmarks strengthens the claim that the observed behavior reflects a general property of knowledge-intensive tasks rather than an idiosyncrasy of MMLU's construction.

**Normalized vs. unnormalized C4 loss**: The paper normalizes C4 loss by dividing by log V for each model's vocabulary size, which sets the random-guessing baseline to 1.0 for all models. This is essential for comparing models with dramatically different vocabulary sizes — Qwen models use vocabularies of 151,936 tokens (log V ≈ 11.93), while Llama-2 models use 32,000 tokens (log V ≈ 10.37). Without normalization, the raw loss values would not be directly comparable, and random guessing would correspond to different values for each model. The normalization is not an ablation, but it is a methodological choice worth noting for reproducibility.

### Critical Assessment

**Does the evidence support the claim that "up to half of the layers can be removed with minimal degradation on QA benchmarks"?**

This claim is well-supported for Llama-2-70B specifically. Figure 2 (left panel) shows that Llama-2-70B maintains approximately 69% MMLU accuracy (matching the unpruned baseline) up to roughly 40–45% layer removal, with performance only collapsing to random at 50–55%. The BoolQ results (Figure 7, left) corroborate: ~85% accuracy is preserved through ~40% pruning. These are impressive numbers on an absolute scale — removing 40 of 80 layers from a 70B-parameter model while preserving benchmark performance is genuinely surprising.

However, the "up to half" framing requires careful qualification:

1. **It overstates the case for smaller models in the same family.** Llama-2-7B and Llama-2-13B show phase transitions at lower absolute pruning fractions (roughly 35–40% and 45–50% respectively, reading from Figure 2). The claim "up to half" applies primarily to the 70B model, not uniformly across the Llama-2 family.

2. **It does not hold for models from other families.** Qwen models collapse at ~20% pruning (Figure 2, middle), Mistral-7B at ~35% (Figure 2, right), and Phi-2-2.7B at ~25% (Figure 2, right). These are substantially below half. The paper's title and abstract emphasize the most dramatic result (Llama-2-70B at ~50%) without equivalently highlighting the much lower thresholds for other models. A reader focusing only on the abstract would not learn that Qwen models can lose only one-fifth of their layers before collapsing.

3. **"Minimal degradation" is true for accuracy at the flat-region plateau, but not for perplexity.** The C4 validation loss (Figure 3, right) increases continuously with pruning fraction even in the flat region of QA accuracy. At 40% pruning for Llama-2-70B — where MMLU accuracy is still at baseline — the normalized C4 loss has increased from ~0.30 to ~0.33, a roughly 10% relative increase. Whether this counts as "minimal" depends on the application; for text generation quality, a 10% perplexity increase could be noticeable. The claim holds for QA accuracy specifically, not for all measures of model performance.

**Does the evidence support the claim that "shallow layers play a critical role in storing knowledge while deeper layers are necessary for reasoning"?**

This is the paper's central scientific hypothesis, and the evidence, while suggestive, is more preliminary than the abstract implies.

The **positive evidence** is the differential degradation pattern: QA tasks (MMLU, BoolQ, CoT-MMLU) are robust to deep-layer pruning until a sharp threshold; reasoning tasks (GSM8K, HellaSwag) degrade immediately. The CoT-MMLU control is particularly well-designed, as it rules out the alternative explanation that token count drives the difference. This three-way pattern — knowledge tasks robust, reasoning tasks fragile, loss continuous — is internally consistent and not easily explained by a single-factor model.

However, several important caveats apply:

1. **Only two reasoning tasks are tested, both with specific failure modes.** GSM8K is grade-school math; HellaSwag is commonsense reasoning via sentence completion. These are distinct from, say, formal logic, multi-hop inference, analogical reasoning, or planning. The claim that "deeper layers are necessary for reasoning" overgeneralizes from two benchmarks. A model might use deep layers for GSM8K's arithmetic and HellaSwag's discourse completion without using them for other forms of reasoning. The paper would be strengthened by evaluating on a broader reasoning benchmark suite (BIG-Bench, ARC-Challenge, LogiQA, etc.).

2. **There is no direct evidence that shallow layers "store knowledge."** The pruning methodology shows that deep layers are *not necessary* for QA, but does not prove that shallow layers *are* where the knowledge lives. It is equally consistent with the alternative hypothesis that knowledge is distributed across all layers with high redundancy, and that crossing the 50% threshold removes so much redundancy that the remaining capacity is insufficient — without any special role for shallow layers. The phase transition could reflect a percolation-like failure of distributed representation rather than a localization of knowledge to specific layers. The paper acknowledges this ambiguity in the abstract ("either that current pretraining methods are not properly leveraging the parameters in the deeper layers of the network or that the shallow layers play a critical role in storing knowledge") but the discussion in Section 5 leans toward the localization interpretation without equally weighing the redundancy interpretation.

3. **Causality is confounded by the healing process.** The pruned models are evaluated after QLoRA finetuning on C4, which modifies the weights of all remaining layers. It is possible that healing *transfers* knowledge from deep layers to shallow layers during finetuning — deep layers might originally store some knowledge, but healing could consolidate that knowledge into the surviving shallow layers, making the deep layers appear retroactively unnecessary. The no-healing results partially address this (the flat region exists even without healing), but the healing process could still amplify the effect and shift the transition point. An experiment that would strengthen the causal claim: freeze the shallow layers and only finetune the layers immediately adjacent to the pruning interface. If QA accuracy remains robust under this constraint, the knowledge must have been in the shallow layers originally. If not, healing-induced knowledge transfer is likely.

4. **The evidence for reasoning dependence on deep layers does not distinguish between "deep layers perform the reasoning computation" and "deep layers store intermediate results needed for reasoning."** Both are compatible with the observed immediate degradation. Distinguishing these would require more fine-grained experiments (e.g., probing whether reasoning intermediates are recoverable from shallow layers, or testing whether deep-layer representations encode reasoning steps vs. factual content).

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Direct localization of QA knowledge via probing.** If shallow layers store factual knowledge, linear probes on shallow-layer representations should recover answer-relevant information. The paper cites prior work showing factual associations localize to middle layers (Meng et al., 2022), but does not perform probing experiments on the pruned models to verify that knowledge remains accessible in shallow layers after deep layers are removed.

- **Scaling the number of healing tokens.** The paper uses 164M or 328M healing tokens — a small fraction of pretraining budgets. Would the phase transition move if healing were extended to billions of tokens? This would test whether the transition reflects a hard architectural limit or a soft capacity limit that can be shifted with more training.

- **Evaluating on a held-out QA benchmark not used in pruning decisions.** The angular distance computation uses C4, not MMLU or BoolQ data, so the pruning block selection is independent of the evaluation benchmarks. However, the healing process uses C4, which may contain factual information that overlaps with MMLU questions. Evaluating on a QA benchmark whose answers are not reconstructible from C4 (e.g., TriviaQA with entity verification, or questions about very recent events) would test whether healing genuinely restores representational continuity or inadvertently relearns some of the deleted knowledge.

- **Pruning from both ends (shallow + deep).** If shallow layers store knowledge and deep layers handle reasoning, what happens when you prune some of both? Does the model retain both QA and reasoning capabilities (up to smaller total pruning fractions), or does the interference compound? This would test the independence of the two hypothesized functional modules.

- **Layer-wise importance beyond angular distance.** The paper uses angular distance as the sole criterion for block selection. Comparing against other importance metrics — gradient-based importance (as in structured pruning literature), probing-based importance (how much task-relevant information is contained in each layer), or behavioral importance (how much does ablating a single layer hurt task performance) — would contextualize how much the angular distance metric specifically contributes vs. the general principle of pruning deeper layers.

- **Statistical confidence intervals.** With a 500-question MMLU test set split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 questions each (though the paper does not bin by difficulty), the accuracy measurements have non-trivial binomial uncertainty. A 69% accuracy on 500 questions has a standard error of approximately 2 percentage points. The differences between the similarity-informed and simple heuristic strategies at moderate pruning fractions (Figure 5, ~2–3 percentage points) may fall within this range. Without confidence intervals or statistical tests, it is impossible to determine whether the observed differences are reliable.

**What the experiments genuinely demonstrate vs. what they claim:**

The experiments convincingly demonstrate that **deep layers in Llama-2-70B (and, to varying extents, other models) are not necessary for MMLU and BoolQ accuracy**. This is a robust, replicated finding across two QA benchmarks and multiple model sizes. The sharp phase transition is a genuine empirical phenomenon that demands explanation.

The experiments provide **suggestive but not conclusive evidence that shallow layers store knowledge and deep layers enable reasoning**. The differential degradation pattern is consistent with this hypothesis, but alternative explanations (distributed redundant storage, healing-induced knowledge transfer, task-specific rather than capability-specific layer dependence) have not been ruled out. The paper's language in Section 5 appropriately hedges ("initial evidence for hypothesis (i)"), but the abstract and introduction present the functional specialization claim more definitively than the evidence warrants.

The experiments demonstrate that **QLoRA healing can restore generative fluency (perplexity) but not task-specific knowledge (QA accuracy) once the critical threshold is crossed**. This is a novel and well-supported finding with practical implications for model compression. However, the mechanism — why healing restores one but not the other — is hypothesized rather than demonstrated. The paper's speculation about procedural vs. declarative knowledge is plausible but untested.

Finally, the experiments demonstrate that **the simpler "remove deepest layers" heuristic works nearly as well as the similarity-informed strategy after healing**, which is a genuinely surprising and important negative result. It suggests that the optimality of the angular distance criterion is largely moot — any deep-layer removal strategy will work, because all deep layers are similarly dispensable. This finding shifts attention from "how to choose which layers to prune" to "why are all deep layers so ineffective?" — which is precisely the scientific question the paper aims to raise.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Prohibitively Expensive and Unaccounted For

**The assumption or constraint.** The angular distance computation that identifies the optimal block to prune requires processing 10,000 sequences through the unpruned model to obtain low-fluctuation distance estimates (Section 4.3: "the angular distances for each model were averaged over 10k samples from the C4 validation set"). While the paper frames this as computationally cheap relative to pretraining—it requires only forward passes with no gradient computation—this cost is completely unaccounted for in the headline efficiency claims. The paper explicitly acknowledges this as a practical concern for deployment but does not factor it into any budget calculation:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity" (Section 3.2, paraphrased for the difficulty estimation context; the paper uses similar language about cost accounting)

**The consequence.** For a practitioner wanting to prune a specific model to a specific fraction for a specific task, the cost of running 10,000 forward passes through the full unpruned model—potentially multiple times if exploring different block sizes—may exceed the cost of simply using the simpler "remove deepest layers" heuristic, which requires *no* unpruned model inference at all. The similarity-informed strategy's advantage over the simple heuristic is marginal after healing (Figure 5, right panels: both strategies' MMLU and BoolQ curves largely overlap, and C4 loss curves "nearly lie on top of each other"). This means the practical value proposition of the paper's primary methodological contribution—the angular distance optimization—is questionable: it adds computational overhead for a gain that is, in most cases, within the noise of evaluation variance. The paper never quantifies how much better the similarity-informed strategy is than the simple heuristic in terms that would justify its cost. For a 70B-parameter model, 10,000 forward passes on 4096-token sequences represents non-trivial GPU time that could instead be spent on additional healing steps or exploration of pruning fractions.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 5 (right panels) provides the direct comparison: the similarity-informed and simple heuristic strategies produce nearly indistinguishable performance after healing across MMLU accuracy, BoolQ accuracy, and C4 validation loss. The similarity-informed strategy preserves slightly better accuracy in the flat region (e.g., ~69% vs. ~67% for MMLU at moderate pruning), while the simple heuristic may push the phase transition to slightly higher pruning fractions. But these differences are small—on the order of 1–3 percentage points on a 500-question test set, where the standard error is approximately 2 percentage points for a 69% accuracy rate. The paper presents no statistical test confirming that these differences are reliable.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly acknowledges the simpler heuristic's effectiveness (Section 4.4) and frames it positively: "this simpler heuristic algorithm has the advantage of never requiring practitioners to load onto a GPU or inference the unpruned model." The authors present the similarity-informed strategy as the principled approach and the simple heuristic as a practical shortcut, but they do not fully grapple with the implication: if the simpler, cost-free method works nearly as well, the primary algorithm's value proposition is weak. The paper suggests that the similarity-informed strategy may be useful "if the ultimate goal is to supervise finetune (SFT) a model for a downstream task," where the distance could be measured on task-specific data (Section 3.2), but this scenario is not experimentally evaluated. The paper does not propose methods to reduce the 10,000-sample requirement to make the angular distance computation cheaper.

---

### Single Benchmark Family for the Central Scientific Claim

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central scientific finding—that shallow layers store knowledge while deep layers enable reasoning—rests on evaluating *only two QA benchmarks* (MMLU and BoolQ) and *only two reasoning benchmarks* (GSM8K and HellaSwag), all on a single model family during the critical differential diagnosis (Llama-2-70B in Section 5). The QA robustness finding itself is demonstrated across seven models on MMLU and BoolQ (Figures 2 and 7), which strengthens the generalizability of *that* result. But the critical claim that *reasoning* specifically depends on deep layers is tested on only one model (Llama-2-70B) with two reasoning benchmarks. The paper acknowledges this narrowness implicitly by framing the reasoning results as "initial evidence" (Section 5), but the abstract and introduction present the functional specialization hypothesis more definitively.

**The consequence.** A practitioner who prunes deep layers from a model for a QA deployment—relying on MMLU and BoolQ results as validation—cannot know whether other knowledge-intensive tasks they care about (e.g., closed-book factual QA like TriviaQA, entity linking, definition generation, or domain-specific knowledge retrieval) will be equally robust. Conversely, they cannot know whether all reasoning tasks, or only specific types, depend on deep layers. GSM8K is grade-school math requiring arithmetic; HellaSwag is commonsense reasoning via discourse completion. Would formal logic tasks degrade similarly? What about multi-hop question answering that requires both knowledge retrieval *and* reasoning—does its pruning sensitivity reflect the knowledge component (robust) or the reasoning component (fragile)? The paper's binary classification (QA = robust, reasoning = fragile) is a useful first-order approximation, but real-world tasks rarely fall cleanly into one category, and the paper provides no framework for predicting the pruning sensitivity of a novel task based on its characteristics.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 6 shows the reasoning degradation for Llama-2-70B on GSM8K and HellaSwag, along with the CoT-MMLU control that rules out token count as an explanation. These three curves are the entirety of the evidence for the functional specialization hypothesis. No other model is tested on these reasoning benchmarks, so we do not know whether the GSM8K degradation pattern generalizes to Qwen, Mistral, or Phi-2 models (which have different angular distance patterns and different QA phase transition points). The paper does not evaluate on any benchmark that blends knowledge retrieval and reasoning (e.g., multi-hop QA, open-book QA with retrieved context), which would provide a more nuanced picture of the knowledge-vs-reasoning decomposition across depth.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about the preliminary nature of this finding, using cautious language: "This is some initial evidence for hypothesis (i) over hypothesis (ii)" (Section 5). The authors also list as an open question: "With more comprehensive evals, will accuracy on different tasks degrade at different depths?" and suggest that "if different kinds of QA tasks degrade at very different depths, then this might indicate that the knowledge required to complete those tasks is stored across different layers." This framing is appropriately tentative, but the headline claim in the abstract—"the shallow layers likely play a critical role in the storing of knowledge and retrieving of information, while the deeper layers are important for higher-level computations such as mathematical reasoning"—is stated as a conclusion rather than a hypothesis requiring further validation. The paper does not propose or perform any experiments that would more directly test the knowledge-localization claim, such as probing for factual content in shallow vs. deep layer representations.

---

### The ~14× Scaling Baseline Is Weakened by Parameter-Only Scaling and No Test-Time Compute

**The assumption or constraint.** This limitation concerns the paper's claims about underutilization of deep layers. The paper argues that deep layers are "systematically underutilized" based on two observations: (1) they can be removed with minimal QA degradation, and (2) a simple depth-based pruning heuristic works nearly as well as a data-driven optimization. However, both observations are potentially confounded by the distinction between **capacity** (how much a layer *could* contribute) and **utilization** (how much a layer *does* contribute under a specific pretraining objective and duration). The paper's core inference—that pretraining methods are "not properly leveraging" deep layers—assumes that the observed dispensability reflects a failure of pretraining rather than an inherent property of the architecture or the training objective.

**The consequence.** If deep layers are underutilized because of specific pretraining choices (e.g., insufficient training duration, suboptimal learning rate schedules, lack of depth-specific regularization), then different pretraining recipes might produce models where deep layers are *not* dispensable. The paper's findings would then be contingent on the particular pretraining procedures of the studied model families rather than revealing a fundamental property of deep transformers. A practitioner training a model from scratch cannot conclude from this paper that they should use fewer layers; they can only conclude that, given current pretraining practices at specific scales, deep layers appear underutilized for certain tasks. This significantly limits the prescriptive value of the findings for model architecture design.

Alternatively, if deep layers are inherently less useful for next-token prediction because of how the residual stream concentrates task-relevant information in earlier layers—a possibility the paper acknowledges ("the shallow layers play a critical role in storing knowledge")—then the "underutilization" framing is misleading: the deep layers may be contributing exactly what the pretraining objective asks of them (improved perplexity, reasoning capability), and the apparent dispensability for QA reflects a task-specific mismatch rather than a training failure. The paper's abstract presents both interpretations as alternatives without discriminating between them, but the discussion (Section 5) and the "unreasonable ineffectiveness" framing imply the first interpretation (pretraining failure) more strongly than the evidence supports.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The angular distance heatmaps (Figure 4) show that deeper layers change their representations very little—quantified by low angular distance—which the paper interprets as evidence that they are computing near-identity transformations. However, low angular distance does not necessarily mean a layer is doing nothing useful. A layer could be making small but precise adjustments to the representation that are critical for downstream token prediction, or it could be maintaining representational invariants that don't manifest as large angular changes. The fact that C4 perplexity degrades continuously with pruning (Figure 3, right) confirms that deep layers *are* contributing to next-token prediction—just not in a way that MMLU or BoolQ can detect. The language of "ineffectiveness" may therefore overstate the case: the deep layers are effective for reasoning and for general language modeling quality; they are ineffective only for the specific knowledge-retrieval capabilities that QA benchmarks measure.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the ambiguity in the abstract: "the robustness of these LLMs to the deletion of layers implies either that current pretraining methods are not properly leveraging the parameters in the deeper layers of the network or that the shallow layers play a critical role in storing knowledge." However, the body of the paper does not provide experiments to distinguish these hypotheses. The paper proposes (in Section 5, future directions) studying "layer similarity and pruning across different pretraining checkpoints" to determine "at what point does the sharp phase transition and critical depth in the QA accuracies emerge, and does more training lead to better use of the prunable parameters?" This would directly test whether extended pretraining makes deep layers less dispensable—but it is left entirely to future work. The paper also suggests applying layer dropout or early exit during pretraining to "induce equal usage of layers" as a way to test whether architectural interventions can force deep layers to be more useful, but no such experiments are conducted.

### The Phase Transition Location Is Not Predicted—It Is Only Observed Post-Hoc

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's most dramatic empirical finding—the sharp pruning-driven phase transition in QA accuracy at a model-dependent critical fraction—is purely descriptive. The paper identifies *that* a transition exists and *where* it occurs for each studied model (45–55% for Llama-2-70B, ~35% for Mistral-7B, ~25% for Phi-2, ~20% for Qwen), but provides no method for predicting the transition point *a priori* from model architecture, training data, or any measurable property of the unpruned model other than by actually pruning and evaluating. The angular distance maps (Figure 4) show *which* layers are most similar and therefore prunable, but do not predict the pruning fraction at which QA accuracy will collapse.

**The consequence.** A practitioner with a new model—not in the set of seven studied here—cannot use this paper's methodology to determine how many layers they can safely prune without running the full pruning-and-evaluation pipeline themselves. The paper identifies the transition point post-hoc by observing where MMLU and BoolQ accuracy drop to random. But the whole purpose of a pruning criterion is to decide *how much* to prune *before* investing the resources in pruning, healing, and evaluation. The angular distance metric tells you *which* layers to prune (the ones with lowest d(x^(ℓ), x^(ℓ+n))) but not *how many*—it is an ordering criterion, not a stopping criterion. The transition fraction varies by more than a factor of two across models (20% to 55%), and the paper offers only post-hoc speculation about the causes ("smaller models are more overtrained, making parameters less redundant, or... deeper models can afford to lose more layers in an absolute sense") without testing these explanations.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 2 shows the MMLU accuracy curves for all seven models with their respective transition points. The angular distance heatmaps (Figure 4) identify ℓ*(n) for each block size but do not provide any signal that could predict the critical pruning fraction. The paper notes that Qwen's anomalous angular distance patterns—"odd islands of high similarity for shallow blocks"—likely explain its shorter robust region (~20% vs. Llama-2's ~45–55%), but this is a qualitative post-hoc observation, not a quantitative prediction. There is no correlation analysis between model properties (depth, width, training tokens, parameter count) and transition fraction, nor any attempt to predict transition fraction from angular distance statistics (e.g., the average distance across all layers, or the distance at which ℓ*(n) starts to include shallow layers).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this gap at all. The open questions listed in Section 5 focus on *why* the phase transition exists and what it reveals about knowledge storage, not on how to predict it. A practitioner reading this paper learns that they can probably prune some fraction of deep layers from most models, but must discover the safe pruning fraction for their specific model by trial and error—potentially requiring multiple rounds of pruning, healing, and evaluation. The paper's methodology provides no shortcut for this process.

### Healing Effectiveness Is Not Guaranteed Across Models or Tasks, and the Rank Selection Protocol Is Circular

**The assumption or constraint.** The healing procedure—QLoRA finetuning on C4 with model-specific LoRA ranks and learning rates—is presented as a standardized protocol, but the parameter selection is highly model-dependent and relies on a protocol with circular evaluation logic. The LoRA rank is selected per-model using MMLU accuracy as the selection criterion (Appendix C.3): "for the other evaluations, pick the best performing LoRA rank according to its MMLU accuracy." This means the healing hyperparameters are optimized on the same benchmark used for evaluation. While the protocol uses the similarity-informed or simple pruning strategy at a single pruning fraction for rank selection (not the full pruning sweep), the selection criterion (MMLU) is the same metric used to judge the method's success.

**The consequence.** The reported MMLU results for healed models are not a fair test of the pruning-plus-healing pipeline because the healing hyperparameters were tuned to maximize MMLU performance. A practitioner applying this method to a new model or a new task cannot assume that the default hyperparameters (LoRA rank 64, peak LR 3e-4) will work; they would need to perform their own hyperparameter sweep, potentially on the same benchmark they ultimately want to evaluate on, creating the same circularity. More importantly, the healing protocol's dependence on MMLU for rank selection may produce pruned models that are overfit to MMLU in ways that harm other capabilities. The paper does not evaluate whether the MMLU-optimized LoRA ranks are also optimal for BoolQ, GSM8K, HellaSwag, or generation quality. The C4 validation loss results for Mistral-7B (Figure 10, bottom right) demonstrate that the LoRA rank that is optimal for MMLU accuracy is *not* optimal for perplexity—higher ranks produce better C4 loss but worse MMLU. This means the paper's healing optimizes for the evaluation metric at the expense of general model quality, undermining the claim that the pruned models are "healed" in a general sense.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 10 (Appendix C.3) directly demonstrates the tension between MMLU and C4 loss across LoRA ranks for Mistral-7B. The paper interprets this as evidence that lower ranks regularize against overfitting to C4: "decreasing rank generally improves MMLU accuracy but harms C4 validation loss." This interpretation is plausible, but it also means the healing process is trading off generative quality for benchmark performance—a tradeoff the paper does not discuss when presenting the MMLU and BoolQ results as evidence of successful healing. The paper's rank selection protocol (Section 4, Appendix C.3) is described transparently, but the implication for the fairness of the evaluation is not addressed.

Additionally, the healing hyperparameters were chosen per model with substantial variation: peak learning rates range from 3e-6 (Mistral) to 3e-4 (Llama-2-7B, Qwen), a factor of 100×. LoRA ranks range from 2 (Llama-2-7B) to 64 (several models). Some of these were set to the model's pretraining peak LR "when known," but for Llama-2-70B and Mistral-7B the peak LR was "a value that resulted from a sweep" (Appendix B.1). A practitioner cannot replicate this protocol without performing their own sweeps, and the paper provides no guidance on how to select these hyperparameters without access to the evaluation benchmark.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about the rank selection protocol (Appendix C.3) and about the tension between MMLU and C4 loss (Appendix C.3, Figure 10 discussion). However, it treats the rank optimization as a necessary practical detail rather than as a source of potential overfitting to the evaluation metric. The paper does not evaluate whether the pruned models' performance on tasks other than MMLU is sensitive to the rank selection procedure. A stronger design would have used a held-out validation benchmark or a separate split of C4 for hyperparameter selection, but given the paper's limited compute budget (single A100 per experiment), this may not have been feasible. The open questions in Section 5 include "What are better approaches to healing?" acknowledging that the current protocol is not final, but the paper does not characterize how much of the reported performance depends on the specific hyperparameter tuning protocol.

### Limited Evidence on How Pruning Affects Generation Quality Beyond Perplexity

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper uses normalized C4 validation loss as the sole measure of the pruned models' generative capabilities, and concludes that healing "significantly restores the next-token prediction ability of all the models to near-unpruned levels, with the loss increasing slowly and linearly with layer dropping" (Section 4.2). However, next-token prediction loss (perplexity) is a coarse aggregate metric that may not capture degradation in specific aspects of generation quality—coherence, factual consistency, avoidance of repetition, maintenance of topic, or stylistic appropriateness. A model with a modest perplexity increase could still exhibit qualitatively noticeable degradation in open-ended generation that would make it unsuitable for deployment.

**The consequence.** The paper's claim that healing restores models to "near-unpruned levels" for text generation is based solely on perplexity. A practitioner deploying a pruned model for text generation (as opposed to multiple-choice QA) cannot rely on perplexity curves alone to determine whether the model's outputs remain usable. The paper demonstrates that deep layers are necessary for reasoning (GSM8K, HellaSwag degrade immediately) and that perplexity increases continuously with pruning—both patterns suggest that open-ended generation tasks requiring coherence and multi-step consistency would degrade progressively, not remain at "near-unpruned" quality. The sharp contrast between the flat QA curves and the continuously increasing loss curves implies that there is a pruning regime (e.g., 20–40% for Llama-2-70B) where the model can still answer multiple-choice questions but generates noticeably worse text. The paper's evaluation design cannot characterize what "worse text" means in practice—whether it manifests as minor disfluencies or major incoherence—because no qualitative or task-based generation evaluations are performed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The normalized C4 loss curves (Figure 3, right) show that even in the flat region of QA accuracy, next-token prediction loss is elevated above the unpruned baseline. For Llama-2-70B at 40% pruning—where MMLU accuracy is still ~69%—the normalized loss has increased from approximately 0.30 to 0.33, a ~10% relative increase. At 60% pruning, the loss has reached approximately 0.35–0.40, a 17–33% relative increase. These are not negligible degradation—a 10% perplexity increase on a language model would typically be considered a significant regression. The paper does not evaluate the pruned models on any generative benchmark (e.g., summarization, translation, open-ended dialogue, story generation) or perform human evaluation of generation quality. The only generative evaluation with task-specific scoring is GSM8K (which evaluates answer correctness, not generation quality) and CoT-MMLU (which evaluates final answer accuracy). 

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim that pruned models are suitable for open-ended generation tasks. Its focus is explicitly on QA benchmarks for the robustness findings and on the differential diagnosis between QA and reasoning. The perplexity results are presented primarily to demonstrate the continuity-through-transition phenomenon, not to make claims about generation quality. However, the paper's language in Section 4.2—"healing significantly restores the next-token prediction ability of all the models to near-unpruned levels"—could be misinterpreted by practitioners as implying that the healed pruned models are suitable drop-in replacements for the original models in generation tasks. The paper would benefit from an explicit caveat that perplexity restoration does not guarantee task-level generation quality, and that generation-focused deployments should validate pruned models on task-specific generative benchmarks. The open questions in Section 5 acknowledge the need for "more comprehensive evals" and ask whether "accuracy on different tasks degrade at different depths," but do not specifically call out generation quality as an underexplored dimension.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces layer pruning not as a compression technique—its traditional role in the efficiency literature—but as a **causal instrument for probing the functional organization of knowledge in large language models**. This is a methodological reframing with substantial consequences. Prior work on structured pruning (Voita et al., 2019; Michel et al., 2019; Sajjad et al., 2023; Men et al., 2024) asked: "How much can we remove before the model breaks?" This paper asks: "What does the pattern of breakage tell us about where different capabilities live?" The distinction matters because it transforms pruning from an engineering optimization into a scientific measurement tool—one that can generate testable hypotheses about depth-wise functional specialization, knowledge localization, and the interaction between pretraining objectives and architectural depth.

The magnitude of this shift is not paradigm-shattering—the paper does not overturn the transformer architecture or propose a new training algorithm—but it is **diagnostically generative** in a way that prior pruning work was not. The sharp phase transition in QA accuracy (Figure 2), the decoupling of perplexity from downstream accuracy through the transition (Figure 3), and the immediate degradation of reasoning tasks (Figure 6) collectively form a pattern that is not explainable by any single-factor model of layer importance. This pattern forces the field to reckon with functional specialization across depth as an empirical fact rather than a vague intuition. Prior depth-dependent studies provided correlational evidence—the logit lens (nostalgebraist, 2020) and tuned lens (Belrose et al., 2023) showed predictions converging in deeper layers, but could not demonstrate that those deeper layers were causally unnecessary. Localization studies (Meng et al., 2022; Dai et al., 2021) identified where facts could be found, not where they were required. This paper's pruning methodology provides the missing causal evidence, and in doing so elevates depth-wise functional analysis from a niche interpretability subfield to a central question in understanding how LLMs work.

The paper also **reconciles a latent tension** between two lines of prior evidence. On one hand, the lens literature suggested deep layers were adding little beyond what middle layers already computed—hinting at redundancy. On the other hand, scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022; Kaplan et al., 2020) demonstrated that deeper models generally perform better, implying that those extra layers must be contributing *something*. The present paper dissolves this tension by showing that both perspectives are correct, but about different capabilities: deep layers contribute to reasoning and next-token prediction quality (hence scaling laws hold), but are largely redundant for factual knowledge retrieval as measured by QA benchmarks (hence the lens convergence). The apparent contradiction arose from evaluating different things—aggregate perplexity vs. task-specific accuracy—and this paper's multi-metric evaluation design makes the reconciliation explicit.

Perhaps most importantly, the paper **redirects research attention** from "how do we prune better?" to "why are deep layers so ineffective for knowledge storage, and what would it take to make them useful?" The finding that a blind "remove the deepest layers" heuristic works nearly as well as the angular-distance-optimized strategy (Figure 5, after healing) implies that the optimization problem of *which* layers to prune is largely solved—the deeper, the better—and the interesting scientific questions lie elsewhere. This shifts the field's focus toward understanding pretraining dynamics, objective function design, and architectural choices that might induce deeper layers to encode more task-relevant knowledge. It also makes certain research directions—such as developing increasingly sophisticated layer-importance metrics for pruning—less attractive, since the simple depth-based heuristic already captures most of the available gain.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Does extended pretraining make deep layers less dispensable?** The paper's central claim—that deep layers are underutilized—is inherently tied to the specific pretraining checkpoints studied. If models were trained for substantially longer (beyond the Chinchilla-optimal point), would the phase transition shift to higher pruning fractions? The paper's angular distance heatmaps (Figure 4) are static snapshots; a dynamic study tracking `d(x^(ℓ), x^(ℓ+n))` across pretraining checkpoints for a single model would reveal whether deep-layer similarity increases (layers become more redundant with more training) or decreases (layers specialize and become less redundant). A strong experiment would take intermediate checkpoints from a publicly documented training run (e.g., Pythia or the OLMo suite), perform the same angular distance analysis and pruning evaluation at each checkpoint, and plot the critical pruning fraction as a function of training tokens. The hypothesis: if deep layers are underutilized due to insufficient training, the critical fraction should increase with more pretraining; if they are inherently limited by the next-token prediction objective or residual stream dynamics, it should asymptote. The paper's open question—"does more training lead to better use of the prunable parameters?"—is directly testable with this design.

**Does knowledge actually localize to shallow layers, or is the phase transition a percolation phenomenon?** The paper's functional specialization hypothesis—shallow layers store knowledge, deep layers enable reasoning—is an interpretation of the pruning results, not a directly verified claim. A causal test would combine layer pruning with **knowledge editing** (e.g., ROME from Meng et al., 2022, or MEMIT): if factual knowledge is concentrated in shallow layers, then editing a fact in those layers should affect model outputs, while the same edit applied to deeper layers should have minimal effect. The complementary experiment would prune deep layers from a model, then apply the same knowledge editing procedure to the pruned and unpruned versions. If the pruned model's knowledge can still be edited in its remaining (shallow) layers, this confirms localization. If editing becomes harder or requires touching different layers, knowledge may have been redistributed during pruning and healing. This would directly test whether the phase transition reflects deletion of localized knowledge (the paper's preferred interpretation) or failure of a distributed representation that happens to survive until ~50% pruning (the redundancy alternative).

**Can architectural interventions during pretraining force deep layers to be more useful, and does this improve downstream performance?** The paper speculates about applying layer dropout (Fan et al., 2019) or early exit (Elhoushi et al., 2024) during pretraining to "induce equal usage of layers." A direct experimental test: pretrain two otherwise-identical transformer models—one with standard pretraining, one with stochastic depth (randomly dropping deep layers during training with some probability, forcing shallow layers to remain robust and deep layers to learn non-redundant representations). Then apply the same pruning-and-evaluation protocol from this paper to both models. The prediction: the stochastic-depth model should show a *later* phase transition in QA accuracy (deep layers are less dispensable because they were forced to be useful during training) and potentially *better* reasoning performance at matched depth. The null result—stochastic depth doesn't change the transition point—would suggest that deep-layer dispensability is an inherent property of the autoregressive objective and residual architecture, not a pretraining artifact. This experiment would be computationally intensive (requiring pretraining from scratch) but would provide the strongest causal evidence for or against the "pretraining underutilization" hypothesis. Using a smaller scale (e.g., 1B parameters, where multiple training runs are feasible) would make this tractable while still testing the mechanism.

**How general is the QA-vs-reasoning dissociation across tasks and model families?** The paper's differential diagnosis rests on two reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, HellaSwag) evaluated on a single model (Llama-2-70B). A systematic replication across: (a) a broader reasoning suite (BIG-Bench Hard, ARC-Challenge for science reasoning, LogiQA for deductive reasoning, MuSiQue for multi-hop reasoning, MATH for advanced mathematics); (b) all seven models studied in this paper; and (c) additional model families (e.g., Gemma, DeepSeek, OLMo) would establish whether the functional specialization pattern is universal or model-family-specific. The key question: does every model show immediate reasoning degradation with any pruning, or do some models (perhaps those trained with more reasoning-heavy data, or with different architectural choices) show a robustness region for reasoning as well? If the pattern is universal, it suggests a fundamental constraint on how the residual stream architecture allocates computation across depth. If it varies, it suggests pretraining data mixture or architectural hyperparameters can control functional specialization, which would be a practically actionable finding. This is a straightforward scaling of the paper's existing experimental protocol with more benchmarks and models—a replication study that would substantially sharpen the paper's conclusions.

**Can healing be improved to restore deleted knowledge, and if not, what is the minimal healing needed to recover fluency?** The paper's healing procedure (QLoRA on C4) restores perplexity but not QA accuracy past the phase transition (Figures 2 vs. 3), demonstrating that generic pretraining data cannot recover deleted factual knowledge. A systematic ablation of healing would address two practical questions. First: *what is the minimum healing budget needed* to restore fluency (perplexity) to within 5% of the unpruned baseline? This could be answered by varying healing steps (500, 1000, 2000, 5000, 10000) and measuring both C4 loss and downstream accuracy. Second: *can healing on task-specific data restore knowledge that generic pretraining data cannot*? If healing is performed on MMLU-like questions (or on a factual corpus like Wikipedia) rather than generic C4, does the QA phase transition shift to higher pruning fractions? This would distinguish between two explanations for the persistence of the phase transition: (a) healing cannot transfer knowledge because the pruned layers' capacity is permanently lost, vs. (b) healing on generic text doesn't provide the right training signal, but task-relevant finetuning could partially compensate. The result has direct practical implications: if task-specific healing helps, a deployment pipeline could include both pruning and domain-adaptive finetuning in a single step.

**What structural properties of the angular distance matrix predict the critical pruning fraction a priori?** The paper observes the phase transition post-hoc but cannot predict it from model properties alone. A predictive model would be practically valuable—it would allow practitioners to estimate safe pruning fractions without running the full pruning-and-evaluation pipeline. Potential predictors: the mean or median angular distance across all layer pairs; the depth at which the cumulative angular distance exceeds some threshold; the curvature of the `ℓ*(n)` curve in the heatmaps (Figure 4); or the rate at which the minimum angular distance `d(x^(ℓ*(n)), x^(ℓ*(n)+n))` increases with block size `n`. A straightforward experiment: compute these statistics for all seven studied models, then fit a regression model predicting the critical pruning fraction (observed from Figure 2) from these statistics. If a strong predictor exists, it provides a stopping criterion for pruning—stop when the angular distance of the optimal block exceeds a threshold. If no predictor works, the phase transition is likely driven by factors not captured by angular distance (e.g., the specific distribution of factual knowledge across layers), which would itself be an informative negative result. This analysis could be performed entirely on the existing data without additional experiments.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient deployment of QA systems.** The most directly actionable finding is that Llama-2-70B can have approximately 40–50% of its deepest layers removed while maintaining MMLU accuracy at the unpruned baseline of ~69% (Figure 2, left panel) and BoolQ at ~85% vs. ~88% baseline (Figure 7, left panel). In a production QA system—such as an internal knowledge-base assistant that answers employee questions by selecting from predefined options—this translates to a model with roughly half the memory footprint and approximately 2× faster inference latency (since the removed layers contribute to both memory and sequential computation). The QLoRA healing adds negligible deployment overhead (the LoRA adapters can be merged into the quantized weights). The caveat is that reasoning-dependent queries will degrade immediately (GSM8K drops from ~55% to ~20% by 30% pruning; Figure 6, center), so the pruned model is suitable only when the query distribution skews heavily toward factual retrieval and away from multi-step inference. A practical deployment would pair the pruned model with a lightweight classifier that routes reasoning-heavy queries to the full unpruned model—using the pruned model for the 80–90% of queries that are knowledge-retrieval in nature.

**On-device or edge deployment of smaller models.** The paper shows that Llama-2-7B and Mistral-7B can lose approximately 25–35% of layers with minimal MMLU degradation after healing (Figure 2, left and right panels). For edge deployment scenarios where model size is constrained by device memory (e.g., 4–8 GB of available RAM on a laptop or phone), reducing a 7B-parameter model's layer count by one-third—from 32 to ~21 layers—reduces the memory footprint proportionally, potentially bringing a previously out-of-reach model into the feasible range. Combined with the 4-bit quantization already used for healing, the total footprint reduction could be substantial. The paper's QLoRA-based protocol runs on a single 40GB A100, meaning the pruning itself is accessible; the resulting model could be deployed on consumer hardware. This is particularly relevant for applications like local document QA, privacy-sensitive knowledge retrieval, or offline assistants where cloud-based models are infeasible. The practical limitation is that generative fluency (perplexity) degrades continuously with pruning—the pruned model will produce slightly worse free-form text even if its multiple-choice accuracy is preserved (Figure 3, right).

**Targeted model compression for benchmark-specific leaderboard performance.** For research groups or companies optimizing for specific benchmark scores—a common practice in the LLM evaluation ecosystem—the paper's findings suggest a counterintuitive strategy: rather than training the largest possible model, train a moderately deep model and then prune it to the optimal depth for the target benchmark. Since the phase transition point varies by model family (45–55% for Llama-2, ~20% for Qwen, ~35% for Mistral; Figure 2), a systematic sweep over model families and pruning fractions could identify the highest benchmark score achievable for a given inference budget. The paper implicitly demonstrates this: the pruned Llama-2-70B at 50% layer removal achieves the same MMLU as the full model at half the inference cost. This is not speculative—the results in Figure 2 are direct evidence that benchmark scores can be preserved under substantial compression for certain model-benchmark pairs. The limitation is that this strategy only works for benchmarks that are QA-like (knowledge-retrieval heavy, low reasoning); for reasoning-heavy leaderboards, pruning provides no benefit and actively harms performance.

**Cheap feasibility testing for expensive fine-tuning pipelines.** When a practitioner plans to fine-tune a large model on a domain-specific QA task (e.g., medical board exams, legal bar exams, customer support ticket classification), the key preliminary question is: does the base model already possess the necessary factual knowledge, or will the fine-tuning need to inject new knowledge? The paper's pruning methodology provides a diagnostic: prune deep layers from the base model before fine-tuning. If the pruned model can still be fine-tuned to achieve near-full accuracy on the target task, the necessary knowledge is in the shallow layers and the fine-tuning primarily needs to teach format and task conventions (which is cheap). If the pruned model cannot reach baseline fine-tuned accuracy, the knowledge was in the deeper layers (or distributed), and the fine-tuning will need to do heavier lifting—potentially requiring more data, more compute, or full finetuning rather than parameter-efficient methods. This diagnostic is cheap (a single A100, 5000 healing steps, and evaluation on the target task) compared to committing to a full fine-tuning pipeline without knowing whether the base model is capable. The paper doesn't perform this experiment directly, but it follows from the logic of the QA robustness result.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not position its pruning strategy against named alternative compression or efficiency methods (distillation, quantization-only, sparse attention, or speculative decoding). Its contribution is primarily scientific rather than an engineering proposal competing with specific alternatives. The choice between the similarity-informed pruning strategy and the simpler "remove deepest layers" heuristic is discussed internally (Section 4.4, Figure 5), but the paper does not articulate a decision rule for preferring its pruning approach over, say, knowledge distillation or simply training a smaller model from scratch. Consequently, a "Prefer A when... Prefer B when..." matrix would be fabricating a comparison the paper does not make.

The paper does provide one clear internal tradeoff: **use similarity-informed pruning when** you have the computational resources to run angular distance computation on 10k samples and want the marginally better accuracy preservation in the flat region (1–3 percentage points on MMLU; Figure 5, right top panel); **use the simple "remove deepest layers" heuristic when** you want to avoid loading or inferring the unpruned model entirely, accepting a slightly lower accuracy plateau in exchange for zero pre-pruning computation. The paper explicitly notes this: "this simpler heuristic algorithm has the advantage of never requiring practitioners to load onto a GPU or inference the unpruned model" (Section 4.4). Given the marginal performance difference after healing, the practical default for most use cases would be the simple heuristic—the paper's data supports this, though the authors stop short of recommending it as the primary method.

Beyond this internal comparison, the paper's framework is better understood as a diagnostic toolkit than as a deployment recipe competing with established efficiency methods. Its most practical contribution is not the pruning algorithm per se—which will likely be supplanted by more sophisticated compression techniques—but the empirical demonstration that deep layers are systematically dispensable for knowledge-intensive tasks, which should inform the design of future efficient architectures and pretraining objectives.
