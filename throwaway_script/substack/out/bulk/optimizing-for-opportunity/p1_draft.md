Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (synthesized study of 11 Meta staff+ engineers and directors on optimizing for opportunity beyond the staff level):

**The growth ceiling: ability vs. opportunity**

Ryan opens by framing the core problem: career growth past the staff level often feels out of reach not because of ability, but because access to the right opportunities becomes the limiting factor. To understand how to solve this, he studied the career paths of 11 people who reached at least Engineering Director or Principal Engineer (IC8+) at Meta. What emerged was a clear set of patterns, but also a critical distinction between managerial and individual-contributor paths—and between product and infrastructure work.

**Engineering directors: riding the mission-critical wave**

The directors Ryan studied shared a near-identical story: they worked on a product that was a top company priority, their scope exploded as the product grew, and they delivered outstanding business impact. Their careers grew as quickly as their teams did. Some intentionally sought out mission-critical projects; others landed there by chance. But in both cases, working on top company priorities gave them access to disproportionate resources. One director, for instance, had direct time with top executives and dedicated executive coaching precisely when she needed it—resources that simply aren't available for lower-priority work.

These directors also invested heavily in building the skills demanded by scale. The skill set they needed changed as their teams grew, and they mastered prioritization and delegation reactively, based on need. One director crystallized a prioritization heuristic that Ryan calls out: **“It’s often more impactful to take something from good to great rather than from poor to passable.”** The insight here is counterintuitive—most people's instinct is to triage the worst thing, but the directors recognized that marginal gains on already-strong areas compound more visibly with leadership and create the perception (and reality) of excellence.

**Product engineers: agency, prototyping, and trust**

The product-focused IC engineers also grew through products that were scaling with tremendous business impact, but they had more agency and direct control over opportunity than their managerial counterparts. Their path was more self-directed: they could pick projects that interested them and use prototyping to realize their ideas before anyone asked them to. One engineer's philosophy captures this:

> **“As engineers, we have the unique ability to just build our ideas. Taking the abstract and making it concrete advances discussion; nothing beats experiencing a product yourself. I’ve always looked for simple ways to prototype sooner.”**

This isn't just a platitude—it's a specific tactic. By making the abstract concrete, these engineers short-circuited debate cycles and built organizational momentum around their ideas. Ryan notes that these engineers optimized for opportunity through a two-part playbook: first, they established trust through past success, which granted them first access to future critical projects; second, they were proactive about working with the most skilled people at the company. One engineer even made a point of following the most talented designer at the company from project to project, recognizing that the quality of collaborators is a force multiplier—and that talented people tend to cluster around impactful work.

**Infrastructure engineers: compounding leverage and the luck surface**

The infrastructure engineers Ryan studied solved high-leverage problems that improved large swaths of the engineering organization. Their work had a compounding effect because improvements to tooling or underlying infrastructure touched every engineer at Meta. Unlike product engineers, whose impact depended on product growth, infrastructure engineers had the most agency in creating opportunities—their impact was decoupled from any single product's trajectory.

Across the board, these engineers championed the maxim **"code wins arguments."** Critically, Ryan clarifies that none of them meant this as a way to solve problems single-handedly. They used it as a consensus-building tool: build the prototype, demonstrate the idea, and let the working code do the persuading. One engineer summarized the broader philosophy: **“If you want to go fast, go alone. If you want to go far, go together.”** The prototype opens the door; collaboration carries it through.

Their strategy for finding opportunity was to put themselves in a position to get lucky. They were proactive in chasing the top problems for the engineering organization—often working on these problems outside their team's scope, in their spare time. They also invested in continuing their engineering education by pairing with domain experts and reading technical books, ensuring that when an opportunity appeared, they had the depth to seize it.

**The three behaviors that increase your odds**

Ryan closes by acknowledging the obvious: all 11 people were lucky to work at an incredibly successful, growing company. Growing companies generate more opportunities because **"a rising tide lifts all boats."** If your current company lacks that growth potential, he suggests considering a switch. But beyond company selection, these people did three things that systematically increased their chances.

First, they sought out business-critical projects that excited them. It wasn't enough for a project to be a top priority—they needed to be genuinely passionate about it. Passion drove higher-quality work and more time invested, which led to better outcomes. The advice: if you're not passionate about what you're working on, or if it isn't critical to your company's success, talk to your manager or consider switching teams.

Second, they were intentional about working with the most talented people they knew. This helped them achieve more through collaboration and increased the chances of future opportunities, since talented people are often working on the most impactful projects. The practical takeaway: factor in the talent of your peers when choosing what to work on and who to collaborate with. It's a dimension most people ignore when evaluating projects.

Third, they all honed their skills relentlessly and delivered high-quality work. Their skills increased the probability of success once the right opportunity presented itself. Two specific tactics: collaborating closely with domain experts and reading **"subject-killing" resources**—the one book or paper that captures most of the critical information in a domain (he cites *High Output Management* for engineering management as an example). And crucially, their track record of polished, high-quality work compounded into access to more opportunities over time. "Always hold a high bar for the quality and polish of your work. It will pay off over time."

Ryan ends with a bracing caveat: extraordinary career growth isn't something most people will achieve even with the necessary ability. Growth at the highest levels requires access to increasingly scarce opportunities, so it's not something you should expect. But you can increase your chances with these behavior changes.
