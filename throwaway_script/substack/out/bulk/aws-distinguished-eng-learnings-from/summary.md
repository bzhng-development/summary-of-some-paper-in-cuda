Here are the takeaways from this Marc Brooker interview (Distinguished Engineer at AWS who started as a new grad, learned from 3,000+ postmortems, and co-built Aurora Serverless and DSQL):

**Finding problems that matter**

Brooker believes the key to a great engineering career is finding the most important problems to work on, and his method is to go "super broad." One rich seam is talking directly to customers—he spends a lot of time with AWS customers listening to where they're still spending time they'd prefer not to. The second seam is bottom-up technical trends: "networks have gotten faster, storage has gotten faster," the explosion in multicore and now GPUs. Both seams collided when he was on the Lambda team in 2020. Customers were excited about serverless and containers, but relational databases remained "very serverful"—operationally mismatched to the paradigm. "These customers have brought to me a gift of understanding something that's really important," so he joined the Aurora team, built Aurora Serverless, then DSQL. The deeper technical trend was block storage becoming the default durability layer for databases. For Aurora DSQL, the insight was making S3—an object store designed 20 years earlier—the underlying durability layer, then building a novel architecture on top to handle latency and interfaces. That collision of customer need and technical trend created "an opportunity to build something now that is going to be a market leading product that would be hard to imagine without either of those input signals."

Ryan asks why Brooker stayed on call for 15 years when many senior engineers negotiate out of it. Brooker's answer is blunt: "the majority of my in-practice knowledge about how to build distributed systems has come from being on call and analyzing and deeply understanding these postmortems and COEs." University graduates arrive with strong CS fundamentals but lack "the grounded knowledge of what it actually means to run and understand systems." On-call reveals how systems really behave, how customers really use them in unexpected ways, and where to build resilience. He's careful to distinguish rote ticket-closing from deep investigation: automation should handle the repetitive work; expert time should be spent on "something unexpected or unusual that's happened in the system." At AWS, this flows into a weekly Wednesday-morning meeting where engineers and senior leaders from across the company discuss COEs (postmortems) and extract cross-cutting lessons. He calls that meeting "one of the things that has been a core, almost causal factor behind AWS's success," because it forces leadership bandwidth and the time of the best engineers onto deeply understanding how systems operate. On-call is also a source of product ideas: "we seem to be solving the same problem over and over in different ways and getting it slightly wrong every time—can we extract a tool to do that?"

**Learnings from 3,000 postmortems**

Brooker estimates he's read between 3,000 and 4,000 industry postmortems and Amazon COEs. A great postmortem, he says, starts with genuinely understanding what happened rather than assuming based on biases. If you can't understand what happened, that teaches you about gaps in logging, metrics, and observability. Then it steps through the whys at multiple levels. The code bug is only the most proximal cause—you can't stop there. Why was it missed in testing? Why was testing where it was? Why did we assume a certain thing about the system? A great postmortem produces concrete action items at different levels: fix the bug, fix the testing processes, fix the social or team processes that shaped those technical processes. When patterns emerge across multiple postmortems, the right response is to build a service, a library, or a community of practice that eliminates a whole class of problems.

He gives a concrete example. A "really common outage pattern" with relational databases is a client starting a transaction, then going out to lunch—a GC pause, a lossy network—while holding locks. Traditional relational databases aren't resilient to misbehaving clients. When designing DSQL, the team explicitly asked: how do we avoid this entire class of problems? The answer was eliminating pessimistic locking entirely. Inside a transaction, all reads use multi-version concurrency control—storing a history of versions for every row so readers never block writers. Writes are spooled locally and checked optimistically at commit time. "There is no way that a reader of a piece of data can block other writers." The storage overhead is "surprisingly small," typically under 10% for online workloads, because write traffic is concentrated—"the vast majority of data isn't changing."

Ryan notes a dichotomy between teams that take postmortem culture seriously and those that don't. Brooker says it comes down to where you want to spend your time: improving the product or "fighting the same fire over and over." He identifies two failure modes. The first is a lack of focus on outcomes, fundamentally a leadership and cultural problem of setting the right standards—and he acknowledges standards should vary: places where durability is critical need super-high standards; elsewhere, intentionally accepting a higher defect rate can be fine. The second, harder mode is **normalization of operational heroics**. The team feels good—strong owners, super engaged, working hard—but from the outside, the organization is spending fantastically expensive human investment on a break-fix cycle instead of fixing root causes. "Let's take this energy of this team, fantastic energy, and focus it on improving the service, getting out of the cycle." Persuading these teams is hard because they genuinely believe they're caring for the business at the highest level, when they're actually caring at the wrong level.

**Why caches are bad**

Caching exploits temporal and spatial locality to make systems faster, but the downside in distributed systems is modality. A cache has a mode where it's full of the right data and the system is fast, and a mode where it's empty or contains the wrong data—and the backend isn't scaled for uncached traffic. This is a **metastable failure**: the system switches to a stable but degraded state and can't recover under its own energy, because the saturated backend can't even refill the cache. Brooker says DSQL's storage tier is "essentially a cache, but it is a complete cache"—it contains every row, so there's no empty mode. Aurora's leader continuously warms failover-target caches. In general, he prefers patterns where you have a complete materialized view of slow-moving data, or you use a scalable backend (DynamoDB, DSQL) and "keep your database vendor honest about getting to the scale and performance you need rather than putting a cache in front of things." Metastable failures aren't super common—"you might go years without seeing something like that"—but he says they've been an underlying cause in probably a majority of the industry's biggest, most impactful postmortems, with larger scale, longer recovery times, and more complex fixes. "It's kind of both of these things of being quite uncommon and being rather common."

**How AI will change software engineering**

Brooker's first belief: software has been supply-constrained throughout its 60-year history, and the opportunity is "almost unbounded." The economics of building software are changing quickly, which creates massive room for more, bigger, and more personal software. Careers will look different. He tells an analogy about analog circuits: digital became the mainstream, but analog persisted in niches like radio and power systems, and some people built good careers there. He sees three tiers emerging. First, the craft of software—like joinery with hand saws—will remain a joyful hobby, "not a particularly economically interesting activity anymore, but not everything we do has to be." Second, a shrinking but real slice of the industry will persist with past techniques, languages, and technologies, because "driving change in the real world is always harder than it looks from the outside." Economic opportunity exists there, as it does in analog electronics today. Third, the mainstream will adopt AI-powered, agentic, specification-driven development at a speed and cost unimaginable with old techniques—"that's where the majority of careers are going to be built."

**Advice for junior engineers given AI**

The task shifts earlier in a career to finding problems that matter—understanding customers, business, economics, and systems. What was once senior-engineer work becomes part of the earliest steps. That excites one set of people and frustrates another "who have come into, you know, looking for a pure software development career"—sitting down, opening their IDE, and typing for eight hours will be "harder and harder to build a career around." Paradoxically, there's also "a ton of opportunity for folks who are extremely technically deep" on optimization, infrastructure, or scientific topics, because the boilerplate that blocked them from leveraging that expertise is vanishing. Ryan points out that junior engineers today are often insulated from customers—tech leads hand out tasks—and the prospect of immediately needing senior-level context scares them. Brooker emphasizes aligned incentives: nobody hires people to set them up to fail. "How do you support people on that path? How do you give them the right guardrails?" He recalls his own first customer meeting being "super scary," but he got mentorship and feedback and improved. "It's never been true that people coming into technical or engineering careers straight out of college know everything." The emphasis on what people learn will shift, and leaders must be thoughtful about the new ladder. Even so, "the scale of the opportunity is bigger than it's ever been."

**Thoughts for senior engineers**

For experienced engineers, the challenge is retaining the value of their career's knowledge while not falling behind. Brooker is blunt: "you kind of got to get building, you got to get back in." Hands-on understanding of how the practice of building and designing software has changed is non-negotiable. The good news is that time spent as a practitioner is vastly more leveraged because tools eliminate boilerplate. He also appeals to motivation: "I didn't get into this so I could go to meetings and sound smart. I got into it because I love learning and because I love building technology." It's "really obvious" to him when speaking with practitioners who does and doesn't use modern agentic development practices. Those who do have nuanced insight; those who don't have "such a poor mental model of how they work… the things they say about them tend to be essentially fiction." His warning: **"If you aren't doing it hands on, your opinion about it is very likely to be completely wrong."** That takes humility, especially for people with fancy titles. He points to the builders of S3 two decades ago as the model—they were deep in the details, grounded in use cases, and deep in economics. "That groundedness in the details from early on, from all levels of leadership" is what made S3 and EC2 so enduringly successful.

**Why engineers should write**

Writing is an "incredible multiplier" that lets you scale technical ideas in space and time in a way one-on-one mentorship or even building a product cannot. A blog post from a decade ago can still be shared with someone. Culturally at Amazon, writing forces a level of mental clarity that speaking and slide decks don't; Brooker sometimes writes docs just for himself, never intending to share them, purely to sharpen his own thinking. Ryan notes that many engineers hate writing docs, seeing it as a sign of slow big-company processes. Brooker says it depends on the level of problem. He picks on UML as an example of documentation at "the wrong semantic level." What separates valuable writing from busywork is understanding the purpose: an artifact for future teams, a way to think through a hard one-way-door decision, or external communication. He doesn't write a doc for every decision—only key ones where the cost of getting it wrong is high. He also stresses a subtler value: documenting which decisions were carefully chosen with deep thought versus which were arbitrary guesses made without data. For people improving a system later, knowing whether a design choice was intentional or "the best thing we could guess in the moment" is almost as important as knowing what the choice was, because it signals which things can be changed freely with better data and which require engaging with the original reasoning.

**Visibility and apparent expertise**

Brooker once wrote about a 2×2 matrix with axes doing-vs-discussing and hobby-vs-gear, illustrating a trade-off between expertise and visibility. Someone purely on the doing side has deep expertise but low visibility; someone purely on the discussing/gear side has "apparent competence" but shallow knowledge. Ryan asks how to strike the balance. Brooker says 100% on either end is a failure mode. He personally enjoys working more with the pure-doers, but they'd have more impact if they swung toward communication; the pure-talkers would have more relevant things to say if they swung back. There's also a risk in pure doing: "if your head's down in your IDE all day, you could very likely be working on the wrong thing." His own sweet spot is about **75/25 practitioner-to-communicator**, though he's seen effective people anywhere from 90/10 to 50/50—outside that range, people tend to get into trouble as practitioners. In a moment of rapid change, swinging a bit more toward the practitioner side helps. Ryan asks whether it's better to be underrated or overrated. "Long term, it's probably better to be underrated," Brooker says. Being overrated can feel great in the moment but is rarely sustainable. He draws an analogy to sports and crafts: "you can't fool the world for very long."

**AWS engineers he admires**

Brooker names Al Vermeulen, an early S3 contributor and former Amazon CTO who stepped back from that role. He admired Vermeulen's ability to work at every level—deeply arguing edge cases in the Paxos paper one moment, discussing cloud strategy and executive-level messaging the next. "Wow, this is something I aspire to and want to model my own career after." He also notes that many of these admired engineers "don't want to be celebrities."

**Technical book recommendations**

For distributed systems, Martin Kleppmann's book ("I think there's a second edition coming out soon"), the new edition of *Quantitative Systems Design*, and Hennessy and Patterson's *Computer Architecture*. Brooker mostly reads papers for technical depth, and notes that AI tools make papers far more accessible—"hey, Claude, summarize this for me and then I can dive into it and read the author's words." He also recommends not ignoring old work: some traffic-management algorithms used in Lambda come from Erlang's work on telephone call centers a century ago. "Folks also shouldn't think that, oh well, the industry is changing super fast" and dismiss foundational knowledge—deeply understanding those topics is more leveraged than ever.

**Advice for his younger self**

"Be a little bit bolder." He loved his early teams in EC2 and EBS, but was more hesitant than optimal about leaving when his learning and impact tapered off. He's changed organizations in a big way four times in his career, and thinks five or six would have been optimal—"not a lot more, but some more." His advice: constantly ask what you're learning, who you're learning from, and whether there's a better environment to learn more quickly. He's personally motivated by following his curiosity, and every time he's done that, it's been a valuable move.

---

Brooker's entire career philosophy orbits a single gravitational center: **finding problems that matter**—by triangulating customer pain with bottom-up technical trends, then building at their collision point. That instinct explains both his 15-year commitment to on-call (operational reality as the richest problem-seam) and his three-tier model of software's AI-driven future (where the craft tier, the legacy tier, and the agentic mainstream each offer distinct paths). His **75/25 practitioner-to-communicator** sweet spot and his warning that **"if you aren't doing it hands on, your opinion about it is very likely to be completely wrong"** are two expressions of the same discipline: the groundedness he admired in the S3 builders and practices himself through writing, deep postmortem analysis, and staying on call. The metastable-failure pattern he diagnoses in caches—a system switching to a stable degraded state it can't escape under its own energy—is a useful metaphor for the career risk he warns senior engineers about: getting locked into an influence-without-practice mode that feels sustainable until it isn't.
