# Sparsing Law: Towards Large Language Models with Greater Activation Sparsity

**ArXiv:** [2411.02335](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02335)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper empirically studies the scaling behavior of activation sparsity in large language models, analyzing how the ratio of weakly-contributed neurons in feed-forward networks evolves across varying architectures, activation functions, and training data volumes on models from 0.1B to 2.4B parameters.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper empirically studies the scaling behavior of activation sparsity in large language models, analyzing how the ratio of weakly-contributed neurons in feed-forward networks evolves across varying architectures, activation functions, and training data volumes on models from 0.1B to 2.4B parameters. The core contribution is a performance-friendly sparsity measurement framework centered on **CETT-PPL-1%**—a metric that binary-searches the cumulative error of tail truncation (CETT) hyperparameter such that perplexity increases by only 1% over the dense baseline (e.g., reducing activation ratio by 61% relative to a naïve zero-threshold ReLU metric)—and the empirical discovery of convergent power-law relationships between activation ratio and pre-training data amount: a decreasing logspace power-law for ReLU-activated models (driving sparsity higher with more data) and an increasing vanilla power-law for SiLU-activated models. Under similar width-depth ratios, ReLU activation yields substantially greater sparsity than SiLU at comparable performance, deeper-and-thinner architectures produce sparser models below a bottleneck width-depth ratio, and the limit sparsity approached with infinite data is largely insensitive to parameter scale (e.g., a 2.4B model achieves a limit sparsity ratio of 93.52% with a 4.1× decoding speedup over its dense counterpart). The empirical laws establish that activation sparsity can be systematically engineered through architecture and data choices, but the benefits are constrained to the base model's convergence trajectory—hard problems requiring fundamental capability beyond the model's pass@1 remain unsolved regardless of sparsity.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don't Know How to Measure or Scale Activation Sparsity Systematically

The fundamental question this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **activation sparsity exists in LLMs—so how do we measure it properly, what controls it, and how do we build models to maximize it?** This matters because activation sparsity—the observation that a large fraction of neurons in a model's feed-forward networks contribute negligibly to the output for any given input—offers a compelling efficiency lever: if you can identify and skip those weakly-contributed neurons at inference time, you save FLOPs without changing the model's output. But prior to this work, the field lacked any systematic empirical framework for connecting the design of a model (its architecture, activation function, training data scale) to how sparsely it would activate after training.

This gap is significant for several practical reasons the paper identifies (Section 1, Introduction):

- **Inference acceleration:** Exploiting activation sparsity has already yielded dramatic speedups—Xue et al. (2024) achieved up to 27.8× inference acceleration on smartphones. But these systems treat sparsity as a *property to be discovered* in existing models rather than *a target to be engineered* during training.
- **Training acceleration:** Zhang et al. (2024b) showed that activation sparsity persists through most of pre-training, enabling faster training by skipping computation on weakly-contributed neurons. Knowing how sparsity evolves would let practitioners predict and plan around these savings.
- **Model interpretability:** Sparse activation patterns have become a primary lens for understanding what individual neurons do—identifying specialized "modules" for different functions (Zhang et al., 2023), explaining model behaviors (Sajjad et al., 2022), and even producing natural-language descriptions of neurons (Bills et al., 2023). But interpretability work relies on the *assumption* that sparsity reflects meaningful specialization, which hasn't been quantitatively substantiated across scales.
- **Resource allocation during pre-training:** If sparsity follows predictable scaling laws (analogous to loss scaling laws), organizations planning large training runs could forecast how sparse their model will become, estimate inference savings, and make principled architectural choices before committing compute.

### A Landscape of Scattered Observations, Not a Unified Understanding

The paper is motivated by a genuine fragmentation in the literature. Activation sparsity has been *observed* widely—across language models (Li et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022), vision Transformers, T5 architectures (Raffel et al., 2020), GPT-style models (Brown et al., 2020), and LLaMA-style models (Touvron et al., 2023). But these observations are largely qualitative, use incompatible measurement methods, and draw on different model families at different scales. The result is a collection of anecdotes rather than a coherent picture.

Consider the specific gaps the paper identifies:

**No standard, generalizable sparsity metric exists.** The straightforward approach—count neurons with zero activation (for ReLU) or below some fixed threshold—fails catastrophically for activation functions like SiLU that produce non-negligible negative outputs (Elfwing et al., 2018). Using absolute values with a global threshold helps, but choosing that threshold is arbitrary and the relationship between the threshold and model performance is opaque. The CETT method (Zhang et al., 2024a) addresses this by adaptively setting layer-wise thresholds based on output error, but leaves open the question of *which* error tolerance to use—a hyperparameter choice that directly controls the sparsity-performance tradeoff and has no principled default. Section 4 of the paper addresses this directly, showing that different threshold choices produce wildly different sparsity estimates for the same model (Figure 2).

**Existing metrics conflate sparsity measurement with task-specific performance in unpredictable ways.** As the paper demonstrates in Table 1 and Figures 2–3, the same sparsity ratio can mean very different things depending on *how* weakly-contributed neurons are identified. Top-k selection (used in MoE architectures; Fedus et al., 2022) forces a constant sparsity ratio per layer but ignores that some layers are naturally denser than others. FAT-ϵ with a global threshold applies a one-size-fits-all criterion to layers that may have different activation value distributions. CETT partially solves this by making the threshold layer-adaptive, but until this paper, no one had connected the choice of CETT hyperparameter to downstream task performance to establish a reliable, performance-friendly metric.

**The relationship between training data and sparsity is completely uncharacterized.** Prior work had observed that sparsity tends to emerge during training (Li et al., 2022), but no one had asked: does sparsity increase monotonically with data? Does it converge? Does the relationship depend on architecture? The paper's discovery that ReLU models follow a decreasing logspace power-law while SiLU models follow an increasing power-law (Section 5.1, Figure 4) is not just a curiosity—it means that choosing SiLU over ReLU puts you on a trajectory where *more training data makes your model denser*, fundamentally altering the economics of large-scale training.

**Architectural choices beyond activation function are unexplored.** While prior work (Zhang et al., 2024a) had compared activation functions for sparsity, no systematic study examined how the width-depth ratio—one of the most fundamental architectural decisions in Transformer design—shapes sparsity. The paper's finding of a linear relationship below a bottleneck point (Section 5.2, Figure 5) has direct practical implications: for a given parameter budget, deeper-and-thinner models are substantially sparser, but only up to the point where training becomes unstable.

**Scale dependence is unknown.** Do larger models activate more sparsely? Less sparsely? Converge to the same limit? Li et al. (2022) observed an increasing trend in T5 models, but as the paper notes in Section 5.3, this finding is specific to that architecture and measurement method. The paper's observation of *scale insensitivity* in the limit sparsity ratio (Figure 7) contradicts the natural intuition that larger models would be sparser (or denser), and the proposed explanation—similar neuron specialization patterns across scales, with smaller models converging faster because they have fewer neurons to assign to functional groups (Section 5.3, "Deduction")—opens a new theoretical connection between sparsity dynamics and the combinatorics of neuron specialization.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations in prior approaches along several axes:

**MoE enforces sparsity structurally but sacrifices flexibility and performance.** Mixture-of-experts architectures achieve high activation sparsity by design—each token only activates a fixed top-k subset of experts (Fedus et al., 2022; Zoph et al., 2022). But this rigidity comes at a cost: the model can't adapt its sparsity ratio to the input's difficulty, and recent work has documented performance degradation from inflexible expert assignment (Huang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024b). The paper demonstrates this concretely in Appendix B (Figure 12): MoE models with varying numbers of experts have a substantially worse PPL-vs-sparsity Pareto frontier than a vanilla ReLU Transformer, where sparsity emerges naturally from training dynamics rather than being hard-coded. Moreover, choosing the right sparsity level for MoE requires guessing before training—too low wastes compute, too high degrades performance—whereas the paper's framework reveals what sparsity the training process will naturally produce.

**Weight pruning is static and fragile.** Pruning removes parameters permanently (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023; Xia et al., 2023), meaning the same neurons are dropped for every input. This static sparsity is fundamentally less expressive than activation sparsity, which can use different neurons for different inputs. At the same effective sparsity level, the paper argues that activation sparsity causes "considerably less performance degradation" (Appendix C) because it preserves the model's full capacity—just not all at once on any single input.

**Prior sparsity metrics are either non-generalizable or uncalibrated to performance.** The straightforward zero-threshold ReLU metric (Section 4.1, "Straightforward ReLU") is widely used but only works for ReLU—it can't handle SiLU, GeLU, or other activations with meaningful negative outputs. FAT-ϵ (Kurtz et al., 2020) extends this with a global threshold but requires manually choosing ϵ, with no guidance on what value keeps performance intact. CATS (Lee et al., 2024) enforces equal sparsity per layer, which the paper shows is suboptimal (Figure 2: CETT outperforms Top-k at all sparsity ratios). TEAL (Liu et al., 2024a) addresses input sparsity rather than output sparsity, targeting a different phenomenon. None of these prior metrics established a systematic connection between the measurement hyperparameter and downstream task performance—the paper's CETT-PPL-1% directly fills this gap by tying the CETT tolerance to a 1% PPL degradation, which Table 1 shows is negligible for both commonsense reasoning and reading comprehension.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution explicitly around three research questions (Section 1): measurement (Q1), influential factors (Q2), and practical guidance for building sparse models (Q3). This structure is not just organizational—it reflects a deliberate progression from establishing a reliable observational instrument to using that instrument to discover empirical laws to applying those laws in practice.

**On measurement (Q1):** The paper positions CETT-PPL-1% as a *calibrated engineering tool* rather than a theoretical breakthrough. The novelty is not CETT per se (which comes from Zhang et al., 2024a) but rather the introduction of the PPL increase tolerance as a principled way to choose the CETT hyperparameter, combined with the empirical validation that 1% PPL degradation is performance-neutral on downstream tasks. This matters because it makes sparsity a *reproducible measurement* across models, activations, and scales—a prerequisite for any scaling law analysis. Without such a metric, comparing sparsity between a ReLU model and a SiLU model would be comparing apples to oranges, since the mapping from threshold to performance differs between activation functions.

**On influential factors (Q2):** The paper positions its empirical laws—the convergent power-laws for activation ratio vs. data (Section 5.1), the linear width-depth relationship (Section 5.2), the scale insensitivity of limit sparsity (Section 5.3)—as *analogous to neural scaling laws* (Hoffmann et al., 2022) but for a property that scaling laws typically ignore. Just as Chinchilla scaling laws tell you how to allocate compute between model size and data to minimize loss, the "sparsing laws" in this paper tell you how architecture and data choices determine sparsity, enabling practitioners to forecast sparsity before training and make design decisions accordingly. The paper explicitly draws this parallel in Section 5.1 by fitting parametric forms to the activation-data curves and extracting limit values—the same methodology that underlies loss scaling laws.

**On building sparse models (Q3):** The paper positions its practical recommendations (use ReLU, deeper-than-wider architecture, more training data) as *validated on an independent 2.4B model that was not part of the scaling law fitting*. This out-of-sample validation is crucial: the 2.4B model follows the same logspace power-law trend (Figure 11), achieves a limit sparsity ratio (93.52%) close to the 0.1B–1.2B models (consistent with scale insensitivity), and demonstrates real-world acceleration (4.1× speedup via PowerInfer). This transforms the empirical laws from descriptive to predictive.

The paper also positions activation sparsity as fundamentally different from—and superior to—the dominant paradigm of MoE for achieving sparsity (Appendix B, Figure 12). Where MoE imposes sparsity structurally (potentially harming performance and flexibility), the paper advocates for letting sparsity emerge naturally from training dynamics with the right architectural choices. This is a philosophical stance: sparsity should be a *property of the trained model*, not a *constraint on the architecture*.

### What This Paper Does Not Address

The paper is transparent about scope limitations (Appendix A): it does not account for the computational cost of different width-depth ratios (deeper models train more slowly due to serial dependencies, potentially offsetting inference gains from sparsity), the sensitivity of sparsity measurements to data distribution (different validation sets may yield different sparsity estimates for the same model), or the interaction between data mixing ratios and sparsity (different proportions of code, math, and natural language in pre-training data may shift the sparsity trajectory). These are explicitly flagged as future work, not as flaws—the paper establishes the foundational measurement framework and empirical laws, leaving these extensions for subsequent investigation.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper is primarily an **empirical measurement study** whose core idea is that activation sparsity in LLMs can be rigorously quantified using a performance-calibrated metric, and that this metric reveals predictable, law-like relationships between sparsity and controllable factors—activation function, architecture shape, training data volume, and parameter scale—that enable practitioners to engineer sparsity into models from scratch. The "system" being studied is not a deployed application but rather a **measurement and analysis pipeline**: a method for identifying which neurons in a trained model are weakly-contributed, a protocol for setting the threshold such that downstream task performance is preserved, and a curve-fitting framework that extracts the functional form and limit behavior of sparsity as training data increases. The problem it solves is the lack of a reproducible, generalizable way to talk about activation sparsity across different models, architectures, and activation functions—without such a metric, comparing sparsity between a ReLU model and a SiLU model is meaningless because the same thresholding strategy produces different performance impacts. The solution is two-fold: first, establish CETT-PPL-1% as a principled metric (binary-searching the CETT error tolerance until validation perplexity increases by exactly 1%, which Table 1 shows is performance-neutral on downstream tasks), and second, use this metric to trace how sparsity evolves during training and extract convergent power-law relationships that predict the sparsity a model will achieve given its design and data budget.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The measurement and analysis pipeline has five major components, applied to pre-trained model checkpoints spanning 0.1B to 1.2B parameters (with a 2.4B validation model trained separately):

1. **Pre-trained LLM checkpoints** — a collection of µP Transformer models (Hu et al., 2024) with LLaMA-style gated FFNs (Dauphin et al., 2017; Shazeer, 2020), saved at intervals throughout pre-training. These are the objects being *measured*, not constructed by the paper. Each model variant differs in activation function (ReLU vs. SiLU), parameter scale (0.1B, 0.2B, 0.4B, 0.8B, 1.2B, plus one 2.4B), or width-depth ratio (swept from approximately 48 to 600 for the 0.1B ReLU model to isolate architectural effects).

2. **Weakly-contributed neuron recognition algorithm** — a procedure that, given an FFN layer's activations on a batch of input tokens, identifies which neurons can be skipped with minimal impact on the layer's output. This is the core of the sparsity metric; the paper compares four methods (Straightforward ReLU, Top-k, FAT-ϵ, CETT) and selects CETT (cumulative errors of tail truncation) as the most accurate, but adds a performance-calibration layer on top.

3. **CETT-PPL-p% calibration protocol** — a binary search procedure that finds the CETT hyperparameter (the shared L2-norm relative error tolerance across all layers) such that the model's perplexity on a held-out validation set increases by exactly `p%` relative to the dense baseline (all neurons active). The paper uses `p = 1%` as the standard setting after validating that this PPL degradation is negligible for downstream tasks (Table 1: average commonsense reasoning score drops by only 0.16 points, reading comprehension by 0.30 points across all model scales).

4. **Sparsity-data curve fitting framework** — a post-hoc analysis that takes the activation ratio (1 − sparsity ratio) measured at each checkpoint via CETT-PPL-1%, plots it against the cumulative number of training tokens passed, and fits parametric curves (a decreasing logspace power-law for ReLU models, an increasing vanilla power-law for SiLU models) using the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm (Marquardt, 1963). The fitted curves yield limit values (the activation ratio as data → ∞) and convergence rates (how quickly the model approaches that limit).

5. **Scale- and architecture-comparison framework** — a set of controlled experiments that vary one factor at a time (activation function, width-depth ratio, parameter scale) while holding others constant, using the fitted limit values and convergence dynamics to isolate how each factor influences sparsity. This includes both the main 0.1B–1.2B scaling series and the out-of-sample 2.4B validation model.

Information flows as follows: a sequence of pre-training checkpoints enters the pipeline → for each checkpoint, a candidate CETT hyperparameter is chosen → the model's FFN outputs are computed with weakly-contributed neurons skipped according to that CETT value → validation perplexity is measured → binary search adjusts the CETT value until PPL rises by exactly 1% relative to dense → the resulting sparsity ratio is recorded for that checkpoint → after processing all checkpoints, the sparsity-vs-data curve is fitted to a parametric form → limit values and coefficients are extracted → comparisons across model variants reveal empirical laws.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal neuron decomposition of gated FFNs (Equation 2), which defines what a "neuron" is in this architecture and how its contribution to the layer output is computed—this is the mathematical substrate on which all sparsity metrics operate.
- **Second**, the four weakly-contributed neuron recognition methods compared in the paper (Straightforward ReLU, Top-k, FAT-ϵ, CETT), including their mathematical definitions, their hyperparameters, and their failure modes, because understanding why CETT is selected requires seeing what the alternatives get wrong.
- **Third**, the CETT-PPL-p% calibration protocol in full detail—the binary search algorithm (Algorithm 1), the stabilization strategy that averages over the last five checkpoints to reduce stochastic noise (Appendix E), the validation of p = 1% as performance-neutral (Table 1), and the monotonicity property that makes binary search valid (Figure 15).
- **Fourth**, the model architecture and training configuration that generates the checkpoints being measured—the µP Transformer specification, the hyperparameter policies, the pre-training data mixture, and the decay stage for downstream evaluation—since these are the controlled variables in the scaling law experiments.
- **Fifth**, the curve-fitting methodology for the sparsity-data relationship—the parametric forms (Equations 4 and 5), the fitting algorithm, the normalization of data volume, and the interpretation of coefficients—since these fitted laws are the paper's primary empirical contribution.
- **Sixth**, the scale-insensitivity analysis framework—the neuron specialization model (Equation 6), the dataset-wise and token-wise activation frequency analyses (Figures 9 and 10), and the combinatorial argument for why smaller models converge faster—since this explains the otherwise-surprising observation that limit sparsity does not depend on model size.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical measurement and scaling-law paper**. Its technical contribution is not a new model architecture or training algorithm, but rather: (1) a principled protocol for measuring activation sparsity that is generalizable across activation functions and calibrated to downstream performance, and (2) a systematic application of that protocol to discover empirical relationships between sparsity and controllable design factors. The following subsections unpack each component in the order information flows through the pipeline.

---

#### Neuron Decomposition of Gated Feed-Forward Networks

All sparsity metrics in this paper operate on a specific mathematical decomposition of the FFN computation. Understanding this decomposition is necessary because the definition of "weakly-contributed neuron" depends on it.

The paper uses a gated FFN architecture (Dauphin et al., 2017; Shazeer, 2020), which is the standard FFN in LLaMA-style Transformers (Touvron et al., 2023). With input hidden states `$x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$` (where `$d_h$` is the hidden dimension), and intermediate dimension `$d_f$`, the FFN computes:

$$s = \sigma(W_{\text{gate}} x)$$
$$\text{FFN}(x) = W_{\text{out}}[s \odot (W_{\text{in}} x)]$$

where `$W_{\text{gate}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_f \times d_h}$` and `$W_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_f \times d_h}$` are input projection matrices, `$W_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_f}$` is the output projection matrix, `$\sigma$` is the activation function (ReLU or SiLU in this paper), `$s \in \mathbb{R}^{d_f}$` are the activation values, and `$\odot$` is element-wise multiplication.

**What this does operationally:** The input hidden state `$x$` is projected through two separate linear maps. One projection (`$W_{\text{in}} x$`) produces a candidate value for each intermediate neuron; the other (`$W_{\text{gate}} x$`) goes through the activation function `$\sigma$` to produce a gating weight `$s_i$` for each neuron. The neuron's contribution to the output is the gating weight times the candidate value, projected back to the hidden dimension via the `$i$`-th column of `$W_{\text{out}}$`. The final FFN output is the sum of all neuron contributions.

The critical step for sparsity is decomposing this computation along the intermediate dimension `$d_f$` into `$d_f$` individual neurons. For the `$i$`-th neuron (where `$i \in \{1, \ldots, d_f\}$`):

$$s_i = \sigma(W_{\text{gate}}^{i,:} x)$$
$$n_i = W_{\text{out}}^{:,i} [s_i \odot (W_{\text{in}}^{i,:} x)]$$
$$\text{FFN}(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_f} n_i$$

where `$W_{\text{gate}}^{i,:}$` is the `$i$`-th row of `$W_{\text{gate}}$`, `$W_{\text{in}}^{i,:}$` is the `$i$`-th row of `$W_{\text{in}}$`, `$W_{\text{out}}^{:,i}$` is the `$i$`-th column of `$W_{\text{out}}$`, and `$n_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$` is the output vector contributed by neuron `$i$`.

**What this decomposition computes:** Instead of viewing the FFN as one monolithic matrix multiplication, it views it as `$d_f$` parallel computation paths, each producing an output vector `$n_i$`. A neuron `$i$` is "weakly-contributed" if `$n_i$` has negligible magnitude—i.e., removing it from the sum `$\sum_{i=1}^{d_f} n_i$` changes the FFN output by a negligible amount. The sparsity ratio is `$|\mathcal{D}| / d_f$`, where `$\mathcal{D}$` is the set of weakly-contributed neuron indices for a given input.

**Why this decomposition matters for sparsity:** It converts the abstract notion of "sparse activations" into a concrete, vector-level criterion: a neuron is weak if its *output vector* `$n_i$` (not just its scalar activation `$s_i$`) has small L2 norm relative to the total FFN output. This is more accurate than thresholding `$s_i$` alone because a neuron with a small but non-zero `$s_i$` could still contribute meaningfully if `$W_{\text{in}}^{i,:} x$` is large, or a neuron with `$s_i = 0$` could be doing nothing regardless of `$W_{\text{in}}^{i,:} x$`—the vector-level criterion captures both cases correctly.

#### Weakly-Contributed Neuron Recognition Methods

Given the neuron decomposition above, different sparsity metrics differ in how they determine the set `$\mathcal{D}$` of weakly-contributed neurons. The paper compares four methods (Section 4.1), with figures showing PPL-vs-sparsity Pareto curves (Figure 2) and downstream-task-vs-sparsity Pareto curves (Figure 3) for each method on both ReLU and SiLU models at 0.1B and 0.8B scales.

**Method 1: Straightforward ReLU**

$$\mathcal{D} = \{i \mid s_i = 0\}$$

**What it does:** It marks a neuron as weakly-contributed if and only if its scalar activation value `$s_i$` (the output of the activation function `$\sigma$` applied to `$W_{\text{gate}}^{i,:} x$`) is exactly zero.

**Why it is limited:** This method only makes sense for ReLU (`$\sigma(x) = \max(0, x)$`), which produces exact zeros for negative inputs. For SiLU (`$\sigma(x) = x \cdot \text{sigmoid}(x)$`), the activation is never exactly zero (except at the single point `$x = 0$`), and negative activations have non-negligible magnitude—the SiLU function asymptotically approaches zero as `$x \to -\infty$` but also produces substantial negative values in the range `$x \in (-2, 0)$`. Applying the Straightforward ReLU metric to SiLU would classify almost all neurons as "strongly-contributed" even when many contribute negligibly, making the metric non-generalizable. The paper shows this concretely in Figure 2: the Straightforward ReLU data point (red star) appears only on the ReLU panels, not the SiLU panels, because the method is undefined for SiLU.

**Method 2: Top-k**

$$|\mathcal{D}| = d_f - k$$

where `$k$` is a fixed number of neurons to retain per layer, and the retained neurons are those with the `$k$` largest absolute activation values `$|s_i|$`.

**What it does:** For each layer independently, it sorts all `$d_f$` neurons by `$|s_i|$` and marks the bottom `$d_f - k$` as weakly-contributed, regardless of their actual magnitudes. This enforces a constant sparsity ratio `$(d_f - k) / d_f$` across all layers.

**Why it is suboptimal:** It assumes all layers should have the same sparsity ratio, which empirical evidence contradicts—some layers naturally activate more neurons than others for the same input. On the PPL-vs-sparsity Pareto curves (Figure 2, orange lines across all four panels), Top-k consistently lies *above* (worse PPL at the same sparsity) the CETT curve for ReLU models, and substantially above for SiLU models, especially at high sparsity ratios (low activation ratios). This means that for a given sparsity budget, Top-k discards more genuinely useful neurons than a method that adapts thresholds per layer. Top-k is the method used in MoE architectures (Fedus et al., 2022), which the paper argues imposes inflexible constraints that harm the performance-sparsity tradeoff (Appendix B, Figure 12).

**Method 3: FAT-ϵ (Forced Activation Threshold)**

$$\mathcal{D} = \{i \mid |s_i| < \epsilon\}$$

where `$\epsilon > 0$` is a global threshold shared across all layers.

**What it does:** It uses the absolute activation value `$|s_i|$` with a single global threshold `$\epsilon$`. A neuron is weakly-contributed if its absolute activation falls below `$\epsilon$`, regardless of which layer it is in.

**Why it is suboptimal:** Using a single global threshold ignores that different layers can have different scales of activation values. A neuron with `$|s_i| = 0.01$` might be genuinely negligible in a layer where typical activation values are around 1.0, but meaningfully above the noise floor in a layer where typical values are around 0.001. FAT-ϵ's PPL-vs-sparsity curve (Figure 2, green lines) performs better than Top-k but worse than CETT, especially for SiLU models where activation value distributions vary more across layers due to the activation function's shape. There is also no principled way to choose `$\epsilon$`—it is a free hyperparameter with no connection to model performance.

**Method 4: CETT (Cumulative Errors of Tail Truncation)**

$$\mathcal{D} = \{i \mid \|n_i\|_2 < \epsilon\}$$
$$\text{CETT} = \frac{\|\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}} n_i\|_2}{\|\text{FFN}(x)\|_2}$$

where `$\| \cdot \|_2$` is the L2 norm, `$n_i$` is the output vector of neuron `$i$` (not just the scalar activation `$s_i$`), and `$\epsilon$` is a per-layer threshold that is *adaptively searched* to achieve a target CETT value shared across layers.

**What it computes in operational English:** For a given candidate threshold `$\epsilon$`, we identify all neurons whose output vector `$n_i$` has L2 norm below `$\epsilon$`. We sum those neurons' output vectors to get the total error vector introduced by skipping them. We then compute the L2 norm of that error vector divided by the L2 norm of the full FFN output—this is the CETT value, representing the relative error caused by skipping the weakly-contributed neurons. Because CETT increases monotonically with `$\epsilon$` (Figure 15 demonstrates this empirically: both PPL and activation ratio change monotonically with the CETT hyperparameter), we can binary-search `$\epsilon$` per layer to achieve a target CETT value that is the same for all layers.

**The per-layer adaptivity is the key innovation over FAT-ϵ.** Rather than a single global threshold on scalar activations, CETT sets the threshold based on the *actual vector-level impact* of skipping neurons. A layer where all neurons contribute similar magnitudes will have a small `$\epsilon$` (to avoid removing too much signal); a layer where a few neurons dominate and many are truly negligible will have a larger `$\epsilon$` (since the error from skipping negligible neurons is small relative to the dominant ones). This adaptivity is what makes CETT the best-performing method in both Figure 2 (lowest PPL at any given sparsity ratio) and Figure 3 (highest or comparable downstream task performance at any given sparsity ratio).

**Why CETT outperforms the alternatives:** The alternatives all use scalar activations `$s_i$` as a proxy for neuron importance, but `$s_i$` is only the gating weight—it does not capture the magnitude of `$W_{\text{in}}^{i,:}x$` or the output projection `$W_{\text{out}}^{:,i}$`. A neuron with small `$|s_i|$` but large `$\|W_{\text{in}}^{i,:} x\|$` and large output projection weights could still be important; a neuron with moderate `$|s_i|$` but very small output weights could be negligible. CETT uses the full output vector `$n_i$`, which incorporates all three factors (gating weight, input projection magnitude, output projection magnitude), making it a strictly more accurate importance measure. Furthermore, by operating on the L2 norm of the *aggregated* error (sum of all skipped neuron vectors, not just the sum of their individual norms), CETT accounts for the fact that errors from different skipped neurons may partially cancel—a subtlety that methods operating on scalar per-neuron thresholds cannot capture.

#### CETT-PPL-p% Calibration Protocol

Having established CETT as the best neuron recognition method, the remaining challenge is choosing the shared CETT value—the target relative error tolerance that controls how aggressively neurons are skipped. This is a hyperparameter that directly controls the sparsity-performance tradeoff: lower CETT means fewer neurons skipped, higher performance, lower sparsity; higher CETT means more neurons skipped, potentially lower performance, higher sparsity. The paper's innovation is to *tie this hyperparameter to a specific, measurable performance degradation* rather than treating it as an arbitrary knob.

**The CETT-PPL-p% metric (Section 4.2):**

Given a PPL increase tolerance `$p\%$`, the CETT-PPL-p% sparsity is the sparsity ratio measured by CETT when the CETT hyperparameter is set such that the model's perplexity on a validation dataset increases by exactly `$p\%$` compared to the dense setting (where all neurons are activated).

**Binary search algorithm (Algorithm 1 in Appendix F):** This is a concrete procedure that finds the CETT value satisfying the PPL increase constraint.

```
Input: list of checkpoints CkptList, validation dataset VS, 
       PPL increase tolerance p%, error tolerance eps
Output: CETT hyperparameter value

low = 0, high = 1
while high - low > eps:
    mid = (low + high) / 2
    for each checkpoint in CkptList:
        loss_dense = dense_forward(checkpoint, VS)
        loss_sparse = sparse_forward(checkpoint, VS, cett=mid)
        PPL_ratio = exp(loss_sparse - loss_dense)
    MeanPPLRatio = average of PPL_ratio across checkpoints
    if MeanPPLRatio < 1 + p%:
        low = mid
    else:
        high = mid
return (low + high) / 2
```

**What this algorithm does operationally:** It starts with the CETT range `$[0, 1]$` (0 meaning no neurons skipped, 1 meaning maximum possible skipping). At each iteration, it tests the midpoint CETT value: for each checkpoint, it computes the loss (cross-entropy) with dense computation (all neurons active) and with sparse computation (neurons whose `$\|n_i\|_2$` falls below the per-layer threshold induced by the mid CETT are skipped). The perplexity ratio is `$\exp(\text{loss}_{\text{sparse}} - \text{loss}_{\text{dense}})$`—since `$\text{PPL} = \exp(\text{loss})$` and `$\exp(a - b) = \exp(a) / \exp(b)$`, this is the ratio of sparse PPL to dense PPL. If the average ratio across checkpoints is below `$1 + p\%$`, the CETT value is too conservative (we can skip more neurons without exceeding the PPL budget); if it's above, the CETT value is too aggressive. The binary search narrows the interval until it converges to the CETT value that hits exactly the `$p\%$` PPL increase.

**Why binary search works:** Figure 15 empirically demonstrates that both PPL and activation ratio change *monotonically* with the CETT hyperparameter. This is a necessary property—if PPL dipped and then rose as CETT increased, binary search could converge to a local minimum rather than the target PPL ratio. The monotonicity is intuitive: as CETT increases, the per-layer threshold `$\epsilon$` increases, more neurons are skipped, the error from skipping them grows, and performance degrades.

**The sparsity stabilizing strategy (Appendix E):** A practical challenge is that sparsity measurements are noisy, especially during early training when the model is far from convergence and gradients cause large fluctuations in neuron activation patterns. The paper addresses this in two ways:

1. **Drop early checkpoints:** The sparsity points during the learning rate warmup stage are excluded from curve fitting because "the model is far from convergence with considerable sparsity fluctuations."
2. **Average over the last five pre-trained checkpoints:** The CETT-PPL-p% binary search is conducted on the last five checkpoints of the pre-training run *as a group*—the algorithm finds a single CETT value that makes the *average* PPL of these five checkpoints increase by exactly `$p\%$`. That single CETT value is then applied to *all* checkpoints in the training run to measure their sparsity ratios. This provides a consistent measurement standard across the entire training trajectory, rather than having the measurement hyperparameter drift from checkpoint to checkpoint.

**Why p = 1% is chosen as the standard tolerance:** Table 1 reports the difference in downstream task performance between the dense setting (all neurons active) and CETT-PPL-1%, CETT-PPL-5%, and CETT-PPL-10% across all five model scales and both activation functions. The key findings:

- **CETT-PPL-1%:** On commonsense reasoning, the average score difference across all 10 configurations (5 scales × 2 activation functions) is −0.16 points (i.e., the sparse model performs essentially identically to the dense model). On reading comprehension, the average difference is −0.30 points. These differences are well within the noise of benchmark evaluation.
- **CETT-PPL-5%:** The average score difference grows to −0.50 on commonsense reasoning (still small) but −2.21 on reading comprehension (meaningful degradation).
- **CETT-PPL-10%:** The average degradation is −0.45 on commonsense reasoning but −3.64 on reading comprehension (substantial degradation).

The asymmetry is notable: reading comprehension tasks are more sensitive to neuron skipping than commonsense reasoning tasks. This makes sense because reading comprehension requires tracking fine-grained semantic distinctions that may be distributed across many neurons, while commonsense reasoning often relies on more robust, high-level patterns that survive moderate sparsification. The paper chooses `$p = 1\%$` because it "causes nearly no harm to performance on downstream tasks" across both task groups—the degradation is negligible everywhere, making it a safe default.

**The metric in action (Figure 2):** On the 0.8B ReLU model, CETT-PPL-1% achieves an activation ratio of approximately 7.5% (sparsity ratio ~92.5%), while the Straightforward ReLU metric with zero threshold achieves approximately 19.5% activation ratio (sparsity ratio ~80.5%). This is the "61% relative reduction in activation ratio" claimed in the abstract—CETT-PPL-1% identifies many additional neurons as weakly-contributed that the naive zero-threshold method would keep active, because CETT recognizes that these neurons, despite having non-zero activations, contribute negligibly to the output. The PPL increase from skipping them is only 1%, which Table 1 shows is performance-neutral.

#### Model Architecture and Training Configuration

The paper uses a specific model architecture and training regimen across all experiments. These are the controlled variables that the sparsity measurements aim to study.

**Architecture: µP Transformer (Hu et al., 2024).** This architecture combines three elements: (1) the standard LLaMA decoder-only Transformer backbone (Touvron et al., 2023) with gated FFNs (Dauphin et al., 2017; Shazeer, 2020), (2) µP parametrization (Yang et al., 2022) for training stability across scales, and (3) the WSD (Warmup-Stable-Decay) learning rate schedule from Hu et al. (2024). The µP parametrization is critical for the scaling experiments because it ensures that the optimal learning rate and other hyperparameters transfer across model scales, avoiding the need for expensive per-scale hyperparameter tuning.

**FFN specification:** Across all parameter scales, the ratio of the intermediate dimension `$d_f$` to the hidden dimension `$d_h$` is fixed at 2.5 (i.e., `$d_f = 2.5 \times d_h$`). This is a standard expansion ratio in Transformer FFNs. The number of query heads always matches the number of key and value heads. The width-depth ratio (defined as `$d_h$` divided by the number of layers) ranges from 48 to 56 across the different parameter scales—the paper deliberately keeps this ratio *similar* across scales to isolate the effect of scale, and separately sweeps it from approximately 48 to 600 for the 0.1B ReLU model to study its direct effect (Section 5.2, Figure 5).

**Parameter scales and hyperparameters (Table 3):**

| Parameter Scale | Non-Embedding Parameters | Batch Size (tokens) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1B | `$1.08 \times 10^8$` | `$3.27 \times 10^5$` |
| 0.2B | `$2.41 \times 10^8$` | `$5.90 \times 10^5$` |
| 0.4B | `$4.52 \times 10^8$` | `$7.86 \times 10^5$` |
| 0.8B | `$7.60 \times 10^8$` | `$1.18 \times 10^6$` |
| 1.2B | `$1.18 \times 10^9$` | `$1.57 \times 10^6$` |
| 2.4B | `$2.44 \times 10^9$` | `$2.10 \times 10^6$` |

**Training hyperparameters:** Across all configurations: peak learning rate `$\text{lr} = 0.01$`, Adam betas `$\beta_1 = 0.9, \beta_2 = 0.95$`, weight decay `$= 0.1$`. These are standard values for LLM pre-training. The WSD schedule (Hu et al., 2024) consists of a warmup phase, a stable phase with constant learning rate, and a decay phase.

**Pre-training data:** The training corpus is a mixture of cleaned CommonCrawl, Dolma (Soldaini et al., 2024), C4 (Raffel et al., 2020), Pile (Gao et al., 2020), the Stack (Kocetkov et al., 2022), StarCoder (Li et al., 2023), and other collected raw corpus (Appendix H). This is a diverse mixture spanning natural language, code, and structured data.

**Decay stage for downstream evaluation:** After pre-training, each model undergoes a "decay stage" where instruction-tuning data (UltraChat (Ding et al., 2023), SlimOrca (Colombo et al., 2024), OssInstruct (Wei et al., 2024), EvolInstruct (Xu et al., 2023), and other collected datasets) is added to the training mix. This follows recent LLM practice (Dubey et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024) and is necessary to obtain "reasonable results on benchmarks"—foundation models without any instruction tuning often perform poorly on task-specific evaluations even when their language modeling capabilities are strong. However, importantly, *sparsity is measured on the pre-trained checkpoints before the decay stage*, not on the decayed checkpoints. The training loss, validation loss, and perplexity reported in sparsity analyses are all computed on the pre-trained-only models. The downstream task performance in Table 1 is evaluated on the decayed checkpoints, but the sparsity ratios used in those evaluations are computed using the CETT hyperparameter calibrated on pre-trained checkpoints.

**Validation data for CETT-PPL-p%:** A tiny validation dataset sharing the same distribution as the pre-training data, with deduplication to ensure no overlap with pre-training data (Appendix H). The small size is deliberate—the binary search algorithm (Algorithm 1) requires computing dense and sparse forward passes for each candidate CETT value, and doing this on a large validation set would be prohibitively expensive, especially at larger scales.

**Evaluation benchmarks:** Two groups: (1) Commonsense reasoning—average 0-shot accuracy on PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), SIQA (Sap et al., 2019), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2020), and COPA (Roemmele et al., 2011). (2) Reading comprehension—average 0-shot accuracy on BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), LAMBADA (Paperno et al., 2016), and TyDi QA (Clark et al., 2020). The paper also evaluated on more complex tasks (HumanEval, MBPP, GSM8K, MMLU, BBH, AGI-Eval) but models below 1.2B parameters fail to exceed random performance on these, and those results are relegated to Table 6 in Appendix K.

#### Curve Fitting for the Sparsity-Data Relationship

The central empirical contribution of the paper is the discovery of convergent power-law relationships between activation ratio and pre-training data amount, with fundamentally different functional forms for ReLU and SiLU. This subsection explains how those curves are fitted and what their parameters mean.

**Normalization of the independent variable:** The amount of pre-training data is measured as "number of tokens passed," i.e., the cumulative count of tokens the model has been trained on, measured in billions (B). For curve fitting, this value is divided by `$10^9$` to normalize its magnitude, improving numerical stability during optimization.

**Parametric form for ReLU-activated models (Equation 4):**

$$A_{\text{ReLU}}(D) = \exp(-c D^\alpha + b) + A_0$$

where `$D$` is the amount of pre-training data (in billions of tokens, divided by `$10^9$` for normalization), `$A_{\text{ReLU}}(D)$` is the activation ratio (1 − sparsity ratio) at data volume `$D$`, `$A_0 > 0$` is the limit activation ratio as `$D \to \infty$`, `$c > 0$` controls the initial decay rate, `$\alpha > 0$` controls how the decay rate changes with data scale (power-law exponent), and `$b$` is an offset term that shifts the exponential.

**What this equation computes:** The activation ratio starts at some initial value (determined by `$b$` and initial `$D$`), then decreases toward the limit `$A_0$` following a logspace power-law: the quantity `$A_{\text{ReLU}}(D) - A_0$` (the "excess activation" above the limit) decays as `$\exp(-c D^\alpha)$`. In logspace, `$\ln(A_{\text{ReLU}}(D) - A_0) = -c D^\alpha + b$`, which is a power-law in `$D$` (hence "logspace power-law").

**Why this form:** A standard power-law decay (`$A \propto D^{-\alpha}$`) would approach zero as `$D \to \infty$`, implying the model could become arbitrarily sparse with enough data. But the data (Figure 4, left panel) shows ReLU activation ratios clearly leveling off to a non-zero asymptote—there is some minimum activation ratio below which the model cannot go, plausibly because some neurons must remain active to perform basic language processing functions regardless of how much training data is provided. The exponential-power-law form captures both the initial rapid decay (driven by the power-law term `$-c D^\alpha$` inside the exponential) and the convergence to a non-zero limit `$A_0$`. The form `$\exp(-c D^\alpha + b)$` rather than `$\exp(-c D^\alpha)$` is used because the model does not start at an activation ratio of 1.0 (100%)—the `$b$` offset accounts for the actual initial activation ratio when `$D$` is small.

**Parametric form for SiLU-activated models (Equation 5):**

$$A_{\text{SiLU}}(D) = -\frac{c}{D^\alpha} + A_0$$

where `$D$` is again the training data amount, `$A_{\text{SiLU}}(D)$` is the activation ratio, `$A_0 > 0$` is the limit activation ratio as `$D \to \infty$`, `$c > 0$` controls the magnitude of the data-dependent term, and `$\alpha > 0$` is the power-law exponent.

**What this equation computes:** The activation ratio starts above the limit `$A_0$` (since `$-c/D^\alpha$` is negative for positive `$c$` and `$D$`), and increases *toward* `$A_0$` from below as `$D$` grows. This is a standard decreasing power-law in `$D$` for the gap `$A_0 - A_{\text{SiLU}}(D)$`, which shrinks as `$c/D^\alpha$`.

**Why this form is fundamentally different from the ReLU form:** For ReLU, more data *decreases* the activation ratio (increases sparsity)—the model becomes sparser with training. For SiLU, more data *increases* the activation ratio (decreases sparsity)—the model becomes denser with training. This is the paper's most striking empirical finding: the choice of activation function reverses the direction of the sparsity-data relationship. The ReLU form has `$A_{\text{ReLU}}(D)$` approaching `$A_0$` from *above* (activation ratio decreasing), while the SiLU form has `$A_{\text{SiLU}}(D)$` approaching `$A_0$` from *below* (activation ratio increasing). This means that with sufficient training data, SiLU models converge to a *less sparse* state than they started with, while ReLU models converge to a *more sparse* state. The practical implication is stark: organizations training SiLU-based LLMs on ever-larger datasets are inadvertently making their models denser and thus harder to accelerate via activation sparsity.

**Fitted coefficients (Table 2):** The Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm (Marquardt, 1963) is used to fit the parameters `$c$`, `$\alpha$`, `$b$` (for ReLU), and `$A_0$` for each model scale and activation function. Key fitted values:

- **ReLU limit activation ratios `$A_0$`:** 6.14% (0.1B), 6.74% (0.2B), 6.90% (0.4B), 7.21% (0.8B), 7.82% (1.2B). The limit increases slightly (by 1.68 percentage points from 0.1B to 1.2B), which the paper characterizes as "marginally increases" and the basis for the scale insensitivity claim.
- **SiLU limit activation ratios `$A_0$`:** 40.9% (0.1B), 39.0% (0.2B), 38.5% (0.4B), 38.3% (0.8B), 38.2% (1.2B). The limit decreases slightly (by 2.7 points), also characterized as weakly correlated with scale. The crucial difference: ReLU limit ratios are around 6–8%, while SiLU limit ratios are around 38–41%—ReLU models are approximately 5–6× sparser at convergence.
- **ReLU exponent `$\alpha$`:** Ranges from 0.101 (0.1B) to 1.53 (2.4B), increasing substantially with scale. This means larger ReLU models have a *steeper* logspace power-law—their activation ratio decays faster in the early stages of training relative to the data volume. This is consistent with the derivative analysis in Figure 8 showing faster convergence for smaller models.
- **SiLU exponent `$\alpha$`:** Ranges from 0.479 (0.1B) to 0.967 (1.2B), also increasing with scale. Larger SiLU models approach their (higher) limit activation ratio faster.

**The 2.4B out-of-sample validation (Figure 11):** The 2.4B ReLU model was not used in fitting the 0.1B–1.2B scaling laws. Its activation-data trajectory is independently fitted, yielding `$A_0 = 6.48\%$` (limit activation ratio), `$\alpha = 1.53$`, `$b = -3.46$`, `$c = 1.56 \times 10^{-4}$`. The limit of 6.48% is "quite close to those values of models from 0.1B to 1.2B" (which range from 6.14% to 7.82%), supporting the scale insensitivity finding. The fitted curve is plotted as the brown line in Figure 11, matching the empirical data points closely, confirming that the logspace power-law form generalizes beyond the fitting set.

#### Scale Insensitivity Analysis Framework

Section 5.3 addresses a surprising empirical finding: the *limit* activation ratio (as data → ∞) is only weakly correlated with parameter scale, while the *convergence speed* to that limit is strongly scale-dependent (smaller models converge faster). The paper proposes an explanation grounded in a combinatorial model of neuron specialization.

**The empirical observation:** Figure 7 plots the fitted limit activation ratios `$A_0$` against parameter scale for both ReLU and SiLU. For ReLU, the limit increases from 6.14% (0.1B) to 7.82% (1.2B), a change of only 1.68 percentage points. For SiLU, the limit decreases from 40.9% (0.1B) to 38.2% (1.2B), a change of 2.7 percentage points. Both are small relative to the absolute values and relative to the range of parameter scales (12× from 0.1B to 1.2B). This is the "scale insensitivity" claim.

**The convergence speed observation:** Figure 8 plots the *derivative* of the sparsity-data curve (the rate of change of sparsity with respect to data) against the data-scale ratio (number of training tokens divided by parameter scale). Smaller models have derivatives with much larger absolute magnitudes—their sparsity changes more per unit of data relative to their size. For example, the 0.1B ReLU model shows a derivative magnitude roughly 5–10× larger than the 1.2B ReLU model at the same data-scale ratio. This means smaller models "converge faster to the limit."

**Two supporting experiments for similar activation patterns across scales:**

*Experiment 1—Dataset-wise activation frequency distributions (Figure 9):* For four data subsets (Code, Wikipedia, Math, Chinese), the paper computes the *activation frequency* of each neuron—the fraction of tokens from that subset for which the neuron's activation `$s_i$` exceeds the CETT-PPL-1% threshold (i.e., the neuron is considered "strongly-contributed"). It then plots the distribution of these frequencies across all neurons in the model, for each model scale. The distributions are plotted as cumulative histograms (what fraction of neurons have activation frequency below a given value). Figure 9 shows that for each dataset, the curves for different model scales (0.1B through 1.2B) are nearly overlapping—the distribution of neuron activation frequencies is remarkably similar across scales. For ReLU models on Code, approximately 20% of neurons are activated more than 80% of the time, about 50% are activated between 20% and 80% of the time, and about 10% are activated less than 20% of the time—and this pattern is nearly identical from 0.1B to 1.2B. The paper notes in Appendix J that this similarity holds on average but breaks down for specific layers (the first and last layers show scale-dependent patterns, Figure 16).

*Experiment 2—Token-wise activation ratios (Figure 10):* The paper samples 71,549 tokens from the vocabulary and, for each token, computes its activation ratio (1 − sparsity ratio) on a large amount of data. It then compares these per-token activation ratios across model scales in a pair-wise manner. Figure 10 shows scatter plots where each point is a token—its x-coordinate is its activation ratio in one model scale, its y-coordinate in another. The points cluster tightly around the `$y = x$` line (plotted in red), meaning that the same token produces nearly the same activation ratio regardless of model scale. This is evidence that the input-dependent sparsity pattern is scale-invariant: a given token activates roughly the same fraction of neurons whether the model has 0.1B or 1.2B parameters.

**The combinatorial model of neuron specialization:** To explain why similar activation patterns across scales lead to faster convergence for smaller models, the paper proposes a model of neuron specialization as a *grouping process*. The core assumption is that the set of `$d_f$` neurons must specialize into `$G$` functional groups (e.g., one group for handling code syntax, another for mathematical reasoning, another for named entity recognition, etc.). Each group `$i$` requires `$t_i$` neurons, and the ratios `$t_i / d_f$` are assumed to be invariant across scales (this is what the dataset-wise and token-wise activation pattern similarity supports—if larger models had fundamentally different specialization patterns, the activation frequency distributions would differ). Under this assumption, the number of possible ways to assign `$d_f$` neurons to `$G$` groups is a combinatorial quantity:

$$T(d_f) = \prod_{i=1}^{G} \binom{d_f}{t_i} = \prod_{i=1}^{G} \frac{d_f!}{t_i!(d_f - t_i)!}$$

where `$\binom{d_f}{t_i}$` is the binomial coefficient: the number of ways to choose `$t_i$` neurons from `$d_f$` for group `$i$`.

**What this equation computes:** It multiplies across all groups the number of ways to select `$t_i$` neurons from the total `$d_f$` for that group. The product over groups accounts for the fact that each neuron can be assigned to zero or more groups (the paper allows overlapping groups, noting the "potential existence of dead neurons and versatile neurons"—a neuron could be unused or serve multiple functions).

**Why this explains faster convergence for smaller models:** The binomial coefficient `$\binom{d_f}{t_i}$` grows *factorially* with `$d_f$` for fixed ratios `$t_i/d_f$`. If a larger model has twice as many neurons (`$2d_f$`), with the same ratios `$t_i/d_f$`, the number of ways to form the groups is not twice as large—it is exponentially (in fact, factorially) larger. For example, the number of ways to choose `$0.2 d_f$` neurons from `$d_f$` is `$\frac{d_f!}{(0.2d_f)!(0.8d_f)!}$`, which grows roughly as `$\exp(d_f \cdot H(0.2))$` where `$H$` is the binary entropy function—an exponential growth. This means larger models have astronomically more possible neuron specialization configurations to explore during training. The optimization process must distinguish the one correct (or near-correct) configuration from this vast space, which requires proportionally more training data. Smaller models, with far fewer possible configurations (the combinatorial space is much smaller), converge to their specialization pattern—and thus to their limit activation ratio—much faster.

**Why limit sparsity is scale-insensitive despite different convergence speeds:** The limit is determined by the *ratio* `$t_i / d_f$` for each group—what fraction of neurons are specialized to each function. The empirical evidence (Figures 9 and 10) suggests these ratios are similar across scales. If a 0.1B model converges to a state where 6% of neurons are activated on average, and the *functional specialization pattern* (which neurons handle which linguistic functions) is scale-invariant, then a 1.2B model with the same functional specialization ratios will also converge to roughly 6% activation—it just takes more data to find that configuration in the larger combinatorial space. The limit sparsity is therefore determined by the *nature of the task* (what functions need to be represented) rather than the *size of the model*, since the functional groups just scale proportionally.

**This is a hypothesis, not a proof:** The paper presents this combinatorial argument as an "assumption" and a "deduction"—a plausible mechanism consistent with the observed data, not a rigorous theoretical result. The evidence is the activation pattern similarity across scales, which is correlational; it does not directly demonstrate that neurons in different-sized models are performing the same functions, only that their aggregate statistics are similar. The paper does not, for example, show that specific linguistic features activate the same *proportion* of neurons across scales (though Figure 10's token-wise analysis is suggestive). This is a limitation the paper does not explicitly flag but is inherent to the observational nature of the argument.

#### The 2.4B Out-of-Sample Validation Model

The paper trains a single 2.4B parameter ReLU-activated model specifically to test whether the empirical laws discovered on 0.1B–1.2B models generalize. This model is *not* part of the curve-fitting dataset—its sparsity behavior is predicted by the laws, not used to estimate them.

**Configuration:** ReLU activation, approximately 800B training tokens, a width-depth ratio "close to the above models (also small enough and close to the smallest point of the training stability interval)"—the ratio falls within the 48–56 range of the main experiments. The training hyperparameters match those in Table 3: batch size `$2.10 \times 10^6$` tokens, peak learning rate 0.01, Adam betas (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.1, WSD schedule.

**Results (Figure 11):** The activation ratio follows a decreasing logspace power-law, consistent with the ReLU models in the main experiments. The fitted limit activation ratio is 6.48%, which falls within the range of the 0.1B–1.2B ReLU models (6.14%–7.82%), supporting the scale insensitivity finding. The sparsity ratio is 93.52% (100% − 6.48%), meaning that on average, only about 6.5% of neurons need to be active for any given input to achieve performance within 1% PPL of the dense model.

**Acceleration validation (Appendix L):** To demonstrate that the high sparsity ratio translates to real-world speedups, the paper benchmarks the 2.4B model on two inference frameworks: PowerInfer (Song et al., 2023), which exploits activation sparsity by predicting which neurons will be active and only computing those, and "llama.cpp" (Gerganov, 2023), which computes FFNs densely. Both run on the same machine (104 CPUs, 1 NVIDIA A800 GPU), compiled with CUDA enabled. The test uses 100 prompts of 5 prefix tokens each, sampled from C4. PowerInfer achieves 41.79 tokens per second, while llama.cpp achieves 10.23 tokens per second—a 4.1× speedup. Note that llama.cpp does not support ReLU and cannot correctly perform inference, but this does not affect the FLOPs comparison since the dense FFN computation cost is the same regardless of activation function—the speedup reflects genuine computational savings from skipping weakly-contributed neurons, not an artifact of the activation function.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **CETT over alternative neuron recognition methods:** Uses the full output vector `$n_i$` (not just scalar activation `$s_i$`) as the importance signal, layer-adaptive thresholds, and accounts for error cancellation between skipped neurons—empirically superior PPL-vs-sparsity Pareto frontier (Figures 2 and 3).
- **PPL increase tolerance of 1% over looser tolerances:** Table 1 shows 5% and 10% PPL increases cause substantial degradation on reading comprehension (average −2.21 and −3.64 points, respectively), while 1% is performance-neutral across both task groups. This makes CETT-PPL-1% a "safe" metric that doesn't conflate sparsity with quality loss.
- **Binary search for CETT hyperparameter over manual tuning:** The monotonic relationship between CETT and PPL (Figure 15) makes binary search valid, and it avoids the need for practitioners to manually choose a threshold with no principled guidance. The algorithm is deterministic and reproducible.
- **Sparsity stabilizing strategy (last five checkpoints):** Reduces noise from gradient stochasticity during early training that would otherwise produce unstable sparsity measurements and contaminate curve fitting. The single CETT value applied to all checkpoints ensures a consistent measurement standard across the training trajectory.
- **Levenberg-Marquardt fitting over simpler regression:** The parametric forms are non-linear in the parameters `$\alpha$` and `$c$`, requiring non-linear least squares. Levenberg-Marquardt is a standard algorithm for this class of problems that interpolates between gradient descent and Gauss-Newton, providing robust convergence.
- **Data-scale ratio for derivative comparison (Figure 8) over absolute data volume:** Since optimal training data scales roughly proportionally with parameter count (Hoffmann et al., 2022; Besiroglu et al., 2024), comparing convergence speed at the same *ratio* of data to parameters is more meaningful than comparing at the same absolute data volume—it accounts for the fact that larger models need more data to converge in general.
- **µP parametrization over manual per-scale hyperparameter tuning:** Enables fair comparison across scales by ensuring hyperparameters transfer, removing a confounding variable. Without µP, differences in sparsity across scales could be artifacts of suboptimal hyperparameters rather than genuine scale effects.
- **Decay stage for evaluation but not for sparsity measurement:** Avoids the instruction-tuning data influencing the sparsity measurements (which are computed on pre-trained checkpoints), while still enabling meaningful downstream task evaluation (which requires some instruction following capability). This cleanly separates the sparsity analysis from the evaluation protocol.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Sparsity from a Post-Hoc Observation to a Predictable, Engineerable Training Outcome

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is transforming activation sparsity from a *property you discover* in a trained model to a *property you can forecast and design for* before training begins. Prior to this work, the field treated activation sparsity as an emergent phenomenon—interesting when observed (Li et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022), exploitable for acceleration when present (Liu et al., 2023; Song et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2024), but fundamentally *reactive*: you train a model, measure its sparsity, and then figure out what to do with it. The paper's core intellectual contribution is establishing that sparsity follows convergent, law-like relationships with controllable design variables—activation function, width-depth ratio, and training data volume—making it a *predictable* quantity that can be engineered into models from scratch, just as loss is engineered through scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022).

This reframing matters because it shifts sparsity from a downstream optimization problem (how do I make my already-trained model faster?) to an upstream architectural decision (given my compute budget and target sparsity, what architecture and how much data should I use?). The practical implication crystallizes in the paper's recommendation for building sparse models from scratch (Section 6): use ReLU activation, a small width-depth ratio, and more training data. This is not a post-hoc recipe for sparsifying an existing SiLU model—it is a design specification for a training run that *will produce* a sparse model, with the achievable sparsity predictable from the fitted power-law parameters in Table 2.

What distinguishes this from prior work on scaling laws for sparse models (Frantar et al., 2023; Krajewski et al., 2024) is that those studies focus on *weight sparsity* or *architectural sparsity* (MoE), where the sparsity structure is imposed by design. Activation sparsity is different: it emerges from training dynamics without any explicit sparsity constraint. The paper's discovery that these emergent dynamics follow predictable functional forms—a decreasing logspace power-law for ReLU, an increasing power-law for SiLU (Equations 4 and 5, Figure 4)—is genuinely surprising because there is no obvious reason that the activation function alone should reverse the direction of the sparsity-data relationship. This is not a small refinement of prior observation; it is a fundamental finding about how different activation functions shape the optimization landscape in qualitatively opposite ways. The fitted limit values (Table 2) quantify what practitioners can expect: ReLU models converge to ~93% sparsity, SiLU models to only ~60% sparsity, and in both cases the limit is largely insensitive to parameter scale (Figure 7).

Tangible evidence for this reframing's validity comes from the out-of-sample 2.4B model (Figure 11): its activation-data trajectory follows the predicted logspace power-law, its fitted limit sparsity of 93.52% falls within the range of the 0.1B–1.2B models (consistent with the scale insensitivity finding), and the resulting 4.1× decoding speedup (Appendix L) demonstrates that the engineered sparsity translates to real efficiency gains. If sparsity were not predictable—if the 2.4B model had converged to, say, 80% sparsity instead of ~93%—the entire framework would be descriptive rather than prescriptive. The successful out-of-sample validation is what elevates the empirical laws from curve-fitting to engineering principles.

### Innovation 2: CETT-PPL-1% as a Performance-Calibrated, Activation-Function-Agnostic Measurement Standard

The introduction of CETT-PPL-1% (Section 4.2, Algorithm 1) is more than just "a better metric." It solves a fundamental measurement problem that had made prior sparsity studies non-comparable and, in many cases, misleading. The core difficulty is that activation sparsity has no absolute, model-independent definition—it is always relative to a threshold, and different thresholds produce different sparsity ratios for the same model. Before this paper, the choice of threshold was arbitrary (a global `$\epsilon$` in FAT-ϵ), activation-function-specific (zero threshold for ReLU, undefined for SiLU), or structurally constrained (fixed per-layer sparsity in Top-k / MoE). This meant that comparing sparsity between two models—especially across different activation functions or architectures—was comparing measurements made with incompatible rulers. A ReLU model measured with zero-threshold might appear 80% sparse while a SiLU model measured with FAT-ϵ at `$\epsilon = 0.01$` might appear 60% sparse, but this tells you nothing about their *relative* sparsity under a comparable performance constraint.

The intellectual move that distinguishes CETT-PPL-1% is tying the threshold directly to a measurable, task-relevant consequence: the perplexity increase on held-out validation data. This transforms sparsity from an arbitrary geometric property (what fraction of activations are below some threshold?) to a *functional* property (what fraction of neurons can be skipped while keeping performance within a specified budget?). The 1% PPL tolerance is not chosen arbitrarily—Section 4.2 validates that it is performance-neutral on downstream tasks (Table 1: average score differences of −0.16 for commonsense reasoning and −0.30 for reading comprehension across all model scales and activations), while 5% and 10% tolerances cause meaningful degradation on reading comprehension (−2.21 and −3.64 points, respectively). This calibration step is what makes the metric "performance-friendly"—it guarantees that the sparsity it measures is the sparsity you can actually exploit without hurting the model's real-world capabilities.

Why is this more than an incremental improvement over CETT alone (Zhang et al., 2024a)? CETT per se solves the generalizability problem (by using vector-level neuron importance and layer-adaptive thresholds) but leaves the hyperparameter as a free knob. The paper's contribution is closing that loop: by binary-searching the CETT value to hit a specific PPL degradation target (Algorithm 1), and by empirically validating that a 1% PPL increase is safe, the metric becomes *reproducible* and *comparable* across any model, any activation function, any scale. A sparsity ratio of 93% measured by CETT-PPL-1% on a ReLU model means the same thing as a sparsity ratio of 93% on a SiLU model measured by the same protocol—both can skip 93% of neurons while incurring at most 1% PPL degradation. Prior metrics could never make this guarantee.

Figure 2 provides the direct evidence for this metric's superiority: across both 0.1B and 0.8B scales and both ReLU and SiLU activations, CETT achieves the lowest PPL at any given sparsity ratio (the PPL-vs-sparsity Pareto frontier is strictly better). The gap is especially dramatic for SiLU at the 0.8B scale, where CETT achieves approximately 40% activation ratio at 12 PPL, while Top-k and FAT-ϵ are both above 20 PPL at the same sparsity—nearly 2× worse perplexity for the same sparsity. This is not a marginal improvement; it is the difference between a usable metric and one that conflates sparsity with catastrophic performance loss.

### Innovation 3: The Convergent Power-Laws as a Diagnostic Lens for Training Dynamics (Not Just a Scaling Relationship)

The empirical discovery of the activation-data power-laws (Equations 4 and 5, Figure 4) is often described as a "scaling law," but this understates its intellectual significance. What makes these relationships conceptually novel is not merely their functional form but the *diagnostic insight* they provide into the training process itself—specifically, the observation that activation sparsity converges *much more slowly* than training loss.

This is visible by comparing Figure 4 (activation ratio vs. data) with Figure 13 (training loss vs. data). The training loss for all models largely plateaus well before 50B tokens, with only marginal improvements thereafter. Yet the activation ratio continues to change substantially well past 200B tokens for both ReLU and SiLU models—for ReLU, the ratio continues its logspace power-law descent; for SiLU, it continues its power-law ascent toward the limit. This means that *neuron specialization continues long after loss improvements have saturated*. The paper explicitly makes this connection in Section 7: "the convergence speed of activation sparsity is much slower than loss, indicating the ongoing of neuron specialization even when the loss changes little."

This is a genuinely novel diagnostic perspective. The loss curve—the primary tool practitioners use to monitor training progress and decide when to stop—is largely blind to the internal reorganization of the model's neurons. A model at 50B tokens and a model at 200B tokens might have nearly identical loss yet dramatically different sparsity profiles, with correspondingly different efficiency properties at inference time. The sparsity-data curve thus provides a *second axis* for monitoring training convergence—one that tracks the model's internal structure rather than its output quality. The paper explicitly proposes this application: "the trend of activation sparsity can provide a new lens for inspecting the progress of neuron specialization and training convergence" (Section 7).

This diagnostic value is amplified by the *opposite convergence directions* for ReLU and SiLU. For ReLU, more data makes the model sparser—specialization increases, and fewer neurons are needed per input. For SiLU, more data makes the model denser—specialization *decreases* in terms of activation sparsity, with more neurons being partially active for each input. This suggests fundamentally different mechanisms of specialization under different activation functions: ReLU training encourages neurons to become *binary specialists* (either fully on or fully off for a given input), while SiLU training encourages neurons to become *continuous contributors* (varying their partial activation levels). The paper doesn't fully explore this mechanistic distinction, but the empirical regularity of the opposite-signed power-laws provides strong evidence that it is real and not an artifact of measurement.

The convergence to non-zero limits `$A_0$` (Table 2) is another diagnostic insight. It implies that there is some irreducible minimum activation ratio—around 6–8% for ReLU, around 38% for SiLU—that cannot be eliminated by more training data. This minimum likely corresponds to neurons encoding fundamental, frequently-needed linguistic functions that must be active for almost every input. The fact that this minimum is similar across scales (Figure 7) but differs dramatically between activation functions (~6% vs. ~38%) suggests it is determined by how the activation function shapes the representational geometry of the FFN, not by the model's capacity per se. This is a hypothesis the paper does not fully develop but that the data clearly motivates.

### Innovation 4: The Combinatorial Explanation for Scale-Insensitive Limit Sparsity with Scale-Dependent Convergence Speed

Section 5.3 presents an initially puzzling pair of observations: (1) the limit activation ratio `$A_0$` is nearly constant across a 12× range of parameter scales (Figure 7, change of only 1.68 percentage points for ReLU, 2.7 points for SiLU), yet (2) smaller models converge to that limit much faster than larger models (Figure 8, derivative magnitudes 5–10× larger for 0.1B than 1.2B at the same data-scale ratio). The paper's explanation—that neuron specialization is a combinatorial assignment problem whose search space grows factorially with neuron count—is not just an interpretation of the data; it is a *conceptual model* that makes testable predictions and connects sparsity dynamics to broader questions about how neural networks organize internally.

The argument proceeds in three steps, each supported by evidence:

**First, similar activation patterns across scales:** Figures 9 and 10 provide empirical evidence that the statistical distribution of which neurons fire for which inputs is remarkably scale-invariant. Dataset-wise activation frequency distributions (Figure 9) are nearly overlapping across 0.1B–1.2B models for four distinct data types. Token-wise activation ratios (Figure 10) cluster tightly around the `$y = x$` line when compared pairwise across scales. This means that, on a *population level*, a 0.1B model and a 1.2B model both activate roughly the same *fraction* of their neurons for the same inputs—they just have different absolute numbers of neurons to distribute across the same functional categories.

**Second, the combinatorial model (Equation 6):** If neuron specialization is interpreted as assigning `$d_f$` neurons to `$G$` functional groups, with each group requiring a fixed *fraction* of the total neurons (consistent with the scale invariance observed in step one), then the number of possible assignment configurations grows factorially with `$d_f$`. This is a structural property of combinatorics, not an empirical claim—it follows directly from the binomial coefficient growing super-exponentially. A 1.2B model with ~12× more neurons than a 0.1B model does not have 12× more possible specialization patterns; it has astronomically more, because the number of ways to choose subsets grows as the factorial of the total count.

**Third, convergence speed follows from search space size:** Training involves finding the "correct" specialization pattern from this vast combinatorial space. Larger models, with exponentially larger search spaces, require proportionally more data to converge to the same functional organization. This explains Figure 8: at the same data-scale ratio, smaller models are much further along their convergence trajectory because they are searching a much smaller space.

What makes this an intellectual contribution rather than just a post-hoc story is that it *predicts* the scale insensitivity of the limit sparsity (the limit depends on the *ratios* `$t_i / d_f$`, which are assumed scale-invariant) while *also* predicting the scale dependence of convergence speed (convergence time depends on the *absolute combinatorial complexity*, which grows with `$d_f$`). These are not independent observations that the model happens to fit—they are linked predictions that follow from a single, simple assumption (similar functional specialization ratios across scales). The fact that both predictions hold in the data (Figures 7 and 8) lends credibility to the combinatorial interpretation.

This model also connects activation sparsity to a broader literature on neural network specialization and modularity (Zhang et al., 2023; Bills et al., 2023) by providing a quantitative framework for thinking about *how* specialization emerges during training. It suggests that the training process is not just minimizing loss but also *searching a combinatorial space of functional assignments*—and that the sparsity trajectory is a direct observable of this search process, converging when the assignment stabilizes. This is a richer view than treating sparsity as a static property of the converged model.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All models are pre-trained on a diverse mixture of cleaned CommonCrawl, Dolma (Soldaini et al., 2024), C4 (Raffel et al., 2020), Pile (Gao et al., 2020), the Stack (Kocetkov et al., 2022), StarCoder (Li et al., 2023), and other collected raw corpus (Appendix H). A held-out validation set sharing the same distribution as the pre-training data, with deduplication to eliminate overlap with the training data, is used for CETT-PPL-p% calibration (Section 3, Evaluation settings). Downstream evaluation uses two benchmark groups: commonsense reasoning (0-shot accuracy averaged across PIQA, SIQA, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, and COPA) and reading comprehension (0-shot accuracy averaged across BoolQ, LAMBADA, and TyDi QA). More complex benchmarks (HumanEval, MBPP, GSM8K, MMLU, BBH, AGI-Eval) were attempted but models at 0.1B–1.2B failed to exceed random performance; those results are reported in Appendix K, Table 6.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use the µP Transformer architecture (Hu et al., 2024), which combines the LLaMA decoder-only backbone (Touvron et al., 2023) with gated feed-forward networks (Dauphin et al., 2017; Shazeer, 2020). Five model scales are used for the main scaling analysis: 0.1B, 0.2B, 0.4B, 0.8B, and 1.2B non-embedding parameters (Table 3). A separate out-of-sample 2.4B model is trained to validate the generalizability of the empirical laws. All models share a fixed FFN intermediate-to-hidden dimension ratio of 2.5, similar width-depth ratios ranging from 48 to 56 across scales (to isolate the effect of scale), and µP parametrization (Yang et al., 2022) for hyperparameter transfer. Training uses peak learning rate 0.01, Adam betas (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.1, and the WSD learning rate schedule (Hu et al., 2024). Batch sizes scale with model size (Table 3: from 3.27 × 10^5 tokens at 0.1B to 2.10 × 10^6 tokens at 2.4B). Each model variant is trained under both ReLU and SiLU activation functions for paired comparison.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is the **activation ratio** (1 − sparsity ratio), measured using CETT-PPL-1% as defined in Section 4.2 and computed via the binary search algorithm in Appendix F. Specifically, a single CETT hyperparameter value is found such that the average perplexity of the last five pre-trained checkpoints on the validation set increases by exactly 1% relative to the dense baseline (all neurons active). That CETT value is then applied to all checkpoints in the training run to measure their activation ratios consistently. The sparsity stabilizing strategy (Appendix E) drops checkpoints from the learning rate warmup stage to avoid noise from early-training sparsity fluctuations. Downstream task performance is evaluated as the average 0-shot accuracy on the two benchmark groups, reported as the difference from the dense setting (ΔCETT-PPL-p% in Table 1). Training loss and perplexity are computed on pre-trained-only checkpoints (before the decay stage), while task-specific benchmark scores are evaluated on checkpoints after the decay stage (where instruction-tuning data is added for reasonable benchmark performance; Section 3, Training settings).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares four methods for weakly-contributed neuron recognition (Section 4.1, Figures 2 and 3): **(1) Straightforward ReLU**, which uses a zero threshold on activation values (applicable only to ReLU: `D = {i | s_i = 0}`); **(2) Top-k**, which enforces a fixed number of activated neurons per layer based on the largest absolute activation values (widely used in MoE architectures, e.g., Fedus et al., 2022); **(3) FAT-ϵ** (Kurtz et al., 2020), which uses a single global threshold `ε` on absolute activation values shared across all layers (`D = {i | |s_i| < ε}`); and **(4) CETT** (Zhang et al., 2024a), which adaptively sets per-layer thresholds based on the L2 norm relative error of skipped neuron output vectors (Equation 3). For downstream task evaluation (Table 1), the "Dense" baseline uses all neurons activated (no sparsity). The MoE comparison in Appendix B (Figure 12) uses vanilla ReLU as the reference against MoE models with 16, 30, and 60 experts.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** "Compute" in this paper is not measured in FLOPs or generations but rather in the **amount of pre-training data** (number of tokens passed, measured in billions, B). All sparsity measurements are taken from model checkpoints saved at intervals during pre-training, and the independent variable in scaling law analyses is the cumulative training tokens the model has processed up to that checkpoint. For curve fitting, tokens are normalized by dividing by 10^9 for numerical stability (Appendix G). The width-depth ratio experiments (Section 5.2) do not account for the increased computational cost of deeper models due to serial dependencies, which the paper acknowledges as a limitation (Appendix A). For the inference acceleration experiment (Appendix L), compute is measured in decoding speed (tokens per second) on 100 C4 test prompts with 5 prefix tokens each, running on 104 CPUs and 1 NVIDIA A800 GPU.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** There is no cross-validation or statistical significance testing in this paper. The empirical laws are fitted to the full trajectories of activation ratio vs. data volume for each model variant, with no held-out portion of the training trajectory used for validation. However, the paper uses the **2.4B out-of-sample model** (Section 6, Figure 11) as its primary generalization check: this model was trained independently after the 0.1B–1.2B scaling laws were established, and its sparsity behavior is compared against predictions from those laws (limit sparsity, power-law form). The sparsity stabilizing strategy (Appendix E) uses the last five checkpoints to determine the CETT hyperparameter, which is then applied to all checkpoints—this is a form of temporal smoothing rather than cross-validation. The downstream task performance in Table 1 reports raw score differences (Δ) without confidence intervals.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Comparing Weakly-Contributed Neuron Recognition Methods

The paper's first set of quantitative results establishes CETT as the most accurate method for identifying weakly-contributed neurons. This is the necessary foundation for all subsequent scaling law analyses—if the sparsity metric itself is unreliable, any "laws" derived from it would be artifacts of the measurement method.

**PPL-vs-sparsity Pareto curves (Figure 2):** The paper evaluates all four recognition methods on both ReLU and SiLU models at 0.1B and 0.8B scales. The key results, reading from the Pareto frontiers:

- **ReLU, 0.1B (top-left panel):** CETT (blue curve) achieves an activation ratio of approximately 5% at roughly 21 PPL, while Top-k (orange) and FAT-ϵ (green) are both above 25 PPL at the same activation ratio. At higher activation ratios (lower sparsity), the gap narrows but CETT consistently dominates. Straightforward ReLU (red star) sits at approximately 19% activation ratio and 20 PPL—a single point rather than a curve because there is no hyperparameter to tune (zero threshold is fixed).

- **SiLU, 0.1B (top-right panel):** Because SiLU produces non-zero activations almost everywhere, the Straightforward ReLU method is inapplicable (no data point shown). CETT achieves approximately 25% activation ratio at 21 PPL, while Top-k is at roughly 28 PPL and FAT-ϵ at approximately 26 PPL for the same activation ratio. The gap between CETT and alternatives is smaller than for ReLU at this scale, but CETT remains the best.

- **ReLU, 0.8B (bottom-left panel):** CETT achieves approximately 7.5% activation ratio at 13 PPL. The Straightforward ReLU point sits at roughly 19.5% activation ratio and about 12.5 PPL. This is the "61% relative reduction in activation ratio" cited in the abstract and Introduction: CETT-PPL-1% identifies many additional weakly-contributed neurons (activation ratio drops from ~19.5% to ~7.5%) while the PPL increase is contained at 1%, which Table 1 shows is downstream-task-neutral.

- **SiLU, 0.8B (bottom-right panel):** This is where CETT's advantage is most dramatic. CETT achieves roughly 35% activation ratio at 12 PPL, while Top-k and FAT-ϵ both exceed 20 PPL at the same activation ratio—nearly a 2× gap in perplexity for the same sparsity. This illustrates why a generalizable metric is essential: methods designed primarily for ReLU-like activation patterns catastrophically overestimate which SiLU neurons are "weak," conflating sparsity with performance degradation.

**Downstream-task-vs-sparsity Pareto curves (Figure 3):** These panels show the commonsense reasoning (C.R.) and reading comprehension (R.C.) evaluation scores as a function of sparsity ratio for each method, on the 0.1B models.

- **ReLU on commonsense reasoning (top-left):** At 95% sparsity (5% activation ratio), CETT achieves approximately 48.5% accuracy, while Top-k drops to roughly 37% and FAT-ϵ to about 42%. The Straightforward ReLU point is at ~80% sparsity with ~49% accuracy (similar to dense, since zero-threshold is conservative). The CETT curve is essentially flat from 80% to 92.5% sparsity, then declines gently to ~48.5% at 95% sparsity—only a ~1 point drop from the dense performance of ~49.6%.

- **SiLU on commonsense reasoning (top-right):** At 60% sparsity, CETT achieves roughly 49% accuracy while Top-k and FAT-ϵ drop to approximately 38% and 42%, respectively. At higher sparsity levels, all methods degrade, but CETT degrades more slowly.

- **ReLU on reading comprehension (bottom-left):** The task is substantially more sensitive to sparsity. At 95% sparsity, CETT achieves roughly 25% accuracy (down from ~28% dense), Top-k drops below 15%, and FAT-ϵ falls to approximately 18%. This task-specific sensitivity previews the finding in Table 1 that reading comprehension tolerates less sparsity than commonsense reasoning.

- **SiLU on reading comprehension (bottom-right):** The degradation is even steeper. At 60% sparsity, CETT maintains roughly 25% accuracy (close to the ~28% dense baseline), but Top-k and FAT-ϵ collapse to approximately 12% and 15%, respectively.

**Takeaway from Figures 2 and 3:** CETT is the unambiguous winner—it achieves the lowest PPL and the highest downstream task performance at any given sparsity ratio across both activation functions and both model scales. The advantage is particularly large for SiLU models, where methods using scalar activation thresholds (FAT-ϵ) or fixed per-layer counts (Top-k) fail badly. The fact that CETT uses the full output vector `n_i` rather than just the scalar activation `s_i` (Section 2.2, Equation 3) is the mechanism behind this superiority: it captures the actual vector-level contribution of each neuron to the FFN output, accounting for gating weight, input projection magnitude, and output projection magnitude simultaneously, and accounts for partial error cancellation between skipped neurons through the sum-of-vectors-then-norm computation.

#### CETT-PPL-p% Calibration and the Choice of p = 1%

Having established CETT as the best recognition algorithm, the paper addresses the hyperparameter selection problem: which CETT value (controlling how many neurons are skipped) should be used as the standard measurement setting?

**PPL increase tolerance sweep (Table 1):** The paper evaluates three tolerance levels—1%, 5%, and 10% PPL increase—against the dense baseline for all 10 model configurations (5 scales × 2 activation functions). The results establish a clear ordering of performance degradation:

- **Commonsense reasoning:** At CETT-PPL-1%, the average score difference across all 10 configurations is −0.16 points (range: −0.9 to +0.4). At CETT-PPL-5%, the average is −0.50 points (range: −0.7 to −0.1, with no positive values). At CETT-PPL-10%, the average is −0.45 points (range: −1.2 to +0.2). The degradation is small at all tolerances—commonsense reasoning is largely robust to neuron skipping.

- **Reading comprehension:** At CETT-PPL-1%, the average difference is −0.30 points (range: −1.6 to +1.0). At CETT-PPL-5%, the average drops to −2.21 points (range: −3.6 to +0.1). At CETT-PPL-10%, the average is −3.64 points (range: −6.5 to −0.3, all negative). Reading comprehension degrades substantially and monotonically with increasing PPL tolerance.

**Why p = 1% is selected:** The 1% tolerance is the only setting where the performance degradation is negligible (within typical benchmark noise) on *both* task groups. The 5% and 10% tolerances cause substantial degradation on reading comprehension (average −2.21 and −3.64 points, respectively), meaning that sparsity measured at those tolerances would not be "performance-friendly"—the metric would be counting as "weakly-contributed" some neurons that are actually important for reading comprehension tasks. The paper's explicit claim is that "the average performance of 'CETT-PPL-1%' is comparable to that of the theoretically most accurate 'Dense' setting" (Section 4.2), and Table 1 supports this.

**Monotonicity validation (Figure 15):** Binary search for the CETT hyperparameter (Algorithm 1) is only valid if both PPL and activation ratio change monotonically with CETT. Figure 15 plots both quantities against CETT for the 0.1B and 0.8B models with both activation functions. The curves are visually monotonic: PPL increases smoothly with CETT (top four panels), and activation ratio decreases smoothly with CETT (bottom four panels). No inversions or plateaus are visible, confirming that binary search will converge to a unique CETT value for any target PPL increase.

#### Activation-Data Scaling Laws (Section 5.1)

This is the paper's central empirical contribution. The authors fit parametric curves to the activation ratio vs. training data trajectories for all 10 model configurations, using the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm (Marquardt, 1963). The fitted curves are plotted in brown in Figure 4, overlaid on the measured data points.

**ReLU models follow a decreasing logspace power-law (Equation 4, Figure 4 left):** For all five ReLU model scales (0.1B through 1.2B), the activation ratio decreases monotonically toward a non-zero asymptote `A_0`. The fitted parameters in Table 2 quantify this:

- **0.1B ReLU:** limit activation ratio `A_0 = 6.14%`, exponent `α = 0.101`, coefficient `c = 3.20 × 10^0`. The small α means the initial decay is gradual.
- **0.2B ReLU:** limit `A_0 = 6.74%`, `α = 0.449`, `c = 2.86 × 10^{-1}`. The larger α produces a steeper initial drop.
- **0.4B ReLU:** limit `A_0 = 6.90%`, `α = 0.683`, `c = 7.90 × 10^{-2}`.
- **0.8B ReLU:** limit `A_0 = 7.21%`, `α = 1.01`, `c = 7.97 × 10^{-3}`.
- **1.2B ReLU:** limit `A_0 = 7.82%`, `α = 1.33`, `c = 9.03 × 10^{-4}`.

Key observations: (1) The limit activation ratio `A_0` increases only marginally with scale—from 6.14% (0.1B) to 7.82% (1.2B), a change of just 1.68 percentage points—suggesting scale insensitivity. (2) The exponent `α` increases substantially with scale (from 0.101 to 1.33), meaning larger models have a steeper logspace power-law—their activation ratio drops more sharply in the early training stages relative to data volume, but since they start from a proportionally larger absolute number of activated neurons, the convergence in percentage-point terms is slower. This is explicitly shown in Figure 8, where smaller models have much larger derivative magnitudes. (3) The coefficient `c` decreases dramatically with scale (from 3.20 to 9.03 × 10^{-4}), which is necessary to compensate for the increasing α to keep the curves reachable at realistic data volumes.

**SiLU models follow an increasing vanilla power-law (Equation 5, Figure 4 right):** For all five SiLU model scales, the activation ratio *increases* monotonically toward a higher asymptote `A_0`. The fitted parameters:

- **0.1B SiLU:** limit `A_0 = 40.9%`, `α = 0.479`, `c = 1.02 × 10^{-1}`.
- **0.2B SiLU:** limit `A_0 = 39.0%`, `α = 0.844`, `c = 2.08 × 10^{-1}`.
- **0.4B SiLU:** limit `A_0 = 38.5%`, `α = 1.03`, `c = 4.20 × 10^{-1}`.
- **0.8B SiLU:** limit `A_0 = 38.3%`, `α = 0.995`, `c = 5.62 × 10^{-1}`.
- **1.2B SiLU:** limit `A_0 = 38.2%`, `α = 0.967`, `c = 5.38 × 10^{-1}`.

Key observations: (1) The limit `A_0` decreases slightly with scale—from 40.9% (0.1B) to 38.2% (1.2B), a change of 2.7 percentage points—again showing weak scale correlation. (2) The exponent `α` increases with scale (from 0.479 to 0.967), meaning larger SiLU models approach their limit faster in relative terms. (3) Critically, **ReLU limit activation ratios (~6–8%) are approximately 5–6× smaller than SiLU limits (~38–41%)**—ReLU models are inherently far sparser at convergence. (4) The *direction* of the relationship is opposite: ReLU becomes sparser with more data; SiLU becomes denser.

**The magnitude of the data effect on SiLU models:** The increase in activation ratio for SiLU is not trivial. For the 0.1B SiLU model, the activation ratio at the earliest checkpoint shown (roughly 10B tokens) is approximately 32–33%, and it rises to approximately 37–38% by 250B tokens—an increase of 5–6 percentage points, or about 15–18% relative. For the 0.8B SiLU model, the rise is from roughly 34% to 38% over the same data range. This means that a SiLU model trained on 250B tokens is measurably *denser* than one trained on 10B tokens, which has direct implications for inference efficiency: training SiLU models longer makes them harder to accelerate via activation sparsity.

**Training loss comparison (Figure 13, Appendix D):** The training loss curves for ReLU and SiLU models of the same scale are essentially overlapping. For example, the 1.2B ReLU and 1.2B SiLU curves both start around 3.5–3.6 at early checkpoints and converge to approximately 2.85–2.90 by 250B tokens. The limit training loss values (Figure 14) are also comparable: ReLU models range from ~2.80 (1.2B) to ~3.45 (0.1B); SiLU models range from ~2.82 (1.2B) to ~3.43 (0.1B). This establishes that the dramatic sparsity difference between ReLU and SiLU (limit activation ratios of ~6–8% vs. ~38–41%) is *not* due to a performance gap—the two activation functions achieve comparable training loss at all scales. The sparsity difference is a genuine effect of the activation function on the optimization dynamics and representational geometry, not a consequence of one activation function producing worse models.

#### Width-Depth Ratio Experiments (Section 5.2)

The paper investigates how the width-depth ratio—defined as the hidden dimension `d_h` divided by the number of layers—affects activation sparsity, using the 0.1B ReLU model with 9 different ratios swept from approximately 48 to over 600. Since total parameters are held roughly constant (0.1B), adjusting the ratio means trading off depth (number of layers) for width (hidden dimension): a smaller ratio means more layers with smaller hidden dimension (deeper and thinner); a larger ratio means fewer layers with larger hidden dimension (shallower and wider).

**Limit activation ratio vs. width-depth ratio (Figure 5):** The fitted limit activation ratio `A_0` is plotted against the width-depth ratio. The curve reveals two regimes:

- **Below a bottleneck of approximately 114:** The activation ratio increases *linearly* with the width-depth ratio. At the lowest tested ratio (approximately 48, corresponding to the deepest-and-thinnest configuration), the limit activation ratio is roughly 6.5%. It rises steadily to about 7.5% at the bottleneck. This means deeper-and-thinner models are sparser—for every unit increase in the width-depth ratio (making the model relatively wider and shallower), the activation ratio increases by a roughly constant amount.

- **Above the bottleneck of approximately 114:** The activation ratio fluctuates around 8%, showing no consistent trend with further increases in the width-depth ratio. The model has become shallow enough that further adjustments to the depth-width tradeoff no longer affect sparsity—the activation ratio saturates at around 8%.

**Limit training loss vs. width-depth ratio (Figure 6):** The training loss curve tells a complementary story. At very small width-depth ratios (deep-and-thin), the training loss is elevated: at a ratio of approximately 48, the limit training loss is roughly 3.50. It drops steadily as the ratio increases, reaching a minimum of approximately 3.40 at a ratio of around 114 (right at the bottleneck). For ratios above 114, the loss increases gradually again, reaching about 3.43 at the highest tested ratio. This U-shaped pattern is interpreted as a training stability effect: extremely deep-and-thin models suffer from training instability (consistent with known difficulties in optimizing deep Transformers; Petty et al., 2023; Wu & Tang, 2024), causing worse loss. There is an optimal region—centered around the bottleneck point—where training is stable and loss is minimized.

**Practical implication:** The best width-depth ratio from a joint sparsity-and-performance perspective is "the smallest point of the interval that ensures stable training and the best performance" (Section 5.2). For the 0.1B model, this interval is approximately 74 to 282—the range where the model achieves near-optimal loss. Within this interval, choosing the smallest ratio (deepest-and-thinnest architecture that still trains stably) maximizes sparsity without sacrificing performance. The paper's 0.1B–1.2B models all use width-depth ratios in the 48–56 range (Table 3), which is near the lower bound of the stable-training interval on the 0.1B scale, consistent with this recommendation.

#### Scale-Dependence of Activation Sparsity (Section 5.3)

The paper systematically analyzes how activation sparsity varies with parameter scale, using the five ReLU and five SiLU models which have similar width-depth ratios (48–56) to isolate the effect of scale from the effect of architecture shape.

**Limit activation ratios vs. scale (Figure 7):** The fitted limit activation ratios `A_0` from Table 2 are plotted against the number of parameters for both activation functions. The curves are nearly flat:

- **ReLU:** `A_0` increases from 6.14% (0.1B) to 7.82% (1.2B), a change of only 1.68 percentage points over a 12× increase in parameter count. The paper characterizes this as "marginally increases."
- **SiLU:** `A_0` decreases from 40.9% (0.1B) to 38.2% (1.2B), a change of 2.7 percentage points, described as "decreases slightly."
- The absolute difference between activation functions is massive and consistent: at every scale, ReLU `A_0` is approximately 31–34 percentage points lower than SiLU `A_0`. This dwarfs any within-function scale effect.

**Convergence speed vs. scale (Figure 8):** The derivative of the sparsity-data curve (rate of change of sparsity with respect to data) is plotted against the data-scale ratio (training tokens divided by parameter count) for all 10 configurations. The key finding:

- For both ReLU and SiLU, **smaller models have much larger absolute derivative magnitudes** at the same data-scale ratio. For example, at a data-scale ratio of approximately 40, the 0.1B ReLU model's derivative magnitude is roughly 5–10× larger than the 1.2B ReLU model's derivative magnitude. This means smaller models converge to their limit sparsity much faster in relative terms—their sparsity changes more per unit of data normalized by model size.
- The derivatives for SiLU models are positive (activation ratio increasing toward the limit), while derivatives for ReLU models are negative (activation ratio decreasing toward the limit), consistent with the opposite directions of their power-laws.
- All derivatives approach zero at high data-scale ratios, confirming that both activation functions converge to their respective limits.

**Dataset-wise activation frequency distributions (Figure 9):** This experiment provides evidence for the scale insensitivity of activation patterns. For each of four data subsets (Code, Wikipedia, Math, Chinese), the paper computes the activation frequency of each neuron (the fraction of tokens for which the neuron is classified as "strongly-contributed" under CETT-PPL-1%), then plots the cumulative distribution of these frequencies across all neurons for each model scale.

- Across all four datasets and both activation functions, the curves for different model scales (0.1B through 1.2B) are nearly overlapping. For ReLU models on Code, approximately 15–20% of neurons activate more than 80% of the time, about 50% activate between 30% and 70% of the time, and roughly 10% activate less than 20% of the time—and this distribution is almost identical regardless of whether the model has 0.1B or 1.2B parameters.
- The SiLU distributions are shifted toward higher frequencies (more neurons are frequently active, consistent with the higher overall activation ratio), but again show near-perfect scale invariance in their shape.
- Appendix J (Figure 16) qualifies this: the scale invariance holds for the aggregate distribution but not for specific individual layers—the first layer and last layer show scale-dependent activation frequency distributions, while the exact middle layer is largely scale-insensitive.

**Token-wise activation ratio comparison (Figure 10):** The paper samples 71,549 tokens from the vocabulary and computes each token's activation ratio on a large volume of data for models of different scales. Pairwise scatter plots compare the activation ratios across scales, with each point representing one token.

- The points cluster tightly around the `y = x` line (plotted in red) for all pairwise comparisons. For example, a token with a 6% activation ratio in the 0.1B ReLU model has approximately a 6% activation ratio in the 1.2B ReLU model. Tokens with 15% activation ratios are similarly consistent across scales.
- There is some scatter—individual tokens can deviate by 2–3 percentage points—but no systematic bias. The correlation is tight enough to support the claim that "most tokens maintain a close activation ratio across models of various scales" (Section 5.3).

These two experiments together provide converging evidence that the *functional organization* of neurons—which types of inputs activate which fractions of the model—is remarkably similar across parameter scales. This is the empirical foundation for the combinatorial explanation of why limit sparsity is scale-insensitive while convergence speed is not (Section 5.3, "Deduction").

#### Validation on the 2.4B Out-of-Sample Model (Section 6)

The 2.4B ReLU model was trained to test whether the empirical laws discovered on 0.1B–1.2B models would generalize. It is not part of the curve-fitting dataset.

**Activation-data trajectory (Figure 11):** The measured activation ratios (blue dots) for the 2.4B model are plotted against the number of tokens passed (up to approximately 950B). The fitted logspace power-law curve (brown line) matches the data closely, showing the characteristic decreasing-then-converging shape of ReLU models.

- **Fitted parameters (Table 2):** `A_0 = 6.48%`, `α = 1.53`, `b = −3.46`, `c = 1.56 × 10^{-4}`.
- **Limit activation ratio:** 6.48% falls within the range of the 0.1B–1.2B ReLU models (6.14%–7.82%), specifically close to the 0.2B model's 6.74%. This supports the scale insensitivity claim: a model 24× larger than 0.1B and 2× larger than 1.2B converges to essentially the same limit activation ratio.
- **Sparsity ratio:** 93.52% (= 100% − 6.48%), meaning that on average, only about 6.5% of FFN neurons need to be active for any given input to stay within 1% PPL of dense performance.
- **Exponent α = 1.53:** This continues the trend of increasing α with scale (0.101 at 0.1B, 0.449 at 0.2B, 0.683 at 0.4B, 1.01 at 0.8B, 1.33 at 1.2B, 1.53 at 2.4B). The logspace power-law becomes increasingly steep as models grow, meaning the early-training activation ratio drops faster relative to the data volume, though the convergence in absolute percentage-point terms remains slower for larger models (the derivative analysis in Figure 8 showed this).

**Inference acceleration (Appendix L):** On 100 test prompts of 5 prefix tokens from C4, running on 104 CPUs and 1 NVIDIA A800 GPU:

- **PowerInfer (sparsity-aware):** 41.79 tokens per second.
- **llama.cpp (dense):** 10.23 tokens per second.
- **Speedup:** 4.1×.

This demonstrates that the 93.52% sparsity ratio translates into genuine wall-clock acceleration. The speedup factor (4.1×) is substantially less than the theoretical maximum implied by the sparsity ratio (1 / (1 − 0.9352) ≈ 15.4×), which is expected because PowerInfer must spend additional computation on activation prediction (an offline profiler and online predictors), cannot skip all weakly-contributed neurons with 100% accuracy, and has overhead from irregular memory access patterns. The comparison is against "llama.cpp," which computes FFNs densely and thus serves as a realistic dense inference baseline.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Downstream task sensitivity to PPL increase tolerance (Table 1):** The paper's core calibration—that p = 1% is the right tolerance for CETT-PPL-p%—is validated by comparing three tolerance levels (1%, 5%, 10%) against the dense baseline across all 10 model configurations. The finding: commonsense reasoning is robust even at 10% PPL increase (average Δ = −0.45 points); reading comprehension degrades substantially at 5% (average Δ = −2.21 points) and 10% (Δ = −3.64 points); only at 1% is performance comparable to dense for both task groups. This asymmetry (reading comprehension more sensitive to neuron skipping than commonsense reasoning) is a non-obvious finding that suggests reading comprehension relies on more distributed, fine-grained neural representations.

**Individual benchmark scores (Appendix K, Tables 4 and 5):** Beyond the average scores in Table 1, the paper reports per-benchmark results for all 10 configurations and all four settings (Dense, CETT-PPL-1%, -5%, -10%). The patterns are consistent: on individual commonsense reasoning benchmarks (PIQA, SIQA, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, COPA), scores are nearly flat across the four settings. For example, on PIQA, the 1.2B ReLU model scores 71.6 (Dense), 71.1 (1%), 70.8 (5%), 70.2 (10%)—a total drop of only 1.4 points from Dense to 10%. On reading comprehension benchmarks, the drops are larger: BoolQ for 1.2B ReLU goes from 63.3 (Dense) to 63.4 (1%), 62.1 (5%), 62.6 (10%); LAMBADA drops from 52.5 (Dense) to 47.7 (10%). TyDi QA (both F1 and accuracy) shows more erratic patterns but generally degrades at higher tolerances. The individual benchmark results confirm that the aggregate trends in Table 1 are not driven by outliers in a single dataset.

**Complex task evaluation (Appendix K, Table 6):** The paper reports results on more complex benchmarks (AGIEval, HumanEval, MBPP, GSM8K, MMLU, BBH) for all configurations. As noted in Appendix H, models at 0.1B–1.2B fail to exceed random performance on these tasks. For example, GSM8K 8-shot accuracy for 1.2B ReLU Dense is only 6.4% (Table 6)—well below usable levels. HumanEval pass@1 is 7.3% for the same model. The scores are so low and noisy that no pattern emerges across Dense vs. CETT-PPL-p% settings. The evaluation on these benchmarks is included for completeness but cannot support any conclusions about sparsity effects, which the paper appropriately does not attempt to draw.

**MoE vs. vanilla ReLU PPL-vs-sparsity Pareto curve (Appendix B, Figure 12):** This ablation compares whether architectural sparsity (MoE) can achieve a better performance-sparsity tradeoff than the naturally emergent sparsity in a vanilla ReLU Transformer. The 0.1B MoE models are obtained by tuning the number of activated experts (producing different sparsity ratios), while the vanilla setting adjusts the CETT hyperparameter. The result: at an activation ratio of approximately 5%, the vanilla ReLU model achieves ~25 PPL, while MoE with 16 experts achieves ~30 PPL, MoE with 30 experts achieves ~29 PPL, and MoE with 60 experts achieves ~28 PPL. At higher activation ratios (lower sparsity), the vanilla model consistently achieves 2–5 PPL lower (better) at the same activation ratio. This is evidence that "MoE has a significantly worse performance-sparsity tradeoff" (Appendix B), supporting the paper's choice to study emergent sparsity in vanilla Transformers rather than architecturally imposed sparsity. Note that this comparison is only at 0.1B and only with a specific MoE configuration—generalization to other scales and MoE designs is not demonstrated.

**Activation pattern similarity at individual layers (Appendix J, Figure 16):** While Figure 9 shows that the *aggregate* activation frequency distribution is scale-invariant, Figure 16 breaks this down by layer position (first layer, exact middle layer, last layer). The finding: for the middle layer, the distributions remain nearly overlapping across scales (similar to the aggregate). But for the first layer and last layer, the distributions diverge across scales—the first layer of larger models shows more neurons with high activation frequencies, while the last layer shows the opposite pattern. This is a nuanced qualification of the scale-invariance claim: it applies globally but not uniformly at every layer. The paper does not explore this layer-position effect further, which represents a limitation.

**Training loss convergence vs. sparsity convergence (Figure 13 vs. Figure 4):** Although not explicitly framed as an ablation, the comparison between the training loss trajectory (Figure 13) and the activation ratio trajectory (Figure 4) reveals a temporal dissociation: training loss largely plateaus by 50B tokens for most models, while activation ratio continues to change substantially past 200B tokens. For the 0.8B ReLU model, the training loss drops from ~3.3 (at 10B tokens) to ~3.05 (at 50B) and then only to ~2.95 by 250B—a tiny additional improvement—while the activation ratio drops from ~9% to ~7.5% over the same late-training period. This dissociation is the basis for the paper's claim in Section 7 that sparsity provides "a new lens for inspecting the progress of neuron specialization and training convergence" beyond what the loss curve reveals.

**Activation function comparison on loss (Figure 14):** The limits of training loss for ReLU and SiLU models are directly compared. At 0.1B, both are ~3.43–3.45; at 1.2B, ReLU is ~2.80, SiLU is ~2.82. The near-identical loss limits confirm that the large sparsity gap between ReLU and SiLU is not a consequence of ReLU models being worse at language modeling—they achieve the same loss with far fewer active neurons per input.

**2.4B model as out-of-sample validation (Figure 11):** This is the paper's primary robustness check for the empirical laws. The 2.4B model was not used in fitting the power-law parameters of the 0.1B–1.2B models. Its activation-data trajectory independently follows the logspace power-law form (Equation 4), and its fitted limit `A_0 = 6.48%` falls within the range established by the smaller models (6.14%–7.82%). The fact that the law extrapolates to 2× the parameter scale of the largest fitting model and to nearly 4× the training tokens (~950B vs. ~250B for the 0.1B–1.2B series) strengthens confidence in the generalizability of the functional form.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments provide substantial evidence for the paper's core empirical claims, but with several important boundary conditions and unaddressed threats to generalizability.

**Claim: "There is a convergent power-law relationship between the activation ratio and the amount of pre-training data, with opposite-signed forms for ReLU and SiLU."** The evidence for this claim is strong within the scope of the experiments. Figure 4 shows clear, visually convincing fits of the parametric forms (Equations 4 and 5) for all 10 model configurations. The out-of-sample 2.4B model (Figure 11) independently reproduces the ReLU logspace power-law. Table 2 provides the fitted coefficients, demonstrating consistent patterns (e.g., `A_0` for ReLU clustered in the 6–8% range). However, several qualifications are necessary:

- **The power-law functional forms were chosen by the authors after "careful attempts" (Section 5.1), not derived from theory.** The paper states: "After careful attempts, we find that the curve of activation ratios to the amount of pre-training data is easier to fit than that of sparsity ratios." There is no principled derivation of why an exponential-power-law form (for ReLU) or a decreasing-power-law form (for SiLU) should govern these relationships. The forms fit the data well, but alternative functional forms with similar asymptotic behavior could also fit. The paper does not compare goodness-of-fit against alternative parametric forms (e.g., a stretched exponential, a sum of exponentials, or a rational function).
- **The convergence to non-zero limits `A_0` is inferred from fits to finite data, not directly observed.** The 0.1B–1.2B models are trained on ~250B tokens, and the limit `A_0` is an extrapolation to infinite data. If sparsity dynamics change qualitatively at data scales beyond those tested (e.g., a second transition at the trillion-token scale), the fitted limits could be biased. The 2.4B model, trained on ~950B tokens, provides some additional validation but still only extends the data range by a factor of ~4×, which may not be sufficient to detect a slow second-phase transition.
- **The measurement protocol itself could introduce artifacts.** The sparsity stabilizing strategy (Appendix E) applies a single CETT value (calibrated on the last five checkpoints) to *all* checkpoints. This assumes that the relationship between CETT and PPL degradation is stable throughout training. If early-training checkpoints require a different CETT value to achieve the same 1% PPL increase (because the model's activation-value distribution shifts qualitatively during training), then the measured early-training activation ratios could be systematically biased. The paper does not verify the stability of the CETT-PPL-1% mapping across training time.

**Claim: "ReLU activation yields substantially greater sparsity than SiLU at comparable performance."** The evidence for this claim is strong. Table 1 shows that ReLU and SiLU models of the same scale achieve comparable dense performance on both commonsense reasoning and reading comprehension—e.g., at 1.2B, ReLU Dense scores 60.0 (C.R.) and 53.2 (R.C.); SiLU Dense scores 59.6 (C.R.) and 54.8 (R.C.). The differences are within 1 point, which is noise-level for these benchmarks. Meanwhile, the fitted limit activation ratios (Figure 7, Table 2) show ReLU at ~6–8% vs. SiLU at ~38–41%—a 5–6× difference. Figure 13 and Figure 14 confirm comparable training loss. The claim stands.

**However, the "comparable performance" evidence is limited to the specific benchmarks tested.** The paper only evaluates on commonsense reasoning and reading comprehension tasks (Table 1, Tables 4–5). More complex tasks (code generation, mathematical reasoning, multi-step logical deduction) were attempted but the models were too small to achieve above-random performance (Appendix K, Table 6), so no comparison could be made. It remains unknown whether ReLU and SiLU models of the same scale would show comparable performance on harder tasks that push the boundaries of model capability. If SiLU's continuous activation function enables better representation of subtle features needed for complex reasoning, the sparsity advantage of ReLU might come at a performance cost on more demanding benchmarks—a possibility the paper cannot rule out given its scale constraints.

**Claim: "Deeper-and-thinner architectures produce sparser models below a bottleneck width-depth ratio, while wider-shallower architectures show no further improvement above the bottleneck."** The evidence for this claim comes from a single model scale (0.1B ReLU) with 9 width-depth ratios (Figures 5 and 6). The linear relationship below the bottleneck and the saturation above it are clearly visible. However:

- **The experiment is conducted at only one scale and with only one activation function.** It is unknown whether the bottleneck point (ratio ≈ 114 for 0.1B) generalizes to other scales. If the bottleneck is determined by absolute depth (number of layers) rather than the width-depth ratio, it would shift with scale. If it is determined by the ratio itself, it might be scale-invariant. The paper does not test this.
- **The "training stability" rationale is inferred from the U-shaped loss curve (Figure 6), but no direct stability diagnostics** (gradient norms, attention entropy, rank collapse metrics) are reported. The loss minimum at the bottleneck is consistent with a stability explanation but does not prove it. Alternative explanations—such as the deeper models having fundamentally different representational capacity for the same parameter count—are not ruled out.
- **Computation cost is not accounted for.** The paper acknowledges in Appendix A that "with the substantial increase in the number of layers, the training efficiency is significantly decreased." Deeper models take longer to train per token because layers are computed sequentially. A fair comparison would amortize the increased training cost against the inference savings from sparsity. Without this analysis, the recommendation to use "the smallest width-depth ratio that ensures stable training" may not be Pareto-optimal when total lifecycle compute (training + inference) is considered.

**Claim: "The limit of activation sparsity is weakly correlated to the scale of LLMs, while the convergence speed to the limit is much faster in smaller models."** The evidence from Figure 7 (near-flat limit activation ratios across 0.1B–1.2B) and Figure 8 (much larger derivative magnitudes for smaller models) supports this claim. The out-of-sample 2.4B model strengthens it: its limit `A_0 = 6.48%` is consistent with the 0.1B–1.2B range. The combinatorial explanation (Section 5.3, "Deduction") provides a plausible mechanism.

**However, several concerns limit the strength of this finding:**

- **The parameter scale range is only 12× (0.1B to 1.2B for the main analysis, plus one point at 2.4B).** A 1.68 percentage-point change in `A_0` over a 12× scale range could be interpreted as "weakly correlated" or as "a small but systematic trend"—the distinction depends on whether one focuses on the absolute magnitude or the relative change. If the trend were extrapolated to 10B or 100B parameters, would `A_0` continue its slow increase/decrease, or would it saturate? The data are insufficient to distinguish these possibilities.
- **The width-depth ratio, while "similar" across scales, is not identical (48–56, per Table 3).** Figure 5 shows that in the 48–114 range, the activation ratio is sensitive to the width-depth ratio (a change of ~30 in the ratio corresponds to roughly a 1 percentage-point change in activation ratio). A variation of 48–56 could introduce noise on the order of 0.2–0.3 percentage points into the scale-dependence analysis. This is small but not zero, and the paper does not correct for it.
- **The combinatorial model is a hypothesis, not a validated mechanism.** The evidence for "similar activation patterns across scales" comes from aggregate statistics (Figures 9 and 10). These show that the *distribution* of neuron activation frequencies is scale-invariant, but do not demonstrate that the *functional identity* of individual neurons (what specific linguistic feature each neuron encodes) is the same across scales. It is possible that a 0.1B model and a 1.2B model both activate 6% of neurons on average, but the 0.1B model's activated neurons encode completely different functions than the 1.2B model's—this would be consistent with the aggregate statistics while undermining the combinatorial model's assumption of similar functional specialization. The paper acknowledges this as an "assumption" (Section 5.3) but does not test it directly, e.g., through probing classifiers or activation-pattern matching across scales.
- **The combinatorial model itself has unstated assumptions.** It assumes neurons are assigned to groups independently, that group assignments are binary (a neuron is in a group or not), and that the number of groups `G` is constant across scales. The paper does not justify these assumptions. If neurons could partially belong to groups, or if `G` grows with `d_f` (e.g., larger models discover more fine-grained functional distinctions), the factorial growth argument would need modification.

**Claim: "CETT-PPL-1% is a generalizable and performance-friendly metric."** The evidence strongly supports this claim within the paper's scope. CETT achieves the best PPL-vs-sparsity and task-vs-sparsity Pareto curves across ReLU and SiLU at 0.1B and 0.8B (Figures 2 and 3). The 1% PPL increase tolerance is validated as downstream-task-neutral (Table 1, ΔCETT-PPL-1% row). The binary search algorithm (Algorithm 1) is well-defined and the monotonicity property enabling it is empirically verified (Figure 15).

**However, a significant practical concern is not addressed:**

- **The CETT-PPL-p% metric depends on the validation data distribution.** The paper uses a validation set matching the pre-training distribution. But sparsity is input-dependent: a model might be 93% sparse on average pre-training data but only 80% sparse on out-of-distribution inputs (e.g., a new domain, an adversarial input). The paper acknowledges this limitation in Appendix A: "The same model can have different sparsity ratios on distinct datasets or tasks." If sparsity varies substantially with data distribution, then CETT-PPL-1% measured on one validation set may not reflect the sparsity achievable on deployment data. The paper does not quantify how much sparsity estimates vary across data distributions, making it difficult to assess how "generalizable" the metric really is.
- **The cost of CETT-PPL-p% calibration is not reported but is potentially significant.** Algorithm 1 requires multiple binary search iterations, each involving dense and sparse forward passes on the validation set for all checkpoints. For the sparsity stabilizing strategy (using the last five checkpoints), the algorithm runs binary search on the *group* of five checkpoints, evaluating the average PPL ratio at each iteration. The number of iterations depends on the convergence tolerance `eps`; the paper sets `eps = 1` but does not report the absolute threshold or wall-clock time. For the 2.4B model, this cost could be non-trivial, though it is a one-time calibration cost, not a per-inference cost.

**Missing experiments that would have strengthened the paper:**

- **No direct verification that skipped neurons under CETT-PPL-1% are actually unnecessary for specific tasks.** The paper shows that aggregate PPL increases by 1% and downstream scores are mostly unchanged (Table 1), but does not probe *which* neurons are being skipped and whether skipping them affects specific linguistic phenomena. A case study or probing experiment would connect the metric to mechanistic interpretability.
- **No comparison of sparsity patterns across different data mixtures during pre-training.** The training data is a fixed mixture of CommonCrawl, code, Wikipedia, etc. (Appendix H). The paper acknowledges in Appendix A that "data mixing policies for pre-training can also have a considerable impact on the activation sparsity" but does not test this. Different proportions of code vs. natural language might shift the sparsity trajectory.
- **No measurement of how sparsity evolves during the decay stage.** All sparsity measurements are on pre-trained-only checkpoints. The downstream evaluation uses decayed checkpoints, but the sparsity ratios used in Table 1 are computed using the CETT hyperparameter calibrated on pre-trained checkpoints. If the decay stage changes the activation-value distribution (which seems plausible given the addition of instruction-tuning data), the sparsity of the decayed model might differ from what CETT-PPL-1% measured—and the performance impact of skipping neurons might also differ. The paper does not verify that CETT-PPL-1% calibrated on pre-trained checkpoints remains performance-friendly for decayed checkpoints.
- **No comparison to other activation functions beyond ReLU and SiLU.** The paper's ReLU-vs-SiLU comparison is informative, but the field uses many other activation functions (GeLU, SwiGLU variants, etc.). The finding that ReLU enables greater sparsity than SiLU is valuable, but whether ReLU is the *most* sparsity-friendly activation function—and whether there are activation functions that combine SiLU's performance on hard tasks with ReLU's sparsity—is not explored. The paper cites Zhang et al. (2024a) for introducing ReLU² as an alternative, but does not evaluate it.
- **No latency measurement for different sparsity levels.** The 4.1× speedup on the 2.4B model (Appendix L) is a single data point demonstrating that sparsity translates to acceleration. But there is no scaling curve showing how speedup varies with sparsity ratio, which would connect the sparsity-data scaling laws to predicted inference acceleration as a function of training duration—a natural extension of the paper's framework.

**Conditional claims and their boundary conditions:**

The paper's central prescriptive claim—"To train an LLM with greater activation sparsity from scratch, it is better to adopt the ReLU activation, use a small width-depth ratio on the premise of stable training, and feed more training data" (Section 1)—holds under the specific conditions tested: µP Transformer models from 0.1B to 2.4B, trained on a diverse pre-training mixture, evaluated on commonsense reasoning and reading comprehension, with sparsity measured by CETT-PPL-1%. The key conditions where the claim might not hold are:

- **Models larger than 2.4B:** The scale insensitivity of limit sparsity suggests the claim may generalize, but this is extrapolation.
- **Different pre-training data mixtures:** The paper explicitly flags this as unknown (Appendix A).
- **Tasks requiring more complex reasoning:** The paper's models are too small to evaluate on these tasks, so the performance neutrality of ReLU-vs-SiLU at comparable sparsity is unverified for harder benchmarks.
- **Latency-constrained deployments:** The paper does not account for training cost (deeper models train more slowly) or inference latency (sparsity-aware inference frameworks like PowerInfer have overhead that may vary with sparsity patterns).

The claim is best interpreted as a set of evidence-backed guidelines with demonstrated validity on the tested configurations, rather than as universal laws—and the paper is appropriately cautious in framing the findings as "empirical laws" that "can provide instructional values" rather than as proven theorems.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for in the Difficulty Estimation Framework

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central empirical framework—tracing how activation sparsity evolves during training and fitting convergent power-laws—relies on the CETT-PPL-1% metric, which requires binary-searching a CETT hyperparameter such that average validation perplexity increases by exactly 1% over the dense baseline (Section 4.2, Algorithm 1). The paper acknowledges that the CETT-PPL-p% metric depends on a validation dataset and that "the same model can have different sparsity ratios on distinct datasets or tasks" (Appendix A). However, the paper does not account for the computational cost of the calibration procedure itself, nor does it address how sensitive the fitted power-law parameters are to the choice of validation data.

**The consequence.** Algorithm 1 requires multiple iterations of binary search on the CETT hyperparameter, each involving dense and sparse forward passes on the validation set for multiple checkpoints (the last five pre-trained checkpoints, when using the sparsity stabilizing strategy; Appendix E). The number of iterations, the size of the validation set, and the convergence tolerance `eps` jointly determine the total FLOPs cost of calibration, but none of these are reported. For practitioners attempting to replicate the scaling law analysis on their own models—or to apply the paper's recommendation to "predict the theoretical upper/lower bound sparsity of a model to evaluate its potential" (Section 7)—the calibration cost could be substantial, especially at larger scales (e.g., the 2.4B model). If calibration costs approach or exceed the compute saved by sparsity-aware inference, the practical value of the measurement framework is diminished. Furthermore, because sparsity is input-dependent, the CETT-PPL-1% value measured on one validation set may not reflect the sparsity achievable on the actual deployment data distribution—the paper flags this as a limitation (Appendix A) but does not quantify the variance.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no measurement of calibration wall-clock time, FLOPs cost, or number of binary search iterations for any model scale. There is no ablation studying how sensitive the fitted power-law coefficients (`c`, `α`, `A_0` in Table 2) are to different validation datasets (e.g., code-heavy vs. natural-language-heavy validation sets). The monotonicity validation in Figure 15 confirms binary search is well-defined, but does not report how many function evaluations are required to reach convergence.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges dataset sensitivity as a limitation in Appendix A but defers it to future work: "The correlation between sparsity and influential factors (e.g., the form of power laws) can also have dataset-unique characteristics." No suggestions are made for reducing calibration cost (e.g., using a smaller validation set, early stopping of binary search, or amortizing calibration across model scales via µP-based transfer of the CETT hyperparameter).

### Width-Depth Ratio Experiments Do Not Account for Training or Inference Computation Costs

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 5.2 studies how the width-depth ratio affects activation sparsity using a 0.1B ReLU model with 9 different ratios (Figures 5 and 6). The paper finds that deeper-and-thinner models (smaller width-depth ratios) are sparser, and recommends using "a small width-depth ratio on the premise of stable training" (Sections 1, 6, 8). However, the paper explicitly acknowledges in Appendix A that this analysis is incomplete: "with the substantial increase in the number of layers, the training efficiency is significantly decreased, as we have observed in the training process. Therefore, in addition to performance, the computation costs of a model also deserve consideration."

**The consequence.** The recommendation to use deep-and-thin architectures for sparsity ignores the increased training cost of deeper models. Transformer layers are computed sequentially—a model with 2× more layers takes roughly 2× longer per training step (assuming fixed total FLOPs per layer), even if total parameters are held constant. The inference speedup from higher sparsity (e.g., the 4.1× speedup demonstrated on the 2.4B model; Appendix L) must be weighed against the increased training cost to determine whether the deeper architecture is net-beneficial over the model's full lifecycle. A model that is 20% sparser but costs 50% more to train may not be Pareto-optimal when total FLOPs (training + inference) are considered. Additionally, deeper models increase inference latency due to serial dependencies, which matters for latency-sensitive applications even if throughput (tokens per second) improves from sparsity—a tradeoff the paper does not discuss.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 5 shows the limit activation ratio decreases linearly with width-depth ratio below the bottleneck (~114), from ~7.5% to ~6.5%—a relative reduction of ~13% in activation ratio. Figure 6 shows the training loss is elevated at the lowest ratios (deepest models), with loss at ratio ~48 being ~3.50 vs. ~3.40 at the optimum (~114). Beyond observing these trends, the paper provides no FLOPs accounting for training cost, no measurement of per-step training time vs. width-depth ratio, and no lifecycle cost analysis combining training and inference savings. The paper acknowledges only that "it may be interesting to involve the inference computation as a variable in our study" (Appendix A).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The limitation is acknowledged in Appendix A but no analysis or even rough estimate of the tradeoff is provided. The paper frames it as future work but gives no specific direction for how to incorporate computation costs into the width-depth ratio analysis.

### Activation Function Comparison Is Limited to Two Choices and Small-Scale Models on Simple Benchmarks

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central practical recommendation—"use ReLU as the activation function" for building sparse LLMs (Sections 1, 6)—is based on a comparison between exactly two activation functions (ReLU and SiLU) on models from 0.1B to 1.2B, evaluated only on commonsense reasoning and reading comprehension benchmarks (Table 1, Tables 4–5). The paper states: "the task-specific performance in Table 1 and the trend of training loss in Appendix D reveal the comparable performance between ReLU and SiLU activations" (Section 5.1). However, when the authors attempted to evaluate on more complex tasks (code generation via HumanEval/MBPP, mathematical reasoning via GSM8K, multi-task understanding via MMLU, logical reasoning via BBH, and general reasoning via AGI-Eval), they "fail to obtain performance above the random level" (Appendix H) and the results in Table 6 show near-zero or chance-level scores for all configurations (e.g., GSM8K 8-shot accuracy of 6.4% for the 1.2B ReLU Dense model).

**The consequence.** The claim that ReLU and SiLU have "comparable performance" is empirically supported *only* for the specific benchmarks tested, which are relatively simple tasks (commonsense reasoning, reading comprehension) that do not push the boundaries of these small models' capabilities. It remains unknown whether ReLU's sparsity advantage (limit activation ratio ~6–8% vs. ~38–41%; Figure 7) comes at a cost on more demanding reasoning tasks that require the model to utilize its full representational capacity. SiLU's continuous, non-zero activation function might enable the model to represent more fine-grained feature interactions or maintain more precise gradients for complex multi-step reasoning—capabilities that would only manifest on harder tasks or at larger scales where models actually solve those tasks above chance. Moreover, the paper does not compare ReLU against other common activation functions used in LLMs (GeLU, SwiGLU, GEGLU, ReLU² from Zhang et al., 2024a), leaving open the possibility that some other activation function could match SiLU's performance on hard tasks while approaching ReLU's sparsity. The recommendation to use ReLU is therefore based on a limited comparison set and an incomplete performance evaluation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 shows that ReLU and SiLU have comparable scores on commonsense reasoning and reading comprehension across all scales (0.1B–1.2B). Table 6 in Appendix K shows that both ReLU and SiLU models fail on harder benchmarks—but this means the "comparable performance" claim cannot be verified for these tasks, either supporting or refuting it. The paper provides no comparison with GeLU, SwiGLU, or other activation functions. The training loss comparison (Figures 13 and 14) shows comparable convergence, but loss on the pre-training distribution does not guarantee comparable performance on out-of-distribution reasoning tasks.

**Mitigation status.** Partially acknowledged. The paper notes in Appendix H that harder benchmarks could not be evaluated because models were too small. It does not discuss the possibility that performance differences between ReLU and SiLU might emerge on harder tasks or at larger scales, nor does it condition its recommendation on this uncertainty. The comparison to other activation functions is not addressed at all—the paper implicitly treats ReLU and SiLU as the only two relevant choices, which is not accurate given the diversity of activation functions in modern LLMs.

### Sparsity Is Measured on Pre-Trained Checkpoints but Downstream Evaluation Uses Decayed Checkpoints

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's evaluation protocol (Section 3, Evaluation settings) measures sparsity on pre-trained-only checkpoints (before the decay stage where instruction-tuning data is added), but evaluates downstream task performance on decayed checkpoints. The CETT-PPL-1% binary search is conducted on pre-trained checkpoints (specifically, the last five pre-trained checkpoints; Appendix E), and the resulting CETT hyperparameter is used to compute the sparsity ratios reported in Table 1 for the decayed checkpoints as well. The paper states: "If there are no special statements, the training loss, validation loss, and perplexity (on validation data) are all calculated on models that only complete pre-training, and the task-specific performance is evaluated on checkpoints after the decay stage" (Section 3).

**The consequence.** The decay stage introduces instruction-tuning data into the training mix (UltraChat, SlimOrca, OssInstruct, EvolInstruct, and other collected datasets; Appendix H). This additional training likely changes the model's activation value distributions, neuron specialization patterns, and consequently its activation sparsity characteristics. If the decay stage makes the model systematically denser (more neurons active per input)—which is plausible because instruction-following requires integrating more diverse linguistic contexts—then the sparsity ratios reported in Table 1 (measured using a CETT hyperparameter calibrated on pre-trained checkpoints) may systematically overestimate the sparsity of the decayed model being evaluated. Conversely, if the decay stage makes the model sparser (more specialized), the reported sparsity may be underestimated. In either case, the connection between the sparsity measurement and the downstream task evaluation is through a calibration procedure applied to a *different* set of model weights than those being tested. The 1% PPL increase guarantee is verified on the pre-trained validation set, not on the decayed model's behavior—there is no verification that skipping neurons at the CETT-PPL-1% threshold remains performance-neutral for the decayed model.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** No evidence is provided. The paper does not measure sparsity on decayed checkpoints, does not compare pre-trained vs. decayed activation patterns (e.g., activation frequency distributions), and does not verify that the CETT-PPL-1% calibration transfers across the decay stage. Table 1 reports ΔCETT-PPL-1% as the performance difference between the sparse setting (using the pre-trained-calibrated CETT threshold) and the dense setting, both evaluated on the decayed model—but there is no guarantee that the 1% PPL increase constraint still holds on the decayed model, because PPL is reported only for pre-trained checkpoints (Figure 4).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the impact of the decay stage on sparsity, does not measure sparsity of decayed models, and does not report PPL for decayed models. The discrepancy between sparsity measurement (pre-trained) and task evaluation (decayed) is stated but its implications are not analyzed.

### The Combinatorial Model for Scale-Insensitive Sparsity Is a Plausible Hypothesis, Not a Validated Mechanism

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 5.3 proposes a combinatorial model to explain the empirical findings that the limit activation ratio is nearly scale-invariant (Figure 7) while convergence speed is faster for smaller models (Figure 8). The model assumes: (1) neurons specialize into `G` functional groups, with the fraction of neurons assigned to each group (`t_i / d_f`) being invariant across parameter scales; (2) the number of possible specialization configurations grows combinatorially with the number of neurons (Equation 6), explaining why larger models converge more slowly; (3) the limit activation ratio is determined by these invariant fractions, explaining scale insensitivity. The paper frames this as an "assumption" and a "deduction" (Section 5.3) supported by two empirical observations: dataset-wise activation frequency distributions are similar across scales (Figure 9), and per-token activation ratios are similar across scales (Figure 10).

**The consequence.** The combinatorial model is an *interpretation* that fits the observations, not a *mechanism* that has been directly tested. The evidence for "similar neuron specialization" across scales is purely correlational and aggregate: Figure 9 shows that the *distribution* of activation frequencies (what fraction of neurons are active how often) is scale-invariant, and Figure 10 shows that individual tokens activate similar *fractions* of neurons across scales. Neither experiment demonstrates that *the same specific neurons perform the same functions* across scales. A 0.1B model and a 1.2B model could both activate 6% of their neurons on average for a given input, but the activated neurons in the 0.1B model might encode completely different features than the activated neurons in the 1.2B model—this would be consistent with the aggregate statistics but would invalidate the assumption that functional specialization ratios are invariant. Furthermore, the combinatorial model has unstated and untested assumptions: that neurons are assigned to groups in a binary manner (in or out), that groups are independent, that the number of groups `G` is constant across scales (rather than growing with `d_f` as larger models discover finer-grained functional distinctions), and that the optimization process uniformly explores the combinatorial space. If any of these assumptions fails, the model's explanatory power is compromised, and alternative explanations (e.g., that sparsity limits are set by intrinsic properties of the pre-training data distribution rather than by combinatorial search complexity) become viable.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figures 9 and 10 provide the only evidence for similar activation patterns across scales. Appendix J (Figure 16) partially qualifies this: the aggregate similarity breaks down for the first and last layers, where activation frequency distributions diverge across scales—but the paper does not discuss how this layer-position effect affects the combinatorial model. There is no direct test of functional similarity, such as: probing whether neurons in different-sized models respond to the same linguistic features, checking whether neuron activation patterns can be aligned across scales via canonical correlation analysis, or verifying whether the combinatorial space size (Equation 6) actually predicts convergence time when `G` and `t_i` are estimated from data.

**Mitigation status.** The paper presents the combinatorial model as a plausible explanation but does not claim it is proven—the language is appropriately cautious ("it is reasonable to assume that the specialization of neurons is also scale-insensitive," "we try to explain the above observations"). However, the model is presented in the main text (Section 5.3) as the primary explanatory framework, and the paper does not discuss alternative explanations or the model's untestable assumptions. No future work is proposed to directly validate the combinatorial mechanism (e.g., through controlled experiments varying `d_f` independently of other architectural parameters, or through causal interventions on neuron groupings).

### Single Model Architecture and Pre-Training Data Mixture Limits Generalizability of the Empirical Laws

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the µP Transformer architecture (Hu et al., 2024) with LLaMA-style gated FFNs, a fixed FFN intermediate-to-hidden dimension ratio of 2.5, the WSD learning rate schedule, and a specific pre-training data mixture (cleaned CommonCrawl, Dolma, C4, Pile, the Stack, StarCoder, and other collected raw corpus; Appendix H). The paper makes no claim that the discovered power-law relationships generalize to other architectures (e.g., non-gated FFNs, Mamba-style state-space models, encoder-decoder architectures), other FFN configurations (e.g., different expansion ratios, GLU variants beyond gated FFNs), other learning rate schedules, or different pre-training data distributions. The paper acknowledges that "the data mixing policies for pre-training can also have a considerable impact on the activation sparsity, which we leave for future work" (Appendix A).

**The consequence.** The paper's prescriptive recommendations—use ReLU, deep-and-thin architectures, more training data—are validated only for one specific model architecture trained on one specific data mixture. The logspace power-law for ReLU and the vanilla power-law for SiLU (Equations 4 and 5, Figure 4) might not hold under different conditions. For example: (1) a non-gated FFN architecture (where the activation function is applied directly to the up-projection, without a separate gating projection) might exhibit different sparsity dynamics because the gating mechanism in gated FFNs provides an additional degree of freedom for learning sparse representations; (2) a pre-training data mixture with a higher proportion of code or structured data might produce different sparsity trajectories, since Song et al. (2025) found that "LLMs tend to be sparser on more formatted datasets such as codes and multiple choices;" (3) a different learning rate schedule might change the rate of neuron specialization and thus the convergence speed to the sparsity limit; (4) the fitted limit activation ratios `A_0` (Table 2) might shift with different data mixtures or architectural choices, making the specific numerical values reported in this paper non-transferable. The 2.4B out-of-sample validation (Figure 11) demonstrates that the ReLU logspace power-law generalizes to a larger model within the *same* architecture and data mixture, but this does not test generalization to *different* architectures or data distributions.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All experiments use the µP Transformer with gated FFNs and the same data mixture. There are no ablation studies varying the FFN architecture (e.g., non-gated FFN, different expansion ratios), the learning rate schedule, or the data mixture. The paper cites Song et al. (2025) for the finding that sparsity varies across data types (Section 2.3) and acknowledges the data mixture limitation in Appendix A, but does not explore it experimentally.

**Mitigation status.** Partially acknowledged. The paper notes in Appendix A that "data mixing policies for pre-training can also have a considerable impact on the activation sparsity, which we leave for future work." However, the architectural limitation is not explicitly discussed—the paper does not condition its recommendations on the use of gated FFNs or the µP Transformer, and does not discuss whether the power-law forms might depend on the gating mechanism or the µP parametrization. The 2.4B validation model tests generalization across scale but not across architecture or data.
