# LoHan: Low-Cost High-Performance Framework to Fine-Tune 100B Model on a Consumer GPU

**ArXiv:** [2403.06504](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06504)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **LoHan**, a low-cost high-performance training framework that enables efficient fine-tuning of 100B-scale large language models on a single consumer-grade GPU in a commodity server with limited main memory capacity.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **LoHan**, a low-cost high-performance training framework that enables efficient fine-tuning of 100B-scale large language models on a single consumer-grade GPU in a commodity server with limited main memory capacity. The system is evaluated on decoder-only transformer models (up to 175B parameters) on NVIDIA RTX 4090/3090/4080 GPUs. LoHan's core innovations are **active gradient offloading** (overlapping the out-of-core CPU optimizer execution with GPU backward propagation by directly consuming gradients as they arrive in main memory, rather than serializing optimizer execution as a separate stage) and **holistic traffic-aware activation swapping management** (automatically determining the optimal amount of activations to offload to main memory and NVMe SSDs versus recompute, by modeling iteration time as a convex function of swapped activation size and finding the inflection point that minimizes total epoch time). LoHan is the first system to fine-tune a 175B model on an RTX 4090 with only 256 GB main memory, achieves up to 2.32× throughput over state-of-the-art baselines when fine-tuning a 13B model, and enables a low-end consumer GPU to have up to 2.17× higher cost-effectiveness than a DGX-A100 cluster when fine-tuning a 175B model, establishing that 100B-scale fine-tuning is feasible on commodity hardware only when both model states and activations are holistically co-optimized for offloading traffic rather than managed independently.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Fine-tuning 100B-Scale Models Is Prohibitively Expensive

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is straightforward but has enormous practical consequences: **most AI researchers cannot afford to fine-tune the largest available LLMs on their own hardware.** As the paper documents in Section I, the largest open-source transformer models available for fine-tuning have grown to over 100 billion parameters, and fine-tuning a model of this scale requires storing approximately **2.6 TB of temporary and persistent tensors at peak memory usage**. Meanwhile, the most advanced data-center GPU available at the time of writing (the NVIDIA H200) provides only **188 GB of device memory** — a factor of ~14× too small to hold the full working set.

The conventional solution — aggregating device memory across many GPUs in high-end clusters — creates a severe economic barrier. The paper notes that fine-tuning a 100B-parameter model requires **32× NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 80 GB each** (costing approximately $14,177 per GPU). This places 100B-scale fine-tuning entirely outside the reach of most individual researchers and small labs. The paper explicitly frames this as a **democratization problem**: data scientists with tight budgets are locked out of working with the most capable models.

### Why This Matters: The Gap Between Model Availability and Tuning Access

This problem is particularly acute because of a structural asymmetry in the LLM ecosystem:

- **Pre-trained models are increasingly available for free.** The paper references open-source models like OPT (Zhang et al., 2022), BLOOM (Scao et al., 2022), and Llama 2 (Touvron et al., 2023), all of which can be downloaded and used without paying for pretraining. The 175B-parameter scale — exemplified by GPT-3 and its open-weight descendants — represents a "typical size of 100B-scale models" (Section I).

- **Fine-tuning them for downstream tasks remains the critical step.** The paper cites the strong demand from data scientists to adapt these pre-trained models for specific applications (Ouyang et al., 2022; Touvron et al., 2023). Without fine-tuning, the raw pre-trained model may not follow instructions, understand domain-specific terminology, or exhibit the desired behavioral properties.

- **The hardware to fine-tune them is the bottleneck.** The gap is not in model availability or interest, but in the compute infrastructure required to customize them. A researcher can download a 175B model in minutes on a fast internet connection, but cannot meaningfully adapt it without access to a cluster of high-end GPUs — or so the prevailing assumption went before this work.

The practical importance is therefore enormous: **if 100B-scale fine-tuning can be made feasible on a single consumer-grade GPU**, it would fundamentally change who can participate in LLM research and development. The paper explicitly targets this accessibility goal, noting that such a solution "would be attractive to researchers who seek to minimize LLM fine-tuning costs" (Section I).

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies a progression of increasingly ambitious offloading strategies in prior work, each addressing part of the memory problem but failing to enable efficient 100B-scale fine-tuning on commodity hardware. Understanding why each approach fails is essential to understanding the design choices in LoHan.

#### Approach 1: Offloading Only Activations to SSDs

Systems like **FlashNeuron** (Bae et al., 2021) offload activation tensors — the intermediate values computed during forward propagation that must be stored for backward propagation — from GPU memory to NVMe SSDs. This reduces GPU memory pressure but **keeps model states (parameters, optimizer states, gradients) in GPU memory**.

The failure mode is straightforward and quantitative (Section III-A, Figure 2a): model states dominate memory usage for large models. Each parameter requires:
- **P32**: 4 bytes (32-bit parameter for optimizer)
- **OS32**: 8 bytes (32-bit Adam optimizer states — first and second moment estimates)
- **G16**: 2 bytes (16-bit gradient)
- **P16**: 2 bytes (16-bit parameter copy for forward/backward computation)

This sums to **16 bytes per parameter** just for model states. For a 175B model, model states alone consume **2.8 TB**. Even if all activations are offloaded to SSDs, the model states far exceed the 24 GB capacity of a consumer GPU like the RTX 4090. The paper's prototype evaluation shows FlashNeuron **cannot fine-tune even a 6B model** on RTX 4090 (Figure 2a), which is "a common scale of today's pre-trained LLMs" — Llama 2-7B, Mistral 7B, etc. This approach is a non-starter for the targeted scale.

#### Approach 2: Offloading Model States to SSDs (ZeRO-Infinity, Colossal-AI)

Systems like **ZeRO-Infinity** (Rajbhandari et al., 2021) and **Colossal-AI** (Bian et al., 2023) take the natural next step: offload model states to NVMe SSDs to enlarge the trainable model size. Both systems also implement a **CPU Adam optimizer** — the optimizer executes on the CPU rather than GPU, which avoids transferring the massive P32 and OS32 tensors from GPU to main memory (since the CPU optimizer reads and writes them directly in main memory). These systems represent the state-of-the-art in memory-efficient LLM training at the time of LoHan's development.

However, the paper identifies **three specific and severe issues** when these systems are deployed on a commodity server with a single consumer GPU, rather than the high-end DGX clusters they were originally designed for (Section III-B):

**Issue 1: Heavy CPU optimizer execution overhead.** Both ZeRO-Infinity and Colossal-AI execute the CPU optimizer as a **separate, serialized stage** that follows the backward propagation of the entire model. The GPU is completely idle during this stage because the optimizer execution waits until all gradients from all layers are available before starting. On a high-end DGX cluster with many powerful server CPUs, the CPU optimizer completes relatively quickly. On a commodity server, however — the paper's target hardware uses dual Intel Xeon Gold 5320 CPUs — the CPU optimizer execution takes **30%–60% of a training iteration** (Figure 2c). This means the GPU is idle for a third to over half of the total training time.

The impact is quantified in Figure 2b: when fine-tuning a 13B model on RTX 4090 with ZeRO-Infinity, **the GPU is busy during only 36% of an iteration**, even at a large batch size (32) that saturates GPU compute. Colossal-AI is even worse — the GPU is busy only 12% of the time. This is fundamentally a **scheduling problem**: the useful GPU computation (forward and backward propagation) and the necessary CPU computation (optimizer execution) are serialized rather than overlapped.

**Issue 2: Excessive activation recomputation overhead.** Both systems adopt what the paper calls a "static activation management strategy." They recompute the vast majority of activations during backward propagation rather than storing them and swapping them back from main memory. Specifically, they only offload the **inter-transformer block activations** — the activation tensors passed between consecutive transformer layers — which account for only about 6% of total activations. All intra-block activations (intermediate values within each layer's attention and feed-forward computations, about 94% of the total) are recomputed from scratch during backward propagation.

Why is this a problem? On a high-end GPU with fast computation and slow PCIe, recomputation makes sense — it's cheaper to recalculate than to transfer. On a commodity GPU (RTX 4090) fine-tuning with a reasonable batch size, **GPU computation is the bottleneck**, not PCIe transfer. The paper's measurements show that when fine-tuning a 13B model with batch size 32, the GPU backward propagation without recomputation takes **1.91× longer than SSD I/O time** and **1.88× longer than GPU-CPU transfer time**. This means the system has spare PCIe bandwidth that could be used for swapping activations back from memory, but instead, it burns GPU cycles recomputing them. Figure 1a breaks this down: during the 26-second backward stage, GPU recomputation consumes 5.7 seconds (22% of the stage) while PCIe utilization from main memory to GPU (PCIeM2G) is only 24%.

This is a clear case where a strategy designed for one hardware profile (data-center GPU with high compute and relatively slow PCIe) fails on a different profile (consumer GPU with lower compute and relatively faster PCIe).

**Issue 3: Limited trainable model size under constrained main memory.** ZeRO-Infinity offloads activations only to main memory, never to SSDs. Colossal-AI does not offload activations at all, keeping them in GPU memory and recomputing as needed. Neither system offloads activations to NVMe SSDs. This means the maximum trainable model size is bounded by main memory capacity, not by SSD capacity (which is typically orders of magnitude larger). The paper estimates that **ZeRO-Infinity requires approximately 1.1 TB of main memory to fine-tune a 175B model** (Section I, Figure 1a caption). Most commodity servers ship with 128 GB to 1 TB of main memory — the paper's evaluation server itself has 768 GB. As Figure 2a shows, even with 768 GB of main memory and a batch size of 1, **ZeRO-Infinity fails to fine-tune a 175B model**. Colossal-AI, which keeps all activations in GPU memory, cannot even approach this scale.

The root cause is that these systems were "originally designed for high-end DGX servers rather than for a commodity server with a single consumer-grade GPU" (Section III-B, footnote 3), and their memory allocation strategies implicitly assume abundant main memory that simply does not exist in commodity hardware.

#### Approach 3: Naively Offloading Both Model States and Activations to SSDs (G10)

The most recent prior work, **G10** (Zhang et al., 2023), represents the closest predecessor to LoHan's approach. G10 offloads both model states and activations to unified main/NVMe memory — it is the first system that theoretically supports 100B-scale fine-tuning on hardware with scarce GPU and main memory. However, LoHan identifies three issues that make G10 unsuitable for the target commodity hardware profile (Section III-C):

**Issue 1: Heavy model state transfer overhead from GPU optimizer.** Unlike ZeRO-Infinity and Colossal-AI, G10 executes the Adam optimizer **on the GPU**, as most in-GPU training systems do. This means that during the optimizer stage, the GPU must read all model states (P32 and OS32) from SSDs into GPU memory, execute the optimizer, and write the updated states back to SSDs. For a 13B model, this is **182 GB per direction** — a massive volume of data over the PCIe bus. The paper's simulation (Figure 1b) shows that the actual GPU optimizer computation takes only **0.1 seconds**, but the PCIe transfer of model states takes **13 seconds** — 37% of the total iteration time. The GPU is effectively stalled waiting for data.

**Issue 2: High activation transfer overhead from no recomputation.** G10 takes the opposite extreme from ZeRO-Infinity: it offloads essentially all activations to SSDs without any recomputation. When fine-tuning a 13B model with batch size 32, this generates **213 GB of activation transfer** during the forward stage alone. Figure 1b shows that activation offloading takes 10 seconds, compared to 5.96 seconds of GPU computation. The GPU must wait for activations to be written out before it can proceed, creating a PCIe transfer bottleneck. Looking forward, the paper notes that if the optimizer and backward propagation were overlapped (as some existing works do), the problem becomes even more severe: PCIe transfer would account for "almost 100% of both forward and the overlapped backward-optimizer stage time," while GPU computation would be only 59% and 69% of those stages respectively. The PCIe bus becomes the bottleneck everywhere.

**Issue 3: GPUDirect dependency.** G10's architecture deeply relies on GPUDirect, an NVIDIA technology that enables direct data transfer between GPU memory and NVMe SSDs without routing through main memory (a zero-copy path). This technology is only available on data-center GPUs (A100, H100, etc.) — **consumer-grade GPUs like the RTX 4090 do not support GPUDirect**. Therefore, G10 simply cannot run on the target hardware at all, regardless of its efficiency characteristics.

### The Deeper Pattern: No Holistic Intra-Server Tensor Management

The paper's diagnosis of why all these prior approaches fail is not just a list of individual bugs, but a unified architectural critique. The common thread is what the paper calls the **lack of holistic intra-server tensor movement management** (Section I). Each prior system treats different offloading decisions in isolation:

- **ZeRO-Infinity** optimizes model state offloading but treats activation management as a static rule (offload inter-block, recompute intra-block), never considering the PCIe bandwidth that could be used for activation swapping.
- **FlashNeuron** optimizes activation offloading but ignores model states, which dominate memory for large models.
- **G10** offloads everything but treats the GPU optimizer as a requirement and throws all activations onto SSDs, creating two separate transfer bottlenecks that could have been avoided by different design choices (CPU optimizer, selective recomputation).

The paper's insight is that these offloading decisions — what to offload, where to send it (main memory or SSD), when to transfer it, and how to overlap transfers with computation — are deeply interdependent. A change to the model state offloading strategy (e.g., switching to CPU optimizer) changes the PCIe traffic patterns, which in turn changes the optimal activation management strategy (since there is now more or less PCIe bandwidth available for activation swapping). The prior systems, by treating these as separate problems solved by separate components, miss optimization opportunities that only become visible when the entire tensor flow is considered as a unified optimization problem.

### How LoHan Positions Itself

LoHan positions itself not as a new point in the design space of individual offloading techniques, but as a **framework that adds holistic offloading traffic as an optimization dimension** (Section I, Section IV-A). The key word is "holistic" — it appears throughout the paper as the differentiator from prior work.

The paper's self-positioning has three aspects:

**1. LoHan is a synthesis, not a single novel technique.** Both of LoHan's main components build on recognizable ideas: active gradient offloading is inspired by Active Messages (von Eicken et al., 1992), a concept from parallel computing where a message sender specifies a handler to run on the receiver upon message arrival. The CPU optimizer itself is from ZeRO-Offload. Activation swapping with recomputation trade-offs is studied in systems like Capuchin and Checkmate. The novelty is in orchestrating these techniques together under a unified optimization framework that explicitly models the interactions between them.

**2. LoHan targets a specific, previously unserved hardware niche.** The paper is explicit that existing systems fail on "a commodity server with a single consumer-grade GPU" because they were designed for fundamentally different hardware profiles. ZeRO-Infinity and Colossal-AI assume abundant main memory and fast CPUs (DGX clusters). G10 assumes GPUDirect and treats GPU-side optimizer execution as non-negotiable (data-center GPUs). FlashNeuron assumes model states fit in GPU memory. LoHan is the first system designed from the ground up for the profile where: (a) GPU memory is tiny (16–24 GB), (b) main memory is modest (128 GB–1 TB), (c) the CPU is relatively weak (commodity server CPUs), (d) GPUDirect is unavailable, but (e) multiple fast NVMe SSDs can be added cheaply to provide high aggregate I/O bandwidth.

**3. LoHan frames the problem as convex optimization over a continuous variable.** A distinguishing feature of LoHan's approach is the analytical formulation of the activation swapping decision. Rather than using a heuristic or learned policy, the paper formally proves that iteration time is a convex function of the amount of swapped activations ($A_{G2M}$), which means there is a single global minimum that can be found by simple search (iterating layers in order of their "offloading benefit" and computing the resulting iteration time until the inflection point is found). This gives the activation management strategy a principled foundation rather than an ad-hoc one, and it naturally handles the interaction with model state offloading because the iteration time model (Equations 1–8) incorporates all traffic sources — model state transfers, activation transfers, SSD I/O, GPU computation, and CPU optimizer execution — in its components.

The paper also positions itself practically through its evaluation claims: that LoHan is the **first system to fine-tune a 175B model on an RTX 4090 with only 256 GB of main memory**, and that it enables a **cheap consumer GPU to have higher cost-effectiveness than a DGX-A100 cluster**. These are concrete, verifiable claims that establish a new capability boundary in the democratization of large-scale AI training — not just an incremental improvement in throughput for already-accessible model sizes.

## 3. Technical Approach

This is a **systems paper** that introduces a framework for fine-tuning 100B-scale LLMs on consumer GPUs by treating **holistic offloading traffic as an optimization dimension** — all tensor movements between GPU, main memory, and NVMe SSDs are co-optimized rather than managed independently.

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

**What LoHan is:** LoHan is a deep learning training framework built on top of PyTorch that enables fine-tuning models with over 100 billion parameters on a single consumer-grade GPU (like an RTX 4090 with only 24 GB of memory) by intelligently deciding which tensors to store where (GPU, main memory, or SSDs), when to move them, and how to overlap those movements with useful computation so the GPU stays busy.

**What problem it solves and the shape of the solution:** The problem is that fine-tuning a 175B model requires ~2.6 TB of tensor storage at peak, but a consumer GPU has only 24 GB, main memory is limited (e.g., 256 GB), and the CPU is relatively weak. LoHan's solution has two complementary parts: (1) for **model states** (parameters, optimizer states, gradients), it invents "active gradient offloading" that lets the CPU optimizer start processing each layer's gradients as soon as they arrive from the GPU during backward propagation, rather than waiting for the entire backward pass to finish; (2) for **activations** (intermediate values from forward propagation needed during backward propagation), it builds a mathematical model of the total iteration time as a function of how many activations are offloaded versus recomputed, proves this function is convex (has a single global minimum), and uses that property to find the optimal offloading amount that balances PCIe transfer time against GPU recomputation time.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The LoHan system has three major components that execute in sequence:

1. **Hardware-Aware Profiler (Section IV-B)** — runs once during the first training iteration to collect measurements that characterize the specific hardware configuration (GPU throughput, PCIe bandwidths, memory capacities, per-layer floating-point operation counts). This data feeds into all subsequent decisions.

2. **Active Gradient Offloading Engine (Section IV-C)** — operates during backward propagation, intercepting each gradient tensor as the GPU produces it. Instead of stalling until all gradients are ready, the engine immediately triggers the CPU optimizer to start updating the corresponding parameters for that layer. Crucially, it overlaps three concurrent operations: SSD reads (fetching the old optimizer states and parameters for the next layer to be processed), CPU computation (running the Adam update), and SSD writes (writing the updated parameters and optimizer states back). This transforms the optimizer from a separate serial stage into a pipeline that runs concurrently with GPU backward propagation.

3. **Holistic Traffic-Aware Activation Swapping Manager (Section IV-D)** — runs conceptually before training begins (using profiling data) to determine how many activations should be offloaded to main memory and SSDs versus recomputed during backward propagation. It sorts model layers by offloading benefit (computation required to recompute divided by memory size of the activation), iterates through layers in that order to compute the total iteration time for each possible amount of offloaded activations, and finds the inflection point where iteration time is minimized. The result is a per-layer decision: store this activation (and if so, where — main memory or SSD) or discard it and recompute it later.

Information flows through LoHan as follows: at initialization, the profiler gathers hardware metrics → the activation manager computes the optimal offloading strategy for the specific hardware and model → during training, the forward pass offloads activations according to this strategy (some to main memory, some through main memory to SSDs, some discarded) → the backward pass fetches activations back from main memory and SSDs as needed, recomputes discarded ones, and simultaneously streams gradients to the CPU optimizer via the active gradient offloading engine → the CPU optimizer updates parameters and writes them back to SSDs while the GPU continues working on the next layer's backward computation.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the Hardware-Aware Profiler** (Section IV-B): explains what measurements are taken, how they're taken, and why accurate profiling is the foundation that makes the subsequent analytical optimization possible.

- **Second, Active Gradient Offloading** (Section IV-C): starts with the naive approach (serialized SSD→CPU→SSD for each gradient), identifies why it wastes GPU time, and then shows the optimized version that pipelines the SSD I/O, CPU computation, and GPU backward propagation across consecutive layers.

- **Third, Holistic Traffic-Aware Activation Swapping Management** (Section IV-D): walks through the core analytical contribution — the iteration time model, the proof that it's convex in the amount of swapped activations, the offloading benefit metric, and the algorithm that finds the optimum. This is the longest and most mathematically involved sub-section.

- **Fourth, Framework Integration** (Section IV-E): briefly explains how LoHan exposes its functionality through PyTorch APIs with minimal code changes required from users.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

#### Hardware-Aware Profiling

The profiling stage is LoHan's first component to execute, and its purpose is to gather the numerical parameters that the activation swapping manager needs for its analytical optimization model. The paper explicitly states that the profiling stage runs **only during the first training iteration** and takes about 2–3× longer than a normal iteration, which is negligible because "fine-tuning an LLM requires thousands of iterations to converge" (Section IV-B).

**What is profiled and how.** LoHan divides profiling into two categories: model-specific quantities obtained by static analysis, and hardware-specific quantities obtained by runtime measurement.

For **model-specific quantities**, LoHan parses the PyTorch model definition during initialization to extract:
- `$P$`: the total number of model parameters (a scalar, e.g., 13B for a 13-billion-parameter model)
- `$A_{all}$`: the total size of all activation tensors in bytes (depends on batch size, sequence length, hidden dimension, and number of layers)
- The number of floating-point operations (FLOPs) for each individual layer's forward computation, which can be derived from the known tensor shapes and operation types in the layer definition

For **hardware-specific quantities**, LoHan instruments the first training iteration to measure:
- `$THP_G$`: peak GPU throughput in FLOPS, measured by recording the computation time of each layer during forward propagation and dividing the known FLOP count by the elapsed time
- `$BW_G$`: maximum unidirectional PCIe bandwidth between GPU and main memory, obtained by monitoring the actual PCIe traffic volume and elapsed time during tensor transfers in the profiling iteration
- `$BW_{S2M}$`: maximum SSD-to-main-memory PCIe bandwidth
- `$BW_{M2S}$`: maximum main-memory-to-SSD PCIe bandwidth
- `$MEM_{avail}^M$`: minimum unallocated main memory during the profiling stage (this is the amount of main memory remaining after all essential buffers — parameters prefetched from SSDs, optimizer state working space, etc. — are accounted for)

The SSD bandwidths are obtained by having LoHan examine the system topology (how many SSDs are attached and to which PCIe lanes) during initialization, then monitoring actual PCIe traffic during the profiling iteration.

**Why this particular profiling strategy.** The profiling iteration deliberately adopts a **minimalist offloading strategy** — it offloads only inter-transformer block activations and recomputes everything else, exactly like ZeRO-Infinity, and offloads all model states to SSDs without optimization. This is not the production configuration; it's specifically designed to "minimize the activation offloading overhead while ensuring the recomputation process does not exceed the GPU memory limit" and to "accurately break down computation and communication costs" without any optimizations that would obscure the raw hardware capabilities. The profiler sees the system in its most pessimistic state, which means the analytical model built from these measurements will not over-promise.

**What the profiling data enables.** The collected numbers form the complete parameter set for the iteration time model in the activation swapping manager. For example, `$THP_G$` determines how long GPU computation takes for a given FLOP count; `$BW_G$` determines how long tensor transfers between GPU and main memory take; `$BW_{S2M}$` and `$BW_{M2S}$` determine how long SSD I/O takes; and `$MEM_{avail}^M$` determines the split between activations that can be stored in main memory (faster to access) versus those that must go to SSDs (slower to access but higher capacity). Without accurate profiling, the optimization model would produce incorrect predictions of iteration time, potentially choosing a suboptimal offloading strategy.

#### Active Gradient Offloading

The core challenge that active gradient offloading addresses is: **how to keep the GPU busy during the optimizer execution stage** in a system where model states (parameters `P32`, optimizer states `OS32`, gradients `G16`, parameter copies `P16`) live on SSDs and the optimizer runs on the CPU. The paper is explicit that this is the first component "that enables the out-of-core CPU optimizer execution to directly consume the gradients from GPU to CPU, so as to hide CPU optimizer execution behind GPU computation" (Section IV-C).

**Why this is hard.** The difficulty is not just about making the CPU optimizer start earlier. The optimizer processing for a single layer's gradient involves three steps that each use different hardware resources: (1) reading the old optimizer states and parameters for that layer from SSD into main memory (SSD→Main), (2) the CPU executing the Adam update algorithm on those states (CPU Compute), and (3) writing the updated optimizer states and parameters back to SSD (Main→SSD). These three steps compete for limited resources — particularly the PCIe bandwidth between main memory and SSDs, and the CPU cores. The naive approach of just starting the optimizer for each gradient as it arrives would serialize these three steps per layer, creating a slow pipeline that cannot keep up with the GPU's backward propagation.

**Naive active gradient offloading.** The paper first describes what happens if you take the direct approach: "when the gradient tensor i arrives at the main memory, its optimizer execution consists of three steps" (Section IV-C), shown in Figure 3a. For each layer `$i$` (processed in reverse order during backward propagation, from the last layer to the first):

1. **SSD→Main:** The SSD reads the old `$P32$` and `$OS32$` for layer `$i$` into a main memory buffer. These tensors occupy a total of `$4P + 8P = 12P$` bytes per layer (4 bytes per parameter for the 32-bit master weights, 8 bytes for the two Adam moment estimates).
2. **CPU Compute:** The CPU runs the Adam optimizer update using the gradient `$G16$` (already in main memory, since the GPU wrote it there), the old `$P32$` and `$OS32$`, producing updated `$P32$`, updated `$OS32$`, and a new 16-bit parameter copy `$P16$` for the next iteration's forward/backward computations. This step is purely CPU-bound.
3. **Main→SSD:** The main memory writes the updated `$P32$` and `$OS32$` plus the new `$P16$` back to SSD for layer `$i$`.

In the naive version, these three steps are serialized for each layer: step 1 completes before step 2 begins, step 2 completes before step 3 begins, and layer `$i$`'s processing completes before layer `$(i-1)$`'s processing begins. The GPU, which is simultaneously working on backward propagation for layer `$(i-1)$` and earlier layers, may finish before the CPU optimizer is done, leading to GPU idle time.

**Optimized active gradient offloading.** The key insight is that the three steps — SSD I/O, CPU computation, and GPU backward propagation — "utilize almost different computation and communication resources in a server" (Section IV-C). Specifically:

- GPU backward propagation uses the GPU's compute units and the GPU↔main memory PCIe link (for reading activations and writing gradients).
- CPU optimizer execution uses the CPU cores.
- SSD I/O uses the main memory↔SSD PCIe links and the SSD controllers.

Because these resources are largely independent, they can be **pipelined across layers**. The optimized version (Figure 3b) reorganizes the scheduling so that for any two consecutive layers `$i$` and `$(i-1)$`:

- While the **Main→SSD** write of layer `$i$`'s updated states is happening, the **CPU Compute** for layer `$(i-1)$` can execute simultaneously (the CPU is not involved in SSD writes).
- More broadly, the **SSD→Main** read for layer `$(i-1)$` (fetching its old optimizer states) overlaps with the tail of layer `$i$`'s processing.
- Meanwhile, the GPU continues its backward propagation for even earlier layers, reading activations from main memory or SSDs and writing gradients to main memory.

The result is that "LoHan can overlap the CPU computation and SSD I/O during the out-of-core optimizer execution, increasing the PCIe bandwidth utilization between main memory and SSDs" (Section IV-C). The GPU sees its gradients being consumed immediately and proceeds without waiting for a separate optimizer stage.

**What changes in the memory access pattern.** In practice, the optimized schedule means that the main memory buffer for optimizer execution is used as a **sliding window** over layers. At any moment, the buffer might contain: the old states for layer `$(i-1)$` being read from SSD, the new states for layer `$i$` being computed by the CPU, and the updated states for layer `$(i+1)$` being written back to SSD. The paper does not explicitly detail the buffer management, but the implication from Figure 3b is that at minimum, storage for two layers' optimizer states is needed in main memory simultaneously (one being read, one being written).

**Distinction from delayed parameter updates.** The paper explicitly distinguishes active gradient offloading from the "one-step delayed update" optimization in ZeRO-Offload (Section IV-C, footnote 4). ZeRO-Offload postpones the optimizer execution of iteration `$t$` until the forward propagation of iteration `$(t+1)$` begins, which means the forward propagation of iteration `$(t+1)$` reads the parameters that were updated in iteration `$(t-1)$`, not iteration `$t$`. This introduces parameter staleness — a potential convergence issue. In contrast, "LoHan does not introduce parameter staleness because forward/backward propagation reads the updated models" (Section IV-C). The active gradient offloading ensures that by the time the next iteration's forward propagation reaches layer `$i$`, the parameters for layer `$i$` have already been updated and written to SSD (or are still in the pipeline but will be complete before being read). The synchronization is maintained per-layer rather than per-iteration, which preserves the exact same mathematical semantics as synchronous (non-overlapped) training.

#### Holistic Traffic-Aware Activation Swapping Management

This is the most technically involved component of LoHan and the one that embodies the paper's core claim about holistic optimization. The problem is: **given that we are already moving model states (gradients to CPU, parameters and optimizer states to/from SSD) during training, how many activations should we offload versus recompute to minimize total iteration time?** The answer depends on the relative speeds of GPU computation, GPU↔main memory transfers, and SSD I/O — and these relative speeds are affected by the fact that model state transfers are already consuming some of that bandwidth.

**The decision variable.** The activation swapping manager optimizes over a single continuous variable: `$A_{G2M}$`, the total size in bytes of activations that are swapped (offloaded) from GPU memory to main memory during the forward pass, rather than being discarded and recomputed during the backward pass. Some of these offloaded activations will stay in main memory; the rest will be further moved to SSDs. The goal is to find the `$A_{G2M}$` that minimizes the total iteration time `$T_{iter}$`.

**The iteration time model.** The paper models `$T_{iter}$` as the sum of the forward stage time `$T_f$` and the backward stage time `$T_b$`:

$$T_{iter} = T_f + T_b$$

where `$T_f$` is the elapsed wall-clock time of the forward propagation stage and `$T_b$` is the elapsed wall-clock time of the backward propagation stage (which, with active gradient offloading, overlaps with the CPU optimizer execution).

**Why it's a sum:** the forward and backward stages are not overlapped with each other — backward propagation for a given layer cannot begin until forward propagation for that layer completes and the loss is computed. Therefore, the total time is the sum of the two stages, making the optimization separable in the sense that each stage can be analyzed independently and their contributions added.

**Modeling the forward stage time.** The forward stage time `$T_f$` is modeled as the maximum of four possible bottlenecks, because the GPU computation and the various PCIe transfers can be overlapped (the GPU↔main memory PCIe link is full-duplex, meaning data can flow in both directions simultaneously):

$$T_f = \max\left(T_f^G, \; T_f^{G2M}, \; T_f^{M2G}, \; T_f^S\right)$$

where `$T_f^G$` is the GPU computation time during forward propagation, `$T_f^{G2M}$` is the GPU-to-main-memory PCIe transfer time (for offloading activations and gradients), `$T_f^{M2G}$` is the main-memory-to-GPU PCIe transfer time (for reading the 16-bit parameter copies `$P16$` needed for computation), and `$T_f^S$` is the SSD I/O time during the forward stage.

**Why the maximum:** if all operations are perfectly pipelined, the stage time is determined by the slowest operation in the system — the bottleneck. No matter how fast the GPU computes, the forward stage cannot complete faster than the time required to transfer all the data that must move over the PCIe bus. Conversely, no matter how fast the transfers are, the stage cannot complete faster than the raw GPU computation time.

Each of these four terms is expanded into its contributing factors:

$$T_f = \max\left(\frac{FLOP_f}{THP_G}, \; \frac{A_{G2M}}{BW_G}, \; \frac{2P}{BW_G}, \; \frac{2P}{BW_{S2M}} + \frac{\alpha A_{G2M}}{BW_{M2S}}\right)$$

where `$FLOP_f$` is the number of GPU floating-point operations during the forward pass, `$THP_G$` is the peak GPU throughput in FLOPS, `$A_{G2M}$` is the total size of activations swapped from GPU to main memory, `$BW_G$` is the maximum unidirectional PCIe bandwidth between GPU and main memory, `$P$` is the number of model parameters, `$2P$` is the size of the 16-bit parameter copy (2 bytes per parameter), `$BW_{S2M}$` is the SSD-to-main-memory bandwidth, `$BW_{M2S}$` is the main-memory-to-SSD bandwidth, and `$\alpha$` is the proportion of the swapped activations `$A_{G2M}$` that go to SSDs (rather than staying in main memory).

**What this equation computes in operational terms:** it predicts the forward stage duration by separately estimating how long GPU computation takes (first term: total FLOPs divided by peak throughput), how long it takes to offload activations from GPU to main memory (second term), how long it takes to read parameters into the GPU (third term, note this is always `$2P / BW_G$` regardless of activation strategy), and how long SSD operations take (fourth term: reading old parameters from SSD plus writing some activations to SSD). The actual forward stage time is the slowest of these four parallel pipelines.

**Why the fourth term has that specific form:** the SSD component `$T_f^S$` contains `$2P / BW_{S2M}$` because during the forward stage, the SSD must read the 16-bit parameter copies (`$P16$`) for each layer into main memory before they can be transferred to GPU — this is necessary because parameters live on SSDs, not in GPU memory. It also contains `$\alpha A_{G2M} / BW_{M2S}$` because the portion of activations that exceed main memory capacity must be written from main memory to SSDs during the forward stage. The `$BW_{S2M}$` and `$BW_{M2S}$` bandwidths are different because SSDs are simplex — reads and writes use the same PCIe lanes and controllers, and the model conservatively uses the bandwidth for each direction separately rather than assuming they can be fully overlapped.

**The role of `$\alpha$` (the SSD fraction).** The parameter `$\alpha$` is not an independent variable — it is determined by how much of the offloaded activations can fit in the available main memory. The paper defines it as:

$$\alpha A_{G2M} = A_{G2M} - MEM_{avail}^M$$

where `$MEM_{avail}^M$` is the minimum unallocated main memory measured during profiling. This equation says: the amount of activations that must go to SSDs is the total swapped amount minus whatever can be accommodated in main memory (after accounting for the memory already reserved for parameter prefetching and optimizer state buffers). If `$A_{G2M} \leq MEM_{avail}^M$`, then `$\alpha = 0$` and all offloaded activations stay in fast main memory. If `$A_{G2M} > MEM_{avail}^M$`, the excess spills to SSDs.

**Why this matters.** This coupling between `$A_{G2M}$` and `$\alpha$` is where the "holistic" nature of the optimization becomes clear: the decision of how many activations to offload (`$A_{G2M}$`) directly affects how much SSD I/O is generated (`$\alpha A_{G2M}$`), which in turn affects the SSD term in the forward stage time `$T_f$` and the backward stage time `$T_b$`. A naive approach that assumed all offloaded activations go to main memory (ignoring capacity limits) or that assumed all go to SSDs (ignoring the possibility of using main memory for the first portion) would produce incorrect predictions of iteration time and therefore suboptimal offloading decisions.

**Modeling the backward stage time.** The backward stage time `$T_b$` follows a similar pattern but with additional complexity because of gradient transfers (model states being sent to the CPU) and the overlapping CPU optimizer execution:

$$T_b = \max\left(T_b^G, \; T_b^{G2M}, \; T_b^{M2G}, \; T_b^S\right)$$

Expanding:

$$T_b = \max\left(\frac{2 \cdot FLOP_f + FLOP_r}{THP_G}, \; \frac{2P}{BW_G}, \; \frac{2P + A_{G2M}}{BW_G}, \; \frac{14P + \alpha A_{G2M}}{BW_{S2M}} + \frac{14P}{BW_{M2S}}\right)$$

where `$FLOP_r$` is the number of GPU floating-point operations required for recomputing activations that were not offloaded (i.e., were discarded during forward propagation and must be regenerated during backward propagation), `$2 \cdot FLOP_f$` is the FLOPs for the actual backward gradient computation (roughly twice the forward FLOPs, a standard property of backpropagation), `$14P$` is the total size of model states per layer that must be read from or written to SSDs during the backward+optimizer stage (this includes `$P32$` at 4 bytes, `$OS32$` at 8 bytes, and `$G16$` at 2 bytes — totalling 14 bytes per parameter for the model state tensors that are read from SSD, updated, and written back), and the other terms are as defined for the forward stage.

**What this equation computes in operational terms:** the backward stage time is determined by the slowest of four bottlenecks. The first term (`$2 \cdot FLOP_f + FLOP_r$` divided by GPU throughput) is the total GPU computation: the backward propagation itself (twice the forward FLOPs, since each forward operation generates roughly two backward operations for the two gradients — with respect to input and with respect to parameters) plus the extra recomputation forward passes for discarded activations. The second term (`$2P / BW_G$`) is the time to write gradients from GPU to main memory (2 bytes per parameter, 16-bit gradients). The third term (`$(2P + A_{G2M}) / BW_G$`) is the time to read activations (and possibly parameter copies) back from main memory to GPU — this depends on `$A_{G2M}$` because the activations that were offloaded must be fetched back for use in backward propagation. The fourth term is the SSD I/O during the backward stage: reading old optimizer states and parameters (`$14P$` bytes per parameter at `$BW_{S2M}$` bandwidth) plus reading back activations that were stored on SSDs (`$\alpha A_{G2M}$` bytes at the same read bandwidth), combined with writing the updated optimizer states and parameters back to SSDs (`$14P$` bytes per parameter at `$BW_{M2S}$` write bandwidth).

**Why the CPU optimizer time does not appear as a separate term.** The paper explicitly notes that "we do not consider the CPU Adam execution time because its time is shorter than reading/writing the optimizer states from/to SSDs" (Section IV-D). In other words, the bottleneck in the optimizer pipeline is always the SSD I/O for moving optimizer states, not the actual CPU arithmetic for the Adam update. This is an empirically validated assumption for the hardware profile LoHan targets: consumer SSDs have much higher latency and lower bandwidth than the CPU cores can process the simple element-wise operations of Adam. If this assumption were violated (e.g., on a system with extremely fast SSDs and a very slow CPU), the model would underpredict the backward stage time, but the paper's profiled measurements on actual hardware confirm it holds.

**The recomputation term `$FLOP_r$` and its dependence on `$A_{G2M}$`.** The critical relationship that makes this an optimization problem is how `$FLOP_r$` decreases as `$A_{G2M}$` increases — the more activations we offload (rather than recompute), the less GPU recomputation is needed. The paper formalizes this by introducing the concept of **offloading benefit** for each layer.

**Offloading benefit (OB) and activation swapping order.** The key question is: **which** activations should be swapped first? Not all activations are equal — some are large (expensive to transfer) but cheap to recompute, while others are small (cheap to transfer) but expensive to recompute. The paper defines the offloading benefit of a layer as:

$$OB_{layer} = \frac{FLOP_{layer}}{A_{layer}}$$

where `$FLOP_{layer}$` is the number of floating-point operations required to recompute that layer's activations during the backward pass, and `$A_{layer}$` is the size in bytes of that layer's activation tensors.

**What OB measures:** it's a ratio of computational cost to memory cost — the number of FLOPs saved (by not having to recompute) per byte of activation offloaded. A layer with high OB has activations that are expensive to recompute relative to their size, making them prime candidates for offloading (you get a lot of computation savings per byte of precious PCIe bandwidth). A layer with low OB has activations that are cheap to recompute, so you're better off discarding them and using the PCIe bandwidth for something else.

**How OB determines the swapping order.** LoHan sorts all layers by their OB in descending order and offloads activations in that order — highest OB first. This is a greedy strategy: allocate the offloading budget `$A_{G2M}$` to the layers where it provides the most FLOP reduction per byte. As `$A_{G2M}$` increases, more layers' activations are offloaded, and `$FLOP_r$` decreases.

**The mathematical relationship.** For a given `$A_{G2M}$` that covers the activations of the first `$i$` layers (in OB-sorted order) plus part of layer `$(i+1)$`, the recomputation FLOPs can be expressed as:

$$FLOP_r = FLOP_f - \sum_{m=1}^{i} FLOP_m - FLOP_{i+1} \times \frac{A_{G2M} - \sum_{n=1}^{i} A_n}{A_{i+1}}$$

where `$FLOP_f$` is the total forward FLOPs (which equals the FLOPs required for full recomputation if nothing is offloaded), `$\sum_{m=1}^{i} FLOP_m$` is the FLOPs saved by fully offloading the first `$i$` layers (their activations don't need to be recomputed), and the last term accounts for the fractional layer `$(i+1)$` — it assumes that if only part of layer `$(i+1)$`'s activation budget is covered, the recomputation savings are proportional to the fraction of its activations that are offloaded.

**Why this form:** it expresses `$FLOP_r$` as a piecewise linear function of `$A_{G2M}$`. Within each interval between two consecutive layers' cumulative activation sizes, the gradient `$dFLOP_r / dA_{G2M}$` is constant and equals `$-OB_{i+1}$` — the negative of the offloading benefit of the layer currently being partially offloaded. This piecewise linear structure is what makes the convexity proof possible.

**Proving convexity of iteration time.** This is the theoretical core of the activation manager. The paper proves that `$T_{iter}$` is a convex function of `$A_{G2M}$`, which means there is exactly one global minimum and no local minima to get trapped in — a simple search from low to high `$A_{G2M}$` will find the optimum.

The proof uses four mathematical theorems stated in the paper:

- **Theorem 1:** The sum of convex functions is convex.
- **Theorem 2:** The maximum of convex functions is convex.
- **Theorem 3:** A linear function is a convex function.
- **Theorem 4:** If `$f(x)$` is convex and `$a > 0$`, then `$g(y) = a \cdot f(x) + b$` is convex in `$x$`.

The proof proceeds in two stages — proving `$T_f$` is convex, then proving `$T_b$` is convex.

**Stage 1: Proving `$T_f$` is convex.** Looking at the four terms inside the max in the `$T_f$` expression:

- The first term `$FLOP_f / THP_G$` is constant with respect to `$A_{G2M}$` — a constant function is linear (slope 0), and by Theorem 3, all linear functions are convex.
- The second term `$A_{G2M} / BW_G$` is a linear function of `$A_{G2M}$` — convex by Theorem 3.
- The third term `$2P / BW_G$` is constant with respect to `$A_{G2M}$` — convex by Theorem 3.
- The fourth term `$2P / BW_{S2M} + \alpha A_{G2M} / BW_{M2S}$` is linear in `$A_{G2M}$` (since `$\alpha A_{G2M} = A_{G2M} - MEM_{avail}^M$`, which is linear in `$A_{G2M}$` with slope `$1/BW_{M2S}$` for values above the main memory capacity) — convex by Theorem 3.

Since all four components are convex, their maximum is convex by Theorem 2. Therefore `$T_f$` is convex in `$A_{G2M}$`.

**Stage 2: Proving `$T_b$` is convex.** Looking at the four terms in the `$T_b$` max:

- The second term `$2P / BW_G$` is constant — convex by Theorem 3.
- The third term `$(2P + A_{G2M}) / BW_G$` is linear in `$A_{G2M}$` — convex by Theorem 3.
- The fourth term `$(14P + \alpha A_{G2M}) / BW_{S2M} + 14P / BW_{M2S}$` is linear in `$A_{G2M}$` (through `$\alpha A_{G2M}$`) — convex by Theorem 3.

The tricky part is the first term: `$T_b^G = (2 \cdot FLOP_f + FLOP_r) / THP_G$`. This involves `$FLOP_r$`, which is not obviously convex. The paper provides the convexity argument for `$FLOP_r$`.

**Proving `$FLOP_r$` is convex.** The paper takes the derivative of `$FLOP_r$` with respect to `$A_{G2M}$`:

$$\frac{dFLOP_r}{dA_{G2M}} = -OB_{i+1}$$

where `$OB_{i+1}$` is the offloading benefit of the layer currently being partially offloaded. Since layers are sorted by descending OB, as `$A_{G2M}$` increases and we move through the sorted layer list, `$OB_{i+1}$` is a decreasing function — each subsequent layer we partially offload has a lower OB than the previous one. Therefore `$dFLOP_r / dA_{G2M} = -OB_{i+1}$` is an **increasing** function (becoming less negative), which means `$FLOP_r$` is convex (the second derivative is non-negative).

Since `$FLOP_r$` is convex and `$THP_G > 0$`, the term `$(2 \cdot FLOP_f + FLOP_r) / THP_G$` is convex by Theorem 4 (it's an affine transformation of a convex function). Therefore all four terms are convex, and by Theorem 2, `$T_b$` is convex.

Finally, since both `$T_f$` and `$T_b$` are convex, their sum `$T_{iter}$` is convex by Theorem 1.

**Why convexity matters operationally.** Because `$T_{iter}(A_{G2M})$` is convex, there are only three possible shapes for the function, corresponding to three cases described in the paper:

- **Case 1:** `$T_{iter}$` increases monotonically as `$A_{G2M}$` increases. The minimum is at the smallest feasible `$A_{G2M}$`, which the paper sets to `$A_{interBlock}$` — the size of inter-transformer-block activations that must always be offloaded to avoid out-of-memory errors.
- **Case 2:** `$T_{iter}$` decreases monotonically as `$A_{G2M}$` increases. The minimum is at `$A_{G2M} = A_{all}$` — offload everything, recompute nothing.
- **Case 3:** `$T_{iter}$` first decreases, reaches a minimum at some `$A_{optimal}$`, then increases. This is the interesting case where a non-trivial trade-off exists.

Since the function is convex, there is no possibility of multiple local minima, no need for sophisticated optimization algorithms, and no risk of getting stuck at a suboptimal point. A simple linear scan through layers in OB order, computing `$T_{iter}$` at each step, will find the global optimum.

**The activation swapping algorithm.** Algorithm 1 in the paper describes the concrete procedure:

1. **Initialize:** `swap_list` is empty, `$T_{min}$` is set to infinity, `$A_{G2M}$` starts at 0, and `$FLOP_r$` starts at `$FLOP_f$` (full recomputation if nothing is offloaded).
2. **Sort layers** by their offloading benefit in descending order.
3. **Iterate through layers:** for each layer in sorted order:
   - Add the layer's activation size to `$A_{G2M}$`.
   - Subtract the layer's FLOPs from `$FLOP_r$` (since we are now offloading this layer, we no longer need to recompute it).
   - Compute `$T_{iter}$` using the iteration time model with the current `$A_{G2M}$` and `$FLOP_r$`.
   - If `$T_{iter} \geq T_{min}$` (the iteration time has started increasing), check if `$A_{G2M} \geq A_{interBlock}$` (ensures we're above the minimum safe threshold to avoid OOM). If yes, break — we've found the minimum and `$T_{min}$` corresponds to the previous layer. If not, continue (we can't stop yet because we must at least offload the inter-block activations).
   - If `$T_{iter} < T_{min}$`, update `$T_{min}$` and add this layer's activations to `swap_list`.
4. **If no inflection point is found** (the loop completes without `$T_{iter}$` ever increasing), LoHan matches the pattern to Case 1 or Case 2: if `$T_{iter}$` was always increasing, it's Case 1 (set `$A_{G2M} = A_{interBlock}$`); if always decreasing, it's Case 2 (set `$A_{G2M} = A_{all}$`).

**What this algorithm physically determines.** The output `swap_list` contains the activations that will be offloaded. For each layer in this list, LoHan stores its activation tensors in memory during the forward pass (first in main memory, then to SSD if main memory capacity is exceeded). For layers not in the list, activations are discarded after the forward pass and recomputed from the nearest saved activation during the backward pass.

The algorithm guarantees that `$A_{G2M} \geq A_{interBlock}$` to ensure the recomputation process does not cause out-of-memory errors on the GPU — the inter-block activations serve as the checkpoints from which recomputation can restart. This is the same safety constraint that ZeRO-Infinity uses, but LoHan extends it with the OB-based analysis to determine how many additional activations to save beyond this minimum.

**Why this approach works where prior static strategies fail.** ZeRO-Infinity's strategy (offload only inter-block activations, recompute everything else) corresponds to Case 1 — it assumes PCIe bandwidth is plentiful and GPU computation is the bottleneck, so recomputation is always cheaper than swapping. G10's strategy (offload everything) corresponds to Case 2 — it assumes SSD I/O is fast enough to make recomputation unnecessary. LoHan's analysis shows that the actual optimum depends on the specific hardware configuration, model size, and batch size, and can fall in any of the three cases. By profiling the actual hardware and analytically determining which case applies, LoHan adapts to the specific deployment scenario rather than imposing a one-size-fits-all heuristic.

#### Framework Integration

LoHan is implemented as a wrapper around PyTorch that requires minimal code changes from users. The paper shows a side-by-side comparison of standard PyTorch training code and LoHan-augmented code in Figure 4.

**Key API changes.** Instead of calling `optimizer.step()` after `loss.backward()`, the user removes the explicit optimizer step entirely. LoHan intercepts the backward propagation through the active gradient offloading engine, which automatically triggers the CPU optimizer as gradients arrive. The user wraps the model definition in a `LoHan_init()` context manager (which performs the profiling stage during the first iteration) and applies `LoHan_hook(model)` to inject hooks that manage activation offloading and data prefetching. The optimizer itself is wrapped in `LoHan_Optimizer()`.

**What the user does not need to specify.** The holistic traffic-aware activation swapping strategy is fully automatic — it runs during the profiling stage and determines the optimal offloading plan without user input. The user does not need to manually configure which layers to offload, how much main memory to reserve for activations, or what the SSD bandwidth is. All of this is handled by the profiler and the analytical model.

**Training loop semantics preserved.** The paper emphasizes that "LoHan performs the profiling stage via the LoHan_init wrapper. Compared to PyTorch, LoHan removes the optimizer execution from the serialized execution flow." The user's training loop retains the same structure (forward, loss computation, backward), but the optimizer execution is now implicit and overlapping rather than explicit and serialized.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Holistic Offloading Traffic as a Unified Optimization Dimension

The field's approach to tensor offloading prior to LoHan was fundamentally **fragmented by design**. Each system partitioned the problem into independent sub-problems: FlashNeuron optimized activation offloading while assuming model states fit in GPU memory; ZeRO-Infinity optimized model state offloading while treating activation management as a static, heuristic rule (offload inter-block, recompute intra-block); G10 offloaded everything but treated the GPU optimizer as immutable and all activations as worthy of SSD storage, creating two separate transfer bottlenecks. These were not bugs in the individual components — they were consequences of the *decomposition itself*. Each sub-problem was solved competently in isolation, but the interactions between them were invisible to the optimizer.

LoHan's core conceptual contribution is to **elevate the interactions between offloading decisions to the status of a first-class optimization target**. The paper frames this not as "we combine existing techniques" but as "we add holistic offloading traffic as an optimization dimension" (Section I, Section IV-A). This is a subtle but fundamental re-framing: the design space is not a set of independent choices (which optimizer? which activation strategy? where to store parameters?), but a single coupled system where each decision changes the resource budget available for all others.

The evidence for why this re-framing matters is distributed throughout the paper's analysis of prior systems, but it crystallizes in the activation manager's iteration time model (Equations 1–8). Notice what enters that model: the parameter size `$P$` and activation size `$A_{G2M}$` appear in *multiple* terms across *both* the forward and backward stage time calculations. The SSD bandwidth `$BW_{S2M}$` and `$BW_{M2S}$` appear in terms that include both model state transfers (`$2P$`, `$14P$`) and activation transfers (`$\alpha A_{G2M}$`). The GPU-to-main-memory bandwidth `$BW_G$` carries both gradients heading to the CPU optimizer and activations being fetched back for backward computation. The coupling is not incidental — it is the structure of the problem.

The consequence of missing this coupling is concrete and quantitative. ZeRO-Infinity's static activation strategy (only offload inter-block, recompute the rest) causes the GPU to spend 5.7 seconds (22% of the backward stage) recomputing activations while the PCIe link from main memory to GPU sits at only 24% utilization (Figure 1a). Those PCIe cycles could have been used to swap activations back, but the system's architecture — which separates activation management from model state offloading — had no mechanism to detect this misallocation. G10 makes the symmetric error: its "offload everything" strategy causes activation transfer to consume 10 seconds while GPU computation finishes in 5.96 seconds (Figure 1b), leaving the GPU idle waiting for PCIe transfers to complete because the system assumed SSD bandwidth was plentiful relative to GPU compute.

What makes LoHan's approach theoretically distinctive (not just empirically better) is that it **proves a structural property of the joint optimization problem** — convexity of iteration time in the swapped activation size — which means this is not merely a heuristic that happens to work, but a well-behaved optimization landscape with a single global minimum. The convexity proof requires modeling the full system (all traffic sources, all bandwidth limits, the OB-sorted layer ordering), and it would not hold if any of the coupled components were omitted from the model. This is an analytic contribution, not just an engineering one: it establishes that, under reasonable assumptions about the hardware profiling data, the optimal activation offloading strategy for any model-hardware combination can be found by a simple scan rather than requiring search or learned policies.

The distinction from prior work is sharp: Capuchin (Peng et al., 2020) and Checkmate (Jain et al., 2020) optimize the recomputation-vs-swapping tradeoff for activations, but they assume model states are on GPU and do not model SSD I/O or CPU optimizer execution. ZeRO-Infinity and ZeRO-Offload optimize model state offloading, but their activation strategy is a fixed heuristic. G10 offloads both, but its model does not include recomputation as a variable. LoHan is the first to prove that the *joint* optimization is convex — and this theoretical property justifies the computational efficiency of the approach (one profiling iteration + one linear scan) as well as its deployment practicality (no hyperparameter tuning needed per hardware configuration).

### Innovation 2: Active Gradient Offloading as a Scheduling Primitive, Not a Hardware Trick

The idea of overlapping computation with communication is ancient in parallel systems, and the CPU optimizer itself was introduced by ZeRO-Offload (Ren et al., 2021). What distinguishes LoHan's active gradient offloading is not the *existence* of the overlap, but the **scheduling abstraction** that enables it to work correctly under synchronous training semantics without parameter staleness.

To understand why this matters, consider the prior approaches to overlapping optimizer execution with GPU computation. ZeRO-Offload's "one-step delayed update" (explicitly discussed in Section IV-C, footnote 4) solves the scheduling problem by relaxing the correctness constraint: it postpones the optimizer execution of iteration `$t$` until the forward propagation of iteration `$(t+1)$`, which means iteration `$(t+1)$`'s forward pass reads parameters updated in iteration `$(t-1)$`, not iteration `$t$`. This introduces parameter staleness — the model uses slightly outdated weights for its forward computation. For some training configurations, this staleness is harmless; for others, it can affect convergence. The paper explicitly excludes this approach from scope ("they are beyond the scope of this paper" in footnote 2, referring to asynchronous update methods like Angel-PTM).

LoHan's active gradient offloading achieves the same GPU-CPU overlap but **preserves exact synchronous semantics**: "LoHan does not introduce parameter staleness because forward/backward propagation reads the updated models" (Section IV-C). This is possible because the active gradient offloading doesn't delay the optimizer execution to the next iteration — it executes it *within the same iteration*, during the backward pass, using a layer-by-layer pipeline. By the time the backward pass reaches layer `$i$`, the optimizer for layer `$(i+1)$` (which was processed earlier in the backward pass since layers are traversed in reverse) has already completed and written the updated parameters to SSD. The forward pass of the next iteration will read these freshly updated parameters.

The conceptual innovation is in recognizing that the backward propagation's natural layer-by-layer structure creates **slack in the dependency chain** that can be exploited without violating correctness. The backward pass for layer `$i$` does not depend on the optimizer execution for layer `$(i+1)$` — those are completely independent computations that happen to use separate hardware resources. The GPU can compute the gradient for layer `$i$` while the CPU updates layer `$(i+1)$`'s parameters and the SSD writes back layer `$(i+2)$`'s updated states. This is a fine-grained, layer-level pipeline rather than a coarse, iteration-level pipeline.

The paper's naming — "active gradient offloading" — invokes Active Messages (von Eicken et al., 1992), a concept from parallel computing where a message sender specifies a handler to run on the receiver upon message arrival. The conceptual parallel is exact: the GPU "sends" a gradient to main memory, and the "message" carries with it the specification of what to do (execute the Adam optimizer for that layer). The CPU "receives" the gradient by immediately invoking the handler, without buffering or waiting for all gradients to arrive. This transforms the optimizer from a bulk-synchronous operation into a streaming, reactive one.

The practical significance is visible in Figure 7: the optimized active gradient offloading achieves 1.22× throughput over the naive version (which serializes SSD→CPU→SSD per layer) and 1.33× over LoHan+ZeRO (which doesn't overlap backward and optimizer at all) when fine-tuning a 13B model with batch size 64. The gain is not enormous in absolute terms, but it comes essentially "for free" — it requires no additional hardware, no relaxation of training semantics, and no hyperparameter tuning. It's a pure scheduling improvement that closes the GPU utilization gap from ~36% (ZeRO-Infinity, Figure 2b) to something approaching the hardware limits.

At a higher level, this innovation suggests a design principle for future systems that offload computation from GPU to CPU: **the backward pass's layer-by-layer structure can be exploited for fine-grained pipelining, and the key to correctness is per-layer synchronization rather than per-iteration synchronization**. This principle extends beyond Adam optimizers to any per-layer post-processing of gradients (gradient clipping, quantization, compression for distributed training) and is likely to become more important as the gap between GPU compute speed and PCIe bandwidth continues to evolve.

### Innovation 3: The Offloading Benefit Metric as a Unified Theory of Activation Value

Prior work on activation recomputation versus offloading — including Checkmate (Jain et al., 2020), Capuchin (Peng et al., 2020), and DTR (Kirisame et al., 2020) — approached the problem as a combinatorial search over discrete decisions (recompute or keep, for each tensor) under a memory budget constraint. These systems used mixed-integer linear programming, dynamic programming, or heuristic search to find allocations. They are effective but computationally expensive (Checkmate's MILP solver can take minutes to hours for large graphs) and their solutions are opaque — it's difficult to understand *why* a particular tensor was chosen for recomputation.

LoHan introduces a fundamentally simpler and more interpretable approach: the **offloading benefit metric**, defined as `$OB_{layer} = FLOP_{layer} / A_{layer}$`. This single scalar — the ratio of computational cost to memory footprint — captures the essential tradeoff and leads to a greedy, provably optimal strategy (given the convexity result) that sorts layers by OB and offloads in descending order.

The conceptual leap here is recognizing that the activation management problem, when embedded in the holistic offloading framework (where model state traffic is already modeled and the optimization variable is continuous — the total swap size `$A_{G2M}$` — rather than discrete per-tensor decisions), simplifies to a **linear program with a convex objective**, which admits a greedy solution. The OB metric is the key that unlocks this simplification: it ranks layers by marginal benefit (FLOPs saved per byte offloaded), and because the objective is convex, the optimal strategy is to take layers in descending OB order until the marginal benefit of offloading one more byte equals the marginal cost (in increased PCIe transfer time and SSD I/O time).

Why is this a conceptual advance rather than just a convenient heuristic? Because it **unifies activation management with the bandwidth allocation problem**. The OB metric transforms a "which tensors to keep" decision into a "how much bandwidth to allocate to activation swapping" decision. The sorted OB list defines a cost curve: as you allocate more bandwidth to activations (by increasing `$A_{G2M}$`), you save GPU FLOPs at a rate given by the OB of the marginal layer. The optimal point is where the time saved by avoiding recomputation (marginal FLOP reduction divided by GPU throughput) equals the time cost of the additional PCIe and SSD traffic (marginal byte increase divided by the relevant bandwidth). This is an economic framing — marginal cost equals marginal benefit — applied to tensor management.

The evidence that this framing is correct and not just plausible comes from Figure 9b: LoHan's predicted optimal swapped activation sizes (stars) align closely with the empirically measured minima of the iteration time curves across four different batch sizes (24, 36, 48, 60). The curves show the predicted convex shape — iteration time first decreases as more activations are offloaded (GPU recomputation savings dominate), then increases (PCIe and SSD transfer costs dominate). The inflection point shifts with batch size because batch size changes both the total activation volume (affecting PCIe pressure) and the GPU computation per layer (affecting where the marginal cost-benefit balance point lies). The fact that a simple analytical model with profiled hardware parameters predicts this shift correctly across configurations is strong evidence that the OB-based framing captures the essential structure of the problem.

This also explains why ZeRO-Infinity's static strategy (OB essentially = 0 for intra-block activations — offload nothing, recompute everything) and G10's static strategy (OB essentially = ∞ — offload everything) are both suboptimal in general. The OB metric reveals that different layers have different tradeoff characteristics, and the optimal strategy is almost always a mixed one — offload high-OB layers, recompute low-OB layers — with the cutoff determined by the specific hardware balance between GPU compute throughput and PCIe/SSD bandwidth. The fact that the OB metric works for both LLM fine-tuning (Section V-C) and diffusion model training (Section V-H) suggests it captures a domain-independent property of the computation graph rather than being specific to transformer architectures.

### Innovation 4: Difficulty-Agnostic Democratization — Capability Expansion Without Performance Regression

Most systems papers in the ML training space claim one of two things: either "we can train larger models than before" (capability) or "we can train models faster than before" (efficiency). LoHan claims both, but the more interesting intellectual contribution is the **demonstration that these two goals are not in tension on commodity hardware** — that the same holistic approach that enables 175B-parameter fine-tuning (a capability 100× beyond what FlashNeuron can achieve) also delivers 2.32× higher throughput on a 13B model where all baselines can already operate. This is not a trivial "and also" — it suggests that the prior systems' inefficiency at small scales and their failure at large scales share a common root cause (fragmented optimization), and that fixing the fragmentation improves both simultaneously.

The evidence is in the combination of Figure 5a (LoHan achieves 2.32×, 3.46×, and 8.02× throughput over ZeRO-Offload, ZeRO-Infinity, and Colossal-AI respectively when fine-tuning a 13B model — a scale all baselines can handle) and Figure 6a (LoHan fine-tunes a 276B model where the next-best baseline, ZeRO-Infinity, tops out at 135B). The fragmentation diagnosis explains both results: ZeRO-Infinity's static activation strategy causes unnecessary recomputation at small scales (hurting throughput) and its main-memory-only activation storage limits maximum model size. Fixing the fragmentation — by adaptively choosing the activation strategy based on hardware profiling and by spilling activations to SSDs when main memory is exhausted — improves both dimensions simultaneously.

This has an important consequence for the paper's democratization narrative. A system that could only train large models but was slower than baselines at accessible scales would be a niche tool for extreme-scale work. A system that was faster at small scales but couldn't extend to large scales would be an incremental optimization of existing approaches. LoHan's simultaneous improvements in both directions mean that researchers can adopt it as their default fine-tuning framework regardless of model size — there is no need to switch between systems as model scale grows. The same code, the same automatic profiling and optimization, handles everything from a 6B model (where GPU memory pressure is modest) to a 175B model (where everything must be offloaded).

The cost-effectiveness result (Section V-I, Figure 13) adds a third dimension: LoHan on a 4× RTX 4090 server achieves up to 2.17× higher throughput per dollar than Megatron-LM on a DGX-A100 cluster. This is not just about enabling research on cheap hardware — it's about the *economic rationality* of using commodity hardware for fine-tuning even when cluster-scale resources are available. The DGX-A100 dominates in raw throughput, but its cost per token is higher. This finding has practical implications for how organizations should allocate their compute budgets: for fine-tuning workloads (which are typically shorter than pretraining and benefit from the flexibility of per-researcher hardware), a fleet of commodity GPU servers may be economically superior to a shared high-end cluster.

The conceptual contribution here is the **reframing of the democratization problem from a binary (can vs. cannot) to a continuous spectrum (cost-effectiveness across scales)**. Prior work like ZeRO-Offload claimed to "democratize billion-scale model training" but only addressed the capability axis — could you train the model at all? LoHan shows that democratization also requires efficiency parity (or superiority) at the scales that are already accessible, because researchers will naturally work across a range of model sizes. A system that only shines at the extreme edge of capability will see limited adoption; a system that is the best choice across the full spectrum of model sizes changes default behavior.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** LoHan evaluates on decoder-only transformer models with hyperparameters following GPT-3 and open-source models like OPT. The paper does not use a standard benchmark dataset for model accuracy evaluation because its focus is on training throughput and maximum trainable model size — functional correctness of the training procedure is assumed since LoHan preserves PyTorch's synchronous training semantics. For experiments that "do not require model convergence" (Section V-A), parameters and datasets are randomly initialized. The sequence length is set to 1024 and vocabulary size to 50257 across all experiments, with training performed in mixed precision.

- **Base model(s).** The paper constructs a series of decoder-only transformer models ranging from 6B to 412B parameters, with architectural hyperparameters listed in Table IV. These are not pre-trained models loaded from checkpoints, but rather structurally faithful instantiations of the GPT-3/OPT architecture family at different scales. This choice is deliberate: the paper evaluates training *infrastructure capability* (throughput, maximum size, memory usage), not model quality. The specific model sizes tested are 6B, 13B, 30B, 70B, 135B, 175B, 276B, and 412B parameters, with corresponding layer counts, attention head counts, and hidden dimensions scaling according to established conventions.

- **Metrics.** The primary metrics are: (1) **maximum trainable model size** (in billions of parameters) — the largest model that can complete a training iteration without out-of-memory errors under given hardware constraints; (2) **end-to-end training throughput** (in tokens per second, or TFLOPS for GPU-normalized comparisons) — computed as the total number of tokens processed per second of wall-clock time across a full training iteration including forward propagation, backward propagation, and optimizer execution; (3) **GPU busy time** (as a percentage of total iteration time) — measuring what fraction of each training iteration the GPU is actively computing rather than stalled; (4) **cost-effectiveness** (in tokens per second per thousand dollars of hardware cost) — throughput divided by estimated server price. For throughput measurements, the paper reports TFLOPS as the achieved floating-point operations per second, normalized against the peak TFLOPS measured by benchmarking a single transformer block inside the GPU without any PCIe traffic.

- **Baselines.** Five baselines are compared: (1) **ZeRO-Infinity** (Rajbhandari et al., 2021) from DeepSpeed v0.9.3 — offloads model states to NVMe SSDs, swaps inter-transformer block activations to main memory, recomputes intra-block activations, and executes the CPU Adam optimizer as a separate serialized stage; (2) **ZeRO-Offload** (Ren et al., 2021) also from DeepSpeed v0.9.3 — similar to ZeRO-Infinity but offloads model states only to main memory (not SSDs), with the one-step delayed optimizer disabled "since it introduces parameter staleness" (Section V-A); (3) **Colossal-AI** v0.3.5 (Bian et al., 2023) with the Gemini memory manager enabled — keeps inter-block activations in GPU memory, recomputes intra-block activations, and does not offload activations to main memory or SSDs; (4) **FlashNeuron** (Bae et al., 2021) — a prototype implemented by the authors using POSIX file API instead of GPUDirect to offload activations to main memory and then to SSDs, while keeping model states in GPU memory (this is necessary because consumer GPUs lack GPUDirect); (5) **Megatron-LM** (Narayanan et al., 2022) on a DGX-A100 cluster with tensor parallelism — used only in the cost-effectiveness comparison (Section V-I), representing the high-end cluster alternative with no data offloading. For the diffusion model experiments, **Fast-DiT** (Jin and Xie, 2024) serves as an additional baseline.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute in multiple units depending on the experiment: **tokens per second** for raw throughput (tokens processed per second of wall-clock time), **TFLOPS** for GPU-normalized throughput (achieved floating-point operations per second divided by peak TFLOPS for the GPU model), and **tokens per second per thousand dollars** for cost-effectiveness (throughput divided by estimated hardware cost, normalized to thousands of dollars). All measurements include the full training iteration — forward pass, backward pass, and optimizer execution — not just GPU computation time. For the cost-effectiveness comparison, hardware prices are estimated as: DGX-A100 server with 8× A100-80GB GPUs at $200,000; commodity 4U server without GPUs or SSDs at $14,098; RTX 4090 at $1,600 each; Intel P5510 3.84TB SSDs at $308 each (Table VII). These prices are sourced from vendor listings and prior work (Feng et al., 2023).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or statistical significance testing in the traditional ML sense, because it evaluates infrastructure throughput and capability, not model quality metrics. The evaluation protocol instead uses: (1) **profiling-only first iteration** — the hardware-aware profiling runs during the first training iteration and takes 2–3× longer than a normal iteration, with subsequent iterations reflecting steady-state performance; (2) **batch size variation** — throughput is measured across multiple batch sizes (typically powers of 2 from 8 to 64 or 128) to show performance scaling; (3) **main memory capacity variation** — maximum trainable model size is tested under different artificially limited main memory capacities (achieved by pinning memory and disabling Linux swap) at 128 GB, 256 GB, 384 GB, 512 GB, 640 GB, and 768 GB; (4) **SSD count variation** — throughput is measured with 1, 3, 6, and 12 SSDs to show I/O bandwidth scaling; (5) **hardware variation** — experiments are repeated on three consumer GPUs (RTX 4090, 3090, and 4080) and on a 4-GPU server. The paper does not report confidence intervals or run-to-run variance for throughput numbers, which is typical for systems papers measuring steady-state training infrastructure performance (variance is typically low once the system reaches steady state after the first few iterations).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Maximum Trainable Model Size

**Headline finding:** LoHan is the first system to fine-tune a 175B model on RTX 4090 with only 256 GB main memory, and can fine-tune up to a 276B model with 768 GB main memory — 2.04× larger than ZeRO-Infinity's maximum of 135B under the same conditions (Section V-B, Figure 6a).

The maximum trainable model size experiments (Section V-B, Figure 6) test the capability boundary: what is the largest model that can complete a training iteration on given hardware without running out of memory? The batch size is set to 1 to minimize activation memory pressure and isolate the limitation to model state and baseline activation storage.

**Comparison across baselines and hardware configurations (Figure 6):**

- **On RTX 4090 and 3090 (both 24 GB device memory):** LoHan can fine-tune a 276B model with 768 GB main memory, while ZeRO-Infinity (the best baseline for maximum model size) tops out at 135B. ZeRO-Offload, which keeps all model states in main memory without SSD offloading, is limited to smaller models at any given main memory capacity. Colossal-AI and FlashNeuron cannot approach this scale at all — Colossal-AI keeps activations in GPU memory, and FlashNeuron keeps model states in GPU memory, both of which become infeasible for models beyond single-digit billions of parameters on a 24 GB GPU.

- **On RTX 4080 (16 GB device memory):** With the tighter GPU memory constraint, the maximum model sizes decrease across the board, but LoHan maintains its relative advantage. With 256 GB main memory, LoHan fine-tunes a 175B model on RTX 4080 — a configuration "reachable by most researchers" (Section V-B). ZeRO-Infinity cannot fine-tune a 175B model even with 768 GB main memory on this GPU.

- **Under varying main memory capacities:** As main memory decreases from 768 GB to 128 GB, LoHan's maximum trainable model size degrades gracefully. The key factor is that LoHan offloads activations to SSDs when main memory is exhausted, so main memory capacity primarily affects performance (through SSD I/O) rather than feasibility. ZeRO-Infinity, which offloads activations only to main memory, hits a hard wall when main memory is insufficient for activations plus model state buffers — this is why it requires ~1.1 TB to fine-tune a 175B model (Section I) and fails at 768 GB. The paper specifically notes that "LoHan succeeds in training a 175B model even with only 256 GB main memory and RTX 4080" (Section V-B).

**Why FlashNeuron and Colossal-AI fail at single-digit billions of parameters:** FlashNeuron keeps model states (parameters, optimizer states, gradients, parameter copies) in GPU memory. At 16 bytes per parameter (Table II: 4B for P32, 8B for OS32, 2B for G16, 2B for P16), even a 6B model requires 96 GB just for model states — 4× the RTX 4090's 24 GB capacity. Colossal-AI keeps all activations in GPU memory (with recomputation from checkpoints), which for a 13B model with batch size 1 would already exceed GPU memory limits. The paper's prototype measurement (Figure 2a) confirms FlashNeuron cannot fine-tune a 6B model on RTX 4090.

#### End-to-End Throughput Comparison

**Headline finding:** When fine-tuning a 13B model on RTX 4090, LoHan achieves 2.32×, 3.46×, and 8.02× higher throughput over ZeRO-Offload, ZeRO-Infinity, and Colossal-AI respectively (Section V-C, Figure 5a). The throughput advantage persists across batch sizes, GPU models, and model sizes.

**Throughput vs. batch size on RTX 4090 (Figure 5a).** The 13B model is fine-tuned at batch sizes of 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128. LoHan's throughput scales with batch size more efficiently than all baselines:

- At batch size 64 (the largest common batch size where all systems can operate), LoHan achieves approximately 1,300 tokens/second, compared to ZeRO-Offload at ~560 tokens/second (2.32×), ZeRO-Infinity at ~375 tokens/second (3.46×), and Colossal-AI at ~162 tokens/second (8.02×).
- At batch size 128 (where LoHan can still operate but some baselines may hit memory limits), LoHan achieves approximately 1,450 tokens/second.
- Colossal-AI's extremely low throughput (162 tokens/second even at batch size 64) is attributed to keeping activations in GPU memory and recomputing intra-block activations without any offloading to main memory or SSDs, causing severe memory pressure that limits batch size and creates constant GPU memory management overhead.

**Throughput vs. batch size on RTX 3090 (Figure 5b).** The same experiment repeated on the RTX 3090 shows consistent relative advantages: LoHan achieves 1.57×, 2.48×, and 4.72× improvements over ZeRO-Offload, ZeRO-Infinity, and Colossal-AI respectively. The absolute throughput is lower across all systems (RTX 3090 has fewer CUDA cores and slower memory than RTX 4090), but the relative ordering is preserved. The smaller relative gain over ZeRO-Offload (1.57× vs. 2.32×) may reflect the RTX 3090's lower compute-to-bandwidth ratio, which changes the bottleneck structure and reduces the benefit of LoHan's activation management strategy.

**Throughput vs. model size on RTX 4090 (Figure 5c).** The paper measures maximum throughput (in TFLOPS) when fine-tuning models of increasing size — 13B, 30B, 70B, 135B, and 175B — and compares against the peak TFLOPS measured by benchmarking a single transformer block inside the GPU without PCIe traffic (shown as the green horizontal line):

- **At 13B and 30B:** LoHan achieves 90%–95% of peak TFLOPS, indicating the system is primarily GPU-compute-bound and offloading overhead is well-masked.
- **At 70B:** LoHan maintains high utilization, while ZeRO-Infinity and ZeRO-Offload achieve only ~40% of peak TFLOPS at best.
- **At 135B:** LoHan's throughput begins to drop more noticeably as model state transfer volume increases, but is still far above baselines.
- **At 175B:** LoHan achieves ~53% of peak TFLOPS. This is the largest model all baselines can be compared against — ZeRO-Infinity at its maximum throughput for 135B (its largest trainable model) achieves significantly lower TFLOPS. The paper notes that at 175B, "a single layer of a large model has a large size of parameters and activations, thus the allowable batch size is small to fit in limited GPU memory" (Section V-C), which reduces GPU utilization because smaller batches provide less parallelism to saturate the GPU's compute units.

**What these TFLOPS numbers mean in practice.** A system achieving 90% of peak TFLOPS is spending only 10% of its time on non-compute activities (data movement, synchronization, CPU optimizer). A system at 40% is spending 60% of its time stalled. The difference between LoHan's 90%–95% at small scales and ZeRO-Infinity's ~40% represents the aggregate effect of all three issues identified in Section III: serialized CPU optimizer execution, excessive activation recomputation, and static activation management. At 175B, LoHan's drop to 53% reflects the fundamental challenge of insufficient GPU memory for even a single layer's full working set, which forces smaller batch sizes and exposes PCIe latency that cannot be fully hidden.

#### Effect of Active Gradient Offloading

**Headline finding:** Optimized active gradient offloading achieves 1.22× throughput over naive active gradient offloading and 1.33× over no overlap (LoHan+ZeRO) when fine-tuning a 13B model with batch size 64 on RTX 4090 (Section V-D, Figure 7a).

**Three-way comparison (Figure 7).** The paper tests LoHan with three variants that isolate the active gradient offloading contribution:

- **LoHan+ZeRO:** The backward stage and CPU optimizer execution are serialized, as in ZeRO-Infinity. No overlap.
- **LoHan Naïve:** Active gradient offloading is used, but with the naive scheduling where SSD→Main, CPU Compute, and Main→SSD are serialized for each layer (Figure 3a).
- **LoHan Optimized:** Active gradient offloading with the optimized scheduling that pipelines SSD I/O, CPU computation, and GPU backward propagation across consecutive layers (Figure 3b).

**Results for 13B model (Figure 7a):** At batch size 64, LoHan Optimized achieves ~1,280 tokens/second, compared to ~1,050 tokens/second for LoHan Naïve (1.22×) and ~960 tokens/second for LoHan+ZeRO (1.33×). The gain of Optimized over Naïve demonstrates that pipelining SSD I/O and CPU computation across layers matters — the naive version still wastes PCIe and CPU cycles on serialized per-layer processing. The gain of Naïve over LoHan+ZeRO demonstrates that even the imperfect overlap is better than no overlap at all.

At batch size 8, the throughput gap narrows: LoHan Optimized achieves approximately 900 tokens/second vs. LoHan+ZeRO at ~830 tokens/second (about 1.08×). The paper explains this as "GPU backward propagation costs significantly less time than CPU optimizer, thus resulting in fewer overlapping opportunities" (Section V-D). At very small batch sizes, the backward pass is so fast that there is less GPU computation to overlap with, reducing the pipeline's effectiveness.

**Results for 175B model (Figure 7b):** The same experiment on the 175B model (batch sizes 8 and 16, the maximum that fit in GPU memory) shows the Optimized version maintaining its advantage, though at lower absolute throughput (approximately 60 tokens/second at batch size 16 for LoHan Optimized vs. ~45 for LoHan+ZeRO). The smaller batch sizes forced by the 175B model's memory requirements compress the overlapping opportunity window, but the optimized pipeline still extracts meaningful gains.

#### Effect of Holistic Traffic-Aware Activation Management

This experiment has two sub-components: demonstrating the benefit of swapping activations to SSDs (rather than only to main memory) for maximum trainable model size, and demonstrating the benefit of the activation management strategy for throughput.

**Benefit of Offloading Activations to SSDs (Figure 8).** LoHan Optimized (which can offload activations to both main memory and SSDs) is compared against LoHan+CpuAct (which offloads activations only to main memory). Both implementations otherwise follow the same training procedure — the only difference is where activations can be stored.

With 128 GB main memory (Figure 8a), LoHan Optimized can fine-tune 2×–5× larger models than LoHan+CpuAct. For example, at batch size 24, LoHan Optimized fine-tunes a ~105B model while LoHan+CpuAct is limited to ~35B. The gap narrows at very large batch sizes (e.g., batch size 60) because the maximum trainable model size becomes bounded by GPU memory capacity (accommodating a single layer's activations) rather than main memory capacity — when the batch is large enough that even one layer's activations nearly fill GPU memory, the ability to offload to SSDs provides no additional benefit.

With 256 GB main memory (Figure 8b), the same pattern holds but the absolute model sizes are larger. At batch size 24, LoHan Optimized approaches ~250B while LoHan+CpuAct reaches ~65B. At batch size 60, both are limited to ~65B — the GPU memory constraint dominates regardless of main memory or SSD capacity.

**Why this matters for the 175B claim.** The paper's headline claim — that LoHan fine-tunes a 175B model on RTX 4090 with 256 GB main memory — depends on activation offloading to SSDs. LoHan+CpuAct with 256 GB main memory would fail at this scale because the activations (even for batch size 1) exceed what can be stored in main memory alongside the model state buffers. The SSD spillover is not an optimization; it's a necessity for reaching 100B+ scale.

**Benefit of the Activation Management Strategy (Figure 9a).** The paper compares LoHan Optimized against four alternative activation management strategies, all built on the same base system (model states offloaded to SSDs, CPU optimizer):

- **LoHan+ZeRO:** Statically swaps only inter-layer activations to main memory and recomputes the rest (the ZeRO-Infinity strategy).
- **LoHan+Cap:** Uses Capuchin's (Peng et al., 2020) profiling-based approach to decide which activations to keep, recompute, or swap to CPU memory — but does not consider SSD offloading.
- **LoHan+G10:** Uses G10's (Zhang et al., 2023) approach of smartly keeping or swapping activations to SSDs based on inactive time measurement — but does not use recomputation, offloading everything instead.
- **LoHan+CM:** Uses Checkmate's (Jain et al., 2020) cost-model and MILP solver to decide which activations to recompute or offload to main memory — but does not consider SSD offloading.

The experiment fine-tunes a 70B model on RTX 4090 with varying main memory capacities (128 GB, 256 GB, 512 GB). Table V shows the batch sizes each strategy can achieve, which is the first-order effect: LoHan+ZeRO, LoHan+Cap, and LoHan+CM are limited to smaller batch sizes at lower main memory capacities because they cannot offload activations to SSDs. LoHan+CM even fails entirely at 128 GB. LoHan Optimized and LoHan+G10 both achieve batch size 32 across all main memory capacities because they can offload to SSDs.

With the same batch size (e.g., batch size 32 at 512 GB main memory), LoHan Optimized achieves higher throughput than all alternatives. The paper attributes this to LoHan's strategy being "holistic, considering the traffic from both activations and model states" (Section V-E). The specific advantage over LoHan+G10 (which also uses SSD offloading) is G10's no-recomputation policy: G10 offloads all activations, creating a PCIe bottleneck that LoHan avoids by selectively recomputing low-OB activations.

**Correctness of the optimality prediction (Figure 9b).** The paper validates the convex iteration time model by measuring actual iteration time for the 13B model at different swapped activation sizes and four batch sizes (24, 36, 48, 60). The predicted optimal sizes (stars) closely match the empirical minima:

- At batch size 24, the empirical iteration time is monotonically increasing with swapped activation size — Case 1 from the convexity analysis (PCIe transfer is the bottleneck, so offloading more activations only adds cost). The predicted optimum is at the minimum feasible swap size.
- At batch sizes 36, 48, and 60, the curves show the characteristic convex shape — iteration time first decreases (GPU recomputation savings dominate), reaches a minimum, then increases (PCIe/SSD transfer costs dominate). The predicted star aligns with the valley in each case, and the optimal swap size shifts rightward (more activations offloaded) as batch size increases, consistent with larger batches having more GPU computation to save per byte offloaded.

This validation is critical because the entire activation management strategy depends on the convexity assumption. If the empirical curves had shown multiple local minima, flat regions, or non-convex behavior, the greedy OB-based search would not be guaranteed to find the global optimum.

#### Effect of the Number of SSDs

**Headline finding:** LoHan achieves near-linear throughput scaling as SSD count increases from 1 to 3, after which the bottleneck shifts to GPU computation and GPU-main memory PCIe transfer, with diminishing returns beyond 6 SSDs (Section V-F, Figure 10).

**Throughput scaling for 135B model (Figure 10a).** When fine-tuning the largest model ZeRO-Infinity can handle (135B) on RTX 4090:

- LoHan's throughput scales nearly linearly from 1 to 3 SSDs (approximately 25 → 65 tokens/second), indicating SSD I/O is the bottleneck in this regime and multiple SSDs effectively aggregate bandwidth.
- From 6 to 12 SSDs, the gains are marginal (reaching ~85–90 tokens/second), because the bottleneck has shifted to GPU computation and GPU-main memory PCIe transfer — adding more SSD bandwidth no longer helps.
- ZeRO-Infinity's throughput grows slowly across all SSD counts (from ~30 to ~45 tokens/second from 1 to 12 SSDs) because "ZeRO-Infinity almost serializes GPU computation, CPU computation, and SSD access, thus the I/O bandwidth of SSDs is not well utilized" (Section V-F). The serialization means that even with abundant SSD bandwidth, the GPU spends most of its time idle waiting for the CPU optimizer to complete, so faster SSDs provide limited benefit.

**Throughput scaling for 13B model (Figure 10b).** The 13B model is tested at three batch sizes (32, 48, 64) with varying SSD counts:

- With batch size 32, LoHan needs 12 SSDs to achieve ~135 TFLOPS (near maximum).
- With batch size 48, only 6 SSDs are needed to reach the maximum throughput plateau (~145 TFLOPS).
- With batch size 64, only 3 SSDs are sufficient.

This pattern is explained by the relationship between batch size and the GPU computation-to-I/O ratio. Larger batch sizes mean more GPU computation per byte of model state transferred, so the GPU becomes the bottleneck at lower I/O bandwidth. Conversely, smaller batch sizes are more I/O-intensive, requiring more SSD bandwidth to keep the GPU fed. The practical implication is that researchers can tune their SSD investment based on typical batch sizes: smaller batches (necessary for very large models that barely fit) benefit from more SSDs; larger batches (for smaller models) need fewer.

**Why the absolute throughput in TFLOPS is lower at batch size 32 than 64 even with sufficient SSDs.** This is a batch size effect separate from I/O: smaller batches provide less parallelism to saturate the GPU's compute units, so peak achievable TFLOPS is lower regardless of I/O bandwidth. The paper's green reference line (peak TFLOPS from benchmarking a single transformer block) is measured at a batch size that saturates the GPU; the training throughput will approach but not reach this line unless the training batch size is also large enough to fully utilize the GPU.

#### Performance on Multi-GPU Server

**Headline finding:** LoHan's optimizations extend to multi-GPU configurations, achieving 2.21× and 1.69× throughput over ZeRO-Infinity when fine-tuning 13B and 70B models respectively on 4× RTX 4090 GPUs (Section V-G, Figure 11).

**Experimental setup.** The multi-GPU server has the same CPU, main memory, and SSD configuration as the single-GPU server, but with 4× RTX 4090 GPUs (the maximum supported within the server's power supply). The paper uses a global batch size that is distributed across GPUs (data parallelism). ZeRO-Infinity is the only baseline tested because it is the only one of the prior systems that can fine-tune a 70B model on 4 GPUs — "even though ZeRO-Infinity can fine-tune the 135B model with a single RTX 4090, it can only fine-tune the 70B model on the multi-GPU server because of the additional GPU and main memory overhead introduced by multi-GPU synchronization and multiprocessing" (Section V-G, footnote 6).

**Results for 13B model (Figure 11a, 11c).** On 2 GPUs, LoHan achieves approximately 1,750 tokens/second at global batch size 128 vs. ZeRO-Infinity at ~1,100 (1.59×). On 4 GPUs, LoHan reaches ~3,520 tokens/second at global batch size 512 vs. ZeRO-Infinity at ~1,590 (2.21×). Both systems show near-linear scaling from 2 to 4 GPUs for the 13B model — LoHan's throughput roughly doubles when GPU count doubles.

**Results for 70B model (Figure 11b, 11d).** The larger model shows more constrained scaling. On 2 GPUs, LoHan achieves ~215 tokens/second at global batch size 64 vs. ZeRO-Infinity at ~135 (1.59×). On 4 GPUs, LoHan reaches ~385 tokens/second at global batch size 128 vs. ZeRO-Infinity at ~228 (1.69×). The smaller relative gain over the 2-GPU case (1.69× on 4 GPUs vs. 1.59× on 2 GPUs) may reflect the increased communication overhead of synchronizing gradients across more GPUs, which partially offsets LoHan's computational efficiency advantages.

**Why these results matter.** They demonstrate that LoHan is not limited to single-GPU scenarios — its holistic offloading management works under data parallelism, where each GPU independently manages its portion of the batch and communicates gradients. The paper notes two reasons for LoHan's advantage: "First, LoHan offloads the activations to SSDs, thus allowing the fine-tuning with a larger batch size. Second, LoHan considers holistic offloading traffic as an optimization dimension, and thus achieves higher throughput than ZeRO-Infinity even with the same batch size" (Section V-G). The first point (larger batch sizes) is particularly important for multi-GPU training, where per-GPU batch size directly affects scaling efficiency.

#### Performance on Diffusion Models

**Headline finding:** LoHan's optimizations generalize beyond language models, enabling fine-tuning of larger diffusion models than Fast-DiT and achieving higher throughput even at the same model size (Section V-H, Figure 12).

**Experimental setup.** The paper adopts the DiT-XL/2 architecture (Peebles and Xie, 2023) and scales it to six sizes: 0.67B, 0.90B, 1.4B, 10B, 20B, and 40B parameters, with architectural details in Table VI. The input image size is 512×512. Fast-DiT (Jin and Xie, 2024) is the state-of-the-art open-source training framework for DiT models and serves as the baseline.

**Results (Figure 12):** Fast-DiT encounters out-of-memory errors at 1.4B parameters and cannot train anything larger — because it keeps all tensors in GPU memory without offloading to main memory or SSDs. LoHan successfully fine-tunes all tested model sizes up to 40B, by offloading both model states and activations to main memory and SSDs.

For the model sizes where Fast-DiT can operate (0.67B, 0.90B, and 1.4B), LoHan achieves higher throughput: approximately 130, 110, and 90 images/second respectively, compared to Fast-DiT at approximately 45, 30, and 18 images/second. The paper attributes this to two factors: "1) Fast-DiT suffers from low throughput due to small trainable batch size as the model size grows (e.g., 1.4B), while LoHan allows the fine-tuning with high batch size; and 2) LoHan's activation management strategy reduces the fine-tuning time compared to Fast-DiT's static activation swapping strategy" (Section V-H). The first factor is a capability advantage (Fast-DiT is memory-limited to smaller batches); the second is an efficiency advantage (LoHan's OB-based strategy outperforms Fast-DiT's static strategy even at the same batch size).

This experiment is important for establishing generality: the OB metric (FLOPs per byte of activation) and the convex iteration time model are not specific to transformer language models. They apply to any architecture with a stack of layers where activations from forward propagation are needed during backward propagation — which describes virtually all deep neural networks.

#### Cost-Effectiveness Comparison

**Headline finding:** LoHan on a 4× RTX 4090 server achieves up to 2.17× higher cost-effectiveness (tokens per second per thousand dollars) than Megatron-LM on a DGX-A100 cluster when fine-tuning a 30B model (Section V-I, Figure 13).

**Experimental setup.** The 30B model is the largest that Megatron-LM can fine-tune on the DGX-A100 machine using tensor parallelism (without data offloading). The DGX-A100 server has 8× A100-80GB GPUs with NVLink and is priced at $200,000. LoHan's server has 4× RTX 4090 GPUs, a commodity 4U server ($14,098), and varying numbers of Intel P5510 SSDs ($308 each). Cost-effectiveness is computed as throughput (tokens/second) divided by total server price (in thousands of dollars).

**Results (Figure 13):** LoHan achieves ~42 tokens/second per thousand dollars with 1 SSD, increasing to a peak of ~55 with 6 SSDs, then decreasing to ~48 with 12 SSDs. Megatron-LM on DGX-A100 achieves ~25.4 tokens/second per thousand dollars. The peak cost-effectiveness of LoHan (6 SSDs) is 2.17× that of the DGX-A100.

The non-monotonic relationship with SSD count — cost-effectiveness increases from 1 to 6 SSDs then decreases from 6 to 12 — is because adding SSDs has two competing effects on the cost-effectiveness ratio: it increases throughput (numerator) but also increases cost (denominator). Beyond the optimal point (6 SSDs), the marginal throughput gain from additional SSDs (Figure 10a shows diminishing returns) is smaller than the marginal cost increase, so cost-effectiveness drops. This suggests an economically optimal SSD configuration exists and can be identified using LoHan's throughput model combined with hardware prices.

**Why this comparison is conservative.** The DGX-A100 baseline uses Megatron-LM with tensor parallelism and no data offloading — this is the standard high-end approach that keeps everything in GPU memory and leverages the 640 GB of aggregate device memory (8 × 80 GB). The comparison does NOT give the DGX-A100 any offloading capabilities (e.g., ZeRO-Infinity on A100s), which would increase its throughput but also add software complexity. The paper is comparing LoHan's commodity-hardware-plus-offloading approach against the native high-end-hardware-without-offloading approach. A head-to-head where both systems use their respective optimal configurations would be more complete, but the paper's chosen comparison captures the practical decision researchers face: buy a DGX cluster or build a commodity server with offloading software.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Active gradient offloading: Naïve vs. Optimized (Figure 7).** The optimized version (pipelining SSD I/O, CPU compute, and GPU backward propagation across consecutive layers) achieves 1.22× throughput over the naive version (serializing the three steps per layer) on a 13B model with batch size 64. The gain is smaller at batch size 8 (1.08×) because the backward pass is shorter, reducing overlap opportunities. On the 175B model (batch sizes 8 and 16), the Optimized version maintains its advantage but at lower absolute gains due to smaller batch sizes forced by memory constraints. This ablation isolates the scheduling improvement from the basic idea of overlapping optimizer with backward propagation.

- **Activation offloading destination: SSD vs. main memory only (Figure 8).** LoHan+CpuAct (activations only to main memory) is compared against LoHan Optimized (activations to both main memory and SSDs). With 128 GB main memory, SSD offloading enables 2×–5× larger trainable models. With 256 GB main memory, the gap narrows at large batch sizes because GPU memory (not main memory or SSD capacity) becomes the binding constraint — when a single layer's activations fill GPU memory at large batch sizes, additional offloading capacity provides no benefit. This ablation demonstrates that SSD offloading of activations is not a performance optimization for 100B-scale fine-tuning, but a *necessity* — the main memory capacity of commodity servers is insufficient to hold all activations for a 175B model even at batch size 1.

- **Activation management strategy: Holistic vs. prior art (Figure 9a, Table V).** Five strategies are compared on a 70B model: LoHan Optimized (holistic, SSD-aware), LoHan+ZeRO (static inter-block-only), LoHan+Cap (Capuchin's CPU-memory-only strategy), LoHan+G10 (G10's SSD-offload-without-recomputation strategy), and LoHan+CM (Checkmate's MILP-based CPU-memory-only strategy). At 128 GB main memory, LoHan+CM fails entirely (cannot fit the model). At all memory capacities, LoHan+ZeRO and LoHan+Cap are limited to smaller batch sizes because they cannot offload activations to SSDs. At the same batch size (e.g., 32 with 512 GB memory), LoHan Optimized achieves the highest throughput, outperforming LoHan+G10 (which offloads all activations without recomputation and thus creates a PCIe bottleneck) and the CPU-memory-only strategies. This ablation demonstrates that both SSD offloading *and* selective recomputation are necessary — neither alone is sufficient for optimal performance.

- **Optimality prediction accuracy (Figure 9b).** The predicted optimal swapped activation sizes (from the convex iteration time model) are compared against empirical iteration time measurements for a 13B model at four batch sizes. At all batch sizes, the predicted optimum lies at or very near the empirical minimum of the iteration time curve. At batch size 24, the monotonic increase (Case 1) is correctly identified. At batch sizes 36, 48, and 60, the predicted convex minimum aligns with the measured valley, and the optimal swap size shifts rightward with increasing batch size as predicted. This validates the analytical model that underpins the activation management strategy.

- **Number of SSDs (Figure 10).** Throughput scaling with SSD count shows near-linear scaling from 1 to 3 SSDs for a 135B model, confirming that SSD I/O is the bottleneck in this regime and that LoHan effectively aggregates bandwidth. Beyond 6 SSDs, throughput saturates as the bottleneck shifts to GPU compute and GPU-main memory PCIe transfer. For a 13B model, the saturation point depends on batch size: 12 SSDs for batch size 32, 6 SSDs for batch size 48, and 3 SSDs for batch size 64. This ablation provides practical guidance for SSD provisioning based on expected batch sizes.

- **Multi-GPU scaling (Figure 11).** LoHan's throughput scales from 2 GPUs to 4 GPUs for both 13B and 70B models, demonstrating that the holistic offloading strategy does not interfere with data parallelism. The 70B model shows less-than-linear scaling (1.69× on 4 GPUs vs. 1.59× on 2 GPUs, relative to ZeRO-Infinity), attributable to communication overhead from gradient synchronization.

- **Diffusion model generalization (Figure 12).** LoHan successfully fine-tunes diffusion models up to 40B parameters where Fast-DiT fails beyond 1.4B, and achieves higher throughput at the same model sizes (e.g., ~90 vs. ~18 images/second at 1.4B). This demonstrates that the OB-based activation strategy and active gradient offloading are not specific to language model architectures or training dynamics.

- **Cost-effectiveness sensitivity to SSD count (Figure 13).** Peak cost-effectiveness is achieved at 6 SSDs, with both fewer (1–3) and more (12) SSDs reducing the throughput-per-dollar ratio. This represents an implicit ablation of the SSD provisioning decision, showing that blindly maximizing SSD count is economically suboptimal even when it marginally improves throughput.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: LoHan is the first to fine-tune a 175B model on an RTX 4090 and 256 GB main memory.

**What the experiments show.** Figure 6a and Figure 6b collectively demonstrate this claim. On RTX 4090 with 256 GB main memory (as well as RTX 4080 with 256 GB main memory), LoHan successfully fine-tunes a 175B model while all baselines fail. ZeRO-Infinity (the strongest baseline for maximum model size) cannot fine-tune a 175B model even with 768 GB main memory on RTX 4090 (Figure 6a), and the paper estimates it requires ~1.1 TB for this scale (Section I). The other baselines fail at far smaller scales: FlashNeuron at <6B, Colossal-AI at even smaller sizes, ZeRO-Offload limited by main memory capacity.

**What is and isn't demonstrated.** The claim is specifically about *feasibility* — can a training iteration complete without out-of-memory errors? — not about *practical training throughput* or *convergence quality*. The paper demonstrates that the iteration completes, but:
- The batch size is 1 for these maximum-size experiments. Training a 175B model with batch size 1 may require an impractically large number of iterations to converge, and the paper does not report convergence results.
- The training uses randomly initialized parameters (Section V-A), which eliminates the need to load a pre-trained checkpoint from disk. In practice, loading a 175B checkpoint onto a system with 256 GB main memory and 24 GB GPU memory would itself be a significant I/O challenge that the paper does not address.
- The paper does not measure or report the per-iteration wall-clock time at this scale. The ~53% of peak TFLOPS achieved at 175B (Figure 5c) translates to a specific absolute throughput (not reported explicitly in tokens/second for this configuration), and whether that throughput is viable for practical fine-tuning (which may require thousands of iterations) is an open question.

The claim is genuine — LoHan does what no prior system could — but "fine-tune" here means "execute the training loop" rather than "produce a converged, useful model." The systems community generally treats feasibility as the primary claim for memory-capacity-breaking work, and LoHan's claim is well-supported on those terms. A reader expecting end-to-end fine-tuning results with validation loss curves would be disappointed, but that is not the paper's stated scope.

#### Claim 2: LoHan achieves up to 2.32× throughput over state-of-the-art baselines when fine-tuning a small 13B model.

**What the experiments show.** Figure 5a supports this claim: at batch size 64 on RTX 4090, LoHan achieves ~1,300 tokens/second vs. ZeRO-Offload at ~560 (2.32×), ZeRO-Infinity at ~375 (3.46×), and Colossal-AI at ~162 (8.02×). The 2.32× figure specifically refers to the comparison against ZeRO-Offload, which is the strongest baseline at this model scale (ZeRO-Infinity's SSD offloading provides no benefit when model states fit in main memory, and its serialized CPU optimizer becomes pure overhead).

**Conditions and caveats.** The throughput advantage varies significantly with batch size. At batch size 8 (Figure 5a), the gap narrows — LoHan achieves approximately 900 tokens/second vs. ZeRO-Offload at ~580 (1.55×). The advantage grows with batch size and is maximized at the largest batch size all systems can handle. This means the 2.32× figure is the *best-case* throughput advantage under saturating batch sizes, not the average across all configurations.

The paper does not report throughput at the batch-size-1 extreme that is necessary for the 175B model, making it impossible to assess whether the relative throughput advantage holds at that scale. At 175B, the absolute throughput is low enough that the relative comparison may be less meaningful — the practical question shifts from "how much faster" to "is it fast enough to be useful."

Additionally, the throughput measurements are for a single training iteration under steady-state conditions. First-iteration overhead (profiling, initial memory allocation) is excluded, which is standard for systems benchmarking but means the reported numbers represent the best-case steady-state performance.

#### Claim 3: LoHan enables a cheap low-end consumer GPU to have higher cost-effectiveness than a DGX-A100 cluster when fine-tuning a 175B model.

**What the experiments show.** This claim requires careful reading of what was actually measured. The cost-effectiveness experiment (Section V-I, Figure 13) compares LoHan on 4× RTX 4090 against Megatron-LM on DGX-A100 — but it fine-tunes a **30B model**, not a 175B model. The paper states: "We fine-tune the 30B model (**the largest model Megatron-LM can fine-tune on the DGX machine**) on the two systems" (Section V-I, emphasis added). The claim in the abstract and introduction ("when fine-tuning a 175B model") is broader than what the cost-effectiveness experiment actually tested.

At 30B, LoHan's peak cost-effectiveness is 2.17× that of the DGX-A100. Whether this ratio holds for a 175B model is not directly tested, because Megatron-LM on DGX-A100 cannot fine-tune a 175B model (it runs out of GPU memory even with tensor parallelism across 8× A100-80GB GPUs — 640 GB total is insufficient for 2.6 TB of tensors). So the comparison at 175B is not "LoHan vs. DGX-A100" but rather "LoHan is the only option at this scale on this hardware class." The cost-effectiveness claim at 175B is therefore a qualitative statement (it's possible at all, which is infinitely more cost-effective than impossible) rather than a quantitative throughput-per-dollar ratio.

This is a genuine overstatement in the abstract. The paper could have made a more precise claim: "LoHan enables fine-tuning a 175B model on hardware costing <$30,000, a capability that even $200,000 DGX clusters cannot provide without data offloading." The 2.17× figure is supported for 30B models, but the extrapolation to 175B is not experimentally validated.

#### Claim 4: The holistic offloading approach is necessary — fragmented approaches fail at 100B scale.

**What the experiments show.** This claim is well-supported by the systematic failure analysis in Section III and the capability experiments in Section V-B. Each prior system's failure mode is diagnosed and quantified: FlashNeuron fails at <6B because model states must stay on GPU (Figure 2a); ZeRO-Infinity fails at 135B on 256 GB main memory because activations must stay in main memory (Figure 6a); G10 cannot run at all on consumer GPUs due to GPUDirect dependency (Section III-C); Colossal-AI achieves only 12% GPU utilization (Figure 2b) due to serialized optimizer and GPU-only activation storage. LoHan's success at 175B on 256 GB requires both innovations — active gradient offloading (to keep GPU utilization high) and SSD-based activation offloading (to overcome main memory limits) — simultaneously. Neither alone would suffice.

**What strengthens this claim.** The ablation in Figure 9a is particularly informative: when LoHan+CpuAct (activations only to main memory, but with active gradient offloading) is tested, it fails to reach 175B under limited main memory (Figure 8). When LoHan+ZeRO (SSD-based activation offloading, but with serialized optimizer) is tested, GPU utilization is poor (Figure 7, LoHan+ZeRO variant). The combination is required, and the paper demonstrates this through ablation rather than assertion.

#### What experiments would have strengthened the paper.

Several missing experiments limit the generality and practical applicability of the results:

1. **End-to-end convergence on a real fine-tuning task.** The paper evaluates infrastructure capability (throughput, memory usage) but never fine-tunes a real pre-trained model to convergence on a downstream task. Demonstrating that a 175B model fine-tuned with LoHan achieves comparable validation loss or downstream accuracy to the same model fine-tuned on a DGX cluster would address the most obvious practical concern: does all this offloading affect training dynamics or final model quality? The paper explicitly maintains synchronous training semantics (no parameter staleness), so there is no theoretical reason for quality degradation, but empirical validation would be compelling.

2. **Comparison against parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA.** The paper targets full-model fine-tuning, but many practitioners use LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) or similar methods that train only a small number of adapter parameters while keeping the base model frozen. These methods can fine-tune 175B models on a single GPU without any offloading. The paper does not discuss this alternative or justify when full-model fine-tuning is necessary, which weakens the motivation for the entire approach.

3. **Throughput at the 175B scale with practical batch sizes.** The maximum model size experiments use batch size 1, which is necessary for feasibility but may be impractical for convergence (gradient noise, training instability). The paper does not report what batch sizes are achievable at 175B or the corresponding throughput. This makes it difficult to assess whether fine-tuning at this scale would complete in hours, days, or weeks.

4. **CPU and main memory utilization metrics.** The paper focuses on GPU utilization and PCIe bandwidth utilization, but the CPU optimizer is a critical component. What is the CPU utilization during active gradient offloading? Is the dual-socket Xeon configuration necessary, or would a single socket suffice? These details matter for cost optimization — if the CPU is a bottleneck, a more powerful CPU might be a better investment than more SSDs.

5. **Sensitivity to sequence length.** All experiments use a fixed sequence length of 1024. Activation memory scales linearly with sequence length, so longer sequences (2048, 4096, 8192 — common in modern LLM fine-tuning) would increase memory pressure and potentially change the optimal offloading strategy. The paper does not explore this dimension.

6. **Comparison against gradient checkpointing without offloading.** The paper compares against systems that offload to main memory or SSDs, but not against a pure gradient checkpointing baseline (where only inter-block activations are stored, everything else is recomputed, and model states stay on GPU). Such a baseline would fail for large models (model states exceed GPU memory) but might outperform LoHan for smaller models where model states fit. Including this would help delineate the crossover point where offloading becomes necessary.

Despite these gaps, the paper's experimental evaluation is thorough for a systems paper targeting infrastructure capability. The key claims about feasibility (175B on consumer GPU), throughput advantage (2.32× over best baseline at accessible scales), and the necessity of holistic optimization are well-supported by the reported experiments, with the caveat that the cost-effectiveness claim at 175B is extrapolated rather than directly measured. The systematic comparison across five baselines, three GPU models, multiple main memory capacities, varying SSD counts, and two model architectures (language and diffusion) provides stronger evidence of generality than is typical for a first-version systems paper.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Cost of Difficulty Estimation Is Unaccounted for in the Headline Throughput Numbers

**The assumption or constraint.** LoHan's hardware-aware profiling stage (Section IV-B) runs during the first training iteration and takes 2–3× longer than a normal iteration. The paper explicitly acknowledges this cost but dismisses it as negligible because "fine-tuning an LLM requires thousands of iterations to converge, so the profiling overhead is negligible compared to the whole fine-tuning process" (Section IV-B). For the activation swapping manager (Section IV-D), this profiling iteration must execute with a specific configuration — offloading only inter-layer activations, recomputing everything else, and offloading all model states to SSDs without any optimizations — to "accurately break down computation and communication costs" without the optimizations obscuring raw hardware capabilities.

**The consequence.** There are two distinct but related problems. First, the profiling iteration is 2–3× slower than a normal iteration, which for very large models (175B) with batch size 1 may translate to a significant absolute wall-clock delay before useful training begins. The paper never reports the absolute duration of this profiling iteration at 175B scale, making it impossible to assess whether the overhead is seconds, minutes, or longer.

Second and more subtly, the profiling strategy assumes that the hardware characteristics measured during this first iteration remain stable throughout training. PCIe bandwidth, SSD I/O throughput, and main memory availability can vary due to thermal throttling, other processes on a shared server, SSD wear-leveling, or operating system memory pressure. If the profiled bandwidths are optimistic relative to sustained performance, the activation manager will choose a more aggressive offloading strategy (closer to Case 2 — offload more, recompute less) than is actually optimal, potentially causing PCIe bottlenecks that the model did not predict. The paper offers no mechanism for re-profiling or adapting the strategy if hardware conditions change during a long training run.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no ablation measuring sensitivity of the optimal activation strategy to profiling errors. The validation in Figure 9b shows that the predicted optimal swap sizes match empirical minima, but this is tested under the same hardware conditions as the profiling run — it demonstrates internal consistency, not robustness to profiling noise. The paper does not report profiled bandwidth values for any configuration, making it impossible to assess whether these measurements have high variance. The throughput numbers in Figures 5, 7, 9, and 10 are steady-state measurements after profiling is complete; they exclude the profiling cost.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. There is no exploration of whether the profiling can be accelerated (e.g., by profiling only a subset of layers), whether the optimal strategy is robust to moderate profiling errors (e.g., the iteration time curve might be flat near the minimum, so a 10% error in bandwidth estimates has negligible effect), or whether periodic re-profiling during training would be beneficial. The paper frames the profiling cost as negligible, which is reasonable for multi-thousand-iteration fine-tuning runs of smaller models, but becomes questionable for the 175B regime where per-iteration times are already very high.

---

### No End-to-End Convergence Results on Real Fine-Tuning Tasks

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper measure training infrastructure metrics — throughput (tokens/second, TFLOPS), maximum trainable model size (parameters that fit without OOM), and GPU utilization — using randomly initialized parameters and datasets (Section V-A): "We simply randomly initialize model parameters and datasets for evaluations that do not require model convergence." The paper never loads a pre-trained checkpoint, never fine-tunes on a real downstream dataset, and never reports validation loss, downstream accuracy, or any measure of model quality.

**The consequence.** The paper's central claim — that LoHan enables efficient 100B-scale model fine-tuning on commodity hardware — is supported only for the *mechanics* of fine-tuning (the training loop executes without crashing) but not for the *outcome* (the resulting model is useful). Several properties of LoHan's design could affect training dynamics in ways that infrastructure-only experiments cannot detect:

- **Active gradient offloading preserves synchronous semantics** (Section IV-C, footnote 4) by ensuring per-layer parameter updates complete before the next iteration's forward pass reads them. The paper argues this means no parameter staleness, which is theoretically correct. However, the aggressive pipelining of SSD I/O, CPU optimizer execution, and GPU backward propagation could introduce subtle numerical differences compared to non-overlapped execution — for example, if the CPU optimizer's floating-point arithmetic produces slightly different results than the GPU optimizer, or if the order of operations in the pipelined SSD access pattern creates different memory access interleaving that affects numerical reproducibility.
- **Selective activation recomputation** (determined by the OB-based strategy) changes which activations are recomputed from scratch versus loaded from memory. Recomputed activations may have slight numerical differences from the originally computed ones due to non-deterministic GPU operations (e.g., cuDNN convolution algorithms), which could affect gradient computation and training dynamics.
- **Mixed precision training** with offloading introduces additional numerical considerations. The 16-bit parameter copy `P16` is stored on SSDs and transferred to GPU for forward/backward computation, while the 32-bit master copy `P32` is updated by the CPU optimizer. The paper does not discuss whether the `P16` copy is refreshed from `P32` after each optimizer step or kept as a separate entity, which affects whether gradient quantization errors accumulate.

None of these effects would cause training to crash or produce NaNs — the infrastructure experiments would still pass — but they could cause the fine-tuned model to converge to a different (possibly worse) quality than the same model fine-tuned on a DGX cluster without offloading. Without convergence experiments, there is no evidence that LoHan's optimizations are *quality-preserving*.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides zero evidence about model quality. There are no loss curves, no accuracy metrics, and no comparison of checkpoints trained with LoHan versus a reference implementation. This is a deliberate scope limitation — the paper targets the systems community (it is published as a systems paper) where infrastructure capability claims are the norm — but it leaves the most practically important question unanswered: if I fine-tune Llama-2-70B with LoHan on my RTX 4090, will I get the same model quality as if I rented a DGX cluster?

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, does not discuss numerical reproducibility or training dynamics, and does not suggest convergence experiments as future work. The synchronous semantics argument (Section IV-C, footnote 4) is the only correctness guarantee provided, and it addresses only parameter staleness, not the broader question of whether the offloading-optimized training procedure is equivalent to standard non-offloaded training in terms of final model quality. A reader deploying LoHan for production fine-tuning would need to independently validate that model quality is preserved for their specific model, dataset, and hyperparameters.

---

### The Method Is Evaluated on a Single Model Architecture Family with Fixed Hyperparameters

**The assumption or constraint.** All language model experiments use decoder-only transformer models with architectural hyperparameters following "GPT-3 and open-source pre-trained models like OPT" (Section V-A, Table IV). The sequence length is fixed at 1024, the vocabulary size at 50257, and training uses mixed precision. The diffusion model experiment (Section V-H) uses the DiT-XL/2 architecture scaled to different sizes. The paper's profiling approach and the OB metric (Equation 6) are claimed to be general, but the experimental evidence covers only two architecture families (decoder-only transformers and diffusion transformers).

**The consequence.** Several architecture-specific properties could affect whether LoHan's approach generalizes:

- **Encoder-decoder models** (e.g., T5, BART) have different activation patterns than decoder-only models — the encoder processes the input once and its activations are consumed by cross-attention in each decoder layer, creating a more complex dependency graph for activation recomputation. The simple per-layer OB metric (FLOPs per byte of that layer's activation) may not capture cross-layer dependencies in encoder-decoder architectures, where offloading an encoder activation might save recomputation across all decoder layers.
- **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models** have sparse activation patterns where different tokens route through different experts. The activation memory per layer varies depending on expert load, and the OB metric would need to account for the expected sparsity pattern. LoHan's profiling approach (which measures one iteration's activation sizes) would capture the average case, but MoE models can have high variance in activation memory across iterations.
- **Models with varying sequence lengths** (common in fine-tuning with packed sequences or variable-length inputs) would have activation sizes that change per iteration. LoHan's profiling iteration uses a fixed sequence length of 1024 and determines a static offloading strategy. If sequence lengths vary significantly across batches, the static strategy may be suboptimal — too aggressive (causing memory pressure) for long sequences or too conservative (wasting PCIe bandwidth on unnecessary recomputation) for short sequences.
- **Very deep models** (hundreds of layers) might change the optimal OB-sorted ordering. The paper's experiments go up to 112 layers (276B model, Table IV). Models like DeepSeek-V2 or future architectures with 200+ layers could stress the convexity assumption — the piecewise linear structure of `FLOP_r` with respect to `A_{G2M}` assumes the OB-sorted layer list has sufficient granularity that the convexity proof holds, but with very many layers, the linear segments become very small, and the profiling noise might overwhelm the signal needed to find the true optimum.

Additionally, the fixed hyperparameters mean the paper does not explore how LoHan's strategy adapts to different training configurations. For example, training with a different optimizer (e.g., AdamW with decoupled weight decay, or a memory-efficient variant like Adafactor) would change the optimizer state size (affecting `14P` in the backward SSD I/O term in Equation 5) and the CPU optimizer execution time, potentially shifting the optimal activation strategy. The paper's iteration time model (Equations 1–8) is parameterized by optimizer state sizes, so it *could* adapt, but this is never tested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The diffusion model experiment (Figure 12) provides partial evidence of generality — LoHan works for DiT architecture up to 40B parameters where Fast-DiT fails at 1.4B. However, this is still a transformer-based architecture (DiT uses transformer blocks as its backbone). The paper argues that the OB metric is architecture-agnostic because it only depends on per-layer FLOP counts and activation sizes, but the evidence is limited to transformer variants. No convolutional, recurrent, or state-space model architectures are tested.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim to support encoder-decoder, MoE, or variable-sequence-length models, so these are scope limitations rather than design flaws. However, the paper also does not discuss which architectural assumptions are baked into the iteration time model — for example, whether the model assumes a strict sequential layer structure (each layer's backward pass depends only on the previous layer's backward pass output), which is true for standard transformers but not for architectures with skip connections that create non-sequential dependency graphs. A practitioner considering LoHan for a non-standard architecture would need to verify these assumptions independently.

---

### Latency Is Ignored: The Framework Optimizes Throughput, Not Time-to-Result

**The assumption or constraint.** LoHan's optimization objective is to minimize total iteration time `T_{iter}` (Equation 1), which is a throughput metric — it minimizes the average time per training step, maximizing tokens processed per second. The paper never discusses latency from the perspective of how long a single training run takes to complete, and it never considers whether certain operations in LoHan's pipeline are inherently serial and cannot be parallelized across hardware.

**The consequence.** Throughput and latency trade off against each other in LoHan's design in ways the paper does not surface:

- **Active gradient offloading (Section IV-C)** creates a layer-level pipeline where the GPU backward propagation for layer `i`, the CPU optimizer for layer `(i+1)`, and SSD I/O for layer `(i+2)` run concurrently. This maximizes throughput by keeping all resources busy. However, it means that the backward pass for a given layer cannot complete until the GPU has finished its computation AND the SSD and CPU pipeline for that layer's optimizer execution has drained. The pipeline depth (how many layers are "in flight" simultaneously) adds latency to the backward pass compared to a serialized execution where the backward pass finishes immediately after GPU computation, even though the optimizer executes later. This latency is hidden at the iteration level (the next forward pass doesn't start until the entire backward+optimizer pipeline drains), but in scenarios where training is interleaved with other operations (e.g., evaluation on a validation set after every K iterations, checkpoint saving), the pipeline must drain completely before those operations can begin, adding a fixed latency penalty proportional to the pipeline depth.
- **Activation offloading to SSDs** adds latency for the forward pass because activations must be written to SSDs before the forward pass for a given microbatch can be considered complete. The forward stage time `T_f` model (Equation 2) accounts for this in the throughput calculation (it takes the maximum of GPU compute and PCIe transfer times), but the *latency* of completing one forward pass through all layers is the sum of the per-layer latencies, not the maximum. If activations are being written to SSDs asynchronously, the forward pass may appear "complete" from the GPU's perspective while SSD writes are still pending, creating a risk if a checkpoint or evaluation is triggered before the SSD writes finish.
- **The 175B model with batch size 1** achieves only ~53% of peak TFLOPS (Figure 5c). The absolute throughput in tokens per second is not reported, but it will be extremely low because the GPU is underutilized (small batch) and SSD I/O dominates. A full fine-tuning run of thousands of iterations could take days or weeks. The paper's cost-effectiveness argument (Section V-I) only considers hardware cost per unit of throughput, not the opportunity cost of researcher time waiting for training to complete. A DGX cluster might be more expensive per token but complete the same fine-tuning run in hours rather than weeks — a tradeoff the paper does not quantify.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no latency measurements, no pipeline depth analysis, and no time-to-completion estimates for full fine-tuning runs. The iteration time curves in Figure 9b show total iteration time (throughput inverse) but do not break down into latency components. The paper does not discuss the training iteration as a whole from start to finish — how long the forward pass takes in wall-clock seconds, how long the backward+optimizer pipeline takes, and what the critical path is.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge latency as a concern. The throughput-centric evaluation is standard for systems papers targeting training infrastructure (throughput is the primary metric for batch training jobs), but for the paper's stated audience — "data scientists with a limited budget" (Section I) who are running fine-tuning on their own hardware — the wall-clock time to get a usable model matters as much as the cost per token. A scientist with a single RTX 4090 might accept higher per-token cost if it means getting results in 8 hours rather than 8 days. The paper provides no guidance for making this tradeoff.

### The `14×` Larger Model in the FLOPs-Matched Comparison Is Not Trained Compute-Optimally

**The assumption or constraint.** The cost-effectiveness comparison in Section V-I compares LoHan on 4× RTX 4090 against Megatron-LM on a DGX-A100 cluster with 8× A100-80GB GPUs, using the 30B model — "the largest model Megatron-LM can fine-tune on the DGX machine" (Section V-I). This is a comparison between a small model with test-time compute (offloading) against a ~14× more expensive system, but the comparison has a structural asymmetry: Megatron-LM uses tensor parallelism without any data offloading, keeping all tensors in GPU memory. This is the standard approach for high-end clusters, but it is not necessarily the most cost-effective approach for those clusters — ZeRO-Infinity or a similar offloading strategy could enable the DGX-A100 to fine-tune larger models or achieve higher throughput at the same model size by using larger batch sizes.

**The consequence.** The cost-effectiveness comparison (Figure 13) shows LoHan achieving 2.17× higher tokens-per-second-per-dollar than Megatron-LM on DGX-A100. However, this compares LoHan running its optimal configuration (offloading to SSDs, active gradient offloading, holistic activation management) against the DGX-A100 running a *suboptimal* configuration for cost-effectiveness — keeping everything in GPU memory when offloading to the DGX's fast NVMe SSDs and using its much faster CPU could potentially improve throughput. A fair comparison would give both systems their respective optimal software stacks for the hardware they run on. LoHan on commodity hardware with offloading versus Megatron-LM on DGX with offloading (or versus ZeRO-Infinity on DGX) would likely show a narrower cost-effectiveness gap.

More subtly, the comparison uses the 30B model because it is the largest that *fits* in the DGX's 640 GB of aggregate GPU memory without offloading. This caps the upside for the DGX — it cannot demonstrate any capability advantage at larger scales (where it would need offloading, just like LoHan). A comparison at 70B or 175B (where LoHan can operate but a pure in-memory DGX cannot) would show infinite cost-effectiveness for LoHan (possible vs. impossible), but would also be an unfair comparison because the DGX *could* handle those scales with offloading software. The paper's choice of 30B avoids this unfairness but also avoids demonstrating LoHan's capability advantage at the scales where it is most distinctive.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The cost-effectiveness numbers are clearly reported in Figure 13 and Table VII, with explicit prices for all components. The paper is transparent that the DGX uses Megatron-LM with tensor parallelism and no offloading. The limitation is not in the reporting but in the baseline selection — comparing a system designed for offloading against a system that does not use offloading, rather than against the best achievable configuration on each hardware platform.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss this asymmetry or consider a fairer baseline. The DGX-A100 with ZeRO-Infinity would be a more appropriate comparison for the "high-end cluster with offloading" configuration, but this is not evaluated. The paper also does not discuss whether the DGX-A100's NVLink interconnect (which provides 600 GB/s GPU-to-GPU bandwidth) could be leveraged for more efficient tensor parallelism that changes the cost-effectiveness tradeoff. A practitioner deciding between buying a DGX cluster and building a commodity server with LoHan would need to know whether the DGX, when equipped with equivalent offloading software, recovers some or all of its cost disadvantage through higher throughput.

### No Comparison Against Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning Methods

**The assumption or constraint.** LoHan targets full-model fine-tuning — all parameters are updated during training. The paper does not discuss or compare against parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods such as LoRA (Hu et al., 2021), prefix tuning, or adapter layers, which train only a small number of additional parameters (typically <1% of the base model) while keeping the pre-trained weights frozen. These methods have become the de facto standard for fine-tuning large models on consumer hardware because they dramatically reduce memory requirements — LoRA for a 175B model might require only a few hundred MB of additional GPU memory, easily fitting on an RTX 4090 without any offloading.

**The consequence.** The paper's central motivation — democratizing access to 100B-scale fine-tuning — is significantly weakened by the omission of PEFT comparisons. A data scientist with a tight budget who wants to adapt a 175B model to their downstream task has at least two options: (1) use LoRA on their RTX 4090 with no offloading, no special framework, and standard PyTorch training, or (2) use LoHan for full-model fine-tuning with SSD offloading, active gradient offloading, and a profiling stage. The paper provides no evidence that full-model fine-tuning with LoHan produces better downstream performance than PEFT methods, and no comparison of the throughput, memory usage, or ease of use between the two approaches.

The omission matters because PEFT methods change the nature of the accessibility problem. If LoRA can achieve comparable downstream accuracy to full fine-tuning for most tasks, then LoHan solves a problem that many practitioners do not have — they can already fine-tune 175B models on consumer GPUs using PEFT. The remaining use cases where full fine-tuning is necessary (e.g., continual pre-training, instruction tuning that requires updating all layers, tasks where PEFT methods underperform) become the relevant target, and the paper does not characterize when these cases arise or how large the accuracy gap between full fine-tuning and PEFT is.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper contains no mention of LoRA, PEFT, adapters, or prefix tuning. The Related Work section (Section VI) discusses tensor management methods for LLM fine-tuning but does not cover PEFT as an alternative paradigm. The experimental section evaluates only full-model fine-tuning.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper implicitly assumes that full-model fine-tuning is the relevant use case without justifying this assumption. For the systems community, demonstrating that a new memory-saving technique enables a previously impossible training configuration is sufficient contribution — the paper does this. But for the stated audience of budget-constrained data scientists, the omission of PEFT comparisons leaves a gap: the paper does not help a practitioner decide whether they should invest effort in setting up LoHan (with its SSD requirements, profiling stage, and custom PyTorch wrappers) or simply use LoRA with standard PyTorch.
