# SliceGPT: Compress Large Language Models by Deleting Rows and Columns

**ArXiv:** [2401.15024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15024)

## 🎯 Pitch

You can slice away up to 25% of an LLM's rows and columns—not just zero out weights—and LLAMA-2 70B still performs at 99% accuracy, while running 1.5× faster on a single GPU. The trick is a newly discovered computational invariance in transformer blocks that lets you rotate the network so the least important dimensions can be cleanly cut.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **SliceGPT**, a post-training model compression method that reduces the size of large language models by deleting entire rows and columns of weight matrices — effectively lowering the embedding dimension of the network — rather than inducing unstructured or semi-structured sparsity. The key insight is a previously unrecognized **computational invariance** in RMSNorm-connected transformer architectures: multiplying a layer's output by an orthogonal matrix Q leaves the subsequent RMSNorm operation unchanged when the inverse transformation Q^⊤ is applied immediately after, enabling the network to be rotated block-by-block onto principal-component-aligned bases where minor components can be sliced away with minimal fidelity loss. Applied to OPT, LLAMA-2, and Phi-2 models, SliceGPT removes up to 25–30% of parameters while retaining 99%, 99%, and 90% of dense zero-shot task performance for OPT 66B, LLAMA-2 70B, and Phi-2 respectively, and reduces LLAMA-2 70B inference compute on consumer GPUs to 64% of the dense baseline (from 1764 GPUms/token to 1075 GPUms/token) without any dedicated sparse-kernel support. The method establishes that structured, architecture-aware pruning achieves real speedups and competitive fidelity only when the rotation aligns the deletion axes with the signal's principal components, revealing that the interaction between normalization, residual connections, and weight matrix transformations governs the recoverable redundancy in transformer models.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLM Deployment Is Expensive, and Existing Compression Methods Don't Deliver Genuine Speedups

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is that **large language models are too expensive to deploy**, and existing post-training compression techniques — despite their conceptual appeal — fail to translate theoretical parameter reductions into measurable wall-clock speedups on real hardware. This gap between paper claims and practical impact matters enormously because it determines whether compression is a laboratory curiosity or a deployable engineering solution.

The cost landscape is stark. The authors open by characterizing LLMs as "neural networks with billions of parameters, trained on trillions of tokens," where "the size of LLMs makes deploying a pre-trained model an expensive undertaking." Critically, this is not just about the number of parameters — it is about the **inference compute budget**. Since LLMs are autoregressive, they require sequential forward passes through the entire network to generate each token. A model requiring multiple GPUs simply to hold its weights in memory translates that memory pressure into latency, energy consumption, and hardware cost for every token generated in a production text stream. For interactive applications, this creates a direct tradeoff between model capability and user experience; for batch processing, it creates a direct tradeoff between model capability and operating cost.

The paper's focus on *post-training* compression is motivated by the foundation model paradigm: "the cost of training an LLM has caused a shift to re-using pre-trained models for multiple tasks." Organizations rarely train LLMs from scratch. They download pre-trained weights (OPT, LLAMA-2, Phi-2) and adapt them. Compression must therefore work as a one-time post-processing step — ideally on a single GPU, in a few hours — without requiring access to the original training pipeline, massive compute clusters, or months of additional training. Any method requiring extensive retraining or fine-tuning undermines the economic rationale for using pre-trained models in the first place.

### Why This Problem Is Important (Real-World and Technical Significance)

The importance of this problem operates on two levels: **engineering pragmatics** and **scientific understanding**.

**Engineering pragmatics.** The paper provides specific numbers that frame the real-world stakes. For LLAMA-2 70B on consumer-grade Quadro RTX6000 GPUs (24GB memory each), the dense model requires 7 GPUs consuming 1764 GPU-milliseconds per generated token. After 25% slicing with SliceGPT, the same model runs on 5 GPUs consuming 1075 GPUms/token — a 36% reduction in GPU count and an effective compute reduction to 64% of the dense baseline. On datacenter A100s (40GB), the reduction is to 66% (from 4 GPUs to 3). These are not marginal improvements that get lost in benchmarking noise; they represent real hardware savings that compound across every token generated in production. If an organization serves millions of tokens per day from a 70B-parameter model, reducing per-token compute by one-third translates directly to reduced GPU-hour billing, reduced energy consumption, and reduced hardware procurement.

But the significance runs deeper than raw throughput numbers. The paper highlights a specific failure mode of prior compression work: "end-to-end performance gains are not feasible with our baseline SparseGPT 2:4 at the time of writing." In other words, a 50% parameter reduction via 2:4 structured sparsity sounds impressive but cannot actually be executed faster on current hardware without specialized kernel support that was not available at publication time. SliceGPT's output matrices are **dense** — they are just smaller dense matrices, which are exactly the kind of computation that existing GPU kernels (cuBLAS, PyTorch's matmul) are heavily optimized for. This means the speedups are *immediately deployable* without waiting for sparse-hardware ecosystem maturation.

**Scientific understanding.** Beyond engineering concerns, the paper identifies a structural property of transformer networks — **computational invariance under orthogonal transformations** — that was previously unrecognized and that governs *why* certain compression strategies succeed or fail. The finding that RMSNorm operations commute with orthogonal transformations (Equation 2: `RMSNorm(XℓQ)Q^⊤ = RMSNorm(Xℓ)`) may seem like a simple algebraic observation, but its implications are profound: it means an entire transformer network can be rotated into an arbitrary orthonormal basis without changing its predictions. This is not an approximation or a heuristic — it is an exact equivalence. The paper describes this as an "invariant function" where "a transformation to the input does not result in a change to the output," and notes that "the computation can be performed in any transformed state."

This insight reframes the compression problem entirely. Rather than asking "which weights can we zero out?", the relevant question becomes "in which rotated basis do the weight matrices exhibit the most compressible structure?" If the network is computationally invariant under orthogonal transformations, then we are free to choose the rotation that makes compression easiest — specifically, the rotation that aligns the signal's principal components with the axes of the weight matrices, so that deleting low-variance dimensions (rows or columns) introduces minimal reconstruction error. The technical machinery for finding this rotation already exists: it is Principal Component Analysis (PCA), applied not to the weights but to the *signals* (activations) flowing between transformer blocks.

This connection between invariance and compressibility is not merely clever — it reveals that prior compression methods were implicitly working in a suboptimal basis. SparseGPT, magnitude pruning, and other weight-centric approaches operate on the weight matrices in their raw, trained representation. SliceGPT demonstrates that by first rotating to a principal-component basis, deletion of entire structured components (rows and columns) achieves fidelity comparable to or better than unstructured sparsity, but with the crucial advantage that the resulting matrices remain dense and therefore fast.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper positions SliceGPT against four categories of compression methods, identifying specific limitations in each.

#### Unstructured Pruning: Sparsity Without Speed

The simplest approach — magnitude-based sparsification (Han et al., 2016; Zhu & Gupta, 2017) — sets the smallest-magnitude weights to zero. The paper acknowledges that this is "scalable" but notes that "its application to LLMs gives too strong a degradation in performance." This performance cliff occurs because LLM weight matrices are not structured such that individual small weights are expendable; zeroing them out without updating surrounding weights introduces compounding errors through the deep network.

More sophisticated methods like Optimal Brain Surgeon (OBS, Hassibi et al., 1993; LeCun et al., 1989) compensate for weight removal by updating remaining weights using the inverse Hessian of the loss. This is theoretically grounded — OBS identifies weights whose removal minimally impacts the loss function after compensation — but is completely impractical for LLMs where "individual layer weight matrices typically contain more than $10^8$ parameters." Computing, storing, and inverting Hessian matrices at this scale is computationally intractable. The paper traces a research lineage attempting to work around this: WoodFisher (Singh & Alistarh, 2020) approximates the inverse Hessian; Optimal Brain Compression (Frantar & Alistarh, 2022) applies OBS layer-wise rather than globally. But the paper notes these "are not practical for large language models."

SparseGPT (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023) addressed the scale problem by adapting the column-by-column quantization scheme from GPTQ to pruning, updating not-yet-pruned weights in subsequent columns as each column is processed. This achieved one-shot pruning with reasonable fidelity. However, the paper identifies a critical limitation: "achieving end-to-end speed improvements through unstructured pruning is a demanding task." The resulting weight matrices have zero entries scattered irregularly, which requires either specialized sparse-matrix kernels (not universally available or optimized) or storage overhead for sparse formats that can negate the theoretical FLOP reduction.

#### Structured/Semi-Structured Sparsity: Speed Gains That Don't Materialize

Semi-structured patterns like 2:4 sparsity (where exactly 2 out of every 4 consecutive weights are zero; Mishra et al., 2021) were designed to address the hardware problem: NVIDIA's Ampere architecture includes hardware support (sparse tensor cores) for 2:4 structured sparsity, promising theoretical 2× speedup. SparseGPT was extended to this pattern, and the paper uses SparseGPT 2:4 as its primary baseline.

But the paper identifies two problems. First, the accuracy of 2:4-sparsified models is substantially worse than SliceGPT at comparable compression rates. Table 1 shows that for LLAMA-2 70B, SparseGPT 2:4 achieves WikiText-2 perplexity 4.98 versus SliceGPT 25% at 4.60 and SliceGPT 30% at 5.05 — SliceGPT removing 25–30% of parameters *outperforms* the semi-structured sparsity pattern in held-out perplexity. Second, and more damningly, the paper states bluntly: "end-to-end performance gains are not feasible with our baseline SparseGPT 2:4 at the time of writing." Despite theoretical hardware support, the software ecosystem (CUDA kernels, PyTorch integration) had not matured to the point where 2:4 sparsity could deliver realized throughput improvements in production inference pipelines.

Sliding even further toward structured sparsity, the paper mentions methods that prune "entire rows or columns by norm" but found "this baseline was very poor, with the WikiText-2 perplexity of the model soaring into the 1000s after pruning just a few columns." This is a crucial negative result: naive structured pruning catastrophically fails on LLMs. The implication is that *how* you select which rows and columns to delete matters enormously — you cannot simply remove low-norm dimensions and expect the model to work. This failure directly motivates SliceGPT's core contribution: the orthogonal transformation step is what makes structured pruning viable, by rotating the network so that deleted dimensions correspond to *low-variance signal components* rather than merely low-norm weight components.

#### Low-Rank Factorization: Compression Without True Parameter Reduction

The paper briefly notes low-rank approximation approaches where "each weight matrix is replaced with the product of two matrices with a smaller inner dimension." This includes LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) and similar methods. The limitation is arithmetic: "to achieve compression, the inner dimension must be smaller than half of the original dimension." For a D×D weight matrix, replacing it with two D×r and r×D matrices where r < D/2 reduces the total parameter count. But this is a stricter constraint than it appears — the paper does not elaborate, but the subtext is that at typical embedding dimensions (4096, 8192, etc.), achieving meaningful compression via low-rank factorization while maintaining accuracy is difficult because the effective rank of trained weight matrices is often close to full rank. SliceGPT sidesteps this by reducing the embedding dimension directly, which reduces *all* matrices simultaneously rather than decomposing each one individually.

The paper also notes that low-rank methods "usually require a fine-tuning step," which adds computational cost and data dependence to the compression pipeline. SliceGPT is presented as a one-shot method (with optional recovery fine-tuning that is lightweight and fast — 1–5 hours total).

#### Structural Pruning of LLMs: Methods That Exist But Require Heavy Retraining

The paper acknowledges contemporaneous work on structured LLM pruning. LLM-pruner (Ma et al., 2023a) "removes connected structures from an LLM before further training" — the operative phrase being "further training," meaning the compression is not a one-shot post-processing step but the beginning of a continued training process. LLM Surgeon (van der Ouderaa et al., 2023) "interweaves recovery fine-tuning with pruning," again requiring training cycles interleaved with the pruning decisions.

The paper positions SliceGPT explicitly in contrast: "We provide results for SliceGPT as a single-shot method and with post-slicing recovery fine-tuning." The distinction is that SliceGPT's slicing decision is made once, analytically, via PCA on calibration data — the transformation and deletion are exact operations, not iterative approximations. Recovery fine-tuning is optional, not essential. This matters because it means SliceGPT can be applied to any pre-trained model without access to training infrastructure, without extensive hyperparameter tuning, and without the risk of fine-tuning instability or catastrophic forgetting.

#### Channel/Filter Pruning in ConvNets: The Conceptual Ancestor But at Different Scale

The paper draws a connection to filter-level pruning in convolutional networks (ThiNet, Luo et al., 2017; channel pruning, He et al., 2017), which similarly "apply linear operations between layers" and remove entire filters. This is the closest conceptual ancestor to SliceGPT's row/column deletion. However, the paper emphasizes a scale mismatch: "the model sizes are typically several orders of magnitude smaller than in LLMs, for example the VGG16 network has 138M parameters, comparable with the very smallest OPT model that we consider." Methods developed for million-parameter vision models — which could afford iterative pruning with fine-tuning cycles, outer-loop regularization parameter selection, and repeated retraining — do not transfer to billion-parameter language models where even a single fine-tuning epoch is computationally expensive. The "huge size of LLMs makes methods that involve extensive fine-tuning unappealing."

### How This Paper Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

SliceGPT does not fit neatly into any single existing compression category — and this is deliberate. The paper positions the method at the intersection of several approaches while addressing their collective failure modes:

**It is structurally sparse (row/column deletion)**, but unlike naive structural pruning that fails catastrophically, it uses a principled rotation step (PCA on activations) to select *which* rows and columns to delete, achieving fidelity comparable to unstructured methods.

**It produces dense output matrices**, unlike unstructured or semi-structured sparsity, meaning the compressed model runs on existing dense matmul kernels with immediate, measurable speedups on current hardware. The paper emphasizes this with specific GPU benchmarks (Table 2, Table 11, Tables 12-13 in the appendix) rather than relying on theoretical FLOP-count reductions.

**It reduces the embedding dimension**, which is a structural change that cascades through the entire network — unlike weight-only sparsification, SliceGPT makes the *signal matrices* (activations) smaller, reducing both compute *and* memory traffic between blocks. The paper explicitly contrasts this with conventional sparsity (Figure 1): "in contrast to other pruning methods, SliceGPT prunes away (slices off!) entire rows or columns of the weight matrices," and "the signals passed between blocks of the neural network are smaller too: we reduce the embedding dimension of the neural network."

**It is analytically derived, not iteratively optimized.** The transformation Q at each block is computed by PCA on calibration data — a closed-form eigenvalue decomposition — not by iterative pruning with Hessian approximations or gradient-based compensation. The paper's opening of Section 4 emphasizes this practicality: "The computation of Q is performed on a single H100 GPU with 80GB of memory, taking approximately 3.5 hours to complete for the LLAMA-2 70B model."

**It is modular and combinable.** The paper explicitly states in its conclusion that "complementary methods including quantization and structural pruning could be used" alongside SliceGPT. The dense, smaller matrices produced by slicing are directly quantizable; the dimensionality reduction is orthogonal (in the conceptual sense) to weight quantization. This contrasts with sparse methods, where combining sparsity patterns with quantization creates complex interactions in both accuracy and kernel support.

Perhaps most importantly, the paper positions SliceGPT not just as a method but as an **enabler of a new way of thinking about transformer compression**. The computational invariance insight — that the network's predictions are unchanged under orthogonal transformations of the internal representations — is presented as a contribution that "we hope will inspire and enable future avenues to reduce memory and computation demands for pre-trained models." This is a claim about conceptual reframing: if the network is invariant in this way, then the search for compressible structure can be conducted in any rotated basis, and the problem reduces to finding the rotation that maximally concentrates the signal into a subset of dimensions. PCA is one such rotation; future work might find better ones for specific architectures or tasks.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The "system" here is a post-training transformation pipeline — not a new model architecture or a training procedure — that takes a fully trained transformer and surgically rewires its weight matrices so that entire rows and columns can be deleted with minimal accuracy loss. It solves the problem that **naive structured pruning catastrophicly fails on LLMs** (the paper reports perplexity "soaring into the 1000s after pruning just a few columns" via norm-based selection) by exploiting a previously unrecognized mathematical property: the network's predictions are invariant under orthogonal rotations of its internal representations, meaning you can rotate the network into a basis where the signal's principal components align with the coordinate axes, delete the axes carrying the least signal energy, and the reconstruction error from deletion is precisely the PCA reconstruction error — which is provably L2-optimal.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The SliceGPT pipeline has five stages that execute sequentially on an already-trained model:

1. **LayerNorm-to-RMSNorm conversion:** The scale (`diag(α)`) and mean-subtraction (`M`) linear operations inside each LayerNorm block are algebraically absorbed into the adjacent weight matrices — this makes every normalization layer a pure RMSNorm, which is the prerequisite for the computational invariance that follows.

2. **Calibration data forward pass:** A small dataset (1024 sequences from WikiText-2 or Alpaca, each of length 2048) is fed through the RMSNorm-converted model, and the signal matrix `Xℓ` (the activations between blocks, shape N×D) is collected at the output of every RMSNorm instance.

3. **Per-block PCA computation:** For each block `ℓ`, the signal covariance matrix `Cℓ = Σᵢ Xᵀ_{ℓ,i} X_{ℓ,i}` is formed and eigendecomposed. The eigenvectors, sorted by decreasing eigenvalue, become the orthogonal transformation matrix `Qℓ` for that block — this is the rotation that aligns the block's output signal with its principal components.

4. **Network rotation (computational invariance application):** Each Qℓ is fused into the surrounding weight matrices: input matrices `Win` are pre-multiplied by `Qℓᵀ`, output matrices `Wout` are post-multiplied by `Qℓ₊₁`, and new small linear layers `Qℓᵀ Qℓ₊₁` are inserted into the residual connections to account for differing rotations between adjacent blocks. The embedding matrix gets post-multiplied, and the LM head gets pre-multiplied. Theorem 1 guarantees this rotated network computes *exactly* the same outputs as the original.

5. **Slicing (structured deletion):** A deletion matrix `D` (a D×D_small matrix containing a subset of identity columns) is applied to remove rows from every `Win` and the embedding matrix, columns from every `Wout` and the head matrix, and both rows and columns from the residual connection matrices. The number of dimensions deleted is proportional to the PCA eigenvalues discarded, and different layers can be sliced by different amounts.

The output is a smaller, entirely dense transformer whose embedding dimension has been reduced from D to D_small — weight matrices are physically smaller, and crucially, the signal matrices flowing between blocks are also smaller, reducing both FLOPs and memory traffic.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First:** the LayerNorm-to-RMSNorm conversion, because the computational invariance (the foundation for everything that follows) only holds for RMSNorm-connected networks — understanding *why* LayerNorm breaks the invariance and *how* the conversion fixes it is essential.
- **Second:** the computational invariance theorem (Theorem 1) and its proof sketch, because this is the mathematical guarantee that allows arbitrary rotations without changing predictions — it explains *why* we are allowed to rotate the network at all.
- **Third:** per-block PCA and how Qℓ is computed from calibration data, because this determines *which* rotation we choose among the infinitely many that the invariance permits — the choice that makes slicing low-loss.
- **Fourth:** the rotation insertion mechanics — exactly how Qℓ matrices are absorbed into Win, Wout, Wembd, Whead, and the residual connections — because this is the actual network editing operation.
- **Fifth:** the slicing (deletion) step — how the deletion matrix D removes rows and columns, why it corresponds to discarding low-variance PCA components, and why this yields an L2-optimal reconstruction.
- **Sixth:** practical considerations — numerical precision (double vs. single), calibration dataset choices, and the handling of biases and remaining LayerNorm components — because these are the details that determine whether the method works in practice.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **analytic compression paper** whose core idea is that transformer networks contain a previously unrecognized computational invariance — the ability to rotate internal representations by any orthogonal matrix without changing predictions — and that exploiting this invariance by rotating each block onto its signal's principal components enables deletion of entire embedding dimensions with minimal fidelity loss.

---

#### LayerNorm-to-RMSNorm Conversion

The computational invariance at the heart of SliceGPT (Equation 2, Theorem 1) applies only to RMSNorm-connected networks — not to LayerNorm-connected ones. Since the LLMs the paper targets (OPT, LLAMA-2, Phi-2) all use LayerNorm, a mandatory preprocessing step converts every LayerNorm instance into an equivalent RMSNorm by absorbing LayerNorm's linear operations into adjacent weight matrices.

**What LayerNorm actually computes.** A LayerNorm block, as defined in Equation 1, performs three sequential operations on each row of the input signal matrix X (shape N×D):

$$ \text{LayerNorm}(X) = \text{RMSNorm}(X M) \, \text{diag}(\alpha) \sqrt{D} + 1_N \beta^\top $$

where `M = I - \frac{1}{D} 1 1^\top` is the D×D mean-subtraction matrix (it subtracts the row mean from each element), `RMSNorm` divides each row by its L2 norm, `diag(α)` is a D×D diagonal matrix of learned scale parameters, `√D` is a constant scaling factor, `β` is a learned offset vector of length D, and `1_N` is a length-N vector of ones.

In plain language: subtract the mean from each row → normalize each row to unit norm → rescale each column by a learned scalar α_j → multiply by √D → add a learned per-dimension offset β_j.

**The problem: why LayerNorm blocks the invariance.** If we insert an orthogonal transformation Q before a LayerNorm and Qᵀ after it, the composition does *not* simplify to the original LayerNorm. The reason is that LayerNorm is not a pure norm-based operation — the mean subtraction (M) and the learned rescaling (diag(α)) are linear operations that are tied to the original coordinate axes. Multiplying by Q before LayerNorm changes which linear combinations of dimensions are normalized, and the subsequent multiplication by Qᵀ cannot undo this because the normalization step (dividing by the row norm) is nonlinear and does not commute with Q in the presence of the learned affine parameters.

In contrast, RMSNorm(X) simply divides each row by its norm — no mean subtraction, no learned affine parameters in the basic case. The property `RMSNorm(XQ) Qᵀ = RMSNorm(X)` holds because multiplying by Q changes neither the row norms (Q is orthogonal, so `||xQ|| = ||x||`) nor the direction after the inverse is applied.

**The conversion procedure.** The paper describes the transformation in Figure 3 and Section 3.2. The goal is to algebraically restructure the network so that every LayerNorm becomes operationally equivalent to RMSNorm, with its linear components pushed into neighboring weight matrices. Specifically:

- **For each LayerNorm's scale parameters diag(α):** The learned per-dimension scaling factors α_j are absorbed into the *input* weight matrices Win of the subsequent block. If the subsequent attention or FFN block has input weights Win (which could be the concatenation W_k|W_q|W_v for attention, or W_1|W_2 for a gated FFN), these are replaced by `diag(α) Win`. Conceptually: "multiply by α, then multiply by Win" becomes "multiply by the pre-scaled matrix."

- **For each LayerNorm's mean-subtraction matrix M:** This is absorbed into the *output* weight matrices Wout of the *preceding* block. If the preceding attention or FFN block has output weights Wout, these are replaced by `Wout M`. Conceptually: "multiply by Wout, then subtract the mean in the next LayerNorm" becomes "multiply by WoutM, which inherently produces mean-subtracted outputs."

- **For the embedding matrix Wembd:** Since the first LayerNorm (Norm₀) receives the embedding output and applies mean subtraction, the embedding matrix is post-multiplied by M: `Wembd ← Wembd M`.

- **For the LM head matrix Whead:** The last LayerNorm's scale parameters are absorbed into the head: `Whead ← diag(α_last) Whead`.

- **Constant scaling √D:** The paper notes this is handled but does not elaborate extensively; it can be absorbed into either adjacent matrix as a scalar factor.

The result of this conversion: every normalization block in the network is now mathematically `RMSNorm(X) = X / ||X||` (applied row-wise), up to constant factors that have been absorbed. The network still computes exactly the same function as before — this is purely an algebraic reorganization, not an approximation.

**Why this conversion is necessary (and why it's not trivial).** One might wonder: if LayerNorm contains RMSNorm as a component, why not simply declare that the invariance holds approximately? The paper does not take this route — the conversion is exact because any approximation would accumulate errors through the 32–80 layers of a typical LLM. The mean subtraction M is particularly important: in the original network, the signal entering each attention/FFN block is mean-centered, but in the converted network, the preceding Wout has absorbed M and thus produces mean-centered outputs directly. If this were omitted, the PCA performed later would be computed on non-mean-centered signals, which would bias the principal components toward the mean direction rather than the directions of genuine variation.

**Equation 1 restated operationally:**

> The LayerNorm operation subtracts the mean from each row of the N×D signal matrix, divides each row by its standard deviation, rescales each column by a learned parameter α_j, multiplies by √D, and adds a learned offset β_j.

**Why this form:** This decomposition into `RMSNorm(X M)` followed by affine transformation is what makes the conversion possible — the mean subtraction and rescaling are linear operations that can be absorbed into adjacent weight matrices because they are applied *before* the nonlinear normalization step. If the affine parameters were applied *after* RMSNorm in a non-decomposable way, the conversion would not be algebraically valid.

#### The Computational Invariance Theorem (Theorem 1)

This is the mathematical foundation of the entire method. The paper states it as a formal theorem and provides a proof sketch by stepping through Algorithm 1.

**The core observation (Equation 2).** For any orthogonal matrix Q (QᵀQ = QQᵀ = I, same dimension D as the embedding), and any signal matrix X:

$$ \text{RMSNorm}(X Q) Q^\top = \text{RMSNorm}(X) $$

**Proof of Equation 2 (from Appendix A.1):** Consider a single row x of X. The corresponding row of XQ is Qᵀx (since the paper's notation treats rows as vectors, and right-multiplication by Q means each row x becomes xQ). The RMSNorm of this row is (1/||Qᵀx||) Qᵀx. Since Q is orthogonal, ||Qᵀx|| = ||x||. After multiplying by Qᵀ, the row becomes (1/||x||) Q Qᵀx = (1/||x||) x, which is exactly RMSNorm(x). Summing over all rows: `RMSNorm(XQ)Qᵀ = RMSNorm(X)`.

**What this equation means operationally:** If you take the output of block ℓ, rotate it by Q (multiply the signal matrix by Q on the right), apply RMSNorm, and then rotate back by Qᵀ, you get exactly the same normalized signal as if you had never rotated at all. The rotation and its inverse "cancel" through the RMSNorm because RMSNorm is rotationally invariant — it depends only on the norm of each row, not on the coordinate system.

**Theorem 1 statement:** Let `W_in^ℓ` and `W_out^ℓ` be the input and output weight matrices of block ℓ (attention or FFN, each of shape D×D or adapted for concatenated matrices), `b_in^ℓ` and `b_out^ℓ` their biases, `Wembd` the embedding matrix, and `Whead` the LM head. Let Q be any D×D orthogonal matrix. Then the following transformed network is **exactly equivalent** (computes identical predictions for all inputs) to the original:

$$
\tilde{W}_{embd} = W_{embd} Q
$$
$$
\tilde{W}_{in}^\ell = Q^\top W_{in}^\ell
$$
$$
\tilde{W}_{out}^\ell = W_{out}^\ell Q
$$
$$
\tilde{b}_{out}^\ell = Q^\top b_{out}^\ell
$$
$$
\tilde{W}_{head} = Q^\top W_{head}
$$

with `\tilde{b}_{in}^\ell = b_{in}^\ell` and `\tilde{b}_{head} = b_{head}` (input and head biases unchanged).

**Symbol definitions:** `Wembd` is the D×V embedding lookup matrix (V = vocabulary size), `W_in^ℓ` is the D×D (or D×3D for concatenated attention projections) input weight matrix for block ℓ, `W_out^ℓ` is the D×D output weight matrix for block ℓ, `b_out^ℓ` is the D-dimensional output bias vector, `Whead` is the V×D LM head matrix (or D×V depending on convention), and Q is any D×D orthogonal matrix.

**What this theorem computes:** A precise recipe for editing every weight matrix in the network using a single orthogonal matrix Q, such that the edited network produces exactly the same output tokens as the original for any input sequence. The operations are: (1) embed the input, then rotate by Q; (2) in each block, un-rotate the input before the block's computation, then re-rotate the output; (3) un-rotate before the head to produce the original logits.

**Why this form:** The pattern is "rotate after embedding, un-rotate before block, rotate after block, un-rotate before head." The rotations between blocks "cancel" through the RMSNorm (Equation 2), and the rotations at the block boundaries are absorbed into the linear layers because matrix multiplication is associative: `(XQ)(QᵀW) = X(QQᵀ)W = XW`. The bias transformation `\tilde{b}_{out}^\ell = Q^\top b_{out}^\ell` ensures that after the output linear layer `XW_out^ℓ Q + b_out^ℓ Q?` — wait, actually the paper writes `\tilde{b}_{out}^\ell = Q^\top b_{out}^\ell`, but the correct form for consistency would require careful checking with the broadcasting conventions. The key point is that the bias added after `W_out` must be rotated to match the rotated output signal before it enters the residual connection and RMSNorm.

**Proof sketch (walking through Algorithm 1):** At line 1, the original network computes `X ← Wembd[s, :]` (indexing embeddings), while the transformed network computes `\tilde{X} ← \tilde{W}_{embd}[s, :] = Wembd[s, :] Q = X Q`. At line 2, Norm₀ is applied: the original computes `RMSNorm(X)`, the transformed computes `RMSNorm(\tilde{X}) = RMSNorm(XQ)`. At line 4, the block computation: `Z ← σ(X W_in^ℓ + b_in^ℓ) W_out^ℓ + b_out^ℓ`. The transformed block computes `σ(\tilde{X} \tilde{W}_{in}^ℓ + b_in^ℓ) \tilde{W}_{out}^ℓ + \tilde{b}_{out}^ℓ = σ((XQ)(QᵀW_in^ℓ) + b_in^ℓ) W_out^ℓ Q + Qᵀb_out^ℓ = σ(XW_in^ℓ + b_in^ℓ) W_out^ℓ Q + Qᵀ b_out^ℓ`. The key step: if we define `Z` as the original block output, the transformed block output is `Z Q` (the bias handling requires verifying that `\tilde{b}_{out}^\ell = Q^\top b_{out}^\ell` indeed yields this — the paper's form may have the rotation direction flipped depending on whether the bias is added before or after the rotation in their convention). At line 5, the residual connection: `X + Z` becomes `\tilde{X} + \tilde{Z} = (X+Z)Q`, and RMSNorm of this is `RMSNorm((X+Z)Q) = RMSNorm(X+Z) Q` by Equation 2. So the signal entering block ℓ+1 remains in the rotated frame, and the invariant propagates. At line 7, `X Whead` becomes `\tilde{X} \tilde{W}_{head} = (XQ)(QᵀWhead) = X Whead`.

**The crucial subtlety: this applies *globally* with a single Q.** Theorem 1 uses the *same* Q throughout the network. This means the rotation applied after the embedding is undone before block 1, reapplied after block 1, undone before block 2, etc. — the signal is "passed through" in either the original basis (inside blocks) or the rotated basis (between blocks), with the transitions handled seamlessly by the modified weight matrices.

#### Per-Block PCA and the Transition to Multiple Qℓ

Theorem 1 permits using the *same* Q for every block, but the paper immediately identifies a problem: "We quickly saw that the signals at different blocks of the network were not aligned, and that we would need to apply a different orthogonal matrix at each block, Qℓ." This is an empirical observation — the principal components of the signal change as it propagates through the network because each block applies a different nonlinear transformation. Using a single global Q would mean the PCA basis is dominated by the signal structure at some particular layer, and the deletion of dimensions would be optimal only for that layer.

**Allowing per-block Qℓ while maintaining equivalence.** If Qℓ differs between blocks, the simple cancellation pattern of Theorem 1 breaks at the residual connections. Consider the transition from block ℓ to block ℓ+1. After block ℓ, the output has been rotated by Qℓ (because `W_out^ℓ` was post-multiplied by Qℓ). The residual connection carries the signal from *before* block ℓ, which was rotated by Qℓ₋₁ (through the previous RMSNorm and its post-multiplication). These two signals — block output rotated by Qℓ and residual rotated by Qℓ₋₁ — are in *different* bases and cannot be directly added.

**The fix: insert a linear transformation in the residual path.** The paper explicitly modifies the residual connection by inserting the matrix `Q_{ℓ-1}^\top Q_ℓ` between the residual signal and the addition. If the residual signal is in the Qℓ₋₁ basis (multiplied by Qℓ₋₁), multiplying by `Q_{ℓ-1}^\top Q_ℓ` first un-rotates it (Qℓ₋₁ᵀ) and then re-rotates it into the Qℓ basis. Now the residual and the block output are both in the Qℓ basis, and addition is valid.

**Modified network equations with per-block rotations (from Figure 4):**

- Embedding: `\tilde{W}_{embd} = W_{embd} Q_1`
- Input weights for block ℓ: `\tilde{W}_{in}^\ell = Q_\ell^\top W_{in}^\ell`
- Output weights for block ℓ: `\tilde{W}_{out}^\ell = W_{out}^\ell Q_{\ell+1}` (note: Q_{ℓ+1}, not Qℓ — the output is rotated into the *next* block's basis)
- Residual path between block ℓ and ℓ+1: insert the D×D matrix `Q_\ell^\top Q_{\ell+1}` (actually the paper shows `Q_{\ell-1}^\top Q_\ell` for the residual *into* block ℓ, so the pattern is `Q_ℓ^\top Q_{ℓ+1}` into block ℓ+1)
- Output biases: `\tilde{b}_{out}^\ell = Q_{\ell+1}^\top b_{out}^\ell`
- Head: `\tilde{W}_{head} = Q_{L+1}^\top W_{head}` (the last block's output rotation is Q_{L+1})

The paper notes that the residual connection matrices "cannot be pre-computed and add a small (D×D) overhead to the model." This is the price of using per-block rotations: the residual paths now contain explicit matrix multiplications that were not present in the original network. However, after slicing (Section 3.4), these matrices become smaller (D_small × D_small), mitigating the overhead.

**PCA computation per block (Equation 8).** Given the calibration dataset (sequences indexed by i), the signal matrix at the output of RMSNorm block ℓ for sequence i is `X_{ℓ,i}` (shape: sequence_length × D, though the paper typically flattens across all tokens of all sequences). The covariance matrix is computed as:

$$ C_\ell = \sum_i X_{\ell,i}^\top X_{\ell,i} $$

where `Cℓ` is a D×D symmetric positive semi-definite matrix, and the sum runs over all tokens from all calibration sequences.

**What this equation computes:** The unnormalized empirical covariance of the D-dimensional signal vectors produced at the output of the ℓ-th RMSNorm block, accumulated across all calibration data. Each term `X_{ℓ,i}^\top X_{ℓ,i}` is the outer product of a signal vector (or, more precisely, the matrix product summing over the sequence-length dimension, producing a D×D matrix per sequence). Summing across sequences produces the total scatter matrix — the second-moment matrix of the signal distribution at that layer.

**Why this form:** PCA requires the eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix. Since the signals have been mean-subtracted by the preceding operations (the LayerNorm-to-RMSNorm conversion absorbed M into preceding Wout matrices), the scatter matrix Cℓ is proportional to the covariance matrix (no separate mean subtraction needed). Computing Cℓ as `Σ XᵀX` rather than explicitly mean-centering is both computationally simpler and relies on the guarantee that the signals are already zero-mean.

**Setting Qℓ:** Qℓ is then set to the matrix of eigenvectors of Cℓ, sorted by decreasing eigenvalue. If the eigendecomposition is `Cℓ = V Λ Vᵀ` with eigenvalues `λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ ... ≥ λ_D`, then `Qℓ = V` (the columns of V are the eigenvectors, which are orthonormal since Cℓ is symmetric).

**Why PCA — the design choice.** The paper's stated goal is L2-optimal reconstruction (Equation 9). If we must delete some dimensions of the signal Xℓ, the linear mapping that minimizes `||X - \tilde{X}||_2` where `\tilde{X} = X Q D D^\top Q^\top` (project onto a subspace and reconstruct) is the PCA basis — discarding the eigenvectors with the smallest eigenvalues. Any other orthogonal basis would have larger reconstruction error for the same number of retained dimensions. PCA therefore gives the best possible fidelity for a given compression ratio, under the L2 metric, among all linear dimensionality reductions.

The paper acknowledges that this is the natural first choice ("Our initial plan was to collect signals from the model, construct an orthogonal matrix using those signals and to delete parts of the network") and does not explore alternatives like independent component analysis or non-orthogonal projections, though Section 5 suggests "other methods of computing Q could improve the results."

**Calibration dataset choices.** The paper uses two different calibration sets: WikiText-2 (a language modeling dataset) and Alpaca (an instruction-following dataset). The ablation in Appendix A.3 (Figure 7) shows that calibration set sizes of at least 128 sequences and sequence lengths of at least 2048 tokens are needed for stable results, with larger sets generally improving perplexity. The main experiments use 1024 sequences of length 2048 (Table 1 and Section 4).

**Numerical precision.** The PCA computation uses double precision (FP64) for eigendecomposition. Appendix A.2 (Table 4) shows that using single precision (FP32) causes noticeable degradation, especially for larger models: LLAMA-2 70B with 30% slicing shows perplexity 8.75 with FP32 PCA versus 5.05 with FP64 PCA. The paper attributes this to "numerical errors during the PCA calculation phase" and notes that PyTorch's `torch.linalg` is used for the eigendecomposition.

#### Rotation Insertion Mechanics (Network Editing)

With Qℓ computed for each block, the network is edited according to the per-block generalization of Theorem 1, as illustrated in Figure 4.

**Input weight matrices Win.** For each attention or FFN block ℓ, the input weight matrix (which may be the concatenation of multiple projection matrices) is pre-multiplied by Qℓᵀ:

$$ \tilde{W}_{in}^\ell = Q_\ell^\top W_{in}^\ell $$

**What this computes:** Each column of `W_in^ℓ` is a D-dimensional vector representing how one output dimension of the linear projection combines the D input dimensions. Multiplying by Qℓᵀ rotates these column vectors from the original coordinate basis into the "principal component basis" of the signal that will be input to this block. After this rotation, the rows of the product correspond to principal component directions of the input signal.

**Why this form:** At this point in the forward pass, the signal arriving at block ℓ has been multiplied by Qℓ (from the previous RMSNorm's output, which was rotated by Qℓ via the preceding block's Wout or the residual path). To apply the original linear transformation Win in this rotated basis, we need `(signal) · (something) = (original_signal) · Win`. Since `signal = original_signal · Qℓ` (treating rows as vectors, right-multiplication), we need `something = Qℓᵀ Win` so that `(original_signal · Qℓ) · (Qℓᵀ Win) = original_signal · Win`. This is associativity: the Qℓ and Qℓᵀ cancel.

**Output weight matrices Wout.** For each block ℓ, the output weight matrix is post-multiplied by Qℓ₊₁ (the rotation for the *next* block, not the current one):

$$ \tilde{W}_{out}^\ell = W_{out}^\ell Q_{\ell+1} $$

**What this computes:** Each row of `W_out^ℓ` is a D-dimensional vector representing how one of the D intermediate features contributes to the D output dimensions. Multiplying by Qℓ₊₁ on the right rotates the output into the principal component basis of the *next* block's input signal.

**Why this form:** The block output in the original network is `Z = σ(...) W_out^ℓ`. In the edited network, we want the output to be in the Qℓ₊₁ basis so that it matches the rotation expected by block ℓ+1. By post-multiplying Wout by Qℓ₊₁, the output becomes `Z Qℓ₊₁`, which is exactly the rotated representation.

**Residual connection insertion.** The skip connection that adds the input of block ℓ (which is in the Qℓ basis) to the output of block ℓ (which, after editing, is in the Qℓ₊₁ basis) now requires a basis conversion. A new D×D matrix is inserted:

$$ \tilde{R}_\ell = Q_\ell^\top Q_{\ell+1} $$

where the residual path for block ℓ becomes: `residual_signal × Q_ℓ^\top Q_{ℓ+1}` before addition.

**What this computes:** The residual signal originates from before block ℓ and is in the Qℓ basis (it was the output of the previous RMSNorm, rotated by Qℓ). The block output is in the Qℓ₊₁ basis. To add them, the residual is first un-rotated (Qℓᵀ) and then re-rotated into Qℓ₊₁. Since these are orthogonal matrices, `Qℓᵀ Qℓ₊₁` is also orthogonal.

**Why this form is unavoidable:** This is the price of using per-block rather than global rotations. If Qℓ = Q for all ℓ, then `Qᵀ Q = I` and the residual connection is unchanged. But since the signal statistics change across layers, forcing a global Q would mean the PCA basis is suboptimal for most layers, degrading the quality of the slicing step. The paper accepts the small D×D overhead because it enables per-block optimal rotations and, crucially, because after slicing these matrices become D_small × D_small and the overhead shrinks proportionally.

**Embedding matrix.** The embedding matrix is post-multiplied by Q₁ (the rotation for the first block):

$$ \tilde{W}_{embd} = W_{embd} Q_1 $$

**What this computes:** Each token's embedding vector (a row of `Wembd`) is rotated by Q₁ to enter the first block in the Q₁ basis.

**LM head.** The head matrix is pre-multiplied by Q_{L+1}ᵀ (the inverse of the last block's output rotation):

$$ \tilde{W}_{head} = Q_{L+1}^\top W_{head} $$

**What this computes:** The signal entering the head is in the Q_{L+1} basis (rotated). To produce the same logits as the original network, we need to first un-rotate: `(signal in Q_{L+1} basis) × (Q_{L+1}ᵀ Whead) = (original signal) × Whead`.

**Bias handling.** Input biases `b_in^ℓ` are copied unchanged because the input to the block's linear layer, after rotation by Qℓᵀ, is `X Qℓ`, and the bias is added after the linear transformation: `(X Qℓ)(Qℓᵀ W_in^ℓ) + b_in^ℓ = X W_in^ℓ + b_in^ℓ`. Output biases require rotation: `\tilde{b}_{out}^\ell = Q_{\ell+1}^\top b_{out}^\ell` so that the output `Z Qℓ₊₁ + (Qℓ₊₁ᵀ b_out^ℓ)` equals `(Z + b_out^ℓ) Qℓ₊₁`. The head bias is copied unchanged: `\tilde{b}_{head} = b_{head}`.

**The key guarantee:** After this editing, the network is still mathematically equivalent to the original — every forward pass produces identical token predictions. This is not approximate; it is an exact algebraic identity that follows from the associativity of matrix multiplication and the rotation-invariance of RMSNorm (Equation 2). The only deviation from exact equivalence would come from floating-point rounding, which the paper mitigates by using double precision for the eigenvector computation.

#### Slicing (Structured Deletion)

With the network rotated into principal-component bases, the actual compression step is straightforward: delete the dimensions corresponding to the smallest eigenvalues of each Cℓ.

**The PCA reconstruction formulation (Equation 9).** PCA is traditionally described as a dimensionality reduction technique for a data matrix X. Given X (N×D), the PCA reconstruction with D_small < D retained dimensions is:

$$ Z = X Q D, \quad \tilde{X} = Z D^\top Q^\top = X Q D D^\top Q^\top $$

where Q is the D×D matrix of eigenvectors of XᵀX (sorted by decreasing eigenvalue), and D is a D×D_small "deletion matrix" — specifically, D is a D×D_small matrix consisting of the first D_small columns of the D×D identity matrix. D selects the top D_small principal components and discards the remaining D − D_small.

**What these equations compute:** `Z = XQD` projects the N×D signal matrix X onto the first D_small principal component axes, producing an N×D_small compressed representation. `\tilde{X} = Z D^\top Q^\top` reconstructs an N×D approximation by expanding back to D dimensions (padding with zeros for the discarded components) and rotating back to the original basis. PCA guarantees that this reconstruction minimizes `||X − \tilde{X}||_2` among all linear projections to D_small dimensions.

**Why this form:** The deletion matrix D is how PCA "throws away" information — it literally zeros out the coordinates corresponding to low-variance directions. The product `Q D` is the D×D_small matrix of the top D_small eigenvectors; `Dᵀ Qᵀ` is its transpose. The reconstruction error is the sum of the discarded eigenvalues.

**How slicing applies this to the network.** In SliceGPT, the signal matrix X is never materialized or reconstructed. Instead, the deletion matrix D is "pushed" into the surrounding weight matrices, permanently reducing their dimensions. Specifically:

- **Input matrices Win:** After rotation, `\tilde{W}_{in}^\ell = Q_\ell^\top W_{in}^\ell` with shape D×D (or D×3D for concatenated attention projections). The deletion removes the *rows* corresponding to discarded principal components. If we retain D_small dimensions, the new Win has shape D_small×D_out. The operation is: delete the bottom D−D_small rows of `\tilde{W}_{in}^\ell`.

- **Output matrices Wout:** After rotation, `\tilde{W}_{out}^\ell = W_{out}^\ell Q_{\ell+1}` with shape D×D. The deletion removes the *columns* corresponding to discarded principal components. The new Wout has shape D_in×D_small. The operation is: delete the rightmost D−D_small columns of `\tilde{W}_{out}^\ell`.

- **Embedding matrix:** After rotation, `\tilde{W}_{embd} = W_{embd} Q_1` with shape V×D. Delete the rightmost D−D_small columns, producing a V×D_small embedding matrix.

- **LM head:** After rotation, `\tilde{W}_{head} = Q_{L+1}^\top W_{head}` with shape D×V. Delete the bottom D−D_small rows, producing a D_small×V head matrix.

- **Residual connection matrices:** `R_ℓ = Q_ℓ^\top Q_{\ell+1}` with shape D×D. Delete both the bottom D−D_small rows and the rightmost D−D_small columns, producing a D_small×D_small matrix.

**Why the slicing dimensions align.** The key insight is that the principal component basis makes the deletion "consistent" across layers. The signal entering block ℓ is in the Qℓ basis; deleting the last D−D_small columns of Qℓ removes the lowest-variance signal dimensions. The linear layer `\tilde{W}_{in}^\ell` receives this D_small-dimensional signal, so deleting the corresponding rows of Win ensures dimensional compatibility. The output `\tilde{W}_{out}^\ell` produces a D_small-dimensional signal (after deleting columns), which is exactly what block ℓ+1 expects (since its Qℓ₊₁ has also been sliced to D_small rows). The residual connection matrices similarly shrink to D_small×D_small.

The paper emphasizes: "we never materialize the N×D signal matrix." The deletion is performed entirely at the weight-matrix level, as a one-time post-processing step on the stored model parameters.

**Why slicing is not the same as naive dimension pruning.** Without the PCA rotation, deleting rows and columns by norm (as the paper tested) "was very poor, with the WikiText-2 perplexity of the model soaring into the 1000s after pruning just a few columns." The reason: in the original basis, each dimension carries a mixture of high-variance and low-variance signal components. Deleting a dimension removes both indiscriminately, destroying information that might be distributed across all dimensions. After PCA rotation, the high-variance and low-variance components are separated into distinct dimensions — the first few carry most of the signal energy, the last few carry almost none. Deleting the last dimensions discards minimal information because those dimensions were already near-zero in the signal.

**Uniform vs. variable slicing across layers.** The main experiments use uniform slicing: the same percentage of dimensions is removed from every layer. Appendix A.4 (Figure 8) explores this: the eigenvalue spectrum decays faster in early layers than later ones, suggesting that "a greater amount of slicing could be applied after the orthogonal transformation in these early layers." The paper tests variable slicing by setting a target fraction of total variance to discard per layer rather than a fixed percentage of dimensions. Table 6 shows that variable slicing improves perplexity for OPT models (e.g., OPT 6.7B improves from 12.10 to 11.94 at ~25% total slicing) but *worsens* perplexity for LLAMA-2 models (LLAMA-2 7B degrades from 6.84 to 7.63). The paper does not deeply investigate this asymmetry.

**The end-to-end slicing procedure (operational summary).**

1. Convert all LayerNorm blocks to RMSNorm by absorbing M and diag(α) into adjacent weight matrices.
2. Run calibration data through the converted model, collecting signal matrices Xℓ at each RMSNorm output.
3. For each block ℓ, compute Cℓ = Σ Xℓᵢᵀ Xℓᵢ, eigendecompose to get Qℓ (eigenvectors sorted by decreasing eigenvalue).
4. Edit the network: `W_in^ℓ ← Qℓᵀ W_in^ℓ`, `W_out^ℓ ← W_out^ℓ Q_{ℓ+1}`, insert `Qℓᵀ Q_{ℓ+1}` into residual path, `W_embd ← W_embd Q₁`, `W_head ← Q_{L+1}ᵀ W_head`, adjust biases accordingly.
5. Apply deletion matrix D to all edited weight matrices: remove rows from Win and Whead, remove columns from Wout and Wembd, remove both from residual matrices.
6. The resulting model has embedding dimension D_small and all weight matrices are correspondingly smaller and entirely dense.

#### Practical Considerations and Implementation Details

**Double vs. single precision PCA.** Section 4 and Appendix A.2 establish that PCA must be computed in double precision. Table 4 quantifies the cost of single precision: LLAMA-2 70B with 30% slicing degrades from perplexity 5.05 (double precision, Table 1) to 8.75 (single precision, Table 4). The paper suggests this is due to numerical errors accumulating during eigendecomposition, though it does not diagnose whether the issue is eigenvalue convergence, eigenvector orthogonality violations, or downstream propagation of small errors through 80 layers.

**Calibration dataset and sequence length.** Appendix A.3 (Figure 7) ablates calibration set size (16 to 1024 sequences) and sequence length (128 to 4096 tokens) using OPT 6.7B and LLAMA-2 7B at 25% slicing. Perplexity improves as both increase, with sequence length showing substantial impact: at a fixed number of sequences, longer sequences provide more tokens for the covariance estimate, capturing long-range dependencies in the signal statistics. The main experiments use 1024 sequences × 2048 tokens = ~2M total tokens. Table 5 shows results with only 128 calibration sequences: perplexities are consistently worse across all models, confirming that sufficient calibration data is necessary.

**Compute cost.** The PCA computation (eigendecomposition of D×D matrices for each layer, plus signal collection) takes "approximately 3.5 hours to complete for the LLAMA-2 70B model" on a single H100 80GB GPU. Smaller models are faster: LLAMA-2 7B takes 44 minutes, LLAMA-2 13B takes 1 hour 8 minutes, Phi-2 takes 49 minutes (Table 3). This is a one-time cost per model, making it practical for deployment pipelines.

**Handling of RMSNorm scale parameters.** Some RMSNorm implementations include learnable scale parameters (a per-dimension scaling after normalization). The paper notes: "In some implementations an RMSNorm block may contain scale parameters. We consider these to be special instances of LayerNorm and handle them accordingly" — meaning they are absorbed into adjacent weight matrices during the conversion step.

**Remaining RMSNorm operations.** After slicing, the RMSNorm blocks themselves remain in the network. They now operate on D_small-dimensional vectors instead of D-dimensional ones. Since RMSNorm is a simple row-wise normalization (divide by L2 norm), its computational cost scales linearly with D_small and benefits directly from the dimensionality reduction.

**Optional recovery fine-tuning (RFT).** The paper presents SliceGPT both as a one-shot method and with optional RFT. The RFT uses LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) with `lora_r=32`, `lora_alpha=10`, sequence length 1024, and approximately 1k sequences (WikiText-2) or 5k sequences (Alpaca). RFT takes 23 minutes to 1 hour 35 minutes depending on model size (Table 3). The paper notes that RFT with Alpaca (instruction-following data) significantly outperforms WikiText-2 RFT on zero-shot tasks (Figure 6), attributing this to "the similarity between Alpaca and the benchmark tasks." RFT was not successfully applied to OPT models: "Despite an extensive search, we were not able to find RFT parameters that enabled improved performance in the OPT models."

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Computational Invariance as a Compression Primitive

The dominant paradigm in neural network compression — from Optimal Brain Surgeon (Hassibi et al., 1993) through SparseGPT (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023) — treats the trained weights as *the* object to be compressed. The weights are what they are; your job is to figure out which ones to zero out or quantize while minimizing the effect on the loss. This framing implicitly assumes that the network's representation is fixed and that compression quality depends entirely on how cleverly you select and compensate for removed weights.

SliceGPT proposes a fundamentally different conceptual move. Rather than asking "which weights can we delete?", it asks: **"in which rotated basis do the weights and signals become maximally compressible?"** The paper's key insight is that the transformer architecture possesses a *computational invariance* — the network computes the same function under any orthogonal transformation of its internal representations — which means the search for compressible structure can be conducted not in the fixed, trained representation but in an entire equivalence class of representations linked by orthogonal rotations. Theorem 1 proves this is not an approximation or a heuristic: it is an exact algebraic identity that follows from the rotation-invariance of RMSNorm (`RMSNorm(XQ) Q^⊤ = RMSNorm(X)`, Equation 2) and the associativity of matrix multiplication in the surrounding linear layers.

The significance of this reframing is that it **decouples the representation from the computation**. The network's weights encode a specific function, but the coordinate system in which those weights are expressed is a free parameter. Prior compression methods operated in whatever coordinate system the optimizer happened to produce during training — a basis that is almost certainly suboptimal for compression because the training loss cares about output accuracy, not about the compressibility of intermediate representations. SliceGPT's innovation is recognizing that you are *allowed* to change the basis, and that doing so before deletion transforms an intractable problem (naive row/column pruning fails catastrophically, with perplexity "soaring into the 1000s after pruning just a few columns") into a well-posed one (PCA reconstruction error is L2-optimal for linear dimensionality reduction).

This is a **fundamental conceptual shift**, not an incremental refinement. It does not improve on SparseGPT's weight selection algorithm or propose a better Hessian approximation. It changes the object being optimized from "which weights are expendable" to "which coordinate axes carry the least signal energy." The mathematical guarantee of exact equivalence (Theorem 1) makes this more than a heuristic — it is a provably lossless preprocessing step that sets up the subsequent lossy deletion step with the best possible alignment.

The evidence supporting this innovation is indirect but pervasive: the method works at all. Without the rotation, structured pruning is useless. With the rotation, SliceGPT matches or exceeds the fidelity of SparseGPT 2:4 on WikiText-2 perplexity (Table 1: LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing achieves 4.60 vs. SparseGPT 2:4 at 4.98) while producing dense matrices that run on existing GPU kernels. The rotation is what converts a failing baseline into a competitive method, and the invariance theorem is what justifies the rotation.

---

### Innovation 2: Signals, Not Weights, Determine Which Dimensions Are Important

The second conceptual move follows from the first but is logically distinct. Given that any orthogonal basis can represent the network, *which* basis should we choose? The answer — PCA on the signal (activation) matrices — is not obvious, because the dominant tradition in pruning has been to judge weight importance by weight properties: magnitude (Han et al., 2016), Hessian influence on the loss (LeCun et al., 1989; Hassibi et al., 1993), or column-wise reconstruction error propagated through subsequent columns (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023). All of these are *weight-centric* metrics. They ask: how important is this weight (or this column of weights) to the function the network computes?

SliceGPT inverts this logic. It asks: **how much of the signal's variance is carried by each dimension of the representation?** The principal components are computed on `X_ℓ`, the activations produced by the ℓ-th block — not on the weight matrices themselves. Equation 8 (`C_ℓ = Σ X_{ℓ,i}^⊤ X_{ℓ,i}`) computes the covariance of the *signals*, and Q_ℓ is set to the eigenvectors of this signal covariance. The dimensions that get deleted are those along which the signal varies the least across the calibration data.

This is a **diagnostic insight** as much as a method: the compressibility of a transformer layer is not a property of its weights in isolation, but of the interaction between the weights and the data distribution processed by the layer. Two layers with identical weight matrix norms could have entirely different compressibility depending on whether the preceding layers have already decorrelated the signal along certain dimensions. Figure 8 in Appendix A.4 makes this empirical: the eigenvalue spectrum decays faster in early layers than later ones, indicating that early-layer signals are more compressible — a property invisible to weight-only analysis.

The significance of this shift extends beyond the specific PCA choice. It implies that **weight importance is not an intrinsic property**; it is a function of the data distribution. This explains why magnitude pruning fails on LLMs (weights with small magnitudes might process high-variance signal components, and vice versa) and why methods that compensate for weight removal using Hessian information (SparseGPT) work better than pure magnitude pruning — the Hessian implicitly captures data-dependence through the loss curvature, but PCA on the signal does so directly and with a closed-form solution. The paper's finding that naive norm-based row/column pruning produces perplexity in the thousands while PCA-aligned slicing produces competitive perplexity (Table 1) is a stark empirical demonstration that *what you delete matters less than whether you've aligned the deletion axes with the signal structure*.

This is not a paradigmatic break with prior pruning theory — OBS and its descendants have always *used* data-dependent Hessian information, and optimal brain damage (LeCun et al., 1989) used second derivatives of the loss, which are data-dependent. But it is a **sharp conceptual clarification**: the data-dependence in SliceGPT is explicit, geometric (signal variance along coordinate axes), and computationally cheap (one eigendecomposition per layer on accumulated covariance matrices), whereas the data-dependence in Hessian-based methods is implicit, optimization-theoretic (loss curvature), and computationally intractable at scale. The move from "how much would the loss change if we removed this weight?" to "how much signal energy lives on this axis?" is a genuine simplification that makes structured pruning computationally feasible for models with ~10^8 parameters per layer.

---

### Innovation 3: The Privileged Role of RMSNorm Commutation

Most readers encountering Theorem 1 for the first time will immediately ask: why doesn't this work with LayerNorm? The paper's answer — that you must first convert LayerNorm to RMSNorm by absorbing the mean-subtraction matrix M and the scaling matrix diag(α) into adjacent weight matrices — is not just a preprocessing detail. It is a **structural diagnosis** of why transformers are compressible in this particular way.

The critical observation is that LayerNorm contains *two* types of operations: a **rotation-equivariant** core (dividing by the row norm) and **coordinate-tied** affine transformations (mean subtraction, per-dimension rescaling, offset addition). The affine components are tied to the specific coordinate axes of the trained representation — they say "dimension 37 should be scaled by 1.2 and shifted by 0.05," which only makes sense if dimension 37 consistently represents a particular feature. If you rotate the representation, dimension 37 becomes a linear combination of the original dimensions, and the learned affine parameters no longer apply.

The conversion procedure (Section 3.2, Figure 3) *dissolves* this coordinate-dependence by moving the affine operations into the weight matrices, where they become part of the linear transformations rather than sitting between them. After conversion, the normalization is pure RMSNorm — rotation-equivariant — and the network is free to be rotated. This reveals something subtle about transformer training: **the coordinate-tied affines in LayerNorm are an artifact of the optimization process, not a functional requirement of the architecture.** The network learned to use them to represent information that could equally well be represented in the adjacent weight matrices — the conversion just relocates that information algebraically.

This has implications beyond SliceGPT. It suggests that the distinction between LayerNorm and RMSNorm in transformer architectures is less fundamental than it appears, and that many of the learned affine parameters may be redundant with the weight matrices they sit between. It also explains a practical observation: the PCA computation requires the signals to be *zero-mean* (for PCA to be interpretable as variance maximization), and the conversion of M into the preceding Wout guarantees this without an explicit centering step.

This is an **incremental refinement** on top of the computational invariance idea, but it bridges a critical gap between theory (the invariance holds for RMSNorm) and practice (LLMs use LayerNorm). Without it, SliceGPT would be restricted to a minority of architectures that use pure RMSNorm. The fact that the conversion is exact (not an approximation) and reversible means it does not introduce any source of fidelity loss beyond what the slicing step itself causes.

---

### Innovation 4: Practical Viability via Dense Matrix Multiplication

The fourth innovation is not a mathematical insight but an **engineering reframing** of what "good compression" means. The paper establishes a new evaluation criterion: compression methods should be judged not just by parameter count reduction or theoretical FLOP reduction, but by **realized throughput on existing hardware using standard dense kernels**.

SparseGPT 2:4 is the explicit foil. At the 2:4 sparsity ratio (50% of weights pruned), SparseGPT matches or exceeds SliceGPT 25% in parameter reduction. In a benchmark that only measured model size, SparseGPT would appear competitive or superior. But the paper demonstrates (Section 4, "Benchmarking Throughput") that SliceGPT's 25% slicing achieves comparable or better real-world speedup as SparseGPT 2:4 on large models (Tables 12-13), and the paper states outright that "end-to-end performance gains are not feasible with our baseline SparseGPT 2:4 at the time of writing."

This result exposes a mismatch between the compression literature's evaluation norms and deployment reality. A pruning method that produces weight matrices with irregular sparsity patterns has done something mathematically impressive, but if the resulting sparse matmul cannot be executed faster than a dense matmul by existing GPU kernels, it has failed at the operational goal of compression (which is to reduce inference cost). SliceGPT's output is *dense and smaller* — exactly the kind of computation that cuBLAS and PyTorch's matmul are tuned to execute at near-peak throughput. The paper quantifies this with specific hardware benchmarks: LLAMA-2 70B inference time on consumer GPUs drops from 1764 GPUms/token (7 GPUs, dense) to 1075 GPUms/token (5 GPUs, 25% sliced), a genuine 36% GPU-count reduction and ~33% per-token compute reduction (Table 2).

This is a **methodological innovation** rather than a theoretical one: the paper argues, through its benchmarking choices, that the field should prioritize compress-to-dense methods over sparsify-to-sparse methods until the sparse-hardware ecosystem matures. It also demonstrates that structured pruning (row/column deletion) can be *more* competitive with unstructured sparsity than previously believed — provided the deletion is aligned with the signal structure via the rotation step. The key evidence is in the throughput benchmarks (Appendix A.6, A.7) rather than in a single figure, but they collectively make the case that SliceGPT's compression translates directly into deployable speedups without waiting for kernel development.

This innovation is **incremental** in that it does not change the compression algorithm's mathematics, but it is **practically significant** because it reorients the evaluation criteria toward operational outcomes. A method that produces impressive sparsity patterns but cannot actually run faster is a laboratory result; SliceGPT's design — produce dense, smaller matrices — makes it an engineering solution.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary evaluation uses **WikiText-2** (Merity et al., 2016) for language generation perplexity, presumably on the standard test split. For zero-shot downstream tasks, the paper evaluates on **PIQA** (Bisk et al., 2020), **WinoGrande** (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), **HellaSwag** (Zellers et al., 2019), **ARC-e** and **ARC-c** (Clark et al., 2018) using the LM Evaluation Harness (Gao et al., 2021) with default parameters. Calibration data for computing Qℓ comes from two sources: the **WikiText-2 training set** and the **Alpaca training dataset** (Taori et al., 2023), with 1024 sequences of length 2048 used in the main experiments (ablated in Appendix A.3).

- **Base model(s).** The paper evaluates on three model families: **OPT** (Zhang et al., 2022) at scales 125M, 1.3B, 2.7B, 6.7B, 13B, 30B, and 66B parameters; **LLAMA-2** (Touvron et al., 2023) at scales 7B, 13B, and 70B parameters; and **Phi-2** (2.8B parameters, used only in zero-shot task evaluations). OPT 175B is explicitly excluded as it "is outperformed by smaller LLAMA-2 models." The scale range is deliberate: it spans from model sizes where structured compression is expected to be difficult (the paper notes "smaller but dense LMs perform better than LMs with 13B parameters or less pruned to similar sizes") to the largest generally available open-weight models (LLAMA-2 70B), allowing the paper to demonstrate that SliceGPT's effectiveness scales with model size.

- **Metrics.** For language generation, the primary metric is **WikiText-2 perplexity (PPL)**, computed on the test set using the compressed model with standard autoregressive evaluation. For zero-shot tasks, the metric is **accuracy** on each individual benchmark, aggregated as **mean accuracy** across all five tasks (visualized in Figures 5 and 6, with per-task breakdowns in Appendix A.5 Tables 7–10). For throughput and inference benchmarks, the paper measures **tokens per second** (Table 11) and **milliseconds per token** (Table 2), along with **GPU count** and **total GPUms** (GPU count × ms/token) as a combined measure of compute resources consumed.

- **Baselines.** The primary compression baseline is **SparseGPT 2:4** (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023), which produces semi-structured sparsity with exactly 2 non-zero weights in every block of 4. The paper uses SparseGPT 2:4 because it is "the only sparsity scheme which achieves speedup" (via NVIDIA sparse tensor core support). The dense (uncompressed) model performance is reported for all metrics as a ceiling. The paper also tested a naive structured pruning baseline (pruning columns or rows by smallest norm) but reports it was "very poor, with the WikiText-2 perplexity of the model soaring into the 1000s after pruning just a few columns" and does not include it in formal comparisons.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** SliceGPT does not involve dynamic generation budgets — it is a one-time static compression step. Compression cost is measured in **wall-clock time** on specific hardware (Table 3): LLAMA-2 7B takes 44 minutes, LLAMA-2 70B takes 3 hours 31 minutes on a single H100 80GB GPU. Inference efficiency is measured in **GPUms per token** (Table 2: GPU count × milliseconds per generated token), which captures both per-token latency and the number of GPUs required to hold the model in memory. Throughput is measured as **tokens per second** at maximum feasible batch size (Table 11). For the per-layer timing comparison against SparseGPT 2:4 (Appendix A.7), the cost is **milliseconds per matrix multiplication** for each operation type (down projection, up/gate projection, key/value, query, output), summed to a layer total and compared via relative speedup factors.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation. The compression is deterministic given the calibration data and slicing percentage. All evaluations are on standard benchmark test sets. The only "selection" is the choice of calibration dataset (WikiText-2 vs. Alpaca) and calibration set size (ablated in Appendix A.3), which are treated as hyperparameter choices explored via ablation rather than selected via held-out validation. Recovery fine-tuning uses LoRA hyperparameters (`lora_r=32`, `lora_alpha=10`, sequence length 1024) with defaults from the Hugging Face PEFT package (Mangrulkar et al., 2022); the paper reports that "despite an extensive search, we were not able to find RFT parameters that enabled improved performance in the OPT models," indicating some hyperparameter exploration was conducted but not systematically varying all RFT parameters.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Language Generation Perplexity (WikiText-2)

The central compression-quality results appear in **Table 1**, which reports WikiText-2 perplexity for all OPT and LLAMA-2 model sizes across slicing levels of 10%, 20%, 25%, and 30%, alongside the dense baseline and SparseGPT 2:4.

**Headline finding: SliceGPT achieves consistent, monotonic degradation with increasing slicing, and at 25% slicing outperforms SparseGPT 2:4 on larger models.** For LLAMA-2 70B, the dense perplexity is 3.32; SliceGPT at 20% yields 4.25, at 25% yields 4.60, and at 30% yields 5.05. SparseGPT 2:4 achieves 4.98 — worse than SliceGPT 25% (4.60 vs. 4.98, a gap of 0.38 PPL) and marginally better than SliceGPT 30% (5.05 vs. 4.98). For OPT 66B, the pattern is similar: dense 9.33, SliceGPT 25% at 9.68, SliceGPT 30% at 9.85, SparseGPT 2:4 at 10.22 — SliceGPT 25% and 30% both outperform SparseGPT 2:4. The pattern is not uniform across model sizes: for the smallest models (OPT 125M, OPT 1.3B), SparseGPT 2:4 outperforms SliceGPT 25% (OPT 1.3B: SparseGPT 29.61 vs. SliceGPT 25% 17.46? Wait — actually checking Table 1, OPT 1.3B dense is 14.61, SparseGPT 2:4 is 29.61, SliceGPT 25% is 17.46. So SliceGPT 25% *is* better than SparseGPT at this size. The direct SparseGPT comparison at 25%: for LLAMA-2 7B, SparseGPT 2:4 is 8.69 vs. SliceGPT 25% at 7.24; LLAMA-2 13B: SparseGPT 7.07 vs. SliceGPT 25% 6.30. Across all models in Table 1 with 7B+ parameters, **SliceGPT 25% has strictly better perplexity than SparseGPT 2:4.** The only exception is OPT 2.7B where the paper notes "30% sliced models beat 2:4 sparsity for all model sizes except 2.7B" — at 2.7B, SliceGPT 30% is 15.83 vs. SparseGPT 2:4 at 14.90, making SliceGPT slightly worse.)

**Scale dependence.** The paper explicitly notes that "SliceGPT exhibits superior performance when applied to OPT models compared to LLAMA-2 models" and that "the performance of SliceGPT improves as the model size increases." This is visible in the gap between dense and sliced perplexity: for OPT 125M, slicing 25% degrades from 27.64 to 37.74 (a 10.1 PPL increase); for OPT 66B, slicing 25% degrades from 9.33 to 9.68 (a 0.35 PPL increase). The degradation is far smaller in absolute terms for larger models. The paper attributes this to larger models having "more promising opportunities for compression."

**Calibration set size sensitivity.** Appendix A.3 (Figure 7 and Table 5) shows the impact of calibration set size. Table 5 (128 sequences, 2048 length) shows consistently worse perplexity than Table 1 (1024 sequences, 2048 length). For LLAMA-2 70B with 25% slicing, 128 calibration sequences yield perplexity 4.89 vs. 4.60 with 1024 sequences — a meaningful degradation. Sequence length matters too: Figure 7 (right) shows that increasing calibration sequence length from 128 to 4096 tokens monotonically improves perplexity for both OPT 6.7B and LLAMA-2 7B.

**Per-layer variable slicing.** Table 6 in Appendix A.4 compares uniform 25% slicing against variable slicing (where each layer's deletion is determined by a target variance threshold rather than a fixed percentage). Variable slicing improves OPT models: OPT 6.7B goes from 12.10 (uniform) to 11.94 (variable, 24.7% total slicing); OPT 66B goes from 9.75 to 9.63 (24.1% total). However, variable slicing *degrades* LLAMA-2 models: LLAMA-2 7B goes from 6.84 to 7.63 (24.1% total slicing); LLAMA-2 70B goes from 4.44 to 4.63 (25.5% total). The paper does not explain this asymmetry in detail but the spectrum analysis in Appendix A.4 (Figure 8) shows that LLAMA-2 has "a more tightly compressed distribution in its embeddings spectrum," with "no dominant principal components with significantly more information," making targeted deletion less productive.

#### Zero-Shot Task Performance

Figures 5 and 6 present aggregate zero-shot task results (mean accuracy across PIQA, WinoGrande, HellaSwag, ARC-e, ARC-c). Detailed per-task breakdowns appear in Appendix A.5, Tables 7–10.

**Without recovery fine-tuning (Figure 5).** The top row uses WikiText-2 calibration, the bottom row uses Alpaca calibration.

- WikiText-2 calibration: For OPT 66B, dense mean accuracy is 66.16%; SliceGPT at 20% drops marginally to 65.74%, at 25% to 65.17%, and at 30% to 64.24%. The degradation at 25% is under 1 percentage point (~1.5% relative). For LLAMA-2 70B, the drop is steeper: dense 76.57%, 20% sliced 72.34%, 25% sliced 69.75%, 30% sliced 66.11%. SliceGPT at 25% retains ~91% of dense accuracy. For Phi-2, the drop is severe: dense 72.24%, 25% sliced 54.46% (retaining only ~75% of dense performance).
- Alpaca calibration: The pattern is similar but generally slightly worse. For OPT 66B, 25% sliced achieves 65.30% (vs. 65.17% with WikiText-2 — essentially identical). For LLAMA-2 70B, 25% sliced achieves 73.59% (vs. 69.75% with WikiText-2 — Alpaca calibration is noticeably *better* here, which is unusual and may reflect different signal statistics).

**With recovery fine-tuning (Figure 6).** RFT is applied only to LLAMA-2 and Phi-2; the paper notes RFT did not help OPT models.

- WikiText-2 calibration + RFT: LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing achieves 71.28% (up from 69.75% without RFT). Phi-2 at 25% slicing remains poor at 55.17% (vs. 54.46% without RFT — minimal recovery).
- Alpaca calibration + RFT: This is where RFT provides dramatic gains. LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing with Alpaca RFT achieves 75.62%, compared to 76.57% dense — retaining **98.8% of dense accuracy**. At 30% slicing, LLAMA-2 70B with Alpaca RFT achieves 74.30% (97.0% of dense). Phi-2 benefits substantially: 25% sliced with Alpaca RFT achieves 65.24% vs. 72.24% dense, retaining **90.3% of dense accuracy**.

The paper states: "We attribute this difference to the similarity between Alpaca and the benchmark tasks." The per-task breakdowns (Table 10 vs. Table 9) confirm this: for LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing, Alpaca RFT improves ARC-c from 54.69 (WikiText-2 RFT) to 58.70 (Alpaca RFT); ARC-e improves from 79.88 to 81.36; HellaSwag improves substantially from 71.41 to 79.04. These are the tasks most similar to instruction-following formats.

**The slicing-accuracy tradeoff across model families.** The paper's abstract claims "99%, 99% and 90% zero-shot task performance of the dense model respectively" for OPT 66B, LLAMA-2 70B, and Phi-2. Checking the numbers:

- OPT 66B at 25% slicing, no RFT (Table 7): dense 66.16, sliced 65.17 → 65.17/66.16 = **98.5%** retained. The paper rounds to 99%.
- LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing with Alpaca RFT (Table 10): dense 76.57, sliced 75.62 → **98.8%** retained. The paper's 99% claim requires RFT for LLAMA-2. Without RFT (Table 8), 25% sliced LLAMA-2 70B achieves 73.59% → 96.1% retained, which does not match the 99% claim. The abstract figure is therefore conditional on RFT, which is reasonable since the abstract mentions the models "can remove up to 25%... while maintaining" this performance — "can" implying the best configuration.
- Phi-2 at 25% slicing with Alpaca RFT (Table 10): dense 72.24, sliced 65.24 → **90.3%** retained. Matches the abstract claim.

#### Throughput and Inference Speed Benchmarks

The paper provides three sets of timing benchmarks to demonstrate that SliceGPT's theoretical parameter reduction translates to real-world speedups.

**Token throughput at maximum batch size (Table 11, Appendix A.6).** On 80GB H100 GPUs with sequence length 128, batch sizes are doubled until GPU memory is exhausted or throughput drops off. For LLAMA-2 70B, the dense model requires 2 GPUs and achieves 541 tokens/s at batch size 128. At 25% slicing, the same 2 GPUs can fit batch size 256 (double the dense batch size), achieving 839 tokens/s — a **1.55× throughput improvement**. At 50% slicing, the model fits on a single GPU with batch size 128 and achieves 1014 tokens/s — compared to the dense model's 2-GPU requirement, this is a **3.75× throughput improvement per GPU** (or equivalently, 3.75× the total throughput since one GPU now does the work of two).

The paper notes: "For models of size 13B, the performance increase from batch-size increasing is less pronounced because the models take up little of the GPU memory." OPT 13B shows only 1.13× throughput improvement at 25% slicing (2518 → 2846 tokens/s) and 1.22× at 50% slicing (→ 3071 tokens/s). The throughput gains come primarily from two sources: fitting larger batch sizes (which improves GPU utilization) and reduced per-token FLOPs. For models that already saturate GPU utilization at the dense batch size, only the FLOP reduction contributes.

**Per-token inference latency (Table 2).** This benchmark measures the time to generate a single token (batch size 1, sequence length 128) on both consumer (Quadro RTX6000, 24GB) and datacenter (A100, 40GB) GPUs. The metric is total GPUms = (number of GPUs) × (ms per token), which captures both per-token speed and the GPU count required. For LLAMA-2 70B:

- A100 40GB: Dense requires 4 GPUs × 125ms = 500 GPUms. 25% sliced requires 3 GPUs × 110ms = 330 GPUms — a reduction to **66%** of the dense compute.
- RTX6000 24GB: Dense requires 7 GPUs × 252ms = 1764 GPUms. 25% sliced requires 5 GPUs × 215ms = 1075 GPUms — a reduction to **~61%** of the dense compute (the paper quotes 64%, presumably from a different rounding of intermediate values).

For OPT 66B, similar reductions: A100 40GB goes from 4 GPUs × 114ms = 456 GPUms to 3 GPUs × 102ms = 306 GPUms (67% of dense); RTX6000 goes from 6 GPUs × 237ms = 1422 GPUms to 5 GPUs × 204ms = 1020 GPUms (72% of dense).

The paper attributes "this improvement to our approach of substituting weight matrices with smaller ones and using dense kernels in our compressed models, which is infeasible with other pruning schemes."

**Per-operation timing comparison against SparseGPT 2:4 (Tables 12–13, Appendix A.7).** This is the most detailed hardware comparison. Using CuSparseLT 0.5 for 2:4 sparse matmuls and PyTorch for dense/SliceGPT matmuls on an 80GB A100 with sequence length 2048, the paper times each matrix multiplication type in one transformer layer and sums them.

For LLAMA-2 70B (Table 12): SparseGPT 2:4 achieves 12.20ms total per layer vs. 16.13ms dense, a 1.32× speedup. SliceGPT 25% also achieves 12.20ms total — **the same 1.32× speedup**. SliceGPT 30% is not reported in this table, but SliceGPT 50% achieves 8.63ms (1.87× speedup) at the cost of perplexity 8.86. For LLAMA-2 7B (Table 12): SparseGPT 2:4 achieves 2.70ms vs. 3.99ms dense (1.48× speedup), while SliceGPT 25% achieves 2.99ms (1.33× speedup) — SparseGPT is faster at this smaller scale. For LLAMA-2 13B, the pattern is intermediate: SparseGPT 1.50×, SliceGPT 25% 1.30×.

The critical finding is in the combination of perplexity and speedup. For LLAMA-2 70B, SliceGPT 25% achieves the same speedup as SparseGPT 2:4 (1.32×) but with *better* perplexity (4.60 vs. 4.98). At 7B, SparseGPT achieves better speedup (1.48× vs. 1.33×) but with *worse* perplexity (8.69 vs. 7.24). The paper summarizes: "For larger models, SliceGPT (25%) gives the same speedup as SparseGPT 2:4 but with better WikiText-2 perplexity. For smaller models SparseGPT 2:4 provides better speedup albeit at worse perplexity."

#### Recovery Fine-Tuning (RFT) Results

The RFT results appear in Figure 6 and Appendix A.5 Tables 9–10. Key observations:

**RFT with WikiText-2 provides minimal improvement.** Comparing Table 7 (no RFT, WikiText-2 calibration) and Table 9 (RFT, WikiText-2 calibration): for LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing, no RFT achieves 69.75% mean accuracy, RFT achieves 71.28% — a modest +1.53 percentage point gain. For Phi-2 at 25% slicing, no RFT is 54.46%, RFT is 55.17% — negligible. The paper does not report the RFT training data source, but the implication is that WikiText-2 (a language modeling corpus) does not transfer well to zero-shot task accuracy.

**RFT with Alpaca provides large gains.** Comparing Table 8 (no RFT, Alpaca calibration) and Table 10 (RFT, Alpaca calibration): for LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing, no RFT achieves 73.59%, RFT achieves 75.62% — a +2.03 point gain that brings performance within 1% of dense. For Phi-2 at 25% slicing, no RFT achieves 62.52%, RFT achieves 65.24% — a +2.72 point gain. The gains are largest on tasks most similar to instruction-following: HellaSwag for Phi-2 at 25% improves from 58.19 (no RFT) to 63.39 (RFT), ARC-c for LLAMA-2 70B improves from 52.65 to 58.70.

**RFT does not converge for OPT models.** The paper states in its Figure 6 caption: "Despite an extensive search, we were not able to find RFT parameters that enabled improved performance in the OPT models." This is a notable negative result — the method that rescues LLAMA-2 and Phi-2 accuracy does not transfer to OPT, suggesting either differences in how the model families represent recoverable information or that the RFT hyperparameter space is model-specific enough that the same search did not transfer.

**RFT time cost.** Table 3 breaks down the compute cost. For LLAMA-2 70B, RFT takes 1 hour 35 minutes on 4× H100 80GB GPUs, compared to 3 hours 31 minutes for the slicing itself on a single H100. Total compression time is 5 hours 6 minutes. For smaller models, total time is 1–3 hours. The paper frames this as practical: compression can be completed in an afternoon on accessible hardware.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

Each ablation addresses a specific design choice in the SliceGPT pipeline.

**Numerical precision of PCA computation:** Using single precision (FP32) for eigendecomposition significantly degrades larger models, as shown in Table 4 (Appendix A.2). LLAMA-2 70B at 30% slicing with FP32 PCA achieves perplexity 8.75 vs. 5.05 with FP64 PCA (Table 1) — a 3.70 PPL degradation. LLAMA-2 13B degrades from 6.99 to 7.49 at 30% slicing. Smaller models are less affected: OPT 125M at 30% slicing shows 44.17 (FP32) vs. 43.98 (FP64). The paper attributes this to "numerical errors during the PCA calculation phase" without deeper diagnosis, but the pattern (larger models more affected) suggests error accumulation through the deeper network or ill-conditioned covariance matrices at higher embedding dimensions.

**Calibration set size:** Figure 7 (left) sweeps calibration set size from 16 to 1024 sequences for OPT 6.7B and LLAMA-2 7B at 25% slicing. For OPT 6.7B, WikiText-2 perplexity drops from ~14.5 at 16 sequences to ~12.1 at 1024 sequences. For LLAMA-2 7B, the drop is from ~8.5 to ~7.2. The improvement saturates after ~128–256 sequences; the main experiments use 1024 sequences as a conservative choice. Table 5 provides the full perplexity table with 128 calibration sequences for comparison: all models show worse perplexity than with 1024 sequences, confirming that sufficient calibration data matters.

**Calibration sequence length:** Figure 7 (right) sweeps sequence length from 128 to 4096 tokens at a fixed number of sequences (128 for OPT 6.7B, unspecified for LLAMA-2 7B — presumably the same). Longer sequences consistently improve perplexity: OPT 6.7B improves from ~12.8 at sequence length 128 to ~12.1 at 4096. The paper concludes "having a larger sequence length can result in better perplexity." The main experiments use 2048, which appears close to the point of diminishing returns.

**Calibration dataset choice (WikiText-2 vs. Alpaca):** The zero-shot results in Figure 5 and Tables 7–8 show that Alpaca calibration is sometimes better, sometimes worse than WikiText-2 calibration. For LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing with no RFT, Alpaca achieves 73.59% vs. WikiText-2 at 69.75% (Alpaca is better). For OPT 66B at 25%, Alpaca achieves 65.30% vs. WikiText-2 at 65.17% (near-identical). For Phi-2 at 25%, Alpaca achieves 62.52% vs. WikiText-2 at 54.46% (Alpaca substantially better). No systematic explanation is provided; the variance suggests the calibration data distribution matters, but the method is not catastrophically sensitive to it.

**Uniform vs. variable slicing across layers:** Table 6 evaluates whether allowing different layers to be sliced by different amounts (based on the fraction of variance discarded in that layer's PCA) improves over uniform slicing. For OPT models, variable slicing improves perplexity modestly: OPT 66B improves from 9.75 (25% uniform) to 9.63 (24.1% total variable). For LLAMA-2 models, variable slicing worsens perplexity: LLAMA-2 7B degrades from 6.84 to 7.63. The paper attributes this to the eigenvalue spectrum (Figure 8): LLAMA-2 has a more uniform spectrum across dimensions, so "there are no dominant principal components with significantly more information, making the pruning of these components a more challenging task." Variable slicing likely over-prunes dimensions that, while having smaller eigenvalues than others, still carry near-equal information content in the flat spectrum.

**Recovery fine-tuning with different calibration datasets:** The comparison between Figures 6 left (WikiText-2 calibration and RFT) and right (Alpaca calibration and RFT) is itself an ablation of the RFT data source. The dramatic difference — Alpaca RFT restores up to 99% of dense accuracy while WikiText-2 RFT provides minimal gains — demonstrates that RFT data must be in-distribution with respect to the evaluation tasks. This is not surprising but is empirically quantified.

**Slicing percentage sweep:** The four slicing levels (10%, 20%, 25%, 30%) in Table 1 constitute an implicit ablation of the compression-aggressiveness tradeoff. The degradation is monotonic across all model sizes and families, with no threshold effects observed — performance declines smoothly as more dimensions are removed. This is consistent with PCA removing dimensions in order of increasing signal variance.

**Double-check: adding a linear layer in the skip connection:** The paper does not ablate the necessity of the `Q_ℓ^⊤ Q_{ℓ+1}` transformation in the residual path. This is treated as mathematically required for per-block rotations (Section 3.3), not as an optional design choice. The small overhead is acknowledged: "these additional operations cannot be pre-computed and add a small (D×D) overhead to the model." No experiment compares against omitting them.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments demonstrate a specific and practically important result: SliceGPT reduces the embedding dimension of transformer LLMs in a way that produces immediately deployable speedups on standard dense matmul kernels, while maintaining competitive language modeling perplexity and zero-shot task accuracy on the largest model scales tested. The evidence for this is multi-faceted — perplexity benchmarks (Table 1), zero-shot accuracy (Figures 5–6), inference latency (Table 2), token throughput (Table 11), and per-operation matmul timing (Tables 12–13) — and collectively makes a convincing case for the method's practical viability.

However, the experimental scope has several limitations that bound the strength of the paper's claims.

**The claim "SliceGPT can remove up to 25%... while maintaining 99%, 99% and 90% zero-shot task performance" is supported but conditional.** The 99% figure for OPT 66B is supported without RFT (98.5% at 25% slicing, Table 7). The 99% figure for LLAMA-2 70B requires Alpaca-calibrated RFT (98.8%, Table 10) — without RFT, the figure is 96.1% (25% slicing, Alpaca calibration, Table 8) or 91.1% (25% slicing, WikiText-2 calibration, Table 7). The 90% figure for Phi-2 also requires Alpaca RFT (90.3%, Table 10). Since the abstract says "can remove up to 25%... while maintaining," this conditional presentation is defensible (the models *can* maintain that performance under the best configuration), but readers should understand that the highest-fidelity numbers require both a specific calibration dataset and a post-slicing fine-tuning step. The one-shot (no RFT) fidelity is lower.

**The comparison against SparseGPT 2:4 is informative but incomplete.** SparseGPT 2:4 is the only structured/semi-structured pruning baseline compared. Other methods — LLM-pruner (Ma et al., 2023a), LLM Surgeon (van der Ouderaa et al., 2023), Wanda (Sun et al., 2023) — are discussed in related work but not benchmarked. The paper acknowledges LLM Surgeon is contemporaneous, but experimental comparison would strengthen the claim of superiority. The SparseGPT comparison itself uses only 2:4 sparsity; SparseGPT also supports unstructured sparsity at arbitrary levels, and the paper does not report SliceGPT vs. SparseGPT-unstructured at matched parameter counts. Such a comparison might show that unstructured sparsity achieves better perplexity than structured slicing at the same compression ratio — the tradeoff would then be fidelity vs. hardware deployability, which is the argument the paper makes but without quantifying the fidelity gap.

**The zero-shot evaluation uses five tasks, all from the same evaluation suite (LM Evaluation Harness).** These are standard benchmarks, but they represent a particular slice of LLM capability — commonsense reasoning and basic science knowledge. The paper does not evaluate on code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), mathematical reasoning (GSM8K, MATH), long-form generation quality, or instruction-following benchmarks (AlpacaEval, MT-Bench). The strong performance of Alpaca RFT (Figure 6) suggests the zero-shot tasks are aligned with Alpaca's instruction-following distribution; performance on tasks requiring different capabilities (e.g., mathematical derivation, multi-step reasoning) might degrade differently under slicing. The lack of diversity in evaluation tasks is a meaningful gap.

**The RFT failure on OPT is unexplained.** The paper reports that RFT did not improve OPT models despite "an extensive search" but provides no analysis of why. OPT and LLAMA-2 differ in training data, architecture details (e.g., OPT uses ReLU activation, LLAMA-2 uses SiLU), and pre-training objectives. Understanding whether the failure is due to activation function differences, training data distribution, or LoRA hyperparameter sensitivity would clarify the generality of the RFT recovery strategy. As it stands, the paper demonstrates that RFT works for LLAMA-2 and Phi-2 but cannot claim it as a universal post-slicing recovery technique.

**The throughput benchmarks show strong speedups but under specific conditions.** The 1.55× throughput improvement for LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing (Table 11) comes primarily from being able to double the batch size (128 → 256) on the same 2 GPUs. This is a memory-capacity benefit: the smaller model leaves room for larger batches. In a deployment scenario where latency constraints cap batch size at 1 (interactive chat), the throughput improvement would be smaller and determined only by per-token FLOP reduction. The per-token latency measurements (Table 2) are at batch size 1 and show a per-token speedup of 125ms → 110ms (12% faster) on A100 — the larger gains (500 GPUms → 330 GPUms, 34% reduction) come primarily from reducing GPU count from 4 to 3. If a deployment already has exactly 4 GPUs available and cannot reconfigure, the speedup is only the per-token improvement. The paper would benefit from benchmarking at fixed GPU count (e.g., 4 GPUs with the sliced model running at larger batch size) to separate the memory-capacity benefit from the raw-compute benefit.

**The residual connection overhead is acknowledged but not benchmarked in isolation.** The per-block Q_ℓ^⊤ Q_{ℓ+1} matrices add D×D matmuls to the residual paths. After slicing to D_small, these become D_small×D_small — but they are still additional operations not present in the original network. The per-operation timing tables (12–13) include these implicitly in the layer totals, but an ablation showing inference time with and without these residual path transformations (even if mathematically required) would quantify their contribution to the remaining overhead. The paper states the overhead is "small" but does not provide the specific FLOP count or timing breakdown.

**The calibration data requirement (1024 sequences of 2048 tokens) is modest but dataset-dependent.** The paper's main results use WikiText-2 and Alpaca, both English text corpora. Whether the PCA transformation computed on English text transfers to non-English inputs, code, or other modalities is untested. If a deployment needs the compressed model to work well across multiple languages or domains, the calibration data would need to be representative of that distribution — and the paper provides no guidance on how sensitive the results are to calibration data domain mismatch.

**The paper does not evaluate the compressed models on generation quality metrics beyond perplexity.** Perplexity measures the model's uncertainty about held-out text, which correlates with but is not identical to generation quality (coherence, factual accuracy, diversity). A compressed model with acceptable perplexity might still produce noticeably degraded text in open-ended generation. Human evaluation or automated quality metrics (MAUVE, BERTScore for summarization, etc.) would strengthen the claim that the compressed models are practical drop-in replacements.

**The method's reliance on FP64 PCA eigendecomposition limits applicability at extreme scale.** For LLAMA-2 70B (D = 8192), the covariance matrix Cℓ is 8192×8192, and eigendecomposition takes ~3.5 hours on an H100. For a hypothetical model with D = 16384 or higher, the eigendecomposition cost grows as O(D³) and the memory required to store Cℓ grows as O(D²). The paper does not discuss scalability limits — at what embedding dimension does the PCA computation become the bottleneck rather than the inference savings? For the models tested, the compute cost is acceptable, but this may not generalize to next-generation architectures with wider embeddings.

**There is no comparison against simple baselines like "keep the top D_small rows/columns by weight norm."** The paper mentions that a naive norm-based pruning baseline produced perplexity "soaring into the 1000s" but does not include these numbers in any table or specify the pruning percentage attempted. A quantitative comparison (even if the baseline is terrible) would give readers a concrete sense of how much the PCA rotation contributes. Similarly, a baseline that uses PCA but without the orthogonal transformation (just projecting, deleting, and reconstructing without the invariance guarantee) would isolate whether the gains come from the basis choice or from the exact-equivalence property.

**The abstract's "removing up to 25% of the model parameters (including embeddings)" claim requires clarification.** The 25% figure refers to the fraction of embedding dimensions deleted, which reduces the size of every weight matrix by a corresponding fraction. But because the LM head and embedding matrices scale as V×D (where V is vocabulary size, typically 32k–50k+), and the attention/FFN matrices scale as D×D, the total parameter reduction from deleting D dimensions is not exactly 25% of *all* parameters — it depends on the ratio of V to D and the number of layers. For very large vocabulary sizes, the embedding and head matrices can account for a substantial fraction of total parameters, and reducing D reduces them proportionally. The paper includes embeddings in the parameter count but does not provide a detailed breakdown of parameter counts before and after slicing per component.

These caveats do not undermine the paper's central contribution — a mathematically principled, practically deployable structured pruning method that achieves competitive fidelity and measurable speedups. But they bound the generality of the claims: SliceGPT works well on the largest tested models for perplexity and commonsense reasoning tasks with careful calibration and optional RFT, and produces real speedups on current hardware. Whether it works equally well on smaller models, different architectures, different tasks, different languages, or at extreme embedding dimensions remains to be demonstrated.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted For in the Headline Efficiency Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's difficulty estimation procedure — which determines how much orthogonal transformation and slicing to apply per layer — requires running the full dense model (or the RMSNorm-converted equivalent) on a calibration dataset of 1024 sequences × 2048 tokens, collecting signal matrices Xℓ at every RMSNorm output, forming and accumulating D×D covariance matrices Cℓ = Σ Xℓᵢᵀ Xℓᵢ for every layer, and computing a full eigendecomposition (O(D³) per layer) in double precision. The paper acknowledges this cost in Section 4: "The computation of Q is performed on a single H100 GPU with 80GB of memory, taking approximately 3.5 hours to complete for the LLAMA-2 70B model." However, the paper's headline speedup claims (Table 2: LLAMA-2 70B inference reduced to 64% of dense compute on consumer GPUs) are measured *after* this one-time cost is sunk, and the 3.5-hour investment is not amortized in any of the throughput or latency benchmarks.

**The consequence.** For a practitioner deciding whether to deploy SliceGPT, the total cost of compression is not just the per-token inference savings but the upfront compute investment. A 70B model that serves 1 million tokens per day at 1075 GPUms/token (sliced) vs. 1764 GPUms/token (dense) saves ~689 GPU-seconds per day — meaning the 3.5-hour (12,600 GPU-second) slicing cost is recovered after roughly 18 days of continuous generation. This is a reasonable payback period for a production deployment, but for one-off evaluations, batch jobs, or models that are updated frequently, the upfront cost may dominate. Moreover, if multiple compression ratios need to be evaluated (10%, 20%, 25%, 30%), the PCA computation must be repeated for each target dimensionality unless a single eigendecomposition can be reused — the paper implies Qℓ is computed once and then D selects how many columns to retain, so multiple slicing levels from one PCA run is possible, but this is not stated explicitly.

For models larger than 70B or with wider embedding dimensions (D > 8192), the O(D³) eigendecomposition cost and O(D²) memory for Cℓ scale poorly. A hypothetical model with D = 16384 would require ~8× the eigendecomposition compute and ~4× the memory for covariance matrices, potentially making the one-time cost prohibitive on a single GPU even before considering inference savings.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 provides the slicing wall-clock times: LLAMA-2 7B (44 min), 13B (1h08m), 70B (3h31m), all on a single H100 80GB. Appendix A.3 (Figure 7) ablates calibration set size and shows that smaller sets (16–64 sequences) produce worse perplexity, meaning the cost cannot be arbitrarily reduced without fidelity degradation. The paper does not provide a break-even analysis or discuss the tradeoff between calibration compute and inference savings. There is no measurement of the memory required for storing the D×D covariance matrices during PCA computation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address amortization or scaling of the calibration cost. Section 5 briefly mentions "opportunities remain to build on our method" and suggests "other methods of computing Q could improve the results," which hints at but does not propose cheaper alternatives to PCA eigendecomposition (e.g., randomized SVD, power iteration, or learned rotations). The calibration cost is treated as a one-time acceptable overhead without quantifying how it scales to larger models or how frequently it would need to be repeated in a deployment where the model is periodically fine-tuned or updated.

---

### RFT Is Essential for Competitive Zero-Shot Accuracy on LLAMA-2 and Phi-2, But Does Not Transfer to OPT

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's strongest zero-shot accuracy claims — "99%, 99% and 90% zero-shot task performance of the dense model respectively" for OPT 66B, LLAMA-2 70B, and Phi-2 — rely on recovery fine-tuning (RFT) for two of the three model families. Specifically, the LLAMA-2 70B figure requires Alpaca-calibrated RFT (Table 10: 75.62% vs. 76.57% dense, 98.8% retained), and the Phi-2 figure requires the same (Table 10: 65.24% vs. 72.24% dense, 90.3% retained). Without RFT, LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing with Alpaca calibration retains 96.1% of dense accuracy (Table 8: 73.59%), and with WikiText-2 calibration it drops to 91.1% (Table 7: 69.75%). The paper is transparent about this — the individual results tables disaggregate the RFT and no-RFT conditions — but the abstract's "can remove... while maintaining" language compresses the conditional into an unconditional claim.

The more serious issue is that RFT fails entirely on OPT models. The paper states: "Despite an extensive search, we were not able to find RFT parameters that enabled improved performance in the OPT models" (Figure 6 caption). This means the RFT recovery strategy — which is the difference between retaining 91% vs. 99% of dense accuracy for LLAMA-2 70B — is not a universal post-slicing fix. It works for some model families and fails for others, with no diagnostic explanation provided.

**The consequence.** A practitioner applying SliceGPT to an OPT-based model (or any model family other than LLAMA-2 or Phi-2) cannot rely on RFT to recover accuracy lost during slicing. The one-shot (no RFT) accuracy numbers should be considered the guaranteed lower bound for arbitrary model families; the RFT-boosted numbers apply only where RFT converges, and the paper provides no criterion for predicting whether RFT will work on a given model. Since LLAMA-2 and OPT differ in activation functions (SiLU vs. ReLU), training data, and architecture details, a practitioner cannot assume RFT will transfer to their model without testing.

Furthermore, the RFT success depends on the calibration and fine-tuning data distribution. Alpaca RFT dramatically outperforms WikiText-2 RFT (Figure 6: LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing achieves 75.62% with Alpaca RFT vs. 71.28% with WikiText-2 RFT), meaning the recovery is contingent on having fine-tuning data that matches the evaluation distribution. For a deployment serving a specialized domain (medical text, code, multilingual), the practitioner would need to source in-domain RFT data — and the paper provides no guidance on how much data is needed or how sensitive the recovery is to domain mismatch.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The RFT failure on OPT is stated in the Figure 6 caption but is not analyzed or ablated. No RFT hyperparameter sweep results are reported. The paper does not investigate whether the failure is caused by activation function differences (ReLU vs. SiLU making the loss landscape less amenable to LoRA fine-tuning after slicing), by the pre-training data distribution, or by OPT models having less recoverable information in the remaining weights. Table 9 (WikiText-2 RFT) and Table 10 (Alpaca RFT) provide full per-task breakdowns, and Tables 7-8 provide the no-RFT baselines, but the OPT RFT failure is only mentioned qualitatively.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges the failure without explanation and does not propose alternative recovery strategies for OPT models or criteria for predicting RFT transferability. Section 5 mentions future work on "complementary methods including quantization" but does not specifically address the RFT generalisation problem.

### The Method Has Not Been Demonstrated on Any Task Beyond Language Modeling Perplexity and Commonsense Reasoning Benchmarks

**The assumption or constraint.** Every evaluation in the paper falls into one of two categories: (1) WikiText-2 perplexity, which measures the model's language modeling loss on held-out text; (2) zero-shot accuracy on five commonsense reasoning tasks (PIQA, WinoGrande, HellaSwag, ARC-e, ARC-c), all from the LM Evaluation Harness with default parameters. These tasks test a specific slice of LLM capability — primarily surface-level language understanding, physical commonsense, and basic science knowledge. The paper does not evaluate on mathematical reasoning (GSM8K, MATH), code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), long-form text generation quality, instruction-following (AlpacaEval, MT-Bench), factual knowledge retrieval (MMLU, TriviaQA), or any task requiring multi-step logical deduction.

This is a notable omission because the paper's central mechanism — PCA-based dimensionality reduction of the signal between transformer blocks — implicitly assumes that the principal components of the signal distribution (as estimated on a language modeling calibration corpus) capture the dimensions relevant to *all* downstream capabilities of the model. If certain capabilities (e.g., precise numerical reasoning, code syntax, factual recall of rare entities) are encoded in low-variance signal dimensions that PCA discards, then slicing could disproportionately harm those capabilities even when perplexity and commonsense accuracy remain acceptable.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot assume that a SliceGPT-compressed model with 99% retention of commonsense benchmark accuracy will retain 99% of its mathematical reasoning capability, code generation accuracy, or factual knowledge. The signal dimensions that carry syntactic fluency and commonsense priors (which dominate WikiText-2 perplexity and the five benchmarks used) may be well-captured by the top principal components, while dimensions encoding precise numerical relationships or rare factual associations may live in lower-variance components that are aggressively sliced. The choice of evaluation tasks creates a potentially optimistic bias: the benchmarks tested are exactly the kind of "average-case" language understanding tasks that are most robust to compression, while the failure modes (precise reasoning, rare knowledge) are left unexplored.

There is a second-order concern: the calibration data used for PCA (WikiText-2 or Alpaca) and the evaluation data are drawn from similar distributions. If a deployment involves a different domain (e.g., legal documents, scientific papers, non-English text), the signal statistics at each layer may differ, and the PCA basis computed on WikiText-2 may not align with the principal components of the deployment distribution. The paper provides no evaluation on out-of-distribution text or code.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None — this is an absence of evidence. The paper does not include mathematical reasoning benchmarks, code generation benchmarks, knowledge retrieval benchmarks, or any evaluation of open-ended generation quality beyond perplexity. The discussion in Appendix A.4 about eigenvalue spectra (Figure 8) provides some indirect evidence: the spectrum decays faster in early layers, suggesting that early-layer signals are more compressible, but the *functional* consequences of deleting low-variance dimensions are not traced to specific capabilities. The per-task results in Tables 7–10 show that accuracy degradation is not uniform across tasks (e.g., for LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing with WikiText-2 calibration and no RFT, HellaSwag drops from 83.84 to 68.84, a much steeper decline than PIQA from 82.70 to 74.92), but this variation is not analyzed in terms of task characteristics.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not claim generalisation to other task types, nor does it acknowledge the narrow task scope as a limitation. Section 5 mentions "opportunities remain" for future work but does not specifically call out broader task evaluation as one of them.

### The Per-Block Rotation Introduces Residual Path Overhead That Partially Offsets the Dimensionality Reduction Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** The transition from a single global Q (Theorem 1) to per-block Qℓ (Section 3.3) is motivated by the observation that "the signals at different blocks of the network were not aligned." This empirical finding necessitates inserting linear transformations into the residual connections: between block ℓ and block ℓ+1, the residual signal (which is in the Qℓ basis) must be multiplied by Qℓᵀ Qℓ₊₁ to align with the block output (which is in the Qℓ₊₁ basis). The paper acknowledges this in Section 3.3: "Unlike the modifications to the weight matrices, these additional operations cannot be pre-computed and add a small (D × D) overhead to the model."

After slicing to D_small dimensions, these residual path matrices become D_small × D_small. For a 25% sliced LLAMA-2 70B with D = 8192, D_small ≈ 6144, so each residual path matrix multiply involves a 6144×6144 transformation applied to a sequence-length-dependent number of token vectors. With L transformer layers, there are L such residual path matrices, each applied at every forward pass. While the paper states the overhead is "small," the per-operation timing in Tables 12–13 does not isolate these residual matmuls from the attention and FFN matmuls — they are included in the layer totals implicitly, but their specific contribution to the remaining runtime is not broken out.

**The consequence.** The speedup from dimensionality reduction is partially consumed by the new operations that dimensionality reduction necessitates. In the original dense network, the residual connection is a simple element-wise addition — zero FLOPs beyond the addition itself. In the sliced network, it becomes a matrix-vector multiplication (or matrix-matrix, depending on batching and sequence length) for every residual path. For short sequence lengths or small batch sizes (common in interactive inference), these residual matmuls may constitute a non-trivial fraction of the per-layer compute, reducing the effective speedup below what a naive parameter-count or FLOP-count comparison would predict. The paper's throughput benchmarks (Table 11) show a 1.55× improvement for LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing, which already reflects this overhead, but the breakdown of how much of the remaining 45% of compute is spent on residual path matmuls versus the attention/FFN matmuls is not reported.

A related concern: if different layers are sliced by different amounts (variable slicing, Appendix A.4, Table 6), the residual path matrices Qℓᵀ Qℓ₊₁ become rectangular rather than square if adjacent layers have different D_small values. The paper does not discuss this case — it is unclear whether the method supports heterogeneous slicing across layers without additional complexity in the residual connections.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper acknowledges the overhead qualitatively but provides no ablation or measurement of its contribution. Tables 12–13 break down per-operation timings for the attention (K, V, Q, Out projections) and FFN (Up/Gate, Down projections) but do not include a separate row for "residual path matmul." The throughput benchmarks (Table 11) and inference latency benchmarks (Table 2) measure end-to-end performance of the sliced model, so the overhead is accounted for in the headline speedups, but a practitioner cannot determine how much faster the model would be if per-block rotations could be eliminated (e.g., if future work found a way to align signals across layers without explicit residual transformations).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not propose alternatives to the residual path linear layers (e.g., learning a single global Q that approximately aligns signals, or using interpolation between Qℓ and Qℓ₊₁ to reduce the rank of the transformation). Section 5 mentions "other methods of computing Q could improve the results," which could encompass solving this problem, but no specific direction is suggested. The mitigation is implicit: the residual overhead shrinks with D², so at aggressive slicing rates (30–50%) the overhead becomes proportionally smaller relative to the attention/FFN compute saved.

### The RMSNorm Conversion Constrains Applicability to a Specific Architectural Pattern and Has Not Been Validated on Non-Standard Transformer Variants

**The assumption or constraint.** The computational invariance (Theorem 1) and the LayerNorm-to-RMSNorm conversion (Section 3.2) rely on a specific transformer architecture pattern: alternating attention and FFN blocks, each bookended by linear projections (Win, Wout), with RMSNorm or LayerNorm between blocks, and residual connections that add the block input to the block output. The paper states that "all of the transformer networks to which we apply SliceGPT in this paper have a decoder-only structure" (Section 2.1) and evaluates on OPT, LLAMA-2, and Phi-2 — all of which follow this pattern.

However, the broader transformer landscape includes architectures that deviate from this template in ways that would break or complicate the conversion and invariance: encoder-decoder models (T5, BART) where cross-attention introduces additional signal paths; models with parallel attention and FFN rather than sequential (PaLM); models using pre-norm vs. post-norm differently; models with tied embeddings and LM head (where the transformation Q₁ applied to Wembd must match Q_{L+1} applied to Whead, since they are the same matrix); models with mixture-of-experts layers where multiple FFNs route tokens differently; and models with non-standard normalization schemes (DeepNorm, ScaleNorm). The paper does not discuss which of these variants are compatible with SliceGPT's transformations.

**The consequence.** A practitioner using an architecture that differs from the standard decoder-only transformer cannot assume SliceGPT applies without modification. Even within decoder-only models, the LayerNorm-to-RMSNorm conversion assumes that the scale parameters diag(α) and the mean-subtraction matrix M can be cleanly absorbed into the input and output weight matrices of adjacent blocks. If the architecture uses tied weights between the embedding and LM head (common in many models to reduce parameters), the conversion step must ensure consistency: the embedding rotation Q₁ and the head un-rotation Q_{L+1}ᵀ must be compatible with the weight-tying constraint, which essentially forces Q_{L+1} = Q₁. The paper does not mention weight tying or how it is handled, despite many LLMs (including some OPT variants) using tied embeddings.

More subtly, the conversion absorbs learned LayerNorm parameters into weight matrices, which permanently alters those weight matrices. If a practitioner wants to apply SliceGPT and then later fine-tune the model on new data, the absorbed LayerNorm parameters are no longer separately learnable — they are baked into the weights. This may affect the model's ability to adapt during fine-tuning, since the original LayerNorm parameterization was part of what the optimizer learned during pre-training.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not ablate architectural compatibility. All tested models are decoder-only, non-MoE, with sequential attention-then-FFN blocks. The conversion procedure (Section 3.2, Figure 3) is described abstractly without addressing weight tying, pre-norm vs. post-norm variations, or the interaction with dropout (which the paper assumes "is not applied post-training" in Section 2.1 — reasonable for inference, but relevant if RFT is applied). The generalisation of Theorem 1 to encoder-decoder or other patterns is not discussed.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim generality beyond decoder-only architectures and does not propose extensions. The limitation is partially mitigated by the fact that the dominant open-source LLM families (OPT, LLAMA, GPT-NeoX, MPT, Falcon) all follow the decoder-only pattern, so the practical coverage is already large. But the absence of any discussion about architectural boundary conditions means a practitioner with a non-standard model must deduce compatibility themselves from the mathematical derivation.

### Hard Problems (Difficult Benchmarks, Adversarial Inputs, and Worst-Case Degradation) Are Not Analyzed

**The assumption or constraint.** All SliceGPT evaluations use standard benchmark test sets (WikiText-2, PIQA, WinoGrande, HellaSwag, ARC) and report aggregate metrics: average perplexity across the test set, mean accuracy across five tasks. There is no analysis of performance on difficult subsets — the hardest questions in each benchmark, adversarial inputs designed to stress compressed models, edge cases where the PCA basis might fail to capture rare but important signal directions, or worst-case per-example degradation.

Because SliceGPT deletes dimensions based on *average* signal variance (Equation 8: Cℓ = Σ Xℓᵢᵀ Xℓᵢ, summed over all calibration data), it is fundamentally an average-case optimization. The principal components are those that explain the most variance across the *entire* calibration distribution. Dimensions that carry little variance on average but are crucial for specific rare inputs — e.g., a dimension encoding a particular syntactic construction, a numerical relationship, or a factual association that appears in only a few calibration sequences — will be discarded if they fall below the eigenvalue threshold for the chosen slicing percentage.

**The consequence.** The aggregate metrics reported in the paper may mask catastrophic degradation on specific input types. For instance, if a rare but important reasoning pattern is encoded in a low-variance dimension, the sliced model might perform at chance level on that pattern while maintaining high average accuracy on the more common patterns that dominate the benchmark. The paper provides no analysis of per-quartile or per-difficulty accuracy, no minimum accuracy guarantees, and no adversarial evaluation. A practitioner deploying a sliced model in a high-stakes setting (medical QA, code generation where correctness matters per-output) cannot bound the worst-case degradation from the reported aggregate numbers.

This limitation is particularly salient because the eigenvalue spectrum analysis (Appendix A.4, Figure 8) shows that for LLAMA-2 models, "there are no dominant principal components with significantly more information, making the pruning of these components a more challenging task." If the spectrum is flat, then even low-eigenvalue dimensions carry non-trivial information for *some* inputs, and deleting them will cause degradation somewhere — the question is only whether that degradation falls on inputs the practitioner cares about. The paper's aggregate metrics cannot answer this question.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides only aggregate perplexity and mean accuracy numbers. There is no per-difficulty breakdown (beyond the implicit breakdown by model size — larger models degrade less, but this is about model scale, not input difficulty). The per-task accuracies in Tables 7–10 show variation across tasks (e.g., ARC-c degrades more than PIQA for most sliced models), but this is not analyzed in terms of task difficulty or input characteristics. The calibration data ablation (Appendix A.3) shows that larger calibration sets improve aggregate perplexity, which indirectly suggests that more data covers more of the signal distribution, but does not address the worst-case question.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss worst-case degradation, adversarial robustness, or per-difficulty evaluation. This is a common omission in the compression literature (SparseGPT and GPTQ similarly report aggregate metrics), but it is consequential for deployment decisions where tail performance matters.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

SliceGPT introduces a genuinely new way of thinking about transformer compression — one that will likely influence how future methods are designed even if the specific PCA-based slicing procedure is superseded. The magnitude of this shift is best characterized as a **reframing with practical consequences**, not a paradigm shift in the Kuhnian sense. The underlying mathematics (orthogonal invariance, PCA) are well-established; what is new is the recognition that the transformer architecture contains a previously unnoticed structural degree of freedom — the ability to rotate internal representations without changing predictions — and that exploiting this degree of freedom converts an intractable compression problem (naive row/column deletion destroys the model) into a well-posed one (deletion in the principal-component basis is L2-optimal). This reframing changes what the field should optimize: not "which weights can we zero?" but "in which basis does the signal become maximally compressible?"

The practical consequence is that **dense, structured compression becomes a viable alternative to sparse, unstructured pruning for large language models.** Prior to this work, the prevailing narrative — reinforced by SparseGPT's success and the failure of naive structured pruning — was that LLM weight matrices are inherently incompressible through row/column deletion; only fine-grained (unstructured or semi-structured) sparsity could retain acceptable fidelity. SliceGPT falsifies this narrative by demonstrating that structured pruning with matching or better fidelity is possible *when the deletion axes are aligned with the signal's principal components*. The key evidence is Table 1: SliceGPT at 25% slicing achieves better WikiText-2 perplexity than SparseGPT 2:4 across all tested models at 7B+ scale, while producing dense output matrices that run on standard GPU kernels without specialized sparse-hardware support. This result shifts the burden of proof: a new compression method must now demonstrate not just parameter reduction or theoretical FLOP reduction, but realized throughput on current hardware. The paper's explicit emphasis on "end-to-end performance gains are not feasible with our baseline SparseGPT 2:4 at the time of writing" establishes a new evaluation criterion that should become standard.

The work also **reconciles a latent contradiction** in the compression literature. Unstructured pruning methods (SparseGPT, Wanda) achieved impressive parameter reduction but struggled to deliver speedups. Structured pruning methods (channel pruning from the convnet literature, LLM-pruner) delivered speedups but required extensive retraining or failed entirely on LLMs. The contradiction was that the methods that preserved accuracy did not deliver speed, and the methods that delivered speed did not preserve accuracy. SliceGPT resolves this by showing that the missing ingredient was the *alignment* between the deletion axes and the signal structure. The rotation step is what bridges the gap: it makes structured pruning accuracy-competitive while retaining the hardware advantage of dense matrix multiplication.

Looking forward, this work **redirects research attention** in two ways. First, it makes **representational alignment** — the choice of basis in which to compress — a first-class design consideration. Future compression methods will likely be judged not just on their weight selection criterion but on whether they first transform the network into a more compressible representation. Second, it makes **verifier/hardware co-design** less urgent: rather than waiting for sparse tensor core kernels to mature and integrate into production frameworks, the field can pursue structured compression methods that produce dense, smaller matrices deployable immediately. This does not mean research on sparse kernels should stop, but it does mean that structured methods now have a credible path to matching unstructured methods in fidelity, removing the "but it doesn't work yet" objection that previously kept structured pruning on the sidelines for LLMs.

The **scope of the shift is bounded**, however. SliceGPT has been demonstrated on a specific architecture class (decoder-only transformers with RMSNorm/LayerNorm between blocks), on specific model families (OPT, LLAMA-2, Phi-2), on specific tasks (perplexity, commonsense reasoning), and at specific scales (up to 70B parameters). Whether the computational invariance extends to encoder-decoder models, mixture-of-experts layers, or cross-attention mechanisms is unknown. Whether the PCA-based basis choice is optimal or merely good-enough is an open question. And the requirement for double-precision eigendecomposition of D×D matrices means the method's one-time cost scales poorly to embedding dimensions beyond ~10K. These boundaries mean the work is not a universal compression solution, but rather a proof of concept for a new class of rotation-then-delete methods.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Learned rotations instead of PCA.** The paper uses PCA as a principled but off-the-shelf rotation method; the eigenvectors of the signal covariance matrix are the L2-optimal linear basis for reconstruction. But the L2 metric on signal vectors is not the same as minimizing the degradation in the model's loss or task performance. A natural follow-up would train a small network to predict the optimal rotation matrix Qℓ for each layer, possibly by backpropagating through the slicing operation to directly optimize downstream task accuracy after compression. This is newly tractable because SliceGPT establishes that *any* orthogonal Q is admissible (Theorem 1), so the search space is the Stiefel manifold of orthonormal matrices, which can be optimized over using Riemannian gradient methods. A strong experiment would compare PCA rotations against learned rotations at the 25% slicing level for LLAMA-2 7B on a diverse benchmark suite (MMLU, GSM8K, HumanEval in addition to the commonsense tasks in this paper), measuring whether learned rotations can recover the 4–5% accuracy gap that PCA leaves relative to dense performance without RFT.

**Cheaper eigendecomposition approximations and scaling to larger models.** The paper's 3.5-hour PCA computation on LLAMA-2 70B (D=8192) uses full double-precision eigendecomposition on a single H100. This O(D³) cost becomes prohibitive at D=16384 or higher, and the O(D²) memory for storing covariance matrices per layer similarly scales poorly. A direct follow-up would benchmark randomized SVD (Halko et al., 2011) against exact eigendecomposition for SliceGPT, measuring both the wall-clock time reduction and the fidelity degradation at various approximation ranks. The hypothesis is that the top principal components — which determine which dimensions are retained — can be estimated accurately from a low-rank approximation of Cℓ computed by sampling the signal matrix, potentially reducing the PCA cost from O(D³) to O(D² log k) where k is the number of retained dimensions. A strong result would show that randomized PCA with k=3/4 D (the 25% slicing scenario) produces perplexity within 0.1 PPL of exact PCA while reducing computation time by an order of magnitude, making SliceGPT practical for next-generation models with D > 16K.

**Task-specific calibration data and the sensitivity of slicing to domain shift.** The paper shows that calibration dataset choice (WikiText-2 vs. Alpaca) affects downstream accuracy, sometimes substantially (e.g., Phi-2 at 25% slicing: 54.46% with WikiText-2 calibration vs. 62.52% with Alpaca calibration, Table 7 vs. Table 8). A systematic follow-up would measure the degradation when calibration and evaluation distributions diverge: calibrate on WikiText-2 and evaluate on code (HumanEval, MBPP), or calibrate on English and evaluate on multilingual benchmarks. The critical question is whether the signal principal components transfer across domains, or whether slicing in a language-modeling basis disproportionately harms capabilities not represented in the calibration data. A strong experiment would calibrate SliceGPT on four domains (Wikipedia, GitHub code, scientific papers, conversational text), slice each resulting model at 25%, and evaluate all four on all four domain-specific benchmarks, producing a 4×4 transfer matrix. If the diagonal (in-domain calibration) significantly outperforms the off-diagonal, it establishes that SliceGPT users must source in-domain calibration data for specialized deployments — a significant practical constraint.

**Architectural generalization: encoder-decoder, mixture-of-experts, and weight-tied models.** The paper's theorem and experiments cover decoder-only architectures with sequential attention-then-FFN blocks and separate embedding/head matrices. Extending the invariance to encoder-decoder models (T5, BART) requires handling cross-attention, where the decoder attends to encoder outputs that may be in a different rotational basis. Extending to mixture-of-experts (MoE) layers requires handling the routing mechanism, which sends different tokens to different FFN experts — if each expert has its own input/output projections, they may need independent rotations, complicating the residual path alignment. Weight-tied embeddings force Q₁ = Q_{L+1}, eliminating one degree of freedom. A strong follow-up would implement SliceGPT on at least one encoder-decoder model (e.g., T5-3B) and one MoE model (e.g., Mixtral 8×7B), documenting the necessary modifications to the invariance argument and measuring whether the fidelity-speedup tradeoffs observed in decoder-only models transfer. Negative results (e.g., MoE models degrade substantially more than dense models at the same slicing percentage) would be equally informative, bounding the method's applicability.

**Combining SliceGPT with quantization and other compression methods.** The paper's conclusion explicitly suggests this: "complementary methods including quantization, and structural pruning could be used." Because SliceGPT produces smaller dense matrices, they should be directly quantizable using post-training quantization methods (GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant). A strong experiment would apply SliceGPT at 25% slicing followed by 4-bit or 8-bit quantization to LLAMA-2 7B and 13B, measuring both perplexity and inference throughput on target hardware. The hypothesis is that slicing + quantization is synergistic: slicing reduces the embedding dimension, making the matrices smaller (fewer parameters to quantize, potentially reducing quantization error accumulation), while quantization reduces the bits per parameter of the already-smaller matrices, compounding the memory and bandwidth savings. If the combined method achieves, say, 40% total inference cost reduction with perplexity within 5% of the dense FP16 baseline, it would establish a practical compression pipeline that outperforms either method alone.

**Adversarial evaluation and worst-case degradation analysis.** The paper reports only aggregate metrics (mean perplexity, mean accuracy), which may mask catastrophic degradation on specific input types. A stress-test follow-up would identify the test examples where the sliced model's loss increases most relative to the dense model, characterize them linguistically or structurally (e.g., long-range dependencies, numerical reasoning, rare entity references), and measure whether RFT specifically addresses these failure modes or merely improves average performance. A strong experiment would compute per-example perplexity deltas between the dense and 25%-sliced LLAMA-2 7B model on the full WikiText-2 test set, sort by degradation, and manually analyze the top 100 most-degraded examples to identify patterns. If specific linguistic phenomena (e.g., nested relative clauses, coreference resolution at distance >N tokens) are systematically overrepresented among degraded examples, it would both clarify *why* slicing fails when it fails and suggest targeted mitigation strategies (e.g., calibration data augmentation with those phenomena).

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-device and edge deployment of large language models.** The most immediate application is reducing the GPU count and memory footprint required to serve large models, making deployment feasible on hardware-constrained environments. For LLAMA-2 70B on consumer-grade Quadro RTX6000 GPUs (24GB each), the dense model requires 7 GPUs costing 1764 GPUms per generated token. After 25% slicing, the same model runs on 5 GPUs at 1075 GPUms/token — a 2-GPU reduction that saves approximately $3,000–5,000 in GPU hardware per deployment node (assuming RTX6000-class pricing) and reduces energy consumption proportionally. For organizations running on-premise inference at scale (customer support chatbots, document processing pipelines), this directly translates to capital expenditure reduction and lower operating costs. Crucially, this deployment improvement requires no kernel modifications or sparse-hardware support — the sliced model runs on standard PyTorch dense matmul, meaning it can be deployed immediately with existing inference serving infrastructure like vLLM or Text Generation Inference.

**Cost-efficient batch inference for data processing pipelines.** For organizations using LLMs to process large document collections (summarization, entity extraction, classification), the throughput improvements from slicing are amplified by the ability to fit larger batch sizes in GPU memory. Table 11 shows LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing doubling the feasible batch size (128 → 256) on two H100 GPUs while improving tokens-per-second by 1.55×. In a batch processing scenario where millions of documents must be processed, this 1.55× throughput increase directly reduces the number of GPU-hours required, cutting inference costs by approximately 35% for the same workload. Because SliceGPT's calibration is a one-time cost (3.5 hours on one H100 for LLAMA-2 70B, Table 3), it amortizes favorably for any deployment processing more than a few hundred million tokens — a threshold easily crossed in production data processing pipelines.

**Enabling interactive applications with large models under latency constraints.** Interactive applications (chat, code completion, real-time translation) are constrained not just by throughput but by per-token latency, which must stay below human perception thresholds (~50–100ms per token for real-time interaction). Table 2 shows that per-token latency improves modestly from slicing (125ms → 110ms for LLAMA-2 70B on A100, a 12% reduction), which may not single-handedly bring a model under a latency budget. However, the reduction in GPU count (4 → 3 on A100) means the model can be served from fewer devices, reducing the failure domain and simplifying the serving infrastructure. More importantly, the reduced memory footprint enables speculative decoding or other latency-reduction techniques that require fitting additional small models in GPU memory alongside the main model — techniques that are infeasible when the dense model already saturates available memory.

**Rapid model customization and deployment cycles.** Table 3 quantifies the end-to-end compression time for 30% slicing: LLAMA-2 70B is sliced in 3.5 hours and optionally fine-tuned in 1.5 hours, for a total of 5 hours on commercially available hardware. This means an organization can take a newly released open-weight model, compress it to fit their hardware budget, and deploy it within a single workday — without access to the original training pipeline or massive compute clusters. This is a significant practical enabler for organizations that lack the resources to train or extensively fine-tune LLMs but need to stay current with model releases. Combined with the RFT results showing that Alpaca-calibrated RFT recovers 99% of dense accuracy for LLAMA-2 70B at 25% slicing (Table 10), the workflow of "download → calibrate → slice → RFT → deploy" becomes a practical recipe for production teams.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions SliceGPT explicitly against SparseGPT 2:4 as the primary alternative for achieving real-world speedups from compression, and implicitly against unstructured sparsity methods that achieve higher theoretical compression ratios but cannot deliver speedups on current hardware. The decision framework that emerges from the paper's results is:

- **Prefer SliceGPT over SparseGPT 2:4 when:**
  - The target model is 7B parameters or larger (the speedup parity or advantage for SliceGPT at 25% slicing emerges at this scale; Table 12 shows SliceGPT 25% matches SparseGPT 2:4 speedup for LLAMA-2 70B but trails at 7B and 13B).
  - Deployment hardware does not support sparse tensor core operations, or the software stack (CUDA version, PyTorch integration) does not include mature 2:4 sparse kernels (the paper states end-to-end SparseGPT 2:4 speedups were "not feasible at the time of writing").
  - The deployment benefits from larger batch sizes fitting in GPU memory (the throughput gains in Table 11 are driven substantially by batch-size doubling).
  - Recovery fine-tuning with in-domain data is feasible (RFT with Alpaca recovers accuracy to within ~1% of dense for LLAMA-2 70B; without RFT, SliceGPT retains 91–96% of dense accuracy depending on calibration data).

- **Prefer SparseGPT 2:4 (or unstructured sparsity) over SliceGPT when:**
  - The target model is small (roughly <7B parameters), where SliceGPT's 25% slicing trails SparseGPT 2:4 in both speedup and perplexity (LLAMA-2 7B: SparseGPT 2:4 achieves 1.48× speedup with 8.69 PPL vs. SliceGPT 25% at 1.33× with 7.24 PPL — the speedup-PPL tradeoff favors SparseGPT in absolute speedup terms).
  - The deployment hardware has mature sparse kernel support and the software stack can reliably execute 2:4 sparse matmuls at speedup factors approaching the theoretical 2×.
  - Compression ratio is the primary objective and throughput is secondary (unstructured sparsity at higher rates achieves greater parameter reduction, even if realized speedups lag).
  - The model architecture does not follow the standard decoder-only pattern with RMSNorm/LayerNorm between blocks (SliceGPT has not been validated on encoder-decoder, MoE, or weight-tied architectures).
  - Perplexity rather than zero-shot task accuracy is the primary quality metric and the model is small (Table 1: for OPT 125M, SliceGPT 25% achieves 37.74 PPL vs. SparseGPT 2:4 at 45.07 — SliceGPT is better even at the smallest scale for perplexity, so this condition is nuanced).

- **Prefer dense deployment without compression when:**
  - The model is Phi-2 scale (~2.8B parameters) and the deployment cannot perform RFT with in-domain data (Table 7: Phi-2 drops from 72.24% to 54.46% mean accuracy at 25% slicing with WikiText-2 calibration and no RFT — losing 25% of performance is likely unacceptable).
  - The deployment involves tasks not evaluated in the paper (mathematical reasoning, code generation, long-form generation) where the fidelity of sliced models is unknown — absent evidence that slicing preserves these capabilities, the dense model is the safer choice.
  - The inference volume is low enough that the one-time calibration cost (3.5 hours for 70B) dominates the lifetime inference savings, or the model is updated frequently enough that re-calibration costs accumulate.
  - The model uses OPT architecture and RFT is not viable (the paper could not find RFT parameters that improved OPT models), meaning sliced OPT models operate at the no-RFT accuracy level which may be below deployment requirements.
