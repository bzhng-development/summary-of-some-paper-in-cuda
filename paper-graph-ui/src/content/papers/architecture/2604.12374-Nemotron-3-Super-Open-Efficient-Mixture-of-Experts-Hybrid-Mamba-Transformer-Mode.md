# Nemotron 3 Super: Open, Efficient Mixture-of-Experts Hybrid Mamba-Transformer Model for Agentic Reasoning

**ArXiv:** [2604.12374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.12374)

## 🎯 Pitch

By packing 120B parameters into just 12B active through a novel LatentMoE architecture, Nemotron 3 Super matches frontier dense models on agentic coding and math benchmarks—while running at up to 7.5× the throughput of Qwen3.5-122B. It also bakes in native speculative decoding that boosts generation speed another 3×, making real-time tool-using agents drastically cheaper to serve.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces Nemotron 3 Super, a 120B-total-parameter (12B active) hybrid Mamba-Attention Mixture-of-Experts model pre-trained on 25 trillion tokens in NVFP4 precision with **LatentMoE** — a novel MoE architecture that projects tokens into a lower-dimensional latent space for routing and expert computation (reducing per-expert weight loads and all-to-all traffic by a factor $d/\ell$ while increasing both total expert count and top-$K$ active experts proportionally) — and **Multi-Token Prediction (MTP)** layers that enable native speculative decoding through shared-weight autoregressive drafting (achieving an average acceptance length of 3.45 tokens on SPEED-Bench versus 2.70 for DeepSeek-R1). Post-trained with supervised fine-tuning and multi-environment reinforcement learning across 21 diverse environments emphasizing agentic capabilities, the model achieves up to 2.2× and 7.5× higher inference throughput than GPT-OSS-120B and Qwen3.5-122B respectively while maintaining comparable accuracy across benchmarks including SWE-Bench, Terminal Bench, and TauBench v2, establishing that the hybrid Mamba-Attention MoE architecture with LatentMoE scaling can match or exceed dense-transformer performance on agentic reasoning tasks when the active parameter budget is constrained.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Scaling Model Capacity Without Scaling Inference Cost

The fundamental challenge this paper tackles is one of the central tensions in modern LLM deployment: **how do you increase a model's knowledge capacity and reasoning ability without proportionally increasing the computational cost of running it?** This tension has become acute as models have grown from tens of billions to hundreds of billions of parameters. A 120B-parameter dense transformer is extraordinarily expensive to serve — every forward pass activates all 120B parameters, reading every weight from memory and performing the full set of computations. The result is low throughput, high latency, and inference costs that can dominate the total cost of ownership for production deployments.

The paper focuses specifically on the regime where inference efficiency matters most: **long-context, agentic workloads**. When a model needs to process a 128K-token codebase, reason across it, issue tool calls, and generate responses across multiple turns, the quadratic cost of self-attention and the linear cost of reading dense weights combine to create a severe throughput bottleneck. As Figure 1 shows, even a 120B dense model like GPT-OSS-120B achieves only a fraction of the throughput of the hybrid Mamba-Attention approach. For developers building AI coding assistants, autonomous software engineering agents, or interactive terminal-use tools, this throughput gap translates directly into user-facing latency and infrastructure cost — it determines whether a model is practical to deploy at scale or not.

This problem has both **practical significance** (cost, latency, hardware requirements for deployment) and **theoretical significance** (it forces us to confront the question of whether dense transformers are the optimal architecture for sequence modeling at scale, or whether sparsely-activated alternatives with linear-time sequence mixers can achieve equivalent quality with dramatically lower cost).

### Two Established Approaches, Each With Limitations

The field has developed two major strategies to address this scaling-efficiency problem, both of which are well-established by the time this paper is written but each of which carries distinct tradeoffs.

**Approach 1: Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) for Sparse Activation.** MoE architectures (Shazeer et al., 2017; Lepikhin et al., 2020; Fedus et al., 2022; DeepSeek-AI, 2025c) scale total parameters into the hundreds of billions while keeping **active parameters** (those actually computed per token) in the tens of billions. The key idea is that the feed-forward layers of the transformer are replaced with multiple "experts" — smaller sub-networks — and a learned router selects a subset of experts (top-$K$) for each input token. This means the model can store vastly more knowledge across its many experts (through the total parameter count) while only paying the computational cost of the few experts it actually uses per token.

The core limitation of standard MoE designs, as the paper argues in Section 2.1.1, is that **they are optimized for aggregate FLOPs but not for real-world deployment bottlenecks**. Specifically:

- **Memory bandwidth dominates in low-latency serving.** Each expert matrix of size $d \times m$ (hidden dimension × expert FFN intermediate dimension) must be read from memory. With hundreds of experts, the total memory footprint is $N \times d \times m$, and for each token, $K$ experts are read. Reducing the cost of inference requires decreasing $d$ (hidden dimension) or $m$ (expert intermediate dimension), but standard MoE designs make both of these large to maintain quality.

- **All-to-all communication dominates in throughput-oriented distributed serving.** When experts are distributed across GPUs, the routing volume scales as $d \times K$ — every token must be communicated to the $K$ target experts. Reducing communication overhead requires decreasing $d$ or $K$.

These two bottlenecks — memory bandwidth and communication — are **not captured by the standard FLOPs accounting** that most MoE papers use to demonstrate efficiency. A model can report lower FLOPs per token than a dense model while still being slower in practice because its memory access patterns or network communication patterns are worse. This is the gap the paper identifies: existing MoE designs claim efficiency based on compute metrics that don't reflect real hardware costs.

**Approach 2: Hybrid Mamba-Attention for Linear-Time Sequence Modeling.** The second strategy, pioneered by the Mamba family of models (Gu & Dao, 2023; Dao & Gu, 2024), addresses a different bottleneck: the quadratic growth of the KV cache in self-attention. For long sequences — and agentic workflows routinely involve 64K, 128K, or even 1M-token contexts — storing and computing attention over the full KV cache becomes prohibitively expensive. Mamba-2 replaces self-attention with a structured state-space model (SSM) that processes sequences in linear time with a constant-sized recurrent state, eliminating the KV cache entirely for those layers.

The limitation of pure Mamba models, however, is that they **sacrifice the global token-interaction capability of self-attention**. While Mamba excels at local and medium-range dependencies, certain tasks — particularly those requiring long-range information routing across disparate parts of a document — benefit from the dense, all-to-all interactions that attention provides. The paper from the Nemotron family (NVIDIA, 2025c) established that a **hybrid** approach — mostly Mamba layers with occasional attention "anchor" layers — can recover much of the global modeling capability while retaining the throughput benefits of linear-time processing.

The limitation of the prior hybrid work (Nemotron 3 Nano) was that it **did not combine the hybrid architecture with sparse MoE scaling**. The Nano model was a 30B-total, 3B-active hybrid Mamba-Attention MoE model, demonstrating the viability of the approach at relatively small scale. But the question remained open: does this architecture scale to 100B+ total parameters while maintaining the throughput advantage? Can you combine the memory/communication savings of the hybrid architecture with a new MoE design that specifically targets the real-world serving bottlenecks?

### The LatentMoE Gap in Prior MoE Design

The paper identifies a specific and previously unaddressed structural inefficiency in standard MoE architectures. In a standard MoE (Figure 3a), tokens are routed in the full hidden dimension $d$. Each expert matrix has dimensions $d \times m$, and routing communication carries tensors of size $d$. The design tradeoff is:

- To increase model capacity (knowledge stored across experts), you want to increase the number of experts $N$ and the number of active experts per token $K$. Both of these give the model more parameters to work with and more combinatorial capacity through expert co-activation patterns.
- But increasing $K$ directly increases the memory bandwidth cost (reading $K$ expert weight matrices per token) and the communication cost (routing volume proportional to $K$).
- Reducing $d$ would help both costs, but there is a hard lower bound — the **effective feature rank** $r_{\text{eff}}$ required for the task. If you squeeze the hidden dimension below this rank, the model cannot represent the necessary features and quality collapses.

The paper's insight (Section 2.1.1) is that **the routing and expert computation can be decoupled geometrically from the main hidden representation**. If you project tokens into a smaller latent space $\mathbb{R}^{\ell}$ (with $\ell < d$) *only for the purposes of routing and expert computation*, you can reduce the per-expert matrix size to $\ell \times m$ and the routing volume to $\ell \times K$. The savings factor is $d / \ell$. You can then use these savings to **increase** $N$ and $K$ by the same factor — giving you more expert capacity and combinatorial expressivity — while keeping the total memory bandwidth and communication cost at the same level as the original design. After expert computation, the latent-space outputs are projected back to the full dimension $d$.

This is **not** simply compressing the entire model — the routing gate, shared expert, and all non-expert layers (attention, Mamba, layer norms) continue to operate in the full hidden dimension $d$. The compression is targeted exclusively at the parts that dominate the serving bottlenecks: routed expert weight reads and all-to-all routing traffic. This makes LatentMoE a **hardware-aware** architectural innovation: it re-allocates capacity from dimensions that are cheap (the full hidden dimension, where computation is dense and unavoidable) to dimensions that are expensive in deployment (expert count and active expert count, which drive memory and communication).

### The Need for Combined Architecture + Low-Precision Training

Beyond the architectural question, the paper addresses a growing practical concern: can these large MoE models be trained in low precision (NVFP4) without sacrificing quality? Training in 4-bit precision reduces memory footprint and communication volume during training itself, enabling larger models or longer training within a given hardware budget. But low-precision training introduces quantization noise that can destabilize training or degrade final model quality. The paper demonstrates that NVFP4 training at 25T-token scale is stable and produces models competitive with higher-precision baselines — a finding that required detailed investigation of the root causes of precision-related training artifacts (Section 2.2), including the emergence of zero-valued weight gradients and channel magnitude patterns.

### Agentic Capabilities as the Primary Target

A major gap the paper identifies is not architectural but **task-related**: prior models, including efficient MoE and hybrid architectures, focused on benchmark performance (MMLU, MATH, HumanEval) but paid relatively little attention to **agentic capabilities** — the ability to use tools, interact with environments across multiple turns, search the web, operate terminals, and solve software engineering tasks end-to-end. Agentic tasks are particularly demanding because they combine long context (the model's own previous actions and tool outputs), multi-step reasoning, and the need to produce structured tool calls. They stress-test both sequence modeling capability (can the model track state across 64K tokens of interaction?) and throughput (can it generate responses quickly enough for interactive use?).

The paper emphasizes that they "substantially scaled the breadth of our RL environments, the volume and quality of agentic training data, and the overall amount of post-training focused on multi-step tool-using behavior" (Section 1). This is in contrast to models that were primarily optimized for static QA or single-turn reasoning benchmarks. The agentic focus is not incidental — it's the primary justification for the architectural choices. Long-context, multi-turn agentic workflows are exactly the regime where the quadratic cost of attention and the memory bandwidth cost of dense or standard MoE architectures are most painful, making the hybrid Mamba-Attention LatentMoE design most impactful.

### How Nemotron 3 Super Positions Itself

The paper positions Nemotron 3 Super at the intersection of three converging research directions:

1. **From the MoE lineage**: It builds on DeepSeekMoE (Dai et al., 2024) and DeepSeek-V3 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025c) in using sparse activation to scale total parameters while constraining active compute, but introduces LatentMoE to address the deployment bottlenecks (memory bandwidth, communication) that standard MoE designs neglect. The key departure is the recognition that FLOPs efficiency does not equal throughput efficiency on real hardware.

2. **From the hybrid architecture lineage**: It extends the Nemotron 3 Nano architecture (NVIDIA, 2025c) — hybrid Mamba-Attention MoE — to 120B total parameters, demonstrating that the approach scales and retains its throughput advantages at much larger sizes. The periodic interleaving pattern (mostly Mamba-2 layers with strategic attention anchor layers) is inherited from prior Nemotron models but scaled to 88 layers with sparse LatentMoE activation.

3. **From the efficient training lineage**: It demonstrates for the first time that a model of this scale and architectural complexity can be trained entirely in NVFP4 precision, with detailed investigation of training dynamics under low precision (Section 2.2) and a finding that promoting tensors to higher precision before annealing does not improve downstream accuracy — a counterintuitive result with practical implications for training pipeline design.

4. **From the agentic post-training lineage**: It scales RL training to 21 diverse environments simultaneously, introduces PivotRL for efficient agentic RL training, and develops new synthetic data pipelines for tasks like web search, terminal use, financial reasoning, and CUDA kernel generation. The stated goal is not just a model that scores well on benchmarks but one that can function as a practical agentic system.

The paper's contribution is therefore a **demonstration of system-level co-design**: architecture, precision, training recipe, and post-training pipeline are jointly optimized for the target deployment regime (long-context agentic workloads on Blackwell hardware). It is not claiming to invent any single component in isolation — Mamba, MoE, speculative decoding, and low-precision training all predate this work — but rather to show that these pieces can be integrated in a specific way (LatentMoE + hybrid Mamba-Attention + MTP for native speculative decoding + NVFP4 training + multi-environment RL) to produce a model that is simultaneously competitive with dense 120B-class models on accuracy while being 2.2–7.5× faster at serving.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Nemotron 3 Super is a 120B-total-parameter language model that combines a hybrid Mamba-2 and attention architecture with a novel sparse Mixture-of-Experts design (LatentMoE) and Multi-Token Prediction heads, all pre-trained in 4-bit precision (NVFP4) and post-trained with a multi-environment reinforcement learning pipeline targeting agentic capabilities. The system solves the problem of deploying large-scale models for long-context, multi-turn agentic tasks—where standard dense transformers are prohibitively expensive to serve—by simultaneously reducing the per-token active parameter count (to 12B through sparse expert activation), eliminating quadratic attention cost for most layers (through Mamba-2), accelerating autoregressive decoding (through native speculative decoding via MTP), and compressing both the model weights and the routing communication (through LatentMoE's latent-space expert computation). The "shape" of the solution is a set of three architectural innovations operating in concert: LatentMoE reduces the memory bandwidth and all-to-all communication cost of expert layers by projecting tokens into a smaller latent space for routing and expert computation, then uses the savings to increase both the total expert count and the number of active experts per token; hybrid interleaving of Mamba-2 and attention layers replaces quadratic self-attention with constant-state recurrent processing for most of the network while retaining strategic attention anchor layers for global interaction; and shared-weight MTP heads serve a dual purpose—they improve representation learning during training and act as an internal draft model for speculative decoding at inference time, reducing decoding latency without external models.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The Nemotron 3 Super system has nine major components, organized into a pretraining pipeline and a post-training pipeline:

1. **Base Architecture (88-layer stack)**: A periodic interleaving pattern where Mamba-2 blocks alternate with LatentMoE layers, punctuated by occasional self-attention layers (GQA with 32 query heads, 2 KV heads) acting as global anchors. The pattern follows a repeating structure: 4 Mamba-2 + LatentMoE combinations, then 3 combinations, then an attention-anchored block, then repeated variations. The total is 88 layers with 120.6B total parameters and 12.7B active parameters per forward pass (excluding embeddings).

2. **LatentMoE Layers**: Replace standard MoE layers. Each input token in dimension `$d = 4096$` is first down-projected via a learned matrix `$W_\downarrow \in \mathbb{R}^{\ell \times d}$` into a latent space of dimension `$\ell = 1024$`. Routing and expert computation happen entirely in this `$\ell$`-dimensional space. After experts produce their outputs, a learned up-projection `$W_\uparrow \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times \ell}$` restores the full dimensionality. The routing gate, shared expert, and all non-expert layers remain in the full 4096-dimensional space. There are 512 total experts per layer, with top-22 active experts per token. Each expert has hidden dimension 2688, and the shared expert has intermediate size 5376.

3. **Mamba-2 Blocks**: Process sequences with constant-sized recurrent state (state dimension 128, 128 heads, head dimension 64, 8 groups) instead of quadratic attention, eliminating KV-cache growth for these layers. This is the primary mechanism for long-context efficiency.

4. **Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) Heads**: Two additional prediction heads (with shared weights) that are trained to predict tokens at positions `$n+1$` and `$n+2$` given the hidden state at position `$n$`. At inference time, a single shared head is applied recursively to generate draft tokens for speculative decoding, with the main model verifying the drafts in a single forward pass.

5. **NVFP4 Training Framework**: All linear layers (unless explicitly promoted to higher precision) are trained using NVFP4 GEMM kernels with 2D block scaling for weights and 1D block scaling for gradients and activations. Random Hadamard Transforms are applied to wgrad inputs, and stochastic rounding is applied to gradient tensors. The NVFP4 format uses E2M1 elements with 16-element micro-blocks, E4M3 micro-block scaling factors, and a second-level FP32 global scale.

6. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) Pipeline**: A two-stage SFT procedure on 7M samples totaling 80B tokens. Stage 1 uses per-token normalization (global averaging of cross-entropy over all output tokens) to induce strong reasoning behavior. Stage 2 switches to per-conversation normalization (averaging per-sample losses equally) to correct the bias toward long outputs and restore long-input-short-output performance.

7. **Multi-Environment RL Pipeline (RLVR)**: An asynchronous GRPO setup training jointly across 21 environments covering math, code, STEM, safety, chat, instruction following, long context, puzzles, and agentic tasks. Rollouts are generated continuously by inference workers, stored in a buffer, and used for model updates on separate training workers. The pipeline uses in-flight weight updates and one-step-off-policy training.

8. **SWE-RL Stage**: A dedicated RL stage for end-to-end software engineering tasks where each rollout launches an Apptainer container with a target repository, runs an OpenHands agent loop to produce a code patch, and evaluates against ground-truth tests for a binary reward.

9. **RLHF + GenRM**: A separate RLHF stage using a principle-following GenRM (trained from Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507) to provide reward signals for improving instruction-following behavior, robustness, and interaction quality.

Information flows as follows: raw text tokens enter the embedding layer → the 88-layer hybrid stack processes them sequentially (Mamba-2 layers for linear-time sequence mixing, LatentMoE layers for sparse expert computation, attention layers for global interaction) → the final hidden state feeds into both the main language model head (for next-token prediction) and the two shared-weight MTP heads (for multi-token prediction) → during post-training, the base model checkpoint enters SFT on the 7M-sample blend → the SFT'd model enters the multi-environment RLVR stage across 21 environments → the RLVR model enters the SWE-RL stage for end-to-end software engineering → the SWE-RL model enters the RLHF stage with GenRM → the RLHF model undergoes MTP healing (freezing the backbone, training only MTP heads) → the final model is quantized to FP8 and NVFP4 via post-training quantization with AutoQuantize for mixed-precision layer selection.

#### 3.4.1 LatentMoE: Hardware-Aware Expert Design

The LatentMoE architecture (Elango et al., 2026) is the primary architectural contribution in Nemotron 3 Super. It is a redesign of the standard Mixture-of-Experts layer that targets two real-world deployment bottlenecks — memory bandwidth for reading expert weights in low-latency serving and all-to-all communication volume in distributed throughput-oriented serving — that standard FLOPs accounting does not capture.

**The structural inefficiency in standard MoE.** In a standard MoE layer (Figure 3a), each input token `$x \in \mathbb{R}^d$` (where `$d$` is the hidden dimension, 4096 in Nemotron 3 Super) is passed to a routing gate that selects `$K$` experts (top-`$K$` by routing score) from a pool of `$N$` total experts. Each selected expert `$e$` applies a feed-forward transformation to the full-dimensional token: `$\text{FFN}_e(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d$`. The outputs of the `$K$` selected experts are aggregated (typically weighted by the routing scores) and combined with the output of a shared expert and a residual connection. The cost structure is:

- **Memory bandwidth cost per token**: `$K \times d \times m$` weight elements must be read from memory, where `$m$` is the expert FFN intermediate dimension. For 512 experts with top-22 routing and `$d = 4096, m = 2688$`, each token requires reading `$22 \times 4096 \times 2688 \approx 242$` million weight elements just for the routed experts.

- **Communication cost in distributed serving**: When experts are sharded across GPUs, each token must be communicated to the `$K$` target GPUs. The routing volume per token is `$K \times d$` elements. With top-22 routing and `$d = 4096$`, each token generates approximately 90K elements of communication traffic for the routed experts alone.

Both costs scale linearly with `$K$` and quadratically with `$d$` (through the `$d \times m$` expert matrices). Standard MoE designs, motivated by sparsity arguments, attempt to maximize `$N$` and `$K$` while minimizing `$K/N$` (the sparsity ratio). But they leave `$d$` and `$m$` at the values dictated by dense transformer design — values that produce good dense model quality but create excessive deployment costs when multiplied by the expert count.

**The five design principles.** The paper distills five principles from systematic analysis of MoE systems across the throughput-latency Pareto frontier:

1. In low-latency serving, MoE inference is dominated by the memory bandwidth cost of reading expert weights. Each expert matrix has size `$d \times m$`; reducing this cost requires decreasing `$d$` or `$m$`.

2. In throughput-oriented serving, distributed MoE inference is dominated by all-to-all routing, whose volume scales as `$d \times K$`. Reducing communication overhead requires decreasing `$d$` or `$K$`.

3. Preserving model quality requires preserving the effective nonlinear budget `$K \cdot m$`. To relieve memory and communication bottlenecks without sacrificing quality, `$K$` and `$m$` should be held fixed.

4. A task-specific effective feature rank `$r_{\text{eff}}$` imposes a lower limit on how much `$d$` can be reduced; reducing `$d$` below this limit causes model quality to collapse.

5. Scaling both the total number of experts `$N$` and the top-`$K$` improves quality by exponentially expanding the space of expert combinations.

The critical strategic insight comes from combining principles (1)–(3) with principle (5): if `$K$` must be held fixed for quality but drives both memory bandwidth and communication cost, and if `$d$` is the most promising axis for cost reduction (it affects both bottlenecks simultaneously), then the optimal strategy is to **reduce `$d$` only for the routed expert computation and use the savings to increase `$K$` and `$N$` proportionally**. This is the core of LatentMoE: decouple the dimension used for routing and expert computation from the model's internal hidden dimension, compress the former, and expand capacity in the compressed space.

**The latent projection mechanism.** Each LatentMoE layer operates as follows:

1. **Down-projection**: An input token `$x \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is projected into a lower-dimensional latent space via a learned matrix `$W_\downarrow \in \mathbb{R}^{\ell \times d}$`:

$$z = W_\downarrow x$$

where `$d = 4096$` is the model's hidden dimension, `$\ell = 1024$` is the latent dimension, and `$z \in \mathbb{R}^{\ell}$` is the compressed token representation used exclusively for routing and expert computation. The compression ratio is `$d / \ell = 4096 / 1024 = 4$`. This means every dimension-related cost — expert weight reads, routing communication volume — is reduced by a factor of 4 relative to a standard MoE with the same hidden dimension and expert configuration.

2. **Routing in latent space**: The compressed token `$z$` is passed through a sigmoid-based routing gate (operating in `$\mathbb{R}^{\ell}$`) that computes routing scores for all `$N' = N \times d/\ell$` experts. The paper does not specify `$N$` explicitly for the standard-MoE baseline, but the LatentMoE configuration uses `$N' = 512$` total experts with top-`$K' = 22$` active experts. The top-22 experts are selected based on routing scores, and each produces:

$$y_e = \text{FFN}_e(z)$$

where each `$\text{FFN}_e$` operates entirely in `$\mathbb{R}^{\ell}$` and has intermediate dimension `$m = 2688$`. The expert weight matrices are therefore `$\ell \times m = 1024 \times 2688$` rather than `$d \times m = 4096 \times 2688$`, reducing per-expert weight reads by a factor of 4.

3. **Aggregation and up-projection**: The selected expert outputs are aggregated (weighted by routing scores) and projected back to the full hidden dimension via a learned matrix `$W_\uparrow \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times \ell}$`:

$$\hat{x} = W_\uparrow \sum_{e \in \text{top-}K'} s_e \cdot y_e$$

where `$s_e$` is the routing score for expert `$e$`. The result `$\hat{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is then combined with the shared expert output (which operates in the full dimension `$d$`) and the residual connection.

**What stays in the full dimension.** Critically, not everything is compressed. The following operations remain in the full hidden dimension `$d = 4096$`:
- The routing gate itself (the gating network that computes routing scores) operates on `$x \in \mathbb{R}^d$`, not `$z$`. Only the score computation for selecting among `$\ell$`-dimensional experts happens in the latent space. The paper states: "all non-routed computations—including the routing gate (gating network), shared expert computation, and non-expert layers—remain in the full hidden dimension `$d$`, as they do not contribute significantly to the targeted bottlenecks."
- The shared expert FFN, which is activated for every token (not sparsely routed), operates in `$\mathbb{R}^d$` with intermediate size 5376. Since the shared expert is always read, its weight read cost is independent of `$K$` and does not contribute to the routing communication bottleneck.
- The attention layers (when present) and Mamba-2 layers operate in `$\mathbb{R}^d$`.
- The latent projection matrices `$W_\downarrow$` and `$W_\uparrow$` themselves are `$d \times \ell$` and `$\ell \times d$` respectively, but these are relatively small overhead: together they contribute `$2 \times 4096 \times 1024 \approx 8.4$` million parameters per LatentMoE layer, compared to the expert parameters which total `$N' \times 2 \times \ell \times m \approx 512 \times 2 \times 1024 \times 2688 \approx 2.8$` billion parameters per layer.

**The capacity reallocation argument.** The key claim of LatentMoE is that the 4× compression of `$d$` to `$\ell$` can be used to increase `$K$` and `$N$` by the same factor — from some baseline `$(N, K)$` to `$(4N, 4K)$` — while keeping total memory bandwidth and communication cost constant. The paper's configuration uses `$N' = 512$` and `$K' = 22$`, implying that the equivalent standard MoE would have `$N = 128$` and `$K = 5.5$` (roughly top-5 or top-6) if cost-matched. The increased `$K'$` provides more combinatorial expressivity (22 experts co-activated per token, producing `$\binom{512}{22} \approx 10^{49}$` possible expert combinations versus `$\binom{128}{5} \approx 2.6 \times 10^8$` for the cost-matched standard MoE). The increased `$N'$` provides more total capacity for knowledge storage.

**Why this works without quality collapse.** Principle (4) states that there is a task-specific effective feature rank `$r_{\text{eff}}$` below which `$d$` cannot be reduced. The LatentMoE design does not reduce the model's representational dimension — the full hidden dimension `$d = 4096$` is preserved for all operations that need it (attention, Mamba, shared expert, layer norms, residual streams). What gets compressed is only the **expert input/output interface** — the specific computation path where the model processes tokens through specialized sub-networks. The intuition is that individual experts, being narrow specialists, do not need the full 4096-dimensional representation to do their work; a 1024-dimensional compressed representation captures the relevant features for routing decisions and expert computation. The up-projection `$W_\uparrow$` then reconstructs a full-dimensional contribution from the expert outputs, which is added back into the main residual stream. The quality preservation depends on `$W_\downarrow$` being sufficiently expressive to extract the expert-relevant features and `$W_\uparrow$` being sufficiently expressive to map expert outputs back into the full feature space.

**Auxiliary-loss-free load balancing.** Expert utilization is managed through two mechanisms: an auxiliary-loss-free load balancing strategy (Wang et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2025c) with an update rate of `$10^{-3}$`, paired with a standard load balancing loss with coefficient `$10^{-4}$` (Lepikhin et al., 2020). The auxiliary-loss-free strategy adjusts expert biases dynamically during training to encourage uniform expert utilization without imposing an explicit auxiliary loss term that could compete with the language modeling objective. The standard load balancing loss provides a small additional push toward uniform routing. The sigmoid router score function is complemented by these expert biases.

**Expert configuration summary.** The final LatentMoE configuration for Nemotron 3 Super is:
- Total experts per layer: 512
- Activated experts per token (top-`$K$`): 22
- Expert hidden dimension: 2688
- Shared expert intermediate size: 5376
- Latent dimension `$\ell$`: 1024
- Hidden dimension `$d$`: 4096
- Compression ratio `$d/\ell$`: 4

#### 3.4.2 Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) for Training and Speculative Decoding

Nemotron 3 Super incorporates a Multi-Token Prediction objective that serves a dual purpose: it improves representation quality during training by forcing the model to capture multi-step dependencies, and it enables native speculative decoding at inference time by using the prediction heads as an internal draft model.

**Standard MTP and its limitation for speculative decoding.** In conventional MTP training (Gloeckle et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2025c), `$N$` independent prediction heads are attached to the final hidden state of the main model. Given a hidden state `$h_n \in \mathbb{R}^d$` at position `$n$` (produced by the main transformer stack after processing tokens `$x_1, \ldots, x_n$`), each head `$i$` attempts to predict the token at offset `$i$`:

$$\hat{y}^{(i)}_n = \text{head}_i(h_n)$$

The loss for head `$i$` is computed against the ground-truth token `$x_{n+i}$`:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}}^{(i)} = -\log p_{\theta_i}(x_{n+i} \mid h_n)$$

where `$\theta_i$` are the parameters of the `$i$`-th head. Each head is trained independently to predict its specific offset using the **ground-truth hidden state** from the main model — the head never sees its own previous predictions during training.

This creates a problem for speculative decoding. At inference time, to use these heads as a draft model, the system must operate autoregressively: head 1 predicts `$x_{n+1}$`, then the draft token `$\hat{x}_{n+1}$` is fed into the main model to produce the hidden state `$h_{n+1}$`, then head 1 (or head 2, depending on the strategy) predicts `$x_{n+2}$` from `$h_{n+1}$`, and so on. But the heads were trained on ground-truth hidden states — they have never seen hidden states derived from their own (potentially incorrect) predictions. This **training-inference distribution mismatch** causes the acceptance rate to degrade rapidly as draft length increases. The model's internal representations drift away from what the draft heads expect, and the heads produce increasingly poor predictions.

**Shared-weight MTP for robust autoregressive drafting.** Nemotron 3 Super addresses this limitation by **sharing parameters across both MTP heads** during training. Instead of two independent heads, a single set of parameters is used for both prediction offsets. During training, this shared head is exposed to hidden states at position `$n$` and trained to predict both `$x_{n+1}$` and `$x_{n+2}$`:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}} = \sum_{i=1}^{2} -\log p_{\theta_{\text{shared}}}(x_{n+i} \mid h_n)$$

where `$\theta_{\text{shared}}$` are the shared parameters of the single prediction head, and `$i$` indexes the two prediction offsets.

**What this sharing accomplishes.** The shared-weight design acts as a form of regularization: the single head must learn representations that are simultaneously useful for predicting tokens at both offset 1 and offset 2. This multi-offset exposure means the head learns features that are robust to small variations in the hidden state — exactly the kind of robustness needed when the hidden state is derived from the head's own previous drafts rather than from ground-truth tokens. At inference time, the same shared head is applied recursively: from `$h_n$`, it drafts `$\hat{x}_{n+1}$`; after verification, the true `$h_{n+1}$` is obtained; from `$h_{n+1}$`, the same head drafts `$\hat{x}_{n+2}$`; and so on. Because the head was trained at multiple offsets, it generalizes better to the self-conditioned hidden states encountered during autoregressive drafting.

The paper quantifies this robustness in Table 2 and Figure 4. With a fixed draft length of 7, Nemotron 3 Super achieves an average acceptance length of 3.45 tokens, compared to 2.70 for DeepSeek-R1 (which uses offset-trained independent heads) and 3.33 for Qwen3-Next. The per-draft-index acceptance rates in Figure 4 show that Nemotron 3 Super maintains higher acceptance than DeepSeek-R1 at every draft position (from index 1 through 7), with the gap widening at later indices (4–7) where the recursive drafting challenge is most severe. This is direct evidence that the shared-weight training improves robustness to self-generated hidden states.

**Training objective integration.** The MTP loss is combined with the standard next-token prediction loss using a scaling factor. The paper specifies:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{LM}} + \lambda \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}}$$

where `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{LM}}$` is the standard causal language modeling loss (cross-entropy over the vocabulary for the next token), `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}}$` is the multi-token prediction loss averaged over the two offsets (using the shared head), and `$\lambda = 0.3$` is the MTP loss scaling factor. The scaling factor of 0.3 means the MTP objective contributes roughly 23% of the total loss gradient (since `$0.3 / (1 + 0.3) \approx 0.23$`). This makes MTP a meaningful but subordinate training signal — the model primarily learns next-token prediction, with multi-token prediction providing an auxiliary regularization effect.

**Architecture of the MTP head.** The paper states that there are "2 MTP layers with shared parameters." Each MTP layer consists of a separate transformer block (not explicitly described in detail, but following the standard pattern from Gloeckle et al., 2024, this is typically a single transformer layer with its own self-attention and feed-forward components) that takes the main model's final hidden state as input and produces a prediction over the vocabulary. The two layers share the same weights, meaning there are only `$\theta_{\text{shared}}$` parameters regardless of the number of prediction heads. This is more parameter-efficient than having independent heads (which would require `$2 \times |\theta_{\text{head}}|$` parameters) and enforces the multi-offset robustness through weight sharing rather than through explicit regularization.

**Speculative decoding performance on Blackwell hardware.** Figure 5 shows the impact of MTP on the throughput-latency Pareto frontier for NVFP4 inference on a B300 GPU with TRT-LLM (TP=1). With MTP disabled, the model achieves a baseline throughput-vs-latency tradeoff. Enabling MTP with draft length `$D = 1$` (predicting one additional token per step) shifts the curve upward and rightward — higher throughput for the same latency. Increasing to `$D = 3$` (drafting three tokens per step, which requires the shared head to be applied recursively three times) produces a further significant shift. The exact numerical throughputs and latencies are not quoted for specific operating points, but the curves demonstrate that MTP with draft length 3 provides substantially higher aggregate output tokens per second for any given median user latency compared to the no-MTP baseline. This is the practical payoff of the shared-weight MTP design: it improves both training quality (through multi-step supervision) and inference efficiency (through higher-acceptance speculative decoding) without requiring a separate draft model.

**MTP healing during post-training.** After the RLHF stage (Stage 4 of post-training), the model undergoes an MTP healing stage. In this stage, the backbone weights are frozen, and only the MTP heads are trained. The training uses prompts from the RLVR dataset and the negative log-likelihood loss (same as SFT) on the generated responses. The paper notes that this stage "significantly improves MTP accuracy." This is necessary because the MTP heads were trained on the base model's hidden state distribution; after SFT and RL, the hidden state distribution has shifted, degrading MTP head accuracy. The healing stage realigns the MTP heads with the post-trained backbone's representations without affecting the backbone's capabilities.

#### 3.4.3 Hybrid Interleaved Architecture and Global Anchors

Nemotron 3 Super's 88-layer stack is organized as a periodic interleaving of Mamba-2 blocks, LatentMoE layers, and self-attention layers. The architecture is designed to maximize inference throughput for long-context reasoning while preserving the global dependency modeling of dense transformers.

**Layer pattern.** Figure 2 shows the specific interleaving pattern, which repeats in blocks:

- A repeating unit of **4× Mamba-2 + LatentMoE** layers (4 consecutive layers, each consisting of a Mamba-2 block followed by a LatentMoE feed-forward component), then **3× Mamba-2 + LatentMoE** layers, then a block containing **Mamba-2 + Attention + LatentMoE**.
- The full stack is: 4× Mamba-LatentMoE, then 3× Mamba-LatentMoE, then a single Mamba-Attention-LatentMoE layer, then 3× Mamba-LatentMoE, then 3× Mamba-LatentMoE, then another Mamba-Attention-LatentMoE layer, then 4× Mamba-LatentMoE.

The total count is 88 layers (Table 1). Self-attention layers are rare — they appear only as "global anchors" at strategic positions in the stack, providing full-token interaction capability while the majority of sequence processing is handled by linear-time Mamba-2 blocks.

**Mamba-2 block configuration.** Each Mamba-2 block operates with:
- State dimension: 128
- Number of heads: 128
- Head dimension: 64
- Number of groups: 8

The state dimension of 128 means the recurrent state stored for each token position is a fixed-size vector of 128 elements (per head), which is orders of magnitude smaller than a KV cache entry in self-attention (which would be `$2 \times n_{\text{kv-heads}} \times d_{\text{head}} = 2 \times 2 \times 128 = 512$` elements per token per layer in GQA). Because Mamba's state is constant-sized and processed recurrently, the memory overhead for long sequences is dramatically lower — a 1M-token sequence requires the same `$128 \times 128 = 16384$` values per Mamba-2 layer as a 100-token sequence, whereas self-attention would require `$1M \times 512 \approx 512M$` values per attention layer.

**Attention layer configuration.** The sparse attention layers use Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) with:
- Query heads (`$n_q$`): 32
- Key-Value heads (`$n_{kv}$`): 2
- Head dimension: 128

GQA with only 2 KV heads dramatically reduces the KV cache size per attention layer relative to standard multi-head attention (which would have 32 KV heads, or 16× more KV-cache memory). With 2 KV heads at head dimension 128, the KV cache per token per attention layer is `$2 \times 2 \times 128 = 512$` elements (two for keys, two for values). For a 1M-token sequence, this is 512M elements per attention layer — substantial, but because attention layers constitute only a small fraction of the 88-layer stack (the exact count is not specified but appears to be 2–4 based on Figure 2), the total KV-cache memory is dominated by the Mamba layers' constant-sized state, not the attention layers' linear growth.

**Positional embeddings and normalization.** Consistent with prior Nemotron models, the architecture omits positional embeddings entirely — neither absolute nor rotary position encodings are used. This is unusual for transformer models but feasible because Mamba-2 inherently captures sequential position through its recurrent state dynamics, and the attention layers can learn position-agnostic interaction patterns when interleaved with position-aware Mamba layers. The normalization uses RMSNorm, and dropout and bias terms are omitted from linear layers. Embedding and output weights are untied (separate parameter matrices for input embedding and output projection).

**Activation summary per forward pass.** For each token:
- All 88 layers are traversed sequentially.
- Mamba-2 blocks process the token through their recurrent state-space mechanism with constant memory and linear time.
- LatentMoE layers activate 22 out of 512 experts (plus the shared expert), meaning only `$22/512 \approx 4.3$`% of the expert parameters are used per token.
- Attention layers (when encountered) compute full quadratic attention over the sequence history.
- The total active parameters per forward pass is 12.7B (12.1B excluding embeddings).

#### 3.4.4 NVFP4 Pretraining

Nemotron 3 Super was pre-trained for 25 trillion tokens using NVFP4 (NVIDIA 4-bit floating point) for the majority of linear layers. This is a first for a model of this scale and architectural complexity (hybrid Mamba-Attention MoE).

**The NVFP4 format.** NVFP4 uses an E2M1 element format — 1 sign bit, 2 exponent bits, 1 mantissa bit — organized into 16-element micro-blocks. Each micro-block has its own scaling factor stored in E4M3 (4 exponent bits, 3 mantissa bits). A second-level FP32 global scale factor is applied across all micro-blocks in a tensor. This two-level scaling (per-block E4M3 + per-tensor FP32) provides greater dynamic range than single-level block scaling, which is important for training where gradient magnitudes can vary by orders of magnitude across layers and over the course of training.

**Quantization scheme for training.** The paper describes the following per-operation precision details:

- **Weights**: Quantized to NVFP4 using two-dimensional (2D) block scaling. The 2D block structure maintains consistency between the quantized weights used in the forward pass and the backward pass. Weights are stored in NVFP4 and dequantized on-the-fly during computation.

- **Gradients**: Quantized to NVFP4 using one-dimensional (1D) blocks along the GEMM reduction axis. Stochastic rounding is applied to gradient tensors — instead of rounding to the nearest representable value (which introduces systematic bias), the gradient is randomly rounded up or down with probability proportional to the distance to the nearest representable values. This makes the quantization unbiased in expectation, which is critical for training stability over long horizons.

- **Activations**: Quantized to NVFP4 using 1D blocks along the GEMM reduction axis.

- **Random Hadamard Transforms (RHTs)**: Applied to the inputs of the weight-gradient computation (wgrad). The Hadamard transform is an orthogonal matrix that spreads information uniformly across dimensions, reducing the variance of the input distribution and making quantization more accurate. The paper states: "Random Hadamard Transforms (RHTs) are performed on inputs to wgrad."

**Precision assignments by layer type (Table 3).** Not all layers are trained in NVFP4. The paper explicitly assigns formats per layer type:

| Layer Type | Format | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| All linear layers (unless otherwise noted) | NVFP4 | Main training format |
| Final 15% of network | BF16 | Promote training stability at scale |
| Latent projections (`$W_\downarrow$`, `$W_\uparrow$`) | BF16 | Strategically kept in BF16; step-time impact is negligible |
| MTP layers | BF16 | Preserves multi-token prediction capabilities |
| QKV & Attention projections | BF16 | Maintain fidelity of attention layers |
| Mamba output projection | MXFP8 | Mitigates high incidence of underflows observed when quantizing to NVFP4 at smaller scales |
| Embedding layers | BF16 | Standard practice |

The decision to keep the final 15% of the network in BF16 is specifically motivated by training stability: the layers closest to the output experience the largest gradient magnitudes, and quantizing them to NVFP4 would introduce too much noise at a critical point in the gradient flow. The latent projections (`$W_\downarrow$` and `$W_\uparrow$`) are kept in BF16 because their parameter count is small (roughly 8.4M per LatentMoE layer vs. 2.8B for the experts), so the precision upgrade has negligible impact on total training throughput but protects the compression/decompression operations from quantization error. The Mamba output projection uses MXFP8 (8-bit floating point) rather than NVFP4 because the paper observed "high incidence of underflows" when quantizing this specific layer to NVFP4 at smaller scales — the recurrent nature of Mamba makes it more sensitive to quantization noise since errors propagate across timesteps.

**Hardware implementation.** The training uses "open-source NVFP4 GEMM kernels provided by Transformer Engine with the cuBLAS backend for fprop, dgrad, and wgrad GEMMs." Transformer Engine is NVIDIA's library for low-precision training on Hopper and Blackwell GPUs, providing fused kernels that handle quantization, dequantization, and matrix multiplication in a single operation. The cuBLAS backend provides the underlying BLAS routines.

**Training hyperparameters.** The model was trained with:
- Total tokens: 25 trillion
- Sequence length: 8,192 (pretraining); extended to 1,048,576 (1M) during the LC-Phase
- Global batch size: 3,072 sequences, yielding approximately 25.17 million tokens per batch
- Optimizer: AdamW with `$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`, weight decay = 0.1
- Learning rate schedule: Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) (Hu et al., 2024) — warmup over 200B tokens to peak LR `$4.5 \times 10^{-4}$`, stable plateau for 20T tokens, minus-sqrt decay for final 5T tokens to minimum LR `$4.5 \times 10^{-6}$`
- MTP loss scaling factor: 0.3
- Load balancing: auxiliary-loss-free with update rate `$10^{-3}$`, plus standard load balancing loss with coefficient `$10^{-4}$`

**Investigation of training artifacts: zero-valued weight gradients.** During NVFP4 training of Nemotron 3 Super, the authors observed a growth in the number of zero-valued weight gradient elements, reaching 7% of total parameters by the end of pretraining. They investigated the root cause through both analysis of the Super model and controlled experiments on Nemotron 3 Nano.

The paper identifies a specific failure mode related to **channel magnitude patterns** in expert layers (Figure 6). In the FC1 weight matrix (first linear layer of the expert FFN), certain output channels have norms that converge toward zero over the course of training. In the corresponding FC2 weight matrix (second linear layer), certain input channels similarly converge toward zero. These low-norm channels align: a low-norm output channel of FC1 corresponds (by position) to a low-norm input channel of FC2. This creates a feedback loop: the low-norm FC1 output produces a small activation, which produces a small gradient through FC2, which reinforces the low norm.

The root cause is NVFP4 underflow. Figure 7 shows a comparison: an NVFP4-trained Nemotron 3 Nano model at 1T tokens has roughly 3× more zero-valued weight gradients than a BF16-trained model at the same token horizon. When the NVFP4 model is switched to BF16 at 0.5T tokens, the zero count returns to baseline — confirming the zeros are a precision artifact, not a genuine training dynamic. The BF16 model still contains many small-magnitude gradients (`$< 10^{-12}$`), but NVFP4 quantization underflows these small values to exactly zero.

The mechanism is traced in Figure 8. At 500B tokens, zero-valued weight gradients in FC1 are attributed almost entirely to underflows in the gradient of FC2's activations (dgrad of FC2). This happens because NVFP4 weight quantization uses 2D blocks that can span both high-magnitude and low-magnitude channels — the block scaling factor is set by the high-magnitude channels, causing the low-magnitude channels' values to underflow to zero after dequantization. When FC2's weights contain underflow-zeroed elements, their gradients become zero, and these zeros propagate backward through the computation graph to become zeros in FC1's weight gradients. At 750B tokens, a second mechanism joins: underflows in the forward pass of FC1 (fprop of FC1) create zeros in FC2's weight gradients. The two mechanisms combined produce equally high zero percentages in both FC1 and FC2.

The paper's conclusion is that this behavior is not necessarily harmful — the released Nemotron 3 Nano model, trained entirely in BF16, reached a similar zero-valued weight gradient count after 10T tokens (compared to the NVFP4 model at 1T tokens), and inspection of its weight matrices revealed similar channel magnitude patterns. The NVFP4 quantization appears to **accelerate** a natural training dynamic (channel specialization and pruning) that would occur more slowly in higher precision, rather than introducing a qualitatively different phenomenon.

**Attempted precision healing before annealing.** Following prior NVFP4 training work (NVIDIA, 2025d), the authors evaluated whether switching all tensors to MXFP8 before the learning rate annealing phase would improve final model quality. They promoted all tensors to MXFP8 at 19T tokens (1T tokens before annealing) and trained through 20.6T tokens. While this improved the loss trajectory, it yielded **no improvement in downstream task accuracy** (Figure 9). Across eight benchmarks (MMLU Pro, MMLU, MATH 500, GSM8K, MBPP, ARC Challenge, HumanEval, WinoGrande), the MXFP8-healed model showed fluctuations around 0% improvement relative to the pure NVFP4 model, with no sustained positive trend. The final Nemotron 3 Super model was therefore trained with the NVFP4 recipe for the entire 25T-token horizon. This is a practically important finding: it means the common practice of "healing" low-precision models by switching to higher precision before the final annealing phase may not be necessary, at least at this scale and with this architecture.

#### 3.4.5 Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): Two-Stage Objective and Data Blend

The SFT phase trains the base model on 7M samples (approximately 80B tokens) using a two-stage objective designed to balance strong reasoning behavior with long-input-short-output performance.

**The motivation for two-stage SFT.** Single-stage SFT with standard per-token loss normalization (where the total loss is divided by the total number of output tokens across all conversations in the batch) causes a specific failure mode: conversations with long outputs (e.g., detailed reasoning traces) dominate the loss because they contribute many more tokens. The model learns to produce verbose, reasoning-heavy responses at the expense of concise answers. This causes "marked degradation on long-input-short-output scenarios" — when the input is long (e.g., a 256K-token document) but the expected output is short (e.g., a one-sentence answer), the single-stage model tends to produce unnecessarily long responses or fails to appropriately compress its output. The two-stage procedure addresses this by first training with token-level normalization (to establish strong reasoning capabilities) and then switching to per-conversation normalization (to restore appropriate output length control).

**Stage 1: Token-level (global) average.** For a packed global batch `$\mathcal{B}$` containing multiple conversations `$c$`, let `$\mathcal{O}_c$` denote the set of output-token positions for conversation `$c$` and `$|\mathcal{O}_c|$` its output-token count. The per-token negative log-likelihood at position `$t$` is:

$$\ell_t = -\log p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$$

where `$y_t$` is the target token at position `$t$`, `$x$` is the input context, `$y_{<t}$` are the preceding output tokens, and `$p_\theta$` is the model's predicted probability for the target token. The Stage 1 loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{tok}} = \frac{\sum_{c \in \mathcal{B}} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{O}_c} \ell_t}{\sum_{c \in \mathcal{B}} |\mathcal{O}_c|}$$

What this computes: the sum of negative log-likelihoods over all output tokens in the batch, divided by the total number of output tokens. This is the standard causal language modeling loss applied to SFT data.

Why this form: token-level averaging gives equal weight to every output token, regardless of which conversation it belongs to. Conversations with more output tokens (long reasoning traces, multi-turn agent interactions) contribute proportionally more to the loss and gradient. This encourages the model to allocate capacity to detailed, multi-step reasoning — exactly the behavior desired for agentic and math tasks.

**Stage 2: Sample-level (per-conversation) average.** The Stage 2 loss normalizes each conversation independently before averaging:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{samp}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{c \in \mathcal{B}} \left( \frac{1}{|\mathcal{O}_c|} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{O}_c} \ell_t \right)$$

What this computes: for each conversation, compute the average per-token loss over that conversation's output tokens; then average these per-conversation averages across the batch.

Why this form: per-conversation normalization gives equal weight to each conversation, regardless of its output length. A long 4000-token reasoning trace and a short 50-token answer contribute equally to the loss. This removes the bias toward verbose outputs and allows the model to learn appropriate output length control — when the task requires a short answer, the model is not penalized less (per conversation) for producing one.

**Training configuration.** Stage 1 uses 256k sequence length packing, global batch size 64, constant learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-5}$` with 30k warmup samples. Stage 2 uses 512k sequence length packing (to accommodate long-context data up to 512K tokens), global batch size 32, and constant learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-5}$`. The sequence length packing means multiple conversations are concatenated into a single long sequence, with attention masking to prevent cross-conversation attention. This maximizes GPU utilization by avoiding padding waste.

**MTP during SFT.** The shared-weight MTP head used in pretraining is continued during SFT. The combined objective is optimized with the same `$\lambda = 0.3$` scaling factor for the MTP loss. This preserves both the quality benefits of multi-step prediction and the inference-time speculative decoding capability after SFT.

**SFT data blend.** The 7M-sample SFT mixture (Figure 16) is dominated by agentic data:
- Agentic Programming: Largest component, including CLI agent traces, SWE trajectories, and web development tasks.
- Conversational and General-Purpose Tool Use: Trajectories from specialized tool-use simulations across 838 domains and 1.5M general-purpose tool-calling trajectories.
- Terminal Use: 84,864 samples from the dual-stream Terminal-Task-Gen methodology.
- Software Engineering: Trajectories distilled from OpenHands using Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B-Instruct on SWE-Gym, R2E-Gym, and SWE-rebench tasks.
- Search: Synthetic search-agent trajectories grounded in Wikidata knowledge graph walks, averaging 12 tool calls per trajectory.
- Math, Code, STEM: Competition math, competition code, formal proofs, CUDA kernel generation (100K samples), and SQL generation (96.5K samples).
- Financial Reasoning: 366,243 Q&A pairs with reasoning traces generated from SEC filings.
- Long Context: Synthetic multi-document QA with 4–7 hop reasoning chains and multi-record aggregation.
- Safety: Data generated with deliberative alignment framework, covering over-refusals, jailbreaks, and demographic biases.
- Multilingual: Translations into 6 languages with format restoration via Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507, plus Chinese-English parallel corpus.

The blend allocates a much larger proportion to agentic tasks compared to Nemotron 3 Nano, reflecting the paper's emphasis on agentic capabilities. The long-context component includes sequences up to 512K tokens.

**Reasoning control modes.** The SFT recipe introduces three reasoning modes:
- **Reasoning-off**: 3% of samples have reasoning traces stripped, teaching the model to produce direct answers when reasoning is not requested.
- **Regular reasoning**: The default mode with full reasoning traces.
- **Low-effort reasoning**: A new addition using 2% of SFT samples generated by GPT-OSS-120B in its low-effort mode (Du et al., 2025), covering math reasoning, STEM QA, and instruction following. These samples demonstrate abbreviated reasoning that achieves correct answers with fewer tokens.

After the main SFT stage, a short semi-on-policy SFT stage of 350 steps is added for inference-time budget control. The model generates rollouts, and 12% of reasoning traces are truncated to random reasoning budgets. This teaches the model to produce reasoning of variable length depending on the specified budget, giving users control over the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff at inference time.

**Stage 2 data composition.** The Stage 2 blend uses 85% of the Stage 1 mixture augmented with 256K and 512K-token long-context data (15% of the blend). The long-context component targets cross-document multi-hop reasoning and left-to-right sequential context processing.

#### 3.4.6 Multi-Environment RLVR (Stage 1)

The multi-environment Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards stage is the primary RL training phase, conducted asynchronously using GRPO across 21 diverse environments.

**GRPO algorithm and asynchronous setup.** The training uses an asynchronous Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) setup. GRPO is a variant of policy gradient methods adapted for language model training:

- **Inference workers** continuously generate trajectories by sampling from the current policy (the model). Each trajectory consists of a prompt and the model's generated response, including all tool calls and environment interactions.
- These trajectories are stored in a **rollout buffer**.
- When enough trajectories are collected to form a batch, the batch is sent to **training workers** for a model update.
- The updated model weights are pushed to the inference workers as soon as they become available. Critically, weight updates can happen **mid-rollout** (in-flight weight updates; Piché et al., 2025) — a single trajectory may contain tokens produced by different model versions.
- The KV cache is not recomputed after in-flight weight updates, trading some accuracy for substantial speedup.
- To prevent excessive policy lag (where the inference workers are using significantly outdated weights), the system restricts inference workers to be at most **one step behind** the latest model version.

The off-policy effects caused by the training-inference mismatch and policy lag are addressed by **masking the importance sampling ratio**. The importance sampling ratio, computed from the ratio of training and inference log-probabilities, is used in policy gradient methods to correct for the fact that trajectories were sampled from an older policy. Masking this ratio prevents large importance weights from destabilizing training when the policy has shifted significantly from the behavior policy.

**Training configuration.** The RLVR stage uses:
- 256 prompts per step
- 16 responses per prompt (for a total of 4096 rollouts per step)
- Batch size of 4096 (one gradient update per rollout batch)
- Maximum generation length starting at 49K tokens, later increased to 64K tokens
- Tension between single-environment and multi-environment training: training on a single environment leads to severe regressions on other benchmarks, while joint training across all environments yields stable gains

**Environment mixture.** The 21 environments span:
- **Math**: Competitive math problems, trained both with and without Python execution tools, plus formal proof verification.
- **Code**: Competition-style code, single-step patch generation for SWE tasks.
- **STEM**: Scientific problems from Nemotron 3 Nano plus newly curated, more challenging problems.
- **Instruction Following**: A multi-challenge dataset where the agent must follow complex user instructions with rewards computed from a predetermined rubric.
- **Safety**: Two environments — one for reducing over-refusals on safety-related prompts, one for improving robustness to jailbreaks. For jailbreaks, an iterative attack pipeline following PAIR (Chao et al., 2024) attacks an early SFT-only checkpoint to surface harder prompts.
- **Long Context**: The long-context environment from Nemotron 3 Nano.
- **Agentic Tool Use**: Conversational tool use, terminal use, and general-purpose tool calling.
- **Reasoning Gym** (Stojanovski et al., 2025): A diverse suite of reasoning tasks.
- **Low-effort reasoning environments**: 2% of all RL prompts are in low-effort mode (initially math, STEM QA, and competitive coding; later reduced to 1% with only math and STEM QA). The reward for a low-effort prompt is a function of both correctness and the number of generated tokens, encouraging the model to find correct answers with minimal token expenditure.

**Curriculum and prompt filtering.** Following Nemotron 3 Nano, prompts where the SFT model consistently provides correct answers are filtered out (there is no learning signal from tasks the model already solves perfectly). The remaining samples are sorted via a difficulty-based curriculum, with easier tasks presented earlier in training and harder tasks later.

**PivotRL for agentic domains.** Agentic RL introduces a specific tension: SFT on agentic trajectories is cheap but degrades out-of-domain (OOD) performance; end-to-end RL avoids OOD degradation but is expensive because every update requires online interactive rollouts. PivotRL (Yi et al., 2026) addresses this by reusing offline SFT expert trajectories during RL. It focuses training on informative turns ("pivots") within SFT traces where the policy has uncertainty over the next action. It uses a domain-appropriate reward to match the policy's action to the expert action, giving credit for similar actions rather than requiring exact match. PivotRL is applied for all agentic domains: Agentic Programming, Search, Terminal Use, and Conversational Tool Use.

---

#### 3.4.7 SWE-RL, RLHF, and MTP Healing (Stages 2–4)

**SWE-RL (Stage 2).** This dedicated stage trains the model for end-to-end software engineering tasks. Each rollout:
1. Launches an Apptainer container with the target repository (using `.sif` container images for filesystem isolation).
2. Runs a modified OpenHands agent loop that presents the problem statement, executes the agent's step loop up to a configurable turn limit, extracts the git patch, and cleans up.
3. Evaluates against ground-truth tests to compute a binary reward (patch passes all tests = 1, otherwise = 0).

To increase tool diversity, the training environment implements multiple agent classes within OpenHands that match the tool formats of different external harnesses (Claude Code and Codex CLI). This multi-harness approach means the model sees tool calls and prompts in different formats during training, improving generalization to all target harnesses at inference time.

Key infrastructure details include:
- Memory management: A watchdog daemon monitors aggregate RSS of the tmux process tree and kills runaway processes before they cause node-wide OOM.
- Command blocklist: A regex-based interceptor blocks dangerous commands (like `killall`, `pkill`) that could terminate training processes or vLLM servers on shared-kernel nodes.
- Serialization: Python's standard `json` is replaced with `orjson` for HTTP payloads between the gym and model server, since each trajectory turn carries prompt token IDs, generated token IDs, and log probabilities — large payloads where `orjson`'s Rust implementation provides meaningful speedup.

**RLHF (Stage 3).** Following the multi-environment RLVR, a separate RLHF stage improves instruction-following behavior, robustness, and interaction quality. Unlike Nemotron 3 Nano (which used RLHF only within the multi-environment stage), Nemotron 3 Super also runs a standalone RLHF-only stage at the end of post-training.

The reward signal comes from a Generative Reward Model (GenRM) — a large language model fine-tuned to evaluate response quality. Rather than a vanilla GenRM, Nemotron 3 Super uses a **principle-following GenRM** (Wang et al., 2025b), where the GenRM is prompted with behavioral principles that guide its evaluation. For example, principles might specify: "The response should not refuse benign requests," "The response should maintain a helpful tone," or "The response should acknowledge uncertainty when appropriate." These principles allow fine-grained control over the model's behavior on sensitive domains like identity and safety.

The GenRM is trained from Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507 using the Helpsteer 3 dataset (Wang et al., 2025c), commercially friendly subsets of the lmarena-140k dataset (Chiang et al., 2024), and recently collected human preference data. The GenRM provides scalar rewards (or pairwise preferences) that the RL algorithm optimizes against.

**MTP Healing (Stage 4).** The final post-training stage addresses distribution shift in the MTP heads. Throughout SFT and RL, the backbone model's hidden state distribution has shifted from what the MTP heads were trained on during pretraining. This degrades MTP head accuracy, reducing the effectiveness of speculative decoding. The healing stage:
- Freezes all backbone weights (the full 88-layer stack).
- Trains only the shared MTP head parameters.
- Uses prompts from the RLVR dataset.
- Trains with the negative log-likelihood loss (same as SFT) on the model's own generated responses.
- The paper reports this "significantly improves MTP accuracy."

#### 3.4.8 Quantization for Inference

The final post-training step produces two quantized checkpoints for efficient deployment: FP8 (for Hopper GPUs) and NVFP4 (for Blackwell GPUs).

**FP8 checkpoint (W8A8).** Post-training quantization (PTQ) to FP8 uses a small calibration subset of 256 samples with 65536 context length from the SFT dataset. The precision assignments (Table 6):

| Component | FP8 Checkpoint | BF16 Baseline |
|---|---|---|
| Embedding | BF16 | BF16 |
| Attention GEMM (QKV, Output Proj) | BF16 | BF16 |
| KV Cache + Attention BMM1 | FP8 | FP8 |
| Attention BMM2 | BF16 | BF16 |
| MoE GEMM (Sparse + Shared Experts) | FP8 | BF16 |
| MoE Latent Projection GEMM | BF16 | BF16 |
| Router | FP32 | FP32 |
| Mamba GEMM | FP8 | BF16 |
| Mamba SSM Cache | FP16 | FP32 |
| Mamba 1D Conv | BF16 | BF16 |
| Output Layers | BF16 | BF16 |

The FP8 quantization targets the largest GEMMs — MoE and Mamba linear layers — where the memory bandwidth savings are most impactful. The KV cache is kept in FP8 (matching the BF16 baseline's use of FP8 KV cache). The Mamba SSM cache is quantized to FP16 for speed (from the default FP32), with the implications discussed below.

**NVFP4 checkpoint (W4A4).** The NVFP4 PTQ recipe is more sophisticated, combining three techniques:
1. **Calibrated per-block weight scaling**: Weight per-block scales are selected by minimizing mean squared error (MSE) between the original and quantized weights, rather than the default max-based scaling. This offline optimization finds better scale factors at the cost of a one-time calibration step.
2. **Dynamic per-block max-based activation scaling**: Activation scales use max-based scaling because activation quantization must be computed efficiently at runtime — MSE-based search is too expensive for online computation.
3. **AutoQuantize for mixed-precision layer selection**: A neural architecture search-inspired method that assigns per-operator quantization formats to minimize a sensitivity-weighted cost objective.

**AutoQuantize algorithm (Section B.2).** For each operation `$i$` and quantization format choice `$f$`, the sensitivity is measured at the immediate output:

$$S(\text{Op}_i, Q_{i,f}) = \left(Y^{\text{bf16}}_i - Y^{Q_{i,f}}_i\right)^\top H_i \left(Y^{\text{bf16}}_i - Y^{Q_{i,f}}_i\right)$$

where `$Y^{\text{bf16}}_i$` is the BF16 output of the operation, `$Y^{Q_{i,f}}_i$` is the quantized output under format `$f$`, and `$H_i$` is the local Hessian approximation at the measurement point.

Using a diagonal Fisher information matrix approximation (the squared gradient of the loss with respect to the operation output), the sensitivity proxy becomes:

$$S(\text{Op}_i, Q_{i,f}) \approx \sum_{k=1}^{d} (\Delta Y_{i,k})^2 (g_{i,k})^2$$

where `$\Delta Y_{i,k}$` is the quantization error in the `$k$`-th dimension and `$g_{i,k}$` is the gradient of the loss with respect to that dimension. This approximates the second-order impact of quantization on the final loss — errors in dimensions with large gradients (high sensitivity) matter more than errors in dimensions with small gradients.

The performance cost of format `$f$` for operation `$i$` is defined as the FLOPs:

$$C(\text{Op}_i, Q_{i,f}) = \text{FLOPs}(\text{Op}_i, Q_{i,f})$$

AutoQuantize then solves the constrained optimization:

$$\min_{\{f\}} \sum_i S(\text{Op}_i, Q_{i,f}) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_i C(\text{Op}_i, Q_{i,f}) \leq B$$

where `$B$` is the total deployment cost budget (set to an effective precision of 4.75 bits in the paper's experiments).

**Deployment constraint handling.** The search respects two deployment constraints:
- **Linear layer fusion**: Within each attention layer, Q, K, and V projections are fused (required to share one quantization format). The sensitivity and cost are aggregated across the three projections.
- **MoE layer constraints**: All sparse experts within a single MoE layer must share one quantization format (required by vLLM and TRT-LLM quantized MoE APIs). The sensitivity is measured at the MoE block output to capture combined contributions from all sparse experts.

**Resulting NVFP4 checkpoint (Table 7).** The AutoQuantize search across candidate precisions {NVFP4, FP8, BF16} with a 4.75-bit budget produces:
- Sparse expert GEMMs: NVFP4 throughout (these are the largest operations, so quantizing them to 4 bits provides the most benefit)
- Attention and Mamba projection GEMMs: FP8 or BF16 (more sensitive to quantization)
- Shared expert GEMMs: Mixed NVFP4, FP8, and BF16
- MoE latent projection GEMMs: FP8 or BF16
- QKV projections, Mamba conv, output layers: BF16 (most sensitive)

The full PTQ process completes in less than 2 hours on a single B200 node with 8 GPUs using 512 samples at sequence length 4096. The resulting model achieves 99.8% median accuracy relative to the BF16 baseline (Table 8).

**Mamba state cache quantization.** Quantizing the Mamba SSM cache from FP32 to FP16 introduces a subtle problem: non-local error accumulation due to the recurrent nature of Mamba decoding.

When the SSM cache is stored in FP16 but arithmetic is performed in FP32, the cache is fetched from memory in FP16, upcast to FP32 for the recurrent update `$h_t = A_t h_{t-1} + B_t x_t$`, and cast back to FP16 for storage. Let the quantization error at step `$t$` be `$e_t$` (so the stored state is `$h_{q,t} = h_t + e_t$`). Unrolling the recurrence:

$$h_{q,t} = h_t + e_t + A_t e_{t-1} + A_t A_{t-1} e_{t-2} + \cdots + A_t A_{t-1} \cdots A_1 e_0 = h_t + \sum_{i=0}^{t} \left( \prod_{j=i+1}^{t} A_j \right) e_i$$

This shows that quantization error from **earlier steps propagates through subsequent recurrent transitions and accumulates over decoding time**. Each `$e_i$` is multiplied by a product of transition matrices — if these matrices have eigenvalues with magnitude near 1, the error neither decays nor explodes, and it simply accumulates. This is qualitatively different from feedforward quantization, where error at one layer does not directly affect subsequent tokens.

The empirical consequence (Table 9) is dramatic: naively casting the SSM cache to FP16 causes up to 40% increase in verbosity (number of generated tokens) on LiveCodeBench when combined with W8A8 quantization, and 37% increase even with W16A16. The model generates longer, more rambling outputs because the accumulated state noise degrades its ability to determine when an answer is complete.

**Stochastic rounding as a solution.** The key insight is that round-to-nearest-even (RTNE) introduces **biased** quantization error — for any given input, the rounded value is deterministic, so the error has non-zero mean (the difference between the true value and the fixed rounded value). In a recurrent system, this bias accumulates coherently over time, systematically drifting the state away from its true value. Stochastic rounding (SR) replaces this with **zero-mean noise**: the value is randomly rounded up or down with probability proportional to the distance to the nearest representable values, making the error unbiased in expectation.

The paper's solution: apply stochastic rounding before casting the SSM cache to FP16, using Philox<5> pseudorandom number generation (5 rounds of the Philox algorithm, balancing statistical quality against generation overhead). This:
- Eliminates the verbosity increase for both BF16 and W8A8 baselines (Table 9).
- Maintains accuracy within 0.5% of the FP32 cache baseline on LiveCodeBench.
- Has acceptable overhead because Blackwell provides a dedicated PTX instruction for stochastic rounding during type conversion and supports Philox-based PRNG through cuRAND.

The alternative of using INT16 with per-block scaling also works (eliminates verbosity) but requires computing, storing, and loading block scale factors, adding complexity. FP16 with stochastic rounding is chosen as the cleaner solution.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: LatentMoE Reframes MoE Design as a Hardware-Aware Capacity Reallocation Problem, Not a Sparsity Problem

Prior MoE research (Shazeer et al., 2017; Lepikhin et al., 2020; Fedus et al., 2022; DeepSeek-AI, 2025c) optimized for aggregate FLOPs efficiency — maximizing total parameters while minimizing per-token compute through sparse activation. The dominant assumption was that FLOPs reduction translates directly to throughput gains. This paper's distinctive intellectual move is to identify and articulate **which specific hardware bottlenecks FLOPs accounting ignores**: memory bandwidth for expert weight reads (dominant in low-latency serving) and all-to-all routing communication volume (dominant in throughput-oriented distributed serving). Both scale with `K × d`, where K is the number of active experts and d is the hidden dimension. Standard MoE designs, by keeping d at the value dictated by dense transformer design, leave these real-world costs high even when FLOPs are low.

The conceptual reframing is that MoE architecture design is **a constrained capacity allocation problem**, not a sparsity problem. The five principles in Section 2.1.1 amount to a budget-allocation framework: you have a fixed memory bandwidth budget (for reading expert weights) and a fixed communication budget (for routing), and you must allocate expert capacity (N and K) and representational dimension (d) under these constraints. The key insight from principles (1)–(5) is that d is the axis you should reduce — it helps both bottlenecks simultaneously — while K and m should be held fixed for quality, and the savings from reducing d should be reinvested in increasing N and K by the same factor. This is genuinely different from the sparsity-first framing of prior MoE work, where the question was "how few experts can we activate per token?" rather than "what can we reduce to make room for more experts?"

LatentMoE's projection-and-recovery mechanism (down-project d → ℓ for routing and expert computation, up-project back to d for everything else) operationalizes this reframing. The innovation is not the existence of projection layers (dimensionality reduction is ancient) but the **surgical precision of what gets compressed**: only the operations that dominate the two hardware bottlenecks (routed expert weight reads and routing communication). The routing gate, shared expert, attention layers, and Mamba blocks all stay in the full hidden dimension. This is the difference between "let's make the model smaller" and "let's move the expensive parts into a cheaper space while preserving quality where it matters."

The significance goes beyond this specific model. LatentMoE provides a **diagnostic framework** for MoE efficiency: rather than evaluating designs by FLOPs-per-token, evaluate them by their impact on the specific hardware operations that dominate in the target deployment regime. This framework naturally extends to future hardware (different bottleneck hierarchies would yield different optimal compression targets) and is what distinguishes the contribution from a one-off architectural tweak. The throughput numbers in Figure 1 (2.2× vs. GPT-OSS-120B, 7.5× vs. Qwen3.5-122B) validate the hardware-aware design, but the conceptual contribution is the reframing itself.

---

### Innovation 2: Shared-Weight Multi-Token Prediction Resolves the Training-Inference Distribution Mismatch in Autoregressive Speculative Decoding

Multi-Token Prediction has been used both as a training objective to improve representation learning (Gloeckle et al., 2024) and as a mechanism for native speculative decoding (DeepSeek-AI, 2025c). But prior MTP implementations share a specific fragility: each prediction head is trained to predict a **fixed offset** from the ground-truth hidden state, and at inference time, these heads must operate on hidden states derived from their own (potentially incorrect) previous predictions. This training-inference distribution mismatch causes acceptance rates to degrade rapidly as draft length increases — the model's internal representations drift away from what the draft heads expect.

The conceptual contribution is recognizing that this mismatch is not an implementation detail but a **first-class architectural problem** with a specific structural solution: parameter sharing across prediction offsets. Training a single shared head to predict tokens at multiple offsets (n+1, n+2) acts as a form of **implicit distributional robustness regularization**. The head cannot over-specialize to ground-truth hidden states at any single offset because it must simultaneously perform well at multiple offsets with different hidden-state characteristics. This forced generalization makes the head more tolerant of the self-conditioned hidden states encountered during autoregressive drafting.

This is fundamentally different from the approaches prior work took to improve speculative decoding: increasing the number of heads (more draft tokens per step, but each head still suffers from distribution drift), training a separate draft model (adds memory and complexity), or using more sophisticated tree attention for verification (helps with acceptance but doesn't fix the underlying head quality). The shared-weight approach is parameter-efficient (one head instead of N independent heads), requires no external draft model, and addresses the root cause (distribution mismatch) rather than its symptoms (low acceptance rates).

The evidence in Table 2 and Figure 4 tells a clear story: Nemotron 3 Super's shared-weight MTP achieves an average acceptance length of 3.45 versus DeepSeek-R1's 2.70, with the gap widening at later draft indices (4–7) where recursive drafting is most challenging. This is not just a performance gain — it's evidence for the specific mechanism claimed (distribution shift robustness). If the improvement were just from better base model quality, the advantage would be uniform across draft indices; the widening gap specifically at indices where distribution shift is worst supports the robustness interpretation. The throughput-latency improvement in Figure 5 (MTP D=3 shifting the Pareto frontier significantly over MTP-off) demonstrates the practical payoff, but the intellectual contribution is the diagnosis of the distribution mismatch problem and the weight-sharing solution.

---

### Innovation 3: The Two-Stage SFT Objective Diagnoses and Fixes a Specific Failure Mode in Multi-Objective Fine-Tuning

Supervised fine-tuning on mixed datasets (short answers, long reasoning traces, multi-turn agent interactions) creates an inherent tension: per-token loss normalization causes long outputs to dominate the gradient, biasing the model toward verbosity and degrading performance on tasks requiring concise responses. This is a known practical problem, but prior work has largely addressed it through data balancing heuristics (down-weighting long samples, up-weighting short samples, or filtering) rather than through objective design.

The paper's two-stage approach — token-level averaging followed by per-conversation averaging — reframes this as an **optimization curriculum problem** rather than a data engineering problem. Stage 1 uses standard token-level normalization because the model needs the strong gradient signal from long reasoning traces to learn complex multi-step behaviors. This is the "skill acquisition" phase — verbosity bias is accepted as a necessary cost of learning detailed reasoning. Stage 2 switches to per-conversation normalization because the model now has the reasoning capabilities and needs to learn output length control — the "skill refinement" phase where the verbosity bias introduced by Stage 1 is corrected.

What makes this an innovation rather than an obvious fix is the **explicit staging** with different objectives, grounded in a diagnosis of *why* the problem occurs. The problem isn't just "too much long data" (which could be solved by rebalancing); it's that the loss function's normalization scheme determines which tasks the model prioritizes. Switching objectives mid-training leverages the fact that capabilities acquired under one loss normalization can be preserved and refined under a different one — the model doesn't "unlearn" reasoning when switched to per-conversation averaging; it learns to deploy that reasoning with appropriate output length.

This has implications beyond this specific model. The two-stage objective pattern could apply to any multi-task SFT regime where tasks have systematically different output lengths and the desired behavior includes both complex long-form reasoning and concise short-form responses. The paper doesn't claim universality, but the diagnostic framing (identifying the specific failure mode, matching the objective design to the failure mechanism) is a generalizable contribution to the methodology of LLM fine-tuning.

---

### Innovation 4: NVFP4 Training at 25T-Token Scale Is Not Just Feasible but Confirms That Low-Precision Training Accelerates Natural Channel Specialization

Training large language models in 4-bit precision is widely assumed to be risky — quantization noise can destabilize training, reduce final model quality, or introduce artifacts that high-precision training avoids. Prior NVFP4 work (NVIDIA, 2025d) demonstrated feasibility at smaller scales, but the question of whether 4-bit training at 25-trillion-token scale would produce a model competitive with higher-precision baselines remained open. The paper's primary finding here is that NVFP4 training at this scale is not just feasible but stable, with no meaningful downstream accuracy penalty (Figure 9 shows no sustained improvement from switching to MXFP8 before annealing).

The deeper intellectual contribution, however, is the **investigation of the training artifacts** that NVFP4 produces. The emergence of zero-valued weight gradients (7% by end of training) and channel magnitude patterns (Figure 6) could be interpreted as harmful quantization damage. The paper's diagnostic move is to show that BF16 training produces the *same* artifacts at the same magnitudes, just more slowly (Figure 7): the released BF16 Nemotron 3 Nano model reached similar zero-gradient counts at 10T tokens, and its expert layers showed the same channel magnitude convergence patterns. The NVFP4 quantization appears to **accelerate a natural specialization dynamic** — the tendency of expert channels to specialize into high-importance and low-importance channels, with low-importance channels eventually being effectively pruned — rather than introducing a qualitatively different phenomenon.

This reframes low-precision training from "necessary evil with acceptable quality tradeoffs" to "potentially beneficial accelerator of natural training dynamics." The finding that low-norm channels are attenuated faster in NVFP4 (because small gradients that would survive in BF16 underflow to zero in NVFP4) suggests that 4-bit training may act as a form of **implicit structural regularization**, accelerating the pruning of redundant expert capacity. The paper doesn't make strong claims here — it's cautious, noting that the behavior "appears to correlate" and using phrases like "we believe" — but the evidence is suggestive and points toward a reframing of low-precision training that goes beyond the standard accuracy-efficiency tradeoff narrative.

The negative result in Figure 9 — that precision healing before annealing does not improve downstream accuracy — is also significant. The common practice in low-precision training is to "heal" the model by switching to higher precision for the final annealing phase, under the assumption that the final fine-tuning benefits from higher precision. The paper finds that this provides no benefit, which has direct practical implications: training pipelines can remain in low precision for the full training horizon, simplifying implementation and reducing the engineering complexity of precision-switching. This is an incremental but practically valuable finding validated across eight diverse benchmarks.

---

### Innovation 5: Agentic RL at Scale Requires a Distinctive Infrastructure-Aware Methodology, Not Just More Environments

The paper demonstrates successful RL training across 21 environments simultaneously, including long-horizon software engineering tasks with containerized execution and per-task reward verification. The straightforward engineering contribution — "we built a system that handles 21 environments" — would be incremental. What makes this distinctive is the set of **methodological decisions** that arise specifically from the tension between training scale and agentic task complexity:

- **Isolating SWE-RL into a separate stage** because SWE rollouts are substantially slower and require longer contexts, creating a throughput bottleneck when co-trained with shorter-horizon environments. This is not a trivial scheduling decision — it reflects a deeper insight that **not all environments can or should be trained jointly**. The heterogeneity of agentic tasks (some require 2K tokens and 1 second, others require 64K tokens and 5 minutes) means that a uniform training loop would be bottlenecked by the slowest environments, wasting GPU hours.

- **PivotRL** (Yi et al., 2026) as a method for efficient agentic RL that reuses offline SFT trajectories and focuses training on informative turns where the policy has uncertainty. This addresses a specific failure mode of pure SFT for agentic tasks (out-of-domain degradation) and a specific cost problem of pure RL (expensive online rollouts). The conceptual contribution is recognizing that the SFT data already contains high-quality trajectories — the RL problem is not to generate new trajectories from scratch but to **selectively reinforce the parts of those trajectories that the policy hasn't yet mastered**.

- **The infrastructure investigation of Mamba SSM cache quantization** (Section 4.3). The finding that naive FP16 caching causes up to 40% verbosity increase, and that stochastic rounding fixes this, is a specific diagnostic contribution. The unrolling analysis (`h_{q,t} = h_t + Σ Π A_j e_i`) is a crisp theoretical explanation for an otherwise mysterious empirical phenomenon (why does a 16-bit cache cause the model to ramble?). The stochastic rounding fix is elegant because it leverages the unbiasedness property to replace systematic error accumulation with zero-mean noise that doesn't coherently drift the state.

- **In-flight weight updates** as a speedup technique that accepts some off-policy data in exchange for dramatically faster training iteration. The paper's specific contribution is quantifying the acceptable staleness (one-step policy lag) and the mitigation (masking importance sampling ratios), providing a practical recipe for asynchronous RL at scale.

Taken together, these methodological decisions constitute a **playbook for scaling agentic RL** that addresses the heterogeneity problem (different tasks, different timescales, different infrastructure requirements) rather than assuming all environments can be uniformly batched. This is a systems-level contribution that goes beyond "we trained on more tasks" — it's a set of principles for how to structure multi-environment training when the environments have fundamentally different computational characteristics.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary evaluation suite spans general knowledge (MMLU-Pro, MMLU, GPQA-Diamond), mathematical reasoning (GSM8K, MATH, MATH Level 5, AIME 2024, HMMT Feb25), code generation (HumanEval, MBPP-Sanitized, LiveCodeBench, SciCode), commonsense understanding (ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, OpenBookQA, PIQA, WinoGrande), reading comprehension (RACE), agentic tasks (SWE-Bench, Terminal Bench, TauBench V2, BrowseComp, BIRD Bench), instruction following (IFBench, Scale AI Multi-Challenge, Arena-Hard-V2), long-context retrieval (RULER at 64K–1M, AA-LCR), and multilingual capability (MMLU-ProX, WMT24++, MGSM, Global MMLU-Lite). The base model evaluations (Table 4) use the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021), the Lightman et al. (2022) split of 12,000 training and 500 test questions, and standard few-shot evaluation protocols. The post-trained model evaluations (Table 5) use the Nemotron 3 Nano benchmark suite extended with additional agentic benchmarks, with evaluations run through the Nemo Evaluator SDK and Nemo Skills Harness.

- **Base model.** The pre-trained base model is Nemotron 3 Super 120B-A12B Base, a 120.6B-total-parameter (12.7B active) hybrid Mamba-Attention LatentMoE model pre-trained on 25 trillion tokens in NVFP4. The architectural details (88 layers, 4096 hidden dimension, 512 experts with top-22 routing, Mamba-2 with 128 state dimension and 128 heads, GQA with 32 query heads and 2 KV heads, MTP with 2 shared-weight heads) are described in Section 2.1. For base model comparisons (Table 4), the baselines are Ling-flash-Base-2.0 (total parameters unspecified) and GLM-4.5-Air-Base (total parameters unspecified, though the "Air" designation in GLM-4.5 terminology indicates a smaller sparse variant).

- **Metrics.** For base model evaluations, the primary metrics are accuracy (exact match or normalized accuracy depending on the benchmark) under few-shot prompting: 5-shot for MMLU and MMLU-Pro, 8-shot for GSM8K, 4-shot for MATH, 0-shot for HumanEval (pass@1 from 32 generations), 3-shot for MBPP (pass@1 from 32 generations), 25-shot for ARC-Challenge, 10-shot for HellaSwag, 0-shot for OpenBookQA and PIQA, 5-shot for WinoGrande, 0-shot for RACE, 5-shot (average) for MMLU Global Lite, 8-shot (native CoT) for MGSM, 0-shot for RULER (100 samples per task), and pass@32 for AIME 2024. For post-trained model evaluations (Table 5), the metrics vary by benchmark but are generally accuracy or exact match, with evaluation protocols following the official settings of each benchmark (e.g., OpenHands harness for SWE-Bench). Throughput is measured in output tokens per second per GPU on B200 GPUs using vLLM and TRT-LLM (best of the two frameworks per model), with the specific configuration of 8K input and 64K output sequence lengths (Figure 1).

- **Baselines.** For the base model (Table 4): **Ling-flash-Base-2.0** (Ling-Team, 2025) and **GLM-4.5-Air-Base** (GLM-4.5-Team, 2025) — both are publicly available base models in a comparable parameter class. For the post-trained model (Table 5): **GPT-OSS-120B** (OpenAI, 2025) — a 120B total, 5B active parameter MoE model with MXFP4 weights, MXFP8 activations, and FP8 KV-cache — and **Qwen3.5-122B-A10B** (Yang et al., 2025) — a 122B total, 10B active parameter MoE model in BF16. Table 5 also includes comparisons on specific benchmarks where certain baseline models may not have been evaluated (e.g., Qwen3.5-122B missing BrowseComp scores, or GPT-OSS-120B missing certain SWE-Bench harness variants). The paper states: "For comparison with GPT-OSS-120B and Qwen-3.5-122B-A10B, we use officially reported numbers whenever available; when not available, we follow the Nemotron 3 Nano report procedure of either sourcing values from reputable public aggregators (when consistent with the official protocol) or computing scores ourselves using the official evaluation settings."

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For base model evaluations, the generation budget is implicit in the few-shot prompting protocols (e.g., pass@1 from 32 generations means 32 complete solutions per problem). For post-trained model evaluations, the generation budget is not standardized across benchmarks — agentic tasks like SWE-Bench and TauBench involve multi-turn interactions with variable token counts. Throughput comparisons (Figure 1) are measured in relative output tokens per second per GPU on B200 GPUs, with the reference baseline being Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B-BF16 (set to 1.0). For speculative decoding evaluation (Table 2, Figure 4), the draft budget is fixed at 7 tokens, and the metric is average acceptance length (the number of draft tokens accepted per verification step). For pretraining compute, the paper notes that the WSD schedule and checkpoint merging saved approximately 4T tokens of compute (roughly 16% of the pretraining FLOP budget) by avoiding dedicated decay runs.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The base model evaluations follow standard benchmark protocols without specific cross-validation. For the SFT and RL stages, the paper describes a "semi-on-policy" approach for inference-time budget control (350 steps of SFT using model-generated rollouts), but does not report cross-validation for the post-training pipeline. For checkpoint merging evaluation (Figure 11), the paper reports average accuracy across 12 benchmarks with sliding merge windows of 125B, 250B, and 500B tokens, selecting the best-of-three merge at each checkpoint. For quantization evaluation (Table 8), the paper reports accuracy on a fixed set of benchmarks without cross-validation, comparing the FP8 and NVFP4 checkpoints to the BF16 reference. The paper does not report confidence intervals, statistical significance tests, or error bars for any of the evaluation results.

#### Base Model Performance (Table 4)

On **MMLU** (5-shot), Nemotron 3 Super achieves 86.01 versus 81.00 for both baselines — a gap of roughly 5 percentage points. On **MMLU-Pro** (5-shot, CoT), the gap widens: 75.65 versus 62.10 (Ling-flash) and 58.20 (GLM-4.5-Air) — a margin of 13.55 to 17.45 points. On **GPQA-Diamond** (5-shot, CoT), Nemotron 3 Super reaches 60.00 versus 36.00 and 23.20 — indicating substantially stronger graduate-level reasoning capability.

The mathematical reasoning results show the most dramatic advantages. On **MATH** (4-shot), Nemotron 3 Super scores 84.84 versus 63.80 (Ling-flash) and 50.36 (GLM-4.5-Air). On the competition-level subset **MATH Level 5** (4-shot), it reaches 70.00 versus 39.80 and 26.30. On **AIME 2024** (pass@32), it scores 53.33 versus 30.00 and 20.00. These gaps — exceeding 20–30 points on MATH Level 5 — suggest that Nemotron 3 Super's pre-training recipe (25T tokens, NVFP4, the two-phase curriculum, and the synthetic math/code datasets) produces substantially stronger mathematical reasoning than comparably-sized base models. However, on **GSM8K** (8-shot), Nemotron 3 Super scores 90.67, slightly below Ling-flash-Base-2.0 at 90.75 — essentially tied, indicating that on simpler math word problems, the advantage narrows.

On long-context tasks measured by **RULER**, Nemotron 3 Super maintains high accuracy across all context lengths where baselines are evaluated: 92.26 (64K), 88.26 (128K), 84.56 (256K), 82.49 (512K), and 71.00 (1M). Ling-flash-Base-2.0 scores 72.12 (64K) and 52.03 (128K) — no results are reported beyond 128K. GLM-4.5-Air-Base scores 80.26 (64K) and 61.70 (128K), with no results beyond. At 128K, Nemotron 3 Super's 88.26 is roughly 36 points above Ling-flash and 26 points above GLM-4.5-Air. The 71.00 at 1M tokens (where neither baseline reports results) demonstrates effective use of the model's full 1M-token context window after the LC-Phase continuous pretraining.

On code tasks, Nemotron 3 Super achieves **HumanEval** (0-shot, pass@1 n=32) of 79.40 versus 70.10 (Ling-flash) and 76.30 (GLM-4.5-Air). On **MBPP-Sanitized** (3-shot, pass@1 n=32), it scores 78.38 versus 77.30 and 77.50 — a much smaller margin, with the three models clustering within roughly 1 point. This suggests that the synthetic code concepts and algorithmic datasets (Section 2.3.2 and 2.3.3) provided meaningful gains on HumanEval but less differentiation on MBPP.

On commonsense understanding, results are mixed. Nemotron 3 Super leads on **ARC-Challenge** (96.08 vs. 94.80 and 93.90) and **HellaSwag** (88.97 vs. 84.69 and 87.70), but lags on **WinoGrande** (78.93 vs. 78.37 and 83.82 — GLM-4.5-Air leads here at 83.82). On **OpenBookQA**, Nemotron 3 Super scores 50.20 versus 47.00 and 48.60 — a smaller gap. On **PIQA**, 85.47 versus 84.00 and 84.22. These smaller margins on commonsense benchmarks suggest that architectural innovations (LatentMoE, hybrid Mamba-Attention) benefit complex reasoning more than simple fact retrieval or commonsense inference.

On multilingual tasks, Nemotron 3 Super achieves **MMLU Global Lite** (5-shot average) of 85.72 versus 74.94 (Ling-flash) and 79.25 (GLM-4.5-Air). On **MGSM** (8-shot average), 87.47 versus 82.73 and 80.33. The 10+ point margin on Global MMLU-Lite suggests effective multilingual pretraining data and the multilingual SFT component.

On reading comprehension (**RACE**, 0-shot), Nemotron 3 Super scores 91.00 versus 90.10 and 89.50 — a narrow advantage.

**Assessment.** The base model results demonstrate that Nemotron 3 Super's architecture and pretraining recipe produce a meaningfully stronger foundation model than Ling-flash-Base-2.0 and GLM-4.5-Air-Base, particularly on mathematical reasoning, graduate-level science (GPQA), and long-context retrieval. The margins on MATH Level 5 (+30.2 and +43.7 points) and GPQA-Diamond (+24 and +36.8 points) are large enough to suggest genuine capability differences rather than evaluation noise. However, the comparison has a limitation: the paper does not report the total parameter counts for Ling-flash-Base-2.0 or GLM-4.5-Air-Base. If these models are substantially smaller than 120B total, the comparison may not be matched on total capacity even if active parameters are similar. Additionally, the GLM-4.5 family's "Air" designation typically indicates a smaller distilled or compressed variant — if GLM-4.5-Air-Base has fewer total parameters, the comparison overstates Nemotron 3 Super's architectural advantage.

#### Checkpoint Merging Analysis (Figure 11, Figure 17)

During the stable phase of the WSD learning rate schedule, offline checkpoint merging (weighted averaging over sliding windows of recent checkpoints) consistently outperforms individual trained checkpoints by 2–4 points on the unweighted average across 12 benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, MMLU, HumanEval, HumanEval+, MBPP, MBPP+, GSM8K, MATH-500, RACE, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, WinoGrande). This gap persists throughout the 20T-token stable phase (Figure 11). During the final 5T-token LR decay phase (shaded region), the gap between merged and trained checkpoints narrows substantially, and by the end of training, the two evaluation trajectories largely coincide.

The per-benchmark breakdowns in Figure 17 show that merging provides consistent improvements across all 12 benchmarks during the stable phase, though the magnitude varies. During the decay phase, per-benchmark performance generally rises for both merged and trained checkpoints, with occasional crossovers. The paper notes that for the Nemotron 3 Nano scale architecture (30B-A3B), merge-based readouts could surpass decay-trained checkpoints for short (~500B) decay windows, but at longer 1T and 1.5T merge horizons, no improvement over decay-trained checkpoints was observed. The final base model checkpoint used for downstream alignment was itself a 500B merge, confirming that short-horizon merging remains practically useful even alongside a full decay schedule.

The FP8 and NVFP4 quantized checkpoints are evaluated against the BF16 reference model across the same benchmark suite as the post-trained model (Table 8). The NVFP4 checkpoint achieves 99.8% median accuracy relative to BF16 (stated in Section 4.2, though not computed explicitly in the table). Examining specific benchmarks:

On **MMLU-Pro**, BF16 scores 83.73, FP8 scores 83.63, NVFP4 scores 83.33 — the NVFP4 degradation is roughly 0.4 points. On **HMMT Feb25** (with tools), BF16 scores 94.73, FP8 scores 94.38, NVFP4 scores 95.36 — the NVFP4 checkpoint actually scores higher, which likely reflects evaluation noise rather than quantization improvement. On **GPQA** (no tools), BF16 79.23, FP8 79.36, NVFP4 79.42 — all within 0.2 points. On **LiveCodeBench v5**, BF16 81.19, FP8 80.99, NVFP4 80.56 — a degradation of roughly 0.63 points from BF16 to NVFP4. On **SciCode**, BF16 42.05, FP8 41.38, NVFP4 40.83 — degradation of approximately 1.22 points. On **HLE** (no tools), BF16 18.26, FP8 17.42, NVFP4 17.42 — both quantized checkpoints show the same 0.84-point degradation. On **SWE-Bench** (OpenCode), BF16 60.47, NVFP4 59.90 (FP8 not reported) — degradation of 0.57 points. On **TauBench V2** (average), BF16 61.15, FP8 61.07, NVFP4 60.46 — degradation of roughly 0.69 points. On **IFBench**, BF16 72.58, FP8 72.32, NVFP4 73.30 — NVFP4 scores higher than BF16. On **Scale AI Multi-Challenge**, BF16 55.23, FP8 54.35, NVFP4 52.80 — degradation of roughly 2.43 points from BF16 to NVFP4, the largest drop in the table. On **Arena-Hard-V2**, BF16 73.88, FP8 76.06, NVFP4 76.00 — both quantized checkpoints score higher. On **RULER 1M**, BF16 91.64, FP8 91.43, NVFP4 91.60 — all within 0.21 points. On **MMLU-ProX**, BF16 79.35, FP8 79.21, NVFP4 79.37 — within 0.16 points.

The Mamba SSM cache quantization investigation (Table 9) evaluates different caching strategies on an early checkpoint of Nemotron 3 Super, reporting both accuracy and verbosity (completion tokens) on LiveCodeBench and SciCode. The key findings are:

- **FP32 cache (baseline)**: LiveCodeBench pass@1 avg-of-8 = 72.91, completion tokens = 21,769; SciCode = 40.90, 3,680 tokens.
- **FP16 cache (naive)**: LiveCodeBench = 73.24, completion tokens = 29,812 — a 36.95% increase in verbosity. SciCode = 42.01, 3,760 — only 2.19% increase, suggesting SciCode is less sensitive to state noise.
- **FP16 + stochastic rounding (Philox 5)**: LiveCodeBench = 72.00, completion tokens = 21,392 — verbosity returns to within 1.73% of baseline. SciCode = 41.94, 3,580 — within 2.72%.
- **W8A8 + FP16 (naive)**: LiveCodeBench = 73.13, completion tokens = 30,536 — a 40.27% increase. This shows the verbosity problem compounds with weight/activation quantization.
- **W8A8 + INT16 + block128 scaling**: LiveCodeBench = 72.22, completion tokens = 22,406 — verbosity increase only 2.90%, demonstrating that alternative integer formats with block scaling can also solve the problem.
- **W8A8 + FP16 + SR (Philox 5)**: LiveCodeBench = 72.63, completion tokens = 22,159 — verbosity increase 1.79%. This is the selected recipe.

The Philox round count is varied (Philox 3, 4, 5, 10), with Philox<5> chosen to maintain accuracy and verbosity while minimizing pseudorandom number generation overhead. At Philox<3> (W8A8 + FP16 + SR), LiveCodeBench drops to 70.07 with a 10.70% verbosity increase — too few rounds reduce the statistical quality of the rounding. At Philox<4>, accuracy is 72.85 with a slight verbosity decrease (-0.63% on LiveCodeBench). Philox<5> is the minimal round count that eliminates verbosity increase across both benchmarks and both precision settings.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Precision healing before annealing (Figure 9)**: Switching all tensors to MXFP8 at 19T tokens (1T tokens before the final annealing phase) improved the loss trajectory but produced no sustained improvement in downstream task accuracy across eight benchmarks (MMLU Pro, MMLU, MATH 500, GSM8K, MBPP, ARC Challenge, HumanEval, WinoGrande). The improvement percentages oscillate around 0% with noise on the order of 5–10% for some benchmarks (MATH 500, GSM8K) and less than 5% for others. This negative result validates that full-horizon NVFP4 training is sufficient and that the additional complexity of precision switching is unnecessary. However, the evaluation is limited to eight benchmarks measured at a few token counts around the healing window (roughly 19.6T to 20.6T) — it is possible that a longer healing period or different benchmarks would show benefits.

**Checkpoint merging window size (Figure 11, Figure 17)**: Sweeping merge windows of 125B, 250B, and 500B tokens and selecting the best-of-three at each checkpoint consistently outperforms individual trained checkpoints by 2–4 points on the unweighted average across 12 benchmarks. The specific contribution is the demonstration that this holds throughout the full 25T-token training run, not just at a few evaluation points. The finding that the gap narrows during the LR decay phase (shaded region) and largely disappears by the end of training suggests that the primary value of merging is during stable-LR training phases where individual checkpoints are noisy. This is consistent with the interpretation that merging acts as a variance reduction technique, averaging out the noise in individual weight snapshots rather than discovering genuinely better weight configurations.

**MTP shared-weight vs. independent heads (Table 2, Figure 4)**: The primary ablation for MTP design is implicit in the comparison with DeepSeek-R1 (which uses independently-trained offset heads) and Qwen3-Next (whose MTP design is not specified but likely also uses independent heads). The shared-weight design produces an average acceptance length of 3.45 versus DeepSeek-R1's 2.70 — a 27.8% relative improvement. Figure 4 shows that this advantage grows at later draft indices (indices 4–7), exactly where the training-inference distribution mismatch is most severe. This pattern is consistent with the claimed mechanism (shared-weight training provides robustness to self-generated hidden states) but does not isolate the effect from other differences between the models (base model quality, training data, MTP head architecture, training duration). A direct ablation comparing shared-weight vs. independent-head MTP on the same base model would be needed to establish causality.

**Two-stage SFT objective**: The paper describes the two-stage SFT recipe (token-level average then per-conversation average) and claims that single-stage SFT "led to a marked degradation on long-input-short-output scenarios," but does not report quantitative results comparing single-stage vs. two-stage SFT on any benchmark. The effectiveness of the approach is therefore asserted rather than empirically validated within this paper. The reader must rely on the qualitative description and the intuition that per-conversation normalization corrects verbosity bias. This is a significant omission — given that the two-stage objective is presented as a methodological innovation, quantitative ablation results would substantially strengthen the claim.

**Multi-environment vs. single-environment RL**: The paper states that "training on all environments simultaneously yields stable gains, whereas single-environment training leads to severe regressions on other benchmarks," but does not report quantitative results for single-environment RL baselines. No table or figure compares multi-environment RLVR against sequential single-environment training. The claim is based on internal experimentation but is not empirically substantiated in the paper. This is another significant omission given that multi-environment training is a core component of the post-training methodology.

**PivotRL vs. standard RL for agentic tasks**: PivotRL is described as an efficient RL method for agentic domains, but no quantitative comparison against standard end-to-end RL or against pure SFT baselines is provided. The paper states that PivotRL "greatly improves the efficiency of our agentic RL, without facing the OOD degradation issues of SFT," but no table or figure quantifies this efficiency improvement. A forthcoming manuscript is cited (Yi et al., 2026), but the current paper provides no empirical support.

**PRM step-wise aggregation (Appendix E of prior Nemotron work — not reproduced)**: The paper does not include PRM training or evaluation, as it uses a GenRM for RLHF rather than a process reward model. There is no ablation of GenRM training data, GenRM architecture, or the principle-following mechanism.

**NVFP4 PTQ algorithm ablation (Table 10)**: Several PTQ algorithms are compared for the NVFP4 checkpoint on four benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, GPQA, LiveCodeBench, AA-LCR). The BF16 baseline achieves 83.49, 79.92, 72.907, 53.00. The default NVFP4 PTQ (static per-tensor scales with max-value calibration, dynamic per-block scales from block maxima) achieves 82.99, 79.29, 70.18, 55.50 — a degradation of 0.5, 0.63, 2.73, and an improvement of 2.50 points. Weight per-block scales minimizing MSE achieves 83.31, 79.92, 71.37, 56.75 — better than default on all four benchmarks, matching or exceeding BF16 on MMLU-Pro and GPQA. Weight per-block scales minimizing output MSE achieves 83.05, 78.98, 71.00, 57.06 — slightly worse on GPQA and LiveCodeBench. GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) achieves 83.11, 80.05, 69.79, 57.87 — best on GPQA and AA-LCR but worst on LiveCodeBench. The hybrid recipe combining weight MSE-based scales and dynamic max-based activation scales (used in the final model) is not explicitly shown in this table but is described as providing the "best overall results."

**AutoQuantize mixed-precision assignment (Tables 7, 8)**: The effectiveness of AutoQuantize is demonstrated implicitly by the accuracy of the final NVFP4 checkpoint in Table 8 (99.8% median accuracy relative to BF16). However, no ablation compares AutoQuantize against a uniform NVFP4 assignment (all GEMMs quantized to NVFP4 without selective promotion). Table 10 provides the closest comparison: the default NVFP4 PTQ (which likely represents uniform quantization without mixed precision) achieves MMLU-Pro 82.99 (vs. 83.33 for final NVFP4), GPQA 79.29 (vs. 79.42), LiveCodeBench 70.18 (vs. 80.56), and AA-LCR 55.50 (vs. 58.06). The final NVFP4 checkpoint substantially outperforms the default PTQ on LiveCodeBench and AA-LCR, suggesting AutoQuantize provides meaningful gains. However, the default PTQ in Table 10 uses a different calibration approach (max-value calibration vs. the final recipe's MSE-based weight scales), so the improvement cannot be attributed solely to AutoQuantize.

**SSM cache quantization (Table 9)**: Multiple alternatives to naive FP16 caching are explored: INT16 with block-128 scaling, FP16 with stochastic rounding at various Philox round counts. Both INT16+block128 and FP16+SR(Philox 5) eliminate the verbosity increase. The ablation of Philox round count (Philox 3, 4, 5, 10) shows that Philox 3 is insufficient (10.70% verbosity increase on LiveCodeBench W8A8), Philox 4 is marginally acceptable (-0.63% verbosity change), Philox 5 eliminates the issue, and Philox 10 is similar to Philox 5 but with higher computation. This is a thorough investigation of the solution space for a specific, practically important problem.

**MTP healing**: The paper states that the MTP healing stage "significantly improves MTP accuracy" but does not report quantitative results (acceptance rates before vs. after healing). Given that MTP acceptance directly impacts throughput, this is a noteworthy omission.

### Critical Assessment

**Central Claim 1: LatentMoE improves accuracy per parameter and per FLOP compared to regular MoEs.** The paper's title and introduction claim that LatentMoE "optimizes for both accuracy per FLOP and accuracy per parameter." However, the experimental section provides no direct comparison between LatentMoE and a cost-matched standard MoE on the same base model. The base model comparisons (Table 4) pit the full Nemotron 3 Super architecture (which includes LatentMoE, hybrid Mamba-Attention, MTP, NVFP4 training, and the specific data mixture) against Ling-flash and GLM-4.5-Air — models with different architectures, training data, and possibly different total parameter counts. The throughput comparisons (Figure 1) compare full systems, not isolated architectural components. The claim that LatentMoE specifically provides better accuracy per FLOP is not isolated in any experiment. To demonstrate this, the paper would need to compare Nemotron 3 Super against a version of itself with standard MoE layers (same number of experts, same total parameters, but routing and expert computation in the full hidden dimension) trained on the same data. This ablation is not performed.

What *is* demonstrated is that the integrated system (LatentMoE + hybrid Mamba-Attention + MTP + NVFP4 + the specific training recipe) produces a model that is competitive with or better than comparably-sized dense/baseline MoE models while being substantially faster. This validates the overall architecture but does not isolate the contribution of LatentMoE specifically. The reader should understand the claim as "the system as a whole achieves good accuracy-throughput tradeoffs" rather than "LatentMoE is proven superior to standard MoE."

**Central Claim 2: NVFP4 training at 25T-token scale is stable and produces competitive models.** This claim is well-supported by the base model results (Table 4), which show strong performance against BF16-trained baselines, and by the failure of precision healing to improve accuracy (Figure 9). The investigation of zero-valued weight gradients (Figures 6, 7, 8) provides a detailed mechanistic account of training dynamics under NVFP4. However, the claim that NVFP4 training is "stable" is not quantified — there is no training loss curve, no gradient norm plot, and no comparison of training stability metrics (loss spikes, divergence events) between NVFP4 and higher-precision training. The fact that the model completed 25T tokens of training without catastrophic failure demonstrates basic feasibility, but "stable" implies more than just "didn't diverge." The 7% zero-valued weight gradient rate is presented as an acceptable artifact rather than a stability concern, with the argument that BF16 training eventually reaches similar levels — but the paper does not quantify when in training this zero-gradient rate stabilizes, whether it correlates with any performance degradation, or whether 7% is the ceiling or would continue growing with more tokens.

**Central Claim 3: Shared-weight MTP enables more effective speculative decoding than independently-trained offset heads.** This claim is supported by the comparison with DeepSeek-R1 in Table 2 and Figure 4. The 27.8% relative improvement in average acceptance length and the widening gap at later draft indices are consistent with the distribution-shift-robustness mechanism. However, the comparison is between entirely different model families (Nemotron 3 Super vs. DeepSeek-R1) with different architectures, training data, and MTP implementations. The paper does not isolate the weight-sharing variable: we don't know DeepSeek-R1's MTP head architecture, whether it uses 2D block scaling or a different approach to handle the distribution shift, or whether its lower acceptance rates are due to the head design or simply to lower base model quality. A direct experiment training Nemotron 3 Super with both shared-weight and independent-head MTP (same model, same data, different head designs) would definitively establish causality, but this is not performed.

**Central Claim 4: The two-stage SFT objective corrects verbosity bias.** This claim is asserted but not empirically validated. No table or figure compares the two-stage SFT against single-stage SFT on any benchmark, let alone on metrics that would directly measure verbosity bias (e.g., response length distributions, accuracy on short-answer tasks). The paper describes the problem (long inputs with short expected outputs degrade under single-stage SFT) and the solution (per-conversation normalization in Stage 2), but the reader cannot assess the magnitude of the problem or the effectiveness of the solution from the reported results. This is a significant gap in an otherwise detailed paper.

**Central Claim 5: Multi-environment RLVR prevents the regressions that single-environment RL causes.** This claim is stated but not quantified. No results compare multi-environment against single-environment RLVR. Given that this is presented as the motivation for the joint training approach, the absence of supporting data is notable. The paper reports that multi-environment RL yields "stable gains" (Section 3.2.1), but the actual gains are not quantified against a pre-RL baseline — there is no table comparing SFT-only vs. SFT+RLVR on the full benchmark suite, making it difficult to assess how much RLVR actually improves the model.

**Central Claim 6: 2.2× and 7.5× throughput advantages while maintaining comparable accuracy.** This claim requires careful parsing. The throughput advantage is measured on B200 GPUs using the best inference framework per model (Figure 1). The accuracy comparison spans many benchmarks (Table 5), and the results are mixed: Nemotron 3 Super outperforms GPT-OSS-120B on most agentic benchmarks but lags on Arena-Hard-V2 (73.88 vs. 90.26) and LiveCodeBench (81.19 vs. 88.00). Compared to Qwen3.5-122B, Nemotron 3 Super lags on most benchmarks (SWE-Bench, TauBench, GPQA, HLE, Arena-Hard-V2) while matching on math and long-context. The phrase "comparable accuracy" is generally accurate when comparing against GPT-OSS-120B (Nemotron 3 Super is better on some, worse on others, roughly comparable on average), but against Qwen3.5-122B, Nemotron 3 Super is generally behind on accuracy. The enormous throughput advantage (7.5×) comes partly from Nemotron 3 Super's efficiency and partly from Qwen3.5 being evaluated in BF16 without quantization — a fairer comparison might give Qwen3.5 its best available quantized format if one exists. The paper's evaluation protocol states they use "officially reported numbers whenever available," suggesting Qwen3.5 may not have released quantized checkpoints.

**Genuine weaknesses:**

1. **Absence of direct architectural ablations.** The paper introduces LatentMoE, shared-weight MTP, and the hybrid interleaving pattern as architectural innovations, but none are ablated against simpler alternatives on the same model. The architecture is evaluated as a monolithic whole against external baselines (Ling-flash, GLM-4.5, GPT-OSS, Qwen3.5), which differ in many dimensions beyond the specific architectural choices of interest. This makes it impossible to attribute performance gains to specific components.

2. **Missing SFT and RL ablations.** The two-stage SFT objective, the multi-environment RL strategy, and PivotRL are all presented as methodological contributions but without quantitative ablation results. The reader cannot assess whether these design choices matter or whether simpler alternatives would perform equivalently.

3. **Throughput measured only on B200/B300 GPUs with best-of-two-frameworks selection.** The throughput advantage is hardware-specific and inference-framework-specific. The 7.5× advantage over Qwen3.5 partly reflects that Qwen3.5 is evaluated in BF16. The conclusion that Nemotron 3 Super is more efficient would be strengthened by showing the advantage on a broader range of hardware (A100, H100, consumer GPUs) and with standardized inference settings.

4. **No training cost reporting.** The paper does not report the total GPU-hours, FLOPs, or dollar cost of pretraining Nemotron 3 Super, making it impossible to assess whether the claimed efficiency gains at inference time come at the expense of higher training costs. The checkpoint merging technique is described as saving roughly 4T tokens of compute, but the absolute cost is not reported.

5. **Base model baselines may not be size-matched.** The total parameter counts of Ling-flash-Base-2.0 and GLM-4.5-Air-Base are not reported. If these models are substantially smaller than Nemotron 3 Super's 120.6B total parameters, the performance comparison in Table 4 overstates Nemotron 3 Super's architectural advantage.

6. **No error bars, confidence intervals, or significance tests.** All evaluation results are reported as point estimates without any measure of uncertainty. For benchmarks with small test sets (e.g., MATH Level 5, AIME 2024 with a limited number of problems), the variance could be substantial. The MMLU-ProX "average over languages" and TauBench V2 "average" are composites without reported variance.

7. **Speculative decoding evaluated on a single benchmark (SPEED-Bench) with a single draft length (7).** The acceptance rate behavior for other draft lengths, other benchmarks, or real-world deployment scenarios is not characterized.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- A direct comparison of Nemotron 3 Super with standard MoE layers (same total experts, top-K, and total parameters, but routing in full dimension d rather than latent dimension ℓ) trained on a subset of the training data (e.g., 1T tokens) to isolate the LatentMoE contribution.

- A comparison of shared-weight vs. independent-head MTP on the same base model, measuring both training perplexity and inference-time acceptance rates.

- An ablation of the two-stage SFT objective against single-stage SFT on a subset of the benchmark suite, with specific measurement of response length distributions and accuracy on short-answer tasks.

- Quantitative comparison of multi-environment vs. single-environment RLVR, showing the regression magnitudes that multi-environment training prevents.

- A comparison of Nemotron 3 Super against itself at an earlier training stage (e.g., 10T, 15T, 20T tokens) to characterize the scaling behavior and whether 25T tokens was necessary.

- Evaluation of Qwen3.5-122B in its best available quantized format (FP8 or INT4 if available) for a fairer throughput comparison.

- Per-benchmark sample sizes, variance estimates, and confidence intervals for the main results tables.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Cost of Difficulty Estimation and Strategy Selection Is Not Accounted for in the $4\times$ Efficiency Claim

The entire compute-optimal framework described in the reference example—adaptively allocating test-time compute based on prompt difficulty—depends on the ability to estimate each prompt's difficulty before deciding how to allocate the inference budget. In the reference paper, this required generating 2,048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness (oracle) or PRM final-answer scores (predicted). The authors acknowledged this explicitly in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

This is a gap with direct consequences for any practitioner trying to deploy the method. The reported $4\times$ efficiency gains over best-of-N are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. If difficulty estimation costs 2,048 generations per question, and the test-time budget being optimized is 64–256 generations, the estimation step alone consumes roughly 8–32× more compute than the solution generation itself. In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution, and the former would dominate the latter. The paper frames cheaper difficulty estimation as future work:

> "our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity, and we leave the investigation of more efficient difficulty estimation methods to future work"

but no concrete path to cheaper estimation is developed or evaluated. Until this gap is closed, the $4\times$ figure should be understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** under the assumption that difficulty is known for free, not a realized deployment gain. The cost could be amortized if difficulty estimates are reused across many similar queries, but the paper provides no analysis of this scenario.

---

### Hard Problems Remain Essentially Unsolved—Test-Time Compute Cannot Create Capability from Nothing

Across all methods studied—search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations—the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5) showed near-zero improvement regardless of compute budget. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all methods and all budgets. In the revision setting (Figure 7, right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%, and the $14\times$ larger model consistently outperforms test-time compute on these problems across all $R$ regimes.

This is a fundamental capability bound, not a mere limitation of the specific methods. Test-time compute can amplify existing capability—it finds correct solutions that the base model is *capable* of producing at some non-zero rate—but it cannot create the capability to solve a problem that the base model simply does not understand. The paper is transparent about this in Section 7:

> "Test-time compute provides minimal gains on problems that are fundamentally outside the base model's capability range"

The consequence for practitioners is that this approach offers no path forward for genuinely novel or out-of-distribution reasoning tasks. If the model's pass@1 on a problem class is near zero, no amount of search or revision will help—there are no correct solutions in the proposal distribution to find or refine. For such problems, pretraining a larger or differently-trained model remains the only viable path. The paper does not attempt to mitigate this; it identifies the boundary and characterizes it empirically across difficulty bins, but the boundary is inherent to the test-time compute paradigm itself.

---

### Single Benchmark, Single Model Family—Generalization to Other Domains and Architectures Is Unverified

All experiments use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions) with PaLM 2-S\* as the base model. The authors state they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this claim is unverified in the paper. Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or domain-specific in ways that matter for deployment:

- The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend on PaLM 2-S\*'s output distribution and calibration properties. A model with different error patterns or different calibration could exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves—for instance, a better-calibrated model might show less PRM over-optimization, changing the optimal strategy per difficulty bin.
- The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families (GPT-4 class vs. open-source models, for instance).
- The MATH benchmark consists exclusively of competition-level math problems requiring symbolic reasoning. The paper does not address whether the difficulty-dependent patterns—beam search hurting easy problems, revisions helping them, balanced sequential-parallel ratios being optimal for hard problems—generalize to other reasoning domains: code generation, logical reasoning, scientific question-answering, or tasks requiring factual knowledge rather than inference. Math problems have a specific structure (single correct answer, verifiable via ground-truth matching) that may not transfer to open-ended generation or multi-dimensional evaluation.

The paper acknowledges the model specificity implicitly by studying only one model, but does not discuss domain generalization as a limitation. A practitioner deploying these methods on, say, a code generation task with a different base model cannot assume the specific difficulty-bin-to-strategy mapping transfers. The compute-optimal policy would need to be re-derived for the new domain and model, and the paper provides no guidance on how transferable the *patterns* are versus how task-specific the *policy* is.

---

### The $14\times$ Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained—the Pretraining-vs.-Inference Tradeoff Is Measured Against a Weak Baseline

The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023) rather than Chinchilla-optimal training (Hoffmann et al., 2022), where both data and parameters are scaled according to a power-law budget. The authors explicitly acknowledge this departure:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

This matters concretely for the headline finding that test-time compute can outperform a $14\times$ larger model. A compute-optimally trained larger model (scaling both parameters and data according to Hoffmann et al.'s prescription) would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model at the same total FLOPs budget, making the pretraining baseline **weaker than it could be**. The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining—for example, +27.8% relative improvement on easy-medium questions at $R \ll 1$ for revisions—might shrink or reverse against a properly compute-optimal larger model.

An additional concern: the paper gives the $14\times$ larger model **only greedy decoding**—no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search. The comparison is therefore between a smaller model with compute-optimal test-time strategies and a larger model with the simplest possible inference strategy. A fairer comparison might allocate the larger model its own test-time compute budget (even a modest best-of-8 or best-of-16, which would be cheap relative to the pretraining cost difference). The paper does not explore this, so the FLOPs-matched results represent a lower bound on pretraining's competitiveness. For practitioners deciding between training a larger model versus investing in test-time compute infrastructure, this means the paper's analysis somewhat overstates the case for test-time compute—a compute-optimally trained larger model with even modest inference-time augmentation would close some or all of the reported gap.

---

### The Revision Model Has a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate, and the Fix Is a Patch, Not a Solution

Section 6.1 reports a significant practical problem with the iterative revision approach: approximately **38% of correct answers produced during a revision chain get "revised" back into incorrect answers in the subsequent step**. This is a direct consequence of the training data construction: the revision model is trained exclusively on trajectories where all in-context answers are incorrect, followed by a correct target. It never sees examples where the current answer is already correct, because such examples do not naturally appear in the training data generation process (which samples incorrect answers followed by a correct one). The model therefore has no training signal for what to do when its current answer is already good—it treats every in-context answer as something to be improved, even when "improving" means changing a correct answer to an incorrect one.

The paper mitigates this by selecting the best answer from the entire revision chain (using majority voting or verifier-based selection) rather than always taking the final revision. But this is an inference-time patch—it does not fix the underlying model behavior. The implication for deployment is that revision chains require a selection mechanism, adding latency (the model must generate the full chain before a decision is made) and complexity. The paper does not explore whether this behavior could be fixed at the training level—for example, by including "no-change-needed" examples in the training data, or by training the model with a termination criterion. The 38% reversion rate means that roughly 4 out of 10 of the model's own good outputs will be corrupted by subsequent revisions, which is a substantial inefficiency in the sequential sampling budget.

---

### No Combination of Search and Revisions—the Two Axes Are Studied Independently, Missing Their Complementary Potential

The paper studies two complementary mechanisms for test-time compute—PRM-guided search (modifying the verifier) and iterative revisions (modifying the proposal distribution)—but never combines them. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges this:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

This is a significant gap because the two mechanisms have complementary, difficulty-dependent strengths: revisions improve the proposal distribution (generating better candidates through local refinement), while PRM search improves candidate selection (finding the best among generated candidates through global exploration). The paper's own results show that revisions work best on easy problems (local refinement of already-good answers), while beam search works best on medium-hard problems (global search for correct solutions the model wouldn't find by random sampling alone). Combining them—for instance, using the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search, or using the PRM to guide which revisions to pursue—could yield gains beyond either method alone.

The practical consequence is that the current results represent a **lower bound** on what test-time compute can achieve. The $4\times$ efficiency gain over best-of-N may be conservative—a fully integrated system using both proposal-distribution improvement and verifier-guided search could be substantially more efficient. For practitioners, this means the paper's specific compute-optimal policy (selecting between search and revisions based on difficulty) is likely not the optimal policy for a system that can deploy both simultaneously. The paper does not suggest a concrete path to combining them, leaving this as open-ended future work.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the conversation around LLM architecture design from a single-axis optimization problem (maximize accuracy at a given parameter count) to a **multi-objective co-design problem** where architecture, precision, training recipe, and deployment hardware are jointly considered. This is not a paradigm shift in the sense of introducing a fundamentally new learning algorithm—the components (MoE, Mamba, speculative decoding, low-precision training) all predate this work. Rather, it is a **demonstration that system-level integration across these components can produce a 2.2–7.5× throughput advantage without sacrificing accuracy**, which reframes the question from "which architecture is best?" to "how should we allocate our architectural budget across these mechanisms for a specific deployment target?"

The conceptual contribution that is most likely to influence future work is LatentMoE's reframing of MoE design as a **hardware-aware capacity allocation problem** rather than a sparsity problem. The five design principles in Section 2.1.1—identifying memory bandwidth and all-to-all communication as the true bottlenecks, recognizing that d is the most promising axis for reduction, and reinvesting the savings in increased N and K—provide a diagnostic framework that extends beyond this specific architecture. Future MoE designs on different hardware (where, for instance, communication might be cheap but memory bandwidth is the sole bottleneck, or vice versa) can apply the same analysis to arrive at different optimal compression targets. This makes the contribution more durable than a one-off architectural tweak: even if the specific projection-and-recovery mechanism of LatentMoE is superseded, the diagnostic methodology persists.

The secondary contribution that changes the landscape is the **validation that NVFP4 pretraining at 25T-token scale is not just feasible but produces models competitive with higher-precision baselines**—with the counterintuitive finding that precision healing before annealing provides no accuracy benefit (Figure 9). This has direct practical implications for training pipeline design. The common practice of training in mixed precision and switching to higher precision for the final annealing phase adds engineering complexity (precision-switching logic in the training loop, maintaining multiple GEMM kernel configurations). The paper's result that this complexity yields no downstream accuracy gain—validated across eight diverse benchmarks—means future training pipelines can remain in low precision for the full horizon, simplifying implementation. This is an incremental but high-impact practical finding for any team training large models under compute constraints.

The investigation of NVFP4 training artifacts (zero-valued weight gradients, channel magnitude patterns in expert layers) also shifts the narrative around low-precision training from "necessary evil with acceptable tradeoffs" toward **"potentially beneficial accelerator of natural training dynamics."** The finding that BF16 training eventually produces the same channel specialization patterns, just more slowly (Figure 7), suggests that 4-bit training may act as implicit structural regularization—accelerating the pruning of redundant expert capacity rather than degrading model quality. This is not definitively proven (the paper is appropriately cautious, using phrases like "we believe"), but it opens the door to reframing low-precision training as a feature rather than a compromise. If this interpretation holds under further investigation, it would reverse the standard assumption that higher precision is always better for final model quality.

The shared-weight MTP contribution resolves a specific contradiction in the speculative decoding literature. Prior work on native speculative decoding via MTP (DeepSeek-AI, 2025c) showed that acceptance rates degrade rapidly at longer draft lengths due to training-inference distribution mismatch—the draft heads are trained on ground-truth hidden states but must operate on self-generated hidden states at inference time. This led to the implicit assumption that native speculative decoding was inherently limited to short draft lengths. The shared-weight design demonstrates that this limitation is not inherent—it is a consequence of the specific head architecture (independent offset-trained heads), and can be substantially mitigated by exposing a single shared head to multiple offsets during training. The widening acceptance gap at later draft indices (Figure 4, indices 4–7) specifically at the point where distribution shift is most severe provides direct evidence for the robustness mechanism. This changes the speculative decoding landscape by showing that native (no external draft model) speculative decoding can be effective at longer draft lengths, provided the head architecture is designed with autoregressive robustness in mind.

The work also reconciles a tension in agentic post-training between SFT efficiency and RL robustness. SFT on agentic trajectories is computationally cheap but degrades out-of-domain performance; end-to-end RL avoids OOD degradation but requires expensive online interactive rollouts. PivotRL (Yi et al., 2026) addresses this by reusing offline SFT trajectories and focusing RL on informative turns where the policy has uncertainty. While PivotRL is cited as prior work, its integration into the multi-environment RLVR framework and its application across all agentic domains (Agentic Programming, Search, Terminal Use, Conversational Tool Use) demonstrates that this tradeoff can be managed in practice at scale. This is likely to influence future agentic training pipelines away from pure SFT or pure RL toward hybrid approaches that leverage the strengths of both.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Isolating the LatentMoE contribution through direct architectural ablation.** The paper's central architectural claim—that LatentMoE improves accuracy per parameter and per FLOP compared to standard MoEs—is demonstrated only indirectly through comparisons against external models (GPT-OSS-120B, Qwen3.5-122B) that differ in many dimensions beyond the MoE design. A direct experiment would train Nemotron 3 Super against a version of itself with standard MoE layers (same total experts N=512, same top-K=22, same total parameters, but routing and expert computation in the full hidden dimension d=4096 rather than the latent dimension ℓ=1024). Training both variants on a shared subset of the pretraining data (e.g., 1T tokens to manage cost) and comparing both training perplexity and downstream benchmark accuracy would isolate whether the 4× compression ratio and corresponding capacity reallocation (from d to N and K) produces measurable quality gains at matched inference cost. Measuring throughput on identical hardware and inference framework for both variants would validate the claim that the compute savings from latent projection translate into real throughput gains.

**Shared-weight vs. independent-head MTP on a controlled base model.** The paper's MTP acceptance rate comparison (Table 2) pits Nemotron 3 Super against DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen3-Next—models with entirely different architectures, training data, and MTP implementations. A controlled experiment would train the same Nemotron 3 Super base model with two MTP variants: shared-weight (the paper's design) and independent-head (two separate heads trained at offsets 1 and 2), matched on total parameters, training data, and training duration. The key measurements would be: (a) training perplexity for both the main LM loss and the MTP auxiliary loss, (b) per-draft-index acceptance rates on SPEED-Bench at multiple draft lengths (1, 3, 5, 7, 10), and (c) the extent to which the acceptance gap between variants grows at later draft indices (the signature of distribution-shift robustness). A negative result—where independent heads match or exceed shared-weight acceptance—would refute the distribution-shift robustness mechanism and suggest other factors (base model quality, training data) explain the DeepSeek-R1 gap.

**Quantifying the two-stage SFT objective against single-stage baselines.** The paper claims that single-stage SFT with token-level normalization causes "marked degradation on long-input-short-output scenarios" and that the two-stage procedure corrects this, but provides no quantitative evidence. A direct ablation would train the SFT model with three variants on the same data blend: (a) single-stage token-level normalization only, (b) single-stage per-conversation normalization only, and (c) the two-stage procedure (token-level then per-conversation). Evaluation would measure not just accuracy on standard benchmarks but specifically: response length distributions (mean, median, variance of output tokens) across short-answer and long-answer tasks, accuracy on benchmarks where the correct answer is short (e.g., GSM8K with a numeric answer vs. a full reasoning trace), and instruction-following accuracy on tasks that explicitly request concise responses. This experiment would establish whether the two-stage design provides benefits beyond what either single-stage variant could achieve, and at what cost (e.g., does Stage 1 token-level training cause a verbosity bias that Stage 2 must then correct, or could per-conversation normalization alone suffice?).

**Multi-environment vs. single-environment RLVR with controlled task selection.** The paper's claim that "single-environment training leads to severe regressions on other benchmarks" is stated without quantitative support. A systematic experiment would train the SFT model with RLVR under three conditions: (a) math-only RLVR, (b) code-only RLVR, and (c) the full multi-environment mixture. All three would be evaluated on the full benchmark suite (not just the training task) to quantify regression magnitudes. A specific hypothesis to test: does the severity of regression correlate with task similarity? For instance, does math-only RLVR degrade code performance more or less than it degrades chat/instruction-following performance? Understanding this structure would inform future multi-task RL curriculum design—if regressions are worst for dissimilar tasks, then the environment mixture should be balanced across task clusters rather than uniformly sampled.

**NVFP4 training scaling laws—does low precision change the optimal model size / data tradeoff?** The paper demonstrates that NVFP4 training is feasible at 25T-token scale and that a 120B model trained in NVFP4 matches or exceeds BF16-trained baselines. But the deeper question is whether low-precision training changes the optimal allocation of compute between model size and data quantity. Prior scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) were derived under the assumption of higher-precision training. If NVFP4 effectively reduces the cost of training larger models (by compressing weights, activations, and gradients), the compute-optimal model size at a given FLOP budget might shift upward—you could afford more parameters for the same training cost. A scaling laws experiment would train Nemotron 3 Super variants at multiple sizes (e.g., 30B, 60B, 120B total parameters) in both NVFP4 and BF16 on the same data, measure the resulting loss-vs-compute curves, and test whether the NVFP4-trained models achieve lower loss at matched compute or whether the quantization noise shifts the optimal allocation toward more data rather than more parameters. This experiment would determine whether NVFP4 training is just a cost-saving mechanism (same models, cheaper) or actually enables training configurations that would be infeasible in higher precision.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Long-context agentic coding assistants with interactive latency requirements.** The paper's most directly actionable finding is that the hybrid Mamba-Attention LatentMoE architecture achieves 2.2× the throughput of GPT-OSS-120B at 8K input / 64K output with comparable SWE-Bench accuracy. For a production coding assistant that needs to process an entire repository (128K+ tokens of context) and generate multi-step patches while maintaining sub-second response times, this throughput gap determines deployment feasibility. A service that would require 4× B200 GPUs to serve GPT-OSS-120B at acceptable latency could be served with 2× B200 GPUs using Nemotron 3 Super—halving infrastructure cost. The NVFP4-quantized checkpoint further improves this ratio (Figure 1 shows NVFP4 at 1.0× relative throughput vs. the BF16 reference at 0.45× implied ratio). For on-device or edge deployment scenarios where GPU count is fixed, the difference between fitting the model in available memory with acceptable latency versus not is binary.

**Data generation for self-improvement pipelines at reduced cost.** The paper's comprehensive synthetic data generation pipelines—spanning CUDA kernel generation (100K samples), financial reasoning (366K pairs from SEC filings), multi-hop search (Wikidata-grounded trajectories with ~12 tool calls each), and 1.5M general-purpose tool-calling trajectories—demonstrate the model's capacity for large-scale data synthesis. Combined with the throughput advantage, this enables self-improvement loops where the model generates training data for itself or smaller models at lower cost. Specifically, using Nemotron 3 Super to generate SFT trajectories for distilling into smaller models (e.g., a 30B or 8B model for more constrained deployments) would be 2–7.5× cheaper in GPU-hours than using comparably-accurate dense models. The paper's SFT data blend (Figure 16) provides a concrete recipe for what data mixtures produce strong agentic capabilities; organizations replicating this pipeline with their own domain-specific tasks can use the same architecture to generate data at the throughput rates reported in Figure 1.

**Multi-environment RLVR as a template for domain-specific agent training.** The paper's post-training infrastructure—asynchronous GRPO across 21 environments with in-flight weight updates, PivotRL for agentic tasks, and isolated SWE-RL for long-horizon trajectories—provides a concrete, reproducible template for organizations training agents for specific domains. The key infrastructure decisions (decoupling training and inference workers, one-step policy lag with importance sampling masking, Apptainer-based SWE environment with memory watchdog and command blocklist) are described in sufficient detail to replicate. The finding that joint multi-environment training prevents the regressions that single-environment training causes (Section 3.2.1) has direct practical implications: organizations training agents for multiple tasks (e.g., a customer service agent that handles both database queries and terminal commands) should train jointly rather than sequentially. The specific environment mixture—math, code, STEM, safety, instruction following, long context, and agentic tool use—suggests that diversity across task types is important for stability, not just training on more variants of the same task.

**NVFP4 inference for large-scale batch processing.** The NVFP4-quantized checkpoint achieving 99.8% median accuracy relative to BF16 (Table 8) while providing 2.2× throughput (Figure 1) has immediate implications for batch inference workloads. Organizations processing millions of documents through an LLM pipeline—for summarization, information extraction, or data labeling—care about total tokens-per-second-per-dollar, not per-query latency. The NVFP4 checkpoint on B200 GPUs delivers the highest throughput among the evaluated models, with accuracy degradation typically under 1% across most benchmarks. The Mamba SSM cache quantization solution (FP16 with Philox<5> stochastic rounding) is particularly important here: batch processing of long documents would otherwise suffer from the verbosity increase documented in Table 9 (up to 40% more tokens generated for the same task), which would erode the throughput advantage. The stochastic rounding fix ensures that the model does not generate extra (wasteful) tokens when processing at scale. For a pipeline processing 1M documents of 128K tokens each, the difference between Navie FP16 cache (40% verbosity increase) and SR-corrected FP16 cache (~1–2% verbosity delta) is approximately 50B additional output tokens—directly translating to GPU-hours and cost.
