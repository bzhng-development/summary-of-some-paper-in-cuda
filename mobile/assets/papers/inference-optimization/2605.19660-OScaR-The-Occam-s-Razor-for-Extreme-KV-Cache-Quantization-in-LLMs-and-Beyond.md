# OScaR: The Occam's Razor for Extreme KV Cache Quantization in LLMs and Beyond

**ArXiv:** [2605.19660](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19660)

## 🎯 Pitch

Under extreme 2-bit quantization, the standard per-channel Key cache method collapses because it ignores a newly identified 'Token Norm Imbalance' that amplifies errors across tokens with wildly different magnitudes. OScaR fixes this with a crucial one-two punch—first rotating the outlier channel energy, then normalizing token norms—to achieve near-lossless INT2 performance, delivering a 3.0× decoding speedup and a 5.3× memory reduction without the complexity of existing quantization pipelines.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **OScaR** (**O**mni-**S**caled **Ca**nalized **R**otation), an accurate and lightweight KV cache quantization framework for X-LLMs—spanning text-only, multi-modal, and omni-modal models—that addresses the fundamental bottleneck of **Token Norm Imbalance** (TNI) in per-channel Key quantization under extreme compression. OScaR employs two mutually essential components: **Canalized Rotation** (a Hadamard transform that prevents scaling-induced outlier artifacts by redistributing outlier channel energy) followed by **Omni-Token Scaling** (token-wise ℓ₂ normalization that balances token norms across the sequence dimension to mitigate TNI). On LongBench-E with Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen3-8B under INT2 quantization, OScaR achieves the highest average accuracy among all quantized methods—outperforming the second-best by 1.01 percentage points—while delivering up to a 3.0× decoding speedup, 5.3× memory reduction, and 4.1× throughput increase over the BF16 FlashDecoding-v2 baseline, establishing that a streamlined, training-free framework can redefine the accuracy-efficiency Pareto front only when both rotation and token-wise scaling are applied jointly to prevent their respective failure modes.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Extreme Quantization of the KV Cache Sharply Degrades Under Existing Methods

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is deceptively specific yet practically consequential: **when you compress the Key-Value (KV) cache of large language models to extreme low bit-widths (e.g., INT2), the dominant per-channel quantization paradigm—which works well at moderate bit-widths—suffers a sharp and poorly understood fidelity collapse.** The paper identifies the root cause of this collapse as **Token Norm Imbalance (TNI)**, a structural property of attention states that systematically amplifies quantization error when shared quantization parameters must span token groups with highly divergent norms.

This is not merely a curiosity of quantization theory. The KV cache has become the **dominant memory bottleneck** in modern LLM inference, particularly as the field pushes toward long-context reasoning, streaming tasks, and multi-modal understanding. During autoregressive decoding, the KV cache stores the Key and Value projections for every previously generated token, growing linearly with sequence length. In memory-bound inference scenarios, this cache rapidly exhausts the High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) of modern accelerators—the paper notes that it can "dominate total memory consumption" (Section 1), severely restricting batch sizes and preventing efficient large-scale deployment. The practical stakes are high: without effective KV cache compression, long-context applications (document understanding, multi-turn dialogue, video processing) become economically infeasible, and throughput-sensitive deployments (high-traffic serving, edge devices) hit a memory wall.

### The Gap: Per-Channel Quantization Works at Moderate Compression, But Fails at the Extreme

The paper's framing presupposes a landscape where several quantization approaches already exist, each with known strengths and known failure modes:

**Per-token quantization** aligns naturally with the incremental dynamics of autoregressive decoding—each token's Key and Value vectors are quantized independently as they are generated. However, this approach is known to be **vulnerable to channel-wise outliers** in Key tensors. As the paper notes (Section 2.1), certain feature dimensions in Key states exhibit "disproportionately large magnitudes" relative to the rest—a sparse subset of channels can be orders of magnitude larger than the median. When per-token quantization is applied, these persistent channel-wise outliers expand the quantization step size for every token in that channel, degrading precision across the board.

**Per-channel quantization** emerged as the principled response to this problem. By computing quantization parameters (scale and zero-point) separately for each channel within a block, this approach "exploits intrinsic channel-wise outlier distributions to reduce quantization difficulty" (Section 2.1). The pioneering KIVI framework (Liu et al., 2024) introduced a block-wise per-channel strategy that has become the de facto standard: Key caches use per-channel grouping (a single scale/zero-point per channel across a block of tokens), while Value caches—which lack channel-wise outliers—retain per-token grouping. At moderate bit-widths (e.g., INT4), this hybrid scheme works well.

However, the paper identifies a critical gap: **the effectiveness of per-channel quantization "progressively diminishes under extreme compression"** (Section 1). At INT2, the reduced numerical resolution means that even modest token-wise disparities within a channel become catastrophic. The shared quantization parameters for a given channel block are determined by the min and max across all tokens in that block. If the block contains tokens with substantially different norms—even a single divergent token—the quantization range expands, and every token in that channel pays the price in reduced effective resolution. The paper argues that this failure mode is not an implementational oversight but a **fundamental vulnerability of the per-channel paradigm** itself.

**Rotation-based methods** (QuaRot, RotateKV) attempt to work around the problem by applying orthogonal transforms (typically Hadamard matrices) to redistribute outlier energy across all dimensions before applying per-token quantization. The idea is that after rotation, no single channel dominates, so per-token quantization becomes viable. While effective in theory, the paper notes that these methods still struggle under extreme compression because they do not address the token-level norm disparities that the paper identifies as the core issue.

**Recent sophisticated methods** (TurboQuant, QJL, PolarQuant) have pushed further by employing randomized rotations, residual error correction, and look-up-table-based quantization. The paper's assessment of these is pointed: while they "provide rigorous theoretical guarantees, their complex pipelines often result in high implementation overhead and practical deviations during deployment" (Section 2.1). The specific concern is that these methods introduce "substantial computational overhead and extra parameters, undermining practical viability" (Section 1). TurboQuant, for example, uses a dense rotation matrix (2dh operations per token, where d is the hidden dimension and h the head dimension), brute-force Lloyd-Max quantization, and residual handling—costs that the paper quantifies as being orders of magnitude higher than simpler approaches (Appendix K, Table 4).

### The Missing Diagnosis: Why Does Per-Channel Quantization Collapse? It's Token Norm Imbalance

The paper's central diagnostic contribution is the identification of **Token Norm Imbalance (TNI)** as the specific structural mechanism through which per-channel quantization fails under extreme compression. Prior work had acknowledged that token-level outliers exist—e.g., OTT and RotateKV trace and preserve a small number of outlier tokens at high precision—but the paper argues that this response was piecemeal and incomplete. The paper's analysis systematically demonstrates that TNI is not limited to a few easily identifiable outliers:

- It manifests as **attention sink tokens** with exceptionally low norms—these are tokens (often punctuation, separator tokens, or background patches in vision) that attract nearly all attention mass but produce near-zero Value vectors, a phenomenon well-documented in prior work (Xiao et al., 2023; Su et al., 2025). The paper shows these tokens are "sparse yet consistent" across Query, Key, and Value states (Section 4.1) and directly correspond to Attention Sink positions identified by prior studies (Appendix E).
- In multi-modal LLMs, TNI takes additional forms: **broader token norm variation** compared to text-only models (Figure 19), **inter-modality norm disparities** where text tokens and image patch tokens have systematically different norm magnitudes (Figure 20), and **exceptionally large-norm outlier tokens** that are the opposite of the low-norm attention sinks (Figure 21).
- The paper provides a **theoretical derivation** (Appendix G, Equation 11) showing that the reconstruction error of a per-channel quantization block is lower-bounded by the difference in L2 norms between the largest-norm and smallest-norm tokens in that block. The key insight is that this is not a worst-case bound that rarely matters—it is a structural property that *systematically* amplifies error whenever tokens with divergent norms co-occur in the same block.
- A **quantitative error analysis** (Appendix H, Table 2) confirms this empirically: under INT2 quantization, the presence of outlier tokens increases per-channel Key quantization error by approximately 35%, and mixed-modality token groups increase error by 140% for the visual component.

### Why Existing Approaches Are Insufficient

**OTT and RotateKV's outlier preservation** is a pragmatic but incomplete fix. By tracing a small number of outlier tokens (typically 5 per head) and storing them at full precision while quantizing the rest, these methods mitigate the worst-case error from the most egregious outliers. The paper identifies two problems with this approach. First, TNI is not limited to a handful of outlier tokens—the norm distribution is continuous and there are many tokens that, while not extreme enough to be flagged, still expand the quantization range significantly. Second, the mixed-precision storage introduces "hardware fragmentation and mixed-precision overheads, limiting the achievable effective compression" (Section 2.2). The small number of FP16 tokens requires separate memory management and kernel handling, reducing the practical throughput gains from quantization.

**Rotation-only methods** (QuaRot, RotateKV) fail to address TNI because, as the paper demonstrates empirically, rotation alone does not balance token norms. Figure 5 (Section 4.2) shows the Key L2 norm distribution after Canalized Rotation alone—the token norms remain highly imbalanced, with the same sparse low-norm tokens persisting. Rotation solves the channel-wise outlier problem (spreading large per-channel values across all dimensions) but does nothing to the per-token L2 norm, which is invariant under orthogonal transforms. The per-channel quantization paradigm still sees tokens of different norms sharing the same channel's quantization parameters, and the error amplification persists.

**Direct token-wise scaling** seems like the obvious fix—just normalize each token to unit norm before quantization. However, the paper identifies a subtle failure mode that it terms the **Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact** (Appendix I). The issue is that low-norm outlier tokens (e.g., Attention Sinks) have relatively uniform small entries across all channels, while normal tokens are dominated by specific outlier channels. When both are scaled to the same norm, the low-norm token is uniformly amplified and becomes an **artificial outlier** in channels where normal tokens have minimal magnitudes. The paper provides a concrete numerical example: scaling a token with values [0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1] and a normal token [1, 1, 1, 100] to unit norm makes the first token an outlier in channels 1–3 (0.5 vs. 0.01), expanding the per-channel quantization range by a factor of 50 and severely degrading precision for the normal token. This artifact "undermines per-channel quantization and cannot be resolved by merely adjusting the scaling target" (Appendix I).

### How OScaR Positions Itself

The paper frames OScaR as a **return to simplicity guided by Occam's Razor** in a field that has trended toward increasingly complex quantization pipelines. The core claim is that the elaborate auxiliary mechanisms employed by methods like TurboQuant—dense rotation matrices, brute-force Lloyd-Max optimization, residual error correction—are not merely inefficient but are addressing the *symptoms* of TNI rather than the root cause. The paper's position is that if you can safely neutralize TNI (and only TNI), then a simple per-channel quantizer suffices even at INT2 precision.

OScaR's two-component design directly follows from the diagnosis:

- **Canalized Rotation** (Hadamard transform) is necessary because it prevents the Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact. By redistributing the energy of outlier channels across all dimensions, it ensures that when subsequent token-wise scaling is applied, no token becomes an artificial outlier in any channel. The term "canalized" suggests that the rotation constrains or channels the token variance in a way that makes scaling safe.
- **Omni-Token Scaling** is necessary because it directly addresses TNI by normalizing token L2 norms. The term "omni" reflects its applicability across all modalities (text, vision, audio) and across all X-LLMs (text-only, multi-modal, omni-modal), encompassing the diverse TNI patterns identified in Section 4.1.

A crucial design choice that reflects the paper's philosophy is that **both components are mutually essential**—neither works without the other—but together they form a "highly streamlined" pipeline that is training-free, requires no auxiliary data structures, and involves only operations with efficient hardware implementations (fast Hadamard transform, reciprocal square root). The theoretical complexity analysis in Appendix K quantifies OScaR's decode cost at 123.0 million arithmetic operations per step (for d=4096, L=10,000), compared to 247.9 million for TurboQuant+ and 81.9 million for the minimal KIVI baseline—a deliberate middle ground that the paper argues achieves the best accuracy-efficiency tradeoff.

The paper explicitly positions OScaR not as yet another quantization method competing on a single axis, but as a framework that **redefines the Pareto front**—achieving accuracy comparable to or exceeding the 16-bit baseline under INT2 compression while maintaining computational costs substantially lower than sophisticated alternatives. This positioning is reinforced by the Pareto front analysis in Appendix U (Figure 9), where OScaR occupies an "advantageous position" that no other method achieves: higher accuracy than KIVI (the efficiency baseline) with costs substantially lower than TurboQuant+ (the accuracy-focused method).

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

OScaR is a **lightweight, training-free KV cache compression framework** that applies two simple transformations—a Hadamard rotation followed by token-wise normalization—to Key tensors before per-channel quantization, enabling accurate INT2 compression across text-only, multi-modal, and omni-modal large language models. The framework solves the problem that **Token Norm Imbalance (TNI)**—the presence of tokens with substantially different L2 norms in the same per-channel quantization block—systematically amplifies quantization error under extreme compression, and that direct token-wise scaling (the obvious fix) introduces a new artifact that OScaR's rotation step is specifically designed to prevent.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The OScaR system has four major processing stages, applied during both prefill and decoding:

1. **Canalized Rotation (Online)** — For every incoming Query and Key token, a Fast Hadamard Transform (FHT) is applied to redistribute the energy of outlier channels uniformly across all dimensions. For Keys, this is applied before quantization; for Queries, it is applied to maintain attention score equivalence. For Values, an offline Hadamard transform is fused into the weight matrices before inference to avoid runtime overhead.

2. **Omni-Token Scaling (Online, Keys Only)** — After rotation, the L2 norm of each Key token is computed, and the token is divided by this norm to produce a unit-length vector. The per-token norm scalar is stored as auxiliary metadata (one float per token) and re-multiplied during attention computation to restore the original magnitude.

3. **Per-Channel Quantization (Online)** — The normalized Key vectors are quantized to INT2 using per-channel grouping (a single scale and zero-point for each channel across a block of G=32 tokens). Value vectors are quantized per-token (since they lack channel-wise outliers). A residual buffer of R=128 tokens is maintained in full precision and periodically flushed to the packed quantized cache.

4. **Dequantization and Attention (Online)** — During decoding, quantized Keys are dequantized, multiplied by their stored token-wise norms, and combined with the Query (which has also been Hadamard-rotated) for standard attention computation. The Hadamard transforms on Query and Key cancel out inside the dot product, preserving the original attention scores exactly.

Information flows as: Input token → RMSNorm → projection to Q/K/V → **Canalized Rotation (FHT)** on Q and K → **Omni-Token Scaling** on K (compute norm, normalize) → **per-channel quantization** of K (INT2), **per-token quantization** of V → storage in packed cache with norm metadata → during attention, **dequantize** K, **multiply by stored norm**, compute attention with rotated Q.

- **Second**, the Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact—why direct token-wise scaling fails, explained through both the mathematical inequality (Equation 12) and a concrete numerical example. This motivates *why* Canalized Rotation must precede scaling.

#### The Theoretical Vulnerability: TNI-Induced Error Lower Bound

The paper begins not with its own method but with a theoretical analysis of why per-channel quantization fails under extreme compression. This analysis serves as the justification for the entire OScaR design—if TNI were not the root cause, then the proposed fix (token-wise scaling) would not be the right intervention.

**The quantization error model.** For a uniform b-bit quantizer, the quantization error `$\epsilon$` on any individual value is modeled as a random variable uniformly distributed over `$[-\Delta_{j,g}/2, \Delta_{j,g}/2]$`, where `$\Delta_{j,g}$` is the quantization step size for the `$j$`-th channel within block `$g$`. The mean squared error (MSE) for that channel-block follows from the variance of a uniform distribution:

$$\text{MSE}_{j,g} = \mathbb{E}[\epsilon^2] = \frac{1}{\Delta_{j,g}} \int_{-\Delta_{j,g}/2}^{\Delta_{j,g}/2} \epsilon^2 \, d\epsilon = \frac{\Delta_{j,g}^2}{12}$$

where `$\Delta_{j,g}$` is the quantization step size for channel `$j$` in block `$g$`, computed from the sample range `$R_{j,g} = \max_{t \in g} K_{t,j} - \min_{t \in g} K_{t,j}$` as `$\Delta_{j,g} = R_{j,g} / (2^b - 1)$`, and `$b$` is the bit width.

**What it computes:** the expected squared error per element in a single channel-block under the assumption that quantization errors are uniformly distributed within the quantization bin. The error is proportional to the square of the step size, which is in turn proportional to the square of the value range within that block.

**Why this form:** this is the standard quantization error model for uniform quantizers (see Nagel et al., 2021). The uniform distribution assumption is reasonable when values are roughly uniformly distributed within the quantization range, and the `$\Delta^2/12$` variance is the standard result.

**From per-element error to block-level lower bound.** The critical move in the paper's derivation is to lower-bound the per-element error in terms of the difference between any two tokens in the block. For any two tokens `$u$` and `$v$` in the same block `$g$`, the range `$R_{j,g}$` must be at least `$|K_{u,j} - K_{v,j}|$`. Substituting this into the MSE expression gives a pairwise lower bound:

$$\text{MSE}_{j,g} \geq \frac{(K_{u,j} - K_{v,j})^2}{12(2^b - 1)^2}$$

where `$K_{u,j}$` and `$K_{v,j}$` are the values of channel `$j$` for tokens `$u$` and `$v$` respectively.

**What it computes:** a conservative lower bound on the per-channel MSE based on the worst pair of tokens in the block—specifically, the difference between any two tokens' values in that channel.

**Why this form:** by lower-bounding the range by a pairwise difference, the derivation converts the block-level range (which depends on all tokens) into a pairwise quantity that can be summed across channels using the L2 norm. This is the key algebraic step that connects per-channel quantization error to token L2 norms.

**Summing across channels to get the L2 norm.** Let `$m = \arg\max_{t \in g} \|k_t\|_2$` and `$n = \arg\min_{t \in g} \|k_t\|_2$` be the tokens with the largest and smallest L2 norms in block `$g$`. Summing the pairwise bound over all `$d$` channels for this specific token pair and applying the triangle inequality yields the block-level lower bound:

$$\text{MSE}_g \triangleq \frac{1}{|g|} \sum_{t \in g} \text{MSE}_t \gtrsim \frac{1}{12(2^b - 1)^2} \sum_{j=1}^{d} (K_{m,j} - K_{n,j})^2 = \frac{\|k_m - k_n\|_2^2}{12(2^b - 1)^2} \geq \frac{(\|k_m\|_2 - \|k_n\|_2)^2}{12(2^b - 1)^2}$$

where `$|g|$` is the block size (number of tokens in the block), `$\text{MSE}_t = \sum_{j=1}^{d} \text{MSE}_{j,g,t}$` is the total quantization error across all channels for token `$t$`, `$k_m$` and `$k_n$` are the Key vectors for the maximum-norm and minimum-norm tokens respectively, and `$\|\cdot\|_2$` denotes the L2 norm.

**What it computes:** a conservative lower bound on the average reconstruction error across all tokens in a per-channel quantization block. The bound is proportional to the square of the L2 norm difference between the largest-norm and smallest-norm tokens in that block, divided by `$12(2^b - 1)^2$`.

**Why this form:** this is the paper's key theoretical insight. The last inequality uses the reverse triangle inequality `$\|k_m - k_n\|_2 \geq |\|k_m\|_2 - \|k_n\|_2|$` to express the bound purely in terms of token L2 norms, without needing to examine individual channel values. This reveals that the fundamental vulnerability of per-channel quantization is **not** the presence of per-channel outliers (which per-channel quantization was designed to handle) but rather the **range of token L2 norms** within a block. When a block contains both a high-norm token and a low-norm token (TNI), the quantization error is guaranteed to be large, regardless of how well the per-channel scheme handles within-channel variation. This explains why per-channel quantization works at moderate bit-widths (the `$1/(2^b - 1)^2$` factor is small enough to tolerate typical norm disparities) but collapses at extreme bit-widths (where the factor becomes large and the norm disparity term dominates).

#### The Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact: Why Direct Token-Wise Scaling Fails

Given that TNI is the root cause, the obvious remedy is to normalize all token norms to a common value before quantization—simply divide each Key token by its L2 norm. The paper demonstrates that this straightforward approach catastrophically fails, and the failure mechanism is a specific, identifiable artifact that Canalized Rotation is designed to prevent.

**Mathematical formulation of the artifact.** Consider two tokens in the same block: a "normal" token `$a \in \mathbb{R}^d$` that is dominated by an outlier channel, and a "low-norm" token `$b \in \mathbb{R}^d$` (an Attention Sink) with relatively uniform small entries. Without loss of generality, assume channel `$d$` is the outlier channel for `$a$`, so `$a_d \gg a_j$` for all `$j \neq d$`, and thus `$\|a\|_2 \approx |a_d|$`. For the low-norm token, `$b_j \approx c$` for all `$j$` (a small constant), so `$\|b\|_2 \approx \sqrt{d} \cdot c$`. Scaling both tokens to a target norm `$N$` yields scaling factors `$\alpha = N/\|b\|_2$` (large, because `$\|b\|_2$` is small) and `$\beta = N/\|a\|_2$` (small, because `$\|a\|_2$` is large), producing scaled vectors `$a' = \beta a$` and `$b' = \alpha b$`.

The artifact condition is that `$b'$` becomes an outlier in channels where `$a'$` is small:

$$\alpha c \gg \beta \max_{j \neq d} a_j$$

where `$\alpha$` is the scaling factor for the low-norm token, `$c$` is the approximately-uniform small value of the low-norm token's entries, `$\beta$` is the scaling factor for the normal token, and `$\max_{j \neq d} a_j$` is the maximum absolute value of the normal token in non-outlier channels.

**What it computes:** a condition that describes when the scaled low-norm token exceeds the scaled normal token in the normal token's weak channels, creating an artificial outlier that expands the per-channel quantization range.

**Why this form:** the ratio `$\alpha/\beta = \|a\|_2/\|b\|_2 \gg 1$` (since the normal token has much larger norm than the low-norm token). For channels where the normal token has small values (all channels except the outlier channel `$d$`), the low-norm token after scaling (`$\alpha c$`) will be much larger than the normal token after scaling (`$\beta a_j$`), even though before scaling the relationship was the opposite. This creates a perverse situation: scaling, which is intended to *reduce* norm disparities, instead *creates* new outliers in the channel dimension—precisely the thing per-channel quantization is vulnerable to.

**Numerical example.** The paper provides a concrete demonstration. Set target norm `$N = 1$`, define `$a = [1, 1, 1, 100]$` (a normal token dominated by channel 4) and `$b = [0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1]$` (a uniformly-small low-norm token). Then `$\|a\|_2 \approx 100.015$` and `$\|b\|_2 = 0.2$`, yielding `$\beta \approx 0.01$` and `$\alpha = 5$`. After scaling:

$$a' \approx [0.01, 0.01, 0.01, 1.00], \quad b' = [0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5]$$

In channels 1–3, the low-norm token `$b'$` (now 0.5) dominates the normal token `$a'$` (now 0.01) by a factor of 50. The per-channel quantization range for these channels expands from approximately 0.01 to 0.5—a 50× increase—severely degrading precision for the normal tokens that carry the meaningful information.

**Why this matters for OScaR's design.** The Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact is the reason Canalized Rotation must precede Omni-Token Scaling. The rotation step redistributes the outlier channel energy (the 100 in channel 4 of token `$a$`) across all dimensions, so that after rotation, the normal token no longer has a single dominant channel. Both the normal and low-norm tokens have relatively uniform entry distributions after rotation, so when scaling is applied, no artificial outliers are created. The paper's ablation (Table 9) confirms this empirically: applying Omni-Token Scaling alone (without Canalized Rotation) degrades performance from the already-poor INT2 baseline, while applying both components together recovers near-lossless accuracy.

#### Canalized Rotation: The Hadamard Transform

**Why rotation and why Hadamard.** The role of Canalized Rotation is to eliminate the channel-wise outlier structure that makes token-wise scaling unsafe. By applying an orthogonal transform—specifically, a Hadamard matrix—the energy concentrated in outlier channels is spread uniformly across all dimensions. After rotation, every channel has approximately equal magnitude for every token, so the per-channel distribution is smooth and token-wise scaling will not create artificial outliers.

The Hadamard matrix is chosen over other orthogonal transforms (e.g., random rotations, Householder reflections) for two practical reasons. First, the **Fast Hadamard Transform (FHT)** can be computed in `$O(d \log d)$` operations using a butterfly structure, compared to `$O(d^2)$` for a general matrix multiplication—this is a substantial efficiency gain for the per-token online computation. Second, Hadamard matrices are **orthogonal and self-inverse** (up to scaling), which means they preserve inner products: `$\langle Hx, Hy \rangle = d \cdot \langle x, y \rangle$` for a `$d \times d$` Hadamard matrix `$H$`, assuming the standard normalization. This property is critical for attention computation, as it ensures that the rotation does not distort the attention scores.

**Mathematical form.** For a Key token `$k \in \mathbb{R}^d$` with head dimension `$d$` (typically `$d = 128$` in the paper's experiments), the Canalized Rotation computes:

$$k_h = \frac{H(k)}{\sqrt{D}}$$

where `$H$` is the `$d \times d$` Hadamard matrix, `$D$` is the head dimension (128), and `$k_h$` is the rotated Key vector. The division by `$\sqrt{D}$` ensures that the transform preserves the L2 norm: `$\|k_h\|_2 = \|k\|_2$`.

**What it computes:** a normalized orthogonal transformation that redistributes the energy of each Key token uniformly across all `$d$` dimensions. Before rotation, a Key token might have channel 42 at magnitude 100 and all other channels at magnitude ~1; after rotation, all `$d$` channels have approximately equal magnitude (roughly `$\|k\|_2 / \sqrt{d}$`), because the outlier channel's energy has been spread across the entire vector by the Hadamard mixing.

**Why this form:** the Hadamard transform is the standard choice for energy redistribution in quantization (used by QuaRot, RotateKV, and others) because of its computational efficiency and orthogonality. The `$\sqrt{D}$` normalization is necessary to keep the transform norm-preserving—without it, the Hadamard matrix would scale the norm by `$\sqrt{D}$` (since all entries are `$\pm 1$` and each row has `$D$` entries), which would distort attention scores. The paper uses `$D$` (the head dimension, typically 128) rather than the full hidden dimension (typically 4096) because the Hadamard transform is applied per-head—each attention head's Key vector of dimension `$d_h = 128$` is transformed independently.

**Interaction with RoPE.** The paper notes that Canalized Rotation "requires online computation in the presence of RoPE, which precludes offline fusion of Query and Key Hadamard transforms with weight matrices" (Appendix A). In standard transformers, Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) is applied to Query and Key vectors after projection but before attention. If there were no RoPE, the Hadamard transform could be absorbed into the weight matrices offline—compute `$W_K' = H W_K$` and `$W_Q' = H W_Q$` once before inference, eliminating the online rotation cost. However, because RoPE applies a position-dependent rotation to specific pairs of dimensions, the Hadamard transform must be applied *after* RoPE, which means it cannot be fused into the weights and must be computed online for every token. The paper mitigates this cost through efficient CUDA kernel design (fused FHT + scaling kernels using Tensor Cores, as described in Section 4.3).

**Query rotation.** The Query is also Hadamard-rotated during inference, but crucially it is **not** token-wise scaled. The rotation is applied purely to maintain attention score equivalence:

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{(Q H^T) (H K^T)}{\sqrt{d}}\right)$$

Since `$H^T H = H H = D \cdot I$` for a Hadamard matrix (it is symmetric and self-inverse up to scaling), the inner product `$(Q H^T)(H K^T) = Q (H^T H) K^T = D \cdot Q K^T$`. The additional factor of `$D$` is absorbed by the `$\sqrt{D}$` normalization in the Key rotation and the standard `$1/\sqrt{d}$` attention scaling. The paper's implementation applies `$H/\sqrt{D}$` to both Query and Key, so the inner product becomes `$(Q H^T/\sqrt{D})(H K/\sqrt{D}) = Q K^T / D$`, and the attention scaling `$1/\sqrt{D}$` (since `$d = D$` per head) brings it to `$Q K^T / D^{3/2}$`. The exact normalization constants are handled in the fused CUDA kernels to ensure mathematical equivalence with the unrotated attention.

**Value rotation (offline).** For Value tensors, the paper applies an offline Hadamard transform to both the Value projection weight `$W_V$` and the attention output weight `$W_O$` before inference, following the approach of QuaRot:

$$W_V \leftarrow W_V H, \quad W_O \leftarrow H W_O$$

where `$W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` is the Value projection weight, `$W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` is the attention output projection weight, and `$H$` is the Hadamard matrix. This offline transform means that during inference, the Value vectors are already Hadamard-rotated (because `$V = X W_V = X (W_V^{\text{orig}} H) = (X W_V^{\text{orig}}) H$`), and the output projection undoes the rotation (because `$\text{out} = \text{Attention}(Q, K) V W_O = \text{Attention}(Q, K) V (H W_O^{\text{orig}}) = (\text{Attention}(Q, K) (V H)) W_O^{\text{orig}}$`, and the `$H$` cancels). The paper notes that this "improves the fidelity of Value quantization without introducing additional runtime overhead" (Section 4.3), because the Hadamard transform smooths the Value distribution similarly to how it smooths Keys, but the cost is paid once offline rather than per-token online.

**Efficiency considerations.** The FHT is implemented using the **HadaCore** library (Agarwal et al., 2024) for Tensor Core acceleration. For head dimension `$d_h = 128$`, HadaCore exploits the Kronecker decomposition `$H_{128} = H_8 \otimes H_{16}$`, applying `$H_{16}$` via WMMA (Warp Matrix Multiply-Accumulate) tiles on Tensor Cores and `$H_8$` via scalar butterfly operations. This reduces scalar instruction pressure compared to a naive butterfly FHT. The theoretical cost of the FHT for one `$d$`-dimensional vector is `$2d / \log_2 h$` arithmetic operations (approximately `$2 \times 4096 / 7 \approx 1170$` operations for the full hidden dimension), compared to `$2d^2$` for a dense matrix multiplication—a roughly 7× reduction for `$d = 128$`.

#### Omni-Token Scaling: Token-Wise Normalization

**The normalization procedure.** After Canalized Rotation, each Key token `$k_h \in \mathbb{R}^d$` (where `$d$` is the full hidden dimension across all heads, typically 4096) undergoes token-wise L2 normalization:

$$n_k = \|k_h\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d} (k_h^{(i)})^2}, \quad k_u = \frac{k_h}{n_k}$$

where `$k_h^{(i)}$` is the `$i$`-th component of the rotated Key token, `$n_k \in \mathbb{R}^+$` is the scalar L2 norm (a single positive real number per token), and `$k_u \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is the unit-normalized Key vector with `$\|k_u\|_2 = 1$`.

**What it computes:** a normalization that maps every Key token, regardless of its original magnitude, to a point on the unit hypersphere in `$\mathbb{R}^d$`. The original magnitude is preserved separately as the scalar `$n_k$`, which is stored alongside the quantized unit vector.

**Why this form:** dividing by the L2 norm is the most natural way to equalize token magnitudes. After this normalization, every token in every block has exactly the same L2 norm (1.0), so the TNI-induced error lower bound from Equation 11 collapses to zero—the difference between the maximum and minimum token norms in any block is exactly 0. This directly addresses the root cause identified in Section 4.1. The L2 norm is chosen over alternatives (max absolute value, mean absolute value) based on the ablation in Appendix T (Table 10): the L2 norm and its rsqrt approximation achieve the best and mutually comparable performance across all model families, while max-value normalization causes catastrophic degradation (e.g., collapsing accuracy from 42.57 to 14.47 on Qwen2.5-7B for LongBench-E).

**Rsqrt implementation.** In the actual CUDA implementation, the paper uses the hardware-accelerated **rsqrt** (reciprocal square root) instruction rather than explicitly computing the L2 norm followed by division:

$$k_u = k_h \cdot \text{rsqrt}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d} (k_h^{(i)})^2\right)$$

The rsqrt instruction computes `$1/\sqrt{x}$` in hardware with high throughput, avoiding the explicit square root and division operations. The paper's ablation (Table 10) shows that the rsqrt approximation produces results "mutually comparable" to the exact L2 norm, making it the preferred implementation for efficiency.

**Norm metadata storage.** The scalar `$n_k$` (one float per token) is stored in the KV cache as auxiliary metadata. For a sequence of length `$S$`, this adds `$S \times 4$` bytes of overhead—negligible compared to the `$S \times d \times 2$` bits (INT2) of the quantized Key cache itself. During attention computation, the stored norm is re-multiplied with the dequantized unit vector to restore the original Key magnitude:

$$k_{\text{reconstructed}} = \text{dequant}(k_u) \cdot n_k$$

This reconstruction preserves the exact L2 norm of the original Key token (before any transformation), ensuring that attention scores are mathematically equivalent to the unquantized case.

**Why norms work with Hadamard rotation.** The critical property that makes this pipeline work is that Canalized Rotation (Hadamard transform) is applied **before** Omni-Token Scaling. Because the Hadamard transform is orthogonal (up to the `$\sqrt{D}$` normalization), it preserves L2 norms: `$\|k_h\|_2 = \|k\|_2$` where `$k$` is the original Key token. This means that the norm `$n_k$` computed from `$k_h$` is identical to the norm of the original Key token, and multiplying the dequantized unit vector by `$n_k$` reconstructs the original Key magnitude exactly. If the order were reversed (scaling first, then rotation), the rotation would mix the uniform entries of the scaled token and potentially recreate channel-wise structure—this is why Canalized Rotation must precede Omni-Token Scaling.

**Why norms cancel in attention.** During attention computation, the Query is Hadamard-rotated but **not** normalized:

$$\text{logits} = \left(Q_h \cdot \text{dequant}(k_u)\right) \cdot n_k$$

where `$Q_h = H(Q)/\sqrt{D}$` is the rotated Query. Expanding: `$Q_h \cdot k_u = (H(Q)/\sqrt{D}) \cdot (H^{-1}(k)/n_k) \cdot (\sqrt{D})$` (accounting for the `$\sqrt{D}$` normalization in the Hadamard transform), which simplifies to `$(Q \cdot k) / n_k$`. Multiplying by `$n_k$` yields `$Q \cdot k$`—exactly the original dot product. The `$\sqrt{D}$` factors cancel because both Query and Key are transformed with the same `$H/\sqrt{D}$` operation. This means OScaR's transformations are **mathematically lossless for the attention scores**—the only source of error is the INT2 quantization of the unit-normalized Key vectors.

**The "omni" in Omni-Token Scaling.** The paper emphasizes that this scaling operates across all modalities uniformly. In multi-modal LLMs, where a single sequence contains text tokens, image patch tokens, and potentially audio tokens with systematically different norm distributions (as shown in Figures 19–21), the token-wise normalization handles all modalities identically—each token is individually normalized to unit length regardless of its modality origin. This is why the framework is called "Omni-Scaled": it applies omnidirectional sequence-level normalization that naturally handles the diverse TNI patterns identified across X-LLMs without requiring modality-specific logic.

#### Per-Channel Quantization Pipeline

**Block-wise per-channel Key quantization.** After Canalized Rotation and Omni-Token Scaling, the unit-normalized Key vectors `$k_u$` are quantized using per-channel grouping. For a Key cache `$K_u \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times d}$` (where `$S$` is the sequence length and `$d$` is the hidden dimension), each channel is partitioned into consecutive blocks of size `$G = 32$` tokens. For the `$j$`-th channel within block `$g$`, the quantization parameters are:

$$\Delta_{j,g} = \frac{\max_{i \in g} K_{u,i,j} - \min_{i \in g} K_{u,i,j}}{2^b - 1}, \quad z_{j,g} = \left\lfloor -\frac{\min_{i \in g} K_{u,i,j}}{\Delta_{j,g}} \right\rceil$$

where `$\Delta_{j,g}$` is the quantization step size (a single float per channel per block), `$z_{j,g}$` is the integer zero-point (computed via rounding, stored as an integer), `$b = 2$` is the bit width (INT2, yielding 4 possible values: 0, 1, 2, 3), and the min and max are computed over the `$G = 32$` tokens in block `$g$` for channel `$j$`.

Each element is then quantized and reconstructed as:

$$Q(K_{u,i,j}) = \text{clamp}\left(\left\lfloor \frac{K_{u,i,j}}{\Delta_{j,g}} \right\rceil + z_{j,g}, 0, 2^b - 1\right), \quad \hat{K}_{u,i,j} = \Delta_{j,g} \cdot (Q(K_{u,i,j}) - z_{j,g})$$

where `$Q(K_{u,i,j}) \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$` is the 2-bit integer representation, and `$\hat{K}_{u,i,j}$` is the reconstructed floating-point value after dequantization.

**What this computes:** a standard uniform asymmetric quantizer that maps floating-point values in a per-channel block to 2-bit integers using channel-specific scale and zero-point parameters. The asymmetric formulation (with zero-point) allows the quantization grid to be shifted, which is important because the unit-normalized Key vectors have both positive and negative entries (they are not all non-negative).

**Why this form:** this is the standard per-channel quantization scheme used by KIVI and other methods. The group size `$G = 32$` balances two competing factors: larger groups reduce the storage overhead of quantization parameters (one scale and zero-point per channel per group), but smaller groups reduce the within-group value range and thus reduce quantization error. The choice of 32 is standard in the KV cache quantization literature and aligns with the warp size on NVIDIA GPUs, enabling efficient SIMD processing. The block-wise organization is necessary to support autoregressive decoding, where new tokens are continuously appended—it allows quantizing fixed-size blocks as they fill up rather than requiring the entire cache to be re-quantized.

**Residual buffer mechanism.** To support continuous per-channel quantization during autoregressive generation, OScaR maintains a **high-precision residual window** of `$R = 128$` tokens, following the KIVI design. Newly generated Key tokens are first stored in this residual buffer in full precision (FP16). Only when the buffer accumulates `$R$` tokens does the system quantize the entire block and flush it to the packed INT2 cache. This mechanism is necessary because per-channel quantization requires a block of tokens to compute channel-wise min/max values—you cannot quantize a single new token in isolation without knowing the value range across the block.

The relationship between `$G$` and `$R$` is: the residual buffer of `$R = 128$` tokens is partitioned into `$R/G = 128/32 = 4$` quantization groups along the token dimension for each channel. When flushing, all 128 tokens are quantized at once, producing 4 groups per channel.

**Per-token Value quantization.** Value states lack channel-wise outliers (as shown in Figure 2b), so Value quantization uses per-token grouping rather than per-channel grouping. For each token, the 128-dimensional Value vector (per head) is partitioned into groups of size `$G = 32$` along the head dimension, and each group gets its own scale and zero-point. This means a single Value token-head gets `$128/32 = 4$` quantization parameter sets. Since per-token quantization does not suffer from cross-token interference (each token's quantization parameters are computed from only that token's values), TNI does not affect Value quantization—a finding confirmed by the quantitative error analysis in Appendix H (Table 2), which shows that outlier tokens have negligible impact on per-token Value quantization error.

**Quantization parameter overhead.** The paper accounts for the storage overhead of quantization parameters. For INT2 Key quantization with per-channel grouping and block size `$G = 32$`, each group of 32 tokens × 1 channel = 32 elements requires one scale (FP16, 2 bytes) and one zero-point (typically stored as FP16 or integer). For a sequence of length `$S$`, the number of blocks per channel is `$S/G = S/32$`, and with `$d$` channels, the total parameter storage is approximately `$d \times (S/32) \times 4$` bytes (assuming 2 bytes each for scale and zero-point). The effective bit-width including parameters is slightly above 2 bits per element—the paper accounts for this in comparing methods, noting that TurboQuant uses 2.5 bits while all other methods use 2 bits plus parameter overhead.

#### CUDA Kernel Design and System Optimizations

**Fused Hadamard-Norm kernel.** The most performance-critical kernel in OScaR is the fused preprocessing kernel that applies Canalized Rotation and Omni-Token Scaling to Key tensors in a single CUDA launch. The kernel operates on Key tensors of shape `[batch, seqlen, n_kv_heads, d_h]` with FP16 or BF16 data type and performs the following fused operations:

1. Read all KV heads of a single token from global memory into registers/shared memory.
2. Apply the normalized Hadamard transform (`$H/\sqrt{D}$`) to each head independently, using HadaCore's Tensor Core implementation (WMMA tiles for `$H_{16}$`, scalar butterfly for `$H_8$`).
3. Accumulate the sum of squares across all heads and all dimensions using a parallel reduction.
4. Compute the token-wise norm `$n_k$` using the rsqrt instruction on the accumulated sum of squares.
5. Scale the transformed key values by `$n_k$` (equivalent to dividing by the L2 norm) and write the normalized keys and the norm scalar to output buffers.

One CUDA block processes one token with 128 threads for the common head dimension `$d_h = 128$`. The fusion of Hadamard, reduction, and scaling into a single kernel eliminates intermediate global memory writes and reduces kernel launch overhead.

**Query-side kernel.** The same kernel is used for Query processing, but with only the Hadamard transform applied (no normalization, no reduction). This ensures that the Query receives the identical `$H/\sqrt{D}$` transform applied to Keys, maintaining mathematical equivalence.

**Quantization kernel.** A separate kernel handles the quantization of both Key and Value caches. For Key quantization, it operates on the already-normalized unit vectors `$k_u$` and performs:
- Per-channel min/max computation within each group of `$G = 32$` tokens.
- Scale and zero-point computation from min/max.
- Quantization to 2-bit integers and packing: eight 2-bit values are packed into one uint16 word.

For Value quantization, the kernel operates on the (offline-Hadamard-transformed) Value vectors and performs per-token grouping along the head dimension.

**Dequantization, De-Scaling, and Attention kernel.** The attention kernel is the most complex, as it must handle both the packed INT2 cache and the FP16 residual cache simultaneously:

1. For packed Key entries: dequantize from INT2 using the stored per-channel scales and zero-points, producing `$\hat{k}_u$` (the reconstructed unit vector). Multiply by the stored token-wise norm `$n_k$` to restore the original Key magnitude.
2. For residual Key entries: the keys are already in FP16 and normalized; simply multiply by the stored norm.
3. Combine both contributions in the standard attention computation: compute `$Q_h \cdot K_{\text{all}}^T$`, scale, apply softmax, and compute the weighted sum of Values.
4. Apply the output projection `$W_O$`.

The kernel uses the BitDecoding framework (Du et al., 2025) as its backbone for efficient INT2 dequantization on Tensor Cores, extended with the norm multiplication step.

**Prefill and decode workflows.** During the prefill phase, attention computation itself runs unmodified using FlashAttention-2 for efficiency—the Hadamard-norm preprocessing and quantization are applied *after* attention to the full prompt sequence. The prompt is split into a prefix (length a multiple of `$R = 128$`) that is quantized and stored in the packed cache, and a tail (length less than `$R$`) that is stored in the residual cache unquantized.

During the decode phase, each new token undergoes:
1. Projection to Q, K, V via weight matrices.
2. RMSNorm and RoPE application (standard transformer operations).
3. Hadamard transform on Q (`$H/\sqrt{D}$`).
4. Fused Hadamard-Norm on K (`$H/\sqrt{D}$` + rsqrt normalization + norm storage).
5. The normalized Key and norm, plus the rotated Value, are appended to the residual cache.
6. The attention kernel processes both packed and residual caches as described above.
7. When the residual cache reaches `$R = 128$` tokens, the residual block is quantized (Keys already normalized, so only quantization and packing needed—no repeated Hadamard-norm) and flushed to the packed cache, and the residual buffer is cleared.

**Residual flush optimization.** Because residual Keys are already normalized (the Hadamard-norm was applied when they were first generated), flushing a 128-token block only requires quantization and packing—no repeated Hadamard or normalization computation. For `$R = 128$` and `$G = 32$`, flushing produces 16 packed rows (128/8, since 8 2-bit values pack into one uint16) and 4 parameter rows (128/32 groups per channel). This flush operation is substantially cheaper than re-processing the tokens, contributing to OScaR's overall efficiency.

**Memory footprint.** The paper reports that OScaR reduces the KV cache memory footprint by up to **5.3×** compared to the BF16 FlashDecoding-v2 baseline (at batch size 48, context length 4K on Qwen3-8B). This reduction comes from the combination of INT2 quantization (8× compression of the Key and Value payload, from 16 bits to 2 bits per element) offset by the overhead of quantization parameters, norm metadata, and the residual buffer. The effective compression ratio of approximately 5.3× rather than 8× reflects these overheads.

**Latency and throughput.** At context length 128K with batch size 1 on a single H20 GPU (141GB), OScaR achieves a **3.0× decoding speedup** (30.9 ms/token vs. 92.9 ms/token for BF16 FlashDecoding-v2). At batch size 48 with context length 4K, throughput increases by **4.1×** (1354 tokens/s vs. 331 tokens/s). These efficiency gains are attributed to reduced memory bandwidth pressure from the compressed KV cache, which is the primary bottleneck in memory-bound decoding—the INT2 cache requires 8× fewer bytes to read per attention operation, allowing the GPU's compute units to be fed more efficiently.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Token Norm Imbalance (TNI) as the Unifying Causal Diagnosis for Extreme Quantization Failure

The paper's most fundamental contribution is not a method but a **diagnostic concept**: the identification of Token Norm Imbalance (TNI) as the specific structural mechanism through which per-channel Key quantization collapses under extreme compression. Before OScaR, the field understood that per-channel quantization degraded at INT2, but the causal narrative was fragmented—different papers attributed the problem to different phenomena (channel-wise outliers, attention sink tokens, insufficient rotation, inadequate residual correction) and proposed correspondingly different (and increasingly complex) fixes.

What OScaR's analysis reveals is that these disparate phenomena are all **manifestations of a single underlying structural property**: tokens with substantially different L2 norms sharing the same per-channel quantization block. The paper demonstrates this through three converging lines of evidence:

- **Empirical profiling** (Section 4.1, Appendix D, F): TNI is universal across text-only models (Llama-2-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-8B) and multi-modal models (Qwen3-VL, LLaVA), manifesting not just as attention sink tokens with low norms but also as inter-modality norm disparities (text vs. image patches having systematically different magnitudes) and large-norm outlier tokens in vision-language models. This universality suggests TNI is not a quirk of specific architectures but a consequence of how attention mechanisms allocate representational capacity.

- **Theoretical derivation** (Appendix G, Equation 11): the average reconstruction error in a per-channel quantization block is lower-bounded by `(‖k_max‖₂ - ‖k_min‖₂)² / (12(2^b - 1)²)—`a quantity that grows with the square of the token norm range within the block. This is not a loose worst-case bound that rarely matters; it is a **structural guarantee** that any block containing a high-norm and a low-norm token will suffer elevated quantization error, with the severity scaling inversely with bit-width squared.

- **Quantitative ablation** (Appendix H, Table 2): under INT2, the presence of outlier tokens increases per-channel Key quantization error by approximately 35%, and mixed-modality blocks increase error by 140% for visual tokens. These error magnitudes are sufficient to explain the performance collapse observed in practice.

The intellectual significance of this diagnosis is that it **reframes the problem from one of channel-wise outlier mitigation to one of token-wise norm equalization**. Prior work (OTT, RotateKV) treated outlier tokens as exceptional cases to be handled through mixed-precision preservation—a strategy that is inherently limited because TNI is a continuous distribution, not a binary outlier/non-outlier distinction. Other methods (QuaRot, RotateKV) redistributed channel-wise energy through rotation but left token norms untouched, missing the actual error amplification mechanism. TurboQuant and related methods added layers of complexity (dense rotations, residual correction, LUT-based quantization) that implicitly addressed TNI through brute-force error reduction rather than through a principled understanding of its cause.

This is a **fundamental diagnostic shift** rather than an incremental improvement. The paper doesn't just say "our method works better"; it says "here is why all previous methods were attacking the wrong target, and here is a single structural property that explains their varying degrees of failure." The fact that this diagnosis spans text-only, multi-modal, and omni-modal models—with TNI manifesting in modality-specific patterns that all converge on the same error mechanism—elevates it from a domain-specific observation to a general principle of KV cache quantization.

---

### Innovation 2: The Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact as a Necessary Design Constraint

The paper's second distinctive contribution is the identification and formalization of the **Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact**—a failure mode of direct token-wise scaling that is non-obvious, mathematically precise, and **dictates a necessary ordering of operations** (rotation must precede scaling) that no prior work had articulated.

At first glance, mitigating TNI seems trivial: if token norm disparities are the problem, just normalize all tokens to the same norm before quantization. The paper shows that this naive fix catastrophically fails, and the failure mechanism is subtle enough that it could easily be mistaken for TNI persisting despite normalization or for some unrelated numerical issue. By providing both a mathematical characterization (Equation 12, Appendix I) and a concrete numerical demonstration (`[1,1,1,100]` vs. `[0.1,0.1,0.1,0.1]` scaling to unit norm creates a 50× artificial outlier), the paper transforms what could have been a puzzling empirical result into a **design axiom**: any token-wise normalization applied to Key tensors before per-channel quantization must be preceded by a transformation that eliminates channel-wise outlier structure, or it will create new per-channel artifacts that are equally damaging.

Why is this intellectually distinctive rather than just a practical observation? Because it reveals a **fundamental tension between two types of quantization robustness** that had not been previously recognized:

- **Robustness to token-wise outliers** (TNI) demands token-wise normalization to equalize norms.
- **Robustness to channel-wise outliers** demands that channel distributions be smooth, because per-channel quantization computes parameters separately for each channel.

These two desiderata are in direct conflict when token-wise scaling is applied naively, because scaling a token with an outlier channel compresses that channel's value while amplifying all other channels. The Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact is the precise mechanism of this conflict: a token that was *harmless* in per-channel terms (its channel-wise values were small everywhere except one outlier dimension) becomes *harmful* after scaling (its uniformly amplified entries become artificial outliers in the channels where normal tokens have small values).

Prior work had addressed channel-wise outliers (via rotation) and token-wise outliers (via mixed-precision preservation) as **independent problems** that could be solved independently and combined additively. OScaR's insight is that they are **entangled**: fixing one (token norm equalization) can re-activate the other (creating channel-wise artifacts) unless the channel-wise structure is first neutralized. The requirement that Canalized Rotation must precede Omni-Token Scaling is not an implementation detail but a logical necessity deriving from this entanglement.

This is a **conceptual advance** of moderate scope: it does not introduce new mathematical machinery (Hadamard rotation and L2 normalization are both standard techniques), but it identifies a non-obvious interaction between them that creates a hard design constraint. The paper's ablation (Table 9) provides the empirical smoking gun: Omni-Token Scaling alone degrades performance (confirming the artifact is real and damaging), Canalized Rotation alone is insufficient (TNI persists), but the combination—in the correct order—recovers near-lossless accuracy. This tripartite result (A hurts, B doesn't help, A+B in specific order works) is a clean demonstration that the artifact is not a hypothetical edge case but the dominant effect in practice.

---

### Innovation 3: Mutual Necessity as a Design Principle in Quantization Pipelines

A recurring pattern in the KV cache quantization literature is the **additive accumulation of auxiliary mechanisms**: start with a base quantizer, then add rotation, then add outlier preservation, then add residual correction, then add LUT-based quantization, each contributing incremental improvement. The implicit assumption is that more mechanisms = better fidelity, with complexity as the accepted cost.

OScaR inverts this logic by introducing **mutual necessity** as a design principle: two components (Canalized Rotation and Omni-Token Scaling) are each individually insufficient or harmful, but together—in a specific order—they form a complete solution. The paper argues this explicitly: "both components are mutually essential" (Section 4.2), and the supporting evidence in Table 9 shows that removing either component degrades performance, with Omni-Token Scaling alone actually performing *worse* than the unmodified INT2 baseline on WorldSense.

Why is mutual necessity an innovation rather than just a property of the specific algorithm? Because it represents a **different philosophy of what makes a quantization pipeline "good."** In the additive paradigm, each component is an independent knob that can be tuned or removed, and a method is evaluated on whether adding component X on top of the baseline improves accuracy. In the mutual-necessity paradigm, the components are only meaningful in combination, and a method is evaluated on whether the **minimal set of components that together solve the problem** yields a simpler pipeline than the additive alternatives.

This framing has implications beyond OScaR itself. It suggests that the proliferation of complex quantization pipelines (TurboQuant, QJL, PolarQuant) may reflect not the inherent complexity of the problem but a **failure to identify the correct joint intervention**. If the root cause (TNI) requires two coordinated transformations (rotation + scaling), then any method that applies only one or that applies them in the wrong order will underperform, incentivizing the addition of further mechanisms to compensate—mechanisms that become unnecessary once the correct joint intervention is found. The paper's Pareto front analysis (Appendix U, Figure 9) supports this interpretation: OScaR achieves higher accuracy than TurboQuant+ with less than half the computational cost, suggesting that TurboQuant+'s additional complexity (dense rotations, binary search quantization, residual handling) is compensating for the absence of token-wise normalization rather than providing independent value.

The connection to Occam's Razor is more than rhetorical branding. The paper is making a substantive methodological claim: that in KV cache quantization, **simplicity is not a compromise but an indicator of having correctly identified the causal structure of the problem**. An unnecessarily complex pipeline is, in this view, a symptom of an incomplete diagnosis—the extra components are papering over effects whose root cause hasn't been addressed. This is a **methodological reframing** of moderate significance: it doesn't change what quantization techniques are available, but it changes how one should think about designing and evaluating them.

---

### Innovation 4: Verifier Over-Optimization Replaced by Token-Wise Normalization as the Scaling-Bottleneck Resolution

A less explicit but equally important conceptual shift in OScaR concerns **what limits further scaling of test-time compute (or compression)**. In much of the KV cache quantization literature, the implicit bottleneck model is that quantization introduces a fixed distortion, and the job of more sophisticated methods is to reduce that distortion closer to zero—a framework inherited from rate-distortion theory. Methods like TurboQuant explicitly adopt this framing, with near-optimal distortion guarantees and residual error correction to handle the remaining error.

OScaR's analysis implies a fundamentally different bottleneck model: **the distortion is not a fixed function of the quantization parameters but is instead determined by the structural properties of the data distribution—specifically, TNI**. Under this model, the path to better extreme quantization is not more powerful quantizers (Lloyd-Max optimization, LUT-based lookup, residual coding) but rather **pre-transformations that make the data distribution compatible with simple quantization**. A per-channel INT2 quantizer is sufficient, the paper argues, if the data it sees has uniform token norms and smooth channel distributions.

This is a **reframing of the research agenda**. If the rate-distortion model is correct, then progress will come from better quantizer design—finer grouping strategies, adaptive bit allocation, non-uniform quantization grids. If OScaR's model is correct, then progress will come from better pre-transformations—rotations that more thoroughly smooth channel distributions, normalization strategies that handle more complex TNI patterns, or learned transforms that jointly optimize the pre-processing and quantization stages. The paper's success with a simple INT2 uniform quantizer after careful pre-processing provides strong evidence for the latter view, and the failure of TurboQuant+'s sophisticated quantizer to outperform OScaR despite 3× the computational cost (Appendix K, Table 4) suggests that better quantizer design alone cannot overcome the data distribution problem.

This is not a theoretical proof—it's an **empirical argument from the Pareto front**—but it has the structure of a Kuhnian paradigm shift in miniature: the dominant framework (better quantizers for fixed data) encounters anomalies (complex methods underperform simple ones at extreme compression), and an alternative framework (pre-transform data to be quantization-friendly, then use simple quantizers) resolves those anomalies with a simpler apparatus. The paper's contribution is not just OScaR as a method but OScaR as **evidence for this alternative framework**.

The practical implication is that future work on extreme KV cache quantization should invest more heavily in pre-transformation design (what the paper calls "Canalized Rotation" could be generalized to other orthogonal or approximately-orthogonal transforms optimized for downstream quantization) and less in quantizer complexity. The paper's finding that the rsqrt-based L2 normalization works essentially as well as exact normalization (Table 10) while being hardware-accelerated suggests that efficient pre-transformations are already sufficient—the bottleneck is not the normalization's precision but the rotation's ability to prevent the Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses five benchmarks spanning three model categories: (i) **LongBench-E**—a uniformly sampled subset of the LongBench benchmark [6] where context lengths are evenly distributed across 0–4K, 4K–8K, and 8K+ tokens, covering single-document QA, multi-document QA, summarization, few-shot learning, synthetic retrieval, and code completion across 21 datasets with English average lengths of 6,711 words; (ii) **Needle-in-a-Haystack (NIAH)** [24]—a synthetic retrieval benchmark testing long-context memory where a random fact is inserted at varying depths within contexts up to 42K tokens, with retrieval accuracy as the primary metric; (iii) **OCRBench** [37]—1,000 human-annotated QA pairs across text recognition (300), scene text VQA (200), document VQA (200), key information extraction (200), and handwritten math recognition (100), scored as the number of correctly answered samples; (iv) **DocVQA** [39]—12,000+ document images with 50,000 questions evaluated on a 5,349-sample validation set using Average Normalized Levenshtein Similarity (ANLS); and (v) **MMAU-Pro** [26]—5,305 audio intelligence instances from 49 skills, evaluated on two challenging open-ended subsets scored by an LLM judge (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) on a 1–5 scale across correctness, relevance, completeness, and clarity, plus a deterministic instruction-following subset across 28 constraint types.

- **Base model(s).** Evaluations span three categories: **text-only LLMs**—Llama-3.1-8B [21] and Qwen3-8B [73]; **multi-modal LLMs**—LLaVA-v1.6-vicuna-7B [27, 35, 34], Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct, and Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct [5]; and **omni-modal LLMs**—Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B [72]. These models are chosen to represent a diverse set of open-source families (Llama, Qwen, LLaVA) and scales (4B–30B parameters, though Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B uses mixture-of-experts with ~3B active parameters). All evaluations use models' standard chat templates with `add_generation_prompt=True`.

- **Metrics.** **LongBench-E** reports per-task accuracy (%) and averages across 8 tasks for Llama-3.1-8B and 8 tasks for Qwen3-8B (HotpotQA, Qasper, GovReport, MultiNews, Trec, TriviaQA, PassageRetrieval, RepoBench-P). **NIAH** reports retrieval accuracy (%) across context length × depth positions. **OCRBench** reports the total number of correctly answered samples (out of 1,000) across five subcategories, with the Final Score being the sum. **DocVQA** uses ANLS, which measures normalized edit distance between predicted and ground-truth answers to account for OCR and spelling variations. **MMAU-Pro** reports (i) open-ended QA score as a judge-assigned percentage; (ii) Good Rate—the fraction of open-ended samples receiving an overall score of 4.0/5.0 or higher; and (iii) AIF (Audio Instruction Following) accuracy from deterministic script evaluation.

- **Baselines.** Five competing methods spanning three paradigms: (i) **per-channel Key quantization**—KIVI [38] (block-wise per-channel with residual buffer, no rotation or normalization) and OTT [44] (per-channel with high-precision preservation of 5 outlier tokens per head); (ii) **rotation-based per-token quantization**—QuaRot [4] (online Hadamard transform on Q and K, offline on V and output weights, followed by per-token quantization) and RotateKV [45] (outlier-aware adaptive rotations with high-precision outlier token preservation); and (iii) **LUT-based methods**—TurboQuant+ [62], a widely-adopted open-source implementation of TurboQuant [74] using 2.5-bit mixed-precision (3-bit for 32 outlier channels, 2-bit for 96 normal channels) with binary search quantization and a dense Haar QR rotation matrix, but with the QJL step [75] excluded as it is shown to degrade performance (Table 5). TurboQuant+ is the only baseline not using INT2—it uses 2.5-bit to account for its bit allocation overhead.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use "generation budget" in the LLM inference sense—all methods quantize the KV cache during standard autoregressive decoding. Fairness is maintained through: (i) **bit-width parity**: all methods except TurboQuant+ use INT2 quantization for both Key and Value; TurboQuant+ uses 2.5-bit to reflect its mixed-precision bit allocation; (ii) **parameter overhead accounting**: quantization parameters (scales and zero-points) are included in effective bit-width calculations; (iii) **group size alignment**: all methods use group size 32 or 128 as specified—for OCRBench and DocVQA, group size 128 is used across all methods (Tables 6, 7); for LongBench-E and NIAH, group size 32 is used (Table 1, Figure 29); (iv) **residual length**: for KIVI, OTT, and OScaR, the residual buffer length is uniformly set to R=128 tokens; and (v) **computational cost analysis**: Appendix K provides a separate theoretical complexity analysis in arithmetic operations per token to supplement accuracy comparisons.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation is used—all evaluations report single-run results on the standard test/validation splits of each benchmark. The paper does not report confidence intervals or standard deviations for any quantitative result. For open-ended MMAU-Pro evaluation, the LLM judge (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) scoring introduces a source of non-determinism that is not characterized or averaged over multiple runs. The NIAH evaluation at each (context length, depth) grid point represents a single retrieval trial.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Text-Only LLM Results on LongBench-E

The headline finding on LongBench-E (Table 1) is that **OScaR achieves the highest average accuracy among all quantized methods on both Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen3-8B, with near-lossless performance relative to the 16-bit baseline on Qwen3-8B.**

On **Llama-3.1-8B** (8-task average):
- 16-bit baseline: 41.70%
- OScaR: 41.75%—**exceeding the baseline by 0.05 percentage points**
- Second-best quantized method: OTT at 40.74% (OScaR leads by 1.01 points)
- KIVI: 39.84%, QuaRot: 37.94%, RotateKV: 37.98%, TurboQuant+: 40.03%

Notable per-task patterns: OScaR achieves the highest or near-highest scores on GovReport (29.45%, tied with OTT), MultiNews (5.68%, compared to 5.89% 16-bit), and PassageRetrieval (44.46%, notably above the second-best TurboQuant+ at 41.51% and even the 16-bit baseline at 43.57%). On RepoBench-P, OScaR scores 65.39%—within 0.39 points of the 16-bit baseline (65.78%) and well above KIVI (64.42%). QuaRot and RotateKV show significant degradation on MultiNews (1.75% and 1.28% vs. 5.89% baseline), suggesting that rotation-only approaches are particularly vulnerable on summarization tasks under INT2 compression.

On **Qwen3-8B** (8-task average):
- 16-bit baseline: 49.56%
- OScaR: 48.74%—**only 0.82 percentage points below baseline**
- Second-best quantized method: OTT at 48.21% (OScaR leads by 0.53 points)
- KIVI: 47.95%, TurboQuant+: 47.56%, RotateKV: 42.95%, QuaRot: 40.13%

The gap between OScaR and the baseline on Qwen3-8B (0.82 points) is notably smaller than the typical gaps seen with other methods (KIVI drops 1.61 points, OTT drops 1.35 points, TurboQuant+ drops 2.00 points). On RepoBench-P, OScaR scores 61.57%—exceeding all other quantized methods (next best: OTT at 59.41%) and approaching the 16-bit baseline at 62.47%. On Trec, OScaR achieves 72.00%, matching or exceeding the baseline (71.33%) and outperforming RotateKV (71.00%) and QuaRot (33.00%)—the latter's catastrophic collapse on Trec highlights the fragility of rotation-only approaches on certain task types under INT2.

A key observation is that TurboQuant+—despite its 2.5-bit advantage over the INT2 methods—does not dominate. On Llama-3.1-8B, TurboQuant+ scores 40.03% vs. OScaR's 41.75%. On Qwen3-8B, TurboQuant+ scores 47.56% vs. OScaR's 48.74%. This is significant because TurboQuant+ uses a more sophisticated quantizer (mixed-precision, binary search optimization, dense rotation matrices) at substantially higher computational cost (247.9M decode ops per step vs. OScaR's 123.0M per Appendix K, Table 4), yet underperforms OScaR's simpler INT2 uniform quantizer with canalized rotation and token scaling.

#### Needle-in-a-Haystack Results

The NIAH evaluation (Figure 29, Appendix O) tests retrieval accuracy across context lengths up to 42K tokens and 15 depth positions. The results show that **OScaR achieves the highest retrieval accuracy (96.5%) among all quantized methods, slightly surpassing the 16-bit baseline (96.0%) by 0.5 percentage points.**

Specific method comparison:
- 16-bit baseline: 96.0%
- OScaR: 96.5%
- TurboQuant+ (2.5-bit): 92.7%
- OTT: 90.1%
- KIVI: 88.8%

OScaR's slight over-performance relative to the 16-bit baseline on NIAH is notable—it suggests that the Hadamard rotation and token-wise normalization may have a regularizing effect on attention scores in long-context retrieval, though the paper does not investigate this mechanism. The gap between OScaR and the second-best quantized method (TurboQuant+ at 92.7%) is 3.8 percentage points, which is substantial in absolute terms but should be interpreted cautiously given that the NIAH evaluation at each grid point represents a single trial—the paper does not report variance across multiple needle positions or document instantiations. KIVI's degradation to 88.8% (vs. 96.0% baseline) confirms that unmodified per-channel quantization loses significant retrieval fidelity at INT2 on long contexts.

#### Multi-Modal LLM Results on OCRBench

Table 6 reports OCRBench results across three multi-modal models with group size 128. The consistent pattern is that **OScaR achieves the highest or near-highest Final Score among all quantized methods, approaching the 16-bit baseline closely on the stronger models.**

On **LLaVA-v1.6-vicuna-7B**:
- 16-bit baseline: 536
- OScaR: 519 (gap of 17 points, or 3.2%)
- Second-best quantized: OTT at 513, KIVI at 488, TurboQuant+ at 501, QuaRot at 481, RotateKV at 473

Per-category analysis reveals that OScaR matches the 16-bit baseline exactly on VQAD (document VQA, 82/200) and achieves the highest score among all quantized methods on scene text VQA (158/200 vs. 160 baseline). The handwritten math recognition (HMER) scores are zero across all methods including the 16-bit baseline, indicating this sub-task is beyond LLaVA-v1.6-vicuna-7B's capabilities regardless of quantization.

On **Qwen3-VL-8B**:
- 16-bit baseline: 858
- OScaR: 856 (gap of only 2 points, or 0.2%)
- Second-best quantized: KIVI at 851, OTT at 850, TurboQuant+ at 847, RotateKV at 754, QuaRot at 722

OScaR matches the 16-bit baseline on Recognition (270/300) and Scene Text VQA (180/200 and 181/200 respectively). On HMER, OScaR scores 53/100 vs. 54/100 for OTT and 52/100 for the baseline—all within one point. The large gaps for QuaRot (722, gap of 136) and RotateKV (754, gap of 104) indicate that rotation-only approaches lose substantial fidelity on vision-language tasks under INT2, likely because the visual token distributions have TNI patterns (as shown in Figures 19–21) that rotation alone cannot address.

On **Qwen3-VL-4B**:
- 16-bit baseline: 852
- OScaR: 838 (gap of 14 points, or 1.6%)

OScaR's advantage over OTT (838 vs. 831, or 7 points) is modest but consistent. The catastrophic degradation of RotateKV (638 vs. 852 baseline, gap of 214 points—a 25% relative drop) on the smaller 4B model is striking and underscores the fragility of outlier-token-preservation strategies at smaller model scales where the residual representational capacity may be insufficient to absorb quantization errors.

On **LLaVA-v1.6-vicuna-7B**:
- 16-bit baseline: 68.57
- OScaR: 67.79 (gap of 0.78 ANLS points)
- Second-best quantized: TurboQuant+ at 67.09, OTT at 66.11, QuaRot at 65.55, KIVI at 65.34, RotateKV at 65.26

On **Qwen3-VL-8B**:
- 16-bit baseline: 94.93
- OScaR: 95.01—**exceeding the baseline by 0.08 ANLS points**

Three observations stand out. Second, RotateKV's catastrophic collapse on Qwen3-VL-4B (76.12 vs. 94.23 baseline, gap of 18.11 ANLS points) is even more severe than on OCRBench, confirming that outlier-token-preservation strategies are brittle at small model scales. #### Omni-Modal LLM Results on MMAU-Pro

Table 8 reports results on Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B across three evaluation axes with group size 128. **OScaR achieves the highest scores on all three metrics, surpassing both the 16-bit baseline and all quantized methods** on open-ended QA, Good Rate, and audio instruction following.

On **Open-ended QA** (LLM-judge scored, 1–5 scale converted to percentage):
- OScaR: 67.4 (exceeds 16-bit baseline at 66.2 by 1.2 points)
- TurboQuant+: 66.6, KIVI: 65.8, OTT: 65.8

On **Good Rate** (fraction scoring ≥4.0/5.0):
- OScaR: 29.8 (exceeds 16-bit baseline at 27.8 by 2.0 points)
- TurboQuant+: 27.0, KIVI: 27.0, OTT: 26.9

On **Audio Instruction Following (AIF)** (deterministic script evaluation):
- OScaR: 88.5 (exceeds 16-bit baseline at 87.4 by 1.1 points)
- OTT: 83.9, TurboQuant+: 79.3, KIVI: 78.2

OScaR over-performing the 16-bit baseline on all three MMAU-Pro metrics is the most striking result in the experimental section—it suggests that the Hadamard-norm transform is not merely preserving model capabilities but potentially improving them for omni-modal reasoning, possibly by normalizing cross-modal attention score magnitudes. However, this interpretation should be treated with caution: (i) the LLM judge for open-ended scoring introduces uncharacterized non-determinism; (ii) the paper does not report whether the judge evaluation was run multiple times to assess variance; (iii) the absolute gaps (1.2 points on open-ended, 2.0 on Good Rate, 1.1 on AIF) are modest and may not be statistically significant without confidence intervals; and (iv) the mechanism by which quantization-time normalization could improve over full-precision attention is not investigated or explained.

The large gap between OScaR and the next-best method on AIF (88.5 vs. OTT at 83.9, difference of 4.6 points) is more clearly significant—this is a deterministic metric with script-based evaluation, so the gap cannot be attributed to judge variance. It indicates that OScaR's TNI mitigation is particularly important for audio instruction following, possibly because audio token sequences exhibit distinct norm distributions that per-channel quantization without token-wise normalization handles poorly.

#### Computational Efficiency Results

Figure 6 reports decoding latency (single-batch, varying context lengths) and throughput (fixed 4K context, varying batch sizes) on Qwen3-8B with a single H20 GPU (141GB).

**Decoding latency** (Figure 6a):
- At 128K context: BF16 FlashDecoding-v2 baseline at 92.9 ms/token, OScaR INT2 at 30.9 ms/token → **3.0× speedup**
- OScaR's latency is remarkably stable across context lengths: 25.1 ms/tok at 1K, 24.9 ms/tok at 4K, 25.8 ms/tok at 32K, and 30.9 ms/tok at 128K—only a 23% increase from 1K to 128K
- In contrast, FlashDecoding-v2 latency grows 4.8× over the same range (19.5 to 92.9 ms/tok)

**Throughput and memory** (Figure 6b):
- At batch size 48: BF16 throughput at 331 tokens/s, OScaR INT2 at 1,354 tokens/s → **4.1× throughput increase**
- Memory footprint: BF16 at 5.3× the memory of OScaR INT2 (inferred from the "reduces memory footprint by 5.3×" claim in Section 5.3 and the abstract)

**Comparison with TurboQuant+** (Appendix V, Table 11): TurboQuant+ achieves lower latency than OScaR at short contexts (7.8 ms/tok at 1K vs. OScaR's 25.1 ms/tok, a 3.2× advantage for TurboQuant+) but exhibits strong context dependence, growing to 72.9 ms/tok at 128K vs. OScaR's 30.9 ms/tok—a 2.4× advantage for OScaR at 128K. Relative to the BF16 baseline at 128K, OScaR achieves 3.0× speedup while TurboQuant+ achieves only 1.3× (92.9 → 72.9 ms/tok). This reversal with context length is attributed to TurboQuant+'s dense rotation matrix (2dh operations per token) becoming a bottleneck as sequence length grows, while OScaR's FHT-based rotation and fused kernels scale more gracefully.

The paper notes that OScaR is implemented on the PyTorch runtime while TurboQuant+ relies on llama.cpp—a framework difference that complicates direct latency comparison and may account for some of the short-context advantage of TurboQuant+.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Core component ablation (Table 9)**: On the WorldSense benchmark [19] with Qwen2.5-Omni-3B and Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, the unmodified INT2 per-channel baseline scores 22.64 and 39.62 respectively. Adding Omni-Token Scaling alone worsens performance to 17.61 and 35.22—a degradation of 5.03 and 4.40 points, confirming the Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact is real and damaging. Adding Canalized Rotation alone substantially improves performance to 37.74 and 41.51—gains of 15.10 and 1.89 points over the INT2 baseline. The full OScaR configuration (both components) achieves 38.36 and 42.77—further gains of 0.62 and 1.26 points over rotation alone. This tripartite pattern (scaling alone hurts, rotation alone helps but is insufficient, combination works best) is the cleanest evidence in the paper for the mutual necessity claim.

**Scaling coefficient strategy (Table 10)**: On LongBench-E across Llama-2-7B, Llama-3-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, and Qwen3-8B, L2 norm and its rsqrt approximation achieve mutually comparable performance (e.g., on Qwen3-8B: 42.85 vs. 42.91). Max-value normalization causes catastrophic degradation on some models—Qwen2.5-7B drops from 42.57 (L2) to 14.47 (Max), a 28.1-point collapse. Mean absolute value performs competitively but slightly worse than L2 (41.95 vs. 42.57 on Qwen2.5-7B). This ablation justifies the choice of rsqrt for hardware efficiency while demonstrating that L2 normalization specifically is important—the sensitivity to the normalization strategy indicates that the precise statistical properties of the token distribution matter for quantization compatibility.

**QJL ablation for TurboQuant+ (Table 5)**: On OCRBench with LLaVA-v1.6-vicuna-7B and Qwen3-VL-8B, TurboQuant+ with QJL scores 403 and 626 respectively; without QJL, scores improve to 501 and 847. The QJL step, which applies a Johnson-Lindenstrauss projection for theoretical distortion guarantees, empirically degrades generation quality—consistent with the paper's claim that QJL "increases variance that is subsequently amplified by the softmax function." This negative result supports the paper's broader methodological argument that theoretical optimality (rate-distortion guarantees) does not translate to practical performance when the data distribution—specifically, the interaction between quantization error and the softmax nonlinearity—is not accounted for.

**TNI visualization before and after OScaR (Appendix J, Figures 22–28)**: These qualitative ablations show token norm distributions at each processing stage. In the "Original" state, token norms are highly scattered with clear low-norm outlier tokens. After Omni-Token Scaling alone, token norms are equalized but Key magnitude heatmaps show artificial channel-wise outliers (the Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact). After Canalized Rotation alone, channel distributions are smoothed but token norms remain imbalanced. Only the complete OScaR pipeline shows both balanced token norms and smooth per-channel distributions. These visualizations are provided for Llama-2-7B (Figures 22–24), Llama-3.1-8B (Figure 25), Qwen3-VL-8B (Figure 26), Qwen3-8B (Figure 27), and Qwen2.5-VL-7B (Figure 28), demonstrating the generality of the effect across model families and modalities.

**Pareto front analysis (Appendix U, Figure 9)**: Plotting LongBench-E accuracy against theoretical decode cost (from Appendix K, Table 4) for Qwen3-8B, OScaR occupies a distinct Pareto-optimal position with the highest accuracy (48.74) and a decode cost of ~123M units, compared to KIVI (47.95 accuracy, ~82M cost) and TurboQuant+ (47.56 accuracy, ~248M cost). KIVI dominates on cost but is Pareto-dominated by OScaR on accuracy; TurboQuant+ is strictly dominated by OScaR (lower accuracy at higher cost). This analysis quantifies the paper's claim that OScaR "redefines the Pareto front"—it shows that no existing method achieves both higher accuracy and lower cost than OScaR under the paper's theoretical cost model.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim: OScaR identifies Token Norm Imbalance (TNI) as the fundamental bottleneck of per-channel quantization

The evidence for TNI as a real and damaging phenomenon is strong and multi-faceted: the per-layer L2 norm distribution visualizations (Figures 3, 10–18 for text-only, 19–21 for multi-modal) consistently show substantial token-wise norm variation across diverse models; the theoretical derivation (Equation 11) provides a formal lower bound linking TNI to quantization error; and the quantitative error analysis (Appendix H, Table 2) confirms that outlier tokens increase per-channel Key quantization error by ~35% at INT2. The diversity of models tested (Llama-2-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-VL-8B, LLaVA-v1.5-7B) lends credibility to the universality claim.

However, the paper demonstrates TNI as a **sufficient** explanation for per-channel quantization collapse but does not fully establish it as **uniquely fundamental**. The theoretical lower bound in Equation 11 shows that TNI *can* cause large errors, but it does not rule out other mechanisms (e.g., within-channel skew beyond what the min-max range captures, interactions between quantization error and the softmax nonlinearity) as important contributors. The quantitative error analysis (Table 2) measures the effect of removing outlier tokens or separating modalities, but it does not decompose the total quantization error into TNI-attributable and non-TNI-attributable components. It is possible that per-channel quantization would still degrade at INT2 even with perfect token norm equalization due to other factors—the paper's own results show that OScaR does not fully close the gap to 16-bit on some benchmarks.

Additionally, the TNI analysis in Section 4.1 uses **fixed, short prompts** for visualization (a single four-sentence text for text-only LLMs, a single image QA prompt for multi-modal LLMs). It is unclear whether the TNI patterns observed on these short, simple inputs generalize to the diverse, long-context inputs in LongBench-E or the complex multi-image scenarios in OCRBench/DocVQA. A more thorough analysis would have profiled TNI on the actual evaluation data or at least on a representative sample of long-context and multi-modal inputs.

#### Claim: OScaR achieves near-lossless performance under INT2 quantization

This claim is conditionally supported but with important caveats about what "near-lossless" means and on which benchmarks.

On **LongBench-E with Qwen3-8B**, OScaR's gap to 16-bit is 0.82 percentage points (48.74 vs. 49.56 average)—this is genuinely close and qualifies as near-lossless by any reasonable standard. On **Llama-3.1-8B**, OScaR actually exceeds the 16-bit average (41.75 vs. 41.70), which is technically "lossless" or better.

On **OCRBench with Qwen3-VL-8B**, the gap is 2 points out of 858 (0.2%)—effectively indistinguishable from the baseline. On **DocVQA with Qwen3-VL-8B**, OScaR slightly exceeds the baseline (95.01 vs. 94.93).

However, on **LLaVA-v1.6-vicuna-7B** across OCRBench (519 vs. 536, gap of 3.2%) and DocVQA (67.79 vs. 68.57, gap of 0.78 ANLS), the gap is more substantial. On **Qwen3-VL-4B** for OCRBench (838 vs. 852, gap of 1.6%), the degradation is modest but measurable. The paper's claim of "near-lossless" is best supported on the 8B-scale models (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-VL-8B) and weaker on the 4B and 7B models.

A significant blind spot is the **absence of evaluation on text-only long-context generation tasks**. LongBench-E measures retrieval and understanding, but does not evaluate the quality of *generated* text under INT2 quantization—would a model with an INT2 KV cache produce fluent, coherent long-form outputs, or would the accumulated quantization errors manifest as degradation in generation quality? This is particularly relevant because decoding is autoregressive—quantization errors in early tokens' KV entries propagate to all subsequent tokens' attention computations, potentially compounding over long sequences.

The **MMAU-Pro result where OScaR exceeds the 16-bit baseline** (67.4 vs. 66.2 on open-ended, 29.8 vs. 27.8 on Good Rate, 88.5 vs. 87.4 on AIF) is intriguing but requires methodological scrutiny. The open-ended evaluation uses an LLM judge (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) whose scoring variance is not characterized—if the judge has a standard deviation of 2–3 points, then a 1.2-point difference is not statistically significant. The paper does not report multiple judge runs, confidence intervals, or inter-judge agreement. The Good Rate improvement of 2.0 points (29.8 vs. 27.8) represents about 7% relative improvement, which is notable but again unaccompanied by significance testing. The AIF improvement of 1.1 points (88.5 vs. 87.4) is on a deterministic metric, so the difference is real but small—it could reflect a genuine beneficial effect of the Hadamard-norm transform on audio attention patterns, or it could be noise from the single evaluation run.

#### Claim: OScaR redefines the accuracy-efficiency Pareto front

The theoretical Pareto front analysis (Appendix U, Figure 9) supports this claim, but the empirical efficiency results require a more nuanced reading.

**OScaR vs. KIVI on the Pareto front**: OScaR achieves higher accuracy (48.74 vs. 47.95 on LongBench-E Qwen3-8B) at ~1.5× the theoretical decode cost (123.0M vs. 81.9M ops/step). Whether this tradeoff is "better" depends on the application—latency-sensitive deployments may prefer KIVI's lower cost despite slightly lower accuracy, especially since the accuracy gap (0.79 points on the 8-task average) is modest. The paper's framing of OScaR as Pareto-dominant is correct in the strict sense (no method has both higher accuracy and lower cost), but KIVI occupies a neighboring point on the frontier that may be preferable in practice.

**OScaR vs. TurboQuant+ on the efficiency comparison (Table 11)**: At short contexts (1K–4K), TurboQuant+ achieves substantially lower latency than OScaR (7.8 vs. 25.1 ms/tok at 1K). The paper emphasizes OScaR's advantage at long contexts (30.9 vs. 72.9 ms/tok at 128K), which is real and significant, but the crossover point is not specified—at what context length does OScaR become faster? The data in Table 11 shows TurboQuant+ is faster at 32K (23.9 vs. 25.8 ms/tok for OScaR) and OScaR is faster at 48K (25.7 vs. 32.1 ms/tok), placing the crossover between 32K and 48K. This means that for applications with typical context lengths below 32K tokens, TurboQuant+ may actually be the more efficient choice despite its higher theoretical operation count—a nuance the paper's Pareto front framing (which uses theoretical ops, not wall-clock latency) does not capture.

The **implementation framework difference** (PyTorch for OScaR vs. llama.cpp for TurboQuant+) is a significant confound for latency comparisons. llama.cpp is a heavily optimized C++ inference engine with aggressive kernel fusion and memory management; PyTorch, even with custom CUDA kernels, typically has higher framework overhead. It is possible that OScaR implemented on llama.cpp would be faster than TurboQuant+ at all context lengths, or conversely that TurboQuant+ on PyTorch would be slower than OScaR. The paper acknowledges this (Appendix V notes the framework difference) but does not control for it, making the latency comparison less informative about the algorithmic efficiency of the two approaches than it could be.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Have Strengthened the Paper

1. **Statistical significance and variance**: No confidence intervals, standard deviations, or multiple-run averages are reported for any quantitative result. For LongBench-E (500 test questions per dataset per the LongBench-E uniform sampling), OScaR's claimed 1.01-point advantage over OTT on Llama-3.1-8B may or may not be statistically significant. For NIAH with single-trial retrieval, the 96.5% vs. 92.7% advantage over TurboQuant+ could be noise. For MMAU-Pro with LLM judge evaluation, judge variance is entirely uncharacterized. This is a significant omission that makes it impossible to assess whether the reported improvements are reliable or within noise.

2. **Ablation of group size and residual length**: All experiments use G=32 or G=128 and R=128, but these are important hyperparameters. Would OScaR's advantage over baselines grow or shrink with different group sizes? The paper does not explore the sensitivity of OScaR's TNI mitigation to these parameters.

3. **Full generation quality evaluation**: All text-only evaluations use short-answer benchmarks (LongBench-E is multiple-choice and short-answer extraction). There is no evaluation of OScaR's impact on long-form generation quality (e.g., perplexity on a language modeling corpus, or human evaluation of generated text coherence). This is a gap because quantization errors in the KV cache could manifest more severely in open-ended generation where small attention perturbations compound over many decoding steps.

4. **Scaling to larger models**: The largest model tested is Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B (30B total, ~3B active parameters). Would OScaR's TNI mitigation be more or less important at the 70B+ scale? Larger models may have more severe TNI (more attention heads creating more extreme attention sink tokens) or less severe TNI (more representational capacity to absorb quantization errors). The paper's "X-LLMs" framing implies universality, but this is not tested at scale.

5. **Comparison with learned quantization methods**: The paper's baselines are all training-free. Learned quantization approaches (e.g., using a small calibration set to optimize clipping ranges or to learn per-layer bit allocations) are not compared. This is defensible given the paper's focus on training-free methods, but it limits the claim that OScaR "redefines the Pareto front" to the training-free subset of methods.

6. **Latency measurement protocol details**: The paper does not specify how many warmup runs were used, how many timing iterations were averaged, or what synchronization primitives were employed for GPU timing. These details matter for reproducible latency measurements, and their absence makes the reported speedups difficult to verify independently.

#### Where the Claims Hold Conditionally

- **"Near-lossless INT2" holds most strongly for 8B-scale models on understanding/retrieval tasks** (LongBench-E, DocVQA, OCRBench on Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-VL-8B). It holds less strongly for 4B and 7B models (Qwen3-VL-4B, LLaVA-v1.6-vicuna-7B), where gaps of 1.6–3.2% to baseline persist. It is untested for generation quality.

- **"3.0× decoding speedup" holds at 128K context length with batch size 1**. At shorter contexts, the speedup is smaller or negative relative to BF16 (at 4K, OScaR is slower: 24.9 vs. 20.3 ms/tok for FlashDecoding-v2). At batch size 48 and 4K context, the throughput improvement is 4.1×, but the paper only reports throughput at this single (batch, context) configuration.

- **"Outperforms all baselines on accuracy" holds for the specific baselines tested** (KIVI, OTT, QuaRot, RotateKV, TurboQuant+) at the specific bit-widths (INT2 for OScaR and most baselines, 2.5-bit for TurboQuant+) and group sizes (32 or 128). It is not tested against learned quantization, against INT3 or INT4 variants of these baselines, or against methods that use different group sizes than the ones the paper selected for each benchmark.

- **"OScaR over-performs 16-bit on some metrics" (NIAH, DocVQA Qwen3-VL-8B, MMAU-Pro) is observed but not explained or validated for statistical significance**. These results are intriguing but should be treated as suggestive rather than conclusive until replicated with proper significance testing and variance characterization.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Online Rotation Cost Cannot Be Fully Eliminated Because of RoPE

**The assumption or constraint.** Canalized Rotation requires online computation of the Hadamard transform for both Query and Key tensors during every decoding step. This is not a design choice but a structural necessity imposed by Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE). The paper states this explicitly in Appendix A:

> "Canalized Rotation requires online computation in the presence of RoPE, which precludes offline fusion of Query and Key Hadamard transforms with weight matrices."

If RoPE were not present, the Hadamard transform could be absorbed into the weight matrices offline—computing `W_K' = H W_K` and `W_Q' = H W_Q` once before inference, eliminating the online rotation cost entirely. However, because RoPE applies a position-dependent rotation to specific dimension pairs after projection, the Hadamard transform must be applied after RoPE, making offline fusion impossible.

**The consequence.** This means OScaR carries a persistent per-token computational overhead that is structural rather than optimizable. The theoretical complexity analysis in Appendix K (Table 4) quantifies this at 123.0 million arithmetic operations per decode step for d=4096 and L=10,000—approximately 1.5× the cost of KIVI (81.9M ops) which performs no rotation at all. While the paper argues this overhead is offset by memory bandwidth savings from INT2 compression, the cost is fixed regardless of context length—it does not amortize with longer sequences. For very short contexts (e.g., 1K tokens), the overhead of the Hadamard transform and token-wise normalization may dominate the total decode time, as reflected in the latency comparison (Table 11) where OScaR at 25.1 ms/token is actually slower than BF16 FlashDecoding-v2 at 19.5 ms/token at 1K context.

The paper's mitigation strategies—HadaCore's Tensor Core implementation of the FHT and fused Hadamard-Norm kernels—reduce but do not eliminate this cost. Future work on alternative position embedding schemes that are compatible with offline rotation, or on rotation methods that can be partially absorbed despite RoPE, could reduce this overhead.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 11 (Appendix V) provides the direct evidence: at 1K context, OScaR latency (25.1 ms/token) exceeds the BF16 baseline (19.5 ms/token) by 29%. The crossover point where OScaR becomes faster than the baseline occurs between 4K and 8K context (24.9 vs. 23.8 at 4K; 24.1 vs. 28.3 at 8K, favoring OScaR). The theoretical decode cost analysis in Appendix K, Table 4 shows OScaR at 123.0M ops vs. KIVI at 81.9M ops—a 50% increase in arithmetic operations that is only justified when memory bandwidth savings dominate.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses this through HadaCore's Tensor Core acceleration and kernel fusion (Section 4.3, Appendix L). The authors acknowledge this as a limitation and suggest future work on "alternative Canalized Rotation that further reduce online overhead or enable more efficient hardware-aware implementations" (Appendix A). However, no concrete direction for eliminating the online cost entirely is proposed, and the structural conflict with RoPE suggests this may be a fundamental tradeoff rather than a solvable optimization problem.

---

### The Quantization Parameters and Norm Metadata Overhead Is Not Fully Accounted for in Compression Ratio Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline compression claims—"reduces memory footprint by 5.3×" (Section 5.3, Abstract)—compare OScaR's INT2 representation against a BF16 baseline. However, the INT2 representation carries additional storage overhead that the effective compression ratio must account for: per-channel quantization parameters (scale and zero-point for each channel within each block of G=32 tokens), the residual buffer (R=128 tokens stored in FP16), and the token-wise norm metadata (one float per token).

For full transparency, the effective bits-per-element should be calculated as:

`Effective bits = (2 × S × d + param_overhead + norm_overhead + residual_overhead) / (S × d × 16)`

where S is sequence length, d is hidden dimension, and the denominator is the BF16 baseline storage. The paper states that it "accounts for the average bit overhead introduced by quantization parameters" when comparing methods (Appendix N) and notes that TurboQuant uses 2.5-bit to incorporate its mixed-precision allocation, but a complete accounting including residual buffer and norm metadata is not presented as a single effective bit-width number for OScaR.

**The consequence.** The 5.3× memory reduction is relative to BF16, which is an 8× compression in payload bits (16 → 2). The gap between 8× and 5.3× (a loss of roughly 2.7× of the theoretical maximum) reflects quantization parameter overhead, norm metadata, and residual buffer costs. This overhead grows in relative importance as sequence length decreases—for very short sequences, the fixed-size residual buffer and per-block parameter overhead become a larger fraction of total storage, reducing the effective compression ratio. The paper does not characterize how the compression ratio scales with sequence length or batch size, leaving practitioners without the information needed to estimate memory savings for their specific deployment configurations.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides the component costs that contribute to overhead—group size G=32 (meaning scale/zero-point parameters for every 32 tokens per channel), residual buffer R=128 (128 FP16 tokens stored unquantized at any time), and one float per token for norm metadata—but does not aggregate them into a single effective bit-width number or provide an analytical formula for how compression ratio varies with sequence length. Figure 6b reports the 5.3× memory reduction at a specific operating point (batch size 48, context length 4K) but does not show how this scales.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not provide an analytical model for effective compression ratio as a function of sequence length, does not ablate how the choice of G and R affects memory savings, and does not discuss whether the norm metadata could be further compressed (e.g., quantized to INT8 or FP8). A practitioner cannot determine from the paper what memory savings to expect at their specific sequence length and batch size without reproducing the measurement.

---

### Only a Single Model Family (Qwen) and Scale Regime (4B–30B) Are Thoroughly Evaluated; the Universality Claim for "X-LLMs" Is Lightly Tested

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's framing emphasizes that OScaR is designed for and evaluated on "X-LLMs (i.e., text-only, multi-modal, and omni-modal LLMs)" (Section 1, Abstract). In practice, the thorough evaluation is concentrated on the Qwen model family (Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-VL-4B/8B, Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B) with one Llama model (Llama-3.1-8B) and one LLaVA model (LLaVA-v1.6-vicuna-7B) tested. The qualitative TNI visualizations (Appendix D, F) span Llama-2-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-VL-8B, but the only models with full quantitative benchmark results are Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-8B, LLaVA-v1.6-vicuna-7B, Qwen3-VL-4B/8B, and Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B.

**The consequence.** The claim that OScaR is a "universal framework" for X-LLMs rests on evaluation of essentially two model families (Llama/Llama-derived and Qwen) at a narrow scale range (4B–30B total parameters). It is unknown whether OScaR's TNI mitigation would transfer to:

- **Different architectural families**: models with Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) or Multi-Query Attention (MQA) may have different TNI patterns due to different KV head structures; models with different activation functions (SwiGLU vs. GELU) or normalization schemes may produce different attention state distributions.
- **Larger model scales**: the largest model tested is Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B (with only ~3B active parameters due to Mixture-of-Experts). At the 70B, 405B, or larger scales, TNI patterns may intensify (more attention heads potentially creating more extreme attention sink tokens) or diminish (greater representational capacity may produce more uniform attention patterns). The paper provides no evidence either way.
- **Non-transformer architectures with KV caches**: the paper suggests applicability to "streaming vision models such as StreamVGGT, visual autoregressive models, and diffusion LLMs with KV cache" (Appendix A) but conducts zero experiments in these domains.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The TNI visualizations in Appendix D show consistent patterns across Llama-2-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, and Qwen3-8B—three text-only models from two families. The benchmark results span two model families (Tables 1, 6, 7, 8). The paper explicitly acknowledges this limitation in Appendix A: "our current experiments are primarily conducted on models with LLM backbones... we have not yet conducted extensive experiments in these domains." No results are presented for models at the 70B+ scale, for models with MQA (e.g., older PaLM-style architectures), or for non-LLM architectures with KV caching.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through the authors' explicit acknowledgment in Appendix A and their characterization of this as future work. The consistency of TNI patterns across the tested models provides some inductive support for broader applicability, but the gap between "consistent across two model families at 7B–8B scale" and "universal across X-LLMs at all scales" is substantial. A practitioner deploying a 70B Llama model or a non-Qwen/Llama architecture cannot assume OScaR will be effective without additional validation.

### There Is No Evaluation of Long-Form Text Generation Quality Under INT2 KV Cache Compression

**The assumption or constraint.** All text-only LLM evaluations use benchmarks that require short-answer extraction, multiple-choice selection, or retrieval (LongBench-E and NIAH). The paper does not evaluate the impact of INT2 KV cache quantization on the quality of long-form generated text—for example, perplexity on a language modeling corpus, human evaluation of generated summaries or dialogues, or task-completion metrics for open-ended generation tasks.

**The consequence.** The omission is significant because KV cache quantization errors during decoding are autoregressive: quantization errors in a token's Key and Value entries affect the attention computation for all subsequent tokens. In short-answer tasks, the model only needs to produce a few tokens of output, and quantization errors may not have time to compound significantly. In long-form generation (hundreds or thousands of output tokens), errors in early KV entries propagate through the entire sequence, potentially causing topic drift, factual inconsistency, repetition, or degraded coherence that would not be captured by short-answer accuracy metrics.

The paper's claim of "near-lossless performance under INT2 quantization" (Abstract) is therefore only validated for the specific task types tested—retrieval, short-answer QA, multiple-choice reasoning—and may not hold for generation-heavy applications like dialogue, summarization, or creative writing. A practitioner deploying OScaR for a chatbot or content generation system would be operating in an untested regime.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no perplexity measurements, no generation quality metrics, and no human evaluations of text produced with INT2 KV caches. All text-only benchmarks (LongBench-E tasks in Table 1, NIAH in Figure 29) evaluate either accuracy on short-answer extraction or retrieval correctness. The multi-modal benchmarks (OCRBench, DocVQA, MMAU-Pro) similarly evaluate answer correctness rather than generation quality.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, does not suggest generation quality evaluation as future work, and does not qualify the "near-lossless" claim with respect to task type. This is a significant gap in the evaluation that limits the practical applicability of the results to understanding and retrieval tasks, leaving open the question of whether INT2 KV cache compression is viable for generation-focused deployments.

### No Confidence Intervals, Statistical Significance Testing, or Multiple-Run Averages Are Reported for Any Quantitative Result

**The assumption or constraint.** Every quantitative result in the paper—benchmark accuracies (Tables 1, 6–8), retrieval accuracies (Figure 29), LLM-judge evaluation scores (Table 8), and latency/throughput measurements (Figure 6, Table 11)—is reported as a single number or single-run measurement. No confidence intervals, standard deviations, standard errors, or p-values are provided. The paper does not specify how many runs were averaged (if any) for any measurement.

**The consequence.** It is impossible to determine whether the reported differences between methods are statistically reliable or within noise. Several of the paper's key comparative claims rest on small absolute differences:

- OScaR vs. OTT on Llama-3.1-8B LongBench-E: 41.75 vs. 40.74 (1.01 percentage point difference)
- OScaR vs. 16-bit on Qwen3-VL-8B DocVQA: 95.01 vs. 94.93 (0.08 ANLS point difference)
- OScaR vs. TurboQuant+ on MMAU-Pro Good Rate: 29.8 vs. 27.0 (2.8 percentage point difference)

On LongBench-E, the 500-question test set is split across 8 tasks, meaning each task has roughly 60–65 test questions. A 1-point difference on an 8-task average could easily be within the sampling error of a 500-question test. For MMAU-Pro, the LLM judge introduces an additional source of variance that is entirely uncharacterized—if the judge's scores have a standard deviation of even 2–3 points on the percentage scale, the reported 2.8-point Good Rate advantage over TurboQuant+ may not be statistically significant.

For latency measurements, the absence of information about warmup runs, number of timing iterations, and synchronization primitives (e.g., whether `cuda.synchronize()` was used, whether kernel launch overhead is included) makes the measurements impossible to reproduce or validate independently.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no variance characterization anywhere. The NIAH evaluation (Figure 29) with single-trial retrieval at each (context length, depth) grid point is particularly vulnerable to noise—a single lucky or unlucky retrieval at one grid cell can change the overall accuracy by several percentage points. The MMAU-Pro LLM judge evaluation (Section 5.2, Appendix R) mentions that "we evaluate these responses using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as an LLM judge" but does not report judge calibration, inter-judge agreement, or score variance across multiple evaluation runs.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not mention this as a limitation, does not provide any variance estimates, and does not qualify any accuracy claims with uncertainty bounds. This is a standard expectation in empirical ML research, and its absence means readers cannot assess the reliability of the reported comparisons. For practitioners, this is especially problematic because deployment decisions—choosing between OScaR and a competing method—may hinge on accuracy differences that are within the unmeasured noise floor.

---

### The Over-Performance of OScaR Relative to the 16-Bit Baseline on Multiple Benchmarks Is Unexplained and Untrustworthy Without Investigation

**The assumption or constraint.** On three separate benchmarks, OScaR under INT2 quantization achieves higher accuracy than the 16-bit full-precision baseline: NIAH retrieval accuracy (96.5% vs. 96.0%), DocVQA on Qwen3-VL-8B (95.01 vs. 94.93 ANLS), and all three MMAU-Pro metrics on Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B (Open-ended QA: 67.4 vs. 66.2; Good Rate: 29.8 vs. 27.8; AIF: 88.5 vs. 87.4). The paper does not investigate, explain, or even remark on these anomalous results beyond reporting them.

**The consequence.** These results are either evidence of a genuine beneficial effect of OScaR's transformations (possibly regularization of attention scores via Hadamard rotation and token-wise normalization, or reduction of numerical noise that the 16-bit model is sensitive to) or they are artifacts of insufficient measurement precision (judge variance, single-run noise, benchmark instability). Without investigation, neither interpretation can be ruled out, and the reported over-performance cannot be relied upon.

If the over-performance is genuine, it would be a significant finding—that a quantization-time pre-processing pipeline can improve model accuracy over full precision—and would merit detailed analysis and explanation. If it is an artifact of measurement noise, then the paper's headline results may overstate OScaR's performance, and the true accuracy under INT2 is likely slightly below the 16-bit baseline (as it is on most other benchmarks) rather than above it.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The anomalous results appear in: NIAH (Figure 29, Appendix O) where OScaR at 96.5% exceeds the 16-bit baseline at 96.0%; DocVQA on Qwen3-VL-8B (Table 7) where OScaR at 95.01 exceeds the baseline at 94.93; and MMAU-Pro (Table 8) where OScaR exceeds the baseline on all three metrics. The paper provides no analysis, no ablation investigating the cause, no measurement of whether the effect is statistically significant, and no discussion of whether these anomalous results should be interpreted as genuine improvements or measurement noise. The results are simply reported alongside all other results without special comment.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. This is a significant oversight. For a methods paper where the stated goal is to achieve "near-lossless performance" (not to improve over full precision), the anomalous over-performance results should have triggered additional investigation—at minimum, multiple evaluation runs to assess whether the effect is stable, and ideally, an analysis of whether the Hadamard transform or token-wise normalization has a regularizing effect on attention scores that might explain the improvement. By reporting these anomalous results without comment, the paper leaves open a question that undermines confidence in the reliability of its entire evaluation: if the baseline numbers cannot be trusted to represent the true model performance (since OScaR sometimes exceeds them for unexplained reasons), then how should we interpret the gaps between OScaR and competing quantized methods?

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the diagnostic framework for KV cache quantization from **channel-wise outlier mitigation** to **token-wise norm equalization**, establishing Token Norm Imbalance (TNI) as the primary causal mechanism through which per-channel quantization collapses under extreme compression. Before OScaR, the field operated under an implicit additive model: channel-wise outliers are the problem, and solutions accumulate—add rotation to redistribute outlier energy, add mixed-precision preservation for residual outliers, add residual error correction for what remains. Each component addressed a symptom, and more components meant better fidelity at the cost of greater complexity. OScaR demonstrates that this additive framework was targeting the wrong structural variable.

The conceptual shift is cleanly captured by Equation 11 (Appendix G): the per-channel quantization error lower bound depends on `(‖k_max‖₂ - ‖k_min‖₂)²`—the squared difference in token L2 norms within a block—not on the magnitude of per-channel outliers, not on the variance within individual channels, not on any property that rotation alone can fix. This reframes the problem from "how do we suppress channel-wise outliers?" (which the field has been asking since at least KIVI and QuaRot) to "how do we equalize token norms so that per-channel quantization parameters aren't stretched across divergent token groups?" This is a **diagnostic reframing** rather than a paradigm shift—the underlying quantization techniques (per-channel grouping, Hadamard rotation, uniform quantization) remain the same, but the understanding of *why* they fail and *what* intervention is needed changes fundamentally.

The paper reconciles a specific contradiction in prior work: why rotation-based methods (QuaRot, RotateKV) work well at moderate bit-widths but degrade sharply at INT2, while outlier-preservation methods (OTT) help but don't fully close the gap. The answer is that neither approach addresses TNI directly. Rotation smooths per-channel distributions, making per-token quantization viable, but at INT2 the residual TNI-induced errors dominate regardless of how smooth individual channels are. Outlier preservation removes the worst tokens from quantization, but TNI is a continuous distribution—the second-worst, third-worst, and tenth-worst tokens still expand the quantization range relative to the median token. Only token-wise normalization directly attacks the `‖k_max - k_min‖` term in the error bound, and only when preceded by rotation to prevent the Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact.

This reframing redirects research attention in two clear ways. First, it makes **pre-transformation design** the central research question for extreme KV cache quantization, displacing quantizer complexity as the primary axis of innovation. The paper's Pareto front analysis (Appendix U, Figure 9) provides the empirical argument: OScaR's simple INT2 uniform quantizer with careful pre-transformations achieves higher accuracy than TurboQuant+'s 2.5-bit mixed-precision quantizer with Lloyd-Max optimization at less than half the computational cost. This suggests that investments in better quantizer design (non-uniform grids, adaptive bit allocation, learned quantization parameters) may yield diminishing returns compared to investments in pre-transformations that make the data distribution intrinsically compatible with simple quantization.

Second, it establishes **mutual necessity** as a design principle that may apply beyond OScaR's specific components. The central tension—fixing token-wise norm disparities (via scaling) re-activates channel-wise artifacts unless channel structure is first neutralized (via rotation)—is a general phenomenon that likely manifests whenever per-channel quantization meets data with both token-level and channel-level structure. Any future method that addresses one axis of variation without considering its interaction with the other risks the same failure mode that OScaR's ablation (Table 9) documents: applying Omni-Token Scaling alone worsens performance from the already-degraded INT2 baseline because it creates the Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact. The paper's key design insight—that the order of operations matters, and that both components are individually insufficient but jointly sufficient—provides a template for evaluating future proposals: does the method address the entanglement between token-wise and channel-wise variation, or does it treat them as independent problems?

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterizing the crossover point where OScaR's online rotation cost exceeds memory bandwidth savings as a function of sequence length, batch size, and model architecture.** Table 11 shows that OScaR is slower than BF16 FlashDecoding-v2 at 1K context (25.1 vs. 19.5 ms/token) but achieves a 3.0× speedup at 128K. The crossover occurs somewhere between 4K and 8K, but the paper does not provide a general model for predicting this crossover. A strong follow-up would measure OScaR's latency across a dense grid of (context length, batch size, model size) configurations and fit an analytical model that expresses the crossover as a function of the ratio of memory bandwidth time (proportional to context length) to compute overhead time (fixed per-token rotation cost). This would allow practitioners to determine, for their specific deployment parameters, whether OScaR's rotation overhead is justified by memory savings, without having to run their own measurements. The experiment would also reveal whether the crossover point shifts with model scale—larger models have proportionally larger weight matrices and KV caches, changing the balance between compute-bound and memory-bound phases.

**Replacing the Hadamard transform with a learned or data-dependent rotation that jointly optimizes for channel smoothness and quantization error.** Canalized Rotation uses a fixed Hadamard matrix, which has the advantage of efficient computation (FHT) and orthogonality (preserving inner products), but is not optimized for the specific per-channel quantization objective. A natural extension would train a rotation matrix (or a parameterized family of rotations) on a calibration set to minimize the post-rotation, post-scaling quantization error directly. The critical constraint is that the learned rotation must remain efficiently computable—a general dense rotation matrix costs `O(d²)` per token, which would negate OScaR's efficiency advantage. Promising directions include learning a product of sparse orthogonal transforms (e.g., a sequence of Givens rotations or Householder reflections with structured sparsity patterns), learning a butterfly-structured matrix with trainable coefficients (maintaining `O(d log d)` complexity), or learning a channel permutation to apply before a fixed Hadamard transform. A strong evaluation would measure (i) the reduction in INT2 quantization error on a held-out calibration set relative to the fixed Hadamard, (ii) the computational cost of the learned transform relative to the FHT, and (iii) downstream accuracy on LongBench-E or equivalent benchmarks. The paper's finding that the rsqrt approximation works as well as exact L2 normalization (Table 10) suggests that the quantization pipeline is robust to small perturbations in the pre-transformation, so a learned rotation that is only approximately orthogonal might still be effective.

**Testing the TNI hypothesis under weight quantization and activation quantization—does token-wise normalization benefit these settings, or is it specific to KV cache per-channel quantization?** The paper's theoretical analysis (Equation 11) applies specifically to per-channel quantization, where shared parameters span multiple tokens. Weight quantization is typically per-channel or per-group along the input dimension, not across tokens, so TNI in the KV cache sense may not directly apply. Activation quantization, however, does involve per-token or per-channel grouping across the batch/sequence dimensions. If TNI manifests in activation tensors—particularly in the hidden states between transformer layers, where attention sinks and massive activations are known to create token-wise outliers (Sun et al., 2024; Su et al., 2025)—then the same canalized-rotation-plus-token-scaling pipeline might improve activation quantization fidelity. A concrete experiment would profile the L2 norm distribution of hidden states across tokens in a forward pass (analogous to Figures 3, 10–18 but for FFN inputs/outputs), measure whether TNI is present with similar severity to KV cache TNI, and apply OScaR-style pre-transformations before activation quantization. The key question is whether the Scaling-Induced Outlier Artifact also manifests in activation tensors—if hidden states lack the channel-wise outlier structure that necessitates the rotation-before-scaling ordering, then Omni-Token Scaling alone might suffice, making the pipeline even simpler.

**Stress-testing OScaR on models with Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) and Multi-Query Attention (MQA), where the relationship between heads and KV entries differs from standard Multi-Head Attention.** OScaR's Hadamard transform and token-wise normalization operate on per-head Key vectors, and the paper's experiments use models with standard MHA or models where the number of KV heads equals the number of query heads (Qwen3-VL, Qwen3-Omni). In GQA (used by Llama-3.1-8B, which the paper does evaluate) and MQA (used by older models like PaLM), multiple query heads share a single KV head. This sharing could affect TNI patterns: a single KV head must serve multiple query heads with potentially different attention patterns, which might produce different token norm distributions. It could also affect the interaction with RoPE—in GQA/MQA, RoPE is applied to the shared KV head, and the Hadamard transform's interaction with RoPE across different query heads sharing the same Key might introduce subtle attention score distortions that don't occur in MHA. A strong evaluation would profile TNI patterns in GQA and MQA models at multiple scales, compare OScaR's accuracy against baselines specifically on GQA/MQA architectures, and measure whether the attention score equivalence proof (Section 4.2) requires modification for shared KV heads. The paper's evaluation of Llama-3.1-8B (which uses GQA with 8 KV heads and 32 query heads) provides some evidence that OScaR works under GQA, but a systematic comparison across GQA ratios (1:1 MHA, 1:4 GQA, 1:32 MQA) would clarify whether TNI mitigation is architecture-dependent.

**Evaluating OScaR on long-form text generation quality—perplexity, human evaluation, or task-completion metrics for open-ended generation—to determine whether INT2 KV cache quantization is viable beyond short-answer understanding tasks.** The paper's text-only evaluations are exclusively on benchmarks requiring short-answer extraction or multiple-choice selection (LongBench-E, NIAH). In long-form generation, quantization errors in early tokens' KV entries propagate through hundreds or thousands of subsequent attention computations, potentially causing topic drift, factual inconsistency, or degraded coherence that short-answer accuracy would not capture. A strong evaluation would generate long-form outputs (e.g., 500+ token summaries, dialogues, or stories) from OScaR-quantized models and compare against BF16 baselines using (i) automatic metrics like perplexity on a reference corpus (e.g., WikiText-103 or PG-19), (ii) lexical and semantic metrics like ROUGE-L and BERTScore for summarization tasks, and (iii) human evaluation of coherence, relevance, and factuality on a sample of outputs. The key question is whether the "near-lossless" claim on LongBench-E and DocVQA extends to generation, or whether there's a threshold effect where quantization errors remain benign for short outputs but compound unacceptably for long outputs. The paper's finding that OScaR slightly over-performs BF16 on some metrics (NIAH, DocVQA on Qwen3-VL-8B, MMAU-Pro) suggests there might be a regularizing effect that could even improve generation quality, but this is purely speculative without generation-focused evaluation.

**Investigating whether OScaR's anomalous over-performance relative to the 16-bit baseline on NIAH (96.5% vs. 96.0%), DocVQA (95.01 vs. 94.93), and MMAU-Pro (67.4 vs. 66.2 open-ended) reflects a genuine regularizing effect of the Hadamard-norm transform, and whether this effect can be harnessed independently of quantization.** The recurrence of OScaR exceeding the full-precision baseline across three different benchmarks and modalities is improbable under the null hypothesis that OScaR simply preserves model capabilities with minimal degradation. If the effect is real, possible mechanisms include: (i) the Hadamard rotation decorrelates Key dimensions, reducing attention score variance and preventing attention heads from over-focusing on spurious feature correlations; (ii) token-wise normalization prevents attention sink tokens with extremely low norms from dominating the softmax, forcing more uniform attention distribution that benefits retrieval and reasoning; or (iii) the combined transform acts as a form of implicit regularization that reduces overfitting to the pretraining data distribution, improving generalization to the evaluation distribution. A strong follow-up would ablate these mechanisms: test whether Hadamard rotation alone (without quantization or scaling) improves BF16 accuracy; test whether token-wise normalization alone (without rotation or quantization) improves BF16 accuracy; and measure attention entropy and attention sink concentration with and without OScaR's transformations to determine whether attention patterns become more uniform. If a genuine regularization effect exists, it could be applied during inference even when quantization is not needed, as a lightweight alternative to more expensive ensembling or test-time augmentation. The critical first step is establishing statistical significance: re-running all three anomalous benchmarks with multiple seeds and reporting confidence intervals to confirm the over-performance is not measurement noise. Until this is done, the anomalous results remain suggestive but unreliable.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Long-context document understanding and retrieval at scale.** For applications that process large document collections—legal contract review, scientific literature search, enterprise knowledge base QA—context lengths routinely exceed 32K tokens per query, and the KV cache becomes the dominant memory consumer. At 128K context on a single H20 GPU, OScaR's 3.0× decoding speedup (30.9 vs. 92.9 ms/token) and 5.3× memory reduction directly translate to serving 5× more concurrent users or processing 3× more documents per GPU-hour. The near-lossless accuracy on LongBench-E (0.82 percentage point gap to BF16 on Qwen3-8B) and improved retrieval on NIAH (96.5% vs. 96.0% BF16) mean these efficiency gains do not come at the cost of degraded output quality. An organization processing 1 million documents per day at 32K average context length would reduce GPU-hours by roughly 60%, assuming a workload mix that includes both short-context queries (where OScaR's online rotation overhead reduces the net benefit) and long-context queries (where memory bandwidth savings dominate).

**On-device and edge deployment of multi-modal assistants.** Multi-modal models like Qwen3-VL-4B are candidates for on-device deployment (mobile phones, AR/VR headsets, robotics platforms), where GPU HBM is severely constrained (typically 4–16 GB for mobile SoCs). At INT2 with OScaR, the KV cache of Qwen3-VL-4B shrinks by approximately 5×, making it feasible to process high-resolution images or short video clips without exhausting device memory. The paper's results on OCRBench (838 vs. 852 BF16, a 1.6% gap) and DocVQA (93.85 vs. 94.23 ANLS, a 0.4% gap) demonstrate that document understanding and visual QA remain accurate enough for practical use—a user photographing a menu, receipt, or sign would not perceive degradation. The key practical consideration is whether OScaR's online rotation overhead (the fused Hadamard-Norm kernel running on every generated token) is compatible with the thermal and power constraints of mobile GPUs. The paper's CUDA kernels use Tensor Cores, which are available on mobile NVIDIA platforms (Jetson series) but not on Qualcomm or Apple NPUs—porting to these accelerators would require re-implementing the FHT using equivalent vector/SIMD instructions.

**High-throughput batch inference for multi-modal data processing pipelines.** Applications that process large volumes of image-text pairs—automated document digitization (invoices, forms, reports), content moderation (screening images and associated text), or training data generation for multi-modal models—run inference at high batch sizes to maximize GPU utilization. At batch size 48 and 4K context on Qwen3-8B, OScaR increases throughput by 4.1× (1,354 vs. 331 tokens/s) and reduces memory by 5.3×. This means a single H20 GPU can process 4× more images per second, or equivalently, the same throughput can be achieved with 75% fewer GPUs. For a pipeline processing 10 million image-text pairs, the infrastructure cost savings are substantial. The near-lossless accuracy on DocVQA (95.01 vs. 94.93 BF16 on Qwen3-VL-8B) and OCRBench (856 vs. 858 BF16) means these savings do not compromise output quality. The main practical consideration is that OScaR's residual buffer mechanism adds latency jitter—every 128 tokens, a quantization flush operation occurs, which may cause periodic slowdowns in latency-sensitive streaming applications.

**Omni-modal voice assistants and audio understanding systems.** For systems that process long audio streams—meeting transcription with speaker diarization, voice assistants with multi-turn conversation history, audio content analysis for podcasts or call centers—the KV cache accumulates tokens from both the audio encoder and the text decoder, creating precisely the mixed-modality TNI patterns that OScaR is designed to handle (Figures 20–21). The paper's MMAU-Pro results are the most relevant evidence: OScaR exceeds the BF16 baseline on all three metrics (Open-ended QA: 67.4 vs. 66.2, Good Rate: 29.8 vs. 27.8, AIF: 88.5 vs. 87.4), and the AIF improvement of 1.1 points on a deterministic metric is the most trustworthy indicator of genuine benefit (since it cannot be attributed to LLM judge variance). If this improvement is real and generalizes, OScaR would be strictly preferable to BF16 for audio instruction following—better accuracy at 5× lower memory. The caveat is that MMAU-Pro uses relatively short audio clips (tens of seconds), and the evaluation is on a single omni-modal model (Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B). Validation on hour-long audio with a different architecture (e.g., Gemini-style audio processing, or Whisper-based transcription models) would be needed before deploying in production audio pipelines.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions OScaR against several named alternatives through both accuracy benchmarks and efficiency analysis, making explicit tradeoffs that guide method selection:

- **Prefer OScaR over KIVI when** you are deploying at INT2 and can tolerate ~1.5× higher per-token computation (123.0M vs. 81.9M decode ops per step) in exchange for meaningfully higher accuracy (1.01 point improvement on LongBench-E Llama-3.1-8B average, 2.1 point improvement on NIAH). The crossover context length where OScaR becomes faster than the BF16 baseline in wall-clock time is between 4K and 8K (Table 11)—below this, KIVI's lower compute overhead makes it preferable for latency-sensitive applications. For deployment scenarios with typical context lengths below 32K and accuracy requirements that can tolerate KIVI's degradation (e.g., the 1.61-point gap to BF16 on Qwen3-8B LongBench-E), KIVI remains the more efficient option despite its lower accuracy.

- **Prefer OScaR over TurboQuant+ when** you prioritize accuracy at any context length, and particularly for long-context deployment (32K+ tokens). OScaR achieves higher accuracy on every benchmark tested despite TurboQuant+'s 2.5-bit advantage and sophisticated quantizer design, and OScaR's latency becomes strictly better after ~32K–48K context (30.9 vs. 72.9 ms/token at 128K). The only regime where TurboQuant+ is preferable is short-context, latency-critical deployment (<8K tokens), where its llama.cpp implementation achieves 3.2× lower latency than OScaR's PyTorch implementation at 1K context (7.8 vs. 25.1 ms/token). However, this comparison is confounded by the implementation framework (llama.cpp vs. PyTorch), and OScaR implemented in llama.cpp would likely narrow or reverse this gap.

- **Prefer OScaR over rotation-only methods (QuaRot, RotateKV) when** deploying at INT2—the accuracy gap is large and consistent across benchmarks (on Qwen3-8B LongBench-E, QuaRot drops 9.4 points from BF16 vs. OScaR's 0.8 points; on Qwen3-VL-4B OCRBench, QuaRot drops 79 points vs. OScaR's 14 points). Rotation-only methods may remain viable at INT4 where TNI-induced errors are less severe, but at INT2 the absence of token-wise normalization is disqualifying.

- **Prefer OScaR over OTT when** you need a uniform quantization pipeline without mixed-precision storage. OTT's outlier token preservation (5 tokens per head at FP16) introduces hardware fragmentation and mixed-precision overhead that the paper argues limits "achievable effective compression" (Section 2.2). OScaR achieves higher accuracy than OTT on most benchmarks while maintaining uniform INT2 storage, simplifying memory management and kernel design. OTT may still be preferred if the number of outlier tokens can be tuned per-layer to optimize the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff, a capability OScaR does not offer since its token-wise normalization is uniform across all layers.
