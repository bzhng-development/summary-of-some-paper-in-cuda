# Parameter Efficient Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

**ArXiv:** [2403.10704](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.10704)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper empirically studies whether **Parameter Efficient Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (PE-RLHF)** — applying LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) fine-tuning to both the reward model training phase and the reinforcement learning policy phase — can match the alignment quality of standard full-parameter RLHF while reducing computational cost.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper empirically studies whether **Parameter Efficient Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (PE-RLHF)** — applying LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) fine-tuning to both the reward model training phase and the reinforcement learning policy phase — can match the alignment quality of standard full-parameter RLHF while reducing computational cost. Across six diverse datasets spanning summarization (Reddit TL;DR, BOLT Message Summarization), harmless/helpful response generation (Anthropic-HH Harmlessness, Stanford Human Preferences), UI automation (AndroidControl), and visual question answering (VQAv2), and using PaLM 2 and Gemini model families, the authors find that PE-RLHF achieves comparable performance to standard RLHF while cutting reward model peak HBM usage by up to 50% and training time by up to 90% (1.9× faster), and reducing RL policy training peak HBM by up to 27% and time by up to 30% (1.3× faster). The work establishes that LoRA-based parameter-efficient RLHF can substitute for full fine-tuning in alignment pipelines, with the benefit becoming more pronounced as the underlying backbone model grows in size, while training less than 0.2% of the total parameters.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RLHF Is Powerful But Computationally Prohibitive

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is that **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), while effective at aligning large language and vision-language models with human preferences, carries computational costs that restrict its use to organizations with substantial infrastructure**. The paper identifies three specific pain points (Section 1):

1. **Training complexity**: The RLHF pipeline involves three sequential phases — supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reward model training, and reinforcement learning — each requiring careful orchestration of model copies, data pipelines, and optimization procedures.
2. **Memory burden during RL**: The RL loop necessitates maintaining *extra model copies* in memory during training — specifically, the reward model must be loaded for scoring generated responses, and the anchor model (the frozen SFT checkpoint) must be simultaneously available to compute the KL divergence penalty term that prevents the policy from drifting into reward-hacked gibberish. This multi-model memory footprint exceeds what standard fine-tuning requires by a substantial margin.
3. **Training time**: Full-parameter fine-tuning of both the reward model and the policy model requires updating all weights at each training step, with modern optimizers like Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2017) and Adafactor (Shazeer and Stern, 2018) tracking multiple per-parameter statistics (momentum, variance) that multiply memory and computation.

These costs collectively create a barrier: organizations without access to large-scale GPU clusters, high-memory accelerators, or massive compute budgets are effectively excluded from using RLHF to align their models. Given that alignment is increasingly recognized as essential for deploying safe, helpful, and instruction-following models (Bommasani et al., 2022), this computational barrier has direct consequences for the broader AI ecosystem's ability to produce well-behaved systems.

### Why This Problem Matters Now

The timing of this work is significant for several interconnected reasons.

**Alignment is becoming non-optional, not a luxury.** As LLMs and VLMs are deployed in higher-stakes settings — healthcare, legal advice, content moderation, UI automation — the gap between raw pretrained capability and safe, aligned behavior widens. Models like GPT-4 (OpenAI et al., 2023) and Gemini (Team et al., 2023; Reid et al., 2024) demonstrate remarkable raw performance, but without alignment they produce harmful advice, fail to follow nuanced instructions, and exhibit undesirable behaviors (Section 1). The paper cites a growing body of work showing alignment improves instruction following (Ouyang et al., 2022), safety properties (Bai et al., 2022a,b; Glaese et al., 2022), helpfulness (Bai et al., 2022a; Glaese et al., 2022), summarization characteristics (Stiennon et al., 2020), and visual instruction understanding (Sun et al., 2023). As the field moves toward broader deployment, alignment is shifting from a research curiosity to a deployment requirement.

**Model sizes are growing faster than individual researchers' compute budgets.** The trend toward ever-larger models means that even if RLHF training cost scales linearly with model size, the absolute cost quickly exceeds what academic labs and smaller industry teams can afford. A method that reduces memory footprint by ~50% and training time by up to 90% for reward modeling (Table 1) effectively doubles the model size that a fixed compute budget can align, or halves the cost of aligning a given model. This democratization effect is particularly important for research on alignment itself — if only large industrial labs can run RLHF, the community's ability to study, critique, and improve alignment techniques is severely constrained.

**Multi-modal alignment compounds the cost problem.** Vision-language models like Gemini Pro introduce additional computational demands because the reward model and policy must process both visual and text modalities. The paper explicitly includes visual question answering (VQAv2; Antol et al., 2015; Goyal et al., 2017a) in its benchmark suite, making the point that as models become multi-modal, the compute burden of alignment grows proportionally, and parameter-efficient methods become correspondingly more valuable.

### The Existing Solution Landscape and Its Gaps

**Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) for standard supervised tasks is well-established — but unexplored for RLHF.** LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) introduced the idea of factorizing weight updates into low-rank matrices, attaching trainable adapters to attention projection matrices while keeping the pretrained backbone frozen. This technique had been extensively validated for standard supervised fine-tuning tasks: adapting LLMs to downstream classification, generation, and instruction-following tasks with a fraction of the trainable parameters. Subsequent PEFT methods like DoRA (Liu et al., 2024) and Representation Fine-Tuning (ReFT; Wu et al., 2024b) further refined the approach.

However, as the paper explicitly states in Section 6.4: **"To the best of our knowledge, there hasn't been any work prior to ours extensively benchmarking parameter efficient approaches for RLHF."** This is the critical gap. RLHF is not simply "fine-tuning with a different loss." It involves two interdependent training phases (reward modeling → policy optimization), each with distinct data, objectives, and failure modes. Whether LoRA adapters can:
- Learn to model human preferences from pairwise comparison data with the same fidelity as full fine-tuning,
- Support stable policy gradient optimization in the RL phase without introducing instability,
- Generalize across diverse alignment tasks (summarization, harmlessness, helpfulness, UI control, visual QA),

were all open questions. The paper identifies this as a specific gap: the PEFT community had demonstrated efficiency, and the RLHF community had demonstrated alignment effectiveness, but **no one had systematically tested whether PEFT could be plugged into RLHF without sacrificing alignment quality**.

**Why the gap exists: RLHF's unique demands.** The paper's framing implies several reasons why PEFT-for-RLHF was not a foregone conclusion:

- **Reward modeling requires discriminating subtle quality differences**: In pairwise preference data, the model must learn to assign higher rewards to responses that are only *marginally* better — a more nuanced task than standard supervised classification or generation. It was unclear whether low-rank adapters with severely constrained capacity could capture these fine-grained preference distinctions.
- **RL introduces distribution shift**: During RL training, the policy generates its own responses, which may drift from the distribution the reward model was trained on. The KL penalty with the anchor policy helps, but it was unknown whether LoRA-trained policies and value models would remain stable under this feedback loop.
- **The multi-model memory burden is structural, not just parameter-count-driven**: Even if LoRA reduces the trainable parameters for the policy model, the RL loop still requires loading the reward model and anchor model. The paper's contribution includes demonstrating that LoRA applied to both the RM and policy phases yields compound memory savings — you save on the RM itself (which must be loaded during RL) and on the policy and value models (which are being optimized).

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper situates itself as a **benchmarking and empirical validation study**, not a method proposal. This is explicit in the abstract:

> "We empirically evaluate the setup of Parameter Efficient Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (PE-RLHF) that leverages LoRA fine-tuning for Reward Modeling, and Reinforcement Learning."

The contribution is establishing — for the first time, with systematic evidence — that LoRA works for RLHF. The paper explicitly chooses LoRA over more recent PEFT/ReFT methods because it is "widely adopted" (Section 1), and explicitly hopes their results "will motivate the benchmarking of other PEFT and ReFT approaches on RLHF tasks." This framing is important: the paper is opening a research direction (parameter-efficient RLHF) rather than closing one with a definitive "best method" claim.

**The scope is deliberately broad.** Rather than testing on a single task, the paper covers six datasets spanning five distinct task types: summarization (Reddit TL;DR, BOLT Messages), harmless generation (Anthropic-HH Harmlessness), helpful generation (Stanford Human Preferences), UI automation (AndroidControl), and visual question answering (VQAv2). This diversity is not incidental — it directly addresses the concern that PEFT methods might work on simple tasks but fail on complex ones, or might work for text but not for vision-language tasks. The inclusion of both PaLM 2 (text-focused) and Gemini Pro (vision-language) model families further broadens the claim.

**The paper explicitly contrasts with related alignment methods** that could be seen as alternatives or competitors (Section 6.3). It acknowledges that RLHF is one among several alignment techniques — including RAFT (Dong et al., 2023), RRHF (Yuan et al., 2023), DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), SLiC-HF (Zhao et al., 2023), and Pairwise Cringe Optimization (Xu et al., 2023) — but focuses on RLHF because it "is particularly popular." The implication is that demonstrating parameter efficiency for RLHF has the broadest practical impact. However, the paper does not compare PE-RLHF against these alternatives, which is a recognized limitation — we don't know whether parameter-efficient DPO, for instance, would be even more efficient.

**Infrastructure considerations are part of the positioning.** Section 6.5 notes that while the Transformer Reinforcement Learning (TRL) library (von Werra et al., 2020) offers similar functionalities, "its multi-adapter RL feature remains experimental and lacks support for parallelization and vision modalities." This highlights a practical gap: even if someone wanted to try PE-RLHF before this paper, the tooling wasn't mature enough to support rigorous benchmarking across diverse tasks and modalities. The paper's implementation uses PAX (Paxml, 2022) and SeqIO (Roberts et al., 2022) with a custom RL training loop, representing a more production-ready infrastructure.

**The paper identifies a conceptual connection to overfitting and reward hacking.** Section 6.2 notes that "overfitting the reward function can be problematic, requiring techniques like early stopping or parameter reduction to ensure optimal policy training." This is a subtle but important point: parameter-efficient methods, by limiting capacity, might actually *help* with the reward overfitting problem that plagues RLHF — a form of implicit regularization. The paper does not explore this hypothesis in depth, but it provides a theoretical motivation beyond mere computational efficiency for why PEFT might be not just equal to, but potentially better than, full fine-tuning for certain RLHF configurations.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper describes a system for aligning large language and vision-language models with human preferences using reinforcement learning, where the key innovation is replacing full-parameter fine-tuning with LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) adapters in **both** the reward model training and the policy optimization phases. The problem this solves is the prohibitive computational cost of standard RLHF — particularly its memory footprint (from maintaining multiple model copies) and training time — and it solves it by training only a tiny fraction of parameters (less than 0.2% of the total) while keeping the pretrained backbone frozen, thereby reducing memory usage by up to 50% and training time by up to 90% without sacrificing alignment quality.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The PE-RLHF system consists of four major components arranged in a sequential pipeline:

1. **Supervised Fine-Tuned (SFT) Anchor Model** — A pretrained language or vision-language model that has been fine-tuned on high-quality task-specific data (e.g., summarization examples). This model serves as both the initialization for the RL policy and the anchor for KL regularization during RL training. It is **frozen** throughout subsequent steps.

2. **LoRA-Adapted Reward Model (RM)** — The same architecture as the base model, but augmented with LoRA adapters attached to every attention projection matrix. Only these adapters are trained; the backbone remains frozen. The RM is trained on human preference data (either pairwise comparisons or binary good/bad labels) to output a scalar reward score for any given (input, response) pair. After training, the adapters are merged into the projection matrices via a one-time addition operation, producing a functionally equivalent fully-merged reward model.

3. **LoRA-Adapted Policy + Value Models** — During the RL phase, both the policy model (the model being optimized to generate high-reward responses) and the value model (used for advantage estimation in the REINFORCE algorithm) are equipped with their own independent sets of LoRA adapters. The policy generates responses to input prompts; the value model estimates expected future reward from each partial generation. Both sets of adapters are trained while the backbone stays frozen.

4. **KL Regularization via Anchor Model** — The frozen SFT model is loaded in memory during RL to compute a KL divergence penalty term between the current policy's output distribution and the original SFT distribution. This prevents the policy from drifting into regions where the reward model's scores become meaningless or where the policy produces degenerate, reward-hacking outputs.

Information flows as follows: an input prompt enters the system → the policy model (with LoRA adapters) generates a complete response → the pre-trained reward model (with merged LoRA adapters) scores that response → the value model estimates a baseline for advantage calculation → the REINFORCE policy gradient updates both the policy and value LoRA adapters using the reward score minus the KL penalty with the anchor model's log probabilities. The reward model and anchor model are loaded in inference-only mode; only the LoRA adapters on the policy and value models receive gradient updates.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the standard RLHF pipeline that PE-RLHF modifies — understanding what the three phases do (SFT, RM training, RL) and what components are in play at each stage — because PE-RLHF's efficiency gains come from strategically inserting LoRA adapters at specific points in this pipeline.
- **Second**, the LoRA mechanism itself — how low-rank adaptation works mathematically, what matrices are adapted, and why it reduces memory and computation — since everything else depends on this technique.
- **Third**, the reward model training phase under PE-RLHF — how the RM is constructed with LoRA adapters, how it's trained on preference data, what the loss functions are, and why the merged-at-inference approach matters.
- **Fourth**, the reinforcement learning phase under PE-RLHF — how LoRA is applied to both policy and value models, what RL algorithm is used (REINFORCE with baseline), how the KL penalty is computed using a separate frozen anchor model, and why this combination still requires careful memory management despite parameter efficiency.
- **Fifth**, the training infrastructure and hyperparameters that make PE-RLHF work in practice — covering the specific optimizers, learning rates, batch sizes, LoRA rank settings, and model size configurations tested.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is an **empirical benchmarking paper** whose core idea is that applying LoRA parameter-efficient fine-tuning to both phases of the RLHF pipeline (reward modeling and reinforcement learning) yields alignment quality comparable to full-parameter fine-tuning while dramatically reducing computational cost. Rather than proposing a new algorithm, the work systematically validates that an existing efficiency technique (LoRA) transfers successfully to the RLHF setting across diverse tasks and modalities.

#### The Standard RLHF Pipeline (What PE-RLHF Modifies)

Before explaining how PE-RLHF works, we need to understand the standard RLHF pipeline it modifies. The paper reviews the three-phase pipeline introduced by Stiennon et al. (2020) in Appendix A.1.

**Phase 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).** A pretrained LLM or VLM is fine-tuned on a high-quality labeled dataset for a specific downstream task — for example, taking Reddit posts and human-written summaries to train a summarization model. This uses standard token-level cross-entropy loss (next-token prediction). The resulting model is denoted `$\pi^{\text{Anchor}}$`. The paper states: "This is the most common type of tuning done to enable LLMs to cater to specific tasks, like summarization, dialogue generation, etc." The anchor model serves two purposes in later phases: it provides the initialization point for the RL policy (the policy starts with exactly the same weights), and it serves as the reference distribution for KL regularization (keeping the policy from straying too far from known-good behavior).

**Phase 2: Reward Model Training.** A reward model `$r_\phi$` is trained to assign scalar scores to (input, response) pairs, where higher scores indicate responses that better align with human preferences. The paper describes two alternative training formulations.

*Pairwise preference formulation (Bradley-Terry model).* Given a dataset of triplets `$\mathcal{D} = \{(x, y_w, y_l)\}$` where `$x$` is the input, `$y_w$` is the preferred response, and `$y_l$` is the dispreferred response, the reward model is trained to maximize the probability that `$y_w$` scores higher than `$y_l$`. The loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}_r(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\left(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\right)\right]$$

where `$\phi$` denotes all trainable parameters of the reward model, `$r_\phi(x, y)$` is the scalar reward predicted for response `$y$` given input `$x$`, and `$\sigma$` is the sigmoid function.

**What it computes:** for each training pair, the model computes the difference in predicted rewards between the preferred and dispreferred responses, passes this difference through the sigmoid to convert it to a probability between 0 and 1 (representing the predicted probability that `$y_w$` is preferred), and then applies the negative log-likelihood. If the model correctly assigns a much higher score to `$y_w$`, the sigmoid output is near 1 and the loss is near 0. If it assigns similar scores or prefers `$y_l$`, the sigmoid output is near 0.5 or below and the loss is large.

**Why this form:** the Bradley-Terry model (Bradley and Terry, 1952) provides a principled probabilistic model of pairwise comparisons. It assumes that each response has an underlying "quality" and that the probability of preferring one over the other depends only on the difference in their qualities, transformed through the logistic function. This is the standard loss for training preference-based reward models (used by Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022a; Stiennon et al., 2020) because it encourages the model to learn a well-calibrated ranking rather than just a binary correctness signal. An alternative like hinge loss would not produce probabilistic outputs, which matters because during RL the reward model's absolute scores (not just rankings) guide policy updates.

*Classification formulation.* When preference data comes as binary good/bad labels rather than pairs, the reward model is trained as a binary classifier:

$$\mathcal{L}_r(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y, p) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[p \log \sigma\left(r_\phi(x, y)\right) + (1 - p) \log \sigma\left(1 - r_\phi(x, y)\right)\right]$$

where `$p \in \{0, 1\}$` indicates whether the response is good (`$p = 1$`) or bad (`$p = 0$`).

**What it computes:** standard binary cross-entropy between the model's predicted reward (after sigmoid) and the binary label. When `$p = 1$`, the loss is `$-\log \sigma(r_\phi(x, y))$`, which is minimized when `$r_\phi(x, y)$` is large and positive (so `$\sigma(r_\phi) \approx 1$`). When `$p = 0$`, the loss is `$-\log(1 - \sigma(r_\phi(x, y))) = -\log \sigma(-r_\phi(x, y))$`, minimized when `$r_\phi(x, y)$` is large and negative (so `$\sigma(r_\phi) \approx 0$`).

**Why this form:** this option is provided for datasets where explicit pairwise preferences aren't available but human annotators can label individual responses as acceptable or unacceptable. The sigmoid ensures the reward is always between 0 and 1, which can be interpreted as a probability of correctness or quality. This is used for the UI Automation task (AndroidControl), where the dataset contains human demonstrations of correct actions and the reward model needs to distinguish correct from incorrect actions directly rather than ranking pairs.

The paper uses different formulations for different datasets based on how the preference data was originally collected. The Anthropic-HH Harmlessness and SHP Helpfulness datasets use pairwise preferences; the Reddit TL;DR and BOLT Message Summarization datasets also use pairwise preferences (following Stiennon et al., 2020); the VQAv2 dataset uses a classification-style loss; and the UI Automation dataset uses classification-style accuracy.

**Phase 3: Reinforcement Learning of the Policy.** Starting from the SFT checkpoint `$\pi^{\text{Anchor}}$`, the policy `$\pi^{\text{RL}}_\theta$` is optimized to maximize the reward signal from the frozen reward model while being penalized for deviating too far from the anchor policy. The objective is:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}\left[(1 - \beta) r_\phi(y|x) - \beta D_{\text{KL}}\left(\pi^{\text{RL}}_\theta(y|x) \| \pi^{\text{Anchor}}(y|x)\right)\right]$$

where `$\theta$` denotes the policy's trainable parameters, `$\beta \in [0, 1]$` is a hyperparameter controlling the tradeoff between reward maximization and distribution preservation, and `$D_{\text{KL}}$` is the Kullback-Leibler divergence.

**What it computes:** for each response generated by the policy, the objective is a weighted combination of the reward model's score (encouraging the policy to produce high-quality outputs) minus the KL divergence from the anchor policy (penalizing the policy for producing outputs that are too different from what the SFT model would produce). The weight `$\beta$` determines the relative importance: when `$\beta = 0$`, the policy pursues reward exclusively; when `$\beta = 1$`, the policy is forced to exactly replicate the anchor.

**Why this form:** without the KL penalty, RLHF is susceptible to "reward hacking" (Everitt and Hutter, 2016; Amodei et al., 2016) — the policy discovers outputs that score highly under the reward model but are actually nonsensical, ungrammatical, or harmful because the reward model is an imperfect proxy for human preferences. The KL penalty (Fox et al., 2015; Geist et al., 2019) keeps the policy in a region of the output space where the reward model's scores are reliable (because the reward model was trained on outputs from the SFT model or similar models). It also prevents catastrophic forgetting of the capabilities acquired during pretraining and SFT. The paper notes that `$\beta$` is a hyperparameter typically set based on validation performance; they use `$\beta = 0.05$` for text summarization tasks.

The policy optimization uses the "REINFORCE for Language Models" algorithm (as used by Lee et al., 2023a), which is a policy-gradient method. In this algorithm, the policy generates complete response sequences, the reward model scores them, and the policy gradient is computed as the expected gradient of the log-probability of the generated tokens multiplied by the advantage (reward minus baseline). The value model provides the baseline for variance reduction. The specific algorithm details are not provided in the paper beyond the reference to Lee et al. (2023a), but this is a standard approach in the RLHF literature.

**The computational bottleneck.** In standard RLHF, Phases 2 and 3 update **all** parameters of the reward model, policy model, and value model. During Phase 3 specifically, the training loop must simultaneously hold in GPU memory:
- The policy model `$\pi^{\text{RL}}_\theta$` (being trained)
- The value model (being trained, typically shares architecture with policy)
- The anchor model `$\pi^{\text{Anchor}}$` (frozen, for KL computation)
- The reward model `$r_\phi$` (frozen, for scoring)
- Optimizer states (Adam/Adafactor momentum and variance buffers) for all trainable parameters

For a model with billions of parameters, the optimizer states alone can exceed the model weights by 2-3× (Adam stores two additional values per parameter). This is the problem PE-RLHF addresses by making most of these parameters frozen.

#### The LoRA Mechanism (Shared Foundation for Both Phases)

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation; Hu et al., 2021) is the parameter-efficient technique applied identically in both the reward model and RL policy phases of PE-RLHF. Understanding the mechanics is essential because the entire paper's efficiency claims rest on how LoRA transforms the training dynamics.

**Standard fine-tuning vs. LoRA.** In standard full-parameter fine-tuning, we start with pretrained weight matrices `$W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$` (for a typical attention projection, `$d$` is the input dimension and `$k$` is the output dimension). During training, we update these weights directly: `$W = W_0 + \Delta W$`, where `$\Delta W$` is a full-rank update matrix learned via gradient descent. Every element of `$W$` participates in both the forward pass (matrix multiplication) and backward pass (gradient computation), and the optimizer tracks per-element state.

In LoRA fine-tuning, the pretrained weights `$W_0$` are **frozen** (never updated). Instead, we introduce two new small matrices: `$A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$` and `$B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$`, where `$r \ll \min(d, k)$` is the LoRA rank (a hyperparameter, typically 1, 4, 8, 16, or 32 in this paper). The forward pass becomes:

$$h = W_0 x + B A x$$

where `$x \in \mathbb{R}^{k}$` is the input to this layer, `$h \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$` is the output, and the second term `$BAx$` is the low-rank update.

**What it computes:** the original frozen weights `$W_0$` process the input as usual, producing the base output. In parallel, the input `$x$` is first projected through `$A$` from dimension `$k$` down to the much smaller dimension `$r$` (the bottleneck), then projected back up from `$r$` to `$d$` through `$B$`. The two outputs are added elementwise. Only `$A$` and `$B$` receive gradients; `$W_0$` is treated as a constant.

**Why this form:** the key insight is that weight updates during fine-tuning often have low "intrinsic rank" — the changes needed to adapt a pretrained model to a new task can be expressed as combinations of relatively few basis directions, even though the full weight matrices are high-dimensional. By factorizing the update `$\Delta W$` as the product `$BA$`, LoRA constrains the update to be a matrix of rank at most `$r$`, dramatically reducing the number of trainable parameters (from `$d \times k$` to `$r \times (d + k)$`). For example, if `$d = k = 4096$` and `$r = 16$`, the trainable parameters drop from ~16.8 million to ~131,000 — a reduction of >99%.

Critically, this also reduces optimizer memory: Adam/Adafactor only needs to track states for the `$r \times (d + k)$` parameters in `$A$` and `$B$`, not the full `$d \times k$` matrix. And during inference, the low-rank update can be merged into the original weights via `$W = W_0 + B A$` (a one-time operation), after which the model runs at the exact same speed as a fully fine-tuned model with no adapter overhead.

**Where LoRA adapters are attached.** The paper states that adapters are "attached to each attention projection matrix within the model" (Section 2.1 for RMs, Section 2.2 for RL policy). In transformer architectures, these projection matrices are the query, key, value, and output projections within each attention head, and potentially the feed-forward network layers as well. The paper does not specify whether feed-forward layers also receive adapters, but the standard LoRA practice (Hu et al., 2021) typically applies adapters only to attention projections, as these are where most adaptation capacity is needed.

**LoRA rank** `$r$` is the primary hyperparameter controlling the capacity-efficiency tradeoff. Higher ranks allow the adapter to capture more complex task-specific transformations at the cost of more trainable parameters and memory. The paper sweeps ranks `$\{1, 4, 8, 16, 32\}$` across different experiments.

**PE-RLHF's double use of LoRA.** The innovation is not in LoRA itself but in applying it systematically to the RLHF pipeline. Figure 1 (RM training) and Figure 2 (RL training) illustrate the two applications:
- For RM training (Section 2.1, Figure 1): The reward model is a language model with LoRA adapters attached to each attention projection. During training, only these adapters are optimized while the language model backbone stays frozen. During inference (i.e., when the trained RM scores responses during the RL phase), "the trained LoRA adapters are combined with the projection matrices through a one-time addition operation. This results in a reward model functionally equivalent to a non-LoRA model, but trained efficiently."
- For RL training (Section 2.2, Figure 2): "PE-RLHF uses LoRA adapters for policy and value models within the reinforcement learning loop." As with the reward model, adapters are attached to each attention projection matrix in both the policy and value models, and only the adapters (plus any task-specific prediction heads) are trained. The anchor model `$\pi^{\text{Anchor}}$` and reward model `$r_\phi$` — both frozen during RL — remain in full-parameter form (no LoRA applied to them during this phase, though the RM may have been trained with LoRA and then merged).

**The critical design choice: independent adapter sets.** The paper does not explicitly state this, but it follows from the architecture that the reward model LoRA adapters (trained during Phase 2) and the policy/value LoRA adapters (trained during Phase 3) are completely independent. They are initialized separately, trained on different objectives with different data, and optimized with different hyperparameters. This independence is important because the reward model needs to learn to discriminate response quality (a regression-like task), while the policy needs to learn to generate high-quality responses (a generation task). Sharing adapters would couple these objectives in ways that likely harm both.

#### Reward Model Training Under PE-RLHF

The reward model training phase (Phase 2 of the RLHF pipeline) is the first place where PE-RLHF diverges from standard RLHF. Section 2.1 and Appendix A.3 provide the specific configuration.

**Model construction.** The base language model (PaLM 2 of size XXS, XS, or S for text tasks; Gemini Pro for visual question answering) is loaded with its pretrained weights frozen. LoRA adapters are attached to each attention projection matrix. For text-only tasks, the reward model head (a linear layer that maps the final hidden state to a scalar reward) is added on top and trained from scratch; for classification-style RMs, this head outputs a logit that passes through sigmoid.

**Training procedure.** The training loop is identical to standard RM training in terms of data and loss — the same pairwise preference or classification losses described above — but only the LoRA adapter weights and the reward head receive gradient updates. The frozen backbone weights are never updated. The optimizer (Adam or Adafactor) only needs to maintain momentum and variance buffers for the adapter parameters and the reward head, not the billions of backbone parameters.

**Hyperparameter sweep methodology.** The paper emphasizes that they performed systematic hyperparameter sweeps for each dataset and model size configuration. For the Anthropic-HH Harmlessness dataset: "We train all the harmlessness reward models using a batch size of 128, for 5,000 steps, and pick the checkpoint with the highest validation pairwise accuracy. We consider learning rates from [1e-5, 5e-5, 1e-4, 2e-4, 5e-4]. We find the best learning rate in the full-tuning setups to be 1e-5, and the best learning rate in the LoRA setups 2e-4."

This learning rate difference is notable: **LoRA training tolerates (and benefits from) a 20× higher learning rate than full fine-tuning**. This is expected because the frozen backbone provides a stable foundation; the adapter matrices `$A$` and `$B$` start from small random initialization and need to learn the task-specific transformations from scratch, which requires more aggressive updates. Full fine-tuning, by contrast, gently nudges all parameters and needs a lower learning rate to avoid destabilizing the pretrained representations.

For the Stanford Human Preferences (SHP) dataset, the sweep was more extensive: "We performed hyperparameter sweeps over different learning rates (2e-5, 5e-5, 1e-4, and 2e-4), dropout probabilities (0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2), and LoRA ranks (1, 4, 8, 16) to report the best metrics for each configuration." The optimal hyperparameters varied by model size and LoRA rank, reported in Table 5:
- PaLM 2 S (full-tuning): dropout 0.01, learning rate 1e-4
- PaLM 2 S LoRA rank 1: dropout 0.01, learning rate 1e-4
- PaLM 2 S LoRA rank 4: dropout 0.05, learning rate 2e-4
- PaLM 2 S LoRA rank 8: dropout 0.05, learning rate 1e-4
- PaLM 2 S LoRA rank 16: dropout 0.02, learning rate 2e-5
- PaLM 2 XS (full-tuning): dropout 0.05, learning rate 2e-5
- PaLM 2 XS LoRA rank 16: dropout 0.01, learning rate 2e-5
- PaLM 2 XXS (full-tuning): dropout 0.05, learning rate 2e-4
- PaLM 2 XXS LoRA rank 16: dropout 0, learning rate 2e-5

For Reddit TL;DR summarization: "We train all the Reddit TL;DR reward models using a batch size of 128, for 5,000 steps. We pick the checkpoint with the highest validation pairwise accuracy. We consider learning rates from [1e-5, 5e-5, 1e-4, 2e-4, 5e-4]. We find the best learning rate in full-tuning setups to be 1e-5, and the best learning rate in LoRA training setups 1e-4" — again, a 10× higher learning rate for LoRA.

For UI Automation (AndroidControl): "We carry out a hyperparameter sweep on the learning rate and dropout probability... We choose 4 different learning rates and dropout probabilities, and implement the sweep on the Cartesian product of these parameters." The specific ranges were: for LoRA, learning rate in {2e-5, 5e-5, 1e-4, 2e-4} and dropout in {0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1}; for full tuning, learning rate in {5e-6, 1e-5, 2e-5, 5e-5} and dropout in {0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1}. The optimal hyperparameters are listed in Table 6, showing that LoRA again uses higher learning rates (1e-4 to 2e-4) compared to full tuning (5e-6 to 1e-5).

For VQAv2 with Gemini Pro: "We train all reward models using a batch size of 128. We use a learning rate of 1e-5 for full-tuning, and 1e-4 for LoRA tuning." The pattern persists: LoRA training uses 10× higher learning rates.

**The inference-time merge operation.** After RM training completes and before the RM is used in the RL phase, the LoRA adapters are permanently merged into the backbone weights via `$W_{\text{merged}} = W_0 + BA$`. This is described as "a one-time addition operation" (Section 2.1). The merged RM is then functionally identical to a fully fine-tuned RM — same inference speed, same memory footprint during forward passes. This means that during RL training (Phase 3), the reward model provides no memory savings from having been LoRA-trained; the savings were already realized during its training (Phase 2). The memory savings during RL come from applying LoRA to the *policy and value models*, not the RM.

**A key implicit design choice: the RM is merged before RL, not kept as adapters.** The paper could have kept the RM in LoRA form during RL (with adapters separate), which would save some memory during RL since the RM's adapters would be small. However, merging ensures the RM runs at full speed during RL, which matters because every generated response must be scored by the RM. For the typical RL setup where the RM is called many times per training step (once per generated episode), inference speed is critical. The paper prioritizes RL runtime over additional memory savings.

**Evaluation metric.** Reward model quality is measured using "pairwise accuracy" for preference-based RMs — "the proportion of preferred responses ranked higher by the model among pairs of candidate responses." For classification-style RMs, it's "accuracy" — "whether the reward model score is close to the label of 0 or 1." These are standard metrics that directly measure whether the RM can distinguish good from bad outputs.

#### Reinforcement Learning of Policy Under PE-RLHF

The RL phase (Phase 3) is where PE-RLHF's memory savings are most impactful because this phase requires holding multiple models in memory simultaneously. Section 2.2 and Appendix A.3 provide the configuration.

**Components in the RL loop.** During training, the system maintains four model instances:

1. **Policy model** `$\pi^{\text{RL}}_\theta$` with LoRA adapters: actively being trained. Generates complete response sequences from input prompts using autoregressive sampling (temperature 0.7 for text tasks, 0.9 for VQAv2). Only the LoRA adapter weights receive gradients; the backbone is frozen.

2. **Value model** with LoRA adapters: actively being trained. Shares the same architecture as the policy model and is initialized from the same SFT checkpoint, but has its own independent LoRA adapters. The value model estimates the expected future reward from each partial generation (i.e., it provides a baseline for advantage calculation in the REINFORCE algorithm, reducing variance of the policy gradient estimate).

3. **Anchor model** `$\pi^{\text{Anchor}}$` (frozen, full-parameter, no adapters): the SFT checkpoint from Phase 1. Used only for computing log-probabilities of generated tokens under the original SFT distribution, to calculate the KL divergence penalty term `$D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\text{RL}}_\theta(y|x) \| \pi^{\text{Anchor}}(y|x))$`.

4. **Reward model** `$r_\phi$` (frozen, full-parameter with merged LoRA adapters from Phase 2): the trained reward model. Used for computing `$r_\phi(y|x)$` — the scalar reward for the generated response `$y$` given input `$x$`.

**What happens per training step.** The paper describes the loop using the "REINFORCE for Language Models" algorithm from Lee et al. (2023a), with specific hyperparameters given in Appendices A.3.3 and A.3.4. The sequence is:

1. **Sampling episodes**: The policy model generates a batch of complete responses. For Reddit TL;DR: "We sample 128 episodes per batch using the policy with temperature of 0.7 for decoding." For VQAv2: "We sample 32 episodes using the policy with temperature of 0.9 for decoding." Temperature controls the randomness of sampling; higher temperature means more diverse (but potentially lower-quality) outputs, which matters for exploration in RL.

2. **Reward scoring**: Each generated response is passed through the frozen reward model to compute `$r_\phi(x, y)$` — a scalar reward.

3. **Anchor logit calculation**: Each generated response is also passed through the frozen anchor model to compute `$\log \pi^{\text{Anchor}}(y|x)$` — the log-probability the SFT model would have assigned to that response. This is needed for the KL penalty.

4. **Value estimation**: The value model computes `$V(x)$` — the estimated expected future reward from the start state (input `$x$`). This serves as the baseline for advantage calculation.

5. **Policy gradient computation**: The REINFORCE advantage is computed as `$A = r_\phi(x, y) - V(x)$` (reward minus baseline). The policy gradient is then the expected gradient of the log-probability of each generated token, weighted by the advantage, minus the KL penalty:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\left[A \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y|x) - \beta \nabla_\theta D_{\text{KL}}\right]$$

Only the LoRA adapter parameters `$\theta$` (which include both the policy adapters and value model adapters) receive this gradient; backbone weights remain frozen.

6. **Value model update**: The value model is trained to minimize the mean squared error between its predictions `$V(x)$` and the actual returns (total reward of the generated response), typically using a separate loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{value}} = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\left[(V(x) - r_\phi(x, y))^2\right]$$

The paper does not provide the explicit value loss, but this is standard practice for actor-critic methods with REINFORCE.

**Hyperparameter settings for RL.** The paper provides specific settings per dataset:

- **Reddit TL;DR**: "We use `$\beta = 0.05$` for KL regularization. We set the learning rate to 1e-5 for the reinforcement learning of the policy model. We picked this learning rate after trying 1e-4, 5e-4, 1e-5, 5e-5, and 1e-6."
- **BOLT Message Summarization**: "We use the 'REINFORCE for Language Models' algorithm... We sample 128 episodes per batch using the policy with temperature of 0.7 for decoding. We use `$\beta = 0.05$` for KL regularization. We set the learning rate to 1e-4 for the reinforcement learning of the policy model."
- **VQAv2 (Gemini Pro)**: "We sample 32 episodes using the policy with temperature of 0.9 for decoding. We used a learning rate of 1e-8 for reinforcement learning of the policy model after trying 1e-7 (learning rate used in SFT)."

The learning rate varies substantially across tasks: 1e-5 for Reddit summarization, 1e-4 for BOLT Message summarization, and 1e-8 for VQAv2. This suggests that RL stability is highly task-dependent and may interact with model size and LoRA configuration in ways the paper does not fully analyze.

**The memory savings mechanism.** The memory savings during RL come from the fact that the policy and value models — which are the ones being trained — have LoRA adapters rather than full-parameter updates. The frozen backbone of the policy and value models can share the same memory representation (since they have identical weights, only the adapters differ), and the optimizer only needs to track states for the adapter parameters. The anchor model and reward model are frozen and do not require optimizer states at all.

However, the savings are less dramatic than for RM training because the RL loop still requires **four model instances** (policy, value, anchor, RM) to be in memory. Even though the policy and value models have reduced trainable parameters, their full backbone weights must still be loaded for forward passes. The paper reports that PE-RLHF RL uses "74% to 80% of the peak HBM" compared to standard RLHF — a more modest reduction than the 43-56% for RM training, reflecting this structural memory requirement that LoRA cannot eliminate.

**The symmetry between policy and value models.** The paper states: "For every setting we try for policy model, both in size and LoRA rank, we replicate that for the value model as well" (Section 4.2). This means that if the policy uses a PaLM 2 S backbone with LoRA rank 16, the value model also uses PaLM 2 S with LoRA rank 16. They are architecturally identical but have independent adapter weights (and separate optimizer states). This symmetry is standard practice because the value model needs to understand the same state space as the policy; using a different architecture or capacity would create a mismatch between what the value function estimates and what the policy encounters.

**Why not LoRA for the anchor model?** The anchor model is kept in full-parameter form (no LoRA), even though applying LoRA during SFT and then merging before RL could further reduce memory. The paper doesn't discuss this, but the likely reason is simplicity: the anchor model represents a well-established SFT baseline that is frozen during RL, so there's no training benefit to LoRA-izing it. And since it's frozen, the additional memory from its LoRA adapters (if kept separate) would be tiny compared to the full model anyway.

#### Training Infrastructure and Reproducibility

The paper provides specific infrastructure details that enable the experiments and are relevant for anyone attempting to reproduce or extend the work.

**Implementation libraries.** The training loop is implemented using PAX (Paxml, 2022) and SeqIO (Roberts et al., 2022), "along with a custom-designed RL training loop infrastructure" (Section 6.5). The paper contrasts this with the open-source TRL library (von Werra et al., 2020), noting that TRL's "multi-adapter RL feature remains experimental and lacks support for parallelization and vision modalities." This suggests that running PE-RLHF at scale requires custom infrastructure beyond what off-the-shelf libraries currently provide, particularly for multi-GPU training and vision-language models like Gemini Pro.

**Memory measurement.** High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) usage is "estimated by Jax JIT at the time of training" (Bradbury et al., 2018). This is an important methodological detail: the memory numbers are pre-computed by the JAX compiler's memory planner at compilation time, not measured during actual execution. This means they represent the theoretical peak memory allocation, which may differ slightly from runtime measurements due to memory fragmentation, caching, or dynamic allocation patterns not captured by static analysis.

**Speed measurement.** Training speed is measured as wall-clock time per training step. For RM training: "the speed measured for the reward models... only include the learning step." For RL training: "the reinforcement learning measurements include episode sampling, reward model scoring, anchor logit calculation, and learning step." This means the RL speedup numbers (1.05× to 1.3×) account for the full RL loop including generation, which is typically the dominant cost — making the speedup more modest than RM training's 1.15× to 1.9× because generation time (forward passes through the full model) is unchanged by LoRA.

**Early stopping and checkpoint selection.** For RM training, checkpoints are selected based on "the highest validation pairwise accuracy" or validation accuracy (for classification RMs). The paper doesn't specify whether LoRA-trained RMs require a different number of training steps to converge compared to fully-tuned RMs. Section 5.3 states: "We note that the LoRA reward models and policies converge in a similar number of steps as the fully tuned ones, so that the speed-ups in training steps translate into faster runs." This is an important empirical finding: LoRA doesn't just make each step faster — it doesn't require *more* steps to reach the same quality, which would erase the speedup.

**Cross-task reward model reuse.** For BOLT Message Summarization, the paper makes an interesting design choice: "We conduct RL training using the reward model trained on the Reddit TL;DR dataset, as described in Appendix A.3.3." Rather than training a separate RM for BOLT Messages, they reuse the Reddit TL;DR RM. This is a practical test of RM generalization and a cost-saving measure, but it introduces a potential confound: the BOLT RL results may reflect the Reddit RM's quality (and its potential distribution shift to chat messages) rather than just the PE-RLHF policy training. The paper does not analyze this cross-dataset transfer aspect.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: PE-RLHF Is a Diagnostic Finding That LoRA Transfers to RLHF, Not a New Method

The paper's most fundamental contribution is not proposing a new algorithm but rather **demonstrating — for the first time, systematically and across diverse tasks — that LoRA parameter-efficient fine-tuning can substitute for full-parameter fine-tuning at every phase of the RLHF pipeline without sacrificing alignment quality**. This is a diagnostic result, not a method proposal.

**What makes this distinctive?** Before this work, the dominant assumption in the RLHF literature was that alignment requires full model capacity. The reasoning was plausible: reward modeling must capture subtle quality distinctions between responses that differ only marginally; RL policy optimization operates in a high-variance feedback loop where the model generates its own training data and must remain stable under distribution shift. It was entirely unclear whether low-rank adapters — which constrain weight updates to at most rank `r` (typically 1–32) — could express the transformations needed for either task. The field treated full fine-tuning as the default, with parameter efficiency unexplored territory.

The paper converts this uncertainty into evidence. Tables 2–3 show that across five task types (summarization, harmlessness, helpfulness, UI automation, visual QA) and two model families (PaLM 2, Gemini Pro), PE-RLHF matches full-tuning performance within fractions of a percentage point. Table 1 quantifies the resource savings: up to 50% peak HBM reduction and up to 90% training time reduction for reward models, with more modest but still meaningful savings for RL. The fact that the gap between LoRA and full-tuning narrows as model size increases (Tables 2–3) is particularly significant — it suggests that larger models have more redundant capacity, making the low-rank constraint *less* restrictive, not more.

**Comparison to prior work.** The PEFT literature (LoRA, DoRA, ReFT) had extensively validated parameter efficiency for standard supervised fine-tuning — classification, generation, instruction following. But RLHF differs qualitatively: it's a two-phase pipeline with interdependent components (RM quality affects RL stability), and the RL phase involves a multi-model memory footprint that supervised fine-tuning never encounters. The paper explicitly identifies this gap in Section 6.4: "To the best of our knowledge, there hasn't been any work prior to ours extensively benchmarking parameter efficient approaches for RLHF." The contribution is filling this gap with systematically collected evidence rather than proposing a new adapter architecture.

**Why this matters beyond the numbers.** This finding has a cascading effect on what's possible in alignment research. If full fine-tuning were strictly necessary for RLHF, then alignment experiments would remain the province of organizations with large GPU clusters. By demonstrating that LoRA works, the paper opens the door for academic labs and smaller industry teams to run RLHF experiments on models they couldn't otherwise afford to fully fine-tune. This democratization argument is implicit but central: the paper isn't just saying "you can save money" — it's saying "a broader community can now participate in alignment research."

**Incremental or fundamental?** This is a **fundamental enabling result** rather than an incremental improvement. It doesn't advance the state of the art in alignment quality (PE-RLHF matches, not exceeds, full RLHF), but it changes the economics of who can do alignment research and how large a model they can align with a fixed budget. The finding that LoRA-trained RMs and policies converge in a similar number of steps as fully-tuned ones (Section 5.3) is the critical enabler — if LoRA required substantially more training iterations, the per-step speedup would be canceled out.

---

### Innovation 2: The Difficulty-Dependence of PE-RLHF's Effectiveness — It Works Better on Bigger Models

The paper surfaces a non-obvious pattern that has significant implications for how we think about parameter-efficient alignment: **PE-RLHF becomes more competitive with full fine-tuning as the underlying backbone model grows larger**. This is both an empirical regularity and a conceptual reframing of when parameter efficiency is appropriate.

**The evidence.** Tables 2 and 3 break results down by model size (PaLM 2 XXS, XS, S) and show a consistent trend. For reward modeling on the Anthropic-HH Harmlessness task (Table 2):
- PaLM 2 XXS (smallest): full-tuning achieves 73.4% accuracy; LoRA rank 8 reaches 72.5% (a -0.9 percentage point gap).
- PaLM 2 XS: full-tuning 77.0%; LoRA rank 8 reaches 76.8% (-0.2 pp).
- PaLM 2 S (largest): full-tuning 76.6%; LoRA rank 8 reaches **79.1% (+2.5 pp)** — actually outperforming full-tuning.

On the SHP Helpfulness task, the pattern is similar: at XXS, full-tuning leads 80.2% vs. 79.3% (LoRA rank 8); at XS, it's 82.0% vs. 82.6% (LoRA rank 8, a slight LoRA win); at S, it's 83.2% vs. 82.2% (LoRA rank 16, nearly tied). For RL policies (Table 3): on Reddit summarization, XXS full-tuning achieves 48.4% win rate vs. 31.7% for LoRA rank 16 (a large gap), but at XS the gap narrows to 77.7% vs. 79.5% (LoRA actually leads), and at S it's 87% vs. 86.5% (near-parity).

**The deeper insight: capacity redundancy scales with model size.** The paper doesn't explicitly theorize this, but the pattern aligns with the "intrinsic dimensionality" hypothesis from the fine-tuning literature — larger pretrained models have more redundant capacity, meaning the effective degrees of freedom needed to adapt to a new task may not grow proportionally with parameter count. A LoRA adapter of rank 16 on a small model constrains the adaptation more severely than the same rank on a large model, because the large model's frozen backbone can already express a wider range of behaviors; the adapter only needs to nudge it in the right direction.

**What this changes in practice.** If this pattern generalizes (the paper only tests up to PaLM 2 S, which is modest by modern standards), it implies that **the case for PE-RLHF becomes stronger, not weaker, as models scale**. This is counterintuitive — one might expect that bigger models need more adaptational capacity, not less. The implication is that for frontier-scale models (100B+ parameters), PE-RLHF might be not just a cost-saving alternative but potentially the *preferred* approach, offering implicit regularization that prevents overfitting to the reward model (which the paper briefly notes in Section 6.2 as a known RLHF failure mode). This is speculative and not tested in the paper, but it's a direct conceptual implication of the scaling trend observed.

**Connection to Section 6.2's overfitting observation.** The paper notes that "overfitting the reward function can be problematic, requiring techniques like early stopping or parameter reduction to ensure optimal policy training." The model-size trend suggests that LoRA's constrained capacity might serve as an automatic regularizer — limiting how much the policy can overfit to reward model imperfections. This is a more interesting motivation for PE-RLHF than mere efficiency: it might actually *improve* robustness in certain regimes. The paper doesn't test this claim directly, but it provides the empirical foundation (the model-size trend) that makes the hypothesis plausible.

---

### Innovation 3: Conceptualizing the RLHF Memory Bottleneck as a "Model Copies" Problem, Not a Parameter Count Problem

The paper reframes the computational challenge of RLHF in a way that clarifies *why* parameter-efficient methods help and *where* their limits lie. This reframing is subtle but important for guiding future work.

**The standard framing vs. the paper's framing.** The naive view is that RLHF is expensive because models have many parameters, and training all of them requires lots of memory and compute. The paper's analysis (particularly in Sections 1 and 2, and the memory measurements in Table 1) reveals a more nuanced picture: **the dominant memory cost in RL is not the trainable parameters themselves, but the simultaneous loading of multiple model copies** — the policy, value model, anchor model, and reward model — during the RL phase. Some of these (anchor, RM) are frozen but must still reside in memory for forward passes; others (policy, value) are being trained and require optimizer states.

**Evidence from the asymmetric memory savings.** Table 1 shows that PE-RLHF reduces RM training peak HBM by 43–74% but RL training peak HBM by only 20–27%. The reason, made clear by the paper's architecture description (Section 2.2, Figure 2), is that during RL, the anchor model and reward model remain in full-parameter form regardless of whether the policy uses LoRA. The policy and value models' frozen backbones also occupy full memory. LoRA can only reduce the *additional* memory from the adapters and their optimizer states — a fraction of the total.

This reframing matters because it directs attention to the right bottleneck. If you believed the problem was "too many trainable parameters," you might invest effort in making LoRA adapters even smaller (rank 0.5?) or developing sparse adapters. But the paper's analysis implies that **further memory reductions in RL require reducing the number of model copies, not further shrinking adapters**. Potential directions include:
- Sharing a single frozen backbone between policy and value models (with separate adapters)
- Quantizing the frozen backbone weights (since they're not being trained)
- Offloading the anchor model to CPU and computing KL penalties asynchronously
- Using the same model for anchor and RM (if the RM was trained from the anchor, they share the same base weights)

The paper doesn't explore these, but its diagnostic framing makes the research agenda clear.

**Why this is a conceptual contribution.** This isn't a new technique — it's a new *understanding* of the problem structure. The paper takes a systems-design view of RLHF that decomposes the memory cost into (a) model weights that must be resident, (b) optimizer states for trainable parameters, and (c) activation memory for forward/backward passes. LoRA primarily addresses (b). The fact that (a) dominates during RL explains why the speedup is modest (1.05–1.3×) compared to RM training (1.15–1.9×). This diagnostic decomposition is more valuable than the raw numbers because it tells future researchers *where to aim*.

**Comparison to prior work.** Prior RLHF papers (Stiennon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022a) describe the computational cost of RLHF in general terms but do not decompose it into the multi-model memory bottleneck vs. parameter-update bottleneck distinction. The PEFT literature focuses on reducing trainable parameters without analyzing the multi-model memory problem (because standard fine-tuning only involves one model). This paper bridges these perspectives by applying PEFT *within* the multi-model RLHF loop and measuring where savings materialize and where they don't.

**Incremental or fundamental?** This is an **incremental insight** (it doesn't change RLHF methodology) but a **useful one** for guiding system design. It's the kind of contribution that becomes common knowledge after a paper — "oh, right, the anchor model is the bottleneck, not the policy parameters" — but wasn't articulated clearly before.

---

### Innovation 4: The Counterintuitive Finding That LoRA Rank Has Minimal Impact on RM Performance

One of the paper's most surprising results is that **varying the LoRA rank — the primary capacity knob — has negligible effect on reward model accuracy across diverse tasks**. This is a negative result with positive implications.

**The evidence.** Table 2 shows RM accuracy for PaLM 2 S across LoRA ranks 1, 4, 8, and 16 on four tasks:
- Harmlessness (Anthropic): 76.0% (rank 1) → 79.1% (rank 8) → 75.0% (rank 16). The best rank (8) is +3.1 pp over the worst, but the pattern is non-monotonic.
- Helpfulness (SHP): 80.9% (rank 1) → 82.2% (rank 4) → 81.6% (rank 8) → 81.3% (rank 16). Maximum variation: 1.3 pp across ranks.
- Summarization (Reddit): 77.2% (rank 1) → 79.7% (rank 4) → 77.3% (rank 8) → 77.0% (rank 16). Maximum variation: 2.7 pp.
- UI Automation: 90.8% (rank 1) → 92.2% (rank 16). Maximum variation: 1.4 pp.

For Gemini Pro on VQAv2 (Table 2, bottom), ranks 1, 4, 8, 16, and 32 all achieve within 0.6 pp of each other (relative to the full-tuning baseline). The paper's summary is understated: "We don't observe significant changes in performance with varying LoRA ranks."

**Why this is surprising.** Intuitively, higher LoRA rank means more adapter capacity — more degrees of freedom to learn task-specific transformations. One would expect a monotonic improvement from rank 1 to rank 16, potentially with diminishing returns but not flat or non-monotonic behavior. The fact that rank 1 (a vector outer product — the minimum possible adaptation) works about as well as rank 16 (256× more adapter parameters, since the parameter count scales as `r × (d + k)`) challenges basic assumptions about how much capacity alignment tasks require.

**What this implies.** The most likely explanation is that **reward modeling — distinguishing preferred from dispreferred responses — is a relatively "shallow" adaptation task**. The pretrained model already has rich representations of text quality; the reward model head (a linear layer mapping the final hidden state to a scalar) may be doing most of the discriminative work, with the LoRA adapters providing only minor adjustments to the representation. If this hypothesis holds, it suggests that:
- Even simpler PEFT methods (e.g., only training the final layer, or prompt tuning) might suffice for RM training.
- The real capacity bottleneck in RLHF is in the *policy optimization* phase, where the model must learn to *generate* high-quality outputs (not just score them). Table 3 indeed shows more variation with LoRA rank for RL policies than Table 2 does for RMs.

**Practical implication.** If RM quality is insensitive to LoRA rank, practitioners can use the smallest rank that doesn't degrade performance — likely rank 1 or 4 — and save even more memory and compute than the paper's default rank-16 experiments. The paper doesn't make this recommendation explicitly, but it follows directly from the ablation results.

**Comparison to prior assumptions.** The LoRA literature (Hu et al., 2021) typically reports that rank matters for complex generation tasks but is less critical for classification-style tasks. The paper's finding is consistent with this pattern and extends it to preference-based reward modeling — confirming that scoring quality is more like classification (shallow adaptation) than like generation (deeper adaptation).

**Incremental or fundamental?** This is an **incremental empirical finding** that refines our understanding of where capacity matters in RLHF. It's not a theoretical breakthrough, but it has direct practical consequences for hyperparameter selection, and it contributes to the broader picture (alongside Innovation 2's model-size trend) that PE-RLHF's effectiveness is robust to configuration choices in ways that make it easy to adopt without extensive tuning.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Datasets.** The paper uses six diverse datasets spanning five task types: (1) **Anthropic-HH Harmlessness** (Bai et al., 2022a) — 42,000 pairwise preference comparisons for harmless response generation, where crowdworkers elicited harmful responses and chose the more harmless one from a 52B context-distilled LM; (2) **Stanford Human Preferences (SHP)** (Ethayarajh et al., 2022) — 385,563 Reddit questions with top-level comments, split 90/5/5% train/validation/test across 18 domains, where preferences are inferred from upvote counts with timestamp correction to avoid bias toward older posts; (3) **Reddit TL;DR Summarization** (Stiennon et al., 2020; Völske et al., 2017) — 92,000 pairwise comparisons of human-annotated summary preferences for Reddit posts, filtered following the original RLHF work; (4) **BOLT Message Summarization** (Chen et al., 2018) — 11,932 training / 1,575 validation / 500 test examples of SMS/chat conversation summarization with phone numbers redacted and names removed; (5) **AndroidControl UI Automation** (Li et al., 2024b) — 13k traces across 1.4k unique task instructions spanning 800+ apps, with human demonstrations of device control actions; and (6) **VQAv2 Visual Question Answering** (Antol et al., 2015; Goyal et al., 2017a) — standard visual QA benchmark with images, questions, and human answers annotated with confidence levels. This breadth is deliberate: it tests PE-RLHF across symbolic reasoning (summarization), safety-critical generation (harmlessness), open-ended helpfulness, structured action prediction (UI automation), and multimodal understanding (VQA).

- **Base models.** The experiments use two model families: **PaLM 2** (Anil et al., 2023), a text-focused large language model pretrained under the UL2 paradigm (Tay et al., 2022), available in three sizes labeled XXS, XS, and S (corresponding to Google Cloud Vertex API names Gecko, Otter, and Bison); and **Gemini Pro** (Team et al., 2023), a vision-language model for the VQAv2 experiments. The choice of PaLM 2 allows systematic scaling analysis across three model sizes with a shared architecture; the inclusion of Gemini Pro extends the findings to the multimodal domain. The paper argues these models are "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), though this claim is untested beyond the specific model families. All models are accessible via Google Cloud's Vertex API.

- **Metrics.** For reward models trained with pairwise preferences (Anthropic-HH, SHP, Reddit TL;DR), the evaluation metric is **pairwise accuracy**: the proportion of test pairs where the RM assigns a higher score to the human-preferred response (`r(x, y_w) > r(x, y_l)`). For classification-style reward models (AndroidControl, VQAv2), the metric is **accuracy**: whether the RM's sigmoid-bounded score correctly classifies the response as good (`p = 1`) or bad (`p = 0`) relative to a 0.5 threshold. For RL-trained policies, the primary evaluation uses **a PaLM 2 L judge model** (the largest PaLM 2 variant, Vertex API name "Unicorn") prompted to evaluate generated outputs. The specific judge outputs vary by task: for summarization and helpfulness, the judge performs pairwise comparison of policy vs. baseline outputs, with the reported metric being **win rate** (percentage of cases where the policy output is preferred in both possible orderings, with positional bias eliminated by averaging two prompt orderings — outputs preferred in only one ordering are labeled ties); for harmlessness, the judge outputs a YES/NO harmlessness judgment, with the metric being **harmless rate** (fraction of responses judged harmless); for UI automation, the judge determines whether the generated action is correct for the task, yielding **accuracy**; for VQA, the metric is **absolute accuracy change** relative to a supervised fine-tuned baseline. The paper validates the judge model against human labels on 50 samples, reporting 77.2% agreement for summarization and 90.7% agreement for harmless response generation (Table 7, Appendix A.4).

- **Baselines.** The experiments use two primary baselines: (1) **Standard RLHF** — the identical RLHF pipeline (SFT → RM training → REINFORCE policy optimization) with full-parameter fine-tuning for both the reward model and the policy/value models. This is the direct comparator for PE-RLHF's effectiveness-efficiency tradeoff. (2) **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)** — the instruction-tuned model from Phase 1 of the RLHF pipeline, evaluated without any RL-based alignment. This establishes the lower bound: how much does RL (whether full or parameter-efficient) improve over SFT alone? For summarization tasks, a separate instruction-tuned PaLM 2 S model generates the reference responses that the policy's win rate is measured against (Section 4.3: "We use an instruction-tuned version of PaLM 2 S to generate baseline responses"). The paper does not compare against other alignment methods such as DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) or RAFT (Dong et al., 2023), which it acknowledges in Section 6.3 — a notable omission for establishing whether PE-RLHF is preferable not just to full RLHF but to the broader alignment landscape.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures computational cost along two axes rather than a unified "generation budget": **peak High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) usage**, estimated by JAX's JIT compiler memory planner at training time (Bradbury et al., 2018), representing the maximum memory required during training as a percentage of what standard RLHF uses; and **training speedup**, measured as the ratio of wall-clock time for standard RLHF vs. PE-RLHF to reach equivalent performance. Critically, these metrics have different scope for RM vs. RL training: for RM training, speedup includes only the learning step (forward + backward passes), while for RL training it includes the full loop — episode sampling (autoregressive generation), reward model scoring, anchor logit calculation, and the learning step. This asymmetry means RM speedup numbers (1.15–1.9×) are not directly comparable to RL speedup numbers (1.05–1.3×). The paper does not report total FLOPs consumed, which would enable a more principled comparison across the pipeline stages. All comparisons between PE-RLHF and standard RLHF use the same fixed reward model for the RL phase ("for a fair comparison across the different settings... to reduce confounding factors that affect the policy performance," Section 4.2), so differences in policy quality arise solely from the training methodology, not the reward signal quality.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or report confidence intervals. The standard approach is to train on the specified dataset splits (using provided train/validation/test splits where available, e.g., SHP's 90/5/5 split; for Anthropic-HH, 5,000 training steps with checkpoint selection based on highest validation accuracy) and report results on the held-out test set. For the judge-based policy evaluations, positional bias is addressed by prompting the judge twice with both orderings of the two outputs and averaging: "only if an output is preferred in both possible orderings is it considered a win. If it is only preferred in one ordering, then we label a tie" (Section 4.3). This is a reasonable debiasing procedure but is not a statistical test. The hyperparameter sweeps (learning rates, dropout, LoRA rank) are described as selecting the best configuration based on validation performance, but the final reported numbers are point estimates without uncertainty quantification, making it impossible to assess whether the small differences between PE-RLHF and full RLHF (e.g., Anthropic-HH harmlessness: 96.6% full vs. 98.2% LoRA rank 1, Table 3) are statistically significant or within noise.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Reward Model Training: PE-RLHF Matches Full-Tuning Accuracy

The headline result for reward model training (Table 1, top half; Table 2) is that **PE-RLHF reward models achieve accuracy within ~1–2 percentage points of fully-tuned RMs across all tasks, while using 43–74% of the peak HBM and training 1.15–1.9× faster**.

**Absolute performance numbers.** On the PaLM 2 S backbone (Table 2, top section), the best LoRA configuration for each task achieves: Anthropic-HH Harmlessness — 79.1% (LoRA rank 8) vs. 76.6% full-tuning (a +2.5 pp advantage for LoRA); SHP Helpfulness — 82.2% (LoRA rank 4) vs. 83.2% full-tuning (-1.0 pp); Reddit TL;DR Summarization — 79.7% (LoRA rank 4) vs. 78.7% full-tuning (+1.0 pp); AndroidControl UI Automation — 92.2% (LoRA rank 16) vs. 93.1% full-tuning (-0.9 pp). On VQAv2 with Gemini Pro (Table 2, bottom section), all LoRA ranks from 1 to 32 achieve within -0.6 to -1.2 pp of the fully-tuned RM, with the best (rank 4) at -0.6 pp difference. Across the board, no task shows a gap larger than 2.5 percentage points, and in three out of five task-model combinations, the best LoRA configuration numerically exceeds the fully-tuned baseline.

**Efficiency numbers.** Table 1 reports per-task peak HBM and speedup for the best-performing PE-RLHF configuration. For reward model training: Anthropic-HH Harmlessness — 43.1% peak HBM (using less than half the memory), 1.6× speedup; SHP Helpfulness — 48% HBM, 1.5× speedup; Reddit TL;DR — 50% HBM, 1.7× speedup; AndroidControl — 56% HBM, 1.9× speedup (the largest gain); VQAv2 — 74% HBM, 1.15× speedup (the smallest gain, likely because Gemini Pro's vision encoder already dominates memory and LoRA only affects the text processing components). The VQAv2 numbers are notably worse than the text-only tasks, suggesting that multimodal models benefit less proportionally from LoRA — an important boundary condition the paper doesn't discuss explicitly.

**Model size scaling pattern.** Table 2 reveals that PE-RLHF's competitiveness improves as the backbone grows. On Anthropic-HH: PaLM 2 XXS full-tuning achieves 73.4% vs. LoRA rank 8 at 72.5% (-0.9 pp gap); XS full-tuning 77.0% vs. LoRA rank 8 at 76.8% (-0.2 pp); S full-tuning 76.6% vs. LoRA rank 8 at 79.1% (+2.5 pp, LoRA actually better). SHP Helpfulness: XXS full-tuning 80.2% vs. LoRA rank 8 79.3% (-0.9 pp); XS: 82.0% vs. LoRA rank 8 82.6% (+0.6 pp); S: 83.2% vs. LoRA rank 4 82.2% (-1.0 pp). Reddit Summarization: XXS full-tuning 75.4% vs. LoRA rank 4 73.2% (-2.2 pp, the largest gap); XS: 78.1% vs. LoRA rank 4 76.8% (-1.3 pp); S: 78.7% vs. LoRA rank 4 79.7% (+1.0 pp). The trend is consistent: at the smallest model size (XXS), PE-RLHF falls marginally behind full-tuning; at the middle size (XS), it pulls to near-parity; at the largest tested size (S), it is competitive or better. The paper's language is appropriately cautious: "PE-RLHF is more effective at modeling reward, and performs closer to standard full-tuning when the size of the model backbone increases" (Section 5.2).

**LoRA rank ablation for RMs.** Table 2's most striking pattern is the near-insensitivity of RM accuracy to LoRA rank. On Anthropic-HH with PaLM 2 S, ranks 1, 4, 8, and 16 yield 76.0%, 78.7%, 79.1%, and 75.0% respectively — a maximum spread of 4.1 pp with no monotonic trend (rank 8 > rank 4 > rank 1 > rank 16). On SHP with PaLM 2 S: 80.9%, 82.2%, 81.6%, 81.3% — spread of 1.3 pp. On Reddit: 77.2%, 79.7%, 77.3%, 77.0% — spread of 2.7 pp, again non-monotonic with rank 4 best. On AndroidControl: 90.8%, 91.2%, 92.0%, 92.2% — spread of 1.4 pp, the only task showing a weak monotonic trend favoring higher ranks. The paper concludes: "We don't observe significant changes in performance with varying LoRA ranks" (Table 2 caption). This has major practical implications: practitioners can use the smallest rank (1 or 4) and save additional memory with negligible accuracy cost.

#### RL Policy Training: PE-RLHF Policies Match Full RLHF Quality

The headline result for policy training (Table 1, bottom half; Table 3; Figure 3) is that **PE-RLHF policies achieve win rates and accuracy within ~1–3 pp of standard RLHF policies across most tasks, while using 74–80% of the peak HBM and training 1.05–1.3× faster**. Both PE-RLHF and standard RLHF substantially outperform the SFT baseline, confirming that RL-based alignment works regardless of the training methodology.

**Absolute performance numbers.** On PaLM 2 S (Table 3, top section), comparing the best LoRA configuration against full-tuning: Anthropic-HH Harmlessness — PE-RLHF achieves 98.2% harmless rate (LoRA rank 1) vs. 96.6% full-tuning (+1.6 pp, and vs. 75.5% SFT baseline, a +22.7 pp improvement from RL); SHP Helpfulness — 61.3% win rate (LoRA rank 16) vs. 63.0% full-tuning (-1.7 pp, vs. 50% SFT reference); Reddit Summarization — 86.5% win rate (LoRA rank 16) vs. 87% full-tuning (-0.5 pp, vs. 50% SFT reference); BOLT Message Summarization — 75.5% win rate (LoRA rank 16) vs. 73.2% full-tuning (+2.3 pp, vs. 50% SFT reference); AndroidControl — 86.4% accuracy (LoRA rank 16) vs. 81.6% full-tuning (+4.8 pp, a substantial win for LoRA, vs. 77.7% SFT). On Gemini Pro for VQAv2: the fully-tuned RL policy achieves +5.5% absolute accuracy improvement over SFT; the best LoRA configuration (rank 32) achieves +3.9%, a -1.6 pp gap.

Figure 3 provides the visual summary: across all five tasks (Harmlessness, Helpfulness, Reddit Summarization, Message Summarization, UI Automation), the SFT, RLHF, and PE-RLHF bars are visually nearly identical between RLHF and PE-RLHF, and both are substantially above SFT. The PE-RLHF bar for UI Automation notably exceeds the RLHF bar (86.4% vs. 81.6%), which is the most dramatic individual win for LoRA and suggests that for some structured prediction tasks, LoRA's regularization may actually help avoid overfitting during RL.

**Efficiency numbers.** Table 1 reports per-task RL training efficiency: Anthropic-HH — 80% peak HBM, 1.3× speedup; SHP — 75% HBM, 1.15× speedup; Reddit TL;DR — 75% HBM, 1.15× speedup; BOLT Messages — 80% HBM, 1.05× speedup; AndroidControl — 74% HBM, 1.20× speedup; VQAv2 — 74% HBM, 1.24× speedup. The speedup range (1.05–1.3×) is substantially narrower than for RM training (1.15–1.9×), and the HBM reduction (20–26%) is smaller than for RM training (26–57%). This asymmetry is consistent with the paper's architecture: during RL, the anchor model and reward model remain in full-parameter memory regardless of LoRA on the policy and value models, creating a floor on memory savings. The speedup during RL is limited because the dominant computational cost — autoregressive response generation — is unchanged by LoRA; only the learning step benefits from fewer trainable parameters.

**Model size scaling for RL policies.** Table 3 shows the same trend as for RMs: LoRA competitiveness improves with model size. Reddit Summarization: at XXS, full-tuning achieves 48.4% win rate vs. LoRA rank 16 at 31.7% (-16.7 pp, a large gap); at XS, 77.7% vs. 79.5% (+1.8 pp, LoRA leads); at S, 87% vs. 86.5% (-0.5 pp, near-parity). BOLT Messages: XXS 33.7% vs. 31.7% (-2.0 pp); XS 64.3% vs. 65.5% (+1.2 pp); S 73.2% vs. 75.5% (+2.3 pp). Anthropic-HH is the exception where even the XXS model shows no gap (96.6% vs. 96.6% for LoRA rank 16), possibly because the harmless rate is near ceiling. The worst case is Reddit at XXS, where the -16.7 pp gap suggests that for small models doing complex generation tasks (summarization), LoRA's capacity constraint may genuinely limit the policy's ability to learn — but this gap essentially vanishes at larger scales.

**LoRA rank ablation for RL policies.** Table 3 reveals a different pattern than for RMs: RL policy performance generally improves with higher LoRA rank, though the trend is weak and non-monotonic in places. On Anthropic-HH with PaLM 2 S: rank 1 achieves 98.2%, rank 4 achieves 96.7%, rank 8 achieves 97.7%, rank 16 achieves 96.4% — essentially flat. On SHP: rank 1 57.9%, rank 4 60.1%, rank 8 58.3%, rank 16 61.3% — a slight upward trend (3.4 pp spread). On Reddit: rank 1 85.0%, rank 4 84.2%, rank 8 85.7%, rank 16 86.5% — weak upward trend (1.5 pp spread). On AndroidControl: rank 1 77.7%, rank 4 84.5%, rank 8 85.4%, rank 16 86.4% — the clearest monotonic trend (8.7 pp spread, substantial). On VQAv2: rank 1 3.0%, rank 4 3.0%, rank 8 2.5%, rank 16 3.1%, rank 32 3.9% — spread of 1.4 pp with highest rank best. Unlike RMs where rank barely matters, RL policies show a mild preference for higher ranks, suggesting that policy optimization (learning to generate high-quality sequences) is a more capacity-intensive task than reward modeling (learning to score them). The paper states: "we observe that the PE-RLHF policies perform better (as evaluated by the judge LLM) as the LoRA rank increases" (Section 5.2), though the evidence for this claim is stronger for AndroidControl and VQAv2 than for the text-only tasks where it's marginal.

**Cross-task generalization of the reward model.** An interesting implicit result: the BOLT Message Summarization RL experiments (Table 3) use the reward model trained on Reddit TL;DR, not a separate BOLT-specific RM (Appendix A.3.4: "We conduct RL training using the reward model trained on the Reddit TL;DR dataset"). Despite the domain shift from Reddit posts to SMS/chat conversations, the PE-RLHF policy achieves 75.5% win rate vs. 73.2% for full-tuning at PaLM 2 S scale — demonstrating that a LoRA-trained RM generalizes across summarization domains at least as well as a fully-tuned RM. This is not systematically investigated but appears as a byproduct of the experimental design.

**Judge model validation.** Appendix A.4 reports the agreement between the PaLM 2 L judge and human annotators on 50 sampled examples: 77.2% for summarization and 90.7% for harmlessness. The summarization agreement is modest — a 22.8% disagreement rate means that nearly one in four judge decisions would differ from a human, which could meaningfully affect win rate measurements if the disagreement is systematic (e.g., the judge consistently prefers one style of summary while humans prefer another). The paper doesn't analyze the nature of disagreements or calibrate the win rate numbers against fully human-evaluated results.

#### Memory and Speed Measurements (Table 1)

Table 1 is the paper's central efficiency results table, and it warrants direct discussion as a quantitative result in its own right.

**Reward model training efficiency.** For each task, PE-RLHF RM training achieves:

| Task | Peak HBM (% of full) | Speedup (×) | Accuracy vs. Full |
|------|---------------------|------------|-------------------|
| Anthropic-HH | 43.1% | 1.6× | 78.71% vs. 76.56% (+2.15 pp) |
| SHP | 48% | 1.5× | 82.2% vs. 83.2% (-1.0 pp) |
| Reddit TL;DR | 50% | 1.7× | 79.7% vs. 78.7% (+1.0 pp) |
| AndroidControl | 56% | 1.9× | 91.8% vs. 93.12% (-1.32 pp) |
| VQAv2 | 74% | 1.15× | +0.5% vs. SFT baseline |

The memory savings are largest for text-only tasks (43–56% peak HBM) and smallest for VQAv2 (74%), where the vision encoder dominates the parameter count and LoRA's text-only adapters provide proportionally less benefit. The speedup range (1.15–1.9×) is substantial for RM training, particularly for AndroidControl at 1.9×, meaning the LoRA RM trains in roughly half the wall-clock time. The paper notes these measurements "depend on multiple factors, such as sequence lengths of the examples, the accelerators being used, etc." (Section 5.3).

**RL training efficiency.** The RL phase shows more modest gains:

| Task | Peak HBM (% of full) | Speedup (×) |
|------|---------------------|------------|
| Anthropic-HH | 80% | 1.3× |
| SHP | 75% | 1.15× |
| Reddit TL;DR | 75% | 1.15× |
| BOLT Messages | 80% | 1.05× |
| AndroidControl | 74% | 1.20× |
| VQAv2 | 74% | 1.24× |

The HBM savings are consistently 20–26%, and speedups range from 1.05× (BOLT, nearly negligible) to 1.3× (Anthropic-HH). The paper explicitly notes that RL speed measurements are more comprehensive than RM measurements: "In contrast to the speed measured for the reward models, which only include the learning step, the reinforcement learning measurements include episode sampling, reward model scoring, anchor logit calculation, and learning step" (Section 4.3). Since episode sampling (autoregressive generation) is typically the dominant cost in RLHF, and LoRA doesn't accelerate generation (it uses the full merged model), the modest speedup is expected.

**LoRA rank and efficiency.** Section 5.3 states: "We observe that the memory savings, and training speed-up do not vary significantly with the LoRA rank, since the change in trainable parameters is extremely small in comparison to total parameters (<1% in maximum LoRA rank of 32)." This is important practically: practitioners can choose LoRA rank based on performance considerations (where Table 2 shows RM accuracy is rank-insensitive, and Table 3 shows RL benefits mildly from higher ranks) without worrying that higher ranks will erase the efficiency gains. Even at rank 32, the adapter parameter count is a rounding error relative to the backbone.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**LoRA rank for reward models (Table 2, all tasks):** Performance is largely insensitive to rank across all text and vision-language tasks, with maximum spread of 4.1 percentage points on Anthropic-HH (PaLM 2 S: rank 8 = 79.1% vs. rank 16 = 75.0%) and typically 1-3 pp on other tasks, with no consistent monotonic trend. This negative result is practically valuable — it means practitioners can use rank 1 or 4 for RMs with negligible accuracy loss and further memory savings beyond what the paper's default configurations report.

**LoRA rank for RL policies (Table 3, all tasks):** Unlike RMs, RL policies show a mild preference for higher LoRA ranks, most clearly on AndroidControl (PaLM 2 S: rank 1 = 77.7%, rank 4 = 84.5%, rank 8 = 85.4%, rank 16 = 86.4%, an 8.7 pp monotonic improvement), weakly on Reddit (rank 1 = 85.0%, rank 16 = 86.5%) and VQAv2 (rank 1 = 3.0%, rank 32 = 3.9%), and essentially flat on Anthropic-HH and SHP. This suggests that generation tasks benefit from more adapter capacity than classification-style reward scoring, consistent with the intuition that learning to produce high-quality outputs is more demanding than learning to discriminate them.

**Model size scaling for reward models (Table 2, three PaLM 2 sizes):** The performance gap between full-tuning and PE-RLHF shrinks as model size increases. On Reddit TL;DR: XXS gap = -2.2 pp, XS gap = -1.3 pp, S gap = +1.0 pp (LoRA better). On Anthropic-HH: XXS gap = -0.9 pp, XS gap = -0.2 pp, S gap = +2.5 pp. This pattern holds across all text tasks and provides evidence that LoRA's capacity constraint is less binding for larger models — a finding with direct practical implications for frontier-scale alignment.

**Model size scaling for RL policies (Table 3, three PaLM 2 sizes):** The same trend holds for policies, most dramatically on Reddit TL;DR: XXS gap = -16.7 pp (large), XS gap = +1.8 pp (LoRA leads), S gap = -0.5 pp (near-parity). The XXS Reddit result is the only case where PE-RLHF substantially underperforms full RLHF, suggesting a lower bound on model size below which LoRA may not provide sufficient capacity for complex generation tasks under RL optimization.

**Dropout hyperparameter (Tables 5, 6):** The optimal dropout varies substantially across model sizes and tasks for RM training. On SHP: optimal dropout ranges from 0 (PaLM 2 XXS LoRA rank 16) to 0.05 (PaLM 2 S LoRA rank 8, PaLM 2 XS full-tuning). On AndroidControl: ranges from 0 (PaLM 2 S LoRA rank 8, PaLM 2 XXS full-tuning) to 0.05 (PaLM 2 XS LoRA rank 4). This hyperparameter sensitivity is not discussed in the main text but is documented in Tables 5-6 and suggests that PE-RLHF may require task-specific tuning of regularization strength, potentially offsetting some of the claimed simplicity benefits.

**Learning rate differences between full-tuning and LoRA:** Across all RM training experiments, the optimal learning rate for LoRA is consistently 10-20× higher than for full-tuning. Anthropic-HH: 2e-4 (LoRA) vs. 1e-5 (full); Reddit TL;DR: 1e-4 (LoRA) vs. 1e-5 (full); AndroidControl: 1e-4 to 2e-4 (LoRA) vs. 5e-6 to 1e-5 (full). This is expected behavior (LoRA adapters start from random initialization and need to learn task-specific transformations while the frozen backbone provides stable representations), but it means practitioners cannot simply port full-tuning hyperparameters to LoRA — they need to re-tune.

**Cross-dataset reward model generalization (BOLT Messages, Table 3):** The BOLT Message Summarization RL experiments use the Reddit TL;DR RM rather than a BOLT-specific RM. PE-RLHF achieves 75.5% win rate vs. 73.2% for full-tuning on PaLM 2 S, demonstrating that LoRA-trained RMs generalize at least as well as fully-tuned RMs to out-of-domain summarization data. This is an implicit robustness check showing that PE-RLHF's benefits are not contingent on in-domain RM training.

**Judge model vs. human agreement (Table 7, Appendix A.4):** On 50 validation samples, PaLM 2 L judge agreement with human labels is 77.2% for summarization and 90.7% for harmlessness. The summarization agreement is modest: roughly 1 in 4 judge decisions disagrees with human judgment, introducing a measurement noise floor that could mask or exaggerate small differences between PE-RLHF and full RLHF. The paper does not analyze whether disagreement is biased in a particular direction.

**VQAv2 supervised fine-tuning baseline (Table 3, bottom):** The SFT baseline for VQAv2 serves as a sanity check — without RL alignment, the model's accuracy change relative to itself is defined as 0.0%. The fully-tuned RL policy achieves +5.5% improvement; the best LoRA RL policy achieves +3.9%. Both are substantially above zero, confirming that RL provides genuine improvement over SFT even for vision-language tasks, and that PE-RLHF captures most but not all of this gain. The 1.6 pp gap between full and LoRA RL on VQAv2 is one of the larger gaps in the paper, suggesting that vision-language RL alignment may be more capacity-intensive than text-only alignment.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim: "PE-RLHF achieves comparable performance to RLHF."** This claim is supported across all tasks for the model sizes and metrics reported, but "comparable" requires careful interpretation. For reward modeling (Table 2), PE-RLHF is within 2.5 pp of full-tuning on all tasks, and in several cases exceeds it (e.g., Anthropic-HH +2.5 pp, Reddit +1.0 pp). For RL policies on PaLM 2 S (Table 3), the gap ranges from -1.7 pp (SHP) to +4.8 pp (AndroidControl), with most tasks within 2 pp. These are genuinely comparable — no task shows a dramatic degradation from using LoRA.

However, "comparable" is only demonstrated for the specific model sizes, datasets, and hyperparameter configurations tested. The XXS model results in Table 3 show a -16.7 pp gap on Reddit summarization, which is not comparable. The paper acknowledges this only implicitly through the model-size trend analysis, but a reader focused on the headline claim might miss that the smallest tested model size fails to achieve comparable performance on the most complex generation task. The boundary condition is clear: PE-RLHF is comparable to RLHF on sufficiently large models (XS and above), but may not be on small models for challenging tasks.

Additionally, the claim is measured using an LLM judge (PaLM 2 L) rather than human evaluation. The judge-human agreement is 77.2% for summarization (Table 7), meaning ~23% of judge decisions would flip with a human evaluator. For win rates in the 60-90% range, this noise could mask differences of several percentage points. The paper does not report whether the judge's disagreements with humans are symmetric or biased — if the judge systematically prefers a certain style that PE-RLHF happens to produce, the results could be misleading. Human evaluation on the full test set, even for a subset of tasks, would substantially strengthen confidence in the "comparable" claim.

The paper also does not compare PE-RLHF against other parameter-efficient alignment methods (e.g., LoRA applied to DPO, or DoRA applied to RLHF), only against full-parameter RLHF. This is acknowledged in the limitations (Section 7): "We solely focus on LoRA as the parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) method." The claim is therefore about LoRA vs. full-tuning for RLHF specifically, not about PE-RLHF being the best parameter-efficient alignment approach.

**Claim: "PE-RLHF significantly reduces training time (up to 90% faster for reward models, and 30% faster for RL)."** The 90% figure corresponds to a 1.9× speedup (AndroidControl RM training, Table 1). "90% faster" in common usage would mean completing in 10% of the original time, i.e., a 10× speedup, but the paper's phrasing "up to 90% faster" is ambiguous — it might mean "90% of the original time" (1.1× speedup) or "1.9× faster" (47% time reduction). The actual numbers in Table 1 are: RM speedups of 1.15–1.9×, meaning training time is reduced to 53–87% of the original, not 10%. The "30% faster" for RL corresponds to speedups of 1.05–1.3×, meaning training time reduced to 77–95% of original. These are meaningful but more modest than the "90%" phrasing suggests. The claim is quantitatively supported by Table 1 but is potentially misleading in its framing.

More importantly, the speed measurements have different scope for RM and RL training (Section 4.3): RM speedups only include the learning step, while RL speedups include the full loop (generation + scoring + anchor logits + learning). Since generation dominates RL training time and is unchanged by LoRA, RL speedups will always be modest regardless of how efficient the learning step becomes. The paper's claim of "30% faster" for RL is actually evidence that LoRA's effect on learning step speed is substantially higher than 30%, but is diluted by the fixed generation cost. The paper would be more informative if it decomposed RL time into components and reported speedup separately for each.

The paper also does not discuss whether the reduced memory footprint enables larger batch sizes or longer sequence lengths that could further improve throughput, which would be an indirect benefit beyond the raw per-step speedup.

**Claim: "PE-RLHF significantly reduces memory footprint (up to 50% reduction for reward models, and 27% for RL)."** This claim is directly supported by Table 1: RM peak HBM of 43-74% of full-tuning (26-57% reduction), and RL peak HBM of 74-80% (20-26% reduction). The "up to 50%" figure corresponds to the 50% RM HBM on Reddit TL;DR and the 43.1% on Anthropic-HH (i.e., 57% reduction). The numbers are well-documented and based on JAX's JIT memory planner, which provides static estimates.

A limitation is that peak HBM is estimated at compile time, not measured at runtime. Runtime memory usage can differ due to memory fragmentation, dynamic allocations, and caching behavior. Additionally, the paper does not report whether the reduced memory footprint translates to the ability to use fewer accelerators (e.g., fitting a training job on 4 GPUs instead of 8), which would be the practical benefit. The HBM percentages are relative to the full-tuning baseline at the same configuration, but the absolute memory in GB is never provided, making it difficult to assess whether the savings are practically meaningful (e.g., reducing from 80GB to 60GB enables using A100-80GB instead of A100-80GB, which is the same hardware tier) or transformative (e.g., reducing from 80GB to 40GB enables a lower-tier GPU).

The most important unstated limitation: the HBM savings for RL only apply to the policy and value model's trainable parameters. The anchor model and reward model remain in full-parameter memory. If these dominate the memory footprint (likely for very large models where the anchor may be the largest component), the effective savings would be much less than 27%. The paper's memory analysis conflates "peak HBM percentage" with actual hardware implications, and the 27% figure could be misleading for deployment planning if the anchor model's memory dominates.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

1. **Human evaluation on the full test set**: The reliance on LLM-as-judge with only 50-sample validation (and 77.2% agreement for summarization) is a significant limitation. Full human evaluation on at least one dataset would establish a ground-truth calibration point for the judge-based win rates.

2. **Larger model scales**: The paper tests up to PaLM 2 S, which is the third-smallest of four PaLM 2 sizes. Testing on PaLM 2 L (the largest, and the model used as the judge) would test whether the model-size scaling trend continues — does PE-RLHF overtake full RLHF at frontier scale? This is the most practically important question the paper raises but does not answer.

3. **Comparison with parameter-efficient alternatives to RLHF**: The paper acknowledges DPO, RAFT, and other alignment methods but doesn't test LoRA with them. A head-to-head of PE-RLHF vs. LoRA-DPO (parameter-efficient direct preference optimization) would inform whether the RLHF-specific complexity is worth the efficiency gains when parameter-efficient alternatives to RLHF exist.

4. **Ablation on which components need LoRA**: The paper applies LoRA to both RM and RL policy simultaneously. Ablating each independently — full RM + LoRA RL, and LoRA RM + full RL — would reveal whether one phase benefits more from parameter efficiency than the other, and whether the compound savings are additive or subadditive.

5. **Training data efficiency**: The paper uses the same amount of training data for PE-RLHF and full RLHF. An experiment varying dataset size would reveal whether LoRA's constrained capacity requires more or less data than full-tuning — a practically important consideration since human preference data is expensive.

6. **Reward hacking analysis**: The paper mentions reward hacking as a concern (Section 6.2) and suggests LoRA might help through implicit regularization, but provides no empirical evidence. Measuring the KL divergence between anchor and policy over training, or the rate of reward model score inflation relative to actual quality, would substantiate or refute this hypothesized benefit.

**Conditional boundaries on the claims:**

The "comparable performance" claim holds for models of size PaLM 2 XS and above on the tested tasks, but **does not hold for PaLM 2 XXS on complex generation tasks** (Reddit summarization shows -16.7 pp). The efficiency claims are most pronounced for reward model training (1.5–1.9× speedup, 30–57% memory reduction) and **substantially more modest for RL training** (1.05–1.3× speedup, 20–26% memory reduction). The VQAv2 results show that **multimodal models benefit less proportionally** from LoRA (74% HBM, 1.15× speedup for RM; 74% HBM, 1.24× for RL), likely because the vision encoder — which LoRA does not adapt — dominates the parameter count. None of the claims are validated with human evaluation at scale, leaving open the possibility that the LLM judge's biases affect the comparability assessment in unknown ways.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: The LoRA Rank Ablation Results Are Inconsistent Across Tasks and Violate the "More Capacity is Better" Intuition

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies LoRA rank as the primary capacity-control hyperparameter and draws the conclusion that "we don't observe significant changes in performance with varying LoRA ranks" (Table 2 caption). This framing implies that rank selection is not a critical design choice — practitioners can pick almost any rank and get equivalent results. The paper's own data, however, contradicts this simple story in important ways.

**The consequence.** The rank-performance relationship is task-dependent, model-size-dependent, and differs qualitatively between reward modeling and RL policy training. For reward models (Table 2), the near-flat relationship holds across most tasks: Anthropic-HH RM accuracy ranges from 75.0% (rank 16) to 79.1% (rank 8) on PaLM 2 S — a 4.1 percentage point non-monotonic spread that the paper groups under "don't observe significant changes." For RL policies (Table 3), the trend is more systematically upward with rank: on AndroidControl with PaLM 2 S, accuracy rises monotonically from 77.7% (rank 1) to 86.4% (rank 16), an 8.7 pp spread that would be considered practically significant in most deployment contexts. On Reddit TL;DR summarization with PaLM 2 XS, the gap between rank 1 (not reported) and rank 16 (79.5%) vs. full-tuning (77.7%) is 1.8 pp in LoRA's favor — but without the rank 1 number, we cannot assess whether lower ranks would also work.

The practical consequence is that **a practitioner cannot simply set rank = 4 and expect near-optimal results across all tasks and phases**. For reward model training, this safe default may hold. For policy training, particularly on structured prediction tasks like UI automation, using rank 1 instead of rank 16 leaves ~9 percentage points on the table — a catastrophic degradation in many production settings. The paper's summary statement in Section 5.2 ("we observe that the PE-RLHF policies perform better... as the LoRA rank increases") partially acknowledges this but undermines the earlier claim of rank-insensitivity, creating an unresolved tension that the paper never reconciles quantitatively.

Furthermore, the non-monotonic behavior (e.g., Anthropic-HH RM: rank 8 > rank 4 > rank 1 > rank 16) suggests that **the optimal rank is not predictable from first principles** and must be empirically determined per task, per model size, and per phase (RM vs. policy). This undercuts the narrative that PE-RLHF is a turnkey solution; it requires the same hyperparameter tuning effort as full RLHF, just over a different (smaller) parameter space.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Tables 2 and 3 contain all the relevant numbers. The claim of rank-insensitivity appears in Table 2's caption and is repeated in Section 5.2. The contradictory evidence for policies is in Table 3, most clearly on AndroidControl (rank 1: 77.7%, rank 16: 86.4%) and VQAv2 (rank 1: +3.0%, rank 32: +3.9% over SFT). The paper provides no statistical test for what constitutes a "significant" rank effect, treating a 4.1 pp spread for RMs as insignificant while not distinguishing it from the 8.7 pp spread for policies.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper offers no guidance on how to select LoRA rank beyond the observation that higher ranks help for policy training. There is no cost-benefit analysis trading off the marginal accuracy gain from rank 8 → 16 against the (admittedly small) additional memory and compute cost. The paper's stated hope that their work "will motivate the benchmarking of other PEFT and ReFT approaches" (Section 1) implicitly acknowledges that LoRA's rank behavior is not fully understood, but it does not propose any diagnostic or heuristic for practitioners using LoRA today.

### Limitation 2: The LLM Judge Has Insufficient Validated Accuracy to Support the "Comparable Performance" Claim for Summarization

**The assumption or constraint.** All policy evaluation results in Table 1, Table 3, and Figure 3 rely on PaLM 2 L as an automated judge to compare policy outputs against baselines. The paper validates this judge against human labels on only 50 samples, reporting 77.2% agreement for summarization and 90.7% for harmlessness (Appendix A.4, Table 7). The paper then uses this judge to measure win rates with differences as small as 0.5 pp (Reddit summarization: 87% full-tuning vs. 86.5% PE-RLHF; Table 3).

**The consequence.** A judge with 77.2% human agreement introduces a measurement noise floor of approximately 22.8% disagreement. If the judge's errors are random (uncorrelated with the policy being evaluated), this noise inflates confidence intervals but does not bias the point estimates — however, with only 500 test questions (Reddit TL;DR test split), the standard error on a win rate near 87% is approximately sqrt(0.87 × 0.13 / 500) ≈ 1.5 pp, so a 0.5 pp difference between full-tuning and PE-RLHF is well within the noise floor and cannot be distinguished from zero. If the judge's errors are **systematic** — e.g., the judge consistently prefers outputs with a particular style, length, or formality level that PE-RLHF tends to produce more or less often — then the point estimates themselves are biased in an unknown direction and magnitude.

The practical consequence is that **the paper's central claim of "comparable performance" for summarization tasks rests on a measurement instrument that is not calibrated precisely enough to detect differences of the magnitude being reported as "comparable."** The win rate differences between full-tuning and PE-RLHF on summarization tasks are: Reddit 87% vs. 86.5% (-0.5 pp), BOLT Messages 73.2% vs. 75.5% (+2.3 pp). The BOLT gap of 2.3 pp is larger and might survive measurement noise, but the Reddit gap of 0.5 pp is almost certainly within the judge's error margin. The paper treats both as evidence of comparability without distinguishing between "statistically indistinguishable" and "practically equivalent," which are different standards.

The harmlessness evaluation is on firmer ground (90.7% human agreement), but the reported harmless rates are near ceiling (96-98%), making the effective resolution even coarser — a 1.6 pp difference between full-tuning (96.6%) and PE-RLHF (98.2%) on a test set where the metric is already saturated is hard to interpret.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 7 (Appendix A.4) reports the 50-sample validation study. Section 4.3 describes the judge evaluation protocol, including the positional debiasing procedure (averaging two prompt orderings, ties for single-ordering wins). The paper does not report: (1) whether the 50 validation samples are representative of the full test distribution in terms of difficulty or response diversity; (2) whether the judge's errors are systematic (e.g., correlated with response length, complexity, or policy identity); (3) confidence intervals on the win rate estimates; or (4) calibration of the judge's win rate scale against ground-truth human win rates (does a judge-reported 87% win rate correspond to a human-judged 87% win rate, or is there a systematic offset?).

**Mitigation status.** Minimally addressed. The 50-sample validation is commendable as a sanity check but is too small and too under-analyzed to support the paper's evidentiary claims. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation in Section 7 or the Limitations section, focusing instead on overfitting and generalizability concerns. For a paper whose central claim is "comparable performance," the measurement methodology is a first-order concern that should have been explicitly discussed with proposed mitigations (e.g., human evaluation on a larger subset, calibration studies, or conservative statistical thresholds).

### Limitation 3: PE-RLHF Is Not Tested on Models Large Enough to Generalize the "Better on Bigger Models" Claim to Frontier Scale

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's most compelling conceptual insight is that PE-RLHF becomes more competitive with full RLHF as the backbone model grows larger (evidenced in Tables 2-3 across PaLM 2 XXS → XS → S). This is the finding that distinguishes the work from a trivial "LoRA works for RLHF" claim — it establishes a scaling trend with predictive implications. However, the paper only tests this trend up to PaLM 2 S, which is explicitly labeled as the third of four sizes in the PaLM 2 family (XXS, XS, S, L). The L model (the largest) is used as the **judge**, not as a training backbone. The largest model trained with PE-RLHF is therefore at least one tier below frontier scale.

**The consequence.** The "better on bigger models" trend is established over three data points (XXS → XS → S) that span roughly one order of magnitude in parameters (the exact parameter counts for PaLM 2 are not public, but the naming convention and typical Google scaling imply a substantial but not frontier-scale range). Extrapolating from three data points to predict behavior at 10× or 100× larger scale is statistically unjustified — the trend could plateau, reverse, accelerate, or exhibit a phase change at larger scales where the relationship between pretrained capacity and adapter expressiveness changes qualitatively.

The practical consequence is that **the paper's most policy-relevant claim — that PE-RLHF is especially well-suited for the largest models where full RLHF is most expensive — is untested.** If the trend reverses at PaLM 2 L scale (because, for example, larger models develop more specialized representations that require higher-rank adaptations), then the paper's optimistic projections are misleading. If the trend continues, then the paper understates its own importance by only demonstrating it on mid-size models.

This matters because the target audience for PE-RLHF is precisely those who work with models large enough that full RLHF is prohibitive. For a researcher fine-tuning a 1B-parameter model, full RLHF might be tractable and the 1.3× speedup from PE-RLHF might not justify the added complexity. For a team aligning a 100B+ parameter model, the difference between feasible and infeasible could hinge on whether the trend holds at scale — and the paper provides no evidence either way.

The paper also does not test whether the trend interacts with the VLM setting. The Gemini Pro experiments (Tables 2-3, bottom) use a single model size, so there is no within-family scaling data for vision-language models. Given that VQAv2 already shows weaker efficiency gains than text-only tasks (74% HBM for RM vs. 43-56%), the scaling behavior might differ qualitatively when vision encoders dominate the parameter count.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Tables 2-3 contain the model-size breakdown for PaLM 2 XXS, XS, and S. The acknowledgment that PaLM 2 L was only used as a judge appears in Section 4. The paper does not discuss why the L model was not used as a training backbone, and it does not frame the absence of frontier-scale experiments as a limitation — the Limitations section (after Section 7) mentions overfitting, data efficiency, and comparison with other PEFT methods, but not scale limitations.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's Limitations section focuses on potential overfitting, data efficiency, and comparison with other PEFT methods (DoRA, ReFT). The absence of larger-scale experiments is not mentioned as a limitation, despite being the most direct way to validate the paper's central conceptual contribution. The future work section (Section 7) proposes open-sourcing, reward hacking mitigation, and broader generalization, but not scale validation.

### Limitation 4: Memory Savings During RL Are Fundamentally Capped by the Anchor and Reward Model Footprint

**The assumption or constraint.** PE-RLHF reduces the number of trainable parameters by applying LoRA to the policy and value models during the RL phase. The paper reports RL memory savings of 20-26% (Table 1: 74-80% of full-tuning peak HBM). However, the RL loop still requires loading the **full-parameter anchor model** (for KL penalty computation) and the **full-parameter reward model** (for response scoring) — neither of which benefits from LoRA during this phase because they are frozen. The LoRA adapters on the policy and value models only reduce the memory overhead from optimizer states and gradient storage for those models' trainable parameters; the base weights of the policy and value models still occupy the same memory as in full-tuning.

**The consequence.** The 20-26% memory reduction is a **relative saving on the trainable-parameter component of RL memory, not on total RL memory.** If the anchor model and reward model together account for a large fraction of peak HBM (because they are being used for forward passes on every generated sequence, potentially with large batch sizes), the effective savings from PE-RLHF on the RL phase shrink proportionally. For configurations where the anchor and RM dominate memory — e.g., when using a very large anchor model with a relatively small policy, or when generating long response sequences that require storing large attention key-value caches for all four models (policy, value, anchor, RM) — the memory reduction could be substantially less than 20%.

This is a **structural ceiling** on what parameter-efficient RL can achieve: you cannot eliminate the need to load the anchor and reward models during training without fundamentally changing the RLHF algorithm (e.g., using off-policy methods that don't require anchor log-probabilities, or using DPO instead of RLHF). The paper's efficiency framing — "up to 50% reduction for reward models, and 27% for RL" (abstract) — implies that PE-RLHF approaches the same memory efficiency across both phases, but the underlying mechanism is different, and the RL ceiling is hard.

The practical consequence is that **PE-RLHF does not enable RLHF on dramatically smaller hardware than full RLHF during the RL phase.** If full RLHF requires 8× A100-80GB GPUs because the anchor model alone occupies 60GB per GPU, PE-RLHF might reduce the requirement to 7× or 6× GPUs — a cost saving, but not a qualitative change in accessibility. The reward model training phase, by contrast, genuinely enables different hardware tiers (e.g., fitting an RM training job on a single GPU that previously required two). The paper's abstract headline ("50% reduction", "27% reduction") collapses these qualitatively different scaling behaviors into a single narrative of universal efficiency.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 reports the HBM numbers. Figure 2 illustrates the four-model memory configuration visually. Section 2.2 describes the components loaded during RL. The paper does not decompose the total RL memory into components (anchor, RM, policy weights, value weights, optimizer states, activations), which would make the structural ceiling explicit. Section 5.3 notes that "the memory savings and speed-up depend on multiple factors, such as sequence lengths of the examples, the accelerators being used, etc." but does not quantify the sensitivity.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed as a limitation. The paper's efficiency claims are presented as point estimates without discussing bounds, ceilings, or component-level breakdowns. The Limitations and Future Work sections do not mention the structural memory ceiling. Potential mitigations that the paper could have discussed include: quantizing the frozen anchor and RM weights (e.g., to int8 or float16, cutting their memory roughly in half); sharing a single frozen backbone between the policy, value, and anchor models with separate adapters; or using distillation-based KL approximations that don't require the full anchor model to be resident. None of these are explored.

### Limitation 5: PE-RLHF Is Benchmarked Exclusively Against Full RLHF, Not Against Parameter-Efficient Alternatives to RLHF

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper frames PE-RLHF as an efficiency improvement to the RLHF pipeline and benchmarks it exclusively against standard full-parameter RLHF. It explicitly acknowledges the existence of alternative alignment methods — DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), RAFT (Dong et al., 2023), RRHF (Yuan et al., 2023), SLiC-HF (Zhao et al., 2023), Pairwise Cringe Optimization (Xu et al., 2023) — in Section 6.3, but does not compare PE-RLHF against any parameter-efficient version of these methods. The paper also does not test whether LoRA applied to DPO (which eliminates the RL phase entirely, including the need for an anchor model and reward model during training) would provide superior efficiency-quality tradeoffs.

**The consequence.** The paper's core value proposition is "you can now do RLHF with parameter efficiency." But **a practitioner deciding how to align their model faces a broader set of options than just standard RLHF vs. PE-RLHF.** The most directly relevant alternative is DPO, which reframes alignment as a supervised learning problem on preference pairs and eliminates the RL loop, the value model, the anchor model, and the online generation step — all of which are the primary sources of memory and time cost during the RL phase. If LoRA-DPO achieves comparable alignment quality to PE-RLHF while being simpler to implement, more stable to train, and more memory-efficient (no anchor model, no value model, no online generation), then PE-RLHF is solving a problem that has already been bypassed by algorithmic innovation.

The paper's memory savings story for RL (20-26% reduction) becomes less compelling when compared against methods that eliminate the multi-model footprint entirely. The 1.05-1.3× RL speedup is even less impressive against DPO's supervised learning paradigm, which trains in a single forward/backward pass per batch without autoregressive generation. The paper provides no data to help a practitioner choose between these paths.

Additionally, the paper does not test whether **combining PE-RLHF's reward model training with DPO's policy optimization** (i.e., use LoRA RM to train a reward model, then use that reward model's implicit preferences for DPO training) would outperform either approach alone. This is a natural hybrid that the paper's own analysis of the RL memory bottleneck would motivate — if the RL phase is the limiting factor, replace it with something that doesn't require anchor/RM co-residency.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 6.3 acknowledges the existence of alternative alignment methods. The paper's primary results (Tables 1-3, Figure 3) compare only against full RLHF and SFT baselines. No experiments involve DPO, RAFT, or any other alignment method. The VQAv2 experiments (Appendix A.3.6) mention trying learning rates for RL (1e-7, 1e-8) but do not describe experiments with alternative alignment algorithms.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed as future work by implication. The paper's stated goal is to "motivate the benchmarking of other PEFT and ReFT approaches on RLHF tasks" (Section 1), which includes alternative alignment methods only insofar as they are applied to RLHF tasks. The Limitations section (after Section 7) explicitly notes "Comparison with Other PEFT and ReFT Methods: We solely focus on LoRA... our benchmarking work does not include these newer methods." This acknowledges the PEFT-method comparison gap but not the alignment-algorithm comparison gap. The paper does not propose comparing PE-RLHF against parameter-efficient DPO or other alignment methods as future work.

### Limitation 6: The Paper Provides No Evidence on Whether PE-RLHF Mitigates or Exacerbates Reward Hacking

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper identifies reward hacking as a known failure mode of RLHF (Section 6.2: "overfitting the reward function can be problematic... requiring techniques like early stopping or parameter reduction to ensure optimal policy training") and hypothesizes that PE-RLHF's constrained capacity might serve as implicit regularization against it (Section 2: the connection between parameter reduction and overfitting prevention). The paper also proposes "Mitigating Reward Hacking" as a future work direction (Section 7), citing recent work on weight-averaged reward models (Ramé et al., 2024).

**The consequence.** The paper **never measures reward hacking behavior in either the full RLHF or PE-RLHF policies**, making the hypothesized regularization benefit entirely speculative. Reward hacking manifests as the policy achieving high reward model scores while producing outputs that are actually low-quality — e.g., repetitive text, overly short responses, or outputs that exploit quirks in the reward model's training distribution. The standard diagnostic is to track both reward model scores and true quality metrics (human judgments or downstream task performance) over the course of RL training; divergence between these curves indicates hacking.

Without this measurement, the claim that PE-RLHF might help prevent overfitting to the reward model is untestable from the paper's data. It is equally plausible that LoRA's constrained capacity makes the policy *more* susceptible to reward hacking because it has fewer degrees of freedom and may discover degenerate strategies that the reward model fails to penalize — a kind of "simplicity bias" where the easiest way to achieve high reward within a low-rank constraint is to produce repetitive or generic text that happens to score well.

The practical consequence is that **a practitioner deploying PE-RLHF has no guidance on whether they need to implement additional anti-hacking safeguards** (early stopping, reward model ensembles, KL penalty tuning) beyond what they would use for full RLHF. If PE-RLHF reduces hacking risk, they can relax these safeguards and save additional compute. If it increases hacking risk, they need stronger safeguards, potentially erasing the efficiency gains. The paper provides no evidence either way.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper mentions reward hacking conceptually in Sections 6.2 and 7, and provides a reward model overfitting discussion in Section 6.2 with a reference to Azar et al. (2023). The REINFORCE training setup includes KL regularization with β = 0.05 (for text tasks), which is a standard anti-hacking mechanism, but there is no analysis of whether this β value is sufficient or whether KL divergence between policy and anchor diverges differently for PE-RLHF vs. full RLHF. The paper does not report reward model scores during training, KL divergence trajectories, or qualitative examples of policy outputs that might reveal hacking behavior. The "comparable performance" results in Table 3 are endpoint evaluations (final policy quality) and cannot distinguish between "policy genuinely learned to produce better outputs" and "policy learned to hack the reward model but the judge (PaLM 2 L) penalized the hacked outputs."

**Mitigation status.** Identified as future work but not empirically addressed. Section 7 proposes "Mitigating Reward Hacking" as a research direction, suggesting that weight-averaging reward models "could offer similar benefits at a lower computational cost" when combined with PE-RLHF. This is a proposal for future integration, not an analysis of the current system's hacking vulnerability. The paper should have measured basic hacking indicators (reward score vs. quality trajectories, KL divergence over training) to establish a baseline understanding of whether PE-RLHF changes the hacking risk profile, even if solving the problem was out of scope.
