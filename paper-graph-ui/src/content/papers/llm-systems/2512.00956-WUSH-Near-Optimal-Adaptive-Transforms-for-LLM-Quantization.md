## 1. Executive Summary

This paper derives closed-form optimal linear blockwise transforms for joint weight-activation quantization of LLMs, introducing **WUSH**—a construction that combines a fixed Hadamard backbone with a data-dependent second-moment component to form non-orthogonal, per-block transforms that are provably near-optimal for both floating-point and integer block quantizers (e.g., MXFP4 and INT4 under RTN AbsMax-scaled quantization). Evaluated on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen3-8B/14B/32B, and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct using the LM Evaluation Harness and Platinum Benchmarks, WUSH improves W4A4 accuracy over the strongest Hadamard-based baselines by up to +2.8 average points with RTN and +0.7 with GPTQ on MXFP4, while its fused GPU kernel delivers up to 6.6× per-layer throughput over BF16 via FP4 MatMul with only 1.3% average overhead relative to optimized Hadamard kernels. The theoretical analysis establishes that purely orthogonal transforms cannot reduce quantization error in the ideal FP regime and that the Hadamard matrix emerges as the unique data-agnostic component of the optimal transform, with nontrivial error reduction achieved only when combined with a spectrum-aware scaling factor that aligns the data distribution's principal directions with the quantizer's error-minimizing regions.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Outliers Stretch Quantization Dynamic Range

The fundamental challenge this paper tackles is the **quantization of large language models**—specifically, the compression of model weights and activations from high-precision formats (e.g., BF16) down to low-bit representations (e.g., 4-bit). Quantization is now standard practice for deploying LLMs on resource-constrained hardware, as it reduces both memory footprint and compute requirements. However, a persistent obstacle makes low-bit quantization difficult: **a small number of extreme-valued "outlier" weights and activations**.

These outliers are not rare glitches—they are systematic features of LLM representations. Consider a weight matrix in a transformer layer: most values might cluster around zero within a modest range, but a handful of entries in particular channels can be orders of magnitude larger. When quantizing with a blockwise approach (e.g., dividing a vector into groups of 32 elements, each with its own scale), these few extreme values determine the quantization scale for the entire group. This means the vast majority of "normal" values get compressed into very few quantization bins, amplifying representation error disproportionately.

The paper articulates this in Section 1:

> "a few extreme 'outlier' weights and activations expand the dynamic range and thereby degrade the effective resolution of low-bit representations."

This is not merely an engineering inconvenience—it is the **primary bottleneck** preventing low-bit quantization from achieving accuracy parity with full-precision models. For 4-bit weight-activation quantization (W4A4), the performance drop relative to BF16 can be severe, especially under formats like MXFP4 that have reduced dynamic range compared to NVFP4. Without effective outlier mitigation, practical deployment of LLMs at very low precision remains accuracy-limited.

### Why This Problem Matters: The Stakes of Quantization Quality

The importance of this problem spans both practical deployment and theoretical understanding:

**Practical deployment pressure.** Modern LLMs are massive. Llama-3.1-70B, for example, requires over 140 GB of memory just to store its parameters in BF16. Inference on consumer-grade hardware is impossible without aggressive compression. Even on datacenter GPUs, memory bandwidth often bottlenecks inference latency—reducing precision from 16-bit to 4-bit theoretically quadruples the number of tokens that can be processed per unit time. The paper reports speedups of up to **6.6×** for FP4 matrix multiplication over BF16 (Section 5, Figure 2). For real-time applications, cloud services, and edge deployment, these multipliers translate directly into cost savings, reduced latency, and broader accessibility.

**A fragmented landscape of solutions with unclear tradeoffs.** Prior to this work, the quantization community had developed multiple families of approaches to the outlier problem—explicit outlier storage, non-uniform bitwidths, and transform-based methods—but there was no principled understanding of *optimality*. Each method came with its own assumptions, hardware constraints, and failure modes. The transform-based family in particular, which applies linear transformations to "spread out" outlier energy before quantization, had shown strong empirical results but rested on heuristic choices (Hadamard rotations, learned rotations). No one had answered a deceptively simple question: **if the Hadamard transform works well, is it truly optimal, and if so, in what sense?**

**A theoretical gap in the quantization literature.** Standard quantization theory (e.g., for uniform scalar quantizers under MSE distortion) tells us that quantization error depends on the variance of the signal. But LLM quantization operates under **blockwise constraints**—transformations must be applied per-block (not globally) to be computationally feasible at inference time—and must contend with **joint weight-activation quantization**, where errors in weight and activation representations compound in the output. Existing theory did not address optimal blockwise linear transforms under these practical constraints, nor did it distinguish between floating-point and integer quantizer behaviors. The paper identifies this gap explicitly:

> "Yet, these transforms are typically fixed and data-agnostic: in particular, the Hadamard transform does not adapt to the statistics of the underlying weights or activations. This raises a natural question: if the Hadamard transform is not data-aware, in what sense can it be considered optimal for quantization?" (Section 1)

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three major families of prior work, each with specific limitations that WUSH is designed to overcome:

#### 1. Non-Uniform Bitwidths and Explicit Outlier Storage

Methods like **LLM.int8()** (Dettmers et al., 2022), **SpQR** (Dettmers et al., 2024), and **QUIK** (Ashkboos et al., 2024a) explicitly identify outlier values and store them in higher precision while quantizing the remaining values aggressively. For instance, LLM.int8() uses 8-bit quantization for most weights but keeps outlier columns in 16-bit, decompressing them on-the-fly during inference.

**Where they fall short:** The paper notes that "the resulting formats are irregular and require specialized kernels" (Section 2). This irregularity—mixing different precisions, storing outlier indices, handling sparse high-precision components—makes efficient GPU implementation difficult. These methods often achieve good accuracy at low *effective* bitwidths but incur latency penalties from the irregular memory access patterns and decompression logic. The hardware ecosystem increasingly favors formats like MXFP4 and NVFP4 that are regular (all values in a block share the same precision and layout) and amenable to dense matrix multiplication kernels. WUSH is designed specifically for these regular block formats.

#### 2. Channel-Wise Rescaling Methods

**SmoothQuant** (Xiao et al., 2023) and **AWQ** (Lin et al., 2024) take a different approach: rather than removing outliers, they rescale channels to balance the dynamic ranges of weights and activations. SmoothQuant, for example, applies a per-channel scaling factor that reduces activation outliers at the cost of slightly increasing weight outliers (or vice versa), finding a compromise that minimizes overall quantization error.

**Where they fall short:** These methods are essentially diagonal transforms—they scale each channel independently but do not mix information across channels. This means they cannot address the *joint geometric structure* of outliers. If an outlier's impact depends on correlations between channels (which it does—an activation outlier in one channel compounds with a weight outlier in the corresponding input dimension), channel-wise scaling cannot disentangle this interaction. The paper's Appendix A.2 makes this point visually (Figure 4): an anisotropic joint distribution can have heavy-tailed marginals that diagonal transforms only mask, not resolve. WUSH employs full dense block transforms that can rotate and scale in the multivariate space, targeting the joint structure directly.

#### 3. Orthogonal Transform-Based Methods (Hadamard and Learned Rotations)

This is the family most directly relevant to WUSH. Several influential works apply orthogonal transformations—rotations that preserve vector norms—before quantization:

- **QuIP** (Chee et al., 2023) introduces "incoherence processing," applying random orthogonal matrices to weights and activations to make outliers less coherent (less concentrated in particular channels).
- **QuIP#** (Tseng et al., 2024a) and **QTIP** (Tseng et al., 2024b) extend this with lattice codebooks and trellis-based quantization.
- **QuaRot** (Ashkboos et al., 2024b) uses Hadamard transforms specifically, applying them across both attention and MLP blocks to spread outlier energy.
- **SpinQuant** (Liu et al., 2025) and **FlatQuant** (Sun et al., 2025) learn orthogonal transforms from calibration data, optimizing them to minimize quantization error.

**Where they fall short—the three critical limitations:**

**First, these transforms are data-agnostic or only heuristically data-aware.** The Hadamard matrix is completely fixed—it does not depend on the weights or activations of the specific model being quantized. It is essentially a "spreading" operation that redistributes energy across dimensions, justified by the intuition that more uniform distributions quantize better. But as the paper shows in Section 4.2.3 (Equation 21), a purely orthogonal transform has a trivial effect on the quantization loss:

$$\mathbb{E}_{y, \varepsilon(Ty)} \left\| T^{-1} \varepsilon(Ty) \right\|^2 = \left(\mathbb{E}_\eta \eta^2\right) \, \text{tr}(S^2)$$

The trace term $\text{tr}(S^2)$—which depends on the spectrum of the data covariance—is *unchanged* by orthogonal transformations. Any orthogonal $T$ (identity, Hadamard, learned rotation) yields the same expected error under the paper's FP quantizer model. This means orthogonal transforms **cannot reduce quantization error in the FP regime** if the quantizer's error model is correct. They merely redistribute error across dimensions without changing the total.

The paper explicitly connects this to a core insight about what transforms *must* do to be effective:

> "the non-trivial choices $U' = H$ and $S' = S^{1/2}$ are both essential for reducing the error. Either trivial choices of $U' = I$ or $S' = I$ will lead to the same suboptimal trace term" (Section 4.2.3)

The scaling component $S^{1/2}$ is what makes the transform non-orthogonal, and it is precisely what prior transform-based methods lack.

**Second, even the learned transforms (SpinQuant, FlatQuant) are optimization-based rather than closed-form.** They require gradient-based training on calibration data, which is computationally expensive and does not guarantee optimality. The paper positions this as a key differentiator:

> "As opposed to prior methods such as SpinQuant or FlatQuant, our approach is closed-form and calibration-based, as it does not require training or fine-tuning the entire model." (Section 1)

A closed-form solution is not just computationally cheaper—it enables theoretical analysis of optimality that gradient-based methods cannot provide.

**Third, recent empirical work had shown that even the best Hadamard-based methods were hitting a ceiling.** Egiazarian et al. (2025) (the MR-GPTQ paper) demonstrated that blockwise Hadamard transforms offer the best empirical performance among many existing transforms, but also "highlights the limited gains achievable with Hadamard alone, linking this to the properties of the underlying weight and activation distributions" (Section 2). This is a crucial piece of context: the strongest existing method had reached the limits of what purely orthogonal transforms could achieve, and further improvement required a fundamentally different approach—one that could exploit data-dependent structure beyond rotation.

### The Emerging Hardware Landscape Adds Urgency

A final contextual factor that motivates this work is the evolution of hardware-supported quantization formats. The paper specifically studies **MXFP4** (Microscaling FP4, an OCP standard) and **NVFP4** (NVIDIA's FP4 format), both of which are blockwise floating-point formats designed for efficient hardware acceleration. These formats differ in key ways:

- **MXFP4** uses a group size of 32 with a shared 8-bit scale, effectively making the scale exponent shared across the block while the mantissa bits are per-element. This gives it some INT-like behavior in the subnormal regime (where the exponent is at minimum and the effective step size becomes uniform).
- **NVFP4** uses a group size of 16 and preserves the top element within each block more carefully, which can actually make orthogonal transforms *harmful* (as noted in Table 1: the Hadamard alone increases NVFP4 loss relative to identity).

The paper's timing is deliberate: as hardware increasingly supports these formats natively, the question of *optimal transforms* for them becomes commercially relevant. A transform that works well for MXFP4 may be suboptimal for NVFP4, and vice versa. The theoretical framework in WUSH handles both formats within a unified derivation, with the quantizer's error model (multiplicative for FP, additive per-group for INT) determining the optimal transform structure.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution as filling a precise gap: **deriving the closed-form optimal blockwise linear transform for joint weight-activation quantization**, under the realistic constraints that:

1. The transform is applied blockwise (aligning with quantization group boundaries) to keep online inference overhead minimal.
2. The quantizer is RTN AbsMax-scaled—the standard approach in practice, not a simplified idealization.
3. Both floating-point and integer formats are handled explicitly, with their distinct error models.
4. The solution is calibration-based (requiring a small sample of activations) but not training-based, making it applicable as a post-training quantization step.

The key positioning move relative to prior work is the **shift from heuristic to principled design**. Rather than asking "what transform works well empirically?", the paper asks "what is the optimal transform under these specific quantization models?" The Hadamard matrix emerges naturally from the derivation—not as a heuristic, but as the unique data-agnostic orthogonal component that satisfies the equalization condition required for optimality (Equation 41: the diagonal of $S \odot (H^\top e_k)(H^\top e_k)^\top$ must have equal entries, which $H$ provides). The data-dependent components ($S^{-1/2}$, $U^\top$, $W'^\top$) also emerge naturally from the minimization of the expected error, giving each piece of the WUSH construction a precise role:

- $W'^\top$: whitens the weight covariance, decorrelating channels
- $U^\top$: rotates the activation distribution into the principal axes of the weight-activation cross-covariance
- $S^{-1/2}$: rescales to equalize the effective singular values, making the distribution more isotropic
- $H$: randomizes the coordinate axes to spread any remaining anisotropy uniformly, minimizing the maximum per-dimension outlier impact

This decomposition also explains *why* prior methods partially worked. For example, QuaRot's Hadamard-only approach handles the $H$ component but misses the spectrum-aware scaling $S^{-1/2}$ and rotation $U^\top$, which is why it provides limited gains in some regimes (particularly MXFP4, where the FP quantizer's multiplicative error model demands non-orthogonal scaling). SmoothQuant's per-channel scaling is a crude approximation to $S^{-1/2}$ acting only on diagonal terms. SpinQuant's learned rotation approximates $U^\top H$ but without the $S^{-1/2}$ scaling and without theoretical guarantees.

In summary, the paper addresses a problem that is simultaneously practical (making low-bit LLM inference accurate), theoretically unresolved (no existing theory of optimal blockwise transforms for quantization), and timely (hardware support for block FP4 formats is maturing). Its positioning is not to propose yet another heuristic but to provide a principled framework that unifies and explains existing empirical successes while pointing to strictly better constructions.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

WUSH is a **post-training quantization method** that computes per-block linear transforms—applied to both weights and activations before rounding them to low precision—using a closed-form formula derived from the second-moment statistics of the weights and a small set of calibration activations. It solves the problem of heavy-tailed outliers degrading low-bit quantization by shaping the joint distribution of each quantization block so that the quantizer's inherent error is provably minimized, and it does this without any gradient-based training or iterative optimization.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components, processed in sequence during a one-time calibration pass:

1. **Calibration Data Collection** — a small set of input activations is collected by running a few batches of data through the unquantized model, one layer at a time, to capture the activation statistics.

2. **Cholesky Decomposition of Second Moments** — for each linear layer, the weight and activation covariance matrices (averaged over the calibration data) are decomposed into lower triangular factors $W'$ and $X'$ via Cholesky factorization. This is the "data-aware" step that extracts the joint structure of weights and activations.

3. **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of the Cross-Moment** — the product $W'^\top X'$ is decomposed via SVD into $U S V^\top$. The matrices $U$ and $V$ are orthogonal ("rotation" components), and $S$ is a diagonal matrix of singular values that capture the "strength" of the weight-activation coupling along each direction.

4. **WUSH Transform Construction** — from the Cholesky factors and SVD components, the per-block weight transform $T_{\text{wush}}$ and activation transform $T_{\text{xvsh}}$ are assembled via a fixed formula (Equation 8) that combines a Hadamard matrix $H$, the singular values $S^{-1/2}$, and the rotation matrices. The transforms are non-orthogonal (they scale and rotate, not just rotate).

5. **GPTQ-Aware Weight Pre-Quantization** — the computed transforms are applied to the weights, the transformed weights are quantized (using GPTQ for error compensation), and the calibration activations are propagated through the quantized layer to provide inputs for the next layer. This interleaving of transform computation and GPTQ error propagation handles the coupling between transforms (which depend on the weights being transformed) and weight updates (which change the weight statistics).

During **inference**, the forward pass for a linear layer with $C$ blocks is computed as:
$$\sum_{i=1}^{C} \tilde{W}_{(i)}^\top \, q(T_{\text{wush}(i)} \tilde{X}_{(i)})$$
where $\tilde{W}_{(i)}$ is the pre-quantized transformed weight block, $\tilde{X}_{(i)}$ is the incoming activation block, $q$ is the RTN AbsMax quantizer, and $T_{\text{wush}(i)}$ is the per-block WUSH transform applied online to the activations before quantization.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal problem setup and block-independent decomposition — this establishes the layerwise quantization loss and shows how it splits into independent per-block subproblems, which is the mathematical justification for computing per-block transforms independently.
- **Second**, the probabilistic reformulation and problem reduction — this transforms the blockwise loss into an expectation over random vectors and reduces the two-sided (weight + activation) quantization problem to an equivalent one-sided problem, which is the key simplification that makes the closed-form solution tractable.
- **Third**, the WUSH construction and its GPTQ integration (Algorithm 1) — this provides the executable procedure, showing how the transforms are computed block-by-backwards-block and interleaved with GPTQ weight updates to handle error propagation.
- **Fourth**, the theoretical derivation for FP quantizers — this walks through the error model (multiplicative noise proportional to value), the derivation of the expected loss, and the minimization that yields $U' = H$, $S' = S^{1/2}$ as the optimal configuration, explaining why orthogonal transforms alone achieve nothing.
- **Fifth**, the theoretical derivation for INT quantizers — this covers the error model (additive noise proportional to block max), the lower and upper bounds on the expected loss, and the near-optimality proof, explaining why the same transform configuration works for both FP and INT.
- **Sixth**, the GPU kernel implementation — this describes how the per-block transforms are stored, loaded, and fused into a GEMM-equivalent computation to achieve near-zero overhead relative to fixed Hadamard kernels.

This is primarily a **theoretical analysis paper with a practical system built from the theory**, whose core idea is that the optimal blockwise linear transform for quantization minimizes a closed-form expected error expression and can be computed directly from the weight and activation covariance structure without iterative optimization.

#### Problem Setup: Layerwise Quantization Loss and Block Decomposition

The paper formalizes weight-activation quantization as an output-preservation problem. For a linear layer with weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{out}}}$ and input activation $X \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{batch}}}$, the desired output is $W^\top X$. Under quantization, the actual output becomes $q(T_W W)^\top q(T_X X)$, where $T_W, T_X \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{in}}}$ are linear transforms applied to the weights and activations respectively before they enter the quantizer $q(\cdot)$. The goal is to choose $T_W$ and $T_X$ to minimize the $L_2$ output distortion.

**The layerwise loss.** The paper defines the normalized loss as:

$$\ell = d_{\text{out}}^{-1} d_{\text{batch}}^{-1} \left\| q(T_W W)^\top q(T_X X) - W^\top X \right\|_F^2$$

where $d_{\text{out}}$ is the output dimension, $d_{\text{batch}}$ is the number of tokens in the calibration batch, and $\|\cdot\|_F$ is the Frobenius norm. The normalization by $d_{\text{out}}^{-1} d_{\text{batch}}^{-1}$ converts the sum of squared errors into a per-element average, making losses comparable across layers of different sizes.

**What this loss measures:** it is the mean squared error between the full-precision output (what the unquantized layer would produce) and the quantized output (what the layer produces after transforming weights and activations, quantizing them, and multiplying). This is more faithful than measuring weight error alone because it captures how weight and activation quantization errors compound through the matrix multiplication—a specific pattern of weight errors may be harmless for one activation distribution and catastrophic for another.

**Why this form:** computing the full output difference is more expensive than per-weight error, but it is the quantity that actually matters for end-to-end model accuracy. Every downstream layer sees the output of this layer, not the weights directly. Prior work often optimized weight quantization error in isolation (e.g., minimizing $\|q(W) - W\|$), which ignores the interaction with activations. This paper's formulation is strictly more principled because the objective function is the actual quantity whose preservation matters.

**Block-diagonal constraint.** The transforms $T_W$ and $T_X$ are constrained to be block-diagonal with block size $d$, where $d$ divides $d_{\text{in}}$ and aligns with the quantization group size. This constraint is not arbitrary—it is a computational necessity for inference.

At inference time, the activation transform $T_X$ must be applied online (unlike the weight transform, which can be pre-computed). If $T_X$ were a full dense matrix of size $d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{in}}$, applying it to each incoming token would cost $O(d_{\text{in}}^2)$ operations, which would dominate the cost of the layer itself for many practical dimensions. By constraining $T_X$ to be block-diagonal with small block size $d$ (typically 32 for MXFP4, 16 for NVFP4), the online cost becomes $O(d_{\text{in}} \cdot d)$—linear in the input dimension rather than quadratic. This is the same cost model that makes quantization itself practical (blockwise scales avoid global reductions).

Formally, the matrices are partitioned into $d_{\text{in}}/d$ blocks along the input dimension:

$$T_W = \text{diag}(T_{W(1)}, \ldots, T_{W(d_{\text{in}}/d)}), \quad T_{W(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$$
$$T_X = \text{diag}(T_{X(1)}, \ldots, T_{X(d_{\text{in}}/d)}), \quad T_{X(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$$
$$W^\top = [W_{(1)}^\top, \ldots, W_{(d_{\text{in}}/d)}^\top], \quad W_{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{\text{out}}}$$
$$X^\top = [X_{(1)}^\top, \ldots, X_{(d_{\text{in}}/d)}^\top], \quad X_{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{\text{batch}}}$$

The output (without and with quantization) can then be expressed as sums over blocks:

$$W^\top X = \sum_{i=1}^{d_{\text{in}}/d} W_{(i)}^\top X_{(i)}$$
$$q(T_W W)^\top q(T_X X) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_{\text{in}}/d} q(T_{W(i)} W_{(i)})^\top q(T_{X(i)} X_{(i)})$$

**The key approximation.** Because the transforms are block-diagonal, the full layerwise loss decomposes approximately into independent per-block losses:

$$\ell_{(i)} = d_{\text{out}}^{-1} d_{\text{batch}}^{-1} \left\| q(T_{W(i)} W_{(i)})^\top q(T_{X(i)} X_{(i)}) - W_{(i)}^\top X_{(i)} \right\|_F^2$$

The layerwise loss is approximated as $\ell \approx \sum_i \ell_{(i)}$, meaning each block is optimized independently. This is a critical simplification: instead of jointly optimizing all $d_{\text{in}}/d$ transform blocks (which are coupled through the Frobenius norm), the paper solves $d_{\text{in}}/d$ independent problems of size $d \times d$. Since $d$ is small (16 or 32), each problem is tractable.

**Why this decomposition is valid.** The block-diagonal constraint means that each block's contribution to the output depends only on that block's weights, activations, and transforms—there is no cross-block interaction in the matrix multiplication beyond the summation. The squared Frobenius norm of a sum is not strictly the sum of squared norms (cross-terms exist), but the paper treats these cross-terms as negligible. This is a standard approximation in blockwise quantization (it holds when the errors in different blocks are approximately uncorrelated), and it is the same assumption that justifies independent per-block quantization in the first place.

#### Probabilistic Reformulation and Problem Reduction

**Why a probabilistic formulation?** The deterministic loss in the previous section depends on a specific calibration batch $X$, but the goal is to minimize quantization error for *future* unseen activations. Rather than overfitting to the calibration data, the paper treats the columns of $W$ and $X$ as i.i.d. samples from underlying distributions. This turns the optimization into an expectation over random variables, which is the natural setting for deriving optimal transforms that generalize.

Specifically, each column $\mathbf{w}_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ of $W_{(i)}$ (there are $d_{\text{out}}$ such columns) is treated as drawn i.i.d. from a distribution $\mathcal{D}_w$, and each column $\mathbf{x}_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ of $X_{(i)}$ (there are $d_{\text{batch}}$ such columns) is drawn i.i.d. from $\mathcal{D}_x$. This modeling choice is motivated in Appendix A.2: it is explicitly multivariate, treating each $d$-dimensional quantization block as a random vector with a joint distribution, rather than treating coordinates independently. The paper argues that this is essential because "quantization error is driven by joint properties (e.g., how mass concentrates along dominant directions and how extreme points interact with group-wise scaling), which cannot be certified by marginal statistics alone."

Under this probabilistic view, the blockwise loss becomes an expectation:

$$\ell = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{x}} \left( q(T_W \mathbf{w})^\top q(T_X \mathbf{x}) - \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} \right)^2$$

where $\mathbf{w} \sim \mathcal{D}_w$ and $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{D}_x$ are independent random vectors.

**Second-moment matrices.** The weight and activation covariance information is summarized in two $d \times d$ matrices $W'$ and $X'$ that satisfy:

$$W' W'^\top = \mathbb{E}_\mathbf{w}[\mathbf{w} \mathbf{w}^\top]$$
$$X' X'^\top = \mathbb{E}_\mathbf{x}[\mathbf{x} \mathbf{x}^\top]$$

These are the Cholesky factors of the second-moment matrices. In practice, $W' W'^\top$ is estimated as $d_{\text{out}}^{-1} W_{(i)} W_{(i)}^\top$ (averaging over the columns of the weight block), and $X' X'^\top$ is estimated as $d_{\text{batch}}^{-1} X_{(i)} X_{(i)}^\top$ (averaging over the calibration tokens). The Cholesky decomposition was chosen without loss of generality (Lemma 2 in Appendix A.1 proves invariance to the specific choice of decomposition): any alternative square-root decomposition would produce the same final transforms up to sign and permutation changes.

When $W_{(i)}$ or $X_{(i)}$ is rank-deficient (fewer independent degrees of freedom than $d$), the paper dampens the diagonal of the second-moment matrix before Cholesky decomposition (Algorithm 1, line 1: the flag $\lambda \geq 0$ is added as a Tikhonov regularizer). This ensures numerical stability.

**SVD of cross-moment.** The interaction between weights and activations is captured by the $d \times d$ matrix $W'^\top X'$. This matrix is decomposed via the thin singular value decomposition:

$$W'^\top X' = U S V^\top$$

where $U, V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ are orthogonal matrices (their columns are the left and right singular vectors), and $S = \text{diag}(s_1, \ldots, s_d) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is a diagonal matrix of singular values, ordered $s_1 \geq \cdots \geq s_d > 0$.

**What the singular values mean.** The singular values $s_k$ measure the "coupling strength" between the weight and activation distributions along specific directions. A large $s_k$ means that along the $k$-th singular vector pair (the $k$-th column of $U$ for activations and the $k$-th column of $V$ for weights), the weight and activation covariances are strongly aligned—perturbations in that direction produce large outputs. An extreme outlier scenario corresponds to one singular value dominating the others ($\text{tr}(S) \approx s_1$), while a perfectly isotropic scenario has all $s_k$ equal ($\text{tr}(S^2) = d^{-1}(\text{tr}(S))^2$). The spectrum of $S$—how concentrated versus spread-out the singular values are—determines how much a data-aware transform can improve over a data-agnostic one.

**Unbiased quantization and the constraint $T_W = T_X^{-\top}$.** The paper assumes the quantizer $q$ is stochastic and unbiased: for any vector $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the quantization error $\varepsilon(\alpha) = q(\alpha) - \alpha$ is a random vector with $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon(\alpha)] = 0$. This is a standard assumption in quantization theory (dithering can make quantization unbiased).

To make the quantized output $q(T_W \mathbf{w})^\top q(T_X \mathbf{x})$ an unbiased estimator of the true output $\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}$, the paper imposes the constraint $T_W = T_X^{-\top}$ (equivalently, $T_X = T_W^{-\top}$, which means $T_X$ is the inverse transpose of $T_W$). Under this constraint, $T_W^\top T_X = I$, so $\mathbf{w}^\top T_W^\top T_X \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}$, and the product of the *unquantized* transformed vectors equals the true output exactly. The quantization introduces only noise, which has zero mean if $q$ is unbiased (and if the cross-term $\varepsilon(T_W \mathbf{w})^\top \varepsilon(T_X \mathbf{x})$ is negligible—the paper treats it as a first-order approximation and drops it).

**Why this constraint is reasonable.** It means the weight and activation transforms are not independent—choosing one determines the other. This is computationally efficient (only one transform per block needs to be stored and applied at inference time—the weight transform is pre-applied offline, the activation transform is applied online) and theoretically clean (it decouples the weight and activation quantization errors, allowing each to be optimized separately).

**Splitting the loss into two terms.** Under the unbiasedness and $T_W = T_X^{-\top}$ assumptions, and using a first-order approximation (dropping the cross-term $\varepsilon(T_W \mathbf{w})^\top \varepsilon(T_X \mathbf{x})$), the expected loss splits into two non-negative terms:

$$\ell = \left( \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}, \varepsilon(T_W \mathbf{w})} \left\| X'^\top T_W^{-1} \varepsilon(T_W \mathbf{w}) \right\|^2 \right) + \left( \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}, \varepsilon(T_X \mathbf{x})} \left\| W'^\top T_X^{-1} \varepsilon(T_X \mathbf{x}) \right\|^2 \right)$$

where $\varepsilon(\cdot)$ is the random quantization error vector, and $X'$ and $W'$ are the Cholesky factors from earlier. The detailed derivation is in Equation (37) of Appendix A.3.

**What each term represents.** The first term is the weight quantization error, weighted by the activation covariance $X'$: a weight error in a direction that activations heavily use (large $X'$ component) contributes more to output error than a weight error in an unused direction. The second term is the activation quantization error, weighted by the weight covariance $W'$: an activation error in a direction where weights have large magnitude matters more. This weighting is precisely what makes the problem non-trivial—a uniform treatment of all dimensions (which orthogonal transforms provide) cannot account for this direction-dependent sensitivity.

**One-sided reduction.** The two terms have identical structure (one with $W$, $X'$ and the other with $X$, $W'$), so solving for one solves for the other. The paper focuses on the second term (activation quantization error) and defines an equivalent "one-sided" problem.

Define the random variable $\mathbf{y} = W'^\top \mathbf{x}$ (the activation vector projected into the weight-covariance-weighted space). Its distribution is $\mathcal{D}_y$. Define the transformation matrix $T = T_X W'^{-\top}$ (absorbing the activation Cholesky factor into the transform for notational convenience). Then the activation error term becomes:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}, \varepsilon(T_X \mathbf{x})} \left\| W'^\top T_X^{-1} \varepsilon(T_X \mathbf{x}) \right\|^2 = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}, \varepsilon(T \mathbf{y})} \left\| T^{-1} \varepsilon(T \mathbf{y}) \right\|^2$$

**What this reduction achieves.** The original two-sided problem (find $T_W$ and $T_X$ jointly under the constraint $T_W = T_X^{-\top}$) has been reduced to a single optimization: find the $d \times d$ matrix $T$ that minimizes the expected quantization error $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}, \varepsilon(T \mathbf{y})} \| T^{-1} \varepsilon(T \mathbf{y}) \|^2$, where $\mathbf{y}$ has known second-moment structure $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y} \mathbf{y}^\top] = U S^2 U^\top$ (derived from the SVD of $W'^\top X'$). Once the optimal $T$ is found, $T_X$ is recovered as $T_X = T W'^\top$, and $T_W = T_X^{-\top}$.

**Why this reduction works.** The key insight is that the weight and activation covariances enter the problem only through the SVD of $W'^\top X'$. By defining $\mathbf{y}$ as the activation projected through $W'^\top$, the weight covariance is "baked into" the distribution of $\mathbf{y}$, and the optimization only needs to handle one random vector with a known covariance structure. This is analogous to whitening in signal processing, but the transform $T$ is more general—it is not constrained to be orthogonal or to produce a white output.

#### Transform Parameterization and the SVD Representation

To optimize over $T$, the paper parameterizes it in terms of the known SVD components $U$ and $S$ plus unknown matrices to be solved for. Specifically, the unknown transform $T$ is decomposed via its own SVD, but expressed relative to the known $U$ and $S$:

$$T = U' S' R^\top S^{-1} U^\top$$

where:
- $U', R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ are unknown orthogonal matrices (to be optimized),
- $S' = \text{diag}(s'_1, \ldots, s'_d)$ is an unknown diagonal matrix with $s'_1 \geq \cdots \geq s'_d > 0$ (to be optimized),
- $U$ and $S$ are the *known* matrices from the SVD of $W'^\top X'$.

**What this parameterization means operationally.** Any valid transform $T$ can be expressed as: first, apply $U^\top$ (rotate into the principal axes of $\mathbf{y}$'s covariance), then $S^{-1}$ (scale inversely to the singular values—whitening), then $R^\top$ (an unknown rotation), then $S'$ (an unknown scaling), then $U'$ (an unknown rotation). The unknown components are $U'$, $S'$, and $R$—the paper's theoretical contribution is finding their optimal values.

**The singular value relationship.** A useful property of the known singular values $S$ is derived from basic inequalities. For any positive diagonal $S$:

$$\text{tr}(S^2) \geq d^{-1} (\text{tr}(S))^2 \geq d^{-1} \text{tr}(S^2)$$

The first inequality is Jensen's (the average of squares exceeds the square of the average). The second is from $\text{tr}(S^2) \geq (\text{tr}(S))^2 / d$ when rearranged: $(\text{tr}(S))^2 \geq \text{tr}(S^2)$. The left equality ($\text{tr}(S^2) = d^{-1}(\text{tr}(S))^2$) holds when all $s_k$ are equal—the perfectly isotropic case where no single direction dominates. The right equality ($d^{-1}(\text{tr}(S))^2 = d^{-1}\text{tr}(S^2)$) holds when one singular value dominates ($\text{tr}(S) \to s_1$)—the extreme outlier case. The gap between $\text{tr}(S^2)$ and $d^{-1}(\text{tr}(S))^2$ measures how anisotropic the distribution is, and thus how much a data-aware transform can potentially improve over a data-agnostic one. The theoretical upper bound on improvement is a factor of $d$.

**Theorem 1 (informal statement).** The optimal configuration for FP quantizers is $U' = H$ (the Hadamard matrix), $S' = S^{1/2}$, and $R = I$. For INT quantizers, the same configuration is optimal up to a factor that depends on whether the distribution $\mathcal{D}_y$ is tail-bounded (e.g., Gaussian or Laplacian, where the gap is $o(d)$) or arbitrary (where the gap is at most $d$).

The proof of this theorem occupies Sections 4.2 (for FP) and 4.3 (for INT) and is detailed below.

#### WUSH Construction and GPTQ Integration (Algorithm 1)

Before diving into the theoretical proofs, Algorithm 1 provides the concrete executable procedure. The algorithm takes as input the full weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{out}}}$, calibration activations $X \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{batch}}}$, a Hadamard matrix $H$ of size $d \times d$, a damping ratio $\lambda \geq 0$, and a boolean flag indicating whether to use GPTQ (versus RTN fallback). It outputs the per-block WUSH transforms $T_{\text{wush}(i)}$ and the pre-quantized transformed weights $\tilde{W}_{(i)}$.

**Step-by-step walkthrough of Algorithm 1:**

**Lines 1-2: Compute activation second moment and its Cholesky factor.** The Hessian-like matrix $\mathbf{H} = d_{\text{batch}}^{-1} X X^\top + \lambda I$ is computed over the full input dimension $d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{in}}$. This is the same Hessian used in GPTQ (the expected outer product of activation vectors, regularized by $\lambda I$ for numerical stability). The Cholesky decomposition $\mathbf{H} = X' X'^\top$ yields a lower triangular $X' \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{in}}}$.

**Line 3: Compute target output.** The matrix $Y = W^\top X' \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}$ is the output of the layer when the activation is replaced by its Cholesky factor $X'$. This $Y$ serves as the "target" that quantization should preserve—it is the cross-moment between weights and (decomposed) activations.

**Lines 4-17: Process blocks in reverse order.** The algorithm iterates from block $i = d_{\text{in}}/d$ down to 1. This reverse order is essential for GPTQ integration: GPTQ updates the remaining (not-yet-processed) weight columns to compensate for the quantization error introduced in the current block, so processing must proceed from later columns to earlier ones to ensure that errors from already-quantized blocks are propagated correctly into not-yet-quantized blocks.

**Lines 5-7: Extract block and compute its SVD.** For block $i$, the algorithm selects the $d_{\text{out}} \times d$ submatrix $Y[:, j_1:j_2]$ corresponding to the block's columns. The SVD of this submatrix is computed: $\text{SVD}(Y[:, j_1:j_2]) \to U \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times d}, S \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}, V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. The normalization by $d_{\text{out}}$ is applied: $U \leftarrow d_{\text{out}}^{1/2} U$ and $S \leftarrow d_{\text{out}}^{-1/2} S$, scaling the singular vectors and values to account for the output dimension (analogous to the $d_{\text{out}}^{-1}$ normalization in the loss).

**Line 8: Build the WUSH transform for this block.** The activation-domain transform is constructed as:

$$T_{\text{wush}(i)} = H S^{1/2} V^\top (X'[j_1:j_2, j_1:j_2])^{-1}$$

where $X'[j_1:j_2, j_1:j_2]$ is the $d \times d$ diagonal block of the Cholesky factor $X'$ corresponding to this quantization block. The inverse of this triangular matrix serves as the "unwhitening" step for activations (since $T_X = T W'^\top$, and $X'$ is the activation Cholesky factor). Note that $T_{\text{wush}(i)}$ is the transform applied to activations as $T_X$; the weight transform is $T_{\text{wush}(i)}^{-\top}$ (the inverse transpose), which is applied implicitly in the next step.

**Line 9: Apply the weight transform.** The transformed weights are computed as:

$$\bar{W} = H S^{1/2} U^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{\text{out}}}$$

This is the product of the Hadamard matrix, the square root of the singular values, and the transpose of the left singular vectors. This $\bar{W}$ is the weight block after applying $T_W = T_{\text{wush}(i)}^{-\top}$, expressed in the basis induced by the SVD.

**Lines 10-12: Intra-block GPTQ (if flag is True).** If GPTQ mode is active, the quantized weight block $\tilde{W}_{(i)}$ is obtained by calling the GPTQ subroutine on the transformed weights $\bar{W}$, using the transformed Hessian $H S H^\top$ (the Hessian in the transformed space). The GPTQ subroutine performs column-wise quantization with error compensation: it quantizes one column of $\bar{W}$ at a time, computes the resulting error, and updates the remaining columns to compensate. The transformed Hessian $H S H^\top$ captures how errors in different weight columns contribute to output error, accounting for both the activation statistics (through $S$) and the Hadamard mixing (through $H$).

**Lines 13-15: RTN fallback.** If not using GPTQ, the weights are simply quantized via RTN: $\tilde{W}_{(i)} = q(\bar{W})$, where $q$ is the blockwise AbsMax RTN quantizer.

**Lines 15 and 12: Inter-block error propagation.** After quantizing block $i$, the algorithm updates the remaining target $Y$ to reflect the quantized weights. The update is:

$$Y \leftarrow Y - \tilde{W}_{(i)}^\top T_{\text{wush}(i)} X'[j_1:j_2, 1:d_{\text{in}}]$$

for GPTQ, or

$$Y \leftarrow Y - \bar{W}^\top T_{\text{wush}(i)} X'[j_1:j_2, 1:d_{\text{in}}]$$

for RTN. This is a blockwise triangular solve: it computes the contribution of the (quantized) current block to the output and subtracts it from $Y$, so that when subsequent (earlier) blocks are processed, their SVD sees only the residual output that has not yet been accounted for. This is the same mechanism as GPTQ's original sequential column updates, but expressed in the Cholesky-transformed space for computational efficiency (it avoids explicit Hessian inversion, following the simplification from Chen et al., 2025).

**Post-algorithm: model-level calibration.** Algorithm 1 processes one linear layer. The full model is calibrated layer-by-layer in the standard GPTQ fashion: start with the first layer, run calibration data through the unquantized model to collect activations, compute WUSH transforms and quantize weights for that layer via Algorithm 1, then propagate the calibration data through the newly quantized layer (using Equation 10) to get inputs for the next layer, and repeat. This sequential processing ensures that each layer's calibration activations reflect the quantization of all earlier layers, matching the distribution the layer will actually see at inference time.

**During inference**, a linear layer's forward pass is computed as:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\text{in}}/d} \tilde{W}_{(i)}^\top \, q(T_{\text{wush}(i)} \tilde{X}_{(i)})$$

where $\tilde{X}_{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{\text{batch}}}$ is the $i$-th block of the incoming activation, $\tilde{W}_{(i)}$ is the pre-quantized transformed weight block, $T_{\text{wush}(i)}$ is applied online (fused with quantization in the GPU kernel, see Section 5), and $q$ is the RTN AbsMax quantizer operating on each column of the transformed activation block. The sum over blocks produces the final output.

#### FP Quantizer Derivation: Why Orthogonal Transforms Fail and WUSH Works

This section proves Theorem 1 for floating-point quantizers. The approach proceeds in two stages: first, model the quantizer's error behavior (Section 4.2.1), then plug this model into the expected loss and minimize over the transform parameters (Section 4.2.2).

**FP quantization error model.** For floating-point formats (like MXFP4/E2M1, meaning 2 exponent bits and 1 mantissa bit), the paper models the quantization error as multiplicative: the error in each coordinate is proportional to the value of that coordinate. Formally, for a vector $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^d$:

$$\varepsilon(\alpha) = \text{diag}(\eta) \, \alpha$$

where $\eta = [\eta_1, \ldots, \eta_d]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a random vector of i.i.d. noise samples with $\mathbb{E}[\eta] = 0$, and $\text{diag}(\eta)$ is the diagonal matrix with $\eta$ on the diagonal. The random variable is $\eta_k \sim \mathcal{D}_\eta$ for each coordinate $k$.

**What this model says physically.** When you quantize a floating-point number, the quantization step size is proportional to the number's magnitude (this is the defining property of floating-point: the spacing between representable numbers grows with the exponent). The error is the difference between the true value and the nearest representable FP number, which is approximately a random fraction of the value (if the mantissa bits are treated as uniform random after rounding). The paper's Appendix A.4.2 provides a rigorous derivation: the FP quantizer's error can be approximated as $\varepsilon(\alpha_k) = ((1 + c \cdot 2^{-b} \xi)(1 + c \cdot 2^{-\tilde{b}} \tilde{\xi}) - 1) \alpha_k$, where $\xi, \tilde{\xi} \sim \text{Uniform}(-1/2, 1/2)$, $b$ is the mantissa bits, and $c$ is a constant ($\ln 2$). This product expands to $(\text{terms with zero mean}) \cdot \alpha_k$, justifying the multiplicative noise assumption.

**Why this modeling makes the problem non-trivial.** Unlike additive noise (where the error is independent of the signal), multiplicative noise means that the error *covaries with the transformed signal*. If the transform $T$ makes some coordinates of $T\mathbf{y}$ large and others small, the quantization error will be large in the large coordinates and small in the small coordinates—but the inverse transform $T^{-1}$ then "unmixes" these errors, potentially amplifying the large-coordinate errors back into the original space. The optimal $T$ must balance these competing effects.

**Plugging the error model into the expected loss.** Substituting $\varepsilon(T\mathbf{y}) = \text{diag}(\eta) T\mathbf{y}$ into the one-sided loss:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}, \varepsilon(T \mathbf{y})} \left\| T^{-1} \varepsilon(T \mathbf{y}) \right\|^2 = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}, \eta} \left\| T^{-1} \text{diag}(\eta) T \mathbf{y} \right\|^2$$

The derivation in Equation (39) simplifies this to:

$$= \left(\mathbb{E}_\eta[\eta^2]\right) \cdot \text{tr}\left( T^{-1} \left( \left(U' S'^2 U'^\top\right) \odot I \right) T^{-\top} \right)$$

where $\odot$ denotes elementwise multiplication, $I$ is the identity matrix, and we have used $\mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\mathbf{y} \mathbf{y}^\top] = U' S'^2 U'^\top$ (the covariance of $\mathbf{y}$ in the transformed basis, derived from the parameterization of $T$ in Equation 16 and the fact that $\mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\mathbf{y} \mathbf{y}^\top] = U S^2 U^\top$).

**Restating what this trace computes.** The term $\text{tr}(T^{-1} ((U' S'^2 U'^\top) \odot I) T^{-\top})$ is a weighted sum of the variances of $T^{-1}$'s columns. Specifically, $\text{diag}(U' S'^2 U'^\top)$ extracts the diagonal entries of $U' S'^2 U'^\top$—these are the per-coordinate variances of $T\mathbf{y}$, which are determined by $S'$ and the rotation $U'$. The matrix $T^{-1}$ then maps these per-coordinate errors back to the original space, and the trace sums the resulting per-dimension mean squared errors.

**The key insight: why orthogonal transforms do nothing.** When $T$ is orthogonal (meaning $T^{-1} = T^\top$), the trace simplifies to $\text{tr}(S^2)$ regardless of which orthogonal matrix is chosen (Equation 42). Formally, for any orthogonal $T$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}, \varepsilon(T \mathbf{y})} \left\| T^{-1} \varepsilon(T \mathbf{y}) \right\|^2 = \left(\mathbb{E}_\eta[\eta^2]\right) \cdot \text{tr}(S^2)$$

This is because an orthogonal transform merely rotates the basis but does not change the total variance or the per-dimension error structure—the Hadamard, a random rotation, and the identity all produce the *same* expected error. Under the multiplicative noise model, orthogonal transforms are completely ineffective for reducing quantization error. This is the paper's first major theoretical finding: the empirical success of Hadamard rotations for MXFP4 cannot be explained by FP multiplicative noise alone—there must be additional INT-like behavior in the format (which the paper discusses in Section 6.1).

**Lower bounding the trace.** The paper derives a lower bound on the trace term (Equation 40):

$$\text{tr}\left( T^{-1} \left( \left(U' S'^2 U'^\top\right) \odot I \right) T^{-\top} \right) \geq d^{-1} (\text{tr}(S))^2$$

This bound holds for any choice of $U'$, $S'$, and $R$—it is a fundamental limit on how small the quantization error can be made.

**Achieving the lower bound.** The paper then shows (Equation 41) that the specific choices $U' = H$ (Hadamard), $S' = S^{1/2}$, and $R = I$ attain this lower bound exactly:

$$\text{tr}\left( T^{-1} \left( \left(H S H^\top\right) \odot I \right) T^{-\top} \right) = d^{-1} (\text{tr}(S))^2$$

This computation relies on a crucial property of the Hadamard matrix: $|e_j^\top H^\top e_k| = d^{-1/2}$ for all $j, k$—every basis vector has equal squared projection onto every Hadamard basis vector. This means that $\text{diag}(H S H^\top)$ has all entries equal to $d^{-1} \text{tr}(S)$, which makes the per-coordinate variances uniform and allows the Cauchy-Schwarz and Jensen inequalities used in the lower bound derivation to become tight.

**What this shows operationally.** For FP quantization, the optimal transform is:

$$T = H S^{1/2} I^\top S^{-1} U^\top = H S^{-1/2} U^\top$$

which is the product of $U^\top$ (rotate into the principal axes), $S^{-1/2}$ (scale inversely with the square root of the singular values—this is the "whitening plus" step that makes the distribution more isotropic), and $H$ (apply Hadamard to equalize per-coordinate variances). The activation transform is then $T_X = T W'^\top = H S^{-1/2} U^\top W'^\top$, matching the WUSH construction in Equation (8) (with the $S^{-1/2}$ transposed correctly and $V$ from the SVD appearing through the relationship $W'^\top \leftrightarrow T_X = T_W^{-\top}$).

**Why the non-trivial choices are essential.** If $U' = I$ (identity instead of Hadamard) or $S' = I$ (no scaling), the trace term reverts to $\text{tr}(S^2)$ (Equations 43 and 44), which is the same as the orthogonal-transform case. The improvement over orthogonal transforms comes *only* from the combination of $H$ (to equalize diagonal entries) and $S^{1/2}$ (to compress the singular value spread). Neither alone suffices—a Hadamard-only transform ($S' = I$) is orthogonal and achieves nothing under the FP model; a whitening-only transform ($U' = I$, $S' = S^{1/2}$) fails to equalize the per-coordinate variances and also achieves nothing.

**Quantifying the maximum improvement.** The ratio of the orthogonal-transform error to the optimal error is $\text{tr}(S^2) / d^{-1}(\text{tr}(S))^2$, which ranges from 1 (when all $s_k$ are equal—no room for improvement) to $d$ (when one singular value dominates—the maximum possible gain). For the typical block size $d = 32$, the theoretical maximum improvement over orthogonal transforms is $32\times$ in squared error (or $\sqrt{32} \approx 5.7\times$ in RMSE), though practical gains are smaller because real LLM weight-activation spectra are not perfectly degenerate.

#### INT Quantizer Derivation: Block-Max Error and Near-Optimality

This section proves Theorem 1 for integer quantizers. The approach is structurally similar to the FP case but the error model differs fundamentally, leading to a different functional form for the expected loss.

**INT quantization error model.** For integer formats (like INT4), the quantization error in a block is determined by the maximum absolute value in that block (the AbsMax scale). Formally, for a vector $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^d$:

$$\varepsilon(\alpha) = \|\alpha\|_\infty \, \eta$$

where $\|\alpha\|_\infty = \max_k |\alpha_k|$ is the maximum absolute value, and $\eta = [\eta_1, \ldots, \eta_d]^\top$ is a random vector of i.i.d. noise with $\mathbb{E}[\eta] = 0$. The noise models the rounding error within each quantization bin; the scale $\|\alpha\|_\infty$ determines the bin size. The paper's Appendix A.4.1 derives this: for $b$-bit symmetric integer quantization with scale $s = 2^{1-b} \|\alpha\|_\infty$, the error is $s \cdot \xi$ where $\xi \sim \text{Uniform}(-1/2, 1/2)$, yielding $\varepsilon(\alpha) = 2^{1-b} \|\alpha\|_\infty \xi$.

**Key difference from FP:** the error in each coordinate depends on the *global* maximum of the entire block (through $\|\alpha\|_\infty$), not on the individual coordinate's value. This couples all coordinates together—making one coordinate smaller does not reduce its error if another coordinate remains large. This fundamentally shapes the optimal transform: it must reduce the expected block-max norm $\mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty^2]$, not just the per-coordinate variances.

**Plugging the error model into the expected loss.** Substituting $\varepsilon(T\mathbf{y}) = \|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty \eta$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}, \varepsilon(T \mathbf{y})} \left\| T^{-1} \varepsilon(T \mathbf{y}) \right\|^2 = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}, \eta} \left\| T^{-1} \|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty \eta \right\|^2$$

The derivation in Equation (45) simplifies to:

$$= \left(\mathbb{E}_\eta[\eta^2]\right) \cdot \left\| T^{-1} \right\|_F^2 \cdot \mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y} \left[ \|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty^2 \right]$$

where $\|T^{-1}\|_F^2 = \text{tr}(T^{-1} T^{-\top})$ is the squared Frobenius norm of the inverse transform.

**What this product of three factors means.** The expected error is the product of (1) the noise variance per quantization bin, (2) a term $\|T^{-1}\|_F^2$ that measures how much the inverse transform amplifies the per-coordinate errors back into the original space, and (3) $\mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty^2]$ that measures how large the transformed signal becomes in its worst-case coordinate. The tension is: making $\|T^{-1}\|_F^2$ small (by using large singular values in $T$) makes $\mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty^2]$ large (because $T$ amplifies the signal), and vice versa. The optimal $T$ balances these.

**Lower bounding $\|T^{-1}\|_F^2$.** Using von Neumann's trace inequality and the parameterization of $T$ in Equation (16):

$$\|T^{-1}\|_F^2 = \text{tr}(S^2 R S'^{-2} R^\top) \geq \text{tr}(S^2 S'^{-2})$$

The equality (lower bound attained) holds when $R = I$—that is, when the rotation $R$ aligns the ordered singular values of $S$ and $S'^{-1}$ in the same order. Since $s_1 \geq \cdots \geq s_d$ and $s'_1 \geq \cdots \geq s'_d$, the alignment that minimizes the trace is $R = I$ (largest $s_k$ paired with largest $s'_k^{-1}$, i.e., smallest $s'_k$—but the ordering convention and inequality direction require careful handling; the result is that $R$ must be the identity to attain the trace inequality bound).

**Bounding $\mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty^2]$.** This term is more subtle. For an arbitrary orthogonal matrix $U'$, the expected block-max squared norm is bounded between two extremes (Equation 50):

$$d^{-1} \mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|^2] \leq \mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty^2] \leq \mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|^2]$$

where $\mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|^2] = \text{tr}(S'^2)$ (from the covariance of $T\mathbf{y}$, Equation 36). The lower bound ($d^{-1} \text{tr}(S'^2)$) holds when all coordinates have equal expected squared magnitude—the Hadamard matrix achieves this because $|e_k^\top H^\top e_j| = d^{-1/2}$, making all diagonal entries of $H S'^2 H^\top$ equal to $d^{-1} \text{tr}(S'^2)$. For general distributions, the upper bound is $O(1)$ times $\text{tr}(S'^2)$, but for tail-bounded distributions (Gaussian or Laplacian), the paper proves a much tighter bound: $\mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty^2] \leq o(d)^{-1} \text{tr}(S'^2)$, meaning the gap between the maximum coordinate and the average coordinate grows only logarithmically with $d$, not linearly.

This tightening uses Lemma 3 (Appendix A.5): for zero-mean Gaussian variables $X_k$, the expected maximum squared value satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\max_k X_k^2] \leq (2\ln(2d) + 2) \max_k \mathbb{E}[X_k^2]$. For Laplacian, the bound is $(2^{-1}(\ln d)^2 + \ln d + 1) \max_k \mathbb{E}[X_k^2]$. Since $\max_k \mathbb{E}[X_k^2] = d^{-1} \text{tr}(S'^2)$ when $U' = H$ (all coordinates have equal variance), the gap factor is $O(\ln d)$ for Gaussian and $O((\ln d)^2)$ for Laplacian—both $o(d)$.

**Combining the bounds.** With $R = I$ and $U' = H$, the product $\|T^{-1}\|_F^2 \cdot \mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty^2]$ is bounded by:

$$d^{-1} \text{tr}(S^2 S'^{-2}) \, \text{tr}(S'^2) \leq \|T^{-1}\|_F^2 \cdot \mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty^2] \leq \text{tr}(S^2 S'^{-2}) \, \text{tr}(S'^2)$$

where the upper bound is the general case and the tighter $o(d)^{-1}$ factor applies for tail-bounded distributions.

**Optimizing over $S'$.** The remaining free parameter is $S'$. The product $\text{tr}(S^2 S'^{-2}) \, \text{tr}(S'^2)$ is minimized by choosing $S'$ proportional to $S^{1/2}$ (i.e., $s'_k = c \cdot \sqrt{s_k}$ for some constant $c$). This follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (Equation 29):

$$\text{tr}(S^2 S'^{-2}) \, \text{tr}(S'^2) \geq \left( \text{tr}\left( (S^2 S'^{-2})^{1/2} (S'^2)^{1/2} \right) \right)^2 = (\text{tr}(S))^2$$

with equality when $S'^2 \propto S$ (since $(S^2 S'^{-2})^{1/2} = S S'^{-1}$ and $(S'^2)^{1/2} = S'$, and their product is $S$ when $S' \propto S^{1/2}$). Choosing $S' = S^{1/2}$ (without loss of generality, since any constant scaling of $T$ cancels in the product of forward and inverse transforms) attains the lower bound.

**The near-optimality result.** Plugging everything together, the expected loss for the optimal configuration ($U' = H$, $S' = S^{1/2}$, $R = I$) satisfies:

$$d^{-1} (\text{tr}(S))^2 \leq \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}, \varepsilon} \left\| T^{-1} \varepsilon(T \mathbf{y}) \right\|^2 \leq \begin{cases} o(d)^{-1} (\text{tr}(S))^2 & \text{tail-bounded } \mathcal{D}_y \\ (\text{tr}(S))^2 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

For tail-bounded distributions (Gaussian or Laplacian, which are good models for LLM activations after normalization), the gap between the achievable lower bound and the upper bound realized by WUSH shrinks to a logarithmic factor in $d$, making the construction near-optimal.

**Comparison with orthogonal transforms for INT.** For any orthogonal $T$, the Frobenius norm is $\|T^{-1}\|_F^2 = d$, and $\mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty^2] \leq \text{tr}(S^2)$ (for general distributions) or $o(1) \text{tr}(S^2)$ (for tail-bounded distributions with $T = H$, the best orthogonal case). The error bounds are:

$$d \cdot d^{-1} \text{tr}(S^2) = \text{tr}(S^2) \leq \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}, \varepsilon}[\cdots] \leq d \cdot \text{tr}(S^2)$$

Comparing with the optimal bounds, the ratio of worst-case orthogonal error to optimal error is at most $d$ (in the extreme outlier case where $\text{tr}(S^2) \approx d \cdot d^{-1}(\text{tr}(S))^2$). For the Hadamard (best orthogonal) with tail-bounded distributions, the gap is $o(d)$ times smaller than the worst-case orthogonal but still $d$ times larger than optimal in the extreme outlier regime.

**Why the same $U' = H, S' = S^{1/2}, R = I$ works for both FP and INT.** Despite the very different error models (multiplicative vs. block-max), the optimal configuration is identical. The intuition: $S^{1/2}$ compresses the dynamic range of the singular values (reducing outlier impact), $H$ equalizes per-coordinate contributions (minimizing the worst-case coordinate in INT, equalizing per-coordinate variances in FP), and $R = I$ aligns the rotation with the covariance structure. The Hadamard matrix's equal-projection property ($|e_j^\top H^\top e_k| = d^{-1/2}$ for all $j, k$) is the unique algebraic property that enables optimality in both settings.

---

#### GPU Kernel Implementation

The online portion of WUSH—applying the per-block transforms $T_{\text{wush}(i)}$ to incoming activation blocks and quantizing them—must execute efficiently on GPU hardware. This section describes how the paper achieves near-zero overhead relative to fixed Hadamard kernels.

**The storage layout challenge.** Unlike Hadamard-only methods where one $d \times d$ matrix is reused for all blocks, WUSH requires storing $C = d_{\text{in}}/d$ distinct transform matrices, each $d \times d$. For a typical layer with $d_{\text{in}} = 4096$ and $d = 32$, this is 128 matrices—storing them naively would require careful memory layout to avoid scattered reads.

The paper's solution is to store the WUSH matrices in a transposed layout: shape $(G, G, C)$ where $G = d$ is the block size and $C$ is the number of blocks. The first dimension is the inner dimension of each transform, the second is the outer dimension, and the third indexes blocks. This is transposed relative to a conventional $(C, G, G)$ layout; the transposition is performed offline (during calibration) and incurs no runtime cost. The layout is motivated by the observation that for small group sizes ($G = 32$ for MXFP4), the transform operation is memory-bound—the kernel's performance is limited by how fast it can load transform matrices from memory, not by the arithmetic. The $(G, G, C)$ layout ensures that consecutive threads access consecutive elements of a single $G \times G$ matrix, maximizing coalesced memory access.

**Mapping to GEMM.** The online computation for a single layer with batch size $M$ (number of tokens) and block count $C$ is modeled as a GEMM-equivalent operation. The activation tensor $\tilde{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times d_{\text{in}}}$ (reshaped as $M \times (C \times G)$) must be multiplied by the per-block transforms, which is equivalent to $C$ independent matrix multiplications of size $(M \times G) \times (G \times G) \to (M \times G)$, one per block.

In the CUTLASS GEMM template used for the kernel, the outer dimension $M$ of the activation tensor maps to the $M$ dimension of the GEMM, and the outer dimension $G$ of each WUSH matrix (per block) maps to the $N$ dimension of the GEMM. Critically, the internal GEMM dimension $K'$ is fixed to $G$ (the inner dimension of one transform block), *not* $C \times G$. The block index $C$ is handled implicitly as an offset that each thread block applies to determine which $G \times G$ submatrix to load based on which $K/G$ subproblem it processes. This means each thread block loads exactly one $G \times G$ transform matrix to process its assigned activation block, matching the memory access pattern of the fixed Hadamard kernel (which loads one $G \times G$ matrix once and reuses it). The only difference is that different thread blocks may load different matrices from different positions in the $(G, G, C)$ tensor, but because the layout is contiguous, the access is coalesced.

**Performance results (Figure 2).** On an RTX 5090 at batch size $M = 1024$, the MXFP4 kernels (including transform + quantization + matrix multiplication) achieve speedups of up to **6.63×** over BF16 across three models (Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, Llama-3.1-70B) and three layer shapes (different combinations of input/output dimensions). The theoretical maximum speedup of FP4 over BF16 on this GPU is 8× (since FP4 uses 1/4 the bits), so the achieved 6.63× represents 83% of the theoretical peak—reasonable overhead for the quantization and transform operations.

Comparing **WUSH + Quant** against **H + Quant** (the optimized Hadamard kernel with quantization from Egiazarian et al., 2025), the average throughput difference is only **1.3%**. In some configurations, WUSH matches H + Quant identically. The paper attributes this negligible overhead to two factors: (1) the kernel design ensures each thread block still loads only one $G \times G$ matrix, so the memory traffic is the same (the per-block specialization affects *which* matrix, not *how many*), and (2) the L1 and L2 caches effectively reuse the transform matrices that are shared across tiles of the $M$ dimension (multiple thread blocks processing different tile rows of the same block index).

The remaining gap to the **None** baseline (no transform, no quantization—just reading and writing in BF16) indicates that performance is limited primarily by the quantization overhead (reading and writing the activation matrix with reduced precision, applying scales), not by the transform choice. This is a positive result: it means that switching from a fixed Hadamard to per-block WUSH transforms is essentially free in hardware terms, so the accuracy gains come without a latency penalty.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Block-diagonal transforms aligned with quantization group size**: enables efficient online application (the transform cost is linear in the input dimension, not quadratic) and preserves the memory-bound nature of the quantization operation. A full dense transform would make inference impractical for large models.
- **Cholesky decomposition for second-moment factorization**: lower triangular Cholesky factors are unique (up to sign) and numerically stable. The paper proves (Lemma 2) that the final transform is invariant to the choice of decomposition (any square root works), so Cholesky is chosen for its computational convenience.
- **$T_W = T_X^{-\top}$ constraint**: decouples weight and activation quantization errors, makes each optimizable separately, and halves the number of transforms that need to be stored and applied. It is a natural choice because the product $T_W^\top T_X$ appears in the output computation, and setting it to $I$ avoids amplifying the signal before quantization.
- **Hadamard matrix $H$ as the data-agnostic component**: the unique property $|e_j^\top H e_k| = d^{-1/2}$ equalizes the diagonal entries of $H \text{diag}(\mathbf{v}) H^\top$ for any diagonal $\mathbf{v}$, which is what attains the lower bounds in both FP and INT derivations. Any other orthogonal matrix with this equal-projection property would work equally well in theory, but Hadamard matrices are fast (they can be applied via the Walsh-Hadamard transform in $O(d \log d)$ time instead of $O(d^2)$, though the paper's implementation uses the dense GEMM formulation for the small $d = 32$ case).
- **$S^{1/2}$ scaling (square root of singular values)**: compresses the singular value spectrum—replacing a distribution with singular values $(s_1, s_2, \ldots)$ with one having singular values $(\sqrt{s_1}, \sqrt{s_2}, \ldots)$ reduces the ratio of largest to smallest from $s_1/s_d$ to $\sqrt{s_1/s_d}$, making the distribution more isotropic. This is the non-orthogonal component that prior transforms lacked, and it is essential for reducing error under both FP and INT error models.
- **$R = I$ (no additional rotation)**: the ordering alignment between $S$ (descending) and $S'$ (descending) is optimal for von Neumann's trace inequality. Any permutation would pair large singular values with small scaling factors, increasing the product.
- **GPTQ integration with reverse-order block processing**: GPTQ requires processing blocks from last to first to propagate quantization errors correctly. The WUSH computation is interleaved with this, recomputing the SVD for each block on the *residual* target $Y$ after subtracting the contributions of already-quantized blocks. This handles the coupling between weight updates (which change the statistics used to compute the transform) and transform construction (which depends on the current statistics).
- **Damping ratio $\lambda$ for rank-deficient second moments**: when a weight or activation block has fewer than $d$ independent dimensions (e.g., near-zero padding tokens in activations), the second-moment matrix is singular. Adding $\lambda I$ before Cholesky decomposition provides a Tikhonov regularizer that ensures numerical stability without biasing the transform when the matrix is well-conditioned (since $\lambda$ is small).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: A Principled Theory That Derives the Optimal Transform, Rather Than Heuristically Searching for One

Prior to WUSH, the field of LLM quantization had accumulated a catalog of empirically effective transforms—Hadamard rotations (QuaRot), learned orthogonal matrices (SpinQuant, FlatQuant), channel-wise scaling (SmoothQuant, AWQ)—selected through a combination of intuition and trial-and-error optimization. The dominant assumption was that transforms help by making distributions more Gaussian-like or by spreading outlier energy, but there was no rigorous answer to the question: *given a specific quantizer and a statistical model of the data, what transform minimizes the expected output error?* This left the field in an uncomfortable position where the best method (MR-GPTQ with blockwise Hadamard) was known empirically, but no one could explain *why* it worked or whether something strictly better existed.

WUSH changes this by solving the optimization problem in closed form. The paper derives the optimal blockwise linear transform for both floating-point and integer block quantizers under standard RTN AbsMax scaling, starting from first principles: the expected L2 output distortion after joint weight-activation quantization. This is not a learned approximation or a heuristic search—it is the exact minimizer of the expected error under the paper's quantizer models, expressed through matrix decompositions (Cholesky, SVD) that reveal what each component of the transform does.

**What makes this distinctive at the idea level** is the shift from "what transform gives good empirical results?" to "what is the structure of the optimal transform, and how do existing heuristics relate to it?" The answer reveals that the optimal transform has three conceptually distinct components—a whitening step (the data-aware second-moment component), a spectrum-compressing scaling step ($S^{-1/2}$), and a randomization step (the Hadamard matrix)—and that each serves a mathematically precise role. The Hadamard matrix, which prior work used as a generic "outlier spreader," emerges as the unique orthogonal matrix with the equal-projection property ($|e_j^\top H e_k| = d^{-1/2}$) required to make the per-coordinate variances uniform after scaling. This explains *why* Hadamard works: it is the only data-agnostic piece that can saturate the lower bound when combined with the correct data-aware scaling. The $S^{-1/2}$ factor, which has no analog in prior transform-based methods, is what actually reduces the error—the Hadamard alone (or any orthogonal transform alone) achieves nothing under the FP error model (Equation 21), a fact the paper proves explicitly.

The significance of this contribution is primarily **theoretical**: it provides a principled foundation for transform-based quantization that replaces heuristic exploration with an analytic solution. The practical consequence—that WUSH improves accuracy over the state-of-the-art (Table 2, Figures 3, 6–9)—is a validation of the theory, not the primary contribution itself. The theory is also **diagnostic**: it tells us that any transform lacking the $S^{-1/2}$ scaling component is fundamentally limited in how much it can reduce quantization error, and it quantifies this limit (a factor of at most $d$, i.e., 32× in squared error for MXFP4, when one singular value dominates). This is a new kind of understanding that the field did not have before—it transforms transform design from an art into a science.

I classify this as a **fundamental shift**, not an incremental refinement. Prior work on learned rotations (SpinQuant) was refining within the space of orthogonal transforms, which this theory proves is the wrong space—orthogonal transforms alone cannot reduce error under the FP model. WUSH does not tweak an existing heuristic; it derives the transform from the problem statement, and the result is structurally different from what anyone had tried.

---

### Innovation 2: The Insight That FP and INT Quantizers Demand the Same Optimal Transform Structure, Despite Radically Different Error Models

This is a genuinely surprising theoretical result. Floating-point and integer quantization produce error through completely different mechanisms: FP quantization error is multiplicative (proportional to each individual value's magnitude; Equation 18), while INT quantization error is additive per-block and scales with the block's maximum absolute value (Equation 23). A priori, one would expect these distinct error models to favor different transforms—perhaps a whitening transform for FP (to equalize per-coordinate magnitudes) and a range-compressing transform for INT (to minimize the block-max norm). The paper finds otherwise: the same configuration ($U' = H$, $S' = S^{1/2}$, $R = I$) is optimal for FP and near-optimal for INT, with the quality of near-optimality depending on the tail behavior of the data distribution.

**What makes this distinctive** is not the result itself but the **unifying theoretical framework** that produces it. The paper's probabilistic reduction (Section 4.1) shows that both quantizer types, despite their different error mechanisms, lead to optimization problems that factor through the same singular value structure $S$ of the weight-activation cross-moment $W'^\top X'$. In both cases, the key term to minimize involves a product of $\text{tr}(S^2 S'^{-2})$ (which penalizes large inverse transform amplification) and a term capturing the transformed signal's magnitude ($\text{tr}(S'^2)$ or $\mathbb{E}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty^2]$). The Cauchy-Schwarz argument (Equation 29) that drives $S' \propto S^{1/2}$ works identically in both settings, and the Hadamard's equal-projection property saturates the per-coordinate variance equalization needed by both.

Prior work implicitly treated FP and INT quantization as separate problems, with different best practices emerging for each format. The experimental results in Table 1 reflect this fragmentation: under MXFP4, the Hadamard transform alone provides measurable gains (loss drops from 11.1 to 7.24 for the Q projection), but under NVFP4, the Hadamard is actually harmful (loss increases from 4.23 to 5.60 for Q). This suggests the formats behave differently enough that no single heuristic works for both. Yet WUSH improves both—achieving the lowest MXFP4 losses and competitive NVFP4 losses (2.40 vs. 2.26 for WUS, Table 1). The unified theory explains this: the $S^{1/2}$ scaling and the rotation $U^\top$ handle the format-specific challenges, while the Hadamard provides a universal equalization benefit that helps when the format has INT-like behavior (as MXFP4 does in the subnormal regime) but is neutral when the format is purely FP-like.

The significance is both **theoretical** (a single optimization framework subsumes two apparently different problems) and **practical** (the same calibration procedure produces transforms that work across formats without format-specific tuning). It is a **fundamental finding** in the sense that it reveals a structural unity that was not obvious from the error mechanisms alone. However, it is not a complete unification—the INT proof yields only *near*-optimality (with a gap that depends on tail behavior), while the FP proof yields exact optimality—so there remains theoretical work to tighten the INT bounds.

---

### Innovation 3: The Discovery That Orthogonal Transforms Are Fundamentally Incapable of Reducing Quantization Error Under FP Models, Explaining the Empirical Ceiling of Prior Methods

This is the paper's sharpest diagnostic contribution, and it turns a vague empirical observation—"Hadamard helps, but only so much"—into a precise theoretical statement. Equation 21 proves a striking result: under the multiplicative FP quantization error model, *any orthogonal transform* $T$ (including the identity, the Hadamard, a random rotation, or a learned orthogonal matrix) yields the *identical* expected quantization error $\mathbb{E}[\eta^2] \cdot \text{tr}(S^2)$. The error depends only on the spectrum $S$ of the data distribution (through $\text{tr}(S^2)$), not on the choice of orthogonal basis. An orthogonal transform rearranges the basis vectors but preserves the total variance structure; under multiplicative noise proportional to the per-coordinate value, this rearrangement achieves nothing for the overall expected error.

This is not an incremental finding—it is a **negative result with positive implications** that reframes the entire research direction. Before this paper, the dominant approach to transform-based quantization was to search for better orthogonal matrices: SpinQuant learns one via backpropagation, FlatQuant optimizes for "flatness" (equalized per-channel variance), and several works compare different orthogonal families (Hadamard, random, DCT). Equation 21 implies that this entire line of inquiry is operating in a space where improvement is impossible under ideal FP conditions. The *only* way to reduce error is to use a non-orthogonal transform—specifically, one that includes a scaling component ($S' \neq I$) that compresses the singular value spectrum.

This explains the empirical ceiling that Egiazarian et al. (2025) observed: "limited gains achievable with Hadamard alone, linking this to the properties of the underlying weight and activation distributions." WUSH identifies precisely what property limits those gains—the spectrum of $S$—and shows that the limitation is not a shortcoming of the Hadamard specifically but a fundamental property of orthogonal transforms in general. A learned orthogonal matrix cannot outperform a Hadamard under the FP model because *all* orthogonal matrices are equally optimal (or rather, equally suboptimal).

The significance extends beyond this paper. It tells practitioners that if they observe Hadamard-based methods plateauing, they should not invest effort in finding a "better rotation"—they should instead look at non-orthogonal transforms that can reshape the distribution's spectrum. It also explains why channel-wise scaling methods (SmoothQuant, AWQ) sometimes help: they are diagonal non-orthogonal transforms that approximate a crude version of the $S^{-1/2}$ scaling, albeit without the rotation components that handle cross-channel correlations. And it explains a seeming paradox: Hadamard transforms empirically *do* help for MXFP4 (Table 1: loss improves from 11.1 to 7.24 for identity vs. Hadamard), despite the FP theory predicting no improvement. The resolution (discussed in Section 6.1) is that real MXFP4 is not a pure FP quantizer—it has INT-like behavior in the subnormal regime and small exponent ranges where the multiplicative error model breaks down. The Hadamard's benefit comes from its performance under the INT-like component of the error, while the WUSH $S^{-1/2}$ component handles the FP-like component.

I classify this as a **fundamental diagnostic insight**—it does not directly improve accuracy (WUSH does that through the $S^{-1/2}$ component, not through the orthogonal part), but it explains *why* certain approaches succeed or fail and redirects future research toward the productive direction (non-orthogonal, spectrum-aware transforms).

---

### Innovation 4: The Empirical Demonstration That Data-Aware Blockwise Transforms Can Nearly Close the MXFP-NVFP Accuracy Gap, Making MXFP4 a Viable Format for Practical Deployment

This is the applied significance of the theoretical innovations. MXFP4 (the OCP standard with group size 32) has been historically less accurate than NVFP4 (NVIDIA's format with group size 16) for LLM quantization, primarily because the larger group size means a single scale must cover more diverse values, making it more vulnerable to outliers. Prior work accepted this gap as a necessary tradeoff: MXFP4 offers better hardware compatibility (it is an open standard) and potentially higher throughput (fewer scale computations per element), but at an accuracy cost. The MR-GPTQ paper (Egiazarian et al., 2025) pushed MXFP4 performance forward with blockwise Hadamard transforms, but a substantial gap to NVFP4 remained.

WUSH substantially narrows this gap, and in some cases eliminates it. On Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Table 2), the MXFP4 RTN-WUSH combination achieves 73.21 average accuracy vs. 75.28 for NVFP4 RTN-WUSH—a gap of only 2.07 points, compared to a gap of roughly 5.4 points between MXFP4 RTN-H (70.45) and NVFP4 RTN-I (74.73). With GPTQ, MXFP4 GPTQ-WUSH reaches 74.35 average, closing to within 1.75 points of NVFP4 GPTQ-WUSH (76.10). On Qwen3-14B (Table 4), the NVFP4-MXFP4 average gap shrinks to **0.36 points** (79.80 vs. 79.44), with MXFP4 achieving over 98% of BF16 recovery. This is not just an incremental improvement—it changes the practical calculus for format selection. If MXFP4 with WUSH is competitive with NVFP4 in accuracy, then the openness and broader hardware support of MXFP4 become decisive advantages.

**What makes this distinctive** is that the improvement comes from a principled source (the $S^{-1/2}$ scaling that addresses the FP component of the error and the $U^\top$ rotation that handles cross-channel correlations) rather than from task-specific engineering. The gains are consistent across model families (Llama-3, Qwen3), model sizes (3B to 32B), and evaluation benchmarks (LM Evaluation Harness and Platinum Benchmarks), suggesting they arise from a fundamental property of the weight-activation statistics rather than from overfitting to a particular model or dataset.

The significance is primarily **practical**—it makes MXFP4 a genuinely competitive format for production deployment, which has direct implications for the hardware ecosystem (broader adoption of the OCP standard) and for practitioners who can now achieve near-NVFP4 accuracy with an open format. The theoretical significance is secondary: it validates that the FP error model (which predicts no benefit from orthogonal transforms) is a useful approximation but not a complete description of real MXFP4 behavior, and that WUSH's components correctly handle the hybrid FP/INT nature of the format.

I classify this as an **important empirical finding enabled by a theoretical advance**, rather than a fundamental theoretical contribution in itself. The accuracy gains are the downstream consequence of Innovations 1–3; without the theory, one might have stumbled upon a similar transform through extensive hyperparameter search, but the theory makes it systematic and explains *why* it works across formats and models.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary evaluation uses the **LM Evaluation Harness** (Gao et al., 2021) with four tasks: MMLU-CoT (for Llama models; standard MMLU for Qwen), GSM8K, HellaSwag, and WinoGrande. A complementary evaluation uses the **Platinum Benchmarks** (Vendrow et al., 2025), covering 13 tasks spanning math reasoning (SingleOp, SingleQ, MultiArith, SVAMP, GSM8K, MMLU-Math), logical reasoning (BBH Deduction, BBH Counting, BBH Navigate), and reading comprehension (HotpotQA, SQuAD, DROP, Winograd-WSC). All evaluations use "emulated ('fake') quantization," where quantized values are stored in BF16/FP16 but clipped and rounded to match the target format's representable grid, and results are aggregated over multiple random seeds (Appendix B).

- **Base model(s).** Experiments span five models across two families: **Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct**, **Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct**, **Qwen3-8B**, **Qwen3-14B**, and **Qwen3-32B**. The Llama models are chosen to enable direct comparison with the MR-GPTQ baseline (Egiazarian et al., 2025), while the Qwen3 family extends validation to a different architecture and a wider size range. For Qwen models, thinking mode is disabled throughout.

- **Metrics.** Accuracy is measured as **exact-match percentage** on each benchmark task, averaged across tasks to produce an "Average" column in the main result tables (Tables 2 and 4). Recovery rate is computed as the ratio of quantized average accuracy to BF16 average accuracy, expressed as a percentage. For per-layer analysis (Section 6.1), the metric is **normalized L2 quantization loss**: $d_{\text{out}}^{-1} d_{\text{batch}}^{-1} \| q(T_W W)^\top q(T_X X) - W^\top X \|_F^2$, computed on a single transformer block using 32 calibration samples of sequence length 2048 from FineWeb-Edu (Penedo et al., 2024). KL-divergence on a C4 dataset slice is also reported for Qwen3-8B (Table 3).

- **Baselines.** Five methods are compared:
  - **Identity (I)**: no transform applied before quantization—RTN or GPTQ operates directly on the original weights and activations.
  - **Random rotation (R)**: a random orthogonal matrix, averaged over 10 independent runs to account for variance.
  - **Hadamard (H)**: the standard normalized Hadamard matrix $H \in \{\pm d^{-1/2}\}^{d \times d}$, applied blockwise, corresponding to the MR-GPTQ baseline (Egiazarian et al., 2025) when combined with GPTQ.
  - **SmoothQuant** (Xiao et al., 2023) and **QuaRot** (Ashkboos et al., 2024b) and **SpinQuant** (Liu et al., 2025): included in the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct comparison (Table 2) as representative prior transform-based methods.
  - **WUS**: WUSH without the Hadamard component—i.e., $U' = I$—which isolates the effect of the spectrum-aware scaling $S^{1/2}$ and the data-dependent rotations $U^\top$, $V^\top$ from the Hadamard randomization.
  - **WUSH**: the full construction from Equation (8).

  All methods are evaluated with both RTN (round-to-nearest) and GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) weight quantization, as indicated by suffixes (e.g., RTN-I, GPTQ-H, RTN-WUSH).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For the per-layer loss experiments (Table 1), the compute budget is implicitly the number of calibration samples (32) and the quantization group size ($d = 32$ for MXFP4 and INT4, $d = 16$ for NVFP4). For the GPU kernel benchmarks (Figure 2), throughput is measured as per-layer GEMM speedup relative to BF16 at batch size $M = 1024$ on an RTX 5090, with the theoretical maximum speedup being $8\times$ for FP4 over BF16.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ explicit cross-validation for accuracy experiments; instead, it follows the standard GPTQ calibration pipeline: layers are processed sequentially, and after each layer is quantized, the calibration activations are propagated through the quantized layer to provide inputs for the next layer (Section 3, Algorithm 1 description). For the random rotation baseline, 10 independent runs are averaged to report mean performance. For the Platinum Benchmarks results (Figures 3, 6–9), standard deviations across multiple random seeds are shown as error bars on the average accuracy plot.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Per-Layer Quantization Loss (Table 1 and Section 6.1)

The per-layer analysis evaluates the 18th transformer block of Qwen3-8B, examining all seven linear projection layers (attention: Q, K, V, O; MLP: Gate, Up, Down) under MXFP4, NVFP4, and INT4 formats with RTN quantization. The key findings are:

**WUSH consistently achieves the lowest loss for MXFP4 and INT4 across all layer types.** For MXFP4 on the Q projection, WUSH reduces the loss to $3.34 \times 10^{-3}$ compared to $7.24 \times 10^{-3}$ for Hadamard alone and $11.1 \times 10^{-3}$ for identity—a **3.3× reduction over identity** and a **2.2× reduction over Hadamard**. This pattern holds across all seven projections: WUSH losses range from 2.76 to 4.49 ($\times 10^{-3}$), while Hadamard losses range from 3.79 to 8.60. For INT4 on the K projection, the improvement is even more dramatic: WUSH achieves $2.43 \times 10^{-3}$ versus $5.55 \times 10^{-3}$ for Hadamard and $123 \times 10^{-3}$ for identity, though the identity baseline is anomalously high due to INT4's extreme sensitivity to unmitigated outliers.

**For NVFP4, WUSH and WUS are nearly tied, both outperforming identity and Hadamard.** On the Q projection, WUSH achieves $2.40 \times 10^{-3}$ and WUS achieves $2.26 \times 10^{-3}$, compared to $4.23 \times 10^{-3}$ for identity and $5.60 \times 10^{-3}$ for Hadamard. Critically, the Hadamard alone is *harmful* for NVFP4: its loss exceeds identity on every projection (e.g., 5.60 vs. 4.23 on Q, 6.70 vs. 4.37 on V). The paper attributes this to NVFP4's "top-element preservation effect" (Section 6.1), which causes orthogonal rotations to increase rather than decrease the effective quantization range. WUSH overcomes this through its non-orthogonal $S^{1/2}$ scaling component.

**The Hadamard alone provides measurable gains for MXFP4 but not for NVFP4**, which the paper explains as evidence that MXFP4 behaves as a hybrid between ideal FP and INT quantization. Under the pure FP multiplicative error model, orthogonal transforms are predicted to achieve nothing (Equation 21); the fact that Hadamard helps for MXFP4 (reducing loss from 11.1 to 7.24 on Q) indicates that MXFP4 has substantial INT-like behavior—likely in the subnormal regime and small exponent ranges where the effective step size becomes uniform. NVFP4's different scale encoding (including top-element preservation) makes its behavior closer to the pure FP regime where orthogonal transforms have no benefit (or even harm, due to secondary effects not captured by the simplified error model).

**WUS (WUSH without Hadamard) sometimes outperforms full WUSH for NVFP4**, particularly on Q (2.26 vs. 2.40) and V (2.30 vs. 2.28), but these differences are small (below $0.2 \times 10^{-3}$) and likely within noise. For MXFP4 and INT4, WUSH consistently outperforms WUS, confirming the Hadamard component's value for formats with INT-like quantization behavior.

#### End-to-End LLM Accuracy: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Table 2)

Table 2 reports W4A4 accuracy on four LM Evaluation Harness tasks (MMLU-CoT, GSM8K, HellaSwag, WinoGrande) for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct under both NVFP4 and MXFP4 formats, with RTN and GPTQ quantization variants. The baseline BF16 model achieves 78.93 average accuracy.

**MXFP4 RTN-WUSH delivers the headline +2.8 average point gain over Hadamard.** Specifically, MXFP4 RTN-WUSH achieves 73.21 average accuracy versus 70.45 for MXFP4 RTN-H (the MR-GPTQ baseline)—a gain of **+2.76 points**. Breaking this down by task: MMLU-CoT improves from 62.38 to 66.85 (+4.47), GSM8K from 72.48 to 75.16 (+2.68), HellaSwag from 75.29 to 77.28 (+1.99), and WinoGrande from 71.67 to 73.56 (+1.89). The gains are broad rather than concentrated on a single task.

**MXFP4 GPTQ-WUSH achieves 74.35 average, representing 94.20% recovery of BF16 accuracy.** This is the best MXFP4 result in the table, surpassing GPTQ-H (73.65, 93.31% recovery) by **+0.70 points**. Compared to GPTQ-I (70.62, 89.47% recovery), the improvement is **+3.73 points**—demonstrating that WUSH compounds effectively with GPTQ's error compensation, not just with RTN. The GPTQ-WUSH combination reaches within 1.75 points of NVFP4 GPTQ-WUSH (76.10) and within 4.58 points of BF16.

Under WUSH, the gap shrinks to 75.28 − 73.21 = **2.07 points**. Under GPTQ, it shrinks further to 76.10 − 74.35 = **1.75 points**. While NVFP4 retains an advantage (its smaller group size of 16 gives inherently finer granularity), WUSH makes MXFP4 genuinely competitive rather than clearly inferior.

#### GPU Kernel Performance (Figure 2)

**WUSH + Quant matches H + Quant throughput within 1.3% on average across all tested configurations.** Figure 2 reports per-layer MXFP4 GEMM speedups relative to BF16 on an RTX 5090 at batch size 1024 for three models and three representative layer shapes (small, medium, and large in terms of input/output dimensions). Across the nine data points (3 models × 3 shapes), the WUSH + Quant bars consistently track the H + Quant bars, with the paper reporting an "average throughput difference of only 1.3%." In several configurations—notably the larger layer shapes (N=24576, K=4096 for Qwen3-8B; N=57344, K=8192 for Llama-3.1-70B)—the two bars are visually identical, indicating zero measurable overhead.

**Absolute speedups over BF16 reach up to 6.63×**, with the exact maximum depending on layer shape. The largest speedups occur for the largest output dimensions (N), where the matrix multiplication dominates the runtime and the quantization overhead (reading/writing reduced-precision data) is amortized over more computation. The gap between the None baseline (no transform, no quantization—pure BF16 read/write) and the quantized kernels indicates the irreducible cost of the quantization step itself (reading and writing the activation matrix with FP4 precision and applying scales). The paper notes that this gap, not the transform choice, is the primary performance bottleneck.

**The near-zero overhead is a consequence of the kernel design**, which maps the per-block WUSH transforms to a GEMM-equivalent computation where each thread block loads exactly one $G \times G$ matrix (matching the memory access pattern of the fixed Hadamard kernel). The only difference—that different thread blocks load different matrices from different positions in the $(G, G, C)$ storage tensor—is absorbed by coalesced memory access and L1/L2 cache reuse.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**WUS vs. WUSH (Table 1):** Removing the Hadamard component (WUS = WUSH without $H$) reveals that $H$ is essential for MXFP4 and INT4 but sometimes detrimental for NVFP4. For MXFP4 Q projection, WUS achieves $6.27 \times 10^{-3}$ versus WUSH at $3.34 \times 10^{-3}$—the Hadamard contributes a 1.9× reduction on top of the data-aware components. For NVFP4 Q, WUS achieves $2.26 \times 10^{-3}$ versus WUSH at $2.40 \times 10^{-3}$—WUS is slightly better, confirming the theoretical prediction that the Hadamard provides no benefit (and can slightly harm) under near-ideal FP quantization. For INT4, the Hadamard is critical: WUS achieves $142 \times 10^{-3}$ on the K projection (catastrophic failure) versus WUSH at $2.43 \times 10^{-3}$, indicating that without the Hadamard's equal-projection property, the scaled distribution still concentrates error in a way that the INT block-max quantizer cannot handle. Note that the INT4 WUS failure is inconsistent across projections (it only fails catastrophically on Q, K, and Up, while performing reasonably on V, O, Gate, and Down), suggesting that the failure mode depends on the specific weight-activation covariance structure of each projection type.

**Random rotation vs. Hadamard vs. identity (Table 1):** The random rotation baseline (averaged over 10 runs) consistently falls between identity and Hadamard for MXFP4 and INT4, confirming that the Hadamard's specific structure (equal-projection property) outperforms a generic orthogonal matrix. For MXFP4 Q, random rotation achieves $7.61 \times 10^{-3}$ (between identity at 11.1 and Hadamard at 7.24), suggesting that any "spreading" of outliers helps, but the Hadamard's uniformity is better. For NVFP4, random rotation is harmful ($4.98 \times 10^{-3}$ vs. identity at $4.23 \times 10^{-3}$ for Q), and Hadamard is even more harmful ($5.60 \times 10^{-3}$), consistent with the top-element preservation effect that punishes any rotation.

**GPTQ integration vs. RTN (Table 2, all model results):** Across all models and formats, GPTQ-WUSH consistently outperforms RTN-WUSH. On Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct MXFP4, the gap is 74.35 vs. 73.21 (+1.14 points). On Qwen3-8B MXFP4, it is 75.69 vs. 74.91 (+0.78 points). On Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct NVFP4, it is 67.87 vs. 67.34 (+0.53 points). The diminishing gap at smaller model sizes (3B) suggests that GPTQ's error propagation provides less marginal benefit when the weight matrix is smaller (fewer columns over which to propagate errors), which is consistent with GPTQ's design.

**KL-divergence (Table 3):** On Qwen3-8B measured on a C4 dataset slice with calibration performed on FineWeb, the distributional metric confirms WUSH's advantage. For NVFP4, GPTQ-WUSH achieves KL-divergence of 0.054646 versus 0.069518 for GPTQ-H, a 21.4% relative reduction. For MXFP4, GPTQ-WUSH achieves 0.077909 versus 0.093119 for GPTQ-H, a 16.3% relative reduction. The RTN results show a smaller gap: MXFP4 RTN-WUSH at 0.093739 versus GPTQ-H at 0.093119 (WUSH is slightly worse under RTN for this metric), though the paper notes the metric is aggregated over multiple random seeds, suggesting this may be within noise. Importantly, the calibration dataset (FineWeb) differs from the evaluation dataset (C4), providing a mild distribution-shift robustness check.

**Platinum Benchmarks detailed task analysis (Figures 3, 6–9):** The individual task results reveal that WUSH's gains are broad but not uniform. On Qwen3-8B (Figure 3), MXFP4-RTN-WUSH matches or exceeds MXFP4-RTN-H on 12 of 13 tasks, with the largest absolute gains on BBH Counting (89.34 vs. 85.68) and DROP (84.57 vs. 80.00). The one exception is SingleOp, where MXFP4-RTN-H achieves 100.0 versus WUSH at 99.6—a negligible difference. On Qwen3-14B (Figure 6), the per-task pattern is consistent: WUSH leads on math tasks (GSM8K: 97.69 vs. 97.20 for H) and reading comprehension (SQuAD: 87.95 vs. 87.45 for H, DROP: 88.33 vs. 87.46 for H), while the logical reasoning tasks show smaller differences. On Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (Figure 8), the task-level gains are largest on GSM8K (82.61 vs. 76.49 for MXFP4-RTN-H, a +6.12 point gain) and MMLU-Math (56.25 vs. 51.61 for MXFP4-GPTQ-H, a +4.64 point gain), with smaller or negligible gains on Winograd-WSC and certain BBH tasks.

**NVFP4 vs. MXFP4 format differences (Table 1, cross-format comparison):** The identity baseline reveals the intrinsic format gap: NVFP4-RTN-I losses are uniformly lower than MXFP4-RTN-I (e.g., Q: 4.23 vs. 11.1; K: 4.35 vs. 12.0; V: 4.37 vs. 10.7), consistent with NVFP4's smaller group size (16 vs. 32) and better outlier handling. Under WUSH, this gap narrows substantially: Q (2.40 vs. 3.34), K (2.44 vs. 3.34), V (2.28 vs. 3.30). For the MLP projections (G, U, D), WUSH brings MXFP4 losses to within 1–2 × 10⁻³ of NVFP4 losses, demonstrating that the format disadvantage can be largely overcome through optimal transform design.

**Multiple model scales (Table 4, Llama-3.2-3B through Qwen3-32B):** The consistent improvement from WUSH across model sizes (3B, 8B, 14B, 32B) provides evidence against overfitting to a particular scale. However, the magnitude of improvement varies non-monotonically: the largest relative gains are at 3B (where outliers are most extreme per-parameter), and the gains at 32B are smaller and sometimes reversed (e.g., MXFP4 RTN-H vs. RTN-WUSH on Qwen3-32B). This suggests that as model scale increases, the weight and activation distributions become more naturally isotropic (more parameters share the representational burden), reducing the potential for spectrum-aware scaling to provide large benefits.

### Critical Assessment

#### Do the experiments support the central claim that WUSH is "provably near-optimal"?

The theoretical proofs in Section 4 establish optimality under specific modeling assumptions: (a) the columns of $W$ and $X$ are i.i.d. samples from multivariate distributions, (b) the quantizer is unbiased, (c) the FP/INT error models (multiplicative noise proportional to value, additive noise proportional to block max) accurately describe the quantizer's behavior, and (d) the cross-term between weight and activation quantization errors is negligible. The per-layer experiments (Table 1) provide **indirect validation** of these assumptions by showing that the theoretically derived transform (WUSH) produces lower losses than alternatives (identity, random rotation, Hadamard, WUS) across all formats and layer types. However, the experiments do not **directly test** the assumptions themselves—there is no measurement of quantizer bias, no verification that the multiplicative noise model fits real MXFP4 quantization error, and no check of whether the i.i.d. column assumption holds for real weight matrices (where adjacent columns are correlated due to training dynamics).

The gap between "optimal under the model" and "optimal in reality" is apparent in the NVFP4 results, where WUS (without Hadamard) slightly outperforms WUSH on several projections (Table 1: Q at 2.26 vs. 2.40, V at 2.30 vs. 2.28). The theory predicts WUSH should be strictly optimal; the empirical inversion suggests that the FP error model's approximation of NVFP4 behavior is imperfect enough to change the optimal configuration at the margin. This is a minor discrepancy but indicates that the "provably near-optimal" claim is conditional on model accuracy.

#### Do the experiments support the claimed +2.8 average point gain on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct?

**Yes, with the specific qualification that this is MXFP4 RTN.** Table 2 shows MXFP4 RTN-WUSH at 73.21 versus MXFP4 RTN-H at 70.45, a difference of +2.76 points (the abstract rounds to +2.8). This figure is accurately reported. However, the gain shrinks to +0.70 points for GPTQ (74.35 vs. 73.65), and to smaller margins for NVFP4 (RTN: 75.28 vs. 74.05, +1.23; GPTQ: 76.10 vs. 75.84, +0.26). The headline +2.8 number should be understood as the **maximum** gain in the **most favorable configuration** (MXFP4, RTN, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), not as a typical gain. On Qwen3-8B Platinum Benchmarks, the MXFP4 RTN gain is approximately +1.3 points (Figure 3), and on Qwen3-8B LM Evaluation Harness, it is +1.49 points (Table 4). On Qwen3-14B, it is +2.31 points (79.44 vs. 77.13). The gains are real and consistent, but variable across models and formats, with the largest improvements occurring where the baseline (Hadamard-only) performs worst.

#### Do the experiments support the claim that WUSH "nearly closes the gap between NVFP and MXFP formats"?

**Partially.** On Qwen3-14B (Table 4), the NVFP4-MXFP4 average gap under RTN-WUSH is indeed very small: 79.80 vs. 79.44, a gap of only 0.36 points, with MXFP4 achieving 98.30% recovery versus NVFP4's 98.76%. This is a genuinely impressive result that supports "nearly closes." On Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Table 2), the gap is larger: RTN-WUSH gives 75.28 (NVFP4) vs. 73.21 (MXFP4), a gap of 2.07 points. On Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, it is 67.34 vs. 66.43, a gap of 0.91 points. So the degree of closure depends on the model: nearly closed for Qwen3-14B, substantially narrowed but still present for Llama-3.1-8B, and moderately narrowed for Llama-3.2-3B. The claim should be interpreted as "substantially narrows" rather than universally "nearly closes."

#### What experiments are missing that would strengthen the paper?

**1. Ablation of the $S^{1/2}$ scaling exponent.** The theory predicts $S' \propto S^{1/2}$ is optimal. An experiment varying the exponent (e.g., $S^p$ for $p \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1\}$) would directly test whether $p = 0.5$ is empirically optimal or whether real quantization behavior deviates from the model in a way that favors a different exponent. This would be particularly informative for MXFP4, which the paper argues has hybrid FP/INT behavior.

**2. Comparison with learned transforms (SpinQuant, FlatQuant) on more than one model.** Table 2 includes SpinQuant and QuaRot for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct but not for the Qwen models or the 3B/14B/32B scales. A broader comparison would clarify whether WUSH's closed-form solution consistently outperforms gradient-based learned transforms, or whether learned transforms catch up at larger scales or on specific architectures.

**3. Measurement of the difficulty estimation / calibration data sensitivity.** The WUSH transform depends on calibration activations $X$. How many calibration samples are needed? Table 1 uses 32 samples of sequence length 2048; would 8 samples or 128 samples change the results significantly? The paper does not ablate calibration data quantity, which is relevant for practical deployment where calibration data may be limited or out-of-distribution relative to inference data.

**4. Direct measurement of the cross-term approximation error.** The theoretical derivation drops the $\varepsilon(T_W \mathbf{w})^\top \varepsilon(T_X \mathbf{x})$ cross-term as a first-order approximation (Equation 37). An experiment measuring the magnitude of this cross-term for real quantized models would validate or challenge this approximation. If the cross-term is non-negligible for certain formats or layer types, the optimal transform might differ from the WUSH construction.

**5. Latency and memory overhead measurements for the full inference pipeline.** Figure 2 reports per-layer GEMM speedups, but end-to-end inference involves many layers plus attention, normalization, and residual connections. An end-to-end latency measurement comparing BF16, H + Quant, and WUSH + Quant on a complete model would provide a more realistic picture of the practical speedup. The paper's claim of "up to 6.6× per-layer throughput" does not directly translate to end-to-end speedup, since non-linear layers are not accelerated.

#### What are the genuine weaknesses?

**Single GPU architecture (RTX 5090) for kernel benchmarks.** The kernel performance results (Figure 2) are reported only on an RTX 5090. Different GPU architectures have different cache hierarchies, memory bandwidth, and tensor core capabilities; the near-zero overhead of WUSH over Hadamard may not generalize to older architectures with smaller caches or to datacenter GPUs with different SM configurations.

**Calibration distribution mismatch acknowledged but not explored.** The paper uses FineWeb-Edu for calibration and C4 for KL-divergence evaluation (Table 3), which is a positive robustness check. However, all accuracy evaluations use in-distribution benchmarks—there is no evaluation on out-of-distribution data (e.g., domain-specific text, code, or multilingual inputs) that might have different activation statistics than the calibration data. This is a common limitation in PTQ papers but is worth noting given WUSH's stronger dependence on calibration statistics (through the full SVD of $W'^\top X'$) compared to data-agnostic transforms.

**The paper does not ablate the choice of block size $d$ relative to quantization group size.** All experiments use the natural alignment ($d = 32$ for MXFP4, $d = 16$ for NVFP4). Would a larger block size for the transform (e.g., $d = 64$ with a group size of 32) provide additional gains by capturing longer-range correlations? The theory assumes $d$ equals the quantization group size, but this is a design choice, not a theoretical necessity—and exploring it could reveal accuracy-latency tradeoffs.

**The theoretical optimality proof has a gap between upper and lower bounds for INT under general distributions (factor of $d$).** The paper is explicit about this (Equation 30: the gap is $d$ for general distributions, $o(d)$ for tail-bounded). However, no experiment characterizes whether real LLM activation distributions are "tail-bounded" in the relevant sense, so it is unclear whether the tighter $o(d)$ bound or the looser $d$ bound applies in practice. This matters for interpreting the claim "near-optimal": near-optimal up to $o(d)$ is very tight; near-optimal up to $d$ (a factor of 32) is much looser.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Unaccounted Cost of Calibration-Dependent Transform Computation

**Assumption or constraint.** WUSH constructs per-block transforms from the second-moment statistics of weight matrices and a batch of calibration activations, via Cholesky decomposition and SVD of the cross-moment matrix $W'^\top X'$ (Algorithm 1, Lines 1–7). This is fundamentally more expensive than data-agnostic transforms like the Hadamard, which is a fixed matrix that can be hard-coded. The per-layer calibration cost includes: computing the full $d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{in}}$ Hessian matrix $d_{\text{batch}}^{-1} X X^\top$, performing its Cholesky decomposition (an $O(d_{\text{in}}^3)$ operation), and computing $d_{\text{in}}/d$ SVDs of $d \times d$ submatrices—one per quantization block—during the GPTQ backward pass. For a large model like Llama-3.1-70B with $d_{\text{in}}$ up to 8192 in some layers, the Cholesky decomposition alone can be a non-trivial fraction of the calibration time.

The paper does not report calibration time or compare it to the cost of computing fixed transforms (Hadamard) or learned transforms (SpinQuant). This is acknowledged only indirectly: Algorithm 1 describes the procedure but does not characterize its asymptotic or wall-clock cost.

**Consequence.** For one-off quantization jobs (quantize a model once and deploy it many times), calibration cost may be acceptable since it is amortized over inference. But for use cases where models are frequently fine-tuned and re-quantized, or where calibration data is limited and expensive to collect (privacy-sensitive domains, small datasets), the additional calibration overhead could be a meaningful fraction of the total quantization pipeline cost. Furthermore, the dependence on the specific calibration batch raises the question of how much data is needed to reliably estimate the second-moment statistics—the paper uses 32 samples of sequence length 2048 for per-layer analysis (Section 6.1), giving $32 \times 2048 = 65536$ tokens of calibration data, but does not ablate this quantity.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 5 reports only inference-time GPU kernel throughput (Figure 2), showing that WUSH's *online* transform application matches Hadamard kernels within 1.3% overhead. The calibration-time cost is not measured or reported anywhere. Section 3.2 acknowledges that GPTQ calibration processes layers sequentially, but the computational cost of the additional matrix decompositions (Cholesky, SVD) relative to standard GPTQ (which already computes the Hessian and its Cholesky factor) is not quantified.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper frames WUSH as a "post-training quantization" method (Section 1) and treats calibration as a one-time offline cost. For the specific use case it targets (quantize once, deploy many times on compatible hardware), this is a reasonable trade-off. However, the absence of calibration-time measurements means practitioners cannot evaluate the cost-benefit ratio of WUSH relative to simpler transforms (Hadamard) or to learned transforms that might require more calibration iterations but similar total compute. The paper does not suggest future work on reducing calibration cost.

### Hard Problems (Out-of-Distribution or Structurally Different Activations) Are Not Evaluated

**Assumption or constraint.** WUSH computes its transforms from the second-moment statistics of the calibration activations. The implicit assumption is that future inference activations share the same covariance structure—i.e., the calibration data is representative of the deployment distribution. This is the standard assumption in calibration-based PTQ methods (GPTQ, SmoothQuant, etc.), but it is a stronger assumption for WUSH than for data-agnostic transforms because WUSH uses the *full eigenvalue and eigenvector structure* of the cross-moment matrix $W'^\top X'$, not just a summary statistic like per-channel variance.

If the deployment distribution differs from the calibration distribution (e.g., the calibration data is generic web text but the model is deployed for code generation, or for a specific domain like medical or legal text), the SVD computed from calibration data may not align with the singular vectors needed to minimize error on the deployment activations. The paper's theory (Section 4) explicitly optimizes the expected loss over the calibration distribution $\mathcal{D}_x$; if deployment activations come from a different distribution, the transform is no longer guaranteed to be optimal—or even an improvement over data-agnostic transforms.

**Consequence.** This is a different kind of "hard problem" than the difficulty-tier analysis typical in LLM evaluation (where "hard" means problems the model cannot solve). Here, "hard" means activation patterns that are poorly represented in calibration data. For such inputs, WUSH could perform *worse* than a data-agnostic Hadamard transform, because the per-block transforms are specifically tuned to the calibration statistics and may overfit to them—amplifying errors in directions that are unimportant in calibration but important in deployment.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 reports KL-divergence on C4 after calibrating on FineWeb—a mild distribution shift between two web-text datasets. Under MXFP4 GPTQ, WUSH achieves lower KL-divergence than GPTQ-H (0.077909 vs. 0.093119), suggesting it generalizes across this mild shift. However, C4 and FineWeb are both English web-text corpora; their activation distributions are likely highly similar. The Platinum Benchmarks (Figures 3, 6–9) include math and reading comprehension tasks that differ stylistically from the FineWeb-Edu calibration data, and WUSH maintains its advantage—but these are still English-language, largely factual tasks. There is no evaluation on code, multilingual text, structured data inputs, or domain-specific language (legal, medical, scientific) where activation patterns could be substantially different.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges the calibration paradigm (Section 3, Algorithm 1 description) but does not discuss distribution shift robustness. Future work could characterize the sensitivity of WUSH's accuracy to calibration-data mismatch, or explore whether mixing diverse data sources in the calibration set provides robustness to deployment shifts. Adaptive schemes that update the transform based on online activation statistics (without full calibration) are another unexplored direction.

### The Calibration-Time Computational Cost of WUSH Is Not Reported

**Assumption or constraint.** WUSH constructs per-block transforms from the second-moment statistics of weight matrices and a batch of calibration activations, requiring a Cholesky decomposition of the full $d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{in}}$ activation Hessian and an SVD of each $d \times d$ block's cross-moment matrix. Algorithm 1 (Lines 1–7) performs these decompositions for every linear layer during calibration. For a large model with layers where $d_{\text{in}}$ reaches 8192 (Llama-3.1-70B attention projections), the Cholesky decomposition is $O(d_{\text{in}}^3)$, and the per-block SVDs add $\frac{d_{\text{in}}}{d} \times O(d^3) = O(d_{\text{in}} d^2)$ operations. While these are one-time offline costs, they can be non-trivial relative to standard GPTQ calibration, which already computes the Hessian and its Cholesky factor but does not perform additional SVDs.

The paper does not report calibration wall-clock time, peak memory usage during calibration, or the number of floating-point operations required versus standard GPTQ calibration. This is acknowledged indirectly: Algorithm 1 describes the procedure but does not characterize its complexity.

**Consequence.** For practitioners quantizing multiple large models (e.g., experimenting with different quantization bitwidths, formats, or models), the calibration overhead may be a meaningful fraction of the total pipeline time. Additionally, the SVD in Line 6 of Algorithm 1 operates on the *residual* target $Y$ that changes as blocks are processed in reverse order (Line 12/15), meaning the SVD must be recomputed sequentially per block—it cannot be parallelized across blocks. For models with many layers (80 layers in Llama-3.1-70B) and large $d_{\text{in}}$, this sequential dependency could dominate calibration time.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 5's Figure 2 reports only inference-time GPU kernel throughput, showing that WUSH's online transform matches Hadamard kernels within 1.3% overhead. The calibration-time cost is not measured or discussed anywhere in the paper. The per-layer loss experiments (Table 1) process a single transformer block with 32 calibration samples of sequence length 2048; no total calibration-time metric across all layers is provided.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper frames WUSH as a PTQ method where calibration is a one-time offline cost (Section 1: "calibration-based"), and for the use case of quantizing a model once and deploying many times, this is a reasonable trade-off. However, without calibration-time measurements, practitioners cannot assess whether the additional decomposition cost is negligible compared to GPTQ's existing Hessian computation, or whether it introduces a bottleneck for large models. The paper does not suggest future work on reducing calibration overhead, though algorithmic simplifications (e.g., approximating the SVD with power iteration, or using randomized SVD for the blockwise decompositions) are natural next steps.

### Distribution Shift Between Calibration and Deployment Activations Is Not Characterized

**Assumption or constraint.** WUSH's transforms depend on the singular value decomposition of $W'^\top X'$, where $X'$ is the Cholesky factor of the calibration activation covariance $d_{\text{batch}}^{-1} X X^\top$ (Algorithm 1, Line 2). The optimality guarantees in Section 4 assume that future inference activations are drawn from the same distribution $\mathcal{D}_x$ as the calibration data. This is a stronger assumption than that made by data-agnostic transforms (Hadamard), which require no calibration, or by channel-wise scaling methods (SmoothQuant), which use only per-channel variance and are therefore less sensitive to the full covariance structure.

If the deployment distribution differs from the calibration distribution—e.g., calibration on web text but deployment on code, multilingual data, or domain-specific documents—the SVD computed from calibration data reflects the wrong principal directions and the transform is no longer guaranteed to be optimal. It could theoretically perform *worse* than a data-agnostic transform that makes no assumptions about activation statistics.

**Consequence.** This is a practical robustness concern. In production deployments, the exact inference distribution is often unknown at calibration time, or it may drift over time. A quantization method with strong calibration dependence could satisfy benchmarks (where calibration and evaluation data are drawn from similar sources) but degrade in the field. The consequence is not theoretical—it is a failure mode that a practitioner would discover only after deployment.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 provides a mild robustness check: KL-divergence on a C4 dataset slice after calibrating on FineWeb-Edu, a different web-text corpus. MXFP4 GPTQ-WUSH achieves lower KL-divergence than GPTQ-H (0.077909 vs. 0.093119), suggesting the transform generalizes across this English web-text distribution shift. The Platinum Benchmarks (Figures 3, 6–9) include math, reasoning, and reading comprehension tasks that differ stylistically from FineWeb-Edu, and WUSH maintains its advantage in all cases. However, these are still English-language, factual tasks broadly similar in domain to standard web text. There is no evaluation on substantially out-of-distribution inputs: code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), multilingual text, structured data (tables, JSON), or domain-specific language (legal, medical, scientific).

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through the FineWeb-to-C4 generalization check (Table 3), but only for KL-divergence on a single model (Qwen3-8B), and only for a mild shift within English web text. The paper does not discuss the calibration-dependence limitation, does not ablate the quantity or diversity of calibration data needed for robustness, and does not propose mechanisms for handling distribution shift (e.g., mixing diverse calibration sources, online adaptation, or fallback to data-agnostic transforms when shift is detected). The strong dependence on calibration statistics is an inherent trade-off in the method—it is what enables the data-aware optimality—but the paper does not characterize its boundaries.

### Single Hardware Architecture for GPU Kernel Benchmarks

**Assumption or constraint.** The per-layer GEMM speedup results in Figure 2 are measured exclusively on an NVIDIA RTX 5090, a consumer-grade GPU with a specific cache hierarchy, memory bandwidth, and tensor core generation (Blackwell architecture). The kernel's design—mapping per-block WUSH transforms to a GEMM-equivalent computation where each thread block loads one $G \times G$ matrix and relies on L1/L2 caches to reuse matrices across tiles of the batch dimension—may not perform equally well on different GPU architectures. Older architectures with smaller caches (e.g., RTX 3090, A100) may see higher overhead from the per-block matrix loads, since the effective cache reuse assumed by the kernel design could degrade if $C = d_{\text{in}}/d$ transform matrices exceed cache capacity. Datacenter GPUs with different SM configurations (H100, B200) may have different compute-to-bandwidth ratios that change the memory-bound vs. compute-bound regime.

**Consequence.** The paper's headline claim of "up to 6.6× per-layer throughput over BF16" and "only 1.3% average overhead relative to Hadamard kernels" may not generalize to the GPU hardware most commonly used for LLM inference in production. Datacenter deployments typically use H100 or A100 GPUs, not consumer RTX cards. If the WUSH kernel's overhead is larger on these platforms—e.g., if the L2 cache on H100 is insufficient to hold all $C$ transform matrices for large layers, causing repeated DRAM reads—the practical speedup could be lower and the overhead relative to Hadamard could be higher than 1.3%.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 2 reports results for three models (Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, Llama-3.1-70B) and three representative per-layer shapes on an RTX 5090. The paper does not benchmark on any other GPU architecture. The kernel design description (Section 5) explains the CUTLASS GEMM template mapping but does not discuss architecture-specific considerations like cache sizing, warp scheduling, or tensor core instruction throughput for different FP4 formats.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not claim generality across hardware (the figure caption specifies "RTX 5090"), but it also does not discuss the architectural assumptions implicit in the kernel design. For practitioners considering deployment, this is a significant gap: the RTX 5090 is a newly released consumer GPU that is not representative of the datacenter hardware where large-scale LLM inference typically runs. Future work should benchmark on H100/B200 for datacenter relevance and on A100 for broader compatibility, and should report whether the near-zero overhead holds across architectures.

### The Hadamard Component Can Be Quantifiably Harmful for NVFP4, and the Theory Does Not Predict When

**Assumption or constraint.** The theoretical derivation for FP quantizers (Section 4.2) proves that any orthogonal transform (including the Hadamard) yields identical expected error as the identity transform (Equation 21). Under the multiplicative FP error model, the Hadamard is neutral—it neither helps nor hurts. However, the NVFP4 per-layer loss results in Table 1 show the Hadamard transform alone *increases* quantization loss compared to the identity across all projection types: for the Q projection, NVFP4 loss with Hadamard is $5.60 \times 10^{-3}$ versus $4.23 \times 10^{-3}$ for identity (a 32% increase); for the V projection, $6.70 \times 10^{-3}$ versus $4.37 \times 10^{-3}$ (a 53% increase). The paper attributes this to NVFP4's "top-element preservation effect" (Section 6.1), but this effect is not modeled in the theory—the theoretical FP error model predicts zero impact from any orthogonal transform, not harm.

WUSH incorporates the Hadamard as a fixed component ($U' = H$ in the optimal configuration; Equation 41). For NVFP4, the WUS ablation (WUSH without Hadamard) sometimes outperforms full WUSH: on the Q projection, WUS achieves $2.26 \times 10^{-3}$ versus $2.40 \times 10^{-3}$ for WUSH; on the V projection, $2.30 \times 10^{-3}$ versus $2.28 \times 10^{-3}$ (essentially tied). This means that for NVFP4, the theoretically optimal $H$ component can be slightly detrimental, and the empirical optimum may lie somewhere between WUS and WUSH.

**Consequence.** The theory provides no guidance on *when* the Hadamard should be included versus omitted. For MXFP4 and INT4, including $H$ is strongly beneficial (e.g., MXFP4 Q: WUSH at 3.34 vs. WUS at 6.27; INT4 K: WUSH at 2.43 vs. WUS at 142). For NVFP4, the benefit is unclear or slightly negative. The paper's optimality proof (Theorem 1) treats FP and INT quantizers separately but assumes $H$ is always optimal for both; the empirical NVFP4 results reveal an interaction between the Hadamard and format-specific quantization behavior that the simplified error models do not capture. A practitioner quantizing a model for NVFP4 must therefore decide—without theoretical guidance—whether to use the full WUSH or the WUS variant, and the right choice may depend on the specific layer type and model.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 provides per-layer loss for both WUS and WUSH across all three formats and all seven projection types in Qwen3-8B's 18th transformer block. For NVFP4, WUS achieves lower or tied loss on 4 of 7 projections (Q: 2.26 vs. 2.40; K: 2.36 vs. 2.44; O: 2.00 vs. 1.92—WUSH leads; G: 3.04 vs. 3.09; U: 3.01 vs. 3.02; D: 2.23 vs. 2.33), with an average across projections of approximately 2.49 (WUS) vs. 2.53 (WUSH)—a negligible but consistent advantage for omitting $H$. The paper does not ablate the Hadamard systematically across model sizes or formats, and the end-to-end accuracy results (Table 2, Table 4) report only full WUSH, not WUS.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through empirical reporting. The paper transparently includes the WUS ablation in Table 1, allowing readers to observe the NVFP4 behavior. However, the theoretical derivation is not revised to account for the top-element preservation effect or to provide guidance on format-specific Hadamard inclusion. Section 6.1 discusses the hybrid FP/INT behavior of MXFP4 that makes the Hadamard beneficial there, but the NVFP4 harm is attributed to an unmodeled "top-element preservation effect" without further analysis. The paper does not propose a decision rule or diagnostic for determining whether to include $H$ for a given format-model combination, leaving this as a practical ambiguity.

### Rank-Deficient Weight or Activation Blocks Require Heuristic Damping Without Theoretically Grounded Guidance

**Assumption or constraint.** The WUSH construction requires computing the Cholesky decomposition of the second-moment matrices $d_{\text{out}}^{-1} W_{(i)} W_{(i)}^\top$ and $d_{\text{batch}}^{-1} X_{(i)} X_{(i)}^\top$, which must be positive definite for the Cholesky factor to exist and be numerically stable. In practice, weight blocks can be rank-deficient—for example, when $d_{\text{out}} < d$ (the output dimension is smaller than the quantization group size), the weight second moment is at most rank $d_{\text{out}}$, not full rank $d$. Activation blocks can also be rank-deficient when some tokens in the calibration batch have near-zero values for certain channels (e.g., padding tokens, or dimensions that are rarely activated).

Algorithm 1, Line 1 adds a damping term $\lambda I$ to the activation Hessian before Cholesky decomposition: $\mathbf{H} \leftarrow d_{\text{batch}}^{-1} X X^\top + \lambda I$, where $\lambda \geq 0$ is a scalar hyperparameter (the "damping ratio"). The paper mentions this in Section 3 ("In the case of rank $\ldots < d$, we can dampen the diagonal of the second moments before the Cholesky decomposition") and in Algorithm 1's input specification, but it does not report the value of $\lambda$ used in experiments, does not ablate its sensitivity, and does not provide theoretical guidance on how to set it.

**Consequence.** The damping ratio $\lambda$ is a free parameter that directly modifies the second-moment statistics used to compute the WUSH transform. A value too small leads to numerical instability or singular matrices when blocks are rank-deficient; a value too large biases the transform toward the identity (since adding $\lambda I$ makes the covariance more isotropic, reducing the singular value spread that WUSH exploits). Without guidance on setting $\lambda$, a practitioner might choose a default that is suboptimal for their model or format, potentially degrading accuracy or causing calibration failures on layers with low-rank weight blocks. Since modern LLMs often have attention heads where $d_{\text{out}}$ (the per-head dimension) is smaller than the MXFP4 block size of 32—for example, Llama-3.1-8B has 8 key-value heads with $d_{\text{out}} = 1024/8 = 128$ for the full concatenated Q/K/V projections, but the per-head weight blocks within those projections can have effective rank well below 32—this is not a corner case.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The damping ratio $\lambda$ is mentioned in Algorithm 1's input specification and in the Section 3 Remark about rank-deficient cases, but its value is not reported in any experiment. There is no ablation of $\lambda$, no sensitivity analysis showing how accuracy varies with $\lambda$, and no discussion of how sensitive the per-layer loss (Table 1) or end-to-end accuracy (Tables 2, 4) is to this choice. The absence of this detail makes the method less reproducible—a different implementation choosing a different default $\lambda$ could produce different results.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The damping is a practical necessity acknowledged by the paper, but treated as an implementation detail rather than a hyperparameter requiring analysis. In principle, cross-validation on a held-out calibration subset could select $\lambda$, but this adds to the already-unmeasured calibration cost. The paper does not propose a heuristic (e.g., setting $\lambda$ proportional to the trace of the second moment, or based on the condition number), does not report the default used, and does not flag this as a limitation for future work.

### The Theoretical Optimality Proof Has an Unresolved Gap Between Upper and Lower Bounds for INT Quantization Under General Distributions

**Assumption or constraint.** The INT quantizer derivation (Section 4.3.2) establishes that for the optimal configuration ($U' = H$, $S' = S^{1/2}$, $R = I$), the expected quantization error is bounded by:

$$d^{-1} (\text{tr}(S))^2 \leq \|T^{-1}\|_F^2 \cdot \mathbb{E}_\mathbf{y}[\|T\mathbf{y}\|_\infty^2] \leq \begin{cases} o(d)^{-1} (\text{tr}(S))^2 & \text{tail-bounded } \mathcal{D}_y \\ (\text{tr}(S))^2 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

The lower bound is $d^{-1} (\text{tr}(S))^2$. For tail-bounded distributions (Gaussian or Laplacian), the upper bound is $o(d)^{-1} (\text{tr}(S))^2$, meaning the gap between the achievable lower bound and the realized upper bound grows only logarithmically with $d$—this is "near-optimal" in a strong sense. For general distributions without tail bounds, the upper bound is $(\text{tr}(S))^2$, which means the gap between lower and upper bounds is a factor of $d$ (i.e., up to 32× for MXFP4 with $d = 32$). In this regime, the transform is only guaranteed to be within a factor of $d$ of optimal, which is a much weaker statement.

The paper does not analyze whether real LLM weight and activation distributions satisfy the tail-bounded condition. The multivariate Gaussian/Laplacian assumption is used in Lemma 3 (Appendix A.5) to tighten the bound on $\mathbb{E}[\max_k X_k^2]$, but whether the distribution $\mathcal{D}_y = W'^\top \mathcal{D}_x$ (the projected activation distribution after Cholesky weighting) is actually tail-bounded in practice is not verified.

**Consequence.** For practitioners, this theoretical gap has practical implications: if the true activation distribution is heavy-tailed (e.g., power-law rather than exponential decay), the INT quantization error could be up to $d = 32$ times the optimal value even when using WUSH, meaning the "near-optimal" guarantee is very weak. In this regime, a different transform—perhaps one that prioritizes minimizing the block-max norm over the Frobenius norm, or one that uses a different scaling exponent than $1/2$—could substantially outperform WUSH. Without characterizing the tail behavior of real LLM distributions, it is unclear whether WUSH is genuinely near-optimal for INT quantization in practice or only near-optimal under an idealized distributional assumption.

The empirical INT4 results in Table 1 partially address this: WUSH achieves loss of $2.39 \times 10^{-3}$ on the Q projection, dramatically better than identity at $170 \times 10^{-3}$ and Hadamard at $5.57 \times 10^{-3}$. This suggests that for real INT4 quantization, WUSH provides large gains regardless of the theoretical gap—but it does not tell us whether a transform using a different $S'$ (not $S^{1/2}$) could be even better. The theory cannot rule out this possibility for INT quantization in general.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The INT4 per-layer losses in Table 1 show WUSH dramatically outperforming all baselines, which strongly suggests the transform is effective. The WUS ablation for INT4 reveals catastrophic failure on some projections (K: $142 \times 10^{-3}$ for WUS, $2.43 \times 10^{-3}$ for WUSH), indicating that the Hadamard component is essential for INT4 and that the combination of $S^{1/2}$ scaling and $H$ randomization is jointly effective. However, there is no experiment varying the scaling exponent to test whether $S^{1/2}$ is empirically optimal for INT4, and no measurement of the tail behavior of $\mathcal{D}_y$ to assess whether the $o(d)$ or $d$ bound applies.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about the theoretical gap—Equations (30) and (57) explicitly state both the tight (tail-bounded) and loose (general) bounds. Section 6.1 notes that MXFP4 has INT-like behavior in the subnormal regime, which implies the INT analysis is relevant for that format as well. However, the paper does not attempt to characterize the tail behavior of LLM distributions, does not ablate the $S^{1/2}$ choice against other exponents for INT4, and does not propose future work to tighten the gap (e.g., by proving that the projected distribution $\mathcal{D}_y$ satisfies a tail bound for common neural network architectures). The "near-optimal" claim in the abstract and title should be understood as qualified by the tail-bounded assumption, which remains unverified.
