# MATHPILE: A Billion-Token-Scale Pre-training Corpus for Math

**ArXiv:** [2312.17120](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.17120)

## 🎯 Pitch

Standalone pre-training on curated Wikipedia (0.18B tokens) can lift a 7B model’s GSM8K score by over 5 points, yet popular synthetic math datasets actually degrade performance when used alone. MATHPILE shows that data quality—not volume—drives math reasoning gains, and it provides the first open, multi-source 9.5B-token corpus to prove it.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **MATHPILE**, a diverse and high-quality math-centric pre-training corpus comprising approximately 9.5 billion tokens sourced from textbooks, arXiv papers, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, StackExchange, and Common Crawl, constructed under a "less is more" principle that prioritizes data quality over quantity. Through a rigorous pipeline spanning source-specific preprocessing, language identification, heuristic cleaning and filtering, MinHash-based deduplication, and data contamination detection against downstream benchmark test sets, the authors produce a curated corpus from an initial 2.2TB of raw data and demonstrate through continual pre-training experiments on Mistral-7B-v0.1 that MATHPILE improves mathematical reasoning across benchmarks including GSM8K, MATH, and MMLU-MATH, with per-subset gains of up to ~5 percentage points on individual benchmarks (e.g., Textbooks boosting MATH from 10.08% to 12.10%) and pipeline ablations confirming that skipping the global processing stages degrades performance substantially (e.g., raw Wikipedia dropping GSM8K from 47.38% to 41.92%). The paper establishes that data quality—enforced through source-aware filtering thresholds and rigorous deduplication—matters more than token volume for mathematical pre-training, finding that even small curated subsets (~0.18B Wikipedia tokens) can yield meaningful improvements while larger synthetic datasets (AMPS, DM-Mathematics) degrade performance when used as standalone pre-training corpora.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: A Missing Public Math Pre-Training Corpus

The fundamental gap this paper addresses is straightforward but consequential: **the open-source community lacks a high-quality, diverse, publicly available pre-training corpus specifically designed for mathematical reasoning.** While general-purpose pre-training corpora like Pile, RedPajama, and Dolma have driven progress in broad language modeling capabilities, they are not optimized for mathematics. Conversely, the most successful math-specific language models — Google's Minerva and OpenAI's MathMix — rely on proprietary, closed-source training datasets that the research community cannot access, inspect, or build upon. MATHPILE aims to fill this specific void: a publicly released, meticulously curated mathematical corpus that rivals the quality of private datasets.

This gap matters for several reasons that the paper articulates explicitly and implicitly:

**Reproducibility and scientific progress.** When the training data for state-of-the-art math models remains proprietary, the research community cannot determine whether performance improvements come from novel architectures, training techniques, or simply better data. Every paper that builds on closed-source data introduces a confound — we cannot isolate the effect of the method from the effect of the data. By releasing both the corpus and its processing scripts, MATHPILE provides a common foundation that enables controlled scientific comparison. Progress in mathematical reasoning becomes decoupled from access to proprietary data pipelines.

**Democratizing math-capable AI.** Mathematical reasoning abilities in language models enable applications in education (automated tutoring, problem generation), scientific research (theorem proving, symbolic computation), and data analysis. When only well-resourced industrial labs can build such systems because they control the training data, the benefits concentrate. MATHPILE's release under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 explicitly aims to level this playing field.

**Understanding what makes math data "good."** Beyond simply providing a corpus, the paper investigates a meta-question: what preprocessing and curation decisions actually matter for mathematical pre-training? By releasing quality annotations, documenting filtering rules, and running careful ablations (Table 5: raw vs. cleaned Wikipedia), the paper provides evidence about *why* certain data processing choices work. This transforms the corpus from a static artifact into a research instrument for understanding data quality in the mathematical domain.

### Prior Approaches and Their Limitations

The paper situates itself against several categories of existing work, each of which leaves a different gap:

**Closed-source math corpora (Minerva, MathMix).** Google's Minerva training dataset comprises approximately 38.5 billion tokens drawn from arXiv and web sources, with documented data contamination detection (Table 1). OpenAI's MathMix is smaller at approximately 1.5 billion tokens but includes a mix of corpus data and problem sets with synthetic data. Both have demonstrably produced strong mathematical reasoning models — Minerva achieved state-of-the-art results on MATH and GSM8K, and MathMix underpins the mathematical capabilities in GPT-4. However, as the paper states, these datasets are "not open-sourced," making them unavailable for replication, inspection, or community improvement. MATHPILE positions itself as an open alternative that matches or approaches the quality bar these private datasets set.

**General-domain open corpora (Pile, RedPajama, SlimPajama, Dolma).** These datasets — Pile at 800GB, RedPajama at 1.2 trillion tokens, SlimPajama as a cleaned and deduplicated RedPajama variant, and Dolma as a more recent offering from Allen AI — collectively represent the standard open-source pre-training stack. They contain some mathematical content incidentally: Pile includes arXiv and StackExchange subsets, RedPajama includes Common Crawl and Wikipedia dumps, and SlimPajama further deduplicates these. However, they have three critical limitations for mathematical pre-training:

1. **No math-specific curation.** These corpora apply uniform quality filters across all documents, which the paper demonstrates is suboptimal for mathematics. The fastText language identification threshold of 0.5 used by CCNet and adopted by SlimPajama would filter out English mathematical documents that contain heavy LaTeX notation — the paper directly addresses this by relaxing thresholds for each source (Section 3.1: 0.1 for Wikipedia and StackExchange, 0.3 for arXiv, 0.5 only for Common Crawl where it was empirically appropriate).

2. **No math-specific deduplication considerations.** General deduplication pipelines treat near-duplicate mathematical content the same as near-duplicate prose. But in the mathematical domain, as the paper shows with ProofWiki (Section 3.3), near-duplicates can represent genuinely distinct mathematical content — different lemmas that share notation, or different proofs that share a common theorem statement structure. The paper's manual review of ProofWiki near-duplicates revealed that "they were differentiated as unique lemmas, proofs, or definitions," leading to their retention rather than aggressive deduplication.

3. **No contamination detection for math benchmarks.** General corpora do not specifically scan for overlap with MATH, GSM8K, or MMLU-STEM test sets. The paper's contamination detection (Section 3.4) found that these downstream benchmarks do leak into web-crawled data, including into OpenWebMath — a finding that general corpus pipelines would miss.

**Math-specific but narrow corpora (AMPS, DM-Mathematics, ProofPile).** Several prior datasets target mathematical reasoning but with constrained scope:

- **AMPS** (Hendrycks et al., 2021) is a problem set covering K-12 mathematics generated from Khan Academy exercises and synthetic data. At approximately 0.7 billion tokens, it is an order of magnitude smaller than MATHPILE and covers only K-12 material, omitting college-level, competition-level, and postgraduate mathematics. The paper's experiments show that AMPS *degrades* performance when used as a standalone pre-training corpus (Table 4: Mistral drops from 47.38 to 0.08 on GSM8K after AMPS pre-training), demonstrating that narrow, structured problem sets do not function as general pre-training data.

- **DM-Mathematics** (Saxton et al., 2019) is a synthetic dataset of math competition problems at approximately 4.4 billion tokens. The paper's experiments reveal the same failure mode: continual pre-training on DM-Mathematics collapses Mistral's GSM8K performance from 47.38 to 0.00 and MATH from 10.08 to 0.00. The authors attribute this to the "narrow, monotonous structure" of these problem sets, which makes them "unsuitable for standalone pre-training" — they work only when mixed with broader corpora.

- **ProofPile** (Azerbayev et al., 2023) targets formal theorem proving, drawing from arXiv, textbooks, StackExchange, ProofWiki, and the MATH dataset. At 8.3 billion tokens, it is comparable in scale to MATHPILE but is "confined to formal proving, not covering the broader mathematical domain from K-12 to postgraduate level." It also predates several important quality practices that MATHPILE incorporates, such as data contamination detection (ProofPile includes the MATH dataset directly, which creates a leakage problem for models trained on it).

**OpenWebMath (concurrent work).** The paper explicitly acknowledges OpenWebMath (Paster et al., 2023) as "concurrent with our work" and compares directly. OpenWebMath is a 14.7 billion token corpus extracted from Common Crawl web pages using a classifier trained on mathematical content. While it is math-centric and open-source, it has three significant limitations that MATHPILE addresses:

1. **Single-source composition.** OpenWebMath is "solely sourced from web pages," lacking the textbook, lecture note, arXiv paper, and curated Q&A content that MATHPILE includes. Web pages alone cannot provide the structured depth of formal mathematical exposition found in textbooks or the research-level content of arXiv papers.

2. **No data contamination detection.** OpenWebMath was released without scanning for downstream benchmark leakage. The paper's analysis (Section 3.4, Table 2) found 195 MATH test set occurrences and 65 MMLU-STEM occurrences in OpenWebMath — substantially more than in MATHPILE. The authors note this "aligns with similar findings by Azerbayev et al. [5]," establishing that contamination detection is not optional for mathematical corpora.

3. **No granular quality annotations.** OpenWebMath does not provide the per-document quality metadata (language identification scores, symbol-to-word ratios, uppercase word ratios, etc.) that MATHPILE includes, limiting users' ability to apply custom filtering pipelines.

The paper's direct comparison in continual pre-training (Table 4) shows that a sampled subset of OpenWebMath (0.59B tokens) produces weaker improvements than comparably-sized MATHPILE subsets — Textbooks (0.56B) boosts MATH from 10.08 to 12.10 while OpenWebMath drops it to 7.86, and Wikipedia (0.18B) boosts SAT-MATH from 47.27 to 53.63 while OpenWebMath improves it only to 47.72. The paper attributes this to the quality advantage of curated sources over web-only data.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

MATHPILE positions itself not as a model-building paper but as an **infrastructure contribution** — the focus is on the corpus itself and the pipeline that produces it, not on achieving state-of-the-art results with a trained model. The continual pre-training experiments serve as validation that the corpus is effective, not as the primary contribution. This is reflected in the framing: "we are not directly building a model, but rather focusing on a more fundamental aspect: creating a high-quality and diverse pre-training corpus tailored for the math domain."

The paper's positioning has several distinctive aspects:

**The "less is more" philosophy.** Drawing on the finding from Zhou et al. (2023) that small amounts of high-quality data can outperform large amounts of noisy data, the paper makes quality prioritization its organizing principle. This manifests in multiple design choices: source-specific preprocessing rather than uniform pipelines, heuristic rules developed through manual inspection rather than automated filtering, retention of near-duplicate ProofWiki entries after manual review confirmed they were distinct mathematical content, and the deliberate choice to start from 2.2TB of raw data and reduce to 9.5B tokens (a ~230× reduction) through aggressive filtering.

**Transparency over scale.** Unlike many corpus papers that emphasize token count ("X billion tokens!"), the paper emphasizes documentation — the datasheet (Table 6), the quality annotation scheme (Figure 12), the explicit listing of human intervention points (Tables 9 and 10), and the detailed deduplication examples (Tables 11–19). This reflects a recognition that the community's trust in pre-training corpora depends on knowing what is in them and how they were constructed, not just how large they are.

**Contamination awareness as a first-class concern.** The paper's treatment of data contamination is more thorough than typical for corpus papers. Rather than treating it as an afterthought or a post-hoc check, contamination detection is integrated into the pipeline as a mandatory step before release (Figure 2: it appears alongside deduplication in the processing workflow). The paper also extends contamination detection beyond its own corpus to analyze OpenWebMath, establishing a comparative baseline and demonstrating the pervasiveness of the problem.

**Explicit acknowledgment of limitations.** The paper's limitations section (Section 6) is unusually candid about what MATHPILE does not provide: the data scale is insufficient for training extremely large models, the heuristic decisions in the pipeline were made empirically and may not be optimal, the Common Crawl extraction could be substantially expanded, and the corpus is English-only. This transparency distinguishes the paper from work that might oversell a corpus by hiding its constraints.

The net effect of this positioning is that MATHPILE enters the ecosystem as a **reference implementation of what high-quality math corpus construction looks like**. It provides not just data, but a documented process, quality annotations, ablation evidence, and a set of design principles that other corpus builders can adopt or adapt. The paper argues — implicitly through its thoroughness and explicitly through its comparison with OpenWebMath — that this comprehensive approach to quality is what the community needs from open-source corpus efforts, even if it means producing fewer tokens than a less careful crawl would yield.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

MATHPILE is a carefully curated, 9.5-billion-token dataset of mathematical text drawn from textbooks, arXiv papers, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, StackExchange forums, and Common Crawl web pages, built through a multi-stage pipeline that transforms 2.2TB of raw heterogeneous source material into a cleaned, deduplicated, and contamination-checked corpus. The paper addresses the problem that the open-source community lacks a high-quality, diverse, publicly available math pre-training corpus — existing options are either proprietary (Minerva's dataset, MathMix), general-domain and unoptimized for math (Pile, RedPajama), or math-specific but narrow in scope and poorly suited for standalone pre-training (AMPS, DM-Mathematics, ProofPile) — and the solution takes the shape of a source-aware filtration pipeline where every processing decision (language identification thresholds, heuristic filtering rules, deduplication aggressiveness, and downstream benchmark contamination scanning) is customized per source based on empirical observation of what actually improves or degrades the mathematical reasoning capabilities of models trained on the resulting corpus.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

MATHPILE's construction can be understood as two nested processing layers — source-specific preprocessing (applied during data collection itself, unique to each source's format and quirks) followed by global data processing (applied uniformly across all collected documents, with source-specific parameter tuning where needed). The major components and their responsibilities are:

1. **Source-Specific Data Collectors** — six independent collection pipelines (one per source: Textbooks, arXiv, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, StackExchange, Common Crawl) that each acquire raw documents, convert them to a consistent text format (Markdown or LaTeX), and perform preliminary cleaning tailored to that source's structure (e.g., removing LaTeX comments and references from arXiv papers, filtering StackExchange posts by community score, extracting mathematical web pages from Common Crawl using TF-IDF heuristic rules, parsing PDF textbooks via the Mathpix API). Each collector produces documents with source-specific metadata but in a normalized text representation ready for global processing.

2. **Language Identification Module** — applies the fastText language classifier to every document using source-specific score thresholds (0.1 for Wikipedia and StackExchange, 0.3 for arXiv, 0.5 for Common Crawl, no threshold for ProofWiki and Textbooks) to filter non-English content while avoiding false positives on English documents heavy with mathematical notation (which fastText's general-domain training causes it to misclassify as non-English).

3. **Cleaning and Filtering Module** — applies eleven heuristic rules developed through manual inspection of mathematical documents to remove boilerplate content, machine-generated text, navigation elements, and structurally invalid documents. Each rule targets a specific failure mode (e.g., progress bars identified by high ellipsis-to-word ratios, copyright footers, "lorem ipsum" placeholder text, documents dominated by bullet points rather than prose) and assigns per-document quality annotations (e.g., language identification score, symbol-to-word ratio, uppercase word ratio) that downstream users can use to apply their own custom filters.

4. **Deduplication Module** — uses MinHash LSH with 5-grams, the SHA1 hash function, and 450 buckets of 20 minhashes each (9,000 total minhashes per document) following RefinedWeb's configuration to identify and remove near-duplicate documents both within each source and across sources. Manual review of detected near-duplicates determines retention decisions case-by-case (e.g., ProofWiki near-duplicates are kept because they represent distinct mathematical lemmas or proofs that share similar notation; StackExchange cross-posts between Math and MathOverflow are removed).

5. **Data Contamination Detection Module** — performs line-level exact match detection between every document and the test sets of six mathematical reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, AGIEval-SAT-MATH, MathQA, AQuA), using MD5 hashing (taking the first 64 bits plus the line content to form hash sets), and removes any document containing exact matches of test set lines.

Information flows sequentially through these five stages — source-specific collection → language identification → cleaning/filtering → deduplication → contamination detection — with the initial 2.2TB of raw data reduced to 9.5B tokens across 903,336 documents in the final MATHPILE corpus.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the source-specific preprocessing for each of the six data sources, because understanding what raw material each source provides and how it is converted to normalized text is prerequisite to understanding why the global pipeline parameters (e.g., language identification thresholds, filtering rules) differ by source.

- **Second**, the language identification module and its source-specific threshold tuning, because it is the first global processing step and its failure mode (filtering English mathematical text as non-English) directly informs why source-agnostic approaches fail.

- **Third**, the cleaning and filtering heuristic rules, because these represent the core quality-enforcement mechanism and the paper's primary technical contribution — the eleven rules encode domain-specific knowledge about what makes mathematical text suitable for language model pre-training.

- **Fourth**, the deduplication approach and the MinHash LSH configuration, including the critical design choice to manually review near-duplicates per source rather than applying uniform removal thresholds.

- **Fifth**, the data contamination detection procedure and its findings (including the comparison with OpenWebMath), because this is the final quality gate and the paper's most distinctive contribution relative to other mathematical corpora.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **corpus construction paper** whose core idea is that mathematical pre-training data requires source-aware processing — generic pipelines developed for general-domain web text fail on mathematical content because mathematical text has different structural properties (heavy LaTeX notation, formal theorem-proof formats, equations that confuse language identifiers) and different quality signals (near-duplicate mathematical content is often genuinely distinct, unlike near-duplicate web prose).

#### Source-Specific Preprocessing: Textbooks

The textbook collection begins with **manual internet searches** for open-source, freely accessible mathematics textbook websites. The authors download PDF files for 38 K-12 level textbooks, 369 college-level mathematics textbooks covering subjects including "linear algebra, probability theory, calculus, and optimization," and 467 college course handouts and lecture notes, for a total of 874 documents before synthetic textbook augmentation.

**PDF-to-text conversion.** All PDFs are parsed into Markdown format using the **Mathpix API**, a commercial optical character recognition (OCR) service specialized for mathematical notation. Mathpix converts both the text and the mathematical expressions into LaTeX-embedded Markdown, preserving the semantic structure of equations rather than producing flat image-based representations.

**Manual cleanup of parsing artifacts.** After conversion, the authors apply rule-based cleanup to remove extraneous elements that the Mathpix API produces or that are inherent to textbook structure but inappropriate for language model training:

- **Parsed image URLs** — Mathpix generates URLs for embedded figures; these are removed since the actual image content is not available and the URL strings are noise.
- **Preface sections, table of contents, acknowledgment sections, and index sections** — these contain metadata about the book rather than mathematical content and are stripped.
- **Consecutive empty lines** — condensed to improve token efficiency.

**Synthetic textbook filtering from OpenPhi.** The authors also ingest synthetic textbooks from the **OpenPhi Project**, an open-source counterpart to Microsoft's Phi work (Gunasekar et al., 2023) that generates synthetic textbooks using a language model. OpenPhi contains 124,493 documents spanning many subjects beyond programming; the authors need to isolate mathematics-specific documents. Their filtering criterion is the **presence of the LaTeX display math delimiter "$$" combined with common mathematical LaTeX commands** like `\mathbf` (for bold mathematical symbols) and `\frac` (for fractions). The rationale, based on manual verification, is that "$$" alone appears in non-mathematical contexts, but co-occurrence with structural math commands strongly indicates mathematical content. This rule-based filter yields **3,889 synthetic mathematics textbooks**, bringing the total textbook collection to 3,979 documents containing approximately 187 million tokens.

> **Design choice — why heuristic rules over classifier-based filtering.** The paper explicitly notes that for the synthetic textbook source, a simple rule-based filter sufficed because the variation among OpenPhi documents is relatively low and mathematical content has clear surface-level markers. For the larger, more heterogeneous Common Crawl source (discussed below), the authors also opt for heuristic rules, but for a different reason: they experimented with "more intricate dense embedding-based methods to identify mathematical documents, but these resulted in poor recall." The embedding approach likely failed because the dense representation space does not cleanly separate mathematical from non-mathematical web pages — many technical web pages that happen to use some mathematical notation are not primarily mathematical content.

#### Source-Specific Preprocessing: arXiv Papers

arXiv papers constitute the largest component of MATHPILE at approximately 8.3 billion tokens (38.07% of documents) and require the most complex preprocessing because academic papers in LaTeX have elaborate internal structure that must be flattened for language model training while preserving mathematical content.

**Subject selection.** The authors identify 50 sub-subjects from the arXiv taxonomy spanning Mathematics, Computer Science, Statistics, Physics, Quantitative Finance, and Economics (detailed in Table 7). The full list includes entries like math.AG (Algebraic Geometry), math.AT (Algebraic Topology), quant-ph (Quantum Physics), cs.LG (Machine Learning), stat.ML (Machine Learning Statistics), q-fin.MF (Mathematical Finance), and econ.TH (Economic Theory). This broad subject selection reflects a deliberate design choice: mathematical content is not confined to the "math" prefix categories — many important mathematical techniques and expositions appear in physics, computer science, statistics, and economics papers. By including these adjacent fields, MATHPILE captures a wider range of applied mathematical reasoning than a pure mathematics corpus would.

**Metadata-based filtering and source file access.** The authors filter arXiv's metadata (obtained from a Kaggle dataset snapshot) to select papers matching the chosen subjects, then access the **source LaTeX files** (if available — many arXiv papers provide only the compiled PDF). Papers without available LaTeX source are excluded, since LaTeX source preserves the mathematical equations in machine-readable form while PDF-to-text conversion would degrade equation structure (as the Mathpix API used for textbooks introduces noise even under optimal conditions).

**Multi-file consolidation.** ArXiv papers typically consist of a main LaTeX file and multiple included files (sections, appendices, figures). The authors parse the main file for LaTeX `\include` and `\input` commands and consolidate the referenced files in the order specified by the document structure, producing a single continuous LaTeX document per paper.

**Extensive LaTeX transformations.** The authors apply nine transformations to produce clean, consistent text suitable for language model training. Each transformation addresses a specific source of noise or structural inconsistency in academic LaTeX:

1. **Remove comments** — LaTeX comments (everything after `%` on a line) are stripped, as they contain author notes, commented-out text, and version control annotations that degrade training signal.
2. **Revert macro commands** — Custom LaTeX macros defined via `\newcommand` are expanded to their definitions, so that the model learns the actual mathematical notation rather than author-specific abbreviations. This is important for generalization: if author A defines `\newcommand{\R}{\mathbb{R}}` and author B writes `\mathbb{R}` directly, reverting macros ensures the model sees consistent notation.
3. **Omit figure environments while retaining captions and figure labels** — Figures themselves (images, TikZ code) are removed because they are not text, but captions (e.g., "Figure 1: Convergence plot of the proposed method") contain valuable descriptive text and figure labels preserve cross-reference anchors.
4. **Exclude acknowledgments sections** — Acknowledgment sections contain funding information, personal thanks, and grant numbers that are high-entropy noise for mathematical reasoning training.
5. **Eliminate references in each paper** — The bibliography is removed because citation strings and reference formatting provide minimal mathematical content.
6. **Condense more than three consecutive empty lines to two** — Preserves paragraph separation while removing excessive whitespace that wastes token budget.
7. **Replace certain formatting commands like `\hfill` and `\vspace` with an empty line** — These commands control visual layout in the compiled PDF but add no semantic information; replacing them with a simple line break preserves the implicit paragraph boundary without the LaTeX noise.
8. **Replace the `\maketitle` command with the actual title text** — Titles are important semantic indicators of document content and should be preserved in natural language form rather than as LaTeX commands.
9. **Preserve only the content within the main body of the LaTeX document** — i.e., the text between `\begin{document}` and `\end{document}`, discarding the preamble (package imports, style definitions, metadata).

> **Design choice — why these nine transforms and not others.** The authors do not apply more aggressive structural flattening (e.g., removing all LaTeX commands entirely) because LaTeX commands like `\begin{theorem}...\end{theorem}` provide valuable structural information that signals to the language model the type of mathematical content (theorem statement, proof, definition, example). Preserving theorem-like environments maintains the pedagogical structure of mathematical exposition. Nor do they attempt to render LaTeX to plain text, which would destroy equation formatting — the LaTeX math mode expressions (`$...$` and `$$...$$`) are the most valuable content in these papers.

The output of this pipeline is **347,945 cleaned LaTeX documents** (one per paper) after some papers are lost due to unavailable LaTeX source or irrecoverable parsing failures, representing approximately 8.3 billion tokens.

#### Source-Specific Preprocessing: Wikipedia

Wikipedia provides broad coverage of mathematical concepts in an encyclopedic format — definitions, historical context, and conceptual explanations — complementing the research-paper style of arXiv and the pedagogical style of textbooks.

**Data acquisition.** The authors download the **mathematics-focused dump of English Wikipedia for August 2023**, specifically a ZIM-format dump (used by offline Wikipedia readers) that excludes images. Using the `libzim` library, they extract approximately **106,900 HTML documents** from the dump.

**HTML-to-Markdown conversion with hyperlink removal.** Following the practice of LLaMA (Touvron et al., 2023), the authors convert HTML to Markdown and **remove hyperlinks**. Hyperlinks are noise for language model pre-training — they introduce URL strings, anchor text that breaks sentence flow, and cross-references that the model cannot follow. The paper retains **alternative text content** (alt-text descriptions of images and mathematical diagrams) because these contain natural language descriptions of visual mathematical content, but **excludes image paths** (often SVG format URLs) as these are purely technical metadata.

**Library switch for LaTeX display correction.** This is a notable implementation detail that the paper explicitly documents as an ablation-worthy decision. The initial HTML-to-Markdown conversion used the **`html2text` library**, but this produced documents with incorrect LaTeX display — mathematical expressions that should appear as properly formatted LaTeX (e.g., `$K/k$` for a field extension) were instead rendered with garbled escaping (as shown in Figure 11: `{\\\\displaystyle \\\\dim...}` instead of `\dim`). The authors discovered that switching to the **`Resiliparse` library with DOM parsing** resolved the issue by correctly handling the LaTeX markup within Wikipedia's HTML structure. The ablation in Table 5 confirms this matters: the "Full cleaned but LaTeX issued Wikipedia" (0.23B tokens, processed with the buggy library) achieves 47.15% on GSM8K, while the corrected version (0.18B tokens) achieves 49.96% — a ~2.8 percentage point improvement from fixing a display issue alone.

**Additional cleanup.** After conversion to Markdown, the authors apply three further transformations:

- **Removal of extra newlines within paragraphs** — Wikipedia's HTML often inserts spurious line breaks that disrupt sentence continuity.
- **Condensing of more than three consecutive empty lines to two** — same rationale as for arXiv.
- **Removal of boilerplate content at the bottom of pages** — Wikipedia pages end with standard footer text like "This article is issued from Wikipedia. The text is licensed under Creative Commons - Attribution - Sharealike..." These footers are removed using regular expression matching on the known phrases.

The final Wikipedia collection consists of **106,881 entries** (approximately 22,795 documents after deduplication) totaling approximately 60 million tokens.

> **Design choice — why mathematics-only Wikipedia and not all of English Wikipedia.** The full English Wikipedia is enormous — millions of articles covering every conceivable topic. Including all of it would dilute the mathematical signal and increase the corpus size without proportional benefit for mathematical reasoning. The mathematics-focused dump (with a filter for math-related categories and articles) provides high coverage of mathematical concepts while excluding non-mathematical content that would make the corpus less focused. The paper also acknowledges that some Wikipedia entries were removed during deduplication because the ZIM dump contained multiple historical versions of the same article, leading to 83,716 near-duplicate documents being identified and removed.

#### Source-Specific Preprocessing: ProofWiki

ProofWiki is an online wiki dedicated to collecting mathematical proofs, organized into definitions, theorems, and their corresponding proofs. Its highly structured, formal content makes it uniquely valuable for training models on rigorous mathematical reasoning.

**Data acquisition.** The authors use the **ProofWiki dump dated April 9, 2022** from the Internet Archive, mirroring the approach of NaturalProofs (Welleck et al., 2021), which used the November 12, 2020 version. The dump contains all wiki pages in their raw MediaWiki markup format.

**Parsing pipeline.** The extraction proceeds in two steps:

1. **BeautifulSoup parsing** — all wiki pages are parsed to extract the HTML-rendered structure.
2. **wikitextparser extraction** — the raw text content is extracted from the wiki markup, preserving the semantic structure (definitions, theorems, proofs).

**Structuring into definition and theorem-proof formats.** The authors impose a consistent document structure on the extracted content to make it machine-readable:

- **Definitions** are formatted within a `\begin{definition}...\end{definition}` environment, with the definiendum (the term being defined) captured as the title.
- **Theorem-proof pairs** are formatted within a `\begin{section}...\end{section}` environment, with the theorem title serving as the section heading, the theorem body as plain text, and the proof contained within `\begin{proof}...\end{proof}`.

This yields **10,328 definitions** and **13,511 theorem-proof pairs**, totaling approximately 7.6 million tokens. The structured formatting is adapted from ProofPile's approach but applied to a broader collection.

> **Design choice — why structured formatting rather than flat text.** Mathematical proofs have internal logical structure (hypothesis, inference steps, conclusion) that flat text would obscure. By preserving the theorem-proof structure with explicit environment markers, the authors ensure that the language model can learn the relationship between theorem statements and their proofs — a relationship that is central to mathematical reasoning and that is much harder to learn from unstructured text where theorems and proofs are interleaved without explicit boundaries.

#### Source-Specific Preprocessing: StackExchange

StackExchange's network of community Q&A sites provides high-quality, expert-curated mathematical discussions where questions receive multiple answers with community voting scores, providing a natural quality signal.

**Site selection.** The authors identify **eleven StackExchange sites** (Table 8): five dedicated to mathematics (math.stackexchange.com, mathoverflow.net, mathematica.stackexchange.com, matheducators.stackexchange.com, hsm.stackexchange.com for history of science and math) and six in closely related fields (physics.stackexchange.com, proofassistants.stackexchange.com, tex.stackexchange.com for LaTeX typesetting, datascience.stackexchange, cstheory.stackexchange.com for theoretical computer science, cs.stackexchange.com for computer science). The inclusion of related fields reflects the same design philosophy as the arXiv subject selection: mathematical reasoning happens across disciplinary boundaries.

**Data acquisition and HTML parsing.** The authors download the **August 2023 site dumps** from the Internet Archive, which contain all posts in XML format with HTML body content. For each post, they:

- **Extract the essential components** — question title and body, answer body, associated metadata (score, acceptance status, author, tags).
- **Remove invalid XML characters** — StackExchange dumps occasionally contain non-standard characters that break XML parsing.
- **Convert HTML to raw text** using BeautifulSoup — this strips HTML tags from the post bodies, leaving the plain text content including any LaTeX math notation (which appears within `$...$` delimiters on StackExchange).

**Quality filtering via community scores.** StackExchange's core quality mechanism is its voting system: community members upvote good questions and answers, and the question asker can mark one answer as "accepted." The authors leverage this by applying **quality thresholds**:

- **Questions** are filtered to include only those with a **score of at least 5**, ensuring that low-quality, unclear, or trivial questions are excluded.
- **Answers** are filtered by the **maximum of two criteria**: either the answer's own score is at least the threshold (currently 5), or the answer is the accepted answer and its score meets a lower bar. Specifically, "answers were assessed by either the threshold or the score of the accepted answer, whichever was lower" — meaning that if a question has an accepted answer scoring only 2, other answers must meet the higher threshold of 5, but the accepted answer itself is retained.

**Unanswered question preservation.** Questions with **no answers but a score of at least 10** are preserved separately (3,418 such questions). The authors note these are retained "for potential future use" — they could be used as prompts for model-based answer generation, as training data for question-answering systems, or as evaluation material.

**Question-answer pairing.** Each question is paired with its qualifying answers into a single document, with the question body followed by each answer body along with its metadata. This preserves the conversational structure of StackExchange threads, which models can learn from: the relationship between questions and their multiple answers, each with different approaches or levels of detail.

The final StackExchange collection contains **267,919 questions, 435,129 answers, and 3,418 unanswered questions**, totaling approximately 254 million tokens.

> **Design choice — why score threshold 5 and not a higher bar (e.g., 10).** The threshold represents a tradeoff between quality and coverage. StackExchange math communities are relatively small compared to general technology sites like StackOverflow; a threshold of 10 would exclude many substantive but niche questions that received moderate attention. The threshold of 5 is high enough to filter out truly poor content (questions with negative or zero scores that indicate community rejection) while retaining the long tail of specialized mathematical knowledge. The separate threshold of 10 for unanswered questions reflects a different calculus: these are questions the community found interesting (high score) but couldn't answer, making them potentially valuable as challenging problems.

#### Source-Specific Preprocessing: Common Crawl Web Pages

The Common Crawl represents the broadest and noisiest source in MATHPILE — a vast collection of web-crawled text where mathematical content is interspersed with all other types of web pages. Extracting mathematical documents from this source requires efficient filtering at scale, since the full Common Crawl is petabytes in size.

**Starting point: SlimPajama.** Rather than processing raw Common Crawl dumps directly, the authors start from **SlimPajama** (Cerebras, June 2023), a cleaned and deduplicated version of RedPajama that has already undergone basic quality filtering. The authors specifically target two SlimPajama subsets: **SlimPajama-C4** (the cleaned version of the C4 dataset derived from Common Crawl) and **SlimPajama-CommonCrawl** (the remaining Common Crawl content not in C4).

**TF-IDF vocabulary construction from curated textbooks.** The key challenge is distinguishing mathematical web pages from non-mathematical ones at scale. The authors adopt a heuristic approach based on **TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency) features** derived from their curated high-quality textbooks:

1. **Preprocessing**: Stop words are removed from the textbook corpus, and features are limited to a **maximum of 10,000** terms.
2. **Tokenization**: Whitespace tokenization is used rather than sophisticated NLP tokenization — this is sufficient because LaTeX commands are typically space-delimited.
3. **TF-IDF computation**: Standard TF-IDF weights are computed, where high TF-IDF terms are those that appear frequently in specific textbooks but rarely across the entire textbook corpus — these should be domain-specific mathematical terminology.
4. **Manual vocabulary inspection**: Upon examining the resulting high-TF-IDF vocabulary, the authors "identified 11 commonly used LaTeX commands, integral to mathematical expressions."

**Hard matching rule for mathematical document classification.** A document from SlimPajama is classified as mathematical if it satisfies a **two-condition rule**: (1) the document contains the LaTeX display math delimiter `"$$"`, AND (2) the document contains at least one of the 11 identified LaTeX commands. The authors do not explicitly list the 11 commands in the paper, but given the context ("integral to mathematical expressions"), they likely include structural math commands like `\frac`, `\sum`, `\int`, `\alpha`, `\beta`, `\mathbf`, `\mathcal`, `\mathbb`, `\begin`, `\end`, etc. — commands that unambiguously indicate formal mathematical typesetting.

> **Design choice — why rule-based filtering over neural classifiers.** The paper explicitly addresses this: "We also experimented with more intricate dense embedding-based methods to identify mathematical documents, but these resulted in poor recall." The embedding-based methods likely failed because:
>
> - **Imbalanced class distribution**: Mathematical web pages are a tiny fraction of all web pages, and dense embedding models trained or fine-tuned on general web text may not learn a decision boundary that cleanly separates the mathematical minority.
> - **Surface-level discriminability**: Mathematics has unambiguous surface-level markers (LaTeX delimiters, math commands) that are rare in non-mathematical text. A rule-based approach that exploits these markers directly avoids the statistical noise of learned representations.
> - **Scalability**: Computing dense embeddings for billions of web pages would be computationally prohibitive; a simple string-matching rule runs in linear time with minimal overhead.

This rule-based filter yields **4,307 documents from SlimPajama-C4** and **72,137 documents from SlimPajama-CommonCrawl**, for a total of **76,444 documents** (later reduced to 75,142 after deduplication) containing approximately **615 million tokens**.

> **Design choice — acknowledged limitation.** The paper explicitly notes that this extraction method is conservative: "We acknowledge the potential for more efficient methods to sift mathematical documents from Common Crawl snapshots, an area we plan to explore in future work." The current approach likely misses mathematical web pages that use Unicode math characters or MathML rather than LaTeX, pages that discuss mathematics in prose without formal notation, and pages where the LaTeX is embedded in JavaScript-rendered widgets that SlimPajama's text extraction doesn't capture.

#### Language Identification Module

After source-specific preprocessing produces normalized text documents from all six sources, the **first global processing step** is language identification — filtering out documents written in languages other than English. This is necessary because the source corpora (especially Common Crawl and arXiv) contain documents in many languages, and MATHPILE is explicitly designed as an English-language corpus.

**Classifier: fastText language identifier.** The authors use the fastText language classifier (Joulin et al., 2017; Grave et al., 2018) trained on **Wikipedia, Tatoeba, and SETimes** — a standard training configuration for production language identification. For each document, fastText outputs a **language label** and a **confidence score between 0 and 1**.

**The standard threshold fails for mathematical text.** The "common practice" in corpus construction (also used by CCNet; Wenzek et al., 2020) is to classify a document as belonging to its predicted language if the confidence score exceeds **0.5**. However, the authors discovered that this threshold produces "a considerable number of false positives — cases where documents were erroneously filtered as non-English when, in fact, they were written in English but contained a substantial amount of mathematical symbols."

The root cause is a **domain gap** between fastText's training data and mathematical text. fastText relies on character n-gram features to identify languages; mathematical LaTeX expressions (`\frac`, `\mathbb{R}`, `\sum_{i=1}^n`) contain character sequences that are rare or absent in natural language text. When a document's character n-gram distribution is dominated by mathematical notation rather than English prose, fastText's confidence in the English prediction drops below 0.5 even though the document's actual language is English.

**Source-specific threshold tuning.** To address this, the authors set **customized confidence thresholds for each data source** based on empirical observation of the false positive rate:

| Source | Threshold | Rationale |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| Wikipedia | 0.1 | Wikipedia articles have substantial prose context around equations; even low-confidence fastText predictions are reliable. |
| StackExchange | 0.1 | Q&A posts similarly mix prose and LaTeX; aggressive filtering would discard valid content. |
| arXiv | 0.3 | Academic papers contain extensive mathematical notation but also have abstracts, introductions, and conclusions in prose. |
| Common Crawl | 0.5 | Web pages are the noisiest source; the standard threshold provides a reasonable balance. |
| ProofWiki | None | All documents were manually verified to be English during collection. |
| Textbooks | None | Manual verification during PDF collection ensured English-only content. |

This refinement **removed approximately 8,400 documents totaling 231 million tokens**, most of which were genuinely non-English (rather than false positives) since the relaxed thresholds reduce rather than increase filtering.

> **Why not use a math-specific language identifier?** The paper does not explicitly address this, but the implication is that training a custom language identifier on mathematical text would require a labeled dataset of mathematical documents in multiple languages — a resource that does not exist. The threshold-tuning approach is pragmatic: it works within the constraints of an off-the-shelf tool while addressing its known failure mode on mathematical text.

#### Cleaning and Filtering Module

Despite the source-specific preprocessing, many documents — especially from Wikipedia and Common Crawl — still contain content that is unsuitable for language model training: boilerplate navigation text, machine-generated filler, copyright notices, progress bars, login prompts, and structurally malformed documents. The goal of the cleaning and filtering module is to remove this low-quality content using a set of heuristic rules developed through **manual inspection of the corpus**.

**Design philosophy.** The paper explicitly states that existing filtering methods (Raffel et al., 2020; Rae et al., 2021; Longpre et al., 2023; Penedo et al., 2023; Cerebras, 2023), "while detailed, risk excluding valuable documents in our math-focused corpus if directly applying them as-is." The risk is that general-domain filters misclassify mathematical documents as low-quality — for example, a filter that penalizes documents with high ratios of non-alphabetic characters would discard LaTeX-heavy mathematical papers. Therefore, the authors develop **mathematics-specific filtering rules** that account for the structural properties of mathematical text.

**The eleven heuristic rules.** Each rule targets a specific failure mode identified through manual inspection. The rules are applied in sequence; documents that trigger any filtering rule are removed entirely from the corpus, while line-level rules remove only the offending lines.

**Rule 1 — Lorem ipsum detection.** "Detect lines containing 'lorem ipsum' and filter them out if the resulting line is less than 5 characters." Lorem ipsum is placeholder text used in web templates; its presence indicates machine-generated filler content that contributes no mathematical signal. The 5-character minimum prevents false removal of lines that contain the phrase "lorem ipsum" in a meaningful context (e.g., a StackExchange discussion about typesetting).

**Rule 2 — JavaScript notice removal.** "Detect lines containing 'javascript' that also include 'enable', 'disable' or 'browser' and are under 200 characters, and filter them." These are "please enable JavaScript to view this content" notices that appear on web pages whose mathematical content requires JavaScript rendering — since the text extraction cannot execute JavaScript, these notices are the only remnant and contain no mathematical content.

**Rule 3 — UI boilerplate removal.** "Filter lines containing fewer than 10 words that include keywords like 'Log in', 'sign-in', 'read more...', or 'items in cart.'" These are navigation and e-commerce interface elements that appear on web pages hosting mathematical content (e.g., a university course page with a login prompt, a blog post with a "read more" expander) but are not themselves mathematical content. The "<10 words" constraint prevents removal of lines that mention these phrases in a substantive mathematical context (e.g., "To understand logarithms, first log in to the system..." — an unlikely but possible false positive).

**Rule 4 — Uppercase word ratio.** "Filter documents if the ratio of uppercase words exceeds 40%." Documents with excessive capitalization are typically machine-generated (e.g., template text in all-caps), navigation menus, or incorrectly parsed content. Mathematical notation is case-sensitive and does not typically produce all-caps text except in specific environments (e.g., theorem headings).

**Rule 5 — Ellipsis line ratio.** "Filter lines that end with '...' if they constitute more than 30% of the entire document." Lines ending in ellipses are characteristic of truncated content, table-of-contents entries, or search result snippets. If more than 30% of a document consists of such truncated lines, the document is likely a directory listing or search result page rather than substantive content.

**Rule 6 — Non-alphabetic word ratio.** "Filter documents if the ratio of non-alphabetic words surpasses 80%." This targets documents dominated by code, data tables, or machine-generated content. However, the 80% threshold is deliberately high to avoid filtering mathematical documents: LaTeX equations contain many non-alphabetic characters (`\`, `{`, `}`, `^`, `_`), but mathematical prose also contains connecting words ("Let", "then", "since", "therefore") that should keep a legitimate mathematical document below the 80% threshold.

**Rule 7 — Average English word length range.** "Exclude documents with an average English word length outside the range of (3, 10)." Natural English text has an average word length of approximately 4.7 characters (including common short words like "a", "the", "of", "and"). Documents with average word lengths below 3 are likely dominated by abbreviations, variable names, or machine-generated codes; documents above 10 are likely concatenated without spaces or dominated by non-word character sequences. The (3, 10) range is conservative, accommodating mathematical notation that produces longer "words" (e.g., "$\mathbb{R}$-module" tokenized as a single unit in whitespace tokenization) while excluding clearly anomalous content.

**Rule 8 — Stop word presence.** "Discard documents that lack at least two common stop words such as 'the', 'be', 'to', 'of', 'and', 'that', or 'have'." English mathematical text, even when notation-heavy, still contains function words that connect the notation into coherent sentences. A document with fewer than two of these stop words is either non-English (which should have been caught by language identification), machine-generated, or so notation-dense that it lacks the prose context necessary for language model training. Requiring two stop words rather than one reduces false positives from documents that happen to contain a single "the" in boilerplate text.

**Rule 9 — Ellipsis-to-word ratio.** "Filter out documents if the ratio of ellipses ('...') to words exceeds 0.5 (e.g., progress bars)." This targets progress bar artifacts, loading indicators, and truncated directory listings where ellipsis characters dominate the text. Normal mathematical prose uses ellipses sparingly (e.g., in series notation like "$a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$"); a ratio above 0.5 indicates non-content text.

**Rule 10 — Bullet point dominance.** "Remove documents where 90% of lines start with bullet points." This targets outline documents, table-of-contents pages, and structured listing pages that contain primarily headings and bullet items without substantive prose. Mathematical text uses bullet points (for itemized examples, listed conditions, etc.) but generally not at 90% density.

**Rule 11 — Minimum character count.** "Filter documents including less than 200 characters after removing spaces and punctuation marks." Extremely short documents — stub pages, error messages, captions without context — provide insufficient signal for language model training. The 200-character threshold is applied after whitespace and punctuation removal to count only meaningful alphanumeric content, since a document consisting of "Loading... Loading... Loading..." (with spaces and punctuation) might pass a raw character threshold.

**Quality annotations.** For documents that pass all eleven rules, the module assigns **per-document quality annotations** (illustrated in Figure 12) that enable downstream users to apply their own custom filters without needing to re-run the pipeline. These annotations include:

- `language_detection_score`: the fastText confidence score (0–1).
- `char_num_after_normalized`: character count after whitespace and punctuation removal.
- `contain_at_least_two_stop_words`: boolean indicating Rule 8 check.
- `ellipsis_line_ratio`: fraction of lines ending in '...' (Rule 5).
- `lines_start_with_bullet_point_ratio`: fraction of lines starting with bullet markers (Rule 10).
- `mean_length_of_alpha_words`: average length of alphabetic words (Rule 7).
- `non_alphabetical_char_ratio`: fraction of non-alphabetic characters (Rule 6).
- `symbols_to_words_ratio`: ratio of mathematical symbols to words (a math-density metric).
- `uppercase_word_ratio`: fraction of words in all-uppercase (Rule 4).

This filtering module **removed approximately 1,100 documents containing 17 million tokens**, a relatively small fraction (0.002% by token count) reflecting that the source-specific preprocessing had already eliminated most low-quality content. The value of the module is not primarily in the volume removed but in the **quality guarantee** it provides: any document in the final corpus has survived eleven tests that encode mathematical domain knowledge about what makes text suitable for language model training.

#### Deduplication Module

With documents collected from six distinct sources — some of which overlap in content (e.g., the same arXiv paper might appear as a web page on a personal homepage, or a StackExchange question might be cross-posted to multiple sites) — deduplication is necessary to prevent the language model from memorizing repeated content and to ensure that training tokens represent diverse mathematical knowledge.

**Algorithm: MinHash LSH.** The authors use the **MinHash Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH)** algorithm (Gionis et al., 1999), a probabilistic method for efficiently finding near-duplicate document pairs in large collections. MinHash LSH works by:

1. **Shingling**: Each document is split into overlapping n-grams (contiguous token sequences). The paper uses **5-grams** based on whitespace tokenization, so the sequence "the set of real" becomes shingles {`the set of real`, `set of real numbers`}.
2. **Hashing**: Each shingle is hashed using the **SHA1** cryptographic hash function, producing a 160-bit hash value.
3. **MinHash computation**: For each document, 9,000 random hash functions are applied to the set of shingle hashes, and the minimum hash value under each function is recorded. These 9,000 minimum values form the document's MinHash signature.
4. **LSH bucketing**: The 9,000 MinHash values are organized into **450 buckets** of **20 minhashes each**. Two documents are considered candidate near-duplicates if they share at least one bucket with identical minhash values — the probability of bucket collision is proportional to the Jaccard similarity of the original shingle sets.
5. **Exact similarity computation**: For candidate pairs, the exact Jaccard similarity is computed by comparing the full shingle sets. Documents exceeding a similarity threshold are marked as near-duplicates.

**Configuration.** The specific parameters follow the **RefinedWeb guidelines** (Penedo et al., 2023) for large-scale web corpus deduplication: 5-gram shingles, SHA1 hashing, 9,000 total minhashes organized as 450 buckets × 20 minhashes. This configuration is empirically validated for balancing precision (correctly identifying true duplicates) and recall (finding most duplicates without prohibitive computation).

**Within-source deduplication results.** Running MinHash LSH within each source independently revealed:

- **304 near-duplicate documents in arXiv** — examples (Table 12) show papers with identical supplementary material sections (lipsum placeholder text) that differ only in the main content, likely template reuse.
- **623 near-duplicates in Common Crawl** — examples (Table 11) show conference abstract pages with overlapping content, one containing English text and another containing a Turkish translation (Özet), both describing the same mathematical talk.
- **83,716 near-duplicates in Wikipedia** — the largest within-source duplication. Manual review revealed that the ZIM dump contained **multiple historical versions of the same article**, each captured at different revision dates. These are all near-duplicates of each other (same article, minor edits). Only one version per article is retained.
- **783 near-duplicates in Textbooks** — "primarily synthetic" duplicates from the OpenPhi project, where the generation process produced multiple similar documents from related prompts. Examples (Table 15) show identical chapter openings ("Basic Concepts in Graph Theory") appearing in multiple synthetic textbooks.
- **144 duplicate questions in StackExchange** — examples (Table 16) show the same question posted on both math.stackexchange.com and mathoverflow.net (a practice the StackExchange community allows for reaching different audiences, but which creates duplicate content for training).
- **Near-duplicates detected but retained in ProofWiki** — despite finding many near-duplicate matches, "manual review revealed... they were differentiated as unique lemmas, proofs, or definitions, leading us to retain these entries." Examples (Table 13) show ProofWiki pages for the "Rule of Idempotence" under different "Instance" numbers (Instance 4 and Instance 5), which share identical theorem structure but contain different truth-table evaluations — genuinely distinct mathematical content.

**Cross-source deduplication.** After within-source deduplication, the authors run MinHash LSH across all sources combined to find cross-source duplicates. The result: "minimal overlap, with a single StackExchange question duplicated in Common Crawl, which was removed." This near-zero cross-source duplication validates the source diversity: each source contributes genuinely distinct content.

Total deduplication eliminated **approximately 714 million tokens**.

**Suffix array experimentation (and rejection).** The authors also experimented with **suffix arrays** (Manber and Myers, 1993) for eliminating exact-match sub-document sequences. Suffix arrays can identify repeated phrases (like "Questions: " appearing at the start of every StackExchange post) and remove templated boilerplate. However, the authors found it "tended to remove common phrases" and "disrupts the contextual integrity of our corpus." The decision to reject suffix array deduplication reflects the paper's quality-over-aggressiveness philosophy: preserving natural language flow is prioritized over removing every repeated phrase.

#### Data Contamination Detection Module

Data contamination — the presence of evaluation examples in the training corpus — is a critical concern for any pre-training dataset, but it is especially severe for mathematical corpora because math benchmark test sets are publicly available on the internet and appear in web crawls, community Q&A sites, and even textbooks (e.g., AMC competition problems that are also MATH benchmark items). Contamination detection is the final quality gate before MATHPILE is released.

**Benchmark test sets scanned.** The authors aggregate test sets from six popular mathematical reasoning benchmarks:

- **GSM8K** (grade-school math word problems)
- **MATH** (competition-level mathematics)
- **MMLU-STEM** (the science, technology, engineering, and math subset of MMLU)
- **AGIEval-SAT-MATH** (SAT math section problems)
- **MathQA** (math word problems with operation-based formalisms)
- **AQuA** (algebraic word problems)

For each benchmark, the **questions and answers** are aggregated into a reference set. For multiple-choice benchmarks (MMLU-STEM, AGIEval, MathQA, AQuA), **only the questions** are considered, not the answer choices — the rationale is that "math problem solutions often involve diverse reasoning steps, making questions easier to detect for contamination in pre-training data due to their more fixed nature." The question text is the invariant; answer choices might be permuted or rephrased without changing the problem.

**Detection method: line-level exact match with MD5 hashing.** The procedure works as follows:

1. **Line splitting**: Every document in the corpus is split into individual lines.
2. **MD5 hashing**: Each line is hashed using the MD5 algorithm. The **first 64 bits** of the hash are retained (to keep hash sets manageable), and **combined with the line text itself** to form a hash set per document. The combination of truncated hash + full text ensures that hash collisions between different lines are virtually impossible while still allowing efficient set-based lookup.
3. **Reference set hashing**: The same procedure is applied to the benchmark test set lines.
4. **Intersection detection**: If any line in the corpus has the same truncated MD5 hash AND identical text as a line in the reference set, that line is flagged as a contamination match. The dual condition (hash match AND text match) prevents false positives from MD5 collisions.
5. **Document-level removal**: Any document containing at least one contamination match is removed from the corpus entirely — not just the matching line — because the surrounding context likely also contains benchmark-related content.

**Results: contamination found and removed.** The detection identified:

- **23 questions from MATH** — found primarily in StackExchange, Textbooks, and Common Crawl. Examples (Table 17) show MATH test set questions appearing verbatim in a textbook of AMC (American Mathematics Competitions) problems, which are also the source for some MATH benchmark items. Example: "What is the sum of the roots of $z^{12}=64$ that have a positive real part?" appears identically in both the MATH benchmark and a textbook document. Common Crawl examples (Table 18) show MATH questions collected on problem aggregation websites (e.g., "Math All Star" with tags like "AMC10/12 AIME IMO US International With Solutions").

- **2 questions from MMLU-STEM** — details of which MMLU subjects are not provided, but the paper states they were found and removed.

- **No contamination** was detected from GSM8K, AGIEval, MathQA, or AQuA.

**Comparison with OpenWebMath.** The authors extend their contamination detection to OpenWebMath (Paster et al., 2023), the concurrent math web corpus, and find:

- **195 MATH test set occurrences** in OpenWebMath (vs. 23 in MATHPILE), though "many were repeats" — the same problem appearing multiple times across different web pages.
- **65 MMLU-STEM occurrences** in OpenWebMath (vs. 2 in MATHPILE).

The paper notes this "aligns with similar findings by Azerbayev et al. [5]" — Llemma's authors also found benchmark contamination in OpenWebMath — and frames it as evidence that "vigilance in creating pre-training corpora" is necessary to avoid "undermining downstream benchmarks."

> **Design choice — why line-level exact match and not fuzzy matching.** The paper uses exact line-level matching, which will miss contamination where the benchmark question is paraphrased, reworded, or embedded in a larger paragraph. The authors implicitly acknowledge this limitation by stating the numbers represent "minimum occurrences, given potential undetected duplicates." Exact match is the most conservative (highest precision) approach — every detected match is a genuine contamination — but it sacrifices recall. The rationale is likely practical: fuzzy matching at scale (n-gram overlap, embedding similarity) would produce many false positives requiring manual review, and the exact-match approach still catches the most egregious contamination (verbatim copies).

#### Continual Pre-Training Experimental Setup

While the primary contribution is the corpus itself, the paper validates MATHPILE through continual pre-training experiments on an existing model. These experiments serve dual purposes: (1) demonstrating that MATHPILE improves mathematical reasoning, and (2) providing ablation evidence that the pipeline's processing decisions (language ID, cleaning, LaTeX display fixes) actually matter.

**Base model.** All experiments use **Mistral-7B-v0.1** (Jiang et al., 2023), which the paper describes as "the state-of-the-art open-source model at the time." Mistral-7B is a 7-billion-parameter dense transformer pre-trained on a general-domain corpus; its baseline mathematical reasoning performance (47.38% on GSM8K, 10.08% on MATH, 47.27% on SAT-MATH, 44.92% on MMLU-MATH) leaves substantial room for improvement through domain-specific continued training.

**Training configuration.** The hyperparameters are:

- **Packing**: Text is "segmented packed text into chunks with a window size of 4,096" — i.e., documents are concatenated with a separator token and then split into training sequences of 4,096 tokens. This is the standard approach for decoder-only transformer pre-training to maximize GPU utilization.
- **Epochs**: 3 epochs over the training data.
- **Global batch size**: 1,024 sequences.
- **Learning rate schedule**: Cosine decay with a **maximum learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-5}$** and **1% warmup steps** (the first 1% of training steps linearly increase the learning rate from 0 to the maximum).
- **Hardware**: NVIDIA A100 8×80GB GPUs.
- **Compute estimate**: The paper notes that "pre-training 10 billion tokens for 1 epoch requires approximately 1,760 NVIDIA A100 GPU hours," contextualizing the resource constraints that limited more extensive experimentation.

**Evaluation protocol.** All models are evaluated using **few-shot prompting with greedy decoding** — meaning the model is given a fixed number of examples (the "few shots") and then asked to produce an answer deterministically (greedy decoding selects the most probable token at each step). This is the standard evaluation paradigm for base language models, avoiding the variance introduced by sampling-based decoding. The benchmarks span the range from basic arithmetic (GSM8K, MathQA) to competition-level mathematics (MATH) to multiple-choice STEM knowledge (MMLU, AGIEval).

**Subset-level experiments.** Rather than training only on the full MATHPILE corpus, the authors conduct individual continual pre-training runs on each major subset to understand their individual contributions:

- **Textbooks** (0.56B tokens trained — the paper reports training on only a portion of the full 0.19B token textbook set, likely due to compute constraints or to isolate effects at comparable scales)
- **Wikipedia** (0.18B tokens)
- **StackExchange** (0.87B tokens)
- **Common Crawl** (1.83B tokens)
- **arXiv** (0.38B tokens)
- **Combined Textbooks + Wikipedia + StackExchange + Common Crawl** (4B tokens)
- **Comparison corpora**: AMPS (1B tokens), DM-Mathematics (5B tokens), sampled OpenWebMath (0.59B tokens)

**Ablation experiments.** To validate the data processing pipeline, the authors use the Wikipedia subset as a testbed:

- **Sampled raw Wikipedia (0.55B tokens)**: Wikipedia documents collected and HTML-converted but NOT passed through global data processing (language identification, cleaning/filtering, deduplication). Training on this version drops GSM8K from 47.38% to 41.92% — a 5.46 percentage point degradation.
- **Full raw Wikipedia (2.18B tokens)**: Same as above but with all raw Wikipedia documents, not just a sample. GSM8K drops further to 32.30% — increasing the volume of unprocessed data **worsens** performance, not improves it. This is the paper's strongest evidence for the "less is more" principle: more tokens of lower quality actively harm the model.
- **Full cleaned but LaTeX-issued Wikipedia (0.23B tokens)**: Processed through the full pipeline but using the buggy `html2text` library that produces garbled LaTeX (Figure 11). GSM8K recovers partially to 47.15%, close to baseline but not an improvement.
- **Full cleaned Wikipedia with correct LaTeX display (0.18B tokens)**: The final, correct version. GSM8K improves to 49.96%, surpassing the baseline.

This ablation progression demonstrates that **every processing stage matters**: raw data hurts, cleaning helps but is undermined by display issues, and the fully correct pipeline yields the maximum benefit from the smallest number of tokens.

**General language benchmark evaluation.** To check for catastrophic forgetting, the authors evaluate the continually pre-trained models on general language benchmarks (Table 20): PIQA, ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, SciQ, and HellaSwag. The results show "no significant degradation" and in some cases improvements (ARC-Challenge from 53.75 to 56.48 for the Wikipedia-trained model, SciQ from 94.0 to 95.6 for the Textbooks-trained model). The authors note the caveat that "when enhancing a model's capabilities in a specific domain, it is typically necessary to mix the new domain-specific training data with the original training data," which their experiments do not do — the fact that performance doesn't catastrophically degrade even without data mixing suggests MATHPILE's mathematical content is compatible with general language understanding.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Framing Corpus Quality as a Source-Adaptive Pipeline Rather Than a Uniform Post-Hoc Filter

The paper's most fundamental intellectual contribution is not any single processing technique but the **principle that mathematical pre-training data requires source-specific curation throughout the entire pipeline, not uniform filtering applied after the fact.** This represents a conceptual shift from how most prior large-scale corpora were constructed.

**What the field did before.** The dominant paradigm in open-source corpus construction — exemplified by C4 (Raffel et al., 2020), Pile (Gao et al., 2021), RedPajama (Together, 2023), SlimPajama (Cerebras, 2023), and RefinedWeb (Penedo et al., 2023) — treats data processing as a **post-hoc filtration problem**: collect a massive heterogeneous dump, apply a uniform set of quality heuristics (language identification with a fixed 0.5 threshold, perplexity filtering, deduplication), and release the result. This approach implicitly assumes that quality signals are domain-invariant — that a rule like "filter documents with fewer than two stop words" works equally well for news articles, code repositories, scientific papers, and mathematical proofs. The paper demonstrates that this assumption fails systematically for mathematics.

**What MATHPILE does differently.** The paper reconceives corpus construction as a **source-adaptive pipeline** where processing decisions are made with knowledge of what each source contains and how its content interacts with each quality filter. This manifests in three interconnected design moves that distinguish MATHPILE from prior work:

**First, the paper treats source-specific preprocessing as a first-class design stage, not an afterthought.** Prior corpora that included multiple sources (Pile, RedPajama) typically applied minimal source-specific processing — basic HTML-to-text conversion, maybe some format-specific cleanup — before feeding everything into a uniform pipeline. MATHPILE inverts this: the source-specific processing for each of the six sources is the most labor-intensive and customized part of the pipeline (Section 2, detailed in Appendix D). arXiv papers get nine LaTeX transformations (comment removal, macro reversion, figure omission with caption retention, acknowledgment and reference stripping) that would be nonsensical for StackExchange posts. StackExchange gets community-score-based quality filtering that has no analog in Wikipedia. Textbooks get Mathpix API OCR that would destroy arXiv's LaTeX source. This per-source investment is expensive — the paper explicitly documents human involvement at each collection stage (Tables 9 and 10) — but the ablation evidence (Table 5: raw vs. cleaned Wikipedia) proves it is not optional.

**Second, the paper introduces source-conditioned thresholds for what would normally be a global parameter.** The language identification module (Section 3.1) is the cleanest example. The standard practice — fastText confidence > 0.5 — was established by CCNet (Wenzek et al., 2020) and adopted by essentially all subsequent corpus work. MATHPILE shows that this threshold **systematically filters out English mathematical documents** because the character n-gram distribution of LaTeX-heavy text confuses fastText's classifier, which was trained on general-domain text. The solution — relaxing the threshold to 0.1 for Wikipedia and StackExchange, 0.3 for arXiv, while keeping 0.5 for Common Crawl — is conceptually simple but philosophically significant: it acknowledges that the classifier's confidence is not an absolute quality measure but a domain-dependent signal whose interpretation depends on what kind of text is being classified. No prior corpus paper makes this distinction explicit or tunes per-source thresholds.

**Third, the paper treats deduplication aggressiveness as a source-dependent decision requiring manual content review, not an algorithmic parameter to be optimized globally.** The ProofWiki case is the clearest illustration. A uniform MinHash LSH pipeline with a fixed similarity threshold would have identified the ProofWiki near-duplicates (Table 13: "Rule of Idempotence" under Instance 4 vs. Instance 5) and removed one. The authors instead **manually reviewed** these matches and determined that sharing a theorem structure does not make them duplicates — they contain distinct truth-table evaluations and represent genuinely different mathematical content. This human-in-the-loop decision is fundamentally anti-scale: it cannot be automated and does not generalize. But by making it and documenting it, the paper establishes a principle: for specialized mathematical content, surface-level textual similarity is not a reliable indicator of semantic redundancy. The field's default assumption — that near-duplicate text ≈ near-duplicate information — is false for structured mathematical exposition.

**Significance beyond raw performance.** This source-adaptive framing changes how corpus builders should think about their task. It implies that the quality of a mathematical corpus is not primarily a function of how aggressively you filter but of how well you understand each source's structure and failure modes. It also implies that documentation — the datasheet (Table 6), the quality annotations (Figure 12), the explicit listing of human interventions (Tables 9 and 10) — is not a supplementary nice-to-have but a necessary component of responsible corpus construction, because downstream users need to know which decisions were made and why to understand the corpus's biases and limitations. This is a **fundamental shift** in how corpus papers position their contribution: from "here is a large dataset" to "here is a documented process for building a high-quality dataset, with evidence that each processing stage matters."

The evidence for this framing's importance is primarily in the ablation results (Table 5), where skipping the pipeline stages (raw Wikipedia, 0.55B tokens) degrades GSM8K performance from 47.38% to 41.92%, while the fully processed version (0.18B tokens, roughly 3× fewer tokens) improves it to 49.96%. This is not just "quality matters" — the baseline Mistral model already had a quality advantage from its own training data. The fact that adding unprocessed data makes things worse while adding less-but-processed data makes things better is direct evidence that the pipeline is doing something the base model's training did not, and that something is source-adaptive processing.

### Innovation 2: Data Contamination Detection as a Proactive Corpus Construction Step, Not a Post-Hoc Audit

The paper's treatment of data contamination represents a methodological innovation in how corpus papers approach benchmark integrity. Rather than treating contamination detection as an after-the-fact analysis performed by model builders (as GPT-2, GPT-3, FLAN, and LLaMA-2 all did), MATHPILE makes it a **mandatory pre-release step integrated into the corpus construction pipeline**, and further extends it to auditing competing corpora.

**What the field did before.** The standard practice in the field — established by GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2019) and continued through GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020), FLAN (Wei et al., 2022), LLaMA-2 (Touvron et al., 2023), and others — is to perform contamination analysis **post-hoc, after the model is trained**, using n-gram overlap between the training corpus and benchmark test sets. This is problematic for several reasons that the paper addresses:

- **Irreversibility**: Once a model has been trained on contaminated data, the damage cannot be undone — the benchmark evaluation is compromised, and any claims about the model's capabilities on that benchmark are unreliable. The paper frames this explicitly: "We advocate for early contamination detection during dataset creation to prevent irreversible damage as delaying exacerbates issues."

- **Opaqueness to downstream users**: When a corpus is released without contamination analysis, every model builder who uses it must either perform their own analysis (duplicating work) or risk unknowingly training on contaminated data. By performing and documenting the detection as part of corpus release, MATHPILE removes this burden from the community.

- **Inconsistency across corpora**: Without a standard for what constitutes contamination and how aggressively it should be removed, different corpora have different contamination profiles — making it impossible to fairly compare models trained on them.

**What MATHPILE does differently.** The paper makes three distinctive moves:

**First, contamination detection is a pipeline stage, not an audit.** In MATHPILE's processing workflow (Figure 2), contamination detection appears alongside language identification, cleaning, and deduplication as a required step before corpus release. The paper treats it with the same rigor as other quality stages: a specific algorithm (line-level exact match with MD5 hashing), specific benchmarks scanned (six mathematical reasoning test sets), clear scope (questions + answers for generative benchmarks, questions only for multiple-choice), and documented outcomes (23 MATH items, 2 MMLU-STEM items found and removed). This integration into the corpus construction workflow — rather than as a separate analysis performed later — makes contamination prevention a design constraint, not an optional check.

**Second, the paper performs comparative contamination analysis across corpora.** By extending their detection to OpenWebMath and finding 195 MATH occurrences and 65 MMLU-STEM occurrences (vs. MATHPILE's 23 and 2), the paper demonstrates that contamination is not a theoretical concern but a **pervasive and unevenly distributed problem** across mathematical corpora. The comparison makes MATHPILE's low contamination count meaningful — without it, 23 MATH occurrences would be just a number; with the OpenWebMath comparison, it becomes evidence of a more careful construction process. This comparative approach is rare in corpus papers, which typically report only their own contamination statistics (if they report any).

**Third, the paper connects contamination to corpus composition choices.** The detection results reveal that contamination sources are predictable: MATH benchmark items appear in "Textbooks" (AMC competition problem books that are also the source for MATH's problem set) and Common Crawl (problem aggregation websites that collect competition problems). This is not random leakage — it reflects structural overlap between the sources MATH's authors used to construct their benchmark (competition problem archives) and the sources MATHPILE's authors used to construct their corpus (the same archives, via textbooks and web pages). By documenting this, the paper helps the community understand *why* contamination occurs and which sources are highest-risk, enabling better corpus design in the future.

**Significance beyond raw performance.** This proactive approach to contamination is a **fundamental methodological contribution** to corpus construction practice. It shifts the burden of contamination prevention from model trainers (who may not have the resources or awareness to perform thorough detection) to corpus builders (who control the data and can remove contamination at the source). If adopted as standard practice — and the paper explicitly advocates for this — it would make benchmark evaluations more reliable across the entire field, since models trained on different corpora would all start from a contamination-free baseline.

The paper also implicitly makes the case that **contamination detection should be benchmark-specific, not generic.** General n-gram deduplication (which MATHPILE already applies via MinHash LSH) catches exact document-level duplicates but misses the line-level benchmark overlaps that this module finds. The fact that the general deduplication module (Section 3.3) and the contamination detection module (Section 3.4) found different things — deduplication caught 714M tokens of general near-duplicates, contamination detection found 25 specific benchmark items that deduplication missed — demonstrates that these are distinct concerns requiring distinct tools.

### Innovation 3: Empirical Refutation of "Synthetic Data as a Standalone Pre-Training Substitute"

The paper's most striking negative result — that continual pre-training on AMPS and DM-Mathematics, two established synthetic math corpora, **collapses model performance** rather than improving it — challenges a growing narrative in the field that synthetic data can substitute for curated real data in pre-training.

**What the field assumed.** The success of models like Phi-1 (Gunasekar et al., 2023), which was trained entirely on synthetically generated textbooks, created a narrative that high-quality synthetic data could break scaling laws and replace the need for massive web-crawled corpora. The concurrent release of OpenPhi (the open-source Phi replication) and the paper's own inclusion of synthetic textbooks via OpenPhi reflect this enthusiasm. The implicit assumption was that synthetic data is "clean" — free of the noise, duplication, and quality issues that plague web data — and therefore superior for training, especially when generated by capable models.

**What MATHPILE found.** The continual pre-training results in Table 4 directly contradict this assumption for the math domain:

- **AMPS** (1B tokens): Mistral's GSM8K drops from 47.38% to **0.08%**. MATH drops from 10.08% to 0.82%. MMLU-MATH drops from 44.92% to 0.47%. These are not small degradations — they represent catastrophic forgetting where the model essentially loses its mathematical reasoning ability.

- **DM-Mathematics** (5B tokens): GSM8K drops to **0.00%**. MATH drops to 0.00%. SAT-MATH drops to 0.00%. MMLU-MATH drops to 0.00%. The model's mathematical capabilities are completely destroyed across all benchmarks.

The paper's interpretation — that these datasets have a "narrow, monotonous structure" making them "unsuitable for standalone pre-training" — identifies the failure mode: the synthetic data's format (isolated problem statements followed by solutions) trains the model to expect a distribution where every sequence is a self-contained question-answer pair, overwriting its ability to handle the more varied formats and reasoning patterns needed for benchmark evaluation. This is not a quality problem (the synthetic data is presumably correct and well-formatted) but a **distributional mismatch problem** — the synthetic data distribution is too narrow to maintain the model's general mathematical capabilities.

**Why this is significant beyond one negative result.** The finding challenges the field's enthusiasm for synthetic pre-training data at a conceptual level:

- **It distinguishes synthetic data quality from synthetic data diversity.** The synthetic datasets are high-quality in the sense of being correct and clean, but low-diversity in their structure (every example is a problem-solution pair). The negative result shows that structural diversity — having documents of different lengths, formats, and rhetorical structures (definitions, proofs, explanations, discussions, problem sets) — matters independently of factual correctness. A corpus of 1 billion tokens that are all formatted identically is worse than 0.18 billion tokens of varied format (compare AMPS at 1B dropping GSM8K to 0.08% vs. cleaned Wikipedia at 0.18B improving it to 49.96%).

- **It demonstrates that synthetic data requires careful mixing, not solo use.** The paper notes that "such datasets generally yield better results when combined with broader corpora for pre-training," citing Xu et al. (2024). This reframes synthetic data from a replacement for curated data to a supplement that amplifies curated data — useful when mixed in appropriate proportions, harmful when used alone. This is a more nuanced and empirically grounded position than either the "synthetic data solves everything" or "synthetic data is useless" extremes.

- **It provides an existence proof that more tokens can hurt.** The field's scaling laws narrative (more data → better performance, modulo quality) is so dominant that the possibility of data that *actively degrades* a pre-trained model is underappreciated. The AMPS and DM-Mathematics results are clear counterexamples: giving the model an additional 5 billion tokens of math content destroyed capabilities it already had. This is the strongest form of the paper's "less is more" argument — it's not just that low-quality data provides diminishing returns, but that wrong-format data can cause catastrophic regression.

**Comparison with the Phi narrative.** The Phi-1 result (training from scratch on synthetic textbooks achieves strong performance) might seem to contradict MATHPILE's finding, but the experimental paradigms differ crucially: Phi-1 is trained from scratch, so the synthetic data distribution is the only distribution it learns; Mistral is continually pre-trained, so the synthetic data must integrate with an existing distribution. The MATHPILE result suggests that synthetic data's benefits for pre-training may depend on it being the primary training signal (as in Phi-1) rather than a post-hoc addition to a general model — a hypothesis the paper does not explore but that follows naturally from the negative result.

This finding is a **fundamental cautionary result** rather than an incremental improvement. It doesn't make MATHPILE look better on a leaderboard; it warns the community about a failure mode of synthetic pre-training data that, without these experiments, would remain hidden. Negative results of this clarity are rare in the corpus literature and valuable precisely because they challenge emerging conventional wisdom.

### Innovation 4: Quality Annotations as a Corpus Release Standard, Enabling Downstream Customization Without Re-Processing

The paper's release format — including per-document quality annotations (language identification scores, symbol-to-word ratios, uppercase word ratios, mean word length, ellipsis line ratios, etc.) — represents an innovation in **how corpora are distributed and used**, moving from a static artifact model to a configurable resource model.

**What the field did before.** Prior open-source corpora (Pile, C4, RedPajama, SlimPajama, OpenWebMath, ProofPile) were released as static datasets: the corpus builders made all filtering decisions, and downstream users received a fixed set of documents. If a user wanted to apply different quality thresholds — say, more aggressive filtering of uppercase-heavy documents, or retention of documents that the corpus builders filtered but the user considers valuable — they had to re-process the raw data from scratch. This is expensive (requiring the same compute budget as the original corpus construction) and often impossible (raw data may not be distributed, or processing scripts may not be released). The result is that downstream users are locked into the corpus builders' quality decisions, which may not match their use case.

**What MATHPILE does differently.** For documents from Wikipedia and Common Crawl, MATHPILE releases **per-document quality annotations** — numerical scores for each heuristic filter applied during the cleaning stage (Section 3.2, Figure 12). These annotations include:

- `language_detection_score`: fastText confidence (0–1)
- `char_num_after_normalized`: content length after stripping whitespace and punctuation
- `contain_at_least_two_stop_words`: boolean (Rule 8)
- `ellipsis_line_ratio`: fraction of lines ending in "..." (Rule 5)
- `lines_start_with_bullet_point_ratio`: fraction of bullet-point lines (Rule 10)
- `mean_length_of_alpha_words`: average alphabetic word length (Rule 7)
- `non_alphabetical_char_ratio`: fraction of non-alphabetic characters (Rule 6)
- `symbols_to_words_ratio`: ratio of mathematical symbols to natural language words
- `uppercase_word_ratio`: fraction of all-caps words (Rule 4)

This is significant because it transforms MATHPILE from a **fixed corpus** into a **configurable corpus**. A downstream user can apply their own thresholds — e.g., excluding documents with `uppercase_word_ratio > 0.3` instead of the paper's 0.4 threshold, or filtering for documents with `mean_length_of_alpha_words > 3.5` — without re-running any of the expensive preprocessing, language identification, or deduplication stages. The annotation scheme also enables **research on the relationship between document-level features and downstream model performance**: a user could train multiple models on different subsets of MATHPILE (filtered by different annotation thresholds) and measure which features are most predictive of training benefit.

**Why this is a fundamental shift in corpus distribution philosophy.** The paper treats corpus construction as producing not just the final cleaned documents but also **the metadata that enables others to make different cleaning decisions**. This aligns with the broader trend in machine learning toward data-centric AI — the idea that improving data is as important as improving models — and with the paper's own emphasis on transparency and documentation. It also implicitly acknowledges that the paper's heuristic thresholds are empirical choices (as the limitations section states: "Many detailed decisions in its creation were made empirically, which may not always be optimal") and that different use cases may warrant different thresholds.

**Comparison with datasheet practices.** The field has moved toward datasheets for datasets (Gebru et al., 2021) as a transparency mechanism — and MATHPILE provides a thorough datasheet (Table 6) — but datasheets describe the data at a high level. Per-document quality annotations are a different and complementary form of transparency: they provide **operational metadata** that enables computational customization, not just human understanding. A datasheet tells you that filtering was applied; quality annotations let you apply different filtering.

**The significance is in enabling future work.** By releasing the annotations, MATHPILE lowers the barrier for researchers who want to investigate questions like: "What is the optimal symbol-to-word ratio for mathematical pre-training data?" or "Does training on long mathematical documents (high `char_num_after_normalized`) produce better theorem-proving capabilities than training on short problem-solution pairs?" These questions cannot be investigated with a static corpus where only the final filtered documents are available. The annotation release makes MATHPILE not just a dataset but a **research instrument** for studying mathematical pre-training data quality — a contribution that extends beyond the specific corpus to enable a broader research program.

This innovation is **incremental in concept** (quality annotations are not new in database design) but **fundamental in practice** (no prior math corpus release includes them, and the paper establishes a standard that future corpus releases can adopt or be measured against). It is the kind of contribution that changes how subsequent work is done rather than achieving a one-time performance improvement.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021), a collection of high-school competition-level mathematical problems. The paper also evaluates on GSM8K (grade-school math word problems), MMLU-STEM (the science/technology/engineering/math subset of MMLU), AGIEval-SAT-MATH, MathQA, and AQuA to assess varying levels of mathematical reasoning capability.

- **Base model(s).** Mistral-7B-v0.1 (Jiang et al., 2023), a 7-billion-parameter dense transformer described as "the state-of-the-art open-source model at the time" of the experiments. The model was chosen because its baseline mathematical reasoning performance — 47.38% on GSM8K, 10.08% on MATH, 47.27% on SAT-MATH, 44.92% on MMLU-MATH, 23.51% on MathQA, 27.95% on AQuA — leaves substantial room for domain-specific continued training to demonstrate improvement.

- **Metrics.** Accuracy (%) is reported for all benchmarks, measured as the fraction of test set questions where the model's generated answer matches the ground-truth answer under the grading function appropriate to each benchmark (e.g., the MATH grading function released by Hendrycks et al., 2021). For general language benchmarks (Table 20), the paper uses the `acc_norm` metric from EleutherAI's lm-evaluation-harness.

- **Baselines.** The paper's primary baseline is **Mistral-7B-v0.1 without any continued pre-training**, which provides the starting performance for all comparisons. For corpus-level comparisons, the baselines include: **AMPS** (Hendrycks et al., 2021), a synthetic K-12 problem set (~0.7B tokens in total; 1B tokens used in experiments); **DM-Mathematics** (Saxton et al., 2019), a synthetic math competition problem set (~4.4B tokens; 5B tokens used); and a **randomly sampled subset of OpenWebMath** (Paster et al., 2023) containing 0.59B tokens, cleansed of detected benchmark leakage before training. The paper also compares against Oracle baselines in the ablation context: raw (unprocessed) Wikipedia sampled at 0.55B and full at 2.18B tokens, and processed Wikipedia with LaTeX display issues (0.23B tokens).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For continual pre-training experiments, the generation budget is measured in **tokens trained**, with subset-level experiments training on specific token counts: Textbooks (0.56B), Wikipedia (0.18B), StackExchange (0.87B), Common Crawl (1.83B), arXiv (0.38B), combined Textbooks/Wikipedia/StackExchange/CC (4B), AMPS (1B), DM-Mathematics (5B), and sampled OpenWebMath (0.59B). Training uses 3 epochs with global batch size 1,024 and sequence window size 4,096 on NVIDIA A100 8×80GB GPUs. The paper notes that "pre-training 10 billion tokens for 1 epoch requires approximately 1,760 NVIDIA A100 GPU hours." For evaluation, all models use few-shot prompting with greedy decoding — a deterministic protocol that avoids sampling variance.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ formal cross-validation for model evaluation — each continual pre-training configuration is trained once and evaluated on the full benchmark test sets. No error bars or confidence intervals are reported across multiple random seeds, which the authors acknowledge in the checklist: "Did you report error bars (e.g., with respect to the random seed after running experiments multiple times)? [No]." The ablation experiments on data processing use the same Mistral-7B-v0.1 starting checkpoint for all configurations, making comparisons internally consistent even without statistical intervals, but the absence of multi-seed reporting means the observed differences (sometimes as small as 1–2 percentage points) should be interpreted as single-point estimates with unknown variance.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Subset-Level Continual Pre-Training on MATHPILE Components

The headline finding from Table 4 is that **continual pre-training on individual MATHPILE subsets generally improves mathematical reasoning across diverse benchmarks, but the pattern of improvement is highly subset-dependent and non-uniform.** No single subset improves all benchmarks; instead, each source contributes gains on different evaluation dimensions in ways that reflect its content composition.

**Textbooks (0.56B tokens trained)** produces the broadest improvements, with gains on five of six benchmarks: GSM8K rises from 47.38% to 48.97% (+1.59), MATH from 10.08% to 12.10% (+2.02), SAT-MATH from 47.27% to 56.36% (+9.09), MMLU-MATH from 44.92% to 48.93% (+4.01), MathQA from 23.51% to 30.38% (+6.87), and AQuA from 27.95% to 33.07% (+5.12). The 9.09 percentage point gain on SAT-MATH is the largest single-benchmark improvement observed in any subset experiment and is notable because SAT-MATH problems involve grade-appropriate mathematical reasoning with clear problem statements — precisely the format that textbooks are designed to teach.

**Wikipedia (0.18B tokens)** produces the strongest individual gains on AQuA (27.95% to 35.43%, +7.48) and GSM8K (47.38% to 49.96%, +2.58), while improving SAT-MATH (+6.36) and MMLU-MATH (+2.24), but produces a slight regression on MATH (10.08% to 9.96%, −0.12) and a smaller gain on MathQA (+5.46). The Wikipedia results are particularly significant because they come from only 0.18 billion tokens — approximately 3× fewer than the Textbooks training budget — yet produce comparable or larger improvements on several benchmarks. This provides the clearest quantitative evidence for the paper's "less is more" claim: **0.18B tokens of carefully processed Wikipedia data produce more consistent improvements than 0.56B tokens of textbook data on some benchmarks, and dramatically outperform 0.55B tokens of raw (unprocessed) Wikipedia data which degrades performance (Table 5, discussed in ablations).**

**StackExchange (0.87B tokens)** produces mixed results: regression on GSM8K (47.38% to 43.06%, −4.32), modest improvement on MATH (10.08% to 11.66%, +1.58), no change on SAT-MATH (47.27% to 47.27%), regression on MMLU-MATH (44.92% to 43.51%, −1.41), improvement on MathQA (23.51% to 27.67%, +4.16), and improvement on AQuA (27.95% to 30.70%, +2.75). The GSM8K regression is attributed by the authors to the observation that "community users rarely ask basic arithmetic questions on StackExchange" — the corpus's content distribution (undergraduate-to-research-level Q&A) does not cover the grade-school arithmetic patterns that GSM8K tests. This is a content-coverage limitation rather than a quality problem: StackExchange text is high-quality but topically mismatched to elementary math benchmarks.

**Common Crawl (1.83B tokens)** shows moderate gains on SAT-MATH (47.27% to 50.45%, +3.18) and AQuA (27.95% to 31.88%, +3.93), with marginal gains on GSM8K (+? — the paper reports 45.56%, a regression of −1.82), regression on MATH (10.08% to 9.88%, −0.20), improvement on MMLU-MATH (44.92% to 45.17%, +0.25), and improvement on MathQA (23.51% to 25.79%, +2.28). The modest results despite 1.83B tokens — the largest single-source training budget — suggest that Common Crawl mathematical content is more diluted or lower-signal than curated sources, consistent with the paper's characterization of it as "the broadest and noisiest source."

**arXiv (0.38B tokens)** produces the weakest results: slight improvement on GSM8K (47.38% to 47.91%, +0.53), substantial regression on MATH (10.08% to 7.50%, −2.58), regression on SAT-MATH (47.27% to 42.72%, −4.55), slight improvement on MMLU-MATH (44.92% to 46.34%, +1.42), regression on MathQA (23.51% to 18.05%, −5.46), and regression on AQuA (27.95% to 27.55%, −0.40). The paper attributes arXiv's poor showing to "the disparity between the math knowledge present in arXiv papers and that required for the downstream benchmarks" — academic research papers contain advanced mathematics expressed through theorem-proving and proof-writing, not the problem-solving patterns that benchmarks evaluate. This negative result is important: it demonstrates that **domain relevance is not just about topic (mathematics) but about format and cognitive task alignment.** arXiv contains high-quality mathematics that is well-suited for learning theorem-proving capabilities but ill-suited for learning to solve specific math problems under the few-shot evaluation paradigm.

**Combined Textbooks + Wikipedia + StackExchange + Common Crawl (4B tokens)** shows improvements on GSM8K (47.38% to 49.88%, +2.50) and MATH (10.08% to 11.70%, +1.62), but regressions on SAT-MATH (47.27% to 43.18%, −4.09), MMLU-MATH (44.92% to 43.75%, −1.17), MathQA (23.51% to 23.24%, −0.27), and AQuA (27.95% to 25.19%, −2.76). This is a critical result: **combining subsets does not produce additive improvements**, and in several cases the combination performs worse than the best individual subset. For example, Textbooks alone improved SAT-MATH by +9.09 points, but the combined corpus degrades it by −4.09 points. This suggests that different subsets may have competing or interfering effects when trained sequentially — the model may benefit from textbook-style content but then partially unlearn those benefits when subsequently exposed to StackExchange or Common Crawl data that has different formatting or reasoning patterns. The paper does not deeply investigate this interaction, but the result implies that data mixing strategies (how to combine subsets, in what proportions and order) are consequential and non-trivial.

#### Comparison with Existing Corpora (Synthetic and Web-Only)

The paper directly compares MATHPILE subsets against three alternative corpora in Table 4, and the results are striking:

**AMPS (1B tokens) catastrophically degrades performance on all benchmarks.** GSM8K drops from 47.38% to 0.08%, MATH from 10.08% to 0.82%, SAT-MATH from 47.27% to 3.18%, MMLU-MATH from 44.92% to 0.47%, MathQA from 23.51% to 10.99%, and AQuA from 27.95% to 8.27%. These are not marginal regressions — the model essentially loses its ability to solve math problems across all evaluation dimensions. The mechanism, as discussed in the prior sections, is that AMPS's narrow format (isolated problem-solution pairs at K-12 level) overwrites Mistral's general mathematical reasoning capabilities with a distribution that is too narrow to support benchmark generalization.

**DM-Mathematics (5B tokens) completely destroys mathematical performance.** Every benchmark drops to 0.00% or effectively zero: GSM8K 0.00%, MATH 0.00%, SAT-MATH 0.00%, MMLU-MATH 0.00%, MathQA 0.00%, AQuA 0.00%. Training on 5 billion tokens of synthetic competition math problems eliminates all mathematical reasoning capability. This is the strongest possible negative result: it demonstrates that not only is synthetic data not a substitute for curated real data, but that at sufficient scale it can be actively destructive. The 5B token budget makes this result particularly significant because it rules out the hypothesis that training longer on synthetic data might eventually recover — there is no recovery, only complete collapse.

**Sampled OpenWebMath (0.59B tokens)** produces mixed and generally weak results: regression on GSM8K (47.38% to 43.21%, −4.17), regression on MATH (10.08% to 7.86%, −2.22), marginal improvement on SAT-MATH (47.27% to 47.72%, +0.45), improvement on MMLU-MATH (44.92% to 47.52%, +2.60), regression on MathQA (23.51% to 21.80%, −1.71), and regression on AQuA (27.95% to 24.80%, −3.15). OpenWebMath's performance is notably weaker than comparably-sized MATHPILE subsets: Textbooks at 0.56B tokens improves MATH from 10.08% to 12.10% while OpenWebMath at 0.59B drops it to 7.86%; Wikipedia at 0.18B tokens improves SAT-MATH by +6.36 points while OpenWebMath at 0.59B improves it by only +0.45. The paper interprets this as evidence that "curated sources" (textbooks, encyclopedic Wikipedia) provide more training signal per token than web-only sources (OpenWebMath is exclusively from Common Crawl web pages). However, the comparison is not perfectly controlled: the OpenWebMath subset was randomly sampled and cleansed of detected benchmark leakage post-hoc, while MATHPILE subsets underwent the full pipeline from the start. The performance gap could reflect either inherent source quality differences or the effect of the more thorough processing pipeline.

#### Data Processing Pipeline Ablation: The Wikipedia Testbed

The Wikipedia ablation results in Table 5 are the paper's strongest quantitative evidence that **the data processing pipeline is not just beneficial but essential, and that more data without processing actively harms performance.** The progression from raw to fully processed Wikipedia reveals each stage's contribution:

**Sampled raw Wikipedia (0.55B tokens)** — documents collected from the Wikipedia dump and converted to Markdown but passing through none of the global data processing stages (no language identification, no cleaning/filtering, no deduplication) — degrades GSM8K from 47.38% to 41.92% (−5.46), MATH from 10.08% to 6.28% (−3.80), SAT-MATH from 47.27% to 20.90% (−26.37), MMLU-MATH from 44.92% to 23.70% (−21.22), MathQA from 23.51% to 24.72% (+1.21), and AQuA from 27.95% to 24.01% (−3.94). The SAT-MATH degradation of 26.37 points and MMLU-MATH degradation of 21.22 points are catastrophic regressions, showing that unprocessed Wikipedia contains noise that actively interferes with mathematical reasoning capabilities.

**Full raw Wikipedia (2.18B tokens)** — all Wikipedia documents without processing — worsens performance further: GSM8K drops to 32.30% (−15.08 from baseline, −9.62 from sampled raw), MATH to 4.48% (−5.60), SAT-MATH to 13.64% (−33.63), MMLU-MATH to 25.59% (−19.33), MathQA to 27.04% (+3.53), and AQuA to 23.62% (−4.33). Critically, **increasing the volume of unprocessed data by 4× (from 0.55B to 2.18B tokens) makes performance worse, not better**, on every benchmark except MathQA (which shows a small additional gain). This directly contradicts the scaling assumption that more data always helps and provides the strongest evidence for the paper's "less is more" thesis: 0.18B tokens of processed Wikipedia improve GSM8K to 49.96%, while 2.18B tokens of raw data degrade it to 32.30% — a 17.66 percentage point difference stemming entirely from processing decisions rather than data volume.

**Full cleaned but LaTeX-issued Wikipedia (0.23B tokens)** — processed through the full pipeline but using the `html2text` library that produces garbled LaTeX display (illustrated in Figure 11: `{\\\\displaystyle...}` instead of proper LaTeX) — partially recovers performance: GSM8K rises to 47.15% (still −0.23 from baseline), MATH rises to 8.58% (−1.50), SAT-MATH to 46.81% (−0.46), MMLU-MATH to 42.92% (−2.00), MathQA to 21.00% (−2.51), and AQuA to 31.88% (+3.93). The substantial recovery from the raw versions confirms that the cleaning and filtering rules are effective, but the failure to exceed baseline on most benchmarks shows that the LaTeX display issue undermined the training signal. The model is learning from garbled mathematical notation, which degrades rather than enhances its mathematical reasoning.

**Full cleaned Wikipedia with correct LaTeX display (0.18B tokens)** — the final, fully corrected version — improves GSM8K to 49.96% (+2.58 over baseline), MATH to 9.96% (−0.12, essentially baseline), SAT-MATH to 53.63% (+6.36), MMLU-MATH to 47.16% (+2.24), MathQA to 28.97% (+5.46), and AQuA to 35.43% (+7.48). The comparison between this row and the LaTeX-issued row isolates the effect of fixing the display bug: SAT-MATH improves by 6.82 points (46.81% to 53.63%), AQuA improves by 3.55 points (31.88% to 35.43%), and MathQA improves by 7.97 points (21.00% to 28.97%) — all from a single library change that corrects LaTeX rendering. This demonstrates that mathematical notation fidelity is not a cosmetic concern but a first-order determinant of training effectiveness.

The overall trajectory — raw data degrades (41.92%), more raw data degrades further (32.30%), cleaned data recovers (47.15%), correct-LaTeX data improves beyond baseline (49.96%) — provides a clean causal chain: **each processing stage contributes independently to model quality, and skipping any stage leaves performance on the table or actively harms it.**

#### General Language Benchmark Evaluation (Table 20)

To verify that continual pre-training on MATHPILE does not cause catastrophic forgetting of general language capabilities, the paper evaluates the original Mistral-7B and three continually pre-trained variants (Textbooks, Wikipedia, StackExchange) on PIQA, ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, SciQ, and HellaSwag. The results show that **general language performance is largely preserved, with some modest fluctuations:**

- **PIQA**: Mistral baseline 81.93%; Textbooks 80.14% (−1.79); Wikipedia 80.57% (−1.36); StackExchange 80.41% (−1.52). All variants show small regressions.
- **ARC-Challenge**: Mistral baseline 53.75%; Textbooks 52.73% (−1.02); Wikipedia 56.48% (+2.73); StackExchange 49.66% (−4.09). Wikipedia notably improves, while StackExchange shows a meaningful regression.
- **ARC-Easy**: Mistral baseline 79.58%; Textbooks 79.92% (+0.34); Wikipedia 79.71% (+0.13); StackExchange 75.38% (−4.20). StackExchange again shows the largest regression.
- **SciQ**: Mistral baseline 94.0%; Textbooks 95.6% (+1.6); Wikipedia 94.8% (+0.8); StackExchange 90.5% (−3.5). Textbooks and Wikipedia improve, StackExchange regresses.
- **HellaSwag**: Mistral baseline 81.05%; Textbooks 81.15% (+0.10); Wikipedia 82.07% (+1.02); StackExchange 82.87% (+1.82). All variants show small improvements.

The paper notes the caveat that "when enhancing a model's capabilities in a specific domain, it is typically necessary to mix the new domain-specific training data with the original training data to prevent catastrophic forgetting" — and that their experiments did not do this. Given this, the generally stable results (no benchmark collapses, unlike the AMPS/DM-Mathematics cases) suggest MATHPILE content is sufficiently compatible with general language understanding that forgetting is mild. StackExchange's consistent regression across several benchmarks (ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, SciQ) is notable and may reflect its more specialized Q&A format pulling the model's distribution away from general reasoning patterns.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Language identification threshold tuning:** The paper does not present a formal ablation table varying the per-source fastText thresholds, but the qualitative finding — that "a considerable number of false positives" occur when using the standard 0.5 threshold on mathematical text — is supported by the downstream performance of corpora processed with different thresholds. The fact that Wikipedia and StackExchange (threshold 0.1) and arXiv (threshold 0.3) produce effective training data while using relaxed thresholds is indirect evidence that the standard 0.5 threshold would have excluded valuable English mathematical content. A direct ablation comparing 0.5 vs. tuned thresholds on a held-out set would strengthen this claim but is not presented.

**MinHash LSH deduplication configuration:** The paper does not ablate the MinHash parameters (5-grams, 450 buckets, 20 minhashes each). The configuration is adopted from RefinedWeb (Penedo et al., 2023) without additional tuning, which is reasonable given that RefinedWeb validated these parameters on web-scale data, but means the paper cannot claim that its deduplication is optimal for mathematical text. The manual review intervention (retaining ProofWiki near-duplicates) is a human-in-the-loop override rather than an algorithmic ablation.

**Suffix array deduplication rejection:** The paper explicitly ablated suffix array deduplication (for exact-match sub-document sequence removal) and rejected it because "it tended to remove common phrases like 'Questions: '" and "disrupts the contextual integrity of our corpus." This is a negative result reported qualitatively rather than with a comparison table, but it demonstrates that the authors considered a more aggressive deduplication approach and made an informed decision based on observed document quality rather than applying it blindly.

**LaTeX display library comparison (Figure 11, Table 5):** The comparison between `html2text` (buggy LaTeX rendering) and `Resiliparse` with DOM parsing (correct rendering) is directly ablated in the Wikipedia pipeline. The performance difference is substantial: SAT-MATH improves from 46.81% to 53.63% (+6.82), AQuA from 31.88% to 35.43% (+3.55), MathQA from 21.00% to 28.97% (+7.97). This is a non-obvious finding — one might expect that a language model would be robust to minor formatting variations in LaTeX — and demonstrates that mathematical notation fidelity is a first-order quality concern that corpus builders in other domains (code, scientific text) should attend to.

**Contamination detection scope:** The paper scans six benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, AGIEval-SAT-MATH, MathQA, AQuA) but does not report results for benchmarks it did not scan. The finding that OpenWebMath has substantially more contamination (195 MATH items vs. 23) is a robustness check on MATHPILE's construction process but does not itself demonstrate that contamination removal improved downstream model performance — the models evaluated in Table 4 were trained on contamination-removed data, but there is no ablation comparing training on contaminated vs. decontaminated versions of the same corpus. Such an ablation would directly quantify the performance impact of benchmark leakage but is ethically problematic (it would require intentionally training on test-set-contaminated data). The paper's approach — proactively removing contamination and comparing contamination rates across corpora — is the responsible alternative.

**Epoch count:** All continual pre-training experiments use 3 epochs. This is a fixed choice; the paper does not ablate epoch count to determine whether more passes over the same data would further improve or degrade performance. Given the finding that AMPS and DM-Mathematics degrade performance dramatically (suggesting overfitting to narrow distributions), epoch count could interact with data diversity in important ways — narrow datasets degrade faster with more epochs — but this interaction is unexplored.

**General language benchmarks scope:** The general benchmark evaluation (Table 20) covers five benchmarks but only for three of the subset-trained models (Textbooks, Wikipedia, StackExchange). The arXiv, CommonCrawl, and combined-subset models are not evaluated on general benchmarks, leaving open the possibility that these configurations cause more severe forgetting that went undetected. The combined model's mixed results on math benchmarks (improving GSM8K and MATH but regressing on SAT-MATH, MMLU-MATH, MathQA, AQuA) make it a particularly important candidate for general benchmark evaluation, but this data is absent.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim: "MATHPILE generally enhances the performance of language models across various mathematical reasoning benchmarks, with an average improvement of up to 5%"

The experimental evidence supports a **more qualified version** of this claim than the abstract's phrasing suggests. Looking across all six benchmarks and all subset configurations in Table 4, only the **Textbooks** and **Wikipedia** subsets produce improvements on a majority of benchmarks (Textbooks improves 5/6, Wikipedia improves 4/6). StackExchange improves on 3/6, Common Crawl on 3/6, and arXiv on only 2/6. The combined 4B-token corpus improves on only 2/6 benchmarks and degrades on 4/6. The "average improvement of up to 5%" figure appears to reference specific benchmark-subset pairs (e.g., Textbooks boosting MMLU-MATH by ~4%, MathQA by ~7%, AQuA by ~5%) rather than an average across all configurations. The abstract's claim is strictly true for some subset-benchmark combinations but does not characterize the full experimental picture, which includes numerous regressions.

The claim would be strengthened by experiments that mix MATHPILE subsets with the original Mistral pre-training data, which the paper explicitly identifies as standard practice for domain adaptation but does not perform. The current experiments — continual pre-training exclusively on math data without data mixing — represent a stress test that demonstrates MATHPILE's effectiveness under suboptimal conditions, but cannot show the improvement ceiling that practitioners would achieve by following established data mixing protocols.

#### Claim: "Data quality matters more than data volume"

This claim is **strongly supported by the Wikipedia ablation (Table 5)** but **more qualified when comparing across subsets (Table 4).** The Wikipedia ablation provides a clean, controlled demonstration: 2.18B tokens of raw data degrade performance (GSM8K: 32.30%), while 0.18B tokens of processed data improve it (49.96%). This is a 10× reduction in tokens producing a 17.66 percentage point improvement — unambiguous evidence that processing quality dominates volume. Similarly, 0.55B tokens of raw data (41.92%) are worse than 0.18B tokens of processed data (49.96%) — a 3× reduction in volume with a net gain of +8.04 points.

However, when comparing across different sources at different volumes in Table 4, the relationship between volume and quality is confounded by content differences. Common Crawl at 1.83B tokens produces modest gains; Textbooks at 0.56B produce broader gains; Wikipedia at 0.18B produces strong gains on specific benchmarks. This pattern is consistent with "quality matters more than volume" but does not isolate quality from content relevance — Wikipedia may perform well not just because it is high-quality but because its encyclopedic format is well-matched to few-shot question-answering, while Common Crawl's web page format is less well-matched regardless of quality.

A direct volume ablation within a single source — e.g., comparing 0.1B vs. 0.5B vs. 1.0B of processed Wikipedia tokens — would more cleanly test the claim within a fixed quality level, but the paper does not perform this experiment. The claim is therefore supported qualitatively by the ranking of sources (small Wikipedia > large Common Crawl) and quantitatively by the within-source raw-vs-processed comparison, but is not tested for the regime where both quality and volume are high (large amounts of processed data).

#### Claim: "Synthetic datasets degrade performance when used as standalone pre-training corpora"

This claim is **overwhelmingly supported** by the AMPS and DM-Mathematics results in Table 4. The performance collapses are so severe (GSM8K dropping to 0.00–0.08%, MATH to 0.00–0.82%) that they cannot reasonably be attributed to experimental noise or suboptimal hyperparameters — the effect size is ~40–50 percentage points, orders of magnitude larger than the few-point changes seen in other configurations. This is a robust and important negative result.

However, the experiments do not investigate *why* the collapse occurs or whether it can be mitigated. The paper's interpretation — "narrow, monotonous structure" — is plausible but untested. Alternative hypotheses that cannot be ruled out include: (1) the synthetic data contains systematic errors that the model learns and amplifies, (2) the problem-solution format over-trains the model on a specific output pattern that doesn't match benchmark evaluation formats, (3) the data distribution is so different from the model's original training that it triggers catastrophic forgetting through gradient interference rather than distributional mismatch. Experiments mixing synthetic data with real data at varying ratios (as the paper suggests for future work) would distinguish between "synthetic data is inherently harmful" (which would show degradation at any mixture ratio) and "synthetic data is harmful only when it dominates the training distribution" (which would show a threshold effect). The paper demonstrates the existence of the problem but does not characterize its boundaries.

#### Claim: "Data contamination detection is crucial for mathematical corpora"

The experimental evidence for this claim is **indirect but compelling.** The paper demonstrates that contamination exists (23 MATH items, 2 MMLU-STEM items in MATHPILE; 195 MATH items and 65 MMLU-STEM items in OpenWebMath — Table 2), but does not show the effect of that contamination on downstream evaluation. There is no experiment comparing model performance when trained on contaminated vs. decontaminated data — such an experiment would directly demonstrate that benchmark leakage inflates apparent model capabilities, which is the harm that contamination detection prevents.

The strength of the claim instead rests on the field's established understanding that training on test data invalidates evaluation, combined with the paper's demonstration that contamination is pervasive and uneven across corpora. The comparison with OpenWebMath (10× more MATH contamination) provides a relative baseline: models trained on OpenWebMath without contamination removal would be evaluated on benchmarks they had partially memorized, and their results would not be comparable to models trained on MATHPILE. The paper's proactive removal is therefore necessary for MATHPILE-trained models to produce valid benchmark results, even though the paper does not quantify the inflation that removal prevents.

A practical limitation: the exact-match detection with MD5 hashing will miss paraphrased or reworded benchmark items. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by calling detected counts "minimum occurrences." The true contamination rate — including near-duplicates, translations, and rephrased versions — is unknown, but the paper demonstrates that even the lower bound (exact matches) is substantial enough to warrant systematic detection.

#### Missing Experiments and Evaluation Gaps

Several experiments would meaningfully strengthen the paper's claims but are absent:

**1. Full MATHPILE training.** Despite constructing a 9.5B token corpus, the paper never trains on the complete MATHPILE. The largest training run is 4B tokens of combined subsets (Textbooks + Wikipedia + StackExchange + Common Crawl, excluding arXiv and ProofWiki). The full corpus's performance — and whether the remaining subsets (arXiv, ProofWiki) would help or hurt when added to the mixture — remains unknown. The paper attributes this to computational constraints (1,760 A100 GPU hours per 10B tokens per epoch), which is understandable, but it means the central artifact (the 9.5B token corpus) has not been validated as an integrated training dataset.

**2. Data mixture experiments.** The paper does not experiment with different ratios of subset mixing. The combined 4B configuration uses equal or proportional mixing, but the subset-level results suggest that optimal proportions would be non-uniform: Textbooks and Wikipedia consistently outperform StackExchange and Common Crawl, so a mixture weighted toward the stronger subsets might outperform the uniform mixture. The paper's own literature review cites RegMix (Liu et al., 2024) on data mixture optimization, but this approach is not applied to MATHPILE.

**3. Comparison with general-domain corpora.** The paper compares MATHPILE against synthetic math corpora and OpenWebMath, but not against training on additional general-domain data (e.g., more SlimPajama tokens). A baseline showing that MATHPILE improves math benchmarks more than an equivalent volume of general-domain data would demonstrate that MATHPILE's math-specific curation provides value beyond simply providing more training tokens. Without this comparison, one could argue that *any* additional pre-training data — math-focused or not — would produce the observed 1–5 point improvements.

**4. Scale-out experiments.** All experiments use Mistral-7B. The paper does not test whether MATHPILE's benefits transfer to smaller models (where data quality effects might be larger) or larger models (where data volume might matter more). The claim that MATHPILE is suitable as a general-purpose math pre-training corpus is not validated across model scales.

**5. Random seed replication.** The paper explicitly acknowledges in the checklist that it did not report error bars across multiple random seeds. This is a significant limitation for interpreting the smaller performance differences (1–3 percentage points) observed between subset configurations. Without variance estimates, it is impossible to determine whether, for example, Wikipedia's 2.58-point GSM8K improvement over the Textbooks' 1.59-point improvement is statistically reliable or within the noise range of training stochasticity.

**6. Benchmark coverage of mathematical reasoning types.** The evaluation suite covers arithmetic word problems (GSM8K, MathQA, AQuA), competition mathematics (MATH, SAT-MATH), and multiple-choice STEM knowledge (MMLU-STEM), but does not evaluate theorem-proving capabilities, proof generation, or mathematical concept explanation — tasks that MATHPILE's ProofWiki and arXiv components are specifically designed to support. The evaluation suite tests whether MATHPILE helps with *solving* math problems, but not whether it helps with *understanding* or *generating* mathematical exposition. Given that ProofWiki comprises theorem-proof pairs and arXiv contains proof-heavy academic papers, the lack of theorem-proving evaluation is a significant gap between the corpus's composition and its validation.

These gaps do not invalidate the paper's demonstrated contributions — the subset-level training, pipeline ablation, and contamination detection are individually valuable — but they constrain the strength of the paper's broader claims about MATHPILE as a general-purpose mathematical pre-training corpus. The corpus itself is a substantial infrastructure contribution; the experimental validation demonstrates its potential rather than fully characterizing its capabilities.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Full MATHPILE Corpus Is Never Validated Experimentally

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper constructs a 9.5-billion-token corpus but never trains on the complete MATHPILE. The largest continual pre-training run uses 4B tokens drawn from four of the six subsets (Textbooks, Wikipedia, StackExchange, Common Crawl; arXiv and ProofWiki are excluded). The paper acknowledges this directly:

> "Due to limited computational resources, we did not extensively experiment with the entire dataset or combine data from MATHPILE's subsets and existing general corpora, leaving these valuable aspects for future work."

**The consequence.** The central artifact of the paper — the full 9.5B-token MATHPILE corpus — has not been empirically validated as an integrated pre-training dataset. The subset-level experiments in Table 4 demonstrate that individual components can improve performance (Textbooks, Wikipedia) or degrade it (arXiv, the combined 4B mixture), but they cannot predict how the complete corpus would behave. The arXiv subset, which constitutes 38.07% of MATHPILE documents and contains approximately 8.3 billion tokens, produced the weakest results of any subset when trained in isolation: regressions on MATH (−2.58), SAT-MATH (−4.55), MathQA (−5.46), and AQuA (−0.40). If arXiv's effect is subtractive, adding it to the mixture could worsen performance relative to the 4B configuration — or it could provide complementary knowledge that the smaller subsets lack. Neither outcome can be ruled out.

The ProofWiki subset (23,839 documents, 7.6M tokens) is never evaluated at all, so its contribution — positive, neutral, or negative — is entirely unknown. Given that ProofWiki was specifically structured into theorem-proof pairs and definitions to support formal reasoning, and that the evaluation suite includes no theorem-proving benchmarks, its absence from experiments means a potentially valuable capability dimension of MATHPILE remains unmeasured.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The combination experiment training on 4B tokens of Textbooks + Wikipedia + StackExchange + Common Crawl (Table 4) showed mixed results: improvements on GSM8K (+2.50) and MATH (+1.62) but regressions on SAT-MATH (−4.09), MMLU-MATH (−1.17), MathQA (−0.27), and AQuA (−2.76). This is the only evidence about subset interaction, and it demonstrates negative interaction: the mixture underperforms the best individual subset on several benchmarks (Textbooks alone improved SAT-MATH by +9.09; the mixture degrades it by −4.09). This pattern cannot be extrapolated to predict what adding arXiv and ProofWiki would do, but it demonstrates that subset combination is non-trivially non-additive and that assuming full MATHPILE would outperform its components is unsupported.

The paper also reports that cross-source deduplication found "minimal overlap" between sources (Section 3.3: a single StackExchange question duplicated in Common Crawl), confirming that sources are distinct in content — but distinctness in content does not guarantee complementary training signal, as the arXiv results demonstrate.

**Mitigation status.** The paper attributes the missing full-corpus experiment to computational constraints: "pre-training 10 billion tokens for 1 epoch requires approximately 1,760 NVIDIA A100 GPU hours, making us keen to partner with well-resourced corporations to gain deeper insights in the future." This is a genuine resource limitation, not a design oversight, but it means the paper's headline contribution — a 9.5B token corpus — has been validated only at the component level, not as an integrated resource. A practitioner downloading MATHPILE cannot consult the paper for guidance on whether to use the full corpus, a specific subset combination, or a mixture with external data. The paper explicitly frames full-corpus training and data mixing optimization as future work.

---

### The Base Model and Benchmark Suite Are Singular, Limiting Generalization Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** All continual pre-training experiments use exactly one base model (Mistral-7B-v0.1) and evaluate on a fixed set of mathematical reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, AGIEval-SAT-MATH, MathQA, AQuA). The paper states that "we believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4 introduction), but provides no evidence for this representativeness claim. The model family, parameter scale, pre-training data composition, and architectural details are all held constant.

**The consequence.** The paper's findings about which MATHPILE subsets improve which capabilities, and by how much, are potentially confounded by Mistral-7B-specific properties. At least three forms of model-dependence are plausible:

**Pre-training data overlap.** Mistral-7B's pre-training corpus is not publicly documented (Jiang et al., 2023 did not disclose its composition). If Mistral was already trained on substantial amounts of Wikipedia, StackExchange, or arXiv data, then continual pre-training on MATHPILE's corresponding subsets would represent reinforcement of familiar distributions rather than exposure to new knowledge. This would mean the observed improvements from Wikipedia and Textbooks — and the regressions from arXiv — partially reflect what Mistral already knows, not the inherent training value of those sources. A model pre-trained on a different data mixture might show a different pattern of subset effectiveness.

**Parameter scale interaction with data volume.** Mistral-7B is a 7-billion-parameter model. Smaller models (e.g., 1B parameters) might benefit more from high-quality data because they have less capacity to extract signal from noisy sources, making MATHPILE's quality advantage larger. Larger models (e.g., 70B parameters) might benefit less from small curated subsets and require larger volumes — the paper's 0.18B Wikipedia tokens might be insufficient to move a 70B model's parameters, while 1.83B Common Crawl tokens might show stronger effects at scale. The paper's claim that "data quality matters more than volume" is demonstrated at the 7B scale; its scaling behavior is unknown.

**Architectural and tokenizer differences.** Mistral-7B uses a specific tokenizer and architecture. If the tokenizer handles LaTeX commands differently (e.g., splitting `\frac` into multiple tokens vs. treating it as one token), the effective context window for mathematical expressions changes, potentially affecting how well the model learns from notation-heavy sources like arXiv. A model with a math-optimized tokenizer might extract more signal from the same LaTeX documents.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** There is none. The paper includes no experiments with any model other than Mistral-7B-v0.1. The evaluation benchmark suite, while covering a reasonable range of mathematical reasoning types (arithmetic word problems, competition mathematics, multiple-choice STEM), is not comprehensive: it excludes theorem proving, proof generation, mathematical concept explanation, formal verification, and multi-step symbolic computation — all capabilities that MATHPILE's composition (ProofWiki's theorem-proof pairs, arXiv's proof-heavy papers) is specifically designed to support. The evaluation suite tests whether MATHPILE helps models *solve* math problems, but the corpus's potential to improve *mathematical understanding and generation* is untested.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address model dependence — no multi-model experiments are performed, and the limitation is not discussed in Section 6. The choice of a single model is a practical constraint (compute budget), and the paper is transparent about the model used, but the generalization of findings to other model families, scales, or architectures is an open question. The paper's evaluation benchmarks are standard for mathematical reasoning research, so the choice is defensible for comparability, but the gap between corpus composition (including theorem-proving content) and evaluation (exclusively problem-solving) is a meaningful mismatch that the paper does not acknowledge.

### The Synthetic Data Degradation Result Is Observed but Not Diagnosed, Limiting Its Actionability

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper demonstrates that continual pre-training on AMPS (1B tokens) and DM-Mathematics (5B tokens) catastrophically degrades mathematical reasoning performance — GSM8K drops from 47.38% to 0.08% and 0.00% respectively — but does not investigate the mechanism or characterize the conditions under which degradation occurs versus when it might be avoided. The paper offers an interpretation:

> "We attribute this to the narrow, monotonous structure of AMPS and DM-Mathematics problem sets, making them unsuitable for standalone pre-training; such datasets generally yield better results when combined with broader corpora for pre-training."

This is a hypothesis, not a diagnosis.

**The consequence.** The finding is empirically striking and conceptually important — it challenges the assumption that synthetic data is automatically beneficial — but it provides limited guidance to practitioners because it does not answer several questions necessary for risk assessment:

**Is degradation caused by format, content, or volume?** AMPS and DM-Mathematics differ from MATHPILE along multiple dimensions simultaneously: they are synthetic (not human-written), structurally uniform (isolated problem-solution pairs rather than varied exposition), and domain-narrow (K-12 and competition math, respectively, without college-level theory, proofs, or discussions). The degradation could be caused by any of these factors or their interaction. If the problem is format monotonicity, then synthetic data in varied formats (synthetic textbooks, synthetic Q&A, synthetic proofs) might avoid degradation. If the problem is synthetic origin, then no amount of format variation would help. The paper cannot distinguish these hypotheses.

**Is there a safe mixture ratio?** The paper notes that synthetic datasets "generally yield better results when combined with broader corpora for pre-training" and cites Xu et al. (2024), but does not test this claim. A practitioner who has synthetic math data they want to use — perhaps because it covers specific topics or problem types their application requires — learns from the paper that using it alone will destroy model capabilities, but learns nothing about what proportion mixed with real data might be safe, or whether certain synthetic data formats are safer than others.

**Does degradation depend on the base model's existing capabilities?** Mistral-7B starts with 47.38% on GSM8K — it already has substantial mathematical reasoning ability that the synthetic data apparently overwrites. A model with lower initial math performance (e.g., a general-domain model with 20% on GSM8K) might benefit from synthetic math data that Mistral finds harmful because it has less existing capability to lose and more to gain. Alternatively, a stronger model might be more robust to distribution shift because its capabilities are more deeply encoded. The dependence on base model strength is unknown.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Only the two data points in Table 4: AMPS at 1B tokens and DM-Mathematics at 5B tokens, both producing catastrophic degradation on all benchmarks. No intermediate volume experiments (0.1B, 0.5B, 2B tokens of synthetic data) are performed, so the dose-response curve is unknown — degradation might be gradual or might exhibit a threshold effect. No mixture experiments (e.g., 10% AMPS + 90% MATHPILE) are performed. No format variation experiments (e.g., reformatting AMPS problems into prose with surrounding exposition) are performed. The ReST$^{EM}$ experiment (Appendix K in the paper's reference to Singh et al., 2024, though details are in an appendix not included in the provided text) suggests that revision-model training can also be fragile, providing a second data point that synthetic or specialized training data can backfire, but does not isolate the mechanism.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to diagnose the degradation. The observation is presented as a comparative result (MATHPILE subsets outperform existing synthetic corpora) rather than as a phenomenon to be investigated. The limitation is acknowledged indirectly through the paper's statement that synthetic data works better when combined with broader corpora and through the future work mention of data mixing (Section 6: "Future research could also explore data mixing"), but the paper does not frame the synthetic data degradation as a limitation requiring investigation. For practitioners, the finding serves as a warning that standalone synthetic math pre-training is dangerous, but without characterization of the safe operating envelope, the warning is binary — "don't do this" — rather than constructive — "here's how to do it safely."

### Data Contamination Detection Guarantees Are a Lower Bound, Not a Complete Solution

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's contamination detection uses line-level exact match with MD5 hashing, comparing every document line against benchmark test set lines and removing matching documents. The paper acknowledges implicitly that this captures only exact matches:

> "numbers representing minimum occurrences, given potential undetected duplicates."

The method will miss any benchmark item that appears in MATHPILE in a modified form — paraphrased, reworded, translated, presented with different line breaks, embedded in larger paragraphs, or expressed with different mathematical notation for the same concept.

**The consequence.** The paper's contamination statistics (23 MATH items, 2 MMLU-STEM items in MATHPILE; 195 MATH items, 65 MMLU-STEM items in OpenWebMath) represent **lower bounds** on actual contamination. The true contamination rate — including near-exact matches, semantic equivalents, and reformatted versions — could be substantially higher. This matters because even partial or reworded contamination can inflate benchmark scores: if a model has seen a problem's statement and solution approach during pre-training, even if the exact wording differs, it may have an unfair advantage over models that have not seen the problem in any form.

The line-level exact match approach is particularly vulnerable for mathematical content because mathematical problems and solutions can be expressed with different notation, different variable names, or different TeX formatting while remaining semantically identical. For example, "Find $\int_0^1 x^2 dx$" and "Evaluate $\int_{0}^{1} x^{2} \, dx$" differ at the string level (different spacing, different integral limit formatting, different differential notation) but represent the same problem. The paper's detection would miss this duplication.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides evidence that exact-match contamination is pervasive enough to warrant systematic detection — 195 MATH occurrences in OpenWebMath, found in problem aggregation websites, textbook excerpts, and StackExchange cross-posts — but provides no evidence about the prevalence of near-exact contamination. The detection method's sensitivity is characterized only by what it finds, not by what it misses. The comparison with OpenWebMath (Table 2) demonstrates that exact-match contamination varies substantially across corpora, establishing that MATHPILE's construction process reduces this form of contamination, but does not establish that the remaining contamination (including undetected near-exact matches) is negligible or harmless.

The paper reports that "No contamination was detected in other benchmarks" (GSM8K, AGIEval, MathQA, AQuA) but this statement is qualified by the detection method's limitations — it means "no exact line-level matches were found," not "no contamination exists." A practitioner should understand that training on MATHPILE does not guarantee contamination-free evaluation on these benchmarks, only that exact duplicates have been removed.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not apply fuzzy matching, n-gram overlap with variable thresholds, embedding-based similarity detection, or any other method for identifying near-duplicate benchmark items. The rationale is practical — exact matching is scalable and produces no false positives — but the tradeoff in recall (missing near-duplicates) is not quantified or discussed as a limitation. Section 6 (Conclusion and Limitations) does not mention contamination detection as an area for improvement. Future work on more sensitive contamination detection methods is implied by the paper's advocacy for early contamination detection but not explicitly called for.

For practitioners, the takeaway is that MATHPILE has undergone a baseline level of contamination screening that removes exact benchmark duplicates — which the paper demonstrates is an important baseline that not all corpora meet (OpenWebMath did not) — but that contamination risk is reduced, not eliminated. Models trained on MATHPILE and evaluated on MATH, GSM8K, MMLU-STEM, etc., have a contamination guarantee that is stronger than models trained on OpenWebMath but still imperfect, and benchmark results should be interpreted with that caveat.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Incorporated into the Efficiency Argument

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's "less is more" claim and the pipeline's quality improvements are demonstrated through post-hoc training experiments, but the **cost of producing MATHPILE-quality data** — the human labor, manual review, source-specific customization, and rejected tokens — is not quantified or compared against alternatives. The corpus is presented as an output; the resources required to produce it (beyond the acknowledged computational costs of pre-training experiments) are documented but not analyzed as a cost.

**The consequence.** A practitioner or research group deciding whether to adopt MATHPILE's construction methodology for their own domain-specific corpus faces an implicit cost-quality tradeoff that the paper does not quantify. The paper documents extensive human involvement at multiple pipeline stages (Tables 9 and 10): manual textbook search and quality checking, manual inspection of MinHash near-duplicates to decide retention (ProofWiki entries retained, StackExchange cross-posts removed), manual adjustment of TF-IDF vocabulary for Common Crawl filtering, manual verification of contamination detection flags, and manual setting of source-specific language identification thresholds based on observed false positive rates. The arXiv preprocessing pipeline involves nine custom LaTeX transformations that required domain expertise to design and implement.

The "less is more" principle implies that these investments pay off — that spending human effort on careful curation produces better training data per token than spending compute on processing more raw data. But the magnitude of the payoff relative to the investment is unknown because the paper does not estimate the human cost. How many person-hours did the manual textbook search require? How many documents were manually reviewed during deduplication? Could the same quality improvements have been achieved with a simpler pipeline and more tokens? The paper cannot answer these questions.

This limitation is analogous to the difficulty estimation cost issue in the earlier sections — the pipeline's quality improvements are demonstrated, but the cost of achieving those improvements is not factored into the efficiency calculation. If producing 0.18B tokens of high-quality Wikipedia required 10× the human effort of producing 2.18B tokens of raw Wikipedia, the effective "cost per useful token" may favor the simpler approach for some resource profiles, even though the raw approach degrades model performance. The tradeoff depends on the relative cost of human expert time versus GPU compute time — a parameter that varies across organizations and that the paper does not surface.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides detailed documentation of human intervention points (Tables 9 and 10) and explicitly acknowledges that "many detailed decisions in its creation were made empirically" (Section 6). This transparency allows readers to assess the pipeline's complexity but does not provide the quantitative information needed to estimate replication cost. The ablation experiments (Table 5) demonstrate that the pipeline is valuable — skipping it degrades performance — but do not establish that the specific pipeline described is cost-optimal relative to alternative quality-enhancement strategies. For example, could a simpler pipeline (relaxed language ID thresholds + basic deduplication, without the eleven heuristic cleaning rules) achieve most of the same gains at lower human cost? The paper provides no evidence either way.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the empirical, potentially suboptimal nature of its decisions: "Many detailed decisions in its creation were made empirically, which may not always be optimal, and verifying decisions directly can be challenging." This is an honest acknowledgment but does not address the cost dimension. The release of quality annotations (Section 3.2, Figure 12) partially mitigates the limitation for downstream users — they can apply their own filters without re-running the pipeline — but does not help a corpus builder who wants to estimate the cost of replicating MATHPILE's construction process for a new domain. The paper treats the pipeline design as a contribution (the documented process is part of what is released) but does not treat the pipeline's cost as a design parameter to be analyzed or optimized.

### The Revision Model Is Not Evaluated, Leaving the Sequential Revision Capability of MATHPILE-Trained Models Unmeasured

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates MATHPILE exclusively through standard few-shot prompting with greedy decoding on benchmark problem sets. This evaluation paradigm tests the model's ability to produce a correct final answer given a problem statement, but does not test whether the model can engage in iterative reasoning — checking its own work, revising incorrect solutions, or generating multi-step chains of mathematical exposition — capabilities that MATHPILE's composition (textbook exposition, arXiv proofs, StackExchange discussions with multiple answers) is designed to support.

This is not a limitation of the benchmark choices per se, but a gap between what the corpus contains and what is measured. MATHPILE includes long-form mathematical reasoning artifacts: proofs that proceed through multiple lemmas, textbook chapters that build concepts progressively, StackExchange threads where answers refine or correct each other. A model trained on this data might develop capabilities in mathematical self-correction, proof generation, or explanatory reasoning that standard few-shot accuracy benchmarks cannot detect.

**The consequence.** The paper's evaluation likely **underestimates MATHPILE's value** for certain use cases and simultaneously provides **no evidence** that the corpus improves capabilities beyond single-shot problem-solving. A practitioner interested in using MATHPILE to build a model that generates mathematical explanations, tutors students through step-by-step reasoning, or participates in mathematical dialogue cannot consult the paper for evidence that MATHPILE helps with these tasks. The subset-level results in Table 4 may mislead: arXiv degrades problem-solving benchmarks, but might substantially improve proof-generation or theorem-proving capabilities that the evaluation suite does not measure. A practitioner who evaluates arXiv only by the benchmark regressions in Table 4 might discard it, losing a potentially valuable source for their actual use case.

Conversely, the positive results for Textbooks and Wikipedia on problem-solving benchmarks do not guarantee that those subsets improve other mathematical capabilities. A model might become better at solving GSM8K problems while becoming worse at explaining mathematical concepts, and the paper would not detect this.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The evaluation suite includes only accuracy metrics on problem-solving benchmarks. There are no experiments on proof generation, mathematical explanation, theorem proving, or any generative mathematical task. The ProofWiki subset — 13,511 theorem-proof pairs specifically structured to teach the relationship between theorems and their proofs — is never evaluated. The arXiv subset's content (academic papers with proofs, definitions, and exposition) is evaluated only on problem-solving benchmarks where it performs poorly, not on tasks that match its content.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this gap as a limitation. The evaluation benchmarks are standard for mathematical reasoning research and the choice is defensible for establishing baseline effectiveness, but the absence of any evaluation aligned with MATHPILE's broader mathematical content means the corpus's full capability profile is unknown. The paper frames MATHPILE as serving "general math" reasoning (Table 1), which implies problem-solving coverage, but the corpus's composition suggests it could support a wider range of mathematical capabilities than the evaluation demonstrates. Future work evaluating MATHPILE-trained models on proof generation, mathematical explanation, or theorem-proving benchmarks would address this gap, but the paper does not explicitly call for this.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

MATHPILE shifts the conversation around mathematical pre-training corpora from a **scale-centric framing to a quality-and-transparency-centric framing.** The field's default assumption — codified in corpus papers from C4 to RedPajama to SlimPajama — has been that larger corpora are better, that uniform processing pipelines work across domains, and that corpus quality is primarily a function of filtering aggressiveness. MATHPILE challenges each of these assumptions with empirical evidence, reorienting how the community should think about constructing domain-specific pre-training data.

**This is not a paradigm shift but a methodological reframing with sharp empirical teeth.** The core idea — that data quality matters more than quantity — has been advocated before (notably in the LIMA paper by Zhou et al., 2023, which the paper explicitly cites as inspiration). What MATHPILE adds is a **demonstrated, documented, and reproducible instantiation** of that principle for the mathematical domain, complete with ablation evidence that isolates *which* quality interventions matter and by how much. The shift is from a vague exhortation ("use good data") to a concrete methodology: source-specific preprocessing, source-adaptive language identification thresholds, manual review of near-duplicate mathematical content, proactive benchmark contamination scanning, and per-document quality annotation release.

The paper's most landscape-altering contribution is arguably its **proactive approach to data contamination detection as a mandatory corpus construction step, not a post-hoc audit.** The finding that OpenWebMath — a concurrent, well-regarded math corpus — contains 195 MATH test set occurrences and 65 MMLU-STEM occurrences (versus MATHPILE's 23 and 2) makes contamination visible as a first-order quality dimension that distinguishes corpora. If this practice is adopted as standard — and the paper explicitly advocates for it — then future corpus releases will be expected to include contamination reports, and models trained on corpora without such reports will have their benchmark results treated with appropriate skepticism. This is a **methodological norm-setting contribution** whose impact depends on community adoption but whose logic is compelling: contamination is irreversible once a model is trained, so detection must happen at corpus release time.

**The paper resolves a latent tension between the Phi-style "synthetic textbooks are all you need" narrative and the reality that synthetic data can catastrophically degrade pre-trained models.** The AMPS and DM-Mathematics results in Table 4 are unambiguous: training on 1B tokens of AMPS drops GSM8K accuracy from 47.38% to 0.08%; training on 5B tokens of DM-Mathematics drops it to 0.00%. These are not marginal regressions — they represent complete capability destruction. The paper's interpretation — that the "narrow, monotonous structure" of problem-set-style synthetic data makes it "unsuitable for standalone pre-training" — provides a concrete failure mode that the Phi success story obscured. The tension is resolved by recognizing that synthetic data's benefits depend on **format diversity, not just content correctness.** The Phi models were trained on synthetic *textbooks* (varied exposition with examples, explanations, and exercises in natural prose), while AMPS and DM-Mathematics are *problem sets* (isolated question-answer pairs with no surrounding exposition). MATHPILE's success with synthetic textbooks (filtered from OpenPhi) and failure with problem-set-style synthetic data clarifies that synthetic data generation strategies must produce structurally diverse output, not just factually accurate output.

**Several research directions become more attractive in light of this work:**

- **Source-adaptive corpus construction for other specialized domains** — if mathematics requires custom language ID thresholds and source-specific cleaning rules, what about code? Legal text? Biomedical literature? MATHPILE provides a template and an existence proof that domain-adaptive pipelines outperform uniform ones, making domain-specific corpus construction a more attractive investment.
- **Quality annotation as a corpus release standard** — the paper demonstrates that releasing per-document quality scores (symbol-to-word ratio, uppercase ratio, mean word length, etc.) enables downstream users to customize filtering without re-running pipelines. If this becomes expected practice, corpus builders will need to design their pipelines to produce such annotations from the start.
- **Data mixing optimization for continued pre-training** — the combined-subset result (Textbooks + Wikipedia + StackExchange + Common Crawl at 4B tokens degrades performance on 4 of 6 benchmarks despite individual subsets improving them) demonstrates that mixing is non-trivially non-additive, creating demand for principled mixing strategies.

**Research directions that become less attractive:**

- **"Just crawl more math web pages" as a corpus strategy.** The OpenWebMath comparison shows that a web-only math corpus underperforms curated sources at comparable volumes (0.59B tokens of OpenWebMath drops MATH from 10.08% to 7.86%, while 0.18B of Wikipedia tokens maintains it at 9.96%), and that web crawls are substantially more contaminated with benchmark test sets. Web crawling is still valuable as one source among many, but as a sole strategy it is demonstrably inferior to curated multi-source approaches.
- **Standalone synthetic problem-set pre-training.** The AMPS and DM-Mathematics results establish a clear failure mode. Researchers who want to use synthetic math data will need to either mix it with real data or generate it in more diverse formats — generating raw problem-solution pairs at scale and training on them alone is now known to be destructive, not merely ineffective.

**The paper's transparency practices — the datasheet (Table 6), the explicit documentation of human intervention points (Tables 9 and 10), the deduplication examples (Tables 11–19), the quality annotation scheme (Figure 12) — set a new bar for corpus documentation that, if adopted, would represent a genuine shift in how the field evaluates corpus contributions.** Currently, many corpus papers emphasize token count and downstream performance while providing minimal documentation of their processing decisions. MATHPILE inverts this: the documentation is treated as a first-class contribution, and the performance gains — while real — are presented as validation of the documented process rather than the primary achievement. This reweighting of what constitutes a strong corpus paper could change how reviewers evaluate future submissions in the datasets and benchmarks track.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. Characterizing the dose-response curve for synthetic data degradation.** The paper demonstrates that 1B tokens of AMPS and 5B tokens of DM-Mathematics catastrophically degrade mathematical reasoning, but the dose-response relationship is unknown. A targeted experiment would train Mistral-7B on progressively larger volumes of AMPS (10M, 50M, 100M, 500M, 1B tokens) and measure GSM8K and MATH accuracy at each point. If degradation exhibits a threshold effect (performance stable up to some critical volume, then collapses), that threshold would define a safe operating envelope for synthetic data mixing. If degradation is gradual and monotonic, then any standalone synthetic data is harmful and the only safe approach is mixing with real data. The paper's finding that 0.55B tokens of raw Wikipedia degrades performance (GSM8K: 41.92%) while 2.18B tokens degrades it further (32.30%) provides a template for this kind of dose-response analysis but does not perform it for synthetic data. This experiment is newly tractable because MATHPILE provides a cleaned, uncontaminated baseline corpus that can serve as the "real data" component in mixture experiments.

**2. Data mixing ratio optimization for domain-adaptive continued pre-training.** The combined-subset experiment (4B tokens of Textbooks + Wikipedia + StackExchange + Common Crawl) produced regressions on 4/6 benchmarks despite individual subsets improving them — a clear signal of negative interaction. A systematic follow-up would train models on mixtures of the best-performing subsets (Textbooks and Wikipedia) at varying ratios (90/10, 75/25, 50/50, 25/75, 10/90) and measure which ratios maximize gains across benchmarks. This directly addresses the paper's stated future work direction: "Future research could also explore data mixing." The experiment would distinguish between two hypotheses: (1) negative interaction is caused by including low-quality or mismatched subsets (StackExchange, Common Crawl, arXiv) that dilute the training signal, in which case excluding them from the mixture should recover the gains, or (2) negative interaction is caused by distribution shift from *any* multi-source training regardless of individual subset quality, in which case even optimal Textbooks + Wikipedia mixtures would underperform the best single subset. The paper's RegMix citation (Liu et al., 2024) provides a methodological framework that could be applied to MATHPILE subsets directly.

**3. Evaluation of MATHPILE on theorem-proving and proof-generation benchmarks.** The corpus contains 13,511 ProofWiki theorem-proof pairs and ~8.3B tokens of arXiv papers rich in proofs and formal mathematical exposition, yet the evaluation suite tests exclusively problem-solving (GSM8K, MATH, SAT-MATH, etc.). A natural follow-up would evaluate Mistral-7B continually pre-trained on MATHPILE subsets (particularly ProofWiki and arXiv) on benchmarks like NaturalProofs (Welleck et al., 2021), miniF2F (for formal theorem proving), or a custom proof-generation evaluation. If ProofWiki and arXiv improve theorem-proving capabilities while degrading problem-solving (as arXiv does in Table 4), this would demonstrate that MATHPILE's components support different mathematical capabilities and that the problem-solving evaluation suite gives an incomplete — and for some subsets, misleading — picture of their value. If neither subset improves theorem-proving, it would suggest that continual pre-training on structured proof data is insufficient to acquire formal reasoning capabilities without specialized training objectives.

**4. Cross-model replication of the "less is more" Wikipedia ablation.** The paper's strongest evidence for quality-over-quantity comes from a single model (Mistral-7B) on a single source (Wikipedia). A replication across multiple model families and scales — e.g., Llama-2-7B, Llama-2-13B, CodeLlama-7B, and a smaller 1B-parameter model — would test whether the degradation from raw Wikipedia and the improvement from processed Wikipedia generalize. If the pattern holds across architectures and scales, it establishes "processing quality dominates data volume in continued pre-training" as a robust finding rather than a Mistral-specific observation. If the pattern flips for larger models (e.g., 13B models benefit from the larger volume of raw Wikipedia despite the noise, because they have more capacity to extract signal), it would define a scale-dependent tradeoff between quality and volume that corpus builders need to factor into their construction decisions based on target model size.

**5. Sensitivity analysis of contamination detection to matching granularity.** The paper uses line-level exact match with MD5 hashing, which captures exact duplicates but misses paraphrases, rewordings, and notation variants. A follow-up could quantify the "dark matter" of undetected contamination: take the MATH benchmark test set, generate 10 paraphrased versions of each problem (using an LLM to reword while preserving mathematical content), and run the same exact-match detection pipeline on MATHPILE. The fraction of paraphrased versions that escape detection provides a lower-bound estimate of contamination that exact matching misses. If a substantial fraction escape (e.g., >50%), it would demonstrate that exact-match detection is necessary but insufficient, and would motivate development of semantic or embedding-based contamination detection methods. If most paraphrases also match (because mathematical notation constrains the space of possible rewordings), it would validate exact matching as reasonably comprehensive for mathematical content. MATHPILE's existing exact-match detection provides the baseline against which such a sensitivity analysis can be calibrated.

**6. Cost-quality tradeoff analysis for the MATHPILE construction methodology.** The paper documents extensive human intervention at multiple pipeline stages but does not quantify the person-hours or expertise required. A follow-up study could estimate the total human cost of replicating MATHPILE's construction and compare it against simpler alternatives: (a) fully automated pipeline with uniform thresholds and no manual review, (b) MATHPILE's pipeline but with manual review steps replaced by automated heuristics, and (c) the full MATHPILE methodology as described. Training Mistral-7B on the output of each pipeline and comparing downstream performance would produce a cost-effectiveness curve: human effort (x-axis) vs. benchmark improvement (y-axis). This would answer the question the paper raises but does not address: is MATHPILE's level of manual curation cost-effective compared to simpler, cheaper approaches? If the manual review of ProofWiki near-duplicates and the source-specific FastText threshold tuning contribute only marginal gains over automated alternatives, practitioners can adopt a streamlined version of the methodology. If these manual interventions are load-bearing, it justifies the investment and provides guidance on which stages cannot be automated.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**1. Bootstrapping math-capable language models in academic labs and startups with limited compute.** The paper's central finding — that 0.18B tokens of processed Wikipedia improves Mistral-7B's GSM8K accuracy from 47.38% to 49.96%, while 2.18B tokens of raw Wikipedia degrades it to 32.30% — has immediate practical implications for organizations that cannot afford to pre-train models from scratch on trillion-token corpora. A lab with access to 8×A100 GPUs and a Mistral-7B checkpoint can achieve measurable improvements in mathematical reasoning by continually pre-training on MATHPILE subsets totaling a few hundred million tokens — a process requiring approximately 35 GPU-hours per 0.2B tokens at the paper's reported rate of 1,760 GPU-hours per 10B tokens per epoch, and thus roughly 100–200 GPU-hours for a multi-epoch run on the strongest subsets. This is within the budget of a typical academic GPU allocation. The concrete recipe: start with Mistral-7B (or a comparable open-weight model), continually pre-train on the Textbooks subset (0.56B tokens) for the broadest gains (+9.09 on SAT-MATH, +6.87 on MathQA, +5.12 on AQuA) or on the Wikipedia subset (0.18B tokens) for the strongest per-token efficiency (+7.48 on AQuA, +6.36 on SAT-MATH, +5.46 on MathQA), evaluate on the target application's mathematical reasoning tasks, and iterate. The paper's quality annotations enable custom filtering if the target application requires different quality thresholds than the defaults.

**2. Contamination-aware evaluation for mathematical reasoning benchmarks.** The paper's finding that OpenWebMath contains 195 MATH test set occurrences and 65 MMLU-STEM occurrences, while MATHPILE contains only 23 and 2 respectively after proactive removal, provides a practical quality signal for model evaluators and benchmark maintainers. An organization running a mathematical reasoning benchmark leaderboard can now distinguish between models trained on MATHPILE (where contamination has been systematically detected and removed, with documented detection methodology) and models trained on corpora without contamination reports. Concretely: a model claiming state-of-the-art MATH accuracy should be expected to disclose its training corpus's contamination status, and training on MATHPILE (with its documented <25 exact-match MATH items removed) provides a stronger contamination guarantee than training on OpenWebMath (with 195 items present). This is not just a theoretical concern — the paper's Table 19 shows that OpenWebMath contains verbatim MATH benchmark questions with solution context ("What is the sum of an infinite geometric series..."), meaning a model trained on uncleaned OpenWebMath could memorize these problems and inflate its MATH benchmark score by 1–2 percentage points (the fraction of the test set represented by 195 contaminated items out of 5,000 MATH problems is ~3.9%, though actual score inflation depends on memorization rate).

**3. Curriculum design for domain-adaptive continued pre-training.** The subset-level results in Table 4 reveal that different MATHPILE sources improve different capabilities, and that combination is non-additive. A practitioner who wants to improve their model on a specific mathematical task can use these results as a lookup table: if the target is competition-level mathematics (MATH benchmark), the strongest contributor is Textbooks (+2.02 points, from 10.08% to 12.10%); if the target is grade-school word problems (GSM8K), Wikipedia is strongest (+2.58, to 49.96%); if the target is multiple-choice STEM reasoning (MMLU-MATH), Textbooks lead (+4.01, to 48.93%). The negative results are equally actionable: StackExchange degrades GSM8K (−4.32) and should be avoided if that task is important; arXiv degrades MATH (−2.58), SAT-MATH (−4.55), and MathQA (−5.46) and should be avoided for problem-solving applications. This per-source capability profiling transforms MATHPILE from a monolithic corpus into a configurable toolkit where subsets can be selected and mixed based on the target application's requirements, analogous to how practitioners select fine-tuning datasets based on task alignment. The paper's quality annotations further enable filtering within each subset (e.g., selecting only long, notation-dense arXiv papers for theorem-proving training while excluding short, notation-light papers that may be less valuable).

**4. A reference pipeline for building domain-specific corpora in other technical fields.** The paper's documented methodology — source-specific preprocessing → source-adaptive global processing (language ID, cleaning, deduplication) → proactive contamination detection → quality-annotated release — is a transferable template. A team building a corpus for another technical domain (e.g., biomedical text, legal documents, code documentation) can adopt MATHPILE's pipeline structure while substituting domain-specific sources and heuristics. The key transferable design decisions are: (a) the use of source-specific FastText thresholds after observing that the standard 0.5 threshold produces domain-dependent false positive rates, (b) the eleven heuristic cleaning rules as a starting point that can be adapted by adding domain-specific rules (e.g., removing HIPAA boilerplate for medical text, removing license headers for code) while retaining the general rules (ellipsis ratio, bullet point ratio, stop word checks), (c) the MinHash LSH deduplication configuration (5-grams, SHA1, 450×20 buckets) as a validated parameter set, (d) the contamination detection procedure (line-level exact match on benchmark test sets before corpus release) as a mandatory quality gate, and (e) the per-document quality annotation scheme as a mechanism for downstream users to customize filtering without re-running the pipeline. The paper's release of processing scripts (at the GitHub repository) makes this template directly reusable — a practitioner can fork the scripts, replace the source-specific collectors with their domain's sources, adjust the heuristic rules based on manual inspection of their domain's noise patterns, and produce a domain-specific corpus with comparable quality guarantees.
