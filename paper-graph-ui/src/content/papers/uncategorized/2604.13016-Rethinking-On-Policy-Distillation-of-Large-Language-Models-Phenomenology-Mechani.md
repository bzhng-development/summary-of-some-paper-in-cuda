# Rethinking On-Policy Distillation of Large Language Models: Phenomenology, Mechanism, and Recipe

**ArXiv:** [2604.13016](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.13016)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper systematically studies the training dynamics of on-policy distillation (OPD) for large language models, analyzing a range of teacher–student configurations—including Qwen3-1.7B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B/7B, and their RL-fine-tuned variants—on mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME 2024/2025, AMC 2023).

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper systematically studies the training dynamics of on-policy distillation (OPD) for large language models, analyzing a range of teacher–student configurations—including Qwen3-1.7B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B/7B, and their RL-fine-tuned variants—on mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME 2024/2025, AMC 2023). The work identifies two governing conditions for OPD effectiveness: **thinking-pattern consistency** (operationalized as the initial overlap ratio between student and teacher top-𝑘 token distributions) and the presence of **genuinely new knowledge** beyond what the student has already acquired during training (operationalized as the performance gap between a same-family teacher and an RL-post-trained teacher derived from the same base checkpoint). Successful OPD is shown to be driven by **progressive alignment on high-probability overlap tokens**, a self-reinforcing dynamic where shared top-𝑘 tokens concentrate 97–99% of the probability mass and optimizing only these tokens suffices to match standard OPD performance. Two practical recipes—off-policy cold start and teacher-aligned prompt selection—recover failing OPD configurations by bridging the thinking-pattern gap, establishing that OPD’s apparent dense supervision degrades systematically with trajectory depth and is exploitable only when the teacher’s token-level signal aligns with student-visited states.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don't Understand Why On-Policy Distillation Works—or When It Fails

On-policy distillation (OPD) has rapidly become a cornerstone technique in the post-training pipeline of modern large language models. Major industry releases—Qwen3, MiMo, GLM-5—all incorporate OPD, and independent replication efforts have confirmed that on-policy, dense token-level supervision from a teacher model can serve as a computationally efficient alternative to outcome-reward reinforcement learning (RL). The technique is simple in principle: let the student model generate its own rollouts, then use the teacher's per-token log-probabilities as a dense reward signal to refine the student's behavior on states the student actually visits.

Yet despite this widespread adoption and empirical success, **the training dynamics of OPD remain poorly understood**. The paper identifies a striking and counterintuitive failure mode that exposes this gap: *a stronger teacher can completely fail to improve a student, even when a weaker teacher succeeds from lower initial alignment*. Specifically, when DeepSeek-R1-Distill-7B serves as a teacher for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B, the student shows essentially no improvement—despite the 7B model being substantially larger and scoring higher on mathematical reasoning benchmarks. Meanwhile, JustRL-1.5B, a model obtained by applying reinforcement learning to the same 1.5B base checkpoint, succeeds as a teacher and drives meaningful gains.

This failure mode cannot be explained by the conventional wisdom about knowledge distillation. The standard assumption—that a stronger teacher provides a better distillation signal—breaks down. The paper frames this as a fundamental scientific question: what conditions govern whether the teacher's token-level signal actually steers the student distribution in the desired direction, and what conditions cause it to be ineffective or even harmful?

### Why This Problem Matters

The gap in understanding OPD is significant for several practical and theoretical reasons:

**Practical deployment fragility.** OPD is being deployed at scale in production post-training pipelines, but without a principled understanding of when it will work, engineering teams face costly trial-and-error. The paper's observation that a stronger teacher can fail while a weaker one succeeds means that simply choosing the best available teacher model is not a reliable strategy. Understanding the conditions that predict success or failure would allow practitioners to select teacher-student pairs more intelligently, anticipate failure modes before committing computational resources, and apply targeted interventions when OPD is expected to underperform.

**Theoretical opacity of token-level supervision.** Unlike outcome-reward RL, which provides a single scalar signal at the end of a trajectory, OPD provides a per-token signal at every generation step. This dense supervision is often cited as OPD's primary advantage—every token receives feedback, not just the final answer. But the paper's findings suggest that this apparent advantage comes with hidden costs. The quality of the teacher's token-level reward degrades systematically with trajectory depth (Section 6.1), and even when the reward is globally informative about rollout correctness, it may not be locally exploitable for optimization (Section 6.2). Understanding *why* dense supervision works when it does—and what makes it fragile when it doesn't—is a prerequisite for designing more robust distillation objectives.

**Connection to broader distillation phenomena.** The knowledge distillation literature has long recognized that large teacher-student capacity gaps can degrade or even reverse the benefit of distillation (Cho and Hariharan, 2019; Mirzadeh et al., 2020; Busbridge et al., 2025). However, these analyses have been centered on *off-policy* distillation, where the student is trained on fixed teacher-generated sequences. In OPD, the student generates its own rollouts, which introduces a fundamentally different dynamic: the teacher's supervision is evaluated on student-visited states, which may be distributionally far from states the teacher would naturally visit. The paper's findings about thinking-pattern consistency and overlap dynamics suggest that the capacity gap problem manifests differently in on-policy settings, and that existing off-policy analyses do not directly transfer.

**Self-distillation and continual improvement.** Recent work has extended OPD to self-distillation settings where a single model serves as its own teacher given privileged information (e.g., ground-truth solutions, execution feedback). In these settings, thinking-pattern consistency is guaranteed by construction, but the question of new knowledge—what the model learns from its own outputs that it didn't already know—becomes more subtle. The paper's framework for understanding OPD dynamics provides a foundation for analyzing these emerging techniques, even though self-distillation itself is not the paper's primary focus.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies several specific limitations in prior work that motivate its investigation:

**Success stories without failure analyses.** The existing OPD literature is largely a collection of success stories. MiniLLM (Gu et al., 2023) formalized on-policy distillation under a reverse KL objective and demonstrated improvements over off-policy baselines. GKD (Agarwal et al., 2024) introduced a unified framework interpolating between on-policy and off-policy data across multiple divergences. Yang et al. (2026b) provided a theoretical treatment of OPD as dense KL-constrained RL and showed that the teacher's per-token log-ratio constitutes an implicit reward. These works establish that OPD *can* work, but none systematically analyzes *when or why it fails*. The paper positions itself as filling precisely this gap: moving from existence proofs of OPD's effectiveness to a mechanistic understanding of its training dynamics, including failure modes.

**Confounding factors in capacity gap studies.** Prior work on the capacity gap in distillation (Cho and Hariharan, 2019; Mirzadeh et al., 2020) has documented that large teacher-student disparities can hurt performance, but these studies operate in off-policy regimes and often confound model scale with other factors (architecture differences, training data differences, tokenizer mismatches). The paper's reverse distillation experiments (Section 3.3) provide a cleaner test: using JustRL-1.5B as the student and distilling from its own pre-RL checkpoint (R1-Distill-1.5B), the student regresses almost exactly to its pre-RL performance, removing all gains from RL. This demonstrates that the "teacher strength" confound can be isolated: the issue is not scale per se, but whether the teacher offers thinking patterns and knowledge that the student hasn't already internalized.

**No token-level mechanistic account.** Perhaps most critically, prior OPD work provides no account of *which tokens* drive the optimization signal. The paper's key mechanistic finding—that successful OPD is characterized by progressive alignment on a small set of high-probability overlap tokens that carry 97–99% of the probability mass, and that optimizing only these overlap tokens suffices to match standard OPD performance (Section 4.2)—is entirely absent from prior work. This granular understanding is what enables the paper to diagnose failures (stagnant overlap, persistent entropy mismatch) and design targeted interventions (off-policy cold start to raise initial overlap, teacher-aligned prompts to sharpen alignment on shared tokens).

**Exposure bias acknowledged but not analyzed.** The standard motivation for OPD over off-policy distillation is the train-inference distribution mismatch: off-policy methods train on teacher-generated sequences but must generate from the student's own distribution at inference, an instance of exposure bias (Bengio et al., 2015) that accumulates errors over long generations. While this motivation is widely cited, prior work has not analyzed how exposure bias manifests differently across different teacher-student configurations or how OPD's on-policy sampling interacts with teacher quality. The paper's difficulty-level and trajectory-depth analyses (Section 6) provide the first systematic look at how token-level supervision quality varies with the student's divergence from the teacher's familiar territory.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution as a systematic investigation of OPD training dynamics, organized into three layers: **phenomenology** (what conditions distinguish effective from ineffective OPD), **mechanism** (what happens at the token level during successful and failing runs), and **recipe** (how to recover failing configurations based on mechanistic understanding).

This is explicitly not a paper proposing a new distillation algorithm. Rather, it takes an existing technique that is widely used but poorly understood, and provides a unified framework for reasoning about when and why it works. The paper's intellectual contribution is the identification of two governing conditions—thinking-pattern consistency and genuinely new knowledge—and the demonstration that these conditions manifest through a specific token-level mechanism: progressive alignment on high-probability overlap tokens at student-visited states.

The paper draws on concepts from several literatures but synthesizes them in a novel way. The overlap ratio metric (the proportion of tokens appearing in both the student's and teacher's top-𝑘 sets) is inspired by mode-seeking analyses of reverse KL divergence (Gu et al., 2023), but the paper uses it not merely as a diagnostic but as a causal locus of optimization: overlap tokens are where the gradient signal concentrates, and optimizing only these tokens suffices. The finding that benchmark scores do not predict OPD outcomes connects to the capacity gap literature (Cho and Hariharan, 2019; Li et al., 2025) but reframes it: the issue is not capacity per se, but whether the teacher's thinking patterns are compatible with the student's and whether the teacher possesses knowledge beyond what the student has already acquired.

A crucial choice in the paper's positioning is the decision to study OPD in the context of mathematical reasoning (AIME 2024/2025, AMC 2023) rather than, say, general instruction following or open-ended generation. This choice is deliberate: mathematical reasoning provides clean correctness signals for validation, requires multi-step logical deduction where token-level supervision should theoretically help the most, and exhibits a wide range of difficulty that allows studying OPD dynamics across different performance regimes. The paper acknowledges that extending the findings to other domains (code, agentic settings) is an important open question (Section 8).

The paper also explicitly connects its mechanistic findings to practical concerns about OPD's scalability. The trajectory-depth analysis (Section 6.1) showing that teacher reward quality degrades systematically with response length, and that instability originates at later tokens before propagating backward, raises fundamental questions about whether current OPD techniques can extend to the long-horizon reasoning and agentic settings that represent the frontier of LLM applications. This positions the paper not just as a post-hoc analysis of existing techniques, but as a foundation for understanding the limitations that future work must address.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The system under study is not a single model but rather a **training procedure**—on-policy distillation (OPD)—that transfers knowledge from a frozen teacher LLM to a student LLM by having the student generate its own responses and then using the teacher's per-token probability judgments as a dense supervision signal. The paper's core intellectual move is to treat OPD not as a black-box optimization algorithm, but as a **dynamical system** whose behavior can be understood by tracking a specific set of token-level metrics—overlap ratio, overlap-token advantage, entropy gap—that reveal whether the teacher's signal is actually steering the student in a productive direction or merely adding noise.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The OPD training loop has five interacting components, though unlike a deployed system, these are conceptual lenses through which the paper analyzes a shared training process:

1. **Student model (`$\pi_\theta$`)** — the LLM being trained. It generates complete response rollouts autoregressively from input prompts, and its parameters are updated via gradient descent to minimize a divergence from the teacher's distribution. The student is the *only* component whose weights change during OPD.
2. **Teacher model (`$\pi_T$`)** — a frozen LLM that provides the target distribution. The teacher receives the same prefixes the student generated (not its own prefixes) and outputs a probability distribution over the next token at each position. This is the critical on-policy aspect: supervision is computed on student-visited states.
3. **Prompt dataset (`$\mathcal{D}_x$`)** — a collection of input prompts (questions) from which the student samples during training. The choice of prompts (whether they match the teacher's post-training data) turns out to significantly affect OPD dynamics (Section 5.2).
4. **OPD loss function** — the mathematical objective that compares student and teacher distributions at each token position along student-generated trajectories. The paper studies three variants (sampled-token, full-vocabulary, top-`$k$`) that differ in how many tokens per position contribute to the gradient.
5. **Dynamic metrics** — a suite of diagnostic quantities (overlap ratio, overlap-token advantage, entropy, entropy gap) that are monitored during training but do not affect the optimization directly. These metrics are the paper's primary analytical tool for understanding *why* some OPD runs succeed and others fail.

Information flows as follows: a prompt is sampled from `$\mathcal{D}_x$` → the student generates a full response `$\hat{y}$` autoregressively → at each generation step `$t$`, both the student and teacher compute next-token distributions `$p_t$` and `$q_t$` over the vocabulary, conditioned on the same student-generated prefix `$\hat{y}_{<t}$` → the OPD loss compares `$p_t$` against `$q_t$` (restricted to either the full vocabulary, a single sampled token, or the top-`$k$` tokens) → gradients flow back through the student only → the dynamic metrics are computed on the side to track alignment between `$p_t$` and `$q_t$` without affecting the forward-backward pass.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the three OPD loss variants (sampled-token, full-vocabulary, top-`$k$`) and their exact mathematical forms, because these define what "distillation" means operationally and determine the granularity of teacher supervision.
- **Second**, the dynamic metrics (overlap ratio, overlap-token advantage, entropy, entropy gap) and why they were chosen, because these are the lens through which the entire paper interprets training dynamics—they must be defined before any results can be understood.
- **Third**, the experimental design logic behind the paper's controlled comparisons (same student, different teachers; same teachers with and without RL post-training; reverse distillation), because the paper's claims about thinking-pattern consistency and new knowledge depend on isolating specific causal factors.
- **Fourth**, the two-stage intervention design (off-policy cold start and teacher-aligned prompt selection) and how they are motivated by the mechanistic analysis, because these represent the paper's translation of understanding into actionable recipes.
- **Fifth**, the trajectory-depth analysis setup and the global-vs-local reward distinction (Section 6), because this probes the fundamental limits of OPD's dense supervision assumption.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **analytical paper** whose core contribution is a framework for understanding OPD training dynamics through a specific set of token-level metrics applied across carefully controlled teacher-student configurations. No new training algorithm is proposed; rather, existing OPD variants are systematically compared under conditions that isolate the effects of thinking-pattern compatibility and knowledge novelty.

#### The Three OPD Loss Variants

OPD minimizes a divergence between the student's and teacher's next-token distributions, but different implementations differ in *how many tokens per position* contribute to the loss and *how the divergence is estimated*. The paper studies three variants that form a spectrum from minimal to maximal supervision density.

**Sequence-level objective.** All variants start from the same high-level formulation: minimize the sequence-level reverse Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence over student-generated trajectories:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{OPD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_x} \left[ D_{\text{KL}} \left( \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_T(\cdot \mid x) \right) \right] $$

where `$x$` is an input prompt sampled from the prompt dataset `$\mathcal{D}_x$`, `$\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$` is the student's distribution over complete response sequences, `$\pi_T(\cdot \mid x)$` is the teacher's distribution over complete response sequences, and `$D_{\text{KL}}(P \| Q) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} P(v) \log \frac{P(v)}{Q(v)}$` is the KL divergence.

**What it computes:** the expected (over prompts) KL divergence from the teacher's sequence distribution to the student's sequence distribution. Because this is *reverse* KL (`$D_{\text{KL}}(\text{student} \| \text{teacher})$`), the objective penalizes the student for placing probability mass on sequences the teacher considers unlikely (mode-seeking behavior), but does not strongly penalize the student for failing to cover all modes of the teacher (unlike forward KL, which is mass-covering).

**Why this form:** reverse KL is mode-seeking rather than mass-covering. In the context of distillation, this means the student is encouraged to concentrate its probability mass on the teacher's high-probability regions rather than spreading thin across all possible outputs the teacher might generate. MiniLLM (Gu et al., 2023) argued this prevents the student from wasting capacity on regions the teacher considers unlikely. The paper's overlap-token analysis effectively shows that this mode-seeking property manifests as progressive concentration of probability mass onto a small set of shared high-probability tokens.

**Autoregressive decomposition.** Using the chain rule of probability, the sequence-level KL decomposes into a sum of per-token KL divergences:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{OPD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_x,\, \hat{y} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} D_{\text{KL}}(p_t \,\|\, q_t) \right] $$

where `$T = |\hat{y}|$` is the length of the student-generated response, `$\hat{y} = (\hat{y}_1, \ldots, \hat{y}_T)$` is the response sampled autoregressively from the student, `$p_t(v) = \pi_\theta(v \mid x, \hat{y}_{<t})$` is the student's next-token distribution at step `$t$` conditioned on the student-generated prefix `$\hat{y}_{<t}$`, and `$q_t(v) = \pi_T(v \mid x, \hat{y}_{<t})$` is the teacher's next-token distribution at the same step conditioned on the *same student-generated prefix*.

**What it computes:** the expected sum of per-step token-level reverse KL divergences, where each step's distributions are conditioned on the prefix the student actually generated up to that point. The expectation is over both prompts and student-generated trajectories, making this a fully on-policy objective: the student is evaluated on its own induced state distribution.

**Why this form:** the decomposition is exact (no approximation beyond the Markov factorization), and it reveals that OPD provides a *dense* signal—one KL term per generated token—rather than a single scalar reward at sequence end. This density is what makes OPD potentially more sample-efficient than outcome-reward RL, but it also means the signal depends on the teacher being well-calibrated on student-visited prefixes, which Section 6 shows degrades with trajectory depth.

**Sampled-token OPD.** The most computationally lightweight variant evaluates only the single token that the student actually sampled at each position:

$$ \mathcal{L}^{\text{sample}}_{\text{OPD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_x,\, \hat{y} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} \ell^{\text{sample}}_t \right] $$

where `$\ell^{\text{sample}}_t = \log p_t(\hat{y}_t) - \log q_t(\hat{y}_t)$` is the log-ratio for the sampled token `$\hat{y}_t \sim p_t$`.

**What it computes:** at each generation step, the loss is simply the difference between the student's log-probability and the teacher's log-probability for the token the student actually produced. Positive values mean the student assigned higher probability to its chosen token than the teacher would have, indicating divergence; the loss pushes these values toward zero.

**Why this form:** since `$\mathbb{E}_{\hat{y}_t \sim p_t} [\ell^{\text{sample}}_t] = D_{\text{KL}}(p_t \| q_t)$`, each `$\ell^{\text{sample}}_t$` is an unbiased single-sample Monte Carlo estimator of the per-token reverse KL. This eliminates the need to compute and compare full vocabulary distributions at each step, making it the most common implementation in prior work (Lu and Lab, 2025; Xiao et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026b). The paper shows (Section 6.3, Figure 15) that sampled-token OPD performs comparably to top-`$k$` OPD with `$k \geq 4$`, establishing that the variance from single-sample estimation is not a bottleneck in practice for this domain.

**Full-vocabulary OPD.** At the opposite extreme, one computes the divergence exactly over the entire vocabulary:

$$ \mathcal{L}^{\text{full}}_{\text{OPD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_x,\, \hat{y} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} D_{\text{KL}}(p_t \,\|\, q_t) \right] $$

**What it computes:** the same objective as the sequence-level decomposition, but without Monte Carlo approximation—every token in the vocabulary contributes to the gradient at every position, requiring `$\mathcal{O}(B T M)$` memory for batch size `$B$`, sequence length `$T$`, and vocabulary size `$M = |\mathcal{V}|$`.

**Why this form exists:** full-vocabulary OPD provides the densest possible gradient signal and eliminates sampling variance entirely. However, its memory cost makes it impractical for large vocabulary sizes (tens to hundreds of thousands of tokens), especially with long reasoning traces. The paper does not use full-vocabulary OPD in its main experiments; it is included for conceptual completeness and as the theoretical upper bound that top-`$k$` OPD approximates.

**Top-`$k$` OPD.** The paper's primary experimental variant restricts the divergence computation to the `$k$` tokens assigned highest probability by the student:

$$ \mathcal{L}^{\text{top-}k}_{\text{OPD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_x,\, \hat{y} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} D_{\text{KL}} \left( \bar{p}^{(S_t)}_t \,\|\, \bar{q}^{(S_t)}_t \right) \right] $$

where `$S_t = \text{TopK}(p_t, k)$` is the set of `$k$` tokens with highest probability under the student's distribution `$p_t$`, and `$\bar{p}^{(S_t)}_t$` and `$\bar{q}^{(S_t)}_t$` are the student and teacher distributions renormalized over `$S_t$`:

$$ \bar{p}^{(S_t)}_t(v) = \frac{p_t(v) \cdot \mathbf{1}[v \in S_t]}{\sum_{u \in S_t} p_t(u)}, \quad \bar{q}^{(S_t)}_t(v) = \frac{q_t(v) \cdot \mathbf{1}[v \in S_t]}{\sum_{u \in S_t} q_t(u)} $$

**What it computes:** the KL divergence between the student and teacher distributions after both are renormalized to sum to 1 over the student's top-`$k$` tokens. Tokens outside `$S_t$` are ignored entirely—they contribute zero gradient. The student's top-`$k$` selection criterion means the tokens receiving supervision are precisely those the student considers most likely at each position.

**Why this form:** top-`$k$` OPD provides a computationally efficient middle ground between sampled-token and full-vocabulary OPD. By restricting to `$k$` tokens (typically `$k = 16$` in the paper's default configuration), it reduces teacher-query cost by a factor of `$M/k$` compared to full-vocabulary (tens of thousands to hundreds) while preserving multi-token supervision on the student's high-probability region. The choice of *student* top-`$k$` (rather than teacher top-`$k$` or a union) is motivated by the paper's finding that the overlap between student and teacher top-`$k$` sets is what drives optimization (Section 4.2)—selecting the student's high-probability tokens means the computation focuses on the region where alignment matters most. The default value `$k = 16$` is chosen based on the finding (Section 6.3) that increasing `$k$` beyond 4 brings negligible additional gain, though Top-1 (always selecting the argmax token) is unstable because small policy changes can flip which token occupies rank 1, creating a biased, mode-concentrated reward signal.

#### Dynamic Metrics for Monitoring OPD Training

The paper's primary analytical contribution is operationalizing OPD training dynamics through a set of token-level metrics that are computed on the fly during training but do not affect the gradient updates. These metrics are the lens through which the paper distinguishes successful from failing OPD runs and motivates its practical interventions.

**Overlap ratio.** The proportion of tokens that appear in both the student's and teacher's top-`$k$` sets, averaged across generation steps:

$$ \mathcal{M}_{\text{overlap}} = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \frac{|S^{(p)}_t \cap S^{(q)}_t|}{k} \right] $$

where `$S^{(p)}_t = \text{TopK}(p_t, k)$` is the set of `$k$` tokens with highest probability under the student at step `$t$`, `$S^{(q)}_t = \text{TopK}(q_t, k)$` is the corresponding set under the teacher, and `$|\cdot|$` denotes set cardinality. The expectation `$\mathbb{E}_t$` averages over all generation steps across a batch of student-generated trajectories.

**What it computes:** for each generation step, count how many tokens appear in *both* the student's top-`$k$` and the teacher's top-`$k$`, divide by `$k$`, then average over all steps. A ratio of `$0.72$` means 72% of the student's top-`$k$` tokens also appear in the teacher's top-`$k$`, on average; a ratio of `$1.0$` means the two top-`$k$` sets are identical (at the set level, not necessarily in ordering or probability mass).

**Why this metric:** overlap ratio operationalizes "thinking-pattern consistency"—the paper's first governing condition for OPD success. A low initial overlap indicates that the student's probability mass is concentrated on a disjoint set of tokens from the teacher, suggesting significant policy divergence or "mode mismatch" where the student is exploring a different region of the generation space than the teacher occupies. The paper's central dynamic finding (Section 4.1) is that in successful OPD runs, overlap rises steadily from training start (e.g., from 72% to 91%), while in failing runs it stagnates from the outset. This makes overlap ratio both a diagnostic (predicting whether OPD will succeed) and a causal locus: the overlap region is where gradients concentrate, and growing overlap indicates a self-reinforcing dynamic where teacher-favored tokens accumulate student probability mass.

**Overlap-token advantage.** Within the overlap set, this metric measures how well the student's probability mass is calibrated to the teacher's preferences:

$$ \mathcal{M}_{\text{adv}} = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \frac{1}{|S^{(p)}_t \cap S^{(q)}_t|} \sum_{v \in S^{(p)}_t \cap S^{(q)}_t} A_t(v) \right] $$

where `$A_t(v) = \bar{p}_t(v) (\log \bar{q}_t(v) - \log \bar{p}_t(v))$`, and `$\bar{p}_t, \bar{q}_t$` are the student and teacher distributions renormalized over the overlap set `$S^{(p)}_t \cap S^{(q)}_t$` only (not over the full top-`$k$`).

**What it computes:** for each token in the overlap set, compute the student's renormalized probability `$\bar{p}_t(v)$` times the log-ratio of teacher to student probability (also renormalized). This is a signed quantity: negative values mean the student places more mass on token `$v$` than the teacher would (the student is "overconfident" relative to the teacher within the overlap region), positive values mean the student places less mass than the teacher would. The metric averages the absolute advantage (after weighting by `$\bar{p}_t$`) across overlap tokens and then across steps. A value approaching zero indicates that within the overlap region, the student and teacher distributions closely match.

**Why this metric:** overlap ratio tells you whether the student and teacher agree on *which* tokens are likely; overlap-token advantage tells you whether they agree on *how* likely. Two models could have perfect overlap (same top-`$k$` set) but very different probability assignments within that set. The paper finds that successful OPD drives the overlap-token advantage toward zero (the student calibrates its confidence within the shared region), while failing OPD shows persistently large magnitude (the student cannot or does not adjust its within-overlap probability allocation). The renormalization over the overlap set (rather than the full top-`$k$`) is critical: it isolates calibration within the shared region from the effects of the overlap set growing or shrinking over time.

**Entropy and entropy gap.** The entropy of each model's next-token distribution and the absolute difference between them:

$$ \Delta H_t = |H(q_t) - H(p_t)| $$

where `$H(p_t) = -\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_t(v) \log p_t(v)$` is the Shannon entropy of the student's next-token distribution at step `$t$` (and analogously for `$H(q_t)$`).

**What it computes:** entropy measures the uncertainty or "flatness" of a probability distribution—a uniform distribution over the vocabulary has maximum entropy, while a distribution concentrated on a single token has zero entropy. The entropy gap `$\Delta H_t$` compares the student's and teacher's uncertainty at the same student-visited state. A large gap indicates that at step `$t$`, one model is much more confident (lower entropy, peaked distribution) than the other, suggesting a mismatch in how they assess the diversity of plausible continuations from the student-generated prefix.

**Why this metric:** entropy gap is a state-specific indicator of mode alignment. A diverging entropy gap—as seen in failing OPD runs (Figure 6, bottom-right)—indicates that the teacher is becoming systematically more or less certain than the student on the student's own trajectories. The paper finds that successful OPD drives the entropy gap toward zero, meaning the student matches not just which tokens the teacher prefers but also the teacher's level of confidence at each state. This is important because it suggests OPD does more than imitation of token choices; it also transfers the teacher's *uncertainty calibration*, which is crucial for the student to know when it is and isn't reliable.

**Overlap mass.** Beyond set-level overlap, the paper also tracks what fraction of total probability mass the overlap tokens carry for each model:

$$ \mathcal{M}^{(p)}_{\text{overlap-mass}} = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \sum_{v \in S^{(p)}_t \cap S^{(q)}_t} p_t(v) \right], \quad \mathcal{M}^{(q)}_{\text{overlap-mass}} = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \sum_{v \in S^{(p)}_t \cap S^{(q)}_t} q_t(v) \right] $$

**What it computes:** for the student (respectively teacher), sum the raw probabilities `$p_t(v)$` or `$q_t(v)$` of all tokens in the overlap set—without renormalization. Since the overlap set typically has `$k$` or fewer elements while the vocabulary has tens of thousands, a high overlap mass (e.g., 0.97–0.99) means that despite the overlap set being small in cardinality, it accounts for nearly all the probability mass that either model assigns at that step.

**Why this metric:** it addresses a potential misinterpretation of the overlap ratio. A high overlap ratio (both models agree on which `$k$` tokens are top) could be misleading if those tokens collectively carry only a small fraction of the total probability mass, with most mass scattered across a long tail of low-probability tokens. The finding (Appendix B.1) that overlap mass consistently reaches 97–99% in successful runs confirms that the overlap set is genuinely the probabilistically dominant region for both models—the alignment is on the tokens that matter most, not on a set-level coincidence.

#### The Experimental Design Logic: Controlled Comparisons for Isolating Conditions

The paper's claims about thinking-pattern consistency and new knowledge are established through a series of carefully controlled comparisons that vary only one factor at a time. Understanding this design logic is essential for interpreting the results.

**Same student, different teachers with comparable benchmark performance but different thinking patterns (Section 3.1).** The student is Qwen3-1.7B-Base. The two teachers are Qwen3-4B (a non-thinking "standard" model) and Qwen3-4B-Base-GRPO (the same base checkpoint with additional GRPO reinforcement learning that induces chain-of-thought reasoning patterns). Both teachers achieve broadly comparable average accuracy on AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and AMC 2023 (Figure 3: Qwen3-4B Non-thinking achieves 0.212, 0.210, 0.700 respectively; Qwen3-4B-Base-GRPO achieves 0.204, 0.242, 0.599), yet the GRPO teacher has a thinking pattern more aligned with the base student's generation style. The key isolation is: if thinking-pattern consistency matters, the GRPO teacher should outperform despite not being systematically stronger on benchmarks.

**Why GRPO changes thinking patterns.** The GRPO training uses the DAPO-Math-17K dataset with a specific prompt template that asks the model to "reason step by step, and put your final answer within `\boxed{}`." This induces a structured reasoning format (explicit step-by-step reasoning followed by a boxed final answer) that differs from the non-thinking model's more free-form generation style. Since the student Qwen3-1.7B-Base is also a base model (not post-trained for thinking), the paper hypothesizes its natural generation pattern more closely resembles the GRPO-trained teacher than the non-thinking teacher, even though the GRPO teacher is a 4B model and the non-thinking teacher is a 4B model from the same family.

**Same student, matched teacher family but with and without RL post-training (Section 3.2).** Two families are tested. In the DeepSeek family, the student is DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B; teachers are DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (same distillation pipeline, different scale) and Skywork-OR1-Math-7B (the 7B model after additional RL post-training on math). In the Qwen family, the student is Qwen3-1.7B (Non-thinking); teachers are Qwen3-4B (Non-thinking) and Qwen3-4B-Non-Thinking-RL-Math (the 4B model after RL on a 57K subset of DeepMath). In both cases, the post-trained teacher is derived from the same base checkpoint as the non-post-trained teacher, so thinking patterns remain broadly similar (confirmed by comparable initial overlap ratios: 71.5% vs 74.7% in DeepSeek, 70.3% vs 75.7% in Qwen). The key isolation is: if genuinely new knowledge matters beyond pattern consistency, the post-trained teachers should yield significantly larger gains despite similar overlap, because they possess capabilities the student hasn't already internalized from the shared training pipeline.

**Why gap recovery rate is the key metric.** The paper introduces "gap recovery rate" = `$(\text{Acc}_{\text{after OPD}} - \text{Acc}_{\text{before OPD}}) / (\text{Acc}_{\text{teacher}} - \text{Acc}_{\text{before OPD}})$`. This normalizes the OPD improvement by the maximum possible improvement (reaching teacher performance). A same-family teacher with high benchmark scores but no new knowledge might achieve a low gap recovery rate even if absolute accuracy improves somewhat; a post-trained teacher with genuinely new capabilities should achieve a substantially higher gap recovery rate. The paper reports that post-trained teachers in the Qwen family recover 58.6% of the gap vs. 15.6% for the non-post-trained teacher, and in the DeepSeek family 16.9% vs. 5.3%, directly quantifying how much of the teacher's advantage is transferable through OPD.

**Reverse distillation: validation by inverting the direction (Section 3.3).** The student is JustRL-DeepSeek-1.5B, obtained by applying RL to DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B (so the student is the stronger, post-RL version). Two teachers are compared: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B (the student's own pre-RL checkpoint, which is weaker on benchmarks) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-7B (a larger model from the same family that slightly outscores the student on benchmarks). The key isolation: if OPD learns thinking patterns rather than simply transferring benchmark performance, both teachers should drive the student to approximately the same regressed level—back to the pre-RL checkpoint's performance—because both share the same thinking pattern (they are from the same distillation pipeline, just at different scales). Additionally, the 7B teacher's higher benchmark score should *not* translate into better distillation outcomes if new knowledge (not scale) is what matters, since the 7B model was trained on the same data with the same recipe as the 1.5B model.

**What the reverse distillation experiment tests that forward experiments cannot.** In forward distillation, a higher-performing teacher producing limited improvement could be due to either thinking-pattern mismatch or lack of new knowledge—the two conditions are confounded. In reverse distillation, the student starts *above* the teacher, so any regression is definitively caused by the teacher's thinking pattern overwriting the student's RL-acquired patterns. If the 7B teacher also causes regression despite higher benchmark scores, this confirms that OPD training dynamics are governed by local token-level alignment on student-visited states, not by the teacher's global performance ranking.

#### Default Training Configuration and Hyperparameters

Unless otherwise specified, all OPD experiments in the paper use the settings listed in Table 2 (Appendix A.2):

- **Training temperature:** 1.0 (both student sampling and teacher evaluation). A temperature of 1.0 means the raw logit outputs are used without sharpening or flattening.
- **Global batch size:** 64 prompts per update.
- **Mini batch size:** 64 (effectively no gradient accumulation—gradients are computed on the full batch).
- **Rollout number:** 4. For each prompt in the batch, the student generates 4 complete responses, and the OPD loss is averaged across these 4 rollouts. Rollout number > 1 provides a more stable on-policy gradient estimate by averaging over multiple sampled trajectories per prompt.
- **LogProb top-`$K$`:** 16. This is the `$k$` used in the top-`$k$` OPD variant.
- **Top-`$K$` strategy:** "Student Top-`$K$`"—the subset `$S_t$` is selected as the `$k$` tokens with highest probability under the *student's* distribution `$p_t$`, not the teacher's.
- **Top-`$p$`:** 1.0 (nucleus sampling disabled—no tail truncation during generation).
- **Max prompt length:** 1,024 tokens. Prompts longer than this are truncated.
- **Max response length:** 7,168 tokens. Responses longer than this are truncated during training. This default is deliberately moderate; Section 6.1 varies this up to 15,000 tokens to study trajectory-depth effects.
- **Learning rate:** `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`, constant (no schedule mentioned).
- **Epoch:** 1. Training proceeds for 200 steps in most experiments (one pass through the dataset).
- **KL Coefficient:** 0.0. No additional KL penalty toward a reference policy is used—the OPD loss itself is the only objective. This differs from RL fine-tuning setups that typically include a KL penalty toward the base model to prevent reward hacking.
- **Optimizer:** not explicitly specified in the main text (likely AdamW based on standard practice, but this is not stated).
- **Validation protocol:** the student is evaluated on AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and AMC 2023. For each benchmark, 16 solutions are sampled per problem with temperature 0.7 and top-`$p$` 0.95, using a maximum validation response length of 31,744 tokens. The primary metric is `avg@16`—the average accuracy across 16 samples, where each sample's answer is extracted and compared to the ground truth (exact match after parsing, using standard answer extraction for math problems—the specific extraction method is not detailed but is assumed to follow common practice for these benchmarks). `avg@16` measures the expected pass rate if one were to sample 16 times and accept any correct answer, which is strictly higher than pass@1 and provides a more stable metric for comparing training runs.

**Why these defaults were chosen.** The paper does not provide an explicit justification for most defaults, but several choices are interpretable. Learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-6}$` is typical for full-parameter fine-tuning of LLMs in the 1.5B–7B range, chosen to be small enough to avoid catastrophic forgetting of pretrained capabilities but large enough to produce meaningful updates over 200 steps. Rollout number 4 balances gradient variance reduction against the computational cost of generating additional trajectories per prompt—each additional rollout doubles the forward-pass cost (both student generation and teacher evaluation). Temperature 1.0 during training preserves the full entropy of the student's distribution, which is important for the entropy gap analysis (lowering temperature would artificially reduce entropy and mask the true alignment dynamics). The choice of student top-`$k$` over teacher top-`$k$` is motivated by the overlap-token analysis: since the overlap set is what drives optimization, selecting the student's high-probability tokens ensures the supervision covers the region the student is actually exploring, maximizing the chance that teacher-preferred tokens are included in `$S_t$`.

#### The Off-Policy Cold Start Intervention Design

When the student and teacher begin with substantially different thinking patterns, the paper proposes a two-stage training approach (Section 5.1) that first narrows the gap through off-policy supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on teacher-generated rollouts, then proceeds with standard on-policy distillation.

**Stage 1: Cold-start SFT.** The student (Qwen3-1.7B-Base) is fine-tuned on a dataset of teacher-generated rollouts before any OPD begins.

**Teacher rollout generation.** The teacher (Qwen3-4B Non-thinking) generates 200K responses on a subset of the math-domain portion of OpenThoughts3-1.2M. The prompt template used is the teacher-aligned template: `"{Question} Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed{}."` Generation uses temperature 0.7, top-`$p$` 0.95, top-`$k$` = −1 (disabled), and maximum generation length 12,288 tokens. Incomplete and degenerate repetitive responses are filtered out.

**SFT training.** The student is trained with full-parameter SFT on the filtered teacher-generated prompt–response pairs using the LLaMA-Factory framework. Hyperparameters: training epochs = 1, sequence length = 14,336, per-device batch size = 8, gradient accumulation steps = 1 (effective batch size = 8), learning rate = `$1 \times 10^{-5}$`, cosine learning rate schedule with warmup ratio 0.05, BF16 precision. The output of this stage is Qwen3-1.7B-SFT, a student model whose generation distribution has been pulled closer to the teacher's thinking pattern through direct imitation of teacher outputs.

**Stage 2: OPD from SFT initialization.** Starting from Qwen3-1.7B-SFT (not Qwen3-1.7B-Base), the student is further trained with standard OPD using the remaining prompts from OpenThoughts after deduplicating against the SFT prompt subset (approximately 30K prompts). The teacher remains Qwen3-4B (Non-thinking), unchanged from Stage 1. All OPD hyperparameters follow the defaults (Table 2).

**Control baseline.** As a comparison, pure OPD is run starting directly from Qwen3-1.7B-Base using the same teacher and the same 30K OPD prompt set, without any cold-start distillation. The only difference between the two conditions is the initialization of the student—SFT-initialized vs. base-initialized—enabling a clean isolation of the cold start effect.

**Why this design works.** The SFT stage serves as a "thinking-pattern bridge." By training on teacher-generated outputs, the student learns to produce responses in the teacher's style—using similar vocabulary, similar step structures, similar reasoning formats—before OPD begins. This raises the initial overlap ratio (the student's top-`$k$` tokens already significantly overlap with the teacher's before OPD starts), which the paper's mechanistic analysis shows is the prerequisite for the self-reinforcing overlap growth dynamic. The SFT stage is deliberately off-policy (fixed teacher rollouts) because on-policy generation at this stage would produce low-quality trajectories from the untuned base model, providing a weak signal. After SFT, OPD can refine alignment on states the (now closer) student actually visits.

**Why cold-start SFT differs from simply doing more SFT.** A natural question is whether the two-stage approach is necessary—could the same benefit be achieved by simply doing more SFT on teacher rollouts? The paper argues implicitly that SFT alone suffers from exposure bias: the student learns to imitate teacher outputs on teacher-generated prefixes, but at inference time it generates from its own prefixes, which drift from the SFT training distribution. OPD corrects this by providing supervision on the student's own states, but it requires sufficient initial alignment for the supervision to be exploitable. The two-stage design separates these concerns: SFT handles the initial pattern alignment, OPD handles the distribution-matching on student-visited states.

#### The Teacher-Aligned Prompt Selection Intervention Design

When the teacher has undergone RL post-training, its token-level distribution is shaped by the specific prompts it encountered during that post-training. The paper tests whether using those same prompts during OPD improves the distillation signal (Section 5.2).

**Two granularities of alignment.** The paper separates prompt *template* alignment (how the task instruction is formatted) from prompt *content* alignment (which specific math problems appear).

**Prompt template alignment.** The teacher is JustRL-1.5B, the student is R1-Distill-1.5B, and the prompt set is DAPO-Math-17K in both conditions. The only difference is the template wrapping the math problem. The original DAPO template reads: `"Solve the following math problem step by step. The last line of your response should be of the form Answer: $Answer (without quotes) where $Answer is the answer to the problem. {Question} Remember to put your answer on its own line after 'Answer:'."` The teacher-aligned template (matching the format used during JustRL's post-training) reads: `"{Question} Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed{}."` The mathematical content of the questions is identical—the same problems appear in both conditions. This isolates the effect of surface-level formatting on OPD dynamics.

**Prompt content alignment.** The teacher is Qwen3-4B-Base-GRPO, the student is Qwen3-1.7B-Base. Two prompt sets of matched size are compared: DAPO-Math-17K (which overlaps with the teacher's RL training data) and a deduplicated subset of DeepMath (which is in the same mathematical domain but was not part of the teacher's RL training). The deduplication procedure (Appendix C.3) involves two stages: first, exact string matching on the extracted question text (removing any questions that appear verbatim in DAPO-Math-17K); second, semantic deduplication using sentence embeddings from `all-mpnet-base-v2` (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019), where any DeepMath question whose cosine similarity to the nearest DAPO-Math-17K question exceeds 0.6 is removed. Both prompt sets contain challenging math problems, but only DAPO-Math-17K contains the exact prompts the teacher was optimized on.

**Why prompt alignment matters.** The teacher's RL post-training optimized its policy on a specific set of prompts. On those prompts, the teacher's distribution is sharper and better-calibrated—it has learned through RL which reasoning steps lead to correct answers for these exact problems or very similar ones. On out-of-distribution prompts (even in-domain math problems the teacher hasn't seen), the teacher's distribution may be less peaked or less reliable, providing a weaker distillation signal. At the token level, teacher-aligned prompts should produce higher initial overlap (the student's generation on those prompts more closely resembles the states the teacher excelled at) and larger overlap-token advantages (clearer signal on which tokens to prefer).

**The entropy trade-off.** The paper notes an important side effect: using only teacher-aligned prompts during OPD leads to substantially lower student entropy (Figure 10, bottom-right). The student's distribution becomes more peaked, concentrating probability mass on fewer tokens. While this sharpens alignment on shared tokens, it may reduce the student's capacity for exploration and generalization. The paper therefore recommends mixing teacher-aligned prompts with out-of-distribution prompts as a practical strategy to preserve policy entropy while still benefiting from the sharper alignment signal on in-distribution prompts.

#### Trajectory Depth Analysis: Probing Reward Quality Decay

Section 6 investigates whether the teacher's token-level supervision remains reliable at arbitrary generation depths—a fundamental assumption underlying OPD's design.

**Maximum response length sweep.** The student (R1-Distill-1.5B) is trained against the teacher (JustRL-1.5B) under six different maximum response length settings: 0.5K, 1K, 3K, 7K, 10K, and 15K tokens. All other hyperparameters follow the defaults. The question is whether longer responses—which provide more supervised tokens—translate to better downstream performance, or whether supervision quality degrades with depth to the point of being counterproductive.

**Teacher continuation experiment.** For a direct probe of teacher reliability at different prefix depths, the paper conducts a controlled test. Two thousand prompts are sampled from DAPO-Math-17K. The student generates complete responses, and those exceeding 16K tokens are selected. Each rollout is then artificially truncated at multiple positions: 1K, 4K, 8K, and 16K tokens from the start. At each truncation point, the teacher continues generation from the student's prefix (rather than from its own prefix), and the teacher's continuation is evaluated for correctness. The accuracy gain from teacher continuation is defined as `$\text{Acc}_{\text{teacher continuation}} - \text{Acc}_{\text{student}}$`, measuring how much better the teacher performs than the student when both start from the same student-generated prefix.

**What this experiment measures that training doesn't.** During training, the teacher provides *per-token* log-probabilities, not complete continuations. The teacher continuation experiment measures something different: can the teacher, given only the student's (potentially flawed) prefix, recover and produce a correct answer? If the teacher's continuation accuracy degrades substantially at deeper prefix depths, it suggests that the teacher's token-level probabilities at those depths are also becoming unreliable—the teacher doesn't know how to proceed from states the student visits that are far from the teacher's own preferred trajectories. This provides a causal mechanism for the reward degradation observed in the length sweep: the teacher encounters increasingly unfamiliar prefixes and produces noisier token-level log-probabilities that destabilize the student's learning.

**Entropy heatmaps by output position.** For the 15K maximum response length setting, the paper tracks student and teacher entropy as a function of output position across training steps. Entropy is measured in "buckets" along the response length (0K–3K, 3K–6K, ..., 12K–15K) and across training steps (180 through 250, where late-stage collapse is observed). The visualization (Figures 13 and 23) shows whether instability first appears at later tokens and propagates backward (suggesting suffix-triggered collapse) or appears uniformly (suggesting a global degradation).

#### Global vs. Local Reward Informativeness

Section 6.2 tests whether failing OPD is caused by the teacher providing fundamentally uninformative reward, or whether the reward is informative globally but not locally exploitable for optimization.

**Sequence mean reward analysis.** For the controlled comparison with R1-Distill-1.5B as the student and two teachers (JustRL-1.5B, successful; R1-Distill-7B, failed), the paper computes the sequence-level mean reward for each student-generated rollout:

$$ \bar{r}(y) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left[ \log \pi_T(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}) \right] $$

where `$T$` is the rollout length, `$\pi_T(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$` is the teacher's probability of the student-sampled token `$y_t$`, and `$\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$` is the student's own probability. This is the per-token advantage averaged across the sequence—positive when the teacher assigns higher probability to the student's choices than the student itself does.

**What it computes:** for a complete student-generated response, average the token-level log-probability difference between teacher and student. A positive mean reward means the teacher generally "approves" of the student's token choices more than the student's own model does (the student is underestimating good tokens); a negative mean reward means the teacher considers the student's choices less likely than the student does (the student is overconfident in poor tokens).

**AUROC comparison.** The paper computes the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve for using `$\bar{r}(y)$` to discriminate between correct and incorrect student rollouts. AUROC = 1.0 means perfect separation (all correct rollouts have higher mean reward than all incorrect rollouts); AUROC = 0.5 means the reward provides no discriminative signal. The key finding (Figure 14) is that both the successful teacher (JustRL-1.5B, AUROC = 0.73) and the failing teacher (R1-Distill-7B, AUROC = 0.75) provide globally informative reward that separates correct from incorrect rollouts about equally well. The failure of the 7B teacher is therefore not due to the reward being globally uninformative—the teacher *can* tell which rollouts are good.

**The local optimization geometry hypothesis.** If the reward is globally informative in both cases, why does gradient-based optimization fail with the 7B teacher? The paper proposes (but does not verify) an anisotropy hypothesis: the 7B teacher's per-token advantages, while individually large, may point in inconsistent directions across positions within a sequence. When aggregated into a gradient update (via backpropagation through the autoregressive generation), these heterogeneous signals partially cancel, yielding small effective gradients. The JustRL-1.5B teacher, sharing a more compatible thinking pattern with the student, may concentrate its advantage on a more coherent subset of tokens whose gradients point in a consistent direction, producing a more effective update signal despite individually smaller per-token advantages.

**Evidence for the hypothesis.** The paper notes that in the failing run with R1-Distill-7B, the overlap-token advantage becomes *larger in magnitude* than in the successful run during later training stages (Figure 6, middle-bottom), yet the gradient norm remains *persistently smaller* (Appendix B.2, Figure 19, middle panel). This co-occurrence of high per-token advantage and low gradient norm is the paper's primary suggestive evidence for the anisotropy interpretation: large but directionally inconsistent per-token signals produce small gradients, while smaller but directionally consistent signals produce effective updates. The paper explicitly acknowledges this hypothesis is not verified and requires future analysis of the directional structure of per-token gradients.

**Why this distinction matters for OPD design.** If the problem were globally uninformative reward, the solution would be to find better teachers. If the problem is locally anisotropic reward—where the teacher can tell good from bad at the sequence level but its token-level signals don't coherently point toward improvement—then the solution may require different optimization objectives that can aggregate token-level signals more robustly, or methods that identify and weight the most directionally consistent tokens. This is framed as an open question (Section 6.2).

#### Summary of Design Philosophy and Key Choices

- **Reverse KL as the divergence measure:** mode-seeking rather than mass-covering, which the paper shows manifests as progressive probability concentration on shared high-probability tokens.
- **Student top-`$k$` selection for the loss support:** focuses computation on the region the student is actually exploring, where alignment matters most, and where the self-reinforcing overlap growth dynamic operates.
- **Default `$k = 16$`:** empirically sufficient (Section 6.3), with `$k = 1$` (always argmax) being unstable and `$k > 4$` providing negligible additional benefit.
- **Overlap ratio as the operationalization of thinking-pattern consistency:** a continuous, step-level metric that captures token-level agreement between models and is monitored passively (not optimized directly), enabling causal analysis rather than mere correlation.
- **Gap recovery rate as the normalized metric for knowledge transfer:** separates the effect of the teacher simply being better (which raises the ceiling) from the effect of the teacher's knowledge being transferable through OPD (which raises the fraction of the ceiling attained).
- **Reverse distillation as the definitive test of the thinking-pattern hypothesis:** by starting with a student stronger than the teacher, any regression must be due to the teacher's thinking pattern overwriting the student's, ruling out confounds from benchmark performance or model scale.
- **Cold-start SFT as a bridge, not a replacement:** the two-stage design recognizes that off-policy SFT and on-policy distillation serve complementary roles—SFT handles initial pattern alignment, OPD handles distribution matching on student-visited states—and neither alone achieves the full benefit.
- **Deliberately moderate default response length (7,168 tokens):** the trajectory-depth analysis (Section 6.1) shows that longer responses (10K–15K) can destabilize training, suggesting that OPD's dense supervision advantage has a practical ceiling that current techniques do not overcome.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: A Diagnostic Framework for Predicting OPD Success Without Running It

The paper's most fundamental intellectual contribution is transforming OPD from a black-box training procedure—where you run it and check whether accuracy went up—into a **dynamical system whose outcome can be predicted from token-level alignment metrics measured at initialization**. This is not an incremental improvement to OPD; it is a reframing of how to think about the problem entirely.

Prior to this work, the field's approach to OPD was essentially empirical: try a teacher, train for some number of steps, evaluate, and see if it worked. The existence proofs from MiniLLM (Gu et al., 2023), GKD (Agarwal et al., 2024), and the industry pipelines (Qwen3, MiMo, GLM-5) all demonstrated that OPD *can* work, but none provided a framework for predicting *in advance* whether a given teacher–student pair would succeed. The capacity gap literature (Cho and Hariharan, 2019; Mirzadeh et al., 2020) offered coarse heuristics—don't use a teacher that's too much larger than the student—but these were developed for off-policy distillation and don't account for the on-policy dynamic where the student generates its own states. The paper's first innovation is to operationalize "will OPD work?" as a specific, measurable quantity: the **overlap ratio** between the student's and teacher's top-`$k$` token distributions at student-visited states.

The intellectual move here is deeply pragmatic. Overlap ratio is cheap to compute (it requires only forward passes from both models on a batch of student-generated rollouts, without any gradient computation), yet it captures the two conditions the paper identifies as governing OPD success. In the thinking-pattern consistency experiment (Section 3.1, Figure 2), the GRPO-trained teacher achieves a higher initial overlap ratio than the non-thinking teacher, and this difference predicts the downstream performance gap that persists throughout training—even though both teachers have comparable benchmark scores. In the reverse distillation experiment (Section 3.3, Figure 5), the two teachers (1.5B and 7B from the same distillation pipeline) produce nearly indistinguishable training trajectories, which the overlap framework explains as a consequence of their essentially identical thinking patterns at the token level.

What makes this a fundamental insight rather than a mere correlation is the paper's demonstration that overlap ratio is not just a passive diagnostic but a **causal locus of optimization**. Section 4.2's ablation—where training on only the overlap tokens matches full top-`$k$` OPD performance, while training on only non-overlap tokens is substantially weaker (Figure 7)—establishes that the overlap set is where gradients concentrate and where learning happens. The self-reinforcing dynamic (overlap ratio rises steadily from ~72% to ~91% in successful runs) means that initial overlap is not just a predictor but a prerequisite: you need sufficient overlap at initialization for the virtuous cycle of probability concentration and alignment refinement to begin. Failing runs show stagnant overlap from the outset (Figure 6, bottom-left), confirming that they never enter this self-reinforcing regime.

This diagnostic capability has immediate practical implications that the paper only partially explores. Before committing GPU-hours to a full OPD run, a practitioner can sample a batch of rollouts from the candidate student, compute the overlap ratio with the candidate teacher, and make an informed decision about whether OPD is likely to work or whether an intervention (cold-start SFT, different teacher, prompt alignment) is needed first. The paper does not formalize this as a decision rule with threshold values, but the data strongly implies one: initial overlap ratios in the 70–75% range characterize the successful runs in Sections 3.2 and 4.1, while substantially lower values (implied but not directly reported for the base-initialized student in Section 5.1) predict failure.

---

### Innovation 2: Benchmark Performance Does Not Predict Distillation Quality—Token-Level Alignment Does

The paper's second innovation is an empirical finding with counterintuitive force: **the teacher's global benchmark score is essentially orthogonal to its effectiveness as an OPD teacher**. This finding contradicts the natural assumption—widely held if rarely articulated—that a better model makes a better teacher. The evidence is multifaceted and mutually reinforcing.

In the thinking-pattern consistency experiment (Section 3.1, Figure 2), Qwen3-4B-Base-GRPO and Qwen3-4B (Non-thinking) have broadly comparable benchmark performance (Figure 3: 0.204/0.242/0.599 vs. 0.212/0.210/0.700 on AIME 2024/2025/AMC 2023 respectively—the non-thinking model is slightly better or comparable on two of three benchmarks). Yet the GRPO teacher substantially outperforms as a distillation source, with the performance gap persisting throughout training. If benchmark score drove distillation quality, the two teachers would produce similar outcomes; the fact that they don't demonstrates that something else—token-level thinking-pattern compatibility—dominates.

The reverse distillation experiment (Section 3.3, Figure 5) makes this point even more sharply. DeepSeek-R1-Distill-7B achieves slightly higher benchmark scores than JustRL-1.5B (the student in this configuration), yet distilling from the 7B model causes the student to regress to approximately the same level as distilling from the 1.5B version of the same model—which is substantially weaker than both. The 7B model's benchmark advantage provides zero benefit; its training trajectory is "nearly indistinguishable" from the 1.5B teacher's. This is the cleanest demonstration the paper provides that OPD dynamics are governed by the local token-level target distribution the teacher induces on student-visited states, not by any measure of the teacher's global competence.

The new knowledge experiment (Section 3.2, Figure 4) provides the complementary positive case. Post-trained teachers (Skywork-OR1-Math-7B from DeepSeek-R1-Distill-7B; Qwen3-4B-Non-Thinking-RL-Math from Qwen3-4B Non-thinking) are compared against their non-post-trained counterparts. The post-trained teachers achieve moderately higher benchmark scores, but the critical metric is the **gap recovery rate**: 58.6% vs. 15.6% for Qwen, 16.9% vs. 5.3% for DeepSeek. This normalizes the OPD improvement by the maximum possible gain (reaching teacher performance), showing that the post-trained teachers transfer a much larger fraction of their advantage. Crucially, the initial overlap ratios for the post-trained and non-post-trained teachers are similar within each family (70.3% vs. 75.7% for Qwen, 71.5% vs. 74.7% for DeepSeek), so thinking-pattern compatibility is controlled. The difference in transferability must come from the post-trained teachers possessing capabilities that the student hasn't already internalized from their shared training pipeline—genuinely new knowledge.

The intellectual significance of this finding extends beyond OPD. It suggests a fundamental decoupling between a model's standalone performance and its utility as a supervision source for training other models. This has implications for how the field thinks about model selection for distillation, synthetic data generation, and reward modeling: the model with the highest benchmark score may not provide the most useful training signal, particularly when the training objective involves matching token-level distributions on states the student actually visits. The framework the paper provides—overlap ratio for compatibility, gap recovery rate for knowledge transferability—offers a principled way to make these selection decisions that the field previously lacked.

---

### Innovation 3: The Overlap Token Set as Both Diagnostic and Sufficient Training Signal

The paper's third innovation is a mechanistic finding that is simultaneously a significant conceptual reframing: **the entire optimization signal of OPD concentrates on a small set of shared high-probability tokens, and optimizing only those tokens is sufficient**. This is not an ablation that marginally simplifies training; it is a discovery about where the learning actually happens that changes how one thinks about what OPD does.

Prior work on OPD (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2026b) treated the divergence computation as operating over the full vocabulary or a sampled subset, without analyzing *which tokens* carry the gradient signal. The implicit assumption was that more tokens = more information = better learning. The paper's finding that overlap tokens alone suffice (Section 4.2, Figure 7) directly contradicts this: adding non-overlap tokens to the loss function provides negligible additional benefit because those tokens carry very little probability mass and are being actively pushed out of the student's top-`$k$` set by the self-reinforcing dynamic.

The discovery that non-overlap tokens contribute little is not obvious a priori. One could imagine that tokens in the student's top-`$k$` but not the teacher's (the student-only portion of the symmetric difference) would be important for OPD, since the teacher wants to discourage the student from placing mass on those tokens (they are where the student diverges from the teacher). The paper shows empirically that this discouragement happens automatically: as the overlap set grows, student-only tokens are naturally pushed out of the top-`$k$` by the concentration of mass onto shared tokens, without needing explicit loss terms targeting them. The mode-seeking property of reverse KL—which penalizes the student for placing mass where the teacher does not—apparently operates through the shared-token channel, making explicit supervision on non-shared tokens redundant.

The fact that overlap tokens carry 97–99% of the total probability mass for both models throughout training (Appendix B.1, Figure 18) is the crucial context that makes this finding plausible in retrospect. The overlap set is small in cardinality (`$k = 16$` tokens out of a vocabulary of tens of thousands) but probabilistically dominant. OPD is fundamentally about aligning the student's probability distribution with the teacher's, and since both distributions are overwhelmingly concentrated on the same small set of tokens, aligning those tokens is essentially the entire task. Tokens outside the overlap set are in the long tail—they matter individually for very little probability mass, and collectively their mass is bounded by `$1 - 0.97 = 3\%$`.

This finding has immediate practical implications that extend beyond OPD to any training method that uses token-level KL divergences between models. It suggests that full-vocabulary KL computation—which requires `$O(B T M)$` memory and is a computational bottleneck in many setups—is unnecessary. Top-`$k$` OPD with moderate `$k$` (the paper shows `$k = 4$` is nearly as good as `$k = 16$`, and sampled-token OPD is competitive with `$k \geq 4$`) captures essentially the full benefit at dramatically reduced cost. This is not just a minor engineering optimization; it is an algorithmic insight derived from understanding the structure of the optimization landscape. The paper's recommendation that `$k = 1$` (always the argmax token) should be avoided because it creates an unstable, mode-concentrated reward signal that flips with small policy changes further refines the practical guidance, distinguishing between "use few tokens" (good) and "use one biased token" (bad).

### Innovation 4: OPD Learns Thinking Patterns, Not Just Knowledge Transfer

The paper's fourth innovation is the empirical demonstration—through the reverse distillation experiment (Section 3.3)—that **OPD actively acquires and overwrites the student's thinking patterns, and that this pattern acquisition is the primary mechanism, not the transfer of benchmark-measurable knowledge**. This is a conceptual insight that reframes what OPD is *for* and why it sometimes regresses performance rather than improving it.

The standard framing of knowledge distillation, dating back to Hinton et al. (2015), is that the teacher transfers its superior knowledge to the student, and the student benefits from this knowledge transfer. In this framing, distilling from a stronger teacher should always help (or at worst, fail to help if the capacity gap is too large). The reverse distillation experiment blows up this framing. When JustRL-1.5B (which acquired substantial reasoning gains through RL) is distilled back toward its own pre-RL checkpoint (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B), the student *regresses*—it loses the RL-acquired gains and returns almost exactly to its pre-RL performance level. This cannot be explained by knowledge transfer, because the teacher (pre-RL checkpoint) has *less* knowledge than the student (post-RL checkpoint). What is being transferred is not knowledge but thinking patterns: the pre-RL checkpoint's token-level distribution, which reflects a different (less effective) reasoning style, overwrites the post-RL checkpoint's learned patterns through the mode-seeking reverse KL objective.

This reframing has several important implications. First, it explains why thinking-pattern consistency is a governing condition: if the teacher's pattern is incompatible, OPD's attempt to align the student's local token distribution with the teacher's will fail, not because knowledge isn't transferring but because the pattern mismatch prevents the self-reinforcing overlap dynamic from starting. Second, it explains why higher benchmark scores don't predict OPD success: benchmark scores measure global knowledge, but OPD operates on local token-level patterns. A teacher can have excellent benchmark performance (like DeepSeek-R1-Distill-7B) while having token-level patterns on the student's visited states that are essentially identical to a weaker model from the same training pipeline. Third, it explains the success of the cold-start intervention: off-policy SFT on teacher-generated rollouts is effectively a "pattern alignment" phase that brings the student's generation style closer to the teacher's before the more sensitive on-policy refinement begins.

The paper doesn't fully articulate this implication, but the finding that OPD learns thinking patterns rather than knowledge suggests a fundamental distinction between *what to think* (knowledge) and *how to think* (thinking patterns), and implies that OPD is primarily a mechanism for transferring the latter. The "new knowledge" condition (Section 3.2) then becomes: does the teacher possess thinking patterns that the student hasn't already seen? When the teacher is from the same training pipeline as the student (same data, same recipe, different scale), the answer is no—both models internalize similar patterns at their respective capacities, so the teacher offers nothing new to transfer. When the teacher has undergone additional RL post-training, it has learned new patterns (how to approach problems more effectively, how to structure reasoning steps) that the student can acquire through OPD.

This is a significant conceptual advance over prior work that treated distillation as a monolithic process of "making the student more like the teacher" without distinguishing between types of similarity (knowledge vs. patterns) or recognizing that some types of similarity (pattern alignment) can be actively harmful if the teacher's patterns are worse than what the student has independently learned. It also connects to the emerging self-distillation literature (Hübotter et al., 2026; Shenfeld et al., 2026) by suggesting that when a model serves as its own teacher with privileged information, what transfers is not new factual knowledge but improved patterns for deploying existing knowledge—a more precise account of what self-distillation accomplishes.

### Innovation 5: Dense Supervision Has a Depth Ceiling—And the Mechanism Is Suffix-Triggered Collapse

The paper's fifth innovation is the identification of a fundamental limitation in OPD's core premise—that dense token-level supervision is uniformly beneficial—through a detailed analysis of **how and why reward quality degrades with trajectory depth**. This is a negative result with significant implications for OPD's scalability to long-horizon settings.

The standard motivation for OPD over outcome-reward RL is that every token receives a supervision signal, rather than only the final token. This is often presented as an unqualified advantage. The paper's trajectory-depth analysis (Section 6.1) provides the first systematic evidence that this advantage has sharp boundaries. The maximum response length sweep (Figure 11a) reveals an inverted-U relationship: very short responses (0.5K, 1K tokens) provide too few supervised tokens for effective learning, moderate lengths (3K, 7K) are optimal, and longer responses (10K, 15K) cause performance to plateau or decline. The training dynamics (Figure 12) confirm that this decline is not just a failure to improve but an active destabilization: at 10K and 15K, the overlap ratio—which rose smoothly at shorter lengths—collapses in late training, accompanied by spikes in student entropy and gradient norm.

The mechanism behind this collapse is revealed by analyzing entropy as a function of output position across training steps (Figure 13). In the 15K setting, high student entropy first appears at the *end* of responses and progressively propagates backward toward earlier tokens as training proceeds. Teacher entropy exhibits the same suffix-to-prefix pattern (Appendix D.1, Figure 23). This spatial–temporal pattern—instability originating in the suffix and spreading backward—provides a mechanistic account of why long trajectories fail: the teacher encounters student-generated prefixes at later positions that are increasingly distant from states the teacher would naturally visit, producing noisier token-level probabilities. These noisy probabilities provide unstable supervision that first corrupts the student's distribution at the suffix, and this corruption then back-propagates through the autoregressive generation process (since later tokens condition on earlier ones, and gradients flow backward through time) to destabilize the entire trajectory.

The teacher continuation experiment (Figure 11b) provides converging evidence. When the teacher continues generation from student-generated prefixes of increasing depth, its accuracy advantage over the student decreases monotonically: +0.37 at 1K prefix depth to just +0.02 at 16K prefix depth. At deep prefixes, the teacher is essentially no better than the student at producing correct continuations, meaning its token-level supervision at those depths provides no useful guidance.

The global-vs-local reward analysis (Section 6.2, Figure 14) adds a crucial nuance that distinguishes this from a simple "long trajectories are hard" finding. Even the failing 7B teacher provides sequence-level reward that discriminates correct from incorrect rollouts about as well as the successful teacher (AUROC 0.75 vs. 0.73). The problem is not that the reward signal is globally uninformative—it's that the token-level decomposition of that global signal doesn't provide a coherent local optimization landscape that gradient descent can exploit. The paper's anisotropy hypothesis—that the 7B teacher's per-token advantages point in inconsistent directions that cancel when aggregated—offers a specific mechanistic conjecture for future investigation.

The practical implication is substantial: current OPD techniques may not extend cleanly to the long-horizon reasoning and agentic settings that represent the frontier of LLM applications. This is not an incremental limitation but a fundamental tension between supervision density (wanting every token to receive a signal) and supervision reliability (the signal becoming noisier as the student diverges from the teacher's familiar territory). The paper frames this as motivating hybrid approaches that combine dense token-level supervision on short segments with sparse outcome-level rewards for longer horizons, or curriculum strategies that progressively extend the supervised horizon—directions that directly follow from the mechanistic understanding the paper develops.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use **DAPO-Math-17K** (Yu et al., 2025) as the primary training dataset, a collection of approximately 17,000 challenging mathematical reasoning problems. For the cold-start SFT stage (Section 5.1), the prompt source is the **math-domain subset of OpenThoughts3-1.2M** (Guha et al., 2025), from which 200K prompts are sampled for teacher rollout generation. The teacher-aligned prompt content experiment (Section 5.2) additionally uses a **deduplicated subset of DeepMath** (He et al., 2025c) of matched size to DAPO-Math-17K. For validation, three math benchmarks are used: **AIME 2024** (Li et al., 2024), **AIME 2025** (Balunović et al., 2025), and **AMC 2023** (Li et al., 2024), chosen because they provide clean correctness signals and require multi-step logical deduction where OPD's dense token-level supervision should theoretically provide the most benefit.

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses multiple model families and scales to construct controlled comparisons. In the **Qwen family**: Qwen3-1.7B-Base and Qwen3-1.7B (Non-thinking) as students; Qwen3-4B (Non-thinking), Qwen3-4B-Base-GRPO (obtained by applying GRPO to Qwen3-4B-Base), and Qwen3-4B-Non-Thinking-RL-Math (obtained by applying RL to Qwen3-4B Non-thinking on a 57K subset of DeepMath) as teachers. In the **DeepSeek family**: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (R1-Distill-1.5B) and JustRL-DeepSeek-1.5B (JustRL-1.5B, obtained by RL from R1-Distill-1.5B) as students; DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (R1-Distill-7B), Skywork-OR1-Math-7B (obtained by RL post-training on R1-Distill-7B), and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B (R1-Distill-14B) as teachers. These models are chosen to span a range of scales (1.5B to 14B), training pipelines (distillation-only vs. distillation + RL), and thinking patterns (base model, non-thinking post-trained, reasoning-optimized), enabling the paper to isolate the effects of model scale, training recipe, and thinking-pattern compatibility on OPD dynamics.

- **Metrics.** The primary evaluation metric is **avg@16 accuracy**: for each problem in the validation set, 16 solutions are sampled with temperature 0.7 and top-p 0.95, and the fraction of problems where at least one sample is correct is reported. This measures the expected pass rate given 16 attempts per problem, which is strictly higher than pass@1 and provides a more stable estimate of model capability. Answers are extracted from generated responses and compared to ground truth using standard answer parsing for math benchmarks (exact details of the extraction method are not specified but are assumed to follow common practice for these benchmarks). For analyzing training dynamics, the paper tracks several **token-level metrics** (overlap ratio, overlap-token advantage, entropy, entropy gap) computed on student-generated rollouts during training, as well as **gap recovery rate** = (Acc_after_OPD − Acc_before_OPD) / (Acc_teacher − Acc_before_OPD), which normalizes the OPD improvement by the maximum possible improvement.

- **Baselines.** The paper's experiments are primarily **controlled comparisons between OPD runs with different teacher configurations**, rather than comparisons against alternative training methods. Within each controlled setting, the baseline is typically OPD with the weaker or non-post-trained teacher. For example: OPD with Qwen3-4B (Non-thinking) as the baseline vs. Qwen3-4B-Base-GRPO (Section 3.1); OPD with R1-Distill-7B as the baseline vs. Skywork-OR1-Math-7B (Section 3.2); OPD from R1-Distill-1.5B as the baseline vs. JustRL-1.5B (Section 4.1). In the cold-start experiment (Section 5.1), the baseline is pure OPD starting directly from Qwen3-1.7B-Base, compared against two-stage SFT+OPD starting from Qwen3-1.7B-SFT. In the prompt alignment experiments (Section 5.2), the baselines are OPD with the original DAPO template and OPD with deduplicated DeepMath prompts, respectively. In the overlap-token ablation (Section 4.2), the baselines are Student Top-k OPD and Non-Overlap Top-k OPD. The paper does not compare OPD against off-policy distillation, outcome-reward RL, or other post-training methods in its main experiments—the comparisons are designed to isolate specific causal factors within OPD itself, not to benchmark OPD against alternatives.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** All OPD runs are trained for **200 steps** (one epoch through the training data) with default hyperparameters listed in Table 2 (Appendix A.2): global batch size 64, rollout number 4 (4 student-generated responses per prompt), max prompt length 1,024 tokens, default max response length 7,168 tokens, learning rate 1 × 10⁻⁶. The default OPD variant is Student Top-k with k = 16, though sampled-token OPD is compared in Section 6.3. The **rollout number of 4** means each training step involves 64 prompts × 4 rollouts = 256 student-generated responses, each requiring forward passes from both student (for generation) and teacher (for log-probability computation). The paper does not report total GPU-hours or FLOPs for training runs, nor does it compare computational cost between different OPD variants. For the trajectory-depth analysis (Section 6.1), the max response length is swept across {0.5K, 1K, 3K, 7K, 10K, 15K} tokens while keeping all other settings fixed, enabling a controlled comparison of how supervision horizon affects training dynamics and downstream performance. The cold-start SFT stage (Section 5.1) uses 200K teacher-generated rollouts with max generation length 12,288 tokens, trained for 1 epoch with learning rate 1 × 10⁻⁵ on 8 devices (per-device batch size 8).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report any formal cross-validation, statistical significance testing, or confidence intervals on its main results. Results are presented as curves over training steps (typically 200 steps) with accuracy measured at regular intervals on the fixed validation benchmarks (AIME 2024, 2025, AMC 2023). The consistent patterns across multiple benchmarks, multiple model families, and multiple teacher-student configurations serve as the primary source of confidence in the findings. Each controlled comparison is run exactly once under the specified configuration—no repeated runs with different random seeds are reported. The benchmark-wise breakdowns in appendices (Figures 17, 22) show that trends observed in averaged results hold consistently across individual benchmarks rather than being driven by outliers. The reverse distillation experiment (Section 3.3) serves as a kind of conceptual replication by inverting the distillation direction, and the cross-model validation (Appendix B.3) with R1-Distill-7B as student and Skywork-OR1-Math-7B / R1-Distill-14B as teachers provides additional evidence that the overlap dynamics generalize across different model pairs.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Thinking-Pattern Consistency Governs OPD Effectiveness (Section 3.1)

**Headline result:** A teacher with higher initial thinking-pattern compatibility (measured by overlap ratio) produces better OPD outcomes, even when its benchmark performance is comparable to or slightly worse than an alternative teacher with lower compatibility.

Using Qwen3-1.7B-Base as the student, OPD with Qwen3-4B-Base-GRPO as the teacher consistently outperforms OPD with Qwen3-4B (Non-thinking) as the teacher (Figure 2). The GRPO teacher achieves an initial overlap ratio of approximately 0.66–0.69 versus approximately 0.57 for the non-thinking teacher (Figure 2, right), and this gap corresponds to higher avg@16 validation accuracy throughout training (Figure 2, left). The two teachers have broadly comparable benchmark performance: on AIME 2024, Qwen3-4B (Non-thinking) achieves 0.212 vs. Qwen3-4B-Base-GRPO at 0.204; on AIME 2025, 0.210 vs. 0.242; on AMC 2023, 0.700 vs. 0.599 (Figure 3). The GRPO teacher is not systematically stronger—it wins on one benchmark (AIME 2025), loses substantially on another (AMC 2023), and is roughly tied on the third (AIME 2024)—yet it produces consistently better distillation outcomes.

The benchmark-wise breakdown (Appendix A.3, Figure 17) confirms this pattern holds across individual datasets: on AIME 2024, the GRPO teacher reaches approximately 0.18 vs. 0.14 for the non-thinking teacher at 200 steps; on AIME 2025, approximately 0.22 vs. 0.18; on AMC 2023, approximately 0.65 vs. 0.55. The gap in overlap ratio narrows over training, with both curves converging toward similar values by step 200, but the performance gap persists, suggesting that early-stage thinking-pattern mismatch causes an irrecoverable loss of distillation benefit even if overlap eventually catches up.

#### New Knowledge Beyond Same-Family Training Is Required for Substantial Gains (Section 3.2)

**Headline result:** When a teacher is from the same training pipeline as the student (same data, same recipe, different scale), OPD yields only marginal improvement regardless of the teacher's benchmark advantage. Post-training the teacher with RL—giving it genuinely new capabilities—dramatically increases OPD's effectiveness.

In the DeepSeek family (Figure 4, left), using R1-Distill-1.5B as the student: OPD with R1-Distill-7B (same-family teacher, no RL post-training) yields a gap recovery rate of only **5.3%**, improving average accuracy from approximately 0.39 to just over 0.41. OPD with Skywork-OR1-Math-7B (R1-Distill-7B after additional RL post-training) achieves a gap recovery rate of **16.9%**, improving accuracy from approximately 0.39 to about 0.43. The initial overlap ratios are comparable (74.7% for R1-Distill-7B, 71.5% for Skywork-OR1-Math-7B), controlling for thinking-pattern compatibility.

In the Qwen family (Figure 4, right), using Qwen3-1.7B (Non-thinking) as the student: OPD with Qwen3-4B (Non-thinking) achieves a gap recovery rate of only **15.6%**, improving accuracy from approximately 0.28 to about 0.36. OPD with Qwen3-4B-Non-Thinking-RL-Math achieves a gap recovery rate of **58.6%**, improving accuracy from approximately 0.28 to about 0.48. Initial overlap ratios: 75.7% for Qwen3-4B (Non-thinking), 70.3% for Qwen3-4B-Non-Thinking-RL-Math.

The absolute accuracy gains are substantial in the Qwen family: the post-trained teacher more than doubles the improvement achieved by the same-family teacher (approximately +0.20 vs. +0.08 absolute accuracy gain). The gap recovery rates directly quantify how much of the teacher's advantage is transferable through OPD: the post-trained teachers transfer 3–4× more of their advantage than the same-family teachers, despite similar initial thinking-pattern alignment.

#### Reverse Distillation Confirms That OPD Learns Thinking Patterns, Not Benchmark Performance (Section 3.3)

**Headline result:** Distilling a stronger, RL-trained student backward toward a weaker teacher from the same family causes the student to regress to approximately the same level regardless of whether the teacher is 1.5B or 7B, demonstrating that OPD training dynamics are governed by thinking patterns, not model scale or benchmark performance.

Using JustRL-1.5B as the student (avg@16 on AIME 2024: approximately 0.52 at step 0; on AIME 2025: approximately 0.38; on AMC 2023: approximately 0.80), OPD toward R1-Distill-1.5B (the student's own pre-RL checkpoint, which is substantially weaker) causes performance to regress to approximately the pre-RL level across all three benchmarks (Figure 5). On AIME 2024, accuracy drops to about 0.36; on AIME 2025, to about 0.28; on AMC 2023, to about 0.68.

Critically, replacing the teacher with R1-Distill-7B—a larger model from the same family that slightly outscores JustRL-1.5B on benchmarks—produces a **nearly indistinguishable training trajectory** (Figure 5). The two curves overlap almost exactly on all three benchmarks, despite R1-Distill-7B being a stronger model than both JustRL-1.5B and R1-Distill-1.5B. On AIME 2024 at step 600, both reach approximately 0.36–0.37; on AIME 2025, both reach approximately 0.28–0.30; on AMC 2023, both reach approximately 0.68–0.70. The final performance is essentially identical regardless of whether the teacher is the 1.5B or 7B version of the same distillation pipeline.

This experiment simultaneously validates both conditions from Sections 3.1 and 3.2: (i) the two teachers share the same thinking pattern (they are from the same distillation recipe, just at different scales), so both induce the same local target distribution on student-visited states despite their difference in benchmark performance; (ii) neither teacher offers genuinely new knowledge beyond what the student already internalized during its own pre-RL training, since they were trained on the same data with the same recipe—the student already acquired these patterns and then improved beyond them through RL, so OPD simply overwrites the RL-acquired improvements.

#### Successful OPD Is Driven by Progressive Alignment on High-Probability Overlap Tokens (Section 4.1)

**Headline result:** Successful OPD runs exhibit a consistent dynamic signature—steadily rising overlap ratio, overlap-token advantage approaching zero, and narrowing entropy gap—while failing runs show stagnant values in all three metrics from the outset.

Using R1-Distill-1.5B as the student and comparing JustRL-1.5B (successful) against R1-Distill-7B (failed) as teachers (Figure 6): In the successful run with JustRL-1.5B, the overlap ratio rises from approximately **0.72 at step 0 to 0.91 at step 200** (Figure 6, bottom-left). Overlap-token advantage improves from approximately −0.006 toward 0.000 (Figure 6, bottom-middle). The absolute entropy gap narrows from approximately 0.10 to near 0.00 (Figure 6, bottom-right). On benchmarks, accuracy improves from approximately 0.29 to 0.47 on AIME 2024, from 0.23 to 0.32 on AIME 2025, and from 0.65 to 0.77 on AMC 2023 (Figure 6, top row). The final student recovers **more than 80% of the performance gap to the teacher**.

In the failing run with R1-Distill-7B, despite the teacher being stronger overall, the overlap ratio **stagnates** around 0.72–0.74 throughout training (Figure 6, bottom-left). The overlap-token advantage becomes **larger in magnitude** (more negative) than in the successful run during later stages (Figure 6, bottom-middle, reaching approximately −0.006 by step 200, similar where the successful run started but without improvement). The absolute entropy gap remains **large and persistent** around 0.10–0.12 (Figure 6, bottom-right). On benchmarks, accuracy shows essentially no improvement: on AIME 2024, accuracy hovers around 0.29–0.30; on AIME 2025, around 0.24; on AMC 2023, around 0.65–0.66 (Figure 6, top row).

The cross-model validation (Appendix B.3, Figure 20) with R1-Distill-7B as the student shows the same pattern. The successful teacher (Skywork-OR1-Math-7B) drives overlap ratio from approximately 0.82 to 0.95, overlap-token advantage from approximately −0.005 to near 0.000, and entropy gap shrinking from approximately 0.10 to below 0.02. The failing teacher (R1-Distill-14B) shows stagnant overlap around 0.80–0.82, persistent negative overlap-token advantage around −0.005, and an entropy gap that actually grows slightly. Accuracy improvements: on AIME 2024, approximately 0.55 to 0.66 vs. 0.55 to 0.58; on AIME 2025, approximately 0.38 to 0.50 vs. 0.38 to 0.42; on AMC 2023, approximately 0.82 to 0.91 vs. 0.82 to 0.85.

Auxiliary optimization metrics (Appendix B.2, Figure 19) reinforce these findings. In the successful run with JustRL-1.5B, the training loss (PG loss) decreases from approximately 1.6 to 0.2 over 200 steps, the gradient norm starts high and remains substantial (approximately 0.8–1.2 through most of training), and the probability difference on the token with the largest absolute advantage decreases from approximately 0.10 to 0.04. In the failing run with R1-Distill-7B, the PG loss starts much smaller (approximately 0.4) and changes only modestly, the gradient norm is consistently much smaller (approximately 0.1–0.3), and the extreme-token probability discrepancy remains persistently larger (approximately 0.06–0.08 throughout).

#### Optimizing Only Overlap Tokens Suffices (Section 4.2)

**Headline result:** Restricting the OPD loss to only the overlap tokens (the intersection of student and teacher top-k sets) matches the full Student Top-k OPD in both downstream accuracy and training dynamics, while restricting to non-overlap tokens (the symmetric difference) is substantially weaker.

In the successful OPD setting with JustRL-1.5B as teacher and R1-Distill-1.5B as student (Figure 7), three variants are compared at k = 16:

- **Student Top-k** (the full top-k set from the student): AIME 2024 accuracy reaches approximately 0.44 at step 200; AIME 2025 reaches approximately 0.32; AMC 2023 reaches approximately 0.77.
- **Overlap Top-k** (only tokens in both student and teacher top-k): performance is **nearly indistinguishable** from Student Top-k on all three benchmarks, with curves overlapping almost exactly (Figure 7, top row).
- **Non-Overlap Top-k** (only tokens in exactly one of the two top-k sets): performance is **substantially worse**, with AIME 2024 reaching only approximately 0.38, AIME 2025 approximately 0.27, and AMC 2023 approximately 0.70 (Figure 7, top row). This is a gap of roughly 6 percentage points on AIME 2024, 5 on AIME 2025, and 7 on AMC 2023 compared to Student Top-k and Overlap Top-k.

The training dynamics reveal the self-reinforcing nature of overlap optimization (Figure 7, bottom row). Both Student Top-k and Overlap Top-k drive the overlap ratio from approximately **0.72 to above 0.91** by step 200, with nearly identical trajectories. Non-Overlap Top-k initially causes the overlap ratio to **decrease** before only partially recovering to approximately 0.78—a substantially lower final value. The overlap-token advantage curves for Student Top-k and Overlap Top-k are nearly indistinguishable, improving from approximately −0.006 toward 0.000, while Non-Overlap Top-k has much smaller magnitude in the overlap-token advantage, indicating a much weaker effective gradient on the overlap tokens due to the loss not directly targeting them.

The entropy gap remains large and unstable for Non-Overlap Top-k (Figure 7, bottom-right), fluctuating between approximately 0.16 and 0.32, versus a steady decrease toward near 0.05 for the other two variants. This indicates that without direct supervision on overlap tokens, the student fails to match the teacher's confidence calibration at student-visited states.

#### Off-Policy Cold Start Recovers Failing OPD (Section 5.1)

**Headline result:** Two-stage training—off-policy SFT on teacher-generated rollouts followed by standard OPD—substantially outperforms pure OPD from a mismatched initialization, with the benefit persisting throughout training rather than being only an early-stage boost.

Using Qwen3-1.7B-Base as the student and Qwen3-4B (Non-thinking) as the teacher (Figure 8), SFT+OPD (starting from Qwen3-1.7B-SFT) versus pure OPD (starting from Qwen3-1.7B-Base):

- **AIME 2024:** SFT+OPD reaches approximately 0.12 at step 200 vs. 0.08 for pure OPD (a ~50% relative improvement).
- **AIME 2025:** SFT+OPD reaches approximately 0.07 vs. 0.045 for pure OPD.
- **AMC 2023:** SFT+OPD reaches approximately 0.38 vs. 0.28 for pure OPD.

The performance gap is not just an early-stage phenomenon—it **persists and widens throughout training** from approximately step 50 onward on AMC 2023, and from step 100 onward on AIME 2024. This indicates that the cold start improves not only early optimization but also the final performance ceiling.

The overlap dynamics (Figure 8, bottom) show why. The SFT-initialized student begins with a **much higher initial overlap ratio** (approximately 0.63 vs. 0.50 for the base-initialized student) and maintains a **smooth, stable trajectory** rising to approximately 0.72. The base-initialized student starts substantially lower, exhibits pronounced instability in the early stages, and only gradually recovers to approximately 0.68. The entropy gap is also substantially smaller for the SFT-initialized student throughout training (approximately 0.05–0.10 vs. 0.15–0.30 for the base-initialized student), indicating a closer match to the teacher's confidence profile. The overlap-token advantage for SFT+OPD starts closer to zero (approximately −0.004 vs. −0.013) and improves more consistently.

Additional analysis of overlap mass (Appendix C.2, Figure 21) shows that the SFT-initialized student maintains **both student overlap mass and teacher overlap mass at consistently high levels** (near 0.95–0.99 for the teacher side, near 0.90–0.98 for the student side) throughout training, while the base-initialized student exhibits substantially lower and more unstable overlap mass, especially in early training. This explains why Overlap-Token Advantage alone can be misleading: it is averaged only over overlap tokens, so it can appear relatively favorable even when the overlap set misses substantial high-probability teacher tokens.

#### Teacher-Aligned Prompts Sharpen OPD Alignment (Section 5.2)

**Headline result:** Using prompts that match the teacher's post-training data—either in template format or in exact content—improves OPD performance, but at the cost of substantially lower student entropy, suggesting that prompt mixing strategies are needed for robust deployment.

**Prompt template alignment (Figure 9):** With JustRL-1.5B as teacher and R1-Distill-1.5B as student, switching from the original DAPO template to the teacher-aligned template yields higher average validation accuracy throughout training: approximately 0.52 vs. 0.46 at step 200. The overlap ratio starts higher (approximately 0.83 vs. 0.79) and converges to a higher final level (approximately 0.92 vs. 0.90). The benchmark-wise breakdown (Appendix C.4, Figure 22) shows consistent improvements: on AIME 2024, approximately 0.50 vs. 0.42; on AIME 2025, approximately 0.36 vs. 0.32; on AMC 2023, approximately 0.80 vs. 0.76. The teacher-aligned template allows the student to recover roughly 85% of the teacher's performance versus 80% with the original template.

**Prompt content alignment (Figure 10):** With Qwen3-4B-Base-GRPO as teacher and Qwen3-1.7B-Base as student, using DAPO-Math-17K (teacher-aligned) versus deduplicated DeepMath (in-domain but not teacher-aligned):

- **AIME 2024:** DAPO-Math-17K reaches approximately 0.12 vs. 0.09 for DeepMath at step 200.
- **AIME 2025:** DAPO-Math-17K reaches approximately 0.07 vs. 0.055.
- **AMC 2023:** DAPO-Math-17K reaches approximately 0.36 vs. 0.30.

A notable subtlety: despite the downstream performance advantage, teacher-aligned prompts produce a **lower overlap ratio** throughout training (Figure 10, bottom-left): approximately 0.68 vs. 0.71 for DeepMath. However, the **cumulative student probability mass on overlap tokens** is substantially higher: approximately 0.88–0.92 vs. 0.65–0.80 (Figure 10, bottom-middle). This indicates that with teacher-aligned prompts, the student concentrates its probability mass on **fewer but more strongly shared tokens**—the effective alignment on high-probability tokens is stronger even though the overlap set is smaller.

A critical side effect: teacher-aligned prompts lead to **substantially lower student entropy** during training (Figure 10, bottom-right), with entropy dropping to approximately 0.5–1.5 versus 3.0–5.0 for out-of-distribution prompts. The paper interprets this as a warning that using only teacher-aligned prompts can overly suppress policy entropy and recommends mixing teacher-aligned prompts with out-of-distribution prompts.

#### Reward Quality Degrades with Trajectory Depth (Section 6.1)

**Headline result:** OPD's dense supervision advantage has a depth ceiling: performance peaks at moderate response lengths (3K–7K tokens), declines at longer lengths (10K–15K), and the decline is driven by suffix-triggered instability that propagates backward through the trajectory.

Using R1-Distill-1.5B as student and JustRL-1.5B as teacher, training under six maximum response length settings for 200 steps (Figure 11a):

- **0.5K and 1K:** Performance is uniformly weak—too few supervised tokens for sample-efficient learning. On AIME 2024, accuracy reaches only approximately 0.15–0.20.
- **3K and 7K:** Best performance. On AIME 2024, both reach approximately 0.35; on AIME 2025, approximately 0.25; on AMC 2023, approximately 0.68–0.70.
- **10K and 15K:** Performance **plateaus or declines**. On AIME 2024, 10K and 15K reach approximately 0.32 and 0.30 respectively (worse than 3K and 7K). On AIME 2025, approximately 0.22 and 0.20. On AMC 2023, approximately 0.65 and 0.62.

The training dynamics (Figure 12) reveal that 10K and 15K settings exhibit **late-stage collapse**: the overlap ratio, which rises smoothly to approximately 0.88 for 3K and 0.86 for 7K, **drops sharply** after step 200 for 10K (falling to approximately 0.75) and for 15K (falling even more dramatically to approximately 0.68), accompanied by **spikes in student entropy** (from approximately 1.5 to 8.0 for 15K) and **gradient norm** (from approximately 2.5 to 10.0+ for 15K).

**Mechanism: suffix-triggered collapse.** The student entropy heatmap (Figure 13) for the 15K setting shows that high entropy **first appears at the end of the response** (12K–15K position range) and progressively propagates toward earlier tokens over training steps 180–250. At step 180, entropy is low and uniform across positions. By step 210, elevated entropy appears in the 12K–15K suffix. By step 230, elevated entropy has spread to the 9K–12K range. By step 250, high entropy covers the entire trajectory from approximately 3K onward. Teacher entropy exhibits the same suffix-to-prefix pattern (Appendix D.1, Figure 23).

**Teacher continuation degrades with prefix depth (Figure 11b).** Starting from student-generated prefixes at different depths, the teacher's accuracy advantage over the student decreases monotonically:
- At **1K prefix**: +0.3659 accuracy gain
- At **4K prefix**: +0.2709 accuracy gain
- At **8K prefix**: +0.1522 accuracy gain
- At **16K prefix**: +0.0237 accuracy gain (essentially zero)

At deep prefixes, the teacher is no better than the student at producing correct continuations, meaning its token-level supervision at those depths provides no useful guidance.

#### Globally Informative Reward Does Not Guarantee Local Exploitability (Section 6.2)

**Headline result:** The failing 7B teacher provides sequence-level reward that discriminates correct from incorrect rollouts equally well as the successful teacher, suggesting that OPD failure is not due to globally uninformative reward but to local optimization geometry that gradient descent cannot exploit.

In the controlled comparison with R1-Distill-1.5B as student and two teachers (JustRL-1.5B, successful; R1-Distill-7B, failed), the sequence mean reward distributions (Figure 14) show that **both teachers assign higher reward to correct rollouts than incorrect ones**:
- JustRL-1.5B: AUROC = 0.7333
- R1-Distill-7B: AUROC = 0.7511

The AUROC values are comparable (slightly higher for the failing teacher), confirming that the failing teacher's reward signal is **globally discriminative**—it can tell which rollouts are correct about as well as the successful teacher.

The paper hypothesizes that the failure arises from **anisotropic per-token advantages**: the 7B teacher's per-token advantages, while individually large (as shown by the larger-overlap-token advantage magnitude in Figure 6, bottom-middle), point in inconsistent directions across positions within each sequence. When aggregated into a gradient update, these heterogeneous signals partially cancel, yielding small effective gradients despite large per-token rewards. Evidence for this hypothesis: the overlap-token advantage in the failing run becomes **larger in magnitude** than in the successful run during later training stages (Figure 6, bottom-middle), yet the **gradient norm remains persistently smaller** (Appendix B.2, Figure 19, middle panel). The successful teacher, sharing a compatible thinking pattern with the student, may concentrate its advantage on a more coherent subset of tokens whose gradients point in a consistent direction.

#### Sampled-Token OPD Is Already Sufficient (Section 6.3)

**Headline result:** Sampled-token OPD (using only the one token the student actually sampled at each position) performs comparably to Top-k OPD with k ≥ 4, and the only clearly worse configuration is Top-1 (always the argmax token, which creates an unstable, mode-concentrated reward signal).

Using R1-Distill-1.5B as student and JustRL-1.5B as teacher, comparing Top-k OPD with k ∈ {1, 4, 16, 64} against sampled-token OPD (Figure 15):

- **Sampled-token OPD** achieves avg@16 accuracy comparable to Top-16 and Top-64 across all three benchmarks: on AIME 2024, approximately 0.46; on AIME 2025, approximately 0.33; on AMC 2023, approximately 0.78.
- **Top-4 OPD** achieves essentially identical performance: AIME 2024 approximately 0.46; AIME 2025 approximately 0.33; AMC 2023 approximately 0.78–0.79.
- **Top-16 OPD**: AIME 2024 approximately 0.47; AIME 2025 approximately 0.33; AMC 2023 approximately 0.79.
- **Top-64 OPD**: AIME 2024 approximately 0.46; AIME 2025 approximately 0.34; AMC 2023 approximately 0.79.
- **Top-1 OPD** consistently **underperforms**: AIME 2024 approximately 0.45; AIME 2025 approximately 0.31; AMC 2023 approximately 0.77.

The differences between sampled-token, Top-4, Top-16, and Top-64 are small and within the range of training variance. Enlarging k beyond 4 brings negligible additional gain while increasing computational overhead.

The training dynamics (Figure 16) explain why Top-1 fails. Top-1 exhibits **unstable overlap growth**: the overlap ratio shows sharp fluctuations rather than smooth increase, accompanied by **spikes in entropy** (brief excursions to 1.5+ vs. a steady 1.0–1.2 for Top-16) and **large fluctuations in gradient norm** (spikes to 10–20 vs. a steady 2–5 for Top-16). Top-4 is substantially more stable but still shows a **late-stage dip** in overlap ratio (declining to approximately 0.86 vs. 0.91 for Top-16 and Top-64). Top-16 and Top-64 remain smooth throughout, with overlap ratio rising steadily from approximately 0.72 to 0.91 and gradient norm stable.

The failure of Top-1 is due to **biased, mode-concentrated selection**: always selecting the argmax token means small policy changes can flip which token occupies rank 1, creating an unstable reward signal that does not average out over training. Sampled-token OPD avoids this by drawing a different token at each step proportionally to the student's own distribution, providing unbiased coverage of the high-probability region across training steps.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Overlap token sufficiency (Section 4.2, Figure 7):** Training on only the overlap tokens (intersection of student and teacher Top-16 sets) matches full Student Top-16 OPD performance on all three benchmarks, while training on only non-overlap tokens (symmetric difference) is substantially weaker, confirming that the overlap set is the locus of OPD's gradient signal. The overlap-token advantage curves for Student Top-k and Overlap Top-k are nearly indistinguishable, whereas Non-Overlap Top-k has much smaller magnitude, indicating a much weaker effective gradient on overlap tokens.

**Cross-model validation of overlap dynamics (Appendix B.3, Figure 20):** The progressive alignment pattern generalizes to R1-Distill-7B as the student, with Skywork-OR1-Math-7B (successful) driving overlap ratio from 0.82 to 0.95 and R1-Distill-14B (failed) showing stagnant overlap around 0.80–0.82, confirming that the token-level mechanism is not specific to a single model pair.

**Overlap mass complement to overlap ratio (Appendix B.1, Figure 18; Appendix C.2, Figure 21):** In successful runs, overlap tokens consistently carry 97–99% of the total probability mass for both models throughout training, confirming that the overlap set is probabilistically dominant rather than merely a set-level coincidence. During the cold-start analysis, SFT initialization maintains much higher and more stable overlap mass than base initialization, explaining why Overlap-Token Advantage alone can be misleading when the overlap set misses substantial high-probability teacher tokens.

**Auxiliary optimization metrics (Appendix B.2, Figure 19):** Successful OPD with JustRL-1.5B shows decreasing PG loss (1.6 → 0.2), sustained gradient magnitude (0.8–1.2), and decreasing extreme-token probability discrepancy (0.10 → 0.04). Failed OPD with R1-Distill-7B shows much smaller initial loss (0.4, changing modestly), consistently smaller gradient norm (0.1–0.3), and persistently larger probability discrepancy (0.06–0.08).

**Support size k in Top-k OPD (Section 6.3, Figures 15–16):** k ≥ 4 is sufficient; increasing k beyond 4 brings negligible additional gain. Top-1 is unstable and underperforms due to biased, mode-concentrated reward that flips with small policy changes. Sampled-token OPD is competitive with Top-k OPD for k ≥ 4, establishing that single-sample Monte Carlo estimation of the per-token KL is not a bottleneck.

**Prompt template alignment (Section 5.2, Figure 9; Appendix C.4, Figure 22):** Even without changing the math problem content, aligning the prompt template with the teacher's post-training format yields consistent improvements across all three benchmarks, with overlap ratio starting and ending higher.

**Prompt content deduplication (Appendix C.3):** The DeepMath subset used as the out-of-distribution control for teacher-aligned prompts is carefully deduplicated against DAPO-Math-17K using both exact string matching and semantic similarity (cosine similarity threshold 0.6 with all-mpnet-base-v2 embeddings), ensuring that the comparison isolates the effect of prompt alignment rather than confounding with problem overlap.

**Teacher entropy by output position (Appendix D.1, Figure 23):** Teacher entropy exhibits the same suffix-to-prefix propagation pattern as student entropy in the 15K max response length setting, consistent with the interpretation that the teacher encounters increasingly unfamiliar prefixes at later positions, produces noisier log-probabilities, and this noise destabilizes the student from the suffix backward.

**GRPO training details for teacher preparation (Appendix A.1, Table 1):** The Qwen3-4B-Base-GRPO teacher is trained using GRPO with rollout n = 8, max prompt length 1,024, max response length 7,168, learning rate 1 × 10⁻⁶, temperature 1.0, top-p 1.0, KL regularization disabled, and token-mean loss aggregation, on 8 A800 80G GPUs for 1 epoch on DAPO-Math-17K.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim: Thinking-pattern consistency (measured by initial overlap ratio) governs OPD effectiveness.

**What was tested:** Figure 2 compares two teachers with comparable benchmark scores but different overlap ratios, showing the higher-overlap teacher yields better OPD outcomes. The per-benchmark breakdown (Figure 17) confirms this holds across individual datasets. However, only one student (Qwen3-1.7B-Base) and one pair of teachers (Qwen3-4B Non-thinking vs. Qwen3-4B-Base-GRPO) are compared in this specific experiment. The thinking-pattern metric is the overlap ratio with k = 16, but the paper does not test whether the finding is robust to different values of k (would k = 4 or k = 64 produce the same conclusion about which teacher is "more compatible"?). The convergence of overlap curves later in training but persistence of the performance gap is an interesting finding that the paper attributes to "irrecoverable loss from early-stage mismatch," but this causal claim is not experimentally verified (e.g., by showing that delaying OPD until overlap naturally rises through SFT—as in Section 5.1—recovers the lost performance, which it does, but that experiment uses a different student and teacher pair).

**Strength of evidence:** Moderately strong for this specific configuration. The paper would be strengthened by testing the overlap ratio's predictive power across more teacher-student pairs, especially pairs with deliberately manipulated overlap (e.g., by using different prompt templates, different decoding temperatures, or different model families). The GRPO teacher actually has *worse* benchmark performance on two of three benchmarks, so the "comparable performance" framing requires accepting that a 0.101 accuracy gap on AMC 2023 (0.700 vs. 0.599) is "broadly comparable." This weakens the claim that benchmark performance is controlled for.

#### Claim: New knowledge beyond same-family training is required for substantial OPD gains.

**What was tested:** Figure 4 compares same-family teachers (R1-Distill-7B, Qwen3-4B Non-thinking) against RL-post-trained teachers (Skywork-OR1-Math-7B, Qwen3-4B-Non-Thinking-RL-Math), showing dramatically higher gap recovery rates for the post-trained teachers. Initial overlap ratios are similar within each family, controlling for thinking-pattern compatibility. However, the post-trained teachers are also **stronger on benchmarks** (Skywork-OR1-Math-7B has higher accuracy than R1-Distill-7B; Qwen3-4B-Non-Thinking-RL-Math has higher accuracy than Qwen3-4B Non-thinking), so the "new knowledge" and "higher benchmark score" confounds are not fully separated. The paper argues that the reverse distillation experiment (Section 3.3) resolves this by showing a teacher with higher benchmark scores (R1-Distill-7B > JustRL-1.5B) does not produce better outcomes, but that experiment uses a different student and different teachers. The paper would be strengthened by a configuration where a post-trained teacher and its non-post-trained counterpart have matched benchmark scores but different knowledge novelty—a difficult but not impossible experimental design to construct (e.g., by selecting a checkpoint early in RL training where benchmark scores match but thinking patterns have already shifted).

**Strength of evidence:** Strongly suggestive but not definitively established. The gap recovery rate metric is a useful normalization, but it conflates "the teacher is stronger" (which mechanically increases the maximum possible gain) with "the teacher's knowledge is transferable" (which increases the fraction of the gap recovered). A post-trained teacher with much higher benchmark scores would have a lower gap recovery rate even if it transferred the same absolute amount of knowledge, purely because the denominator is larger. The paper does not discuss this mechanical relationship.

#### Claim: OPD learns thinking patterns rather than benchmark performance, demonstrated by reverse distillation.

**What was tested:** Figure 5 shows that distilling JustRL-1.5B backward toward R1-Distill-1.5B or R1-Distill-7B produces nearly identical regression trajectories. This is the paper's cleanest and most convincing experiment. The interpretation—that both teachers induce the same local target distribution on student-visited states despite their difference in scale and benchmark performance—is well-supported by near-identical training curves. However, the experiment does not directly measure the thinking patterns being learned; it infers them from the fact that both teachers produce the same outcome. An additional diagnostic—showing that the token-level target distributions (q_t) from the two teachers are indeed highly similar on student-visited states—would directly validate the mechanism rather than relying on the outcome as evidence for the mechanism. The paper's overlap ratio metric is computed between student and teacher, not between the two teachers, so it doesn't directly quantify how similar the two teachers are to each other on student-visited states.

**Strength of evidence:** Strong for the claim that teacher benchmark score does not predict OPD outcome in this configuration. The causal claim that "OPD learns thinking patterns" is a reasonable inference but would benefit from more direct evidence showing what specifically about the teacher's distribution is being acquired (e.g., analysis of which tokens drive the overlap, or qualitative analysis of how the student's generation style changes).

#### Claim: Overlap tokens are the locus of optimization; optimizing only them suffices.

**What was tested:** Figure 7 shows that Overlap Top-k matches Student Top-k in both accuracy and dynamics, while Non-Overlap Top-k is substantially weaker. This is a well-designed ablation with clear results. The self-reinforcing dynamic claim (overlap ratio grows because optimization concentrates mass on shared tokens, which pushes non-overlap tokens out) is supported by the finding that Non-Overlap Top-k causes overlap ratio to initially decrease. However, the experiment uses the default k = 16 and the successful teacher-student pair; it would be informative to see whether the same finding holds in a failing configuration (where overlap is stagnant) or with different values of k.

**Strength of evidence:** Strong for this specific configuration. The causal interpretation (overlap optimization is self-reinforcing) is well-supported by the divergent overlap ratio trajectories between Overlap Top-k and Non-Overlap Top-k.

#### Claim: Off-policy cold start and teacher-aligned prompts recover failing OPD.

**What was tested:** Figure 8 shows SFT+OPD substantially outperforms pure OPD. Figure 9 shows teacher-aligned templates help. Figure 10 shows teacher-aligned prompt content helps, with the entropy caveat. However, the paper does not demonstrate that these recipes recover OPD in a configuration that *would otherwise fail entirely*—rather, they show improvement over a baseline that already shows some improvement (the pure OPD curve in Figure 8 does rise, just more slowly). The claim of "recovering failing OPD" is therefore somewhat overstated: the recipes improve OPD that is weak, not OPD that is non-functional. The cold-start experiment also uses a different dataset for SFT (OpenThoughts3) than for OPD (remaining OpenThoughts after deduplication), introducing a potential confound where the SFT stage provides additional training data rather than just pattern alignment. A cleaner control would use the same prompts for SFT and OPD.

**Strength of evidence:** Strong for the claim that these interventions improve OPD performance. Weaker for the claim that they specifically address the thinking-pattern gap identified in Section 3—the evidence for the mechanism (overlap ratio dynamics) is correlational, and the paper doesn't compare these interventions against alternative interventions that might work through different mechanisms.

#### Claim: Reward quality degrades with trajectory depth, creating a ceiling for OPD.

**What was tested:** Figure 11a shows the inverted-U in accuracy vs. response length. Figure 11b shows monotonic decline in teacher continuation advantage. Figures 12–13 show late-stage collapse dynamics and suffix-to-prefix entropy propagation. This is a thorough and convincing set of experiments with multiple converging lines of evidence. The teacher continuation experiment (Figure 11b) is particularly clever as a direct probe of teacher reliability at different depths. However, the experiment uses a fixed teacher-student pair (JustRL-1.5B → R1-Distill-1.5B); it's unclear whether the specific depth ceiling (peak at 3K–7K) would generalize to other model pairs, especially those with different thinking-pattern compatibility. A pair with higher initial overlap might maintain useful teacher supervision to greater depths.

**Strength of evidence:** Strong for the existence of a depth ceiling in this configuration. The mechanism (suffix-triggered collapse propagating backward) is well-documented. Generalizability to other model pairs, other domains (code, agentic tasks), and other response length distributions is not tested.

#### Claim: Globally informative reward does not guarantee local exploitability.

**What was tested:** Figure 14 shows comparable AUROC for both teachers (0.73 vs. 0.75). The anisotropy hypothesis is proposed but not verified. The paper explicitly acknowledges this as unverified ("We have not directly verified this anisotropy hypothesis"). The evidence cited—high per-token advantage co-occurring with low gradient norm—is correlational and based on aggregate statistics rather than direct measurement of gradient direction consistency across positions. Verifying the hypothesis would require decomposing the gradient into per-position contributions and measuring their directional alignment (e.g., via cosine similarity between per-token gradient vectors), which the paper does not do.

**Strength of evidence:** The finding that both teachers provide globally discriminative reward is solid. The proposed mechanism (anisotropy) is speculative and identified as future work. The paper should be clearer that this section identifies a puzzle (globally informative reward + local optimization failure) rather than resolving it.

#### Missing Experiments and Genuine Weaknesses

- **Single domain (math)**: All experiments use mathematical reasoning benchmarks. Whether the token-level mechanisms (overlap dynamics, depth ceiling) generalize to code, agentic reasoning, or open-ended generation is untested. The paper acknowledges this as future work.
- **No repeated runs**: Every configuration is run exactly once. Without error bars or multiple seeds, it's impossible to assess whether performance differences between configurations (e.g., the ~2 percentage point gap between Top-4 and Top-16 in Figure 15) are statistically reliable or within training variance.
- **Fixed hyperparameters**: The learning rate (1 × 10⁻⁶), batch size (64), rollout number (4), and k (16) are held constant across all configurations. It's possible that failing OPD runs (e.g., with R1-Distill-7B as teacher) could be recovered with different hyperparameters (lower learning rate, more rollouts, larger k), and the paper does not test this. The claim that these configurations "fail" is therefore conditional on the specific hyperparameter settings used.
- **No comparison to off-policy distillation or RL**: The paper compares OPD configurations against each other but never against alternative post-training methods. This is deliberate (the goal is understanding OPD dynamics, not benchmarking), but it limits the practical significance of the "gains" reported.
- **Difficulty estimation for prompts is absent**: Unlike some related work that stratifies results by problem difficulty, the paper averages across all prompts in DAPO-Math-17K without analyzing whether OPD dynamics differ for easy vs. hard problems. Given the paper's focus on thinking-pattern compatibility, it's plausible that OPD success varies with problem difficulty (as seen in the prior work on test-time compute scaling).
- **Gap recovery rate conflates teacher strength and knowledge transferability**: As noted above, a much stronger teacher mechanically depresses the gap recovery rate even if it transfers the same absolute amount of knowledge. The paper uses this metric to compare teachers within families (where benchmark differences are moderate), but it doesn't discuss this conflation.
- **The 200-step training horizon**: All experiments train for 200 steps. The paper does not test whether failed runs (e.g., stagnant overlap with R1-Distill-7B) would eventually improve with longer training, or whether successful runs eventually plateau. The claim that failure is permanent within a reasonable training budget is implicit but not tested.
- **No investigation of temperature or sampling strategy effects**: All experiments use temperature 1.0 during training. Since temperature directly affects the entropy of the student's distribution (and thus the overlap dynamics), testing whether different temperatures could recover failing configurations or extend the depth ceiling would be informative.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### All Experiments Are Confined to Mathematical Reasoning Benchmarks

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses mathematical reasoning datasets for both training (DAPO-Math-17K, OpenThoughts3 math subset, DeepMath) and validation (AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC 2023). The authors explicitly acknowledge this scope limitation in Section 8: "All experiments in this work are conducted on mathematical benchmarks. Whether the same conditions and token-level mechanisms govern OPD in other domains such as code and open-ended settings remains an important open question."

**The consequence.** Math reasoning has specific structural properties that may make OPD's token-level dynamics unusually favorable or unfavorable. Mathematical solutions typically follow structured step-by-step derivations where token sequences are highly constrained—once you commit to a particular approach, the subsequent tokens are relatively predictable. This means the overlap-ratio dynamic (progressive alignment on high-probability tokens) may be amplified in math relative to domains where the generation space is more diffuse (creative writing, dialogue) or where correctness signals are less crisp (summarization, translation). The trajectory-depth ceiling identified in Section 6.1—where reward quality degrades beyond ~7K tokens—may be specific to the length distribution of math chain-of-thought traces; code generation or multi-turn agentic interaction could have entirely different depth/reliability profiles. The paper's central findings (thinking-pattern compatibility matters, overlap tokens are the locus of optimization, off-policy cold start helps) depend on models being able to converge on shared solution strategies through token-level alignment, which presupposes the task has a well-defined structure that makes such convergence possible. In open-ended generation without ground-truth structure, the teacher's token-level distribution may be less informative, and the student-teacher overlap may never reach the self-reinforcing regime the paper documents.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not include even a token ablation on a non-math task to probe generalizability. All claims are conditioned implicitly on the math reasoning domain. The cross-model validation (Appendix B.3) tests different model pairs (R1-Distill-7B student, Skywork-OR1-Math-7B and R1-Distill-14B teachers) but still within the math domain on the same benchmarks. The consistent patterns across the Qwen and DeepSeek model families (Sections 3.1, 3.2) provide within-domain robustness but do not address cross-domain transfer.

**Mitigation status.** The paper lists "Beyond Mathematical Reasoning" as the first item in future work (Section 8) but does not attempt any cross-domain experiments. The limitation is fully acknowledged but entirely unresolved.

---

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted For, and Difficulty-Dependent Dynamics Are Not Analyzed

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper treats all prompts in the training dataset as exchangeable, analyzing OPD dynamics averaged across the full prompt distribution. There is no analysis of how OPD success or failure varies with problem difficulty, no attempt to estimate difficulty, and no adaptive allocation of compute based on difficulty. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation explicitly, but it is a structural omission given the paper's focus on understanding *when* OPD works.

**The consequence.** The diagnostic framework the paper develops—initial overlap ratio predicts OPD success—is evaluated on aggregate statistics that may mask substantial heterogeneity. It is entirely possible that OPD with a "failing" teacher (e.g., R1-Distill-7B → R1-Distill-1.5B in Section 4.1) actually succeeds on easier problems within the training set while failing catastrophically on harder ones, or vice versa. The stagnation of overlap ratio in the failing run (stuck at ~0.72 in Figure 6, bottom-left) could reflect a mixture of problems where overlap is high and improving versus problems where overlap is near zero and impossible to recover, with the aggregate masking both subpopulations.

This matters for practical deployment: if OPD's effectiveness is difficulty-dependent (as prior work on test-time compute scaling found for completely different methods), then a practitioner deciding whether to use OPD needs to know whether their target problem distribution falls in the "overlap-recoverable" regime. The paper's blanket statements about which teachers "succeed" or "fail" may not hold when the problem distribution is skewed toward particular difficulty levels. A teacher that "fails" on average could be perfectly adequate for easy problems; a teacher that "succeeds" on average could provide zero benefit on the hardest subset that actually matters for a given application.

Additionally, the off-policy cold start and teacher-aligned prompt interventions (Sections 5.1, 5.2) are evaluated on average accuracy. It's possible these interventions help primarily on easy/medium problems while leaving hard problems untouched, or that they trade off performance across difficulty levels (e.g., improving easy-problem accuracy while reducing hard-problem exploration). The paper provides no breakdown to assess this.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not stratify any result by problem difficulty, does not report per-difficulty-bin overlap ratios, and does not test whether difficulty estimation could be used to route problems between OPD and alternative strategies. The DAPO-Math-17K dataset presumably contains problems of varying difficulty, but the paper makes no use of this variation.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed at all. The paper does not mention difficulty-dependent analysis as future work or as a limitation. This is a significant gap for a paper that aims to provide a systematic understanding of when OPD works.

---

### All OPD Runs Are Single-Seed; Statistical Reliability Is Unknown

**The assumption or constraint.** Every OPD training run in the paper is conducted exactly once. There are no repeated runs with different random seeds, no error bars on any metric, and no statistical significance testing. The paper implicitly assumes that the training dynamics and final performance are sufficiently stable that a single run per configuration is representative.

**The consequence.** Many of the performance differences between configurations are small in absolute terms and could plausibly be within the range of training variance. For example, in Figure 15, the difference between sampled-token OPD and Top-4 OPD on AIME 2025 is approximately 0.33 vs. 0.33 (essentially identical), while Top-1 vs. Top-4 differs by about 0.02. Without error bars, it is impossible to know whether the Top-1 "underperformance" is robust or a single-seed artifact. The claim in Section 3.1 that the GRPO teacher "consistently outperforms" the non-thinking teacher rests on accuracy curves that, while persistently separated, could overlap substantially with variance bands. The gap recovery rates in Section 3.2 (5.3% vs. 16.9%; 15.6% vs. 58.6%) are more dramatic and less likely to be explained by seed variance, but the exact magnitudes are unreliable without replicates.

This is not merely a statistical hygiene issue. The paper makes mechanistic claims (e.g., "early-stage thinking-pattern mismatch causes a loss of distillation benefit that cannot be recovered later") that depend on the shape and separation of training curves. If the gap between the GRPO and non-thinking teacher curves in Figure 2 narrowed substantially in some replicate runs, the irrecoverability claim would be weakened. The finding that overlap ratio "stagnates" in failing runs (Figure 6) versus "rises steadily" in successful runs is more robust because the qualitative pattern (stationary vs. monotonically increasing) is less sensitive to seed variance, but the exact overlap values and the point at which stagnation is declared still depend on single-run estimates.

The 200-step training horizon introduces additional seed sensitivity. The paper trains for exactly one epoch (200 steps) without reporting whether training has converged. If some runs have higher variance in their trajectory (plateauing early vs. continuing to improve slowly), the relative ordering at step 200 could differ from the asymptotic ordering, and without replicates there is no way to detect this.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper relies on replication across multiple benchmarks and multiple model families as a form of indirect statistical validation. The consistent qualitative patterns—progressive overlap growth in successful runs, stagnation in failed runs, suffix-triggered collapse in long trajectories—appear across multiple controlled comparisons, which strengthens confidence. However, the benchmark-wise breakdowns (Figures 17, 22) show that some individual benchmarks have noisier curves than the averages, confirming that variance exists but not quantifying it.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge the lack of replicates as a limitation and does not conduct any statistical testing. This is a standard practice in much of the LLM training literature (where full training runs are expensive and single-run reporting is common), but it limits the weight that can be placed on small performance differences and makes the mechanistic claims more qualitative than quantitative.

### The Cold-Start and Prompt-Alignment Recipes Are Not Tested on Truly Failed OPD Configurations

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper claims that off-policy cold start and teacher-aligned prompt selection "recover OPD in otherwise failing configurations" (Section 5 introduction). However, the baseline OPD runs in these experiments are not actually failing—they show clear (if slow) improvement.

**The consequence.** In the cold-start experiment (Figure 8), the pure-OPD baseline (Qwen3-1.7B-Base → Qwen3-4B Non-thinking) improves from approximately 0.02 to 0.08 on AIME 2024 and from approximately 0.12 to 0.28 on AMC 2023 over 200 steps. This is not a "failed" configuration in the sense of the truly stagnant R1-Distill-7B → R1-Distill-1.5B run from Section 4.1 (where accuracy on AIME 2024 stays at ~0.29–0.30). The cold-start experiment demonstrates that SFT + OPD is *better* than OPD alone, but it does not demonstrate recovery of a genuinely non-functional OPD configuration. The paper never tests whether cold-start SFT can rescue the R1-Distill-7B → R1-Distill-1.5B failure documented in Section 4.1—the configuration where the mechanistic analysis identified stagnant overlap and persistent entropy mismatch.

This matters because the mechanistic narrative the paper constructs—OPD fails when initial overlap is too low, and cold-start SFT bridges the thinking-pattern gap by raising initial overlap—makes a specific prediction: applying cold-start SFT to a genuinely failed configuration should raise the initial overlap ratio and unlock the self-reinforcing overlap growth dynamic. Without testing this prediction on a configuration known to fail, the causal mechanism remains a correlation observed in one setting (SFT helps where OPD is weak) rather than a verified intervention that works through the posited mechanism.

Similarly, the prompt alignment experiments (Figures 9, 10) improve OPD that is already working (JustRL-1.5B → R1-Distill-1.5B in Figure 9; Qwen3-4B-Base-GRPO → Qwen3-1.7B-Base in Figure 10). Neither experiment tests whether teacher-aligned prompts can recover a known failed configuration. The practical recipes are therefore validated as *improvements over good OPD*, not as *rescues of bad OPD*, which limits their applicability to the failure modes the paper spends most of its analytical effort characterizing.

The teacher-aligned prompt experiments also introduce a potential confound: the template-aligned run (Figure 9) uses a different format that may affect answer extraction during validation, not just training dynamics. If the teacher-aligned template produces answers that are easier to parse correctly, some of the reported accuracy gain could be an evaluation artifact rather than a genuine distillation improvement. The paper does not discuss or control for this.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The gap between "improving weak OPD" and "recovering failed OPD" is visible by comparing Figure 8 (cold start: baseline improves, intervention improves more) against Figure 6 (true failure: baseline is flat). The paper uses the language of "recovery" and "rescuing failing OPD" in Sections 5.1 and 5.2 without testing the interventions on any configuration shown to fail in Sections 3 or 4.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the distinction between weak-but-improving OPD and genuinely failed OPD, and does not test the interventions on known failure cases. This is a significant gap in the paper's claim that phenomenological understanding (Section 3) directly motivates effective recipes (Section 5)—the recipes are validated on configurations where the phenomenological conditions were already partially met, not on configurations where they were violated.

### The Anisotropic Reward Hypothesis Is Speculative and Unverified

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 6.2 proposes that the failure of the R1-Distill-7B teacher is caused by anisotropic per-token advantages—the teacher's token-level signals point in inconsistent directions across positions, causing gradient cancellation—despite the sequence-level reward being globally informative. The paper explicitly states: "We have not directly verified this anisotropy hypothesis, and doing so would require analyzing the directional structure of per-token gradients, which we leave to future work."

**The consequence.** The mechanistic account of OPD failure is incomplete at its most critical juncture. The paper's central puzzle—why does a teacher with globally informative reward (AUROC 0.75) fail to produce useful gradients?—remains unanswered. The anisotropy hypothesis is plausible but is one of several possible explanations: the 7B teacher might provide token-level rewards that are individually noisier (higher variance in `log q_t(y_t) - log p_t(y_t)` across different student-generated rollouts), the optimization landscape might have pathological curvature (making gradient descent slow even with correct gradient directions), or the student might be stuck in a local minimum of the reverse KL objective where small perturbations in the teacher's distribution produce gradients that don't generalize.

The practical implication of this uncertainty is substantial. If the anisotropy hypothesis is correct, the solution involves identifying and upweighting directionally consistent tokens or changing the optimization objective to aggregate token-level signals more robustly. If the problem is high variance in reward estimates, the solution involves more Monte Carlo samples per token (increasing rollout number or `k`). If the problem is optimization landscape geometry, the solution involves different learning rates, optimizers, or curriculum strategies. Without knowing which mechanism drives the failure, practitioners observing OPD stagnation cannot diagnose the root cause or select an appropriate intervention.

The paper's evidence for the anisotropy hypothesis is correlational: the failing run shows larger overlap-token advantage magnitude but smaller gradient norm. However, gradient norm is a scalar summary that conflates gradient magnitude and gradient direction; a small gradient norm could arise from either small per-token gradients or large gradients that point in different directions and cancel. Decomposing the gradient into per-token contributions and measuring their directional alignment (e.g., via pairwise cosine similarity between per-position gradient vectors) would distinguish these cases but is not attempted.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The AUROC analysis (Figure 14) convincingly shows that both teachers provide globally discriminative sequence-level reward. The co-occurrence of large per-token advantage and small gradient norm in the failing run (Figure 6, bottom-middle vs. Appendix B.2, Figure 19 middle) is documented but not causally analyzed.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly identifies this as unverified and future work. The transparency about the limitation is commendable, but it means one of the paper's most important mechanistic claims—explaining *why* OPD fails when the teacher appears informative—remains a hypothesis rather than an established finding. The practical recipes in Section 5 do not address the anisotropic reward problem; they focus on improving overlap dynamics, which is upstream of the reward exploitation step where Section 6.2 suggests the bottleneck lies.

### The Depth Ceiling Is Characterized but Not Resolved; Trajectory Length Selection Remains Heuristic

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 6.1 demonstrates that OPD performance degrades systematically with trajectory depth, peaking at 3K–7K tokens and declining at 10K–15K, with late-stage training collapse characterized by suffix-triggered entropy propagation. However, the paper identifies no mechanism for predicting the optimal max response length for a given teacher-student pair and provides no method for mitigating the depth-driven degradation other than limiting the response length.

**The consequence.** The paper's finding that response length has an inverted-U relationship with OPD performance means that the max response length hyperparameter is both critical and difficult to set. Setting it too low (0.5K–1K) starves the model of supervised tokens; setting it too high (10K–15K) causes training instability and active performance regression. The optimal value (3K–7K in this specific configuration) is likely dependent on the teacher-student pair, the domain, and the typical length of correct solutions. The paper provides no guidance for selecting this value without running an expensive sweep like the one in Figure 11a.

This limitation is particularly consequential given the trend toward longer reasoning traces in LLM post-training. Models like o1, DeepSeek-R1, and their derivatives routinely produce chain-of-thought traces of 10K–30K tokens, well beyond the ~7K-token sweet spot identified in this paper. If the depth ceiling generalizes, current OPD techniques may be fundamentally incompatible with long-horizon reasoning without architectural or algorithmic changes. The paper's suggestion in Section 8—"hybrid approaches that combine dense token-level supervision on short segments with sparse outcome-level rewards for longer horizons"—is conceptually promising but entirely untested.

The suffix-to-prefix collapse mechanism (Figures 13, 23) is a valuable diagnostic, but it does not translate into an actionable intervention. Knowing that instability originates at later tokens does not, by itself, tell a practitioner how to prevent it—should they truncate trajectories early? Apply lower learning rates to later tokens? Use a different loss weighting that down-weights suffix tokens? The paper explores none of these potential mitigations.

The depth ceiling also interacts problematically with the overlap-token mechanism. The paper shows that successful OPD drives overlap ratio from ~72% to ~91% (Figure 6), but this analysis uses the default 7,168-token max response length. At longer lengths (15K), the overlap ratio *collapses* in late training (Figure 12), suggesting that the self-reinforcing overlap growth dynamic—the paper's central mechanistic finding—is length-dependent and breaks down entirely at sufficient depth. The paper does not characterize this breakdown: at what point in training does the collapse begin? Does it affect all problems equally, or only those that naturally require long solutions? Can the collapse be detected early and training truncated before performance degrades?

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The length sweep (Figure 11a), training dynamics at multiple lengths (Figure 12), entropy heatmaps (Figures 13, 23), and teacher continuation experiment (Figure 11b) together provide detailed characterization of the depth ceiling for one specific teacher-student pair. The paper does not replicate this analysis on any other configuration.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies the depth ceiling as a fundamental limitation and suggests hybrid dense-sparse supervision and curriculum strategies as future work (Section 8), but does not develop or test any mitigation. The max response length in all other experiments is fixed at 7,168 tokens—below the identified ceiling—so the main experimental findings are protected from this limitation, but the generalizability of the findings to longer-trajectory settings is unknown.
