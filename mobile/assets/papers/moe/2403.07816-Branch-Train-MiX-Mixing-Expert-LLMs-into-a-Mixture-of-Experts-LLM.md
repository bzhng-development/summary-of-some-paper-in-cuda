# Branch-Train-MiX: Mixing Expert LLMs into a Mixture-of-Experts LLM

**ArXiv:** [2403.07816](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07816)

## 🎯 Pitch

You can train separate domain experts in parallel with zero communication cost, then merge them into a single MoE model that outperforms a dense 13B model while using 5x less training compute. Crucially, the magic isn't just the parallel training—it's the subsequent MoE finetuning stage that learns token-level routing, without which accuracy actually drops compared to the seed model.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Branch-Train-MiX (BTX)**, a continued pretraining method that improves a generalist LLM's capabilities across multiple specialized domains by first training domain experts in an embarrassingly parallel fashion from a shared seed model, then merging them into a single Mixture-of-Experts model with learned token-level routing. Using Llama-2 7B as the seed model and training experts on math, code, and Wikipedia domains, BTX achieves an average score of 47.9 across all benchmarks, outperforming both the seed model (40.7) and Llama-2 13B (45.4) while using substantially less training compute. The approach generalizes two special cases—Branch-Train-Merge (which omits the MoE finetuning stage to learn routing) and sparse upcycling (which omits the stage of training experts asynchronously)—establishing that the combination of embarrassingly parallel expert training followed by MoE finetuning achieves the best accuracy-efficiency tradeoff, though gains are concentrated in domains where domain experts were explicitly trained, with math and code improving dramatically (+28.9 points on GSM8K and +17.9 points on HumanEval over the seed model, approaching specialized models like Llemma and CodeLlama) while catastrophic forgetting on non-specialist domains is prevented only through retention of the original seed model as a generalist expert.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Training LLMs for Multiple Specialized Domains Is Computationally Inefficient

The fundamental challenge this paper tackles is a practical one with significant economic implications: **how to train a single large language model to excel at multiple specialized domains (like math, code, and world knowledge) without requiring the massive synchronized training infrastructure that typically bottlenecks such efforts.**

The standard approach to building capable LLMs involves training on enormous, diverse corpora spanning thousands of GPUs, with all training workers kept in lockstep synchronization after every weight update. This synchronized training paradigm creates two interlocking problems that become more severe as models and datasets grow:

**First, the communication bottleneck.** In distributed training, each GPU maintains a copy of the model, computes gradients independently on its data shard, and then must communicate those gradients to all other GPUs before the next update. As the number of GPUs scales up, this all-to-all communication dominates wall-clock time, making additional GPUs yield diminishing returns in training throughput. The paper notes this explicitly (Section 1):

> "Training such LLMs requires a large amount of compute and data, exceeding thousands of GPUs and trillions of tokens. The training parallelization is typically done by maintaining multiple copies of the model on different GPUs and keeping them synchronized after each weight update. The cost of this frequent communication is the main bottleneck in scaling the training to more GPUs."

**Second, the fragility problem.** Synchronized training creates a single point of failure at scale: if one GPU fails (hardware fault, memory error, network blip), the entire training run halts because every worker depends on receiving gradients from every other worker. At the scale of thousands of GPUs, hardware failures become statistically frequent rather than exceptional, making training runs increasingly precarious as they scale up.

Both problems are particularly acute during **continued pretraining**—the common practice of taking a pretrained generalist LLM (like Llama-2) and further training it on new domain-specific data to improve capabilities in those domains. Continued pretraining is increasingly important because it allows organizations to adapt existing models to new domains without retraining from scratch, but it inherits all the synchronization bottlenecks of the original pretraining.

### The Dual Challenge: Specialization Without Forgetting

Beyond the infrastructure problems, there is a deeper learning challenge at play. When you continue training a model on domain-specific data (say, math), you face a **catastrophic forgetting** problem: the model's performance on other domains (world knowledge, commonsense reasoning, code) degrades, sometimes dramatically. The paper's data in Table 1 makes this strikingly concrete:

- The **Math expert** achieves 39.5 on GSM8K and 18.8 on MATH (up from 14.7 and 2.5 in the seed model) — impressive gains. But its performance on TriviaQA collapses from 58.5 to 37.1, a 21.4-point drop.
- The **Code expert** similarly improves on HumanEval (12.8 → 31.7) and MBPP (20.8 → 40.2), but drops on MMLU from 46.1 to 39.6 and on TriviaQA from 58.5 to 29.9.
- Even the **Wikipedia expert**, trained on factual knowledge data, actually *improves* on Natural Questions (16.4 → 21.8) and holds steady on TriviaQA (58.5 → 57.2) but regresses on math and code tasks.

This is a fundamental tension: domain-specialized continued pretraining creates strong experts that are useless as generalist models. If you need both code and math capabilities (a common requirement for AI assistants), you cannot simply train a single model on both domains sequentially — the second training phase will partially overwrite the first. And training on all domains simultaneously in a single dense model risks task interference, where gradients from different domains conflict with each other, leading to suboptimal learning on all fronts.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper identifies three existing approaches, each of which addresses part of the problem but introduces new deficiencies:

#### Approach 1: Branch-Train-Merge (BTM)

The Branch-Train-Merge method (Li et al., 2022a; Gururangan et al., 2023) directly tackles the synchronization problem. The procedure is elegantly simple: make multiple copies of a seed LLM, train each copy independently on a different domain's data with zero inter-worker communication, and then at inference time, determine which expert to use by classifying the input prompt's domain (or a mixture of experts by averaging their output probabilities).

This achieves the parallel training goal perfectly — training is $N$-way embarrassingly parallel, communication-free, and resilient to individual worker failures. But the paper identifies a critical deficiency (Section 1):

> "While this approach makes training more efficient, its main drawback is the lack of a unified single model making it impossible to do further supervised finetuning (SFT) or reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) finetuning, both of which can boost performance further, and are crucial steps in building aligned LLMs."

This limitation runs deeper than it might first appear. BTM produces a *collection* of models, not a single model. This means:
- You cannot apply instruction finetuning, which requires a single model with consistent behavior across all inputs.
- You cannot apply RLHF, which needs a single policy to optimize.
- Inference requires a separate domain classification step (typically tf-idf based), adding complexity and potential failure points.
- The model's behavior is fundamentally different from standard LLMs — it's a routing system over multiple frozen experts, not a unified neural network that reasons across domains.

The paper's BTM baseline results (Table 2) confirm the performance ceiling: BTM achieves 43.1 (Top-1) to 43.4 (Top-2) average score, substantially below BTX's 47.9, demonstrating that simple output-level ensembling of experts cannot match learned token-level routing within a unified model.

#### Approach 2: Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Training from Scratch

Mixture-of-Experts (Jacobs et al., 1991; Shazeer et al., 2017) addresses a different problem: *how to scale model capacity without proportionally scaling compute*. In MoE Transformers (Fedus et al., 2022), the feedforward sublayers are replaced with multiple parallel "expert" FF modules, and a learned router selects which experts to activate on a per-token basis. Since only a subset of experts (typically Top-2) are activated for each token, the total parameter count grows while the compute per token stays roughly constant.

This approach has proven highly effective at scale — models like Mixtral (Jiang et al., 2024) demonstrate that MoE models can match dense models with far fewer active parameters. However, the paper identifies a subtle but important limitation of standard MoE training:

> "Unlike Branch-Train-Merge, Mixture-of-Experts are often trained in a fully synchronized fashion, and the communication cost increases with the number of experts due to all-to-all communication."

The all-to-all communication issue is specific to MoE architectures: because tokens are dynamically routed to different experts across different GPUs (in model-parallel deployments), the communication pattern is more complex and voluminous than in dense models. Each GPU must send tokens to potentially any other GPU depending on routing decisions, and this all-to-all pattern scales poorly with the number of experts. Additionally, training MoE from scratch means the router and all experts must converge together through synchronized gradient updates — there is no way to parallelize this by training experts independently.

A crucial empirical observation that emerges from MoE research, which the paper explicitly references (Section 3.3), is that **experts in standard MoE training do not naturally specialize to domains**:

> "The motivation behind this is an observation that MoE experts trained in a standard way do not show domain specialization, but rather are activated uniformly across different domains (Jiang et al., 2024)."

This is a non-obvious finding with important implications. Despite training on diverse data, standard MoE experts do not naturally partition along domain boundaries — the router learns to distribute load roughly uniformly across experts for all domains. This means standard MoE provides capacity scaling but does not, by itself, create domain specialists whose knowledge can be independently trained or updated.

#### Approach 3: Sparse Upcycling

Sparse upcycling (Komatsuzaki et al., 2022) occupies an intermediate position: take a pretrained dense model, replicate its feedforward layers to create multiple identical experts, add a randomly initialized router, and then continue training the MoE model. This avoids training from scratch and leverages the dense model's pretrained knowledge, but it still performs all training in a synchronized MoE fashion — there is no embarrassingly parallel expert training phase.

The paper treats sparse upcycling as a special case of BTX (Section 2 and 3.2):

> "BTX generalizes two special cases, the Branch-Train-Merge method, which does not have the MoE finetuning stage to learn routing, and sparse upcycling, which omits the stage of training experts asynchronously."

The comparison with sparse upcycling is central to the paper's motivation because it isolates the value of embarrassingly parallel expert training. Sparse upcycling's training is entirely synchronized MoE training with no independent expert phase. BTX spends part of its compute budget on asynchronous expert training (which has higher throughput due to no communication) and part on synchronized MoE finetuning. If BTX outperforms sparse upcycling at equal total compute, it demonstrates that the asynchronous phase is not just a convenience — it provides genuine efficiency gains.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions BTX as a synthesis that captures the benefits of both BTM (asynchronous training, high throughput) and MoE (unified model, learnable routing) while mitigating their respective weaknesses.

The intellectual move is to recognize that **the two stages serve complementary purposes and should be combined** rather than treated as alternatives:

- **Stage 1 (Branch & Train):** Use embarrassingly parallel training to create domain experts. This stage benefits from maximum throughput (no communication), resilience to failures, and the ability for each expert to fully specialize without interference from other domains' gradients. The paper explicitly frames this as a solution to the communication bottleneck and fragility of synchronized training.

- **Stage 2 (MiX & Finetune):** Combine the independently trained experts into a single MoE model and finetune. This stage introduces learned token-level routing (a router network $W_l$ at each layer) that can dynamically decide which expert to use for each token, rather than the coarse document-level routing of BTM. It also averages the self-attention and embedding parameters across experts (under the stated assumption that "self-attention layers are less domain specialized than the feedforward layers," Section 3.2), creating a unified model that supports standard SFT and RLHF.

The paper's key insight is that **Stage 1 maximizes training efficiency while Stage 2 maximizes model quality**, and a relatively small amount of Stage 2 compute (80B tokens of MoE finetuning versus 453B+ tokens of expert training) can effectively integrate separately trained experts into a single high-performing model.

This framing connects BTX to several broader research themes while distinguishing it from them:

- **Continual learning:** BTX can be viewed as a parameter-isolation approach to continual learning (Lange et al., 2019), where different parameters handle different domains to prevent catastrophic forgetting. However, unlike classic continual learning which focuses on sequential task learning without access to previous data, BTX retains the original seed model as a generalist expert and uses MoE finetuning on a mixture of all data, giving it access to previous-domain data during the integration phase.

- **Asynchronous parallel training:** BTX extends the line of work on reducing communication in distributed training (Zhang et al., 2015; Douillard et al., 2023) by taking the extreme approach of zero communication during expert training. Unlike elastic averaging methods that periodically synchronize, BTX experts diverge completely during training and are only unified in the subsequent MoE finetuning phase.

- **Domain-specialized architectures:** Prior work on domain-specialized transformers (Gururangan et al., 2021) used domain-conditioned fixed routing within feedforward layers, but lacked the asynchronous training aspect. BTX argues that the combination of asynchronous training + learned routing is what drives the efficiency gains.

The paper's experimental design reinforces this positioning through its baseline comparisons. By comparing BTX against:
- **BTM** (which has async training but no learned routing or unified model)
- **Sparse upcycling** (which has MoE routing but no async training)
- **Dense continued training** (which has neither async training nor MoE)

The paper provides a factorial decomposition showing that both components matter and their combination is synergistic. Table 2 shows BTX at 47.9 average versus BTM at 43.4 (+4.5 from MoE integration) and dense at 44.5 (+3.4 from MoE + async training), while Table 3 shows BTX outperforming compute-matched sparse upcycling (47.9 vs. 47.3) with significantly higher training throughput (2× more data processed in the same GPU-days).

### The Practical Stakes

The stakes of this work are primarily practical and economic. The paper is not proposing a new architectural innovation per se — MoE layers, Top-K routing, and continued pretraining are all established techniques. Rather, it is proposing a **training methodology** that reorders and reallocates how compute is spent during continued pretraining to achieve better results under a fixed budget.

The implications matter for any organization doing large-scale LLM training:

- **Compute allocation strategy:** The results suggest that during the late stages of pretraining (when moving from a generalist model to one with specialized capabilities), it is more compute-efficient to train domain experts separately and then integrate them via MoE finetuning rather than training a single dense model on the combined data or training an MoE model from scratch.

- **Hardware utilization:** The embarrassingly parallel expert training phase can run on separate GPU clusters without high-speed interconnects between them, potentially allowing organizations to use cheaper or geographically distributed compute resources for the bulk of specialized training.

- **Model deployment flexibility:** The resulting BTX model is a standard MoE architecture that can be deployed, finetuned, and served like any other LLM, unlike BTM which requires custom inference logic and resists further finetuning.

The paper is careful not to overclaim: it only tests three domains (math, code, Wikipedia) with four experts total (including the seed model), uses a single seed model family (Llama-2 7B), and acknowledges that it has not combined BTX with instruction finetuning or RLHF. But within these bounds, it makes a clear case that the two-stage approach — asynchronous specialization followed by synchronized integration — represents a Pareto improvement over existing methods for continued pretraining.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Branch-Train-MiX (BTX) is a **three-stage training pipeline** for transforming a single pretrained generalist language model into a unified Mixture-of-Experts model that excels across multiple specialized domains. It solves the problem of *efficiently adding specialized capabilities to an LLM without sacrificing existing general knowledge*, using an embarrassingly parallel training phase to maximize throughput followed by a synchronized MoE integration phase to unify the specialists into a single, finetunable model.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The BTX pipeline consists of three sequential stages, each with distinct computational properties:

**Stage 1 — Branch:** Starting from a pretrained seed LLM (Llama-2 7B), create N identical copies of the entire model. Each copy will become one domain expert. Additionally, retain the original seed model itself as a "generalist" expert.

**Stage 2 — Train:** Each copy is independently trained on a different domain-specific dataset (math, code, Wikipedia) using standard language modeling loss, with zero communication between training processes. This yields N domain expert LLMs plus the original generalist.

**Stage 3 — MiX:** All expert models are combined into a single Mixture-of-Experts architecture. For each Transformer layer, the feedforward sublayers from all experts are placed into an MoE module with a newly initialized learned router. Self-attention and embedding parameters across experts are averaged. The resulting unified MoE model is then finetuned on a mixture of all domains' data so the router learns token-level expert selection.

Information flows in strict sequence: the seed model spawns parallel training processes, each emits an expert, and all experts are structurally merged then jointly finetuned into the final BTX model.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the Stage 1–2 formalism (Branch & Train):** I establish the expert training setup — what gets copied, what data each expert sees, and precisely the training objective — because everything downstream depends on these experts' quality and specialization.

- **Second, the Stage 3 architecture (MiX):** I explain the structural merge operation in detail: how feedforward layers become an MoE module, how self-attention is averaged, and why these choices differ. This is the architectural core of BTX.

- **Third, the MoE routing mechanism:** I walk through the mathematical form of the MoE feedforward layer, the Top-K routing function, and load balancing. These are the only *new* parameters introduced, and understanding how they operate is essential.

- **Fourth, the finetuning procedure:** I cover what data is used, how long training runs, and what optimization hyperparameters govern the router and averaged parameters. This is where the independently-trained parts learn to work together.

- **Fifth, the variations:** I explain the alternative routing methods (Switch, Soft routing, Sample Top-1), the load balancing loss, and the techniques for splitting and blending experts. These are not part of the default BTX recipe but illuminate design tradeoffs.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

BTX is a **continued pretraining methodology paper** whose core idea is that the optimal allocation of compute during skill acquisition is to first train domain specialists independently (maximizing throughput and avoiding interference), then integrate them via MoE with learnable token-level routing (recovering a unified model that can be further finetuned). The paper does not propose fundamentally new architectures or loss functions; rather, it reorders existing techniques (continued pretraining, MoE, sparse upcycling) into a pipeline that empirically Pareto-dominates the alternatives.

---

#### Stage 1 — Branch: Creating Expert Initializations from the Seed Model

The process begins with a pretrained seed model `$M$`, specifically Llama-2 7B (Touvron et al., 2023). The branching operation is conceptually trivial but mechanically precise:

**Step 1:** Create `$N$` identical copies of the seed model's full parameter set. The paper uses `$N = 3$` domain experts (math, code, Wikipedia), plus it retains the original seed model as a fourth "generalist" expert, producing a total of `$N + 1 = 4$` expert models available at merge time.

**Step 2:** Each copy `$M_i$` inherits identical architecture, identical parameter values, and identical tokenizer from the seed. There is no randomization, no parameter perturbation, and no architectural modification at this stage. Every expert starts from exactly the same point in weight space.

**Why start from the same seed rather than training experts from scratch:** The paper builds on prior findings that continued training of a pretrained LLM on domain-specific data converges much faster than training from scratch (Rozière et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2024; Azerbayev et al., 2023). The seed model already possesses general linguistic competence, reasoning ability, and substantial domain knowledge from its original pretraining. Branching from this shared initialization means each expert only needs to *specialize* — to amplify domain-relevant capabilities and learn domain-specific patterns — rather than learn language from scratch. This is the key efficiency argument behind the entire BTX approach.

**Design choice — including the original seed as an expert:** The paper explicitly states that retaining the seed model as a generalist expert is a deliberate choice to prevent catastrophic forgetting:

> "While we can proceed with only these three domain experts, we also include the original seed LLM as a 'generalist' expert so that its general knowledge is transferred to the final model." (Section 4.1.1)

Without this generalist expert, the MoE finetuning stage would only have access to parameters that have been shifted by domain-specific training, potentially losing the broad capabilities of the original seed model on tasks not covered by any domain expert (e.g., commonsense reasoning, general world knowledge). The generalist expert acts as an anchor, providing a stable source of the seed model's original knowledge that the router can access when domain-specific expertise is not needed.

---

#### Stage 2 — Train: Embarrassingly Parallel Domain Specialization

Each expert copy `$M_i$` is trained on its corresponding domain dataset `$D_i$` using the standard autoregressive language modeling objective. The training is explicitly described as "continued pretraining" rather than finetuning — it uses the same next-token prediction loss and optimization setup as the original Llama-2 pretraining.

**Training data for each expert (Section 4.1.1, Appendix A, Table 7):**

- **Math expert:** Trained on the Llemma data mixture (Azerbayev et al., 2023), which comprises AlgebraicStack (13.57% sampling ratio), OpenWebMath (54.27%), Arxiv (27.14%), Github (2.99%), and Commoncrawl (5.01%). The total training volume is 48,000 steps with 201B tokens — deliberately matched to Llemma's training budget to enable fair comparison.

- **Code expert:** Trained on the CodeLlama data mixture (Rozière et al., 2023), comprising code (82.18% sampling ratio), natural language related to code (9.90%), and general natural language (6.93%). The total training volume is 50,000 steps with 210B tokens, matched to the math expert's scale.

- **Wikipedia expert:** Trained on Wikipedia documents extracted between June and August 2022, preprocessed to remove hyperlinks, comments, and formatting boilerplate, plus 9.09% Commoncrawl data. The total training volume is 42B tokens — substantially less than the math and code experts because Wikipedia is a smaller and more homogeneous dataset.

**Optimization hyperparameters (Section 4.1.2):** All experts use the same optimization configuration:
- Optimizer: AdamW with weight decay 0.1
- Learning rate schedule: warmup to a peak of `$1 \times 10^{-4}$` over 100 steps, then cosine decay to 10% of the peak value
- Batch size: 4M tokens with sequence length 4096

**The embarrassingly parallel property:** Each expert training process runs completely independently — there is no gradient synchronization, no parameter sharing, and no communication between the training of different experts. The paper frames this as having three concrete benefits (Section 3.1):

1. **Linear throughput scaling:** Total expert training throughput scales linearly with the number of training processes, because each process uses independent hardware without coordination overhead. Joint training of a single model on all domains would require increasing the global batch size to use more GPUs, which can degrade optimization (the well-known large-batch training problem).

2. **Reduced communication cost:** There is zero all-to-all communication during expert training, unlike synchronized distributed training where gradient reduction across workers dominates wall-clock time at scale.

3. **Failure resilience:** A hardware failure in one expert's training process only affects that expert — the other `$N-1$` training processes continue uninterrupted. In synchronized training, a single GPU failure halts the entire training run.

**What the experts learn (Table 1):** The key empirical results from the expert training stage establish both the upside and the downside of specialization:

- The **Math expert** achieves 39.5 on GSM8K and 18.8 on MATH, compared to the seed model's 14.7 and 2.5 respectively. Interestingly, it also improves on code tasks (HumanEval 25.0 vs. 12.8, MBPP 33.6 vs. 20.8), suggesting transfer between math and code domains. However, it catastrophically forgets world knowledge: TriviaQA drops from 58.5 to 37.1, and Natural Questions drops from 16.4 to 14.4.

- The **Code expert** achieves 31.7 on HumanEval and 40.2 on MBPP (up from 12.8 and 20.8), but drops on TriviaQA (58.5 → 29.9), Natural Questions (16.4 → 11.5), and MMLU (46.1 → 39.6).

- The **Wikipedia expert** modestly improves knowledge tasks (Natural Questions 16.4 → 21.8, TriviaQA holds at 57.2) at the cost of math and code regression.

This pattern — significant gains in the target domain, catastrophic forgetting elsewhere — is the central motivation for the MiX stage. Several independent experts exist, each highly capable in its domain but degraded on others. The challenge is to combine them into a single model that preserves those domain gains without the forgetting.

#### Stage 3 — MiX: Structural Merge into a MoE Architecture

The MiX stage has two sub-operations: a structural parameter merge that constructs the MoE model, followed by a finetuning phase that optimizes the router and averaged parameters. The structural merge is where BTX makes its most distinctive architectural choice.

##### Feedforward Layers Become MoE Modules

The central claim of the merge operation is that **feedforward layers are the locus of domain specialization**, while self-attention layers and embeddings are more domain-agnostic. The paper states this explicitly:

> "The motivation behind this is the assumption that the self-attention layers are less domain specialized than the feedforward layers." (Section 3.2)

This assumption determines the entire structural merge strategy:

**For each Transformer layer `$l$` (out of `$L$` total layers):** Take the feedforward sublayer from each expert `$i$`, denoted `$\text{FF}_i^l(x)$`, and place all `$N+1$` of them (math, code, Wikipedia, and generalist) into a single MoE module. The merged MoE layer computes:

$$\text{FF}_{\text{MoE}}^l(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{N+1} g_i(W_l x) \cdot \text{FF}_i^l(x)$$

where:
- `$x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$` is the input representation at this layer (the output of the preceding self-attention sublayer, after residual connection and layer normalization — following the standard Transformer block structure)
- `$W_l \in \mathbb{R}^{(N+1) \times d_{\text{model}}}$` is a newly introduced linear transformation (the router's weight matrix) that maps the `$d_{\text{model}}$`-dimensional input to `$N+1$` scalar logits, one per expert
- `$g_i(\cdot)$` is a routing function that converts the `$N+1$` logits into `$N+1$` expert weights, typically producing sparse outputs (many zeros) so that only a subset of `$\text{FF}_i^l(x)$` terms are actually computed
- `$\text{FF}_i^l(x)$` is the frozen (or trainable) feedforward sublayer from expert `$i$` at layer `$l$`, which is itself a two-layer MLP with an activation function (likely SwiGLU for Llama-2 architecture, though the paper does not specify)

**What this computes:** For each token at each layer, the router first projects the token's representation to `$N+1$` scalar scores via `$W_l x$`. The routing function `$g$` then converts these scores to weights — in Top-K routing, all but the `$K$` largest are zeroed out. The output is a weighted sum of the selected experts' FF transformations, where each expert processes the same input token `$x$` independently. The sum is over all `$N+1$` experts mathematically, but the sparsity of `$g$` means only `$K$` experts are actually computed.

**Why this form rather than other merging strategies:** Prior work on Branch-Train-Merge (Li et al., 2022a) merges at the output level: the final token probability distributions from each expert are averaged, with mixture weights determined by domain classification. BTX's per-layer, per-token merging provides a strictly more expressive combination: a math problem about code (e.g., "write a Python function to compute the prime factors") can route code-related tokens to the code expert and math-related tokens to the math expert *within the same sequence*. BTM cannot do this — it must select one or more experts for the entire input based on tf-idf similarity. Additionally, MoE routing is learned through gradient descent during finetuning, whereas BTM's routing is a static domain classifier that cannot adapt to token-level nuance.

##### Self-Attention and Embedding Layers Are Averaged

For all other parameters — self-attention layers, embeddings, and any other non-FF parameters — the merge operation is a simple arithmetic mean across all experts:

$$\theta_{\text{merged}} = \frac{1}{N+1} \sum_{i=1}^{N+1} \theta_i$$

where `$\theta_i$` represents the parameter vector for a particular non-FF module from expert `$i$`.

**Why average rather than selecting one expert's parameters:** The assumption is that self-attention layers learn domain-agnostic features like syntactic structure, discourse relations, and general reasoning patterns that transfer across domains. Averaging across experts is a form of ensembling that should preserve the shared structure while reducing noise from any individual expert's domain-specific drift. If the assumption is correct (and the downstream finetuning results support it — the averaged parameters perform well after finetuning), then averaging provides a sensible initialization. If the assumption is wrong, the finetuning stage should be able to adjust the averaged parameters toward better values.

**Why average rather than keep expert-specific attention:** Keeping separate attention parameters per expert would mean the model would need to route attention as well as feedforward — a significantly more complex architecture with higher inference cost (attention is already quadratic in sequence length, so multiplying it by expert count would be prohibitive). The paper's design choice keeps routing decisions confined to the feedforward sublayer, where MoE is well-established and the computational savings from sparsity are most significant.

##### New Parameters Introduced

The only new parameters in the entire BTX architecture are the router weights `$W_l$` at each of the `$L$` layers. Each `$W_l$` is a matrix of dimension `$(N+1) \times d_{\text{model}}$`. For `$N+1 = 4$` experts and `$d_{\text{model}} = 4096$` (Llama-2 7B's hidden dimension), each router has `$4 \times 4096 = 16,384$` parameters. Across 32 layers, this totals approximately 524,000 new parameters — negligible compared to the model's 7 billion existing parameters (less than 0.01% overhead).

The paper emphasizes this point:

> "Note that the only new parameters we introduce are the router's transformation parameters `$W_l$`, which are negligible in size compared to the rest of the network." (Section 3.2)

This minimal parameter increase means the MoE model has essentially the same memory footprint as the seed model plus some routing overhead, despite conceptually containing four full copies of the feedforward layers (increasing the *total* parameter count substantially, though only a subset is active per token).

##### Initialization of the MoE Model Before Finetuning

Before any finetuning begins, the structural merge produces a model with:
- MoE feedforward layers containing all four experts' FF parameters (initialized from the independently-trained experts, not randomly)
- Averaged self-attention and embedding parameters (averaged across all four experts)
- Randomly initialized router weights `$W_l$`

At this point, the model is not expected to perform well — the router has never been trained, so it makes essentially random expert selections, and the averaged attention parameters have not been optimized for this new architecture. The entire point of the finetuning stage is to make these components work together.

---

#### MoE Routing and Load Balancing

The routing function `$g$` in the BTX MoE layer determines which experts process each token. The paper experiments with several routing variants, but the default (and best-performing) is **Top-2 routing with load balancing**.

##### Top-K Routing

For Top-K routing with `$K=2$`, the routing function operates as follows:

$$g(W_l x) = \text{SoftMax}(\text{TopK}(W_l x))$$

where the TopK operation does:
1. Compute `$s = W_l x \in \mathbb{R}^{N+1}$` — the raw logits for each expert
2. Identify the indices of the `$K$` largest values in `$s$`
3. Set all other values to `$-\infty$` (or a very large negative number)
4. Apply SoftMax to the resulting vector

The SoftMax after TopK ensures that the `$K$` selected experts receive normalized probability weights that sum to 1, while all non-selected experts receive zero weight.

**What this computes operationally:** For each token at each MoE layer, the router selects exactly 2 experts to activate and assigns them normalized weights. Only those 2 experts' `$\text{FF}_i^l(x)$` computations are performed; the remaining `$N-1$` experts are skipped entirely, saving compute. The output is a convex combination of the 2 selected experts' FF outputs.

**Why Top-2 rather than Top-1 or Top-All:** Top-1 (Switch routing) provides maximum sparsity but may not give the router enough flexibility — a single expert may not be optimal for all aspects of a token's computation. Top-All (soft routing) provides maximum expressiveness but eliminates the computational efficiency of MoE, since all experts must be computed for every token. Top-2 is the established sweet spot in the MoE literature (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2024) and the paper's ablation in Table 4 confirms it provides the best performance-efficiency tradeoff for BTX.

##### Load Balancing Loss

A well-known failure mode in MoE training is the **dead expert problem**: the router learns to always select a subset of experts, leaving others with zero training signal (if an expert is never selected, its gradients are zero, so it never improves, so it continues to never be selected). The paper uses an auxiliary load balancing loss adopted from Fedus et al. (2022):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{LB}} = \alpha N \sum_{i=1}^{N} u_i p_i$$

where:
- `$\alpha$` is a hyperparameter controlling the strength of the load balancing penalty (set to 0.01 in default BTX)
- `$N$` is the number of experts (4 in the default setup)
- `$u_i = \frac{1}{|B|} \sum_{x \in B} g_i(W_l x)$` is the **fraction of tokens in the current batch `$B$` that are routed to expert `$i$`**, computed as the empirical average of the router's weight for expert `$i$` across all tokens in the batch
- `$p_i = \frac{1}{|B|} \sum_{x \in B} \text{SoftMax}_i(W_l x)$` is the **fraction of the router's probability mass assigned to expert `$i$`**, computed as the empirical average of the softmax probability (before TopK truncation) across all tokens in the batch

**What this loss computes:** The product `$u_i p_i$` is minimized when the routing distribution is uniform — i.e., each expert receives an equal share of tokens and an equal share of probability mass. The sum `$\sum_{i=1}^N u_i p_i$` is a common measure of concentration: it equals `$1/N$` when the distribution is perfectly uniform and approaches 1 when one expert dominates. Multiplying by `$\alpha N$` scales the loss so that the penalty is on a comparable scale regardless of the number of experts. This loss is computed independently at each MoE layer and added to the main negative log-likelihood loss.

**Why this form:** The product form `$u_i p_i$` simultaneously penalizes two related failure modes: (1) an expert that receives many tokens but with low routing probability (the router is uncertain but the expert is used heavily) and (2) an expert that receives high routing probability but few tokens (the router wants to use the expert but truncation prevents it). Alternatives like simply penalizing the variance of `$u_i$` alone would not capture router confidence, and alternatives like penalizing the entropy of `$p_i$` alone would not capture actual token allocation.

**Effect of load balancing (Figure 3, Table 5):** The paper provides detailed analysis of how load balancing changes routing behavior:
- **Without load balancing:** The Math expert dominates routing across almost all tasks and domains. In particular, the Code expert becomes a "dead expert" — the routing probability to the Code expert shifts to zero across most layers (Appendix C, Figure 5, bottom three panels). The aggregated result (Table 5) shows that without load balancing, HumanEval performance drops from 27.4 to 19.5 (a 7.9-point decline), while GSM8K improves from 29.8 to 34.6 (a 4.8-point gain). This is a direct tradeoff: without load balancing, the router greedily uses the Math expert which is good enough for many tasks, but this starves the Code expert of training signal.
- **With load balancing (α=0.01):** The routing distribution becomes more uniform across experts (Figure 3, top panel). The Code expert "comes back to life" and becomes the dominant expert for code domain tasks. The Math expert remains important for math tasks but no longer monopolizes routing for other domains. Performance becomes more balanced across domains: HumanEval recovers to 27.4, while GSM8K settles at 29.8 (versus 34.6 without LB).

##### Alternative Routing Methods

The paper compares three alternatives to the default Top-2 with load balancing (Section 3.3, Table 4):

**Switch Routing (Top-1 with capacity factor):** This is the routing method from Fedus et al. (2022). Only the single highest-scoring expert is selected per token (`$K=1$`), but a capacity factor limits how many tokens can be routed to each expert. If an expert exceeds its capacity (set to 1.5 in the paper — meaning each expert can process up to 1.5× its "fair share" of tokens), excess tokens are dropped (their FF computation is skipped entirely, passing only the residual connection).

The paper reports that Switch routing achieves substantially lower performance than Top-2 (Table 4: average score 24.7 vs. 34.6 at 10B finetuning tokens). The capacity factor dropping mechanism, while efficient, appears to lose too much information during training for BTX's continued pretraining setting.

**Soft Routing:** The routing function is simply SoftMax without any Top-K truncation: `$g(W_l x) = \text{SoftMax}(W_l x)$`. All experts are activated for every token during both training and inference. This provides the best performance (35.8 average score at 10B tokens, 37.3 at 40B — the highest among all routing variants) but eliminates the computational efficiency of MoE entirely, since all FF experts must be computed for every token. The paper includes this as an upper bound on what learnable routing could achieve with unlimited compute, not as a practical recommendation.

**Sample Top-1:** During training, this method uses the Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization (Jang et al., 2016) to produce a differentiable sample from the routing distribution. Specifically:
- Compute `$s = W_l x$` (logits)
- Add Gumbel noise: `$\tilde{s}_i = s_i + \text{Gumbel}(0, 1)$`
- Apply SoftMax with temperature `$\tau$`: `$g_i = \text{SoftMax}_i(\tilde{s} / \tau)$`
- Zero out all but the largest value (hard sample), but use the continuous SoftMax values for the backward pass (straight-through estimator)

At inference time, simple hard sampling is used (no Gumbel noise, argmax). The temperature `$\tau$` is annealed during training according to the schedule `$\tau = \max(0.5, -r t)$` with `$r = 1 \times 10^{-4}$`, where `$t$` is the training step. The initial temperature of 0.5 encourages exploration (the router samples different experts stochastically), and the linear annealing sharpens the distribution toward deterministic selection as training progresses.

Sample Top-1 uses only 6.7B active parameters (vs. 11.1B for Top-2) because it activates only one expert per token, making it more computationally efficient than Top-2 at both training and inference. Performance is competitive: 33.0 at 10B tokens and 36.9 at 160B tokens (Table 4), approaching Top-2 at higher finetuning budgets while using fewer FLOPs per token. For the first layer only, soft routing is used instead of Sample Top-1 (likely to provide a more stable token representation for the subsequent layers' routing decisions).

#### MoE Finetuning: Making Independently-Trained Parts Work Together

After the structural merge, the BTX model must be finetuned so the router learns which experts to select under what conditions, and the averaged attention parameters adjust to work with the new MoE feedforward layers. The paper treats this finetuning stage as a critical integration step, not merely a brief calibration.

**Training data (Section 4.1.1, Appendix A):** The finetuning data is a mixture of all data sources used to train the individual experts, plus the original Llama-2 pretraining data (for the generalist expert). The sampling ratios across datasets are:
- Math expert data: 30.16% of tokens
- Code expert data: 40.31% of tokens
- Wikipedia expert data: 10.30% of tokens
- Original Llama-2 pretraining data: 19.23% of tokens

This mixture ensures the router sees tokens from all domains during finetuning, which is essential — if the router only saw math tokens, it would never learn when to use the code or Wikipedia experts.

**Training volume:** The paper trains the default BTX model for 80B tokens during the MoE finetuning stage. This is substantially less than the expert training phase (201B + 210B + 42B = 453B tokens total across experts), but still a significant continued training run. The ablation in Table 4 shows that performance improves monotonically with more finetuning tokens: Top-2 achieves 34.6 at 10B tokens, 35.9 at 40B tokens, and 37.3 at 80B tokens (on the representative task subset average).

**Optimization hyperparameters:** The same optimization configuration used for expert training is used for MoE finetuning: AdamW with weight decay 0.1, peak learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-4}$` with 100-step warmup and cosine decay to 10% of peak, batch size 4M tokens with sequence length 4096. The router weights `$W_l$` are trained from scratch (random initialization) using this same optimizer configuration.

**What is being optimized during finetuning:** The finetuning loss is the standard negative log-likelihood (NLL) language modeling loss plus the load balancing auxiliary loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = -\frac{1}{|B|} \sum_{x \in B} \log p_\theta(\text{next_token} \mid x) + \mathcal{L}_{\text{LB}}$$

All parameters of the model — including the expert FF parameters, the averaged self-attention and embedding parameters, and the router weights — are trainable during this stage (unless frozen in ablation experiments). The paper does not specify whether different parameter groups use different learning rates, so the default assumption is that all parameters share the same optimizer configuration.

**Freezing expert FF parameters (Table 5 ablation):** The paper tests whether the domain experts' feedforward parameters need further training during the MoE finetuning stage. When expert FF modules are frozen and only the router and averaged parameters are trained, performance is essentially unchanged:

> "Freezing the feedforward modules initialized from each expert, and only training the rest of the MoE model has little impact on performance across all tasks." (Section 4.3.1)

The results in Table 5 show: with load balancing and unfrozen experts, the average score is 34.7; freezing the experts (no LB & freeze experts) yields 34.7 — identical within rounding. This suggests that the domain experts already acquired sufficient domain knowledge during the Branch-Train stage, and the MoE finetuning stage primarily trains the router and the averaged self-attention/embedding parameters to work with the expert FF modules. This is an important practical finding: if expert parameters can be frozen, the finetuning stage requires less GPU memory (no optimizer states for expert parameters) and could potentially train faster.

##### Training Throughput and Compute Allocation

The paper carefully tracks the relationship between training throughput and the allocation of compute between stages. The key comparison is between BTX and sparse upcycling (Table 3):

**BTX compute allocation:** 23% of total training compute is spent on MoE finetuning, and 77% is spent on embarrassingly parallel expert training. Total compute: 926.1 GPU-days, yielding 533B total training tokens (453B from expert training + 80B from MoE finetuning).

**Sparse upcycling compute allocation (compute-matched):** 100% of compute is spent on synchronized MoE training (no expert training phase). Total compute: 1007.1 GPU-days (slightly more than BTX), yielding only 252B total training tokens.

**Why the token count differs:** The embarrassingly parallel expert training in BTX achieves higher throughput per GPU-day because there is no communication overhead and no load balancing computation. So BTX processes more than 2× the number of tokens (533B vs. 252B) in slightly less wall-clock time (926.1 vs. 1007.1 GPU-days). This is the central efficiency argument: the asynchronous phase allows BTX to train on more data within the same compute budget, and that additional data translates to slightly better average performance (47.9 vs. 47.3).

The paper is explicit that this comparison is compute-matching (CM), not data-matching (DM). The DM sparse upcycling baseline (Table 2) trains on the same 533B tokens as BTX but requires more GPU-days because all training is synchronized MoE. This isolates the throughput advantage: given the same data, BTX finishes faster; given the same compute budget, BTX processes more data.

#### Variations: Splitting and Blending Experts

The paper explores two alternative strategies for configuring the MoE experts beyond the default one-to-one mapping from domains to experts.

##### Splitting Experts

The default BTX has `$N+1 = 4$` experts in the MoE layer (one per domain plus the generalist). The paper explores increasing the number of experts without training additional domain models.

**Procedure:** Each domain expert's feedforward sublayer is split into `$C$` chunks along the hidden dimension. If the original FF activation size is `$d_{\text{FF}}$`, each chunk has dimension `$d_{\text{FF}} / C$`. These chunks become separate experts in the MoE layer. With `$N+1 = 4$` original experts and `$C = 2$` splits, the MoE layer contains `$(N+1) \times C = 8$` modules.

**Why this might help:** More fine-grained experts allow more flexible routing — instead of always routing to the entire Math expert, the router could route to half of the Math expert and half of the Code expert for a task that requires both capabilities. Fine-grained experts have been shown beneficial in other MoE work (Dai et al., 2024).

**Results (Table 5):** Split experts with Top-2 routing (of 8) performs poorly: average score 28.0, substantially below the default 4-expert Top-2 score of 34.7. Split experts with Top-4 routing (of 8) — which matches the active parameter count of the default Top-2 model since each split expert has half the FF dimension — recovers to 34.5, essentially matching but not improving upon the default. The paper does not explore `$C > 2$` or other split configurations. The practical implication is that the default coarse-grained domain experts are already an effective granularity for this setup.

##### Blending Experts

The default BTX maps each domain expert's full FF layers to a single MoE expert. The blending variation breaks this one-to-one domain-to-expert mapping.

**Procedure:** For each domain expert, split its FF layers into `$N+1$` chunks (as in splitting, with `$C = N+1$` chunks). Then, build the `$n$`-th MoE expert by taking the `$n$`-th chunk from *all* domain experts and concatenating them. Result: each MoE expert contains equal representation from every domain.

**Motivation:** The paper cites the observation from Jiang et al. (2024) that standard MoE training does not produce domain-specialized experts — experts activate roughly uniformly across domains. The blending variation attempts to replicate this property, hypothesizing that breaking domain specialization might allow the router more flexibility.

**Results (Table 5):** Blending experts performs drastically worse than the default: average score 22.2, a 12.5-point drop from the default's 34.7. Performance collapses across all domains (GSM8K: 13.9 vs. 29.8, HumanEval: 17.1 vs. 27.4, Natural Questions: 9.9 vs. 23.0, ARC Challenge: 34.1 vs. 43.4, MMLU: 36.2 vs. 50.0). The paper concludes:

> "The performance across all tasks dropped when experts are mixed, suggesting that domain FF layers cannot be mixed in this way." (Section 4.3.1)

**Why blending fails:** The domain experts were trained on completely different data distributions for extended periods (200B+ tokens each). Their FF parameters likely encode domain-specific representations that are not simply additive — averaging chunks from different experts' corresponding FF dimensions may produce incoherent transformations that the router cannot effectively utilize. The failure of blending actually validates the core BTX premise: domain specialization persists in the FF parameters, and the router benefits from experts that are genuinely domain-specialized rather than homogenized.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Two-Stage Sequential Allocation of Compute During Continued Pretraining Is a Pareto-Improving Strategy

The most fundamental conceptual contribution of this paper is not the MoE architecture, nor the embarrassingly parallel training, nor even the idea of combining them. It is the **diagnostic insight that the compute budget for continued pretraining can be split into two qualitatively different stages — asynchronous specialization followed by synchronized integration — and that this split Pareto-dominates allocating all compute to either stage alone**.

Before BTX, the field operated with an implicit assumption that training specialized capabilities into an LLM required a single, unified training process. You either trained a dense model on all domains simultaneously (the standard continued pretraining approach), trained an MoE model from scratch with synchronized routing (sparse upcycling), or trained separate experts that could never be reintegrated (BTM). Each approach made a binary choice: synchronized or asynchronous, unified or fragmented, one training phase or many.

The paper demonstrates that this binary choice is false. The key move is recognizing that the two stages optimize for different objectives that are **complementary rather than competing**:

- **The asynchronous expert training stage (Branch & Train)** optimizes for *training throughput and freedom from interference*. Each expert trains without communication overhead, without gradient conflicts from other domains, and without the router's decisions constraining which expert receives gradient signal. This stage maximizes the quality of each domain specialist by allowing it to fully adapt to its target distribution without compromise. The paper shows this stage consumes 77% of total compute but processes over 453B tokens of domain-specific data — token counts that would be substantially lower if the same compute were spent on synchronized MoE training due to communication overhead.

- **The synchronized MoE finetuning stage (MiX)** optimizes for *integration and learnable composition*. The router learns token-level decisions about which expert to consult, and the averaged attention parameters adjust to work with the new FF modules. Critically, this stage is relatively cheap — 23% of total compute, 80B tokens — yet it transforms a collection of independent models into a unified architecture that can be further finetuned with SFT or RLHF.

The **diagnostic evidence** for this complementarity comes from the paper's factorial comparison design. By comparing BTX against its two degenerate cases — BTM (100% async expert training, 0% MoE finetuning) and sparse upcycling (0% async expert training, 100% MoE finetuning) — the paper isolates the marginal value of each stage:

- **BTM (Table 2):** 43.4 average score. This is the ceiling of what asynchronous expert training alone can achieve — good domain specialization but coarse routing (document-level tf-idf classification rather than per-token learned selection) and no unified architecture for further finetuning. The gap between BTX (47.9) and BTM (43.4) — approximately 4.5 points — represents the marginal value of MoE integration with learned routing.

- **Sparse upcycling, data-matched (Table 2):** 46.3 average score. This is the ceiling of what synchronized MoE training alone can achieve on the same data volume. The gap between BTX (47.9) and this baseline (46.3) — approximately 1.6 points — represents the marginal value of the asynchronous expert training phase *beyond* just having MoE architecture.

- **Sparse upcycling, compute-matched (Table 3):** 47.3 average score versus BTX's 47.9. This is the most telling comparison because it holds the compute budget constant. BTX processes 2× more tokens (533B vs. 252B) in slightly fewer GPU-days (926.1 vs. 1007.1), demonstrating that the asynchronous stage's throughput advantage is not merely a convenience — it translates to measurable performance gains at equal cost.

This two-stage decomposition is not a small refinement of an existing approach. It is a **fundamental conceptual reframing of how to think about compute allocation during continued pretraining**, directly parallel to the Chinchilla scaling laws' reframing of pretraining compute allocation (Hoffmann et al., 2022). Just as Chinchilla established that pretraining compute should be split between model size and data quantity in a specific ratio, BTX establishes that continued pretraining compute should be split between asynchronous specialization and synchronized integration — and that the optimal split is not 100/0 or 0/100.

A subtle but important aspect of this insight: it implies that **the late stages of LLM training have fundamentally different computational properties than the early stages**. The seed model (Llama-2 7B) was trained synchronously for 2T tokens because at that stage, the model was learning language fundamentals that required global coordination across all data distributions. But once those fundamentals are in place, adding specialized capabilities becomes a problem that admits asynchronous parallelization. The paper does not explicitly make this argument, but the empirical results support it: BTX outperforms Llama-2 13B (Table 2, 47.9 vs. 45.4) while using less than half the additional training compute, suggesting that the standard approach of continuing synchronous training with more parameters is a misallocation of compute for the skill acquisition phase.

### Innovation 2: The Assumption That Feedforward Layers Encode Domain Specialization While Self-Attention Encodes Domain-General Capabilities — and Its Empirical Validation

BTX's structural merge strategy rests on a specific architectural hypothesis: **that the Transformer's feedforward sublayers are the primary locus of domain specialization, while self-attention layers learn domain-agnostic features that transfer across domains**. This hypothesis is not new in absolute terms — it echoes findings from the interpretability literature that feedforward layers in Transformers often act as key-value memories storing factual and procedural knowledge (Geva et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2022). However, the paper makes this hypothesis **operationally actionable in a training methodology** and provides empirical evidence that validates it in the context of domain specialization.

What makes this an innovation is not the hypothesis itself but **the demonstration that it holds under aggressive domain-specific continued training** and that it can be exploited for efficient model merging. Prior work had shown that MoE layers replacing feedforward sublayers work well when trained from scratch (Fedus et al., 2022), and that simple parameter averaging can produce effective merged models (Wortsman et al., 2022). But no prior work had tested whether independently-trained domain experts — each shifted from the seed model by 200B+ tokens of domain-specific training — could be structurally merged by treating FF layers as expert-specific while simply averaging attention layers, and then finetuned into a performant unified model.

The evidence for this hypothesis comes from several sources in the paper:

**The success of the merge itself (Table 2, main results).** If self-attention layers had diverged significantly across experts (encoding domain-specific attention patterns rather than general syntactic and semantic structure), simply averaging them would produce a degraded initialization that even 80B tokens of finetuning could not fully recover from. The fact that BTX achieves strong performance across all domains — matching or approaching domain experts on their specialties while retaining general capabilities — suggests the averaged attention parameters remain functional for all domains after finetuning.

**The experts-are-sufficiently-trained finding (Table 5, "freeze experts" ablation).** When expert feedforward parameters are frozen during MoE finetuning and only the router and attention parameters are trained, performance is essentially unchanged (34.7 vs. 34.7 average score at 10B finetuning tokens). This tells us two things: (1) the FF parameters from the Branch-Train stage already encode high-quality domain-specific transformations that do not need further adjustment, and (2) the finetuning stage's primary job is to train the router and adjust the averaged attention parameters — precisely the components that were not domain-specialized.

**The failure of expert blending (Table 5).** When domain-specific FF chunks are mixed to create experts that each contain equal representation from all domains, performance collapses (22.2 vs. 34.7 average). This demonstrates that the domain specialization encoded in the FF parameters is real and fragile — breaking the one-to-one correspondence between experts and domains destroys the meaningful specialization that the router relies on. If FF parameters were truly domain-agnostic, blending them would not matter. The fact that blending devastates performance provides strong negative evidence that FF layers genuinely encode domain-specific computation.

**The routing analysis (Figure 3, Figure 6).** The router's learned behavior after finetuning shows partial but not complete domain alignment: the Code expert dominates code tasks, the Wikipedia expert dominates knowledge tasks, and the Math expert is important for MATH (college-level math) but less so for GSM8K (grade-school math, where Code and Llama-2 experts are preferred). This partial specialization — where the router learns to use domain experts for their intended domains but also routes to other experts when appropriate — is exactly what the hypothesis predicts should be possible with learned routing on top of genuinely specialized FF experts.

This insight has practical implications beyond BTX. It suggests that **future architectures for multi-domain models could be designed with this separation in mind from the start**: train domain-specific FF experts asynchronously, share attention parameters across domains with periodic averaging or synchronized updates, and use lightweight finetuning to integrate. It also implies that **forgetting in continued pretraining may be primarily a feedforward phenomenon** — the self-attention layers may remain relatively stable under domain shift, and catastrophic forgetting arises because FF layers overwrite general-purpose knowledge with domain-specific patterns. If true, this would suggest new approaches to continual learning that protect FF parameters from being overwritten rather than trying to protect all parameters uniformly.

This is best characterized as a **diagnostic advance with architectural implications** rather than a fundamental theoretical breakthrough. The paper does not prove a causal relationship between FF layers and domain specialization; it demonstrates a correlation that holds in the specific setting of Llama-2 continued pretraining on three domains. But the convergence of positive evidence (the merge works), negative evidence (blending fails), and ablation evidence (freezing FF doesn't hurt) makes a compelling case that the hypothesis is practically reliable for this class of models and domains.

### Innovation 3: Domain-Specialized Experts as an Alternative to Naturally-Emergent Specialization in MoE Training

A subtle but important finding in this paper challenges an emerging narrative in the MoE literature. Recent work, notably Jiang et al. (2024) with Mixtral, demonstrated that MoE experts trained from scratch in a standard synchronized manner **do not naturally specialize to domains** — they activate roughly uniformly across different types of input, suggesting that standard MoE training produces a form of distributed computation rather than modular domain specialists. This finding has led to a growing assumption that domain specialization in MoEs is either unnecessary or impossible to achieve through standard training.

BTX challenges this assumption not by arguing that standard MoE training *should* produce domain specialists, but by demonstrating that **explicitly constructed domain specialization — achieved through the Branch & Train stage — provides measurable benefits over the uniform-expert baseline**. The evidence comes from two directions:

**First, the failure of blending confirms that enforced specialization matters.** The blending experiment (Table 5) was explicitly motivated by the observation that standard MoE experts are not domain-specialized. If domain specialization were irrelevant — if the router simply needs more capacity regardless of how it's partitioned — then blending experts from different domains should not hurt performance. After all, each blended expert has the same total capacity as the original domain experts, just distributed differently. The catastrophic 12.5-point drop from blending demonstrates that the router *uses* domain specialization, and that removing it (by homogenizing experts) eliminates the information the router needs to make effective per-token routing decisions.

**Second, the routing analysis (Figure 3, Section 4.3.2) shows that domain specialization partially persists after MoE finetuning.** The router does not simply ignore the domain boundaries and route uniformly. Instead, the Code expert becomes dominant for code tasks, the Wikipedia expert for knowledge tasks, and the Math expert for college-level math. This is not an all-or-nothing specialization — the router frequently routes to non-domain experts (e.g., Code expert on GSM8K problems, Llama-2 expert widely across tasks) — but the domain alignment is clearly present and contributes to the model's ability to match specialized models on their target domains.

The implication for the MoE field is significant: **there may be value in training experts with explicit domain specialization, even if standard MoE training does not naturally produce it**. The paper does not claim that domain specialization is always superior to uniform experts — it does not compare BTX against a version where experts are initialized uniformly and trained in the same two-stage setup. But it provides evidence that, given a fixed architecture and training budget, experts that begin the integration phase already specialized to domains produce better final performance than experts that are artificially homogenized.

This is best understood as a **corrective finding** that adds nuance to the "MoE experts don't specialize" narrative. It suggests that the lack of specialization in standard MoE training may be a property of the synchronized, from-scratch training procedure rather than a fundamental property of MoE architectures. When experts are explicitly trained on domain-specific data in an asynchronous phase (which standard MoE training cannot do), they *do* develop specialization, and that specialization *does* contribute to downstream performance when integrated via learned routing.

The innovation is not in the concept of domain specialization itself — that idea is as old as the Branch-Train-Merge method. It is in the demonstration that **domain specialization can coexist with, and benefit from, the dynamic token-level routing that MoE provides**, creating a hybrid where experts are partially specialized (due to their training data) and partially shared (due to the router learning to mix them across domains). This partial specialization may be the sweet spot: enough structure to give the router meaningful choices between experts with different capabilities, but not so rigid that the router cannot route a code token to the math expert when appropriate (as in the GSM8K case).

### Innovation 4: The Throughput Advantage of Asynchronous Training Is Quantitative, Not Just Qualitative

Many papers claim that their asynchronous training approach improves throughput — this is almost definitional when removing synchronization. What makes BTX's treatment of throughput distinctive is the **attempt to quantify it in a way that enables apples-to-apples comparison with synchronized methods**, and the demonstration that the throughput advantage translates to genuine performance gains rather than just wall-clock convenience.

The paper does this through the compute-matched comparison in Table 3. The comparison is carefully constructed: BTX and sparse upcycling both start from the same seed model, both use MoE architecture, both train on the same data mixture during the MoE phase, and both are allocated approximately the same total GPU-days (926.1 vs. 1007.1). The only difference is how those GPU-days are spent: BTX spends 77% on asynchronous expert training and 23% on MoE finetuning; sparse upcycling spends 100% on MoE training.

The result: BTX processes 533B total tokens vs. sparse upcycling's 252B — a 2.1× token advantage — and achieves a slightly higher average score (47.9 vs. 47.3). The modest performance difference (0.6 points) might seem underwhelming, but the right interpretation is that BTX achieves **equivalent or slightly better performance with dramatically more training data processed per GPU-day**. This means that in a fixed-time budget scenario, BTX would finish training substantially sooner, and in a fixed-compute budget scenario, BTX produces a model trained on more data.

What elevates this from a simple "our method is faster" claim to an innovation is the **connection to the broader scaling laws conversation**. If continued pretraining on additional tokens continues to improve performance (as scaling laws suggest it should), then a method that increases token throughput by 2× at equal compute is genuinely more *compute-efficient*, not just more *time-efficient*. The paper does not fully explore this connection — it does not train sparse upcycling for enough GPU-days to match the token count and see if performance matches BTX — but the framework it establishes (measuring throughput in tokens-per-GPU-day, comparing at equal total compute) is the right one for integrating asynchronous training into the scaling laws discourse.

The practical implication is that **asynchronous training should be treated as a first-order efficiency lever, not just a distributed systems convenience**. Prior work on reducing communication in training (Zhang et al., 2015; Douillard et al., 2023) has often been evaluated on throughput metrics alone, without connecting throughput to downstream model quality. BTX shows that when you take the communication savings and reinvest them into more training tokens (by keeping the GPU-day budget fixed), you get better models. This reframes asynchronous training from a "how to train faster" discussion to a "how to train better on a fixed budget" discussion, which is the more economically relevant framing for most organizations.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The experiments use a custom assembled collection of domain-specific datasets, not a single pre-packaged benchmark. For the expert training stage, each domain expert is trained on a different data mixture: the Math expert uses the Llemma data mixture (Azerbayev et al., 2023), comprising AlgebraicStack, OpenWebMath, Arxiv, Github, and Commoncrawl (exact sampling ratios in Appendix A, Table 7), totaling 201B tokens over 48k steps; the Code expert uses the CodeLlama data mixture (Rozière et al., 2023), comprising code, natural language related to code, and general natural language, totaling 210B tokens over 50k steps; the Wikipedia expert uses Wikipedia documents from June–August 2022 plus 9.09% Commoncrawl, totaling 42B tokens. During MoE finetuning, the model trains on a mixture of all domain-specific data plus the original Llama-2 pretraining data, with sampling ratios of 30.16% (math), 40.31% (code), 10.30% (Wikipedia), and 19.23% (Llama-2 pretraining data). **For evaluation**, the paper uses standard downstream benchmarks: GSM8K (8-shot) and MATH (4-shot) for math; HumanEval (0-shot) and MBPP (3-shot) for code; Natural Questions (5-shot) and TriviaQA (5-shot) for world knowledge; ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, SIQA, PIQA, and WinoGrande (all 0-shot) for commonsense reasoning; and MMLU (5-shot) for general knowledge. All evaluations use greedy decoding and the same metrics as Touvron et al. (2023) and Rozière et al. (2023): pass@1 for code tasks, exact match for math and knowledge tasks, and accuracy for MMLU and ARC (Appendix B).

- **Base model.** All experiments use Llama-2 7B (Touvron et al., 2023) as the seed model. The choice is motivated by Llama-2 being a well-established open-weight generalist LLM with publicly reported training details and benchmark performance, providing a reproducible baseline. The paper also compares against Llama-2 13B to assess whether BTX can outperform a larger model trained with the standard synchronous protocol.

- **Metrics.** The primary metrics are per-task accuracy scores on the evaluation benchmarks listed above, reported individually in Table 8 (Appendix B) and aggregated into domain averages in Table 2. The paper also reports an **average score** across all tasks (Table 2, Figure 2 left), computed as the mean of the five domain averages (Math, Code, Knowledge, Reasoning, MMLU). Training efficiency is measured in **GPU-days** and **total training tokens processed**, with comparisons made at equal GPU-day budgets (compute-matching) and equal data volumes (data-matching).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against six baselines, spanning generalist models, specialized models, and alternative training methodologies:
  - **Llama-2 7B** (Touvron et al., 2023): The seed model, providing the lower bound before any continued pretraining.
  - **Llama-2 13B** (Touvron et al., 2023): A larger generalist model, used to assess whether BTX's test-time compute efficiency and training efficiency can match or exceed scaling model size.
  - **Dense continued pretraining (data-matching, DM):** Instead of training separate experts, continues training the seed Llama-2 7B on all domain-specific data combined (same data as used in the expert training stage), followed by training on the same MoE-finetuning data mixture. This isolates the value of the MoE architecture and async training: if dense training on the same data achieves similar performance, BTX's complexity is unnecessary.
  - **Sparse upcycling (data-matching, DM)** (Komatsuzaki et al., 2022): Initializes an MoE model from the seed model by replicating each feedforward layer 4 times (creating 4 identical experts), adds a randomly initialized Top-2 router, and trains on the same data as BTX's expert training + MoE finetuning stages combined. This is equivalent to BTX without the asynchronous expert training phase.
  - **Sparse upcycling (compute-matching, CM):** Same procedure as DM sparse upcycling, but trained for the same total GPU-days as BTX (not the same number of tokens). Because sparse upcycling trains entirely in synchronized MoE mode, its throughput is lower, so it processes fewer tokens in the same compute budget. This isolates the throughput advantage of BTX's asynchronous phase.
  - **Branch-Train-Merge (BTM)** (Li et al., 2022a; Gururangan et al., 2023): Uses the same domain experts as BTX (math, code, Wikipedia, plus the seed model), but does no MoE finetuning. At inference time, the input is classified into domains using tf-idf similarity between the input text and each expert's training data; Top-K experts are selected and their output token probability distributions are averaged. The paper tests both Top-1 and Top-2 BTM. Since BTM has no finetuning stage, it is trained on less total data than BTX.
  - **CodeLlama 7B** (Rozière et al., 2023): A publicly available code-specialized LLM, also derived from Llama-2 7B via continued pretraining on code data.
  - **Llemma 7B** (Azerbayev et al., 2023): A publicly available math-specialized LLM, derived from CodeLlama 7B via continued pretraining on math data.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Training compute is measured in **GPU-days** (the product of number of GPUs and training duration in days). The paper reports GPU-days for all methods that start from Llama-2 7B (Figure 2 left, X-axis; Table 3). For Llama-2 13B, GPU-days are approximated by doubling the training compute of a 7B model trained on the same amount of pretraining data, following the scaling relationship in Touvron et al. (2023, Table 2). Two comparison regimes are used:
  - **Data-matching (DM):** Models are trained on exactly the same data mixture and volume (same number of tokens), enabling comparison of how effectively different methods use the same information.
  - **Compute-matching (CM):** Models are trained for the same total GPU-days, enabling comparison of training efficiency — which method produces a better model given a fixed hardware budget.
  - The paper also reports **active parameters at inference time** (Table 2, Figure 2 left, indicated by circle size), distinguishing between total parameters (which can be much larger due to MoE) and active parameters (which determine inference FLOPs). For BTX Top-2, active parameters are 11.1B (since 2 of 4 experts are active per token, each FF is ~3.5B parameters); for BTX Sample Top-1, active parameters are 6.7B (1 expert active).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report any cross-validation, statistical significance testing, or confidence intervals on benchmark scores. All evaluations are reported as single accuracy values on the standard test sets of each benchmark. The method for selecting hyperparameters (routing method, load balancing coefficient α, number of finetuning tokens, etc.) is not described as a systematic sweep with held-out validation; rather, the paper reports ablations (Table 4, Table 5) that compare variants after 10B tokens of MoE finetuning on a subset of representative tasks (GSM8K, HumanEval, Natural Questions, ARC Challenge, MMLU) to select the default configuration. This is a meaningful limitation: the reported comparisons may be subject to variance from training stochasticity, and the lack of error bars makes it difficult to assess whether small performance differences (e.g., BTX 47.9 vs. sparse upcycling CM 47.3) are statistically reliable.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Domain Expert Training Outcomes (Table 1)

The Branch & Train stage produces domain experts that dramatically improve on their target domains while regressing on others, establishing the need for the integration stage:

- **Math expert vs. seed model:** GSM8K improves from 14.7 to 39.5 (+24.8 points), MATH from 2.5 to 18.8 (+16.3 points). Interestingly, code performance also improves — HumanEval from 12.8 to 25.0 (+12.2), MBPP from 20.8 to 33.6 (+12.8) — suggesting transfer from math to code. However, world knowledge collapses: TriviaQA drops from 58.5 to 37.1 (−21.4), Natural Questions from 16.4 to 14.4 (−2.0), and MMLU drops from 46.1 to 52.0 (a 5.9-point gain, actually, which the paper notes may be because MMLU contains many math subjects).
- **Code expert vs. seed model:** HumanEval improves from 12.8 to 31.7 (+18.9), MBPP from 20.8 to 40.2 (+19.4). But TriviaQA drops from 58.5 to 29.9 (−28.6), MMLU from 46.1 to 39.6 (−6.5), and knowledge tasks generally degrade.
- **Wikipedia expert vs. seed model:** Modest knowledge improvements (Natural Questions 16.4 → 21.8, TriviaQA holds at 57.2), but math and code tasks regress.

The catastrophic forgetting on non-specialist domains demonstrates that single-domain continued pretraining is insufficient for building a generalist model, directly motivating BTX's MiX stage.

#### Overall Performance Comparison (Table 2, Figure 2)

Table 2 reports aggregated domain scores and an overall average for all methods. The key comparisons:

**BTX vs. seed model (Llama-2 7B):** BTX (Top-2) achieves an average score of 47.9 compared to the seed model's 40.7, a 7.2-point improvement. Domain-level gains are concentrated where experts were trained: Math improves from 8.6 to 27.4 (+18.8), Code from 16.8 to 34.0 (+17.2), Knowledge from 37.4 to 41.0 (+3.6). Reasoning remains essentially unchanged (63.3 → 63.5), and MMLU improves from 46.1 to 52.5 (+6.4). BTX Sample Top-1 is slightly lower at 47.3 average, still substantially above the seed model.

**BTX vs. specialized models:** BTX Top-2 approaches but does not surpass the specialized models on their target domains: Math 27.4 vs. Llemma's 28.0 (−0.6), Code 34.0 vs. CodeLlama's 36.3 (−2.3). However, BTX dramatically outperforms the specialized models on non-specialist domains: Knowledge 41.0 vs. Llemma's 17.2 (+23.8) and CodeLlama's 22.2 (+18.8); Reasoning 63.5 vs. Llemma's 38.8 (+24.7) and CodeLlama's 56.6 (+6.9). This demonstrates that BTX successfully retains generalist capabilities while adding domain expertise — the central promise of the method.

**BTX vs. Llama-2 13B:** BTX Top-2 (47.9 average) outperforms Llama-2 13B (45.4) by 2.5 points. Domain breakdown: Math 27.4 vs. 16.3 (+11.1), Code 34.0 vs. 24.5 (+9.5), Knowledge 41.0 vs. 40.0 (+1.0), Reasoning 63.5 vs. 66.1 (−2.6), MMLU 52.5 vs. 52.8 (−0.3). BTX wins on the domains where experts were trained, and is competitive on general reasoning and MMLU despite having fewer active parameters (11.1B vs. 13B) and using substantially less training compute (Figure 2 left: BTX at ~926 GPU-days vs. Llama-2 13B's approximate doubling of 7B training, placing it well to the right on the X-axis).

**BTX vs. BTM:** BTX (47.9) substantially outperforms BTM Top-2 (43.4) by 4.5 points on average. Domain breakdown: Math 27.4 vs. 21.5 (+5.9), Code 34.0 vs. 36.6 (−2.6), Knowledge 41.0 vs. 26.9 (+14.1), Reasoning 63.5 vs. 61.2 (+2.3), MMLU 52.5 vs. 44.3 (+8.2). The largest gains are in Knowledge and MMLU, where BTM's coarse document-level routing is least effective. The Code score actually favors BTM slightly (36.6 vs. 34.0), which the paper does not directly discuss — it may be that BTM's explicit domain classification for code tasks is more reliable than BTX's learned token-level routing for that specific domain, or it may reflect noise given the small evaluation set sizes.

**BTX vs. dense continued pretraining (DM):** BTX (47.9) outperforms dense (44.5) by 3.4 points on average. Dense training on the same data mixture fails to achieve comparable domain specialization: Math 18.3 vs. 27.4 (−9.1), Code 25.8 vs. 34.0 (−8.2). This demonstrates that simply training a single dense model on all domains simultaneously is less effective than BTX's approach of first training domain-specific experts separately and then integrating them via MoE — evidence for the value of the asynchronous, interference-free expert training phase.

**BTX vs. sparse upcycling (DM):** BTX (47.9) outperforms data-matched sparse upcycling (46.3) by 1.6 points on average. Domain breakdown: Math 27.4 vs. 28.1 (−0.7), Code 34.0 vs. 34.7 (−0.7), Knowledge 41.0 vs. 34.0 (+7.0), Reasoning 63.5 vs. 62.3 (+1.2), MMLU 52.5 vs. 51.1 (+1.4). Sparse upcycling actually slightly outperforms BTX on Math and Code, but BTX's advantage on Knowledge (a 7-point gap) drives the overall average difference. This suggests that the asynchronous expert training phase provides the most value for domains where specialization benefits from isolated, high-volume training (like the Wikipedia expert's knowledge accumulation) rather than domains where the seed model already has substantial capability that just needs refinement.

#### Compute Efficiency Comparison (Figure 2 Left, Table 3)

Figure 2 (left) plots average score versus training GPU-days for all methods, with circle size indicating active inference parameters. The key finding:

- BTX models (labeled as "BTX" on the plot, with two variants for different routing) sit in the upper-left region — higher performance at lower compute cost than all baselines that start from Llama-2 7B.
- BTX uses approximately 926 GPU-days to achieve a 47.9 average score. The dense baseline requires similar compute (it trains on the same data) but achieves only 44.5. BTM achieves 43.4 at similar compute (expert training only, no MoE finetuning).
- Sparse upcycling (CM), which is allocated approximately the same GPU-days as BTX (1007.1 vs. 926.1), achieves 47.3 average — slightly below BTX (47.9) but close. However, Table 3 reveals the mechanism: BTX processes 533B total tokens in its GPU-day budget, while sparse upcycling processes only 252B tokens. The asynchronous expert training phase in BTX accounts for 77% of compute but yields 453B tokens of domain-specific training, compared to sparse upcycling where all 1007.1 GPU-days are spent on synchronized MoE training at lower throughput. The result is that BTX achieves slightly higher performance with 2.1× more training tokens in slightly fewer GPU-days.
- Llama-2 13B is shown as a reference point, with approximately 2× the GPU-days of Llama-2 7B (since pretraining compute scales roughly with parameters for the same token count). BTX achieves a higher average score (47.9 vs. 45.4) at substantially lower total training compute (though exact GPU-day values for Llama-2 13B are approximate).

The paper summarizes this finding (Section 4.2.2):

> "BTX uses less than half of the additional training compute compared to Llama-2 13B, but demonstrates improved performance on expert domains (math, code, and knowledge) and achieves better overall performance."

#### Difficulty-Dependent or Domain-Dependent Analysis

Unlike the reference example paper (which analyzed compute-optimal scaling across difficulty bins), BTX does not bin problems by difficulty. Instead, its analysis is inherently **domain-structured**: different experts specialize in different domains, and the router's behavior is analyzed per domain. The key domain-dependent findings are:

**Domain expert specialization is partially retained after MoE finetuning (Figure 3, Table 6).** The routing analysis (Section 4.3.2) examines which experts the router selects for tokens in different downstream tasks, aggregated by domain:
- **Code domain tasks (HumanEval, MBPP):** The Code expert is the dominant choice, especially when load balancing is active. Without load balancing, the Code expert becomes dead (routing probability shifts to zero), and the Math expert dominates all domains.
- **Math domain tasks (GSM8K, MATH):** Routing patterns differ between the two math benchmarks. GSM8K (grade-school math word problems) routes mainly to the Code and Llama-2 7B experts, while MATH (college-level math) relies more on the in-domain Math expert. The paper hypothesizes that GSM8K requires more common sense knowledge and basic arithmetic, which the Code and generalist experts provide, while MATH requires specialized mathematical knowledge.
- **Knowledge domain tasks:** Routing primarily goes to the Wikipedia expert and the Llama-2 generalist expert, as expected from the domain alignment.
- **Reasoning domain tasks (ARC, SIQA, PIQA, WinoGrande):** Routing is relatively uniform across Math and Llama-2 experts, with no single expert dominating.

This domain-structured routing behavior is key evidence that BTX successfully combines domain specialization (experts have genuine domain-specific capabilities) with flexible token-level routing (the router can select non-domain experts when appropriate, as in GSM8K routing to Code).

**Load balancing improves performance on underutilized domains at the cost of dominant-domain performance (Table 5, Figure 3).** The comparison between BTX with and without load balancing (Table 5, both at 10B finetuning tokens) shows:
- Without load balancing: GSM8K 34.6, HumanEval 19.5, Natural Questions 23.2, ARC Challenge 44.4, MMLU 51.6, average 34.6.
- With load balancing: GSM8K 29.8, HumanEval 27.4, Natural Questions 23.0, ARC Challenge 43.4, MMLU 50.0, average 34.7.

The nearly identical average scores (34.6 vs. 34.7) mask a tradeoff: load balancing substantially improves code performance (+7.9 on HumanEval) at the cost of math performance (−4.8 on GSM8K). The routing analysis (Figure 3) explains this: without load balancing, the Math expert monopolizes routing across all domains, suppressing the Code expert entirely (dead expert phenomenon). Load balancing forces the router to distribute load, which "revives" the Code expert and improves code tasks, but at the cost of reduced Math expert utilization on math tasks. The paper's choice of load balancing (α=0.01) as default reflects a preference for balanced domain performance rather than maximizing a single domain.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Routing method comparison:** Table 4 compares Switch Top-1, Sample Top-1, Top-2, and Soft routing, each evaluated at multiple finetuning token budgets (10B, 40B, and 80B/160B tokens). At 10B tokens, the performance ordering is Soft (35.8) > Top-2 (34.6) > Sample Top-1 (33.0) > Switch Top-1 (24.7). Soft routing benefits from dense activation of all experts, providing an upper bound on what routing can achieve. At 40B tokens, the ordering is preserved: Soft (37.3) > Top-2 (35.9) > Sample Top-1 (35.3). At the highest budgets (80B for Top-2 at 37.3, 160B for Sample Top-1 at 36.9), Top-2 and Sample Top-1 approach Soft routing performance, with Sample Top-1 nearly matching Top-2 despite using only one active expert per token. Switch Top-1 is subpar at all budgets, likely due to the capacity factor token dropping mechanism losing information during training.

**Load balancing presence vs. absence:** Table 5 (rows "BTX" vs. "no load-balancing (LB)"): As discussed above, load balancing produces a more uniform expert utilization (Figure 3) and improves code performance at the cost of math performance, with equal average scores at 10B finetuning tokens (both 34.7). The routing analysis (Appendix C, Figure 5) shows that without load balancing, the Code expert's routing probability collapses to zero across most layers — a classic dead expert failure. Load balancing prevents this, at the cost of slightly reducing the dominant Math expert's routing share.

**Freezing expert FF parameters during MoE finetuning:** Table 5 (comparing "no load-balancing & freeze experts" to "no load-balancing (LB)"): When expert FF parameters are frozen and only the router and averaged parameters are trained, performance is essentially unchanged — average score 34.7 in both cases (with minor per-task variation: GSM8K 34.8 vs. 34.6, HumanEval 18.3 vs. 19.5, Natural Questions 24.1 vs. 23.2, ARC Challenge 44.9 vs. 44.4, MMLU 51.4 vs. 51.6). This is a practically significant finding: if expert FF parameters do not need to be trained during MoE finetuning, the finetuning stage requires less GPU memory (no optimizer states for those parameters) and could be accelerated. It also confirms that the Branch & Train stage already produces high-quality domain-specific FF parameters that do not require further adjustment.

**Blending experts (breaking domain specialization):** Table 5 ("blending experts"): When each MoE expert is constructed by taking equal-sized chunks from all domain experts' FF layers (so each expert contains equal representation from math, code, Wikipedia, and the generalist), performance collapses: average score 22.2, down 12.5 points from the default 34.7. Every task degrades severely (GSM8K 13.9 vs. 29.8, HumanEval 17.1 vs. 27.4, Natural Questions 9.9 vs. 23.0, ARC Challenge 34.1 vs. 43.4, MMLU 36.2 vs. 50.0). This negative result demonstrates that domain specialization in the FF parameters is real and functionally important — the router cannot compensate for homogenized experts, even though standard MoE training from scratch does not produce domain-specialized experts (as noted by Jiang et al., 2024). The paper interprets this as evidence that the explicit domain specialization enforced by the Branch & Train stage is critical to BTX's performance.

**Splitting experts (increasing expert granularity):** Table 5 ("split experts, top-2 of 8" and "split experts, top-4 of 8"): When each domain expert's FF layers are split into C=2 chunks, creating 8 total experts in the MoE layer (2 per original domain), performance depends on the number of activated experts. With Top-2 of 8, the average score is 28.0 — significantly worse than the default 4-expert Top-2 (34.7). With Top-4 of 8 (which matches the active parameter count of Top-2 of 4, since each split expert has half the FF dimension), the average score recovers to 34.5, essentially matching but not exceeding the default. The paper concludes that the default coarse-grained domain experts are already at an effective granularity for this setup, and finer-grained splitting does not provide additional benefit.

**Finetuning token budget:** Table 4 shows performance for different routing methods as the MoE finetuning token budget increases. For Top-2: 34.6 (10B tokens), 35.9 (40B tokens), 37.3 (80B tokens) — steady improvement with more finetuning. For Sample Top-1: 33.0 (10B), 35.3 (40B), 36.9 (160B) — a ~3.9 point gain from 10B to 160B tokens, nearly closing the gap with Top-2. This monotonic improvement suggests that BTX benefits from extended MoE finetuning and that the paper's default 80B tokens for Top-2 may not represent a performance plateau — further finetuning could yield additional gains.

**Data-matching vs. compute-matching for sparse upcycling (Table 2, Table 3):** The sparse upcycling baseline is evaluated in both data-matching (DM) and compute-matching (CM) configurations. DM sparse upcycling trains on the same 533B tokens as BTX (but all in MoE mode), achieving 46.3 average. CM sparse upcycling trains for the same ~1000 GPU-days (but processes only 252B tokens), achieving 47.3 average. The fact that CM performs slightly better than DM (47.3 vs. 46.3) despite processing fewer tokens is notable — it suggests that the MoE training data mixture (which includes original Llama-2 pretraining data) may be higher-quality than the pure domain-specific data used in expert training, and that training on a more balanced mixture for fewer tokens is better than training on domain-heavy data for more tokens. However, both are below BTX's 47.9, indicating BTX's two-stage approach extracts more value from the combination of domain-specific and generalist data than either single-stage approach.

**BTM Top-1 vs. Top-2 (Table 2):** BTM with Top-1 vs. Top-2 expert selection at inference time produces nearly identical results: Top-1 achieves 43.1 average, Top-2 achieves 43.4. The minimal difference (0.3 points) suggests that for BTM's document-level routing strategy, selecting one expert is almost as effective as selecting two — which makes intuitive sense since BTM's routing is based on tf-idf domain classification, and the dominant domain for a given input is usually unambiguous. This contrasts with BTX, where Top-2 provides meaningful benefit over Top-1 (via Sample Top-1 comparison), because token-level routing benefits from the ability to select multiple experts for different tokens within the same sequence.

**Individual task performance (Appendix B, Table 8):** The paper reports per-benchmark scores for all methods in Table 8 (Appendix B). Notable individual results include:
- BTX Top-2 achieves 37.1 on GSM8K (vs. Llemma's 39.3) and 17.8 on MATH (vs. Llemma's 16.7 — BTX actually slightly exceeds Llemma on MATH despite Llemma being a specialized math model).
- BTX Top-2 achieves 28.7 on HumanEval (vs. CodeLlama's 31.1) and 39.4 on MBPP (vs. CodeLlama's 41.4) — approaching specialized code models.
- BTX Top-2 achieves 24.8 on Natural Questions and 57.1 on TriviaQA — both exceeding the seed model (16.4 and 58.5, respectively, with NQ improving and TriviaQA holding steady) and dramatically exceeding the domain experts (which had collapsed on these tasks).
- Commonsense reasoning scores (ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, SIQA, PIQA, WinoGrande) are remarkably stable across all BTX variants and baselines — evidence that these tasks rely on capabilities encoded in the self-attention layers and shared parameters that are preserved through averaging.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments provide solid support for the paper's central pragmatic claim — that BTX is a more compute-efficient continued pretraining method than existing alternatives — but with several important boundary conditions that deserve scrutiny.

**Claim: BTX achieves better performance than alternative continued pretraining methods at equal or lower compute.**
The evidence for this is strong when comparing BTX against dense continued pretraining and BTM. Table 2 shows BTX (47.9) > dense DM (44.5) by 3.4 points, and > BTM (43.4) by 4.5 points, with both comparisons using the same or comparable training data. The gap is large enough that even with some variance (no confidence intervals are reported), the direction is credible.

The comparison with sparse upcycling is more nuanced. Data-matched sparse upcycling achieves 46.3, putting BTX ahead by 1.6 points — a meaningful but not dramatic margin. Compute-matched sparse upcycling achieves 47.3, within 0.6 points of BTX. The paper's interpretation of this gap (Section 4.2.2) emphasizes that BTX processes 2× more tokens in the same compute budget, which is the efficiency story. However, a skeptical reading would note: the absolute performance difference between BTX (47.9) and compute-matched sparse upcycling (47.3) is small, and without confidence intervals or multiple training runs, it is unclear whether this difference is statistically reliable or could reverse with a different random seed. The stronger evidence for BTX's efficiency advantage is not the final performance number but the token throughput advantage (533B vs. 252B tokens in the same GPU-days) — this is a direct, reproducible measurement of training efficiency that does not depend on benchmark variance.

**Claim: BTX bridges the gap with specialized models while retaining generalist capabilities.**
This claim is supported but with notable caveats. BTX approaches Llemma on math (27.4 vs. 28.0) and CodeLlama on code (34.0 vs. 36.3), but does not match or exceed them on their specialties. The paper presents this as a success — BTX achieves most of the domain benefit of specialized models without their catastrophic forgetting. This framing is reasonable: a 2.3-point code deficit is a small price for +18.8 points on world knowledge and +6.9 points on reasoning over CodeLlama. However, the claim that BTX "bridges the gap" with specialized models would be strengthened by including a comparison where BTX's domain experts are trained for longer (the paper matched Llemma's 201B tokens and CodeLlama's 210B tokens, but does not explore whether more expert training would close the remaining gap).

The generalist capability retention works well: reasoning (63.5 vs. seed's 63.3) and MMLU (52.5 vs. seed's 46.1) are stable or improved. However, MMLU improvement (+6.4) likely reflects the fact that MMLU contains many math and code questions, not that BTX improved general reasoning. The reasoning benchmark aggregate (ARC, SIQA, PIQA, WinoGrande) is essentially flat, which is the desired outcome — BTX should not degrade these capabilities.

**Claim: BTX is more compute-efficient than training a larger generalist model (Llama-2 13B).**
The comparison with Llama-2 13B is the most problematic in the paper because of how the compute accounting is done. The paper states (Section 4.2.2):

> "The GPU days of Llama-2 13B is an approximate measurement, calculated by doubling the training compute of a 7B model trained with the same amount of pretraining data."

This approximation assumes perfect linear scaling of compute with parameter count for the same token budget, which is roughly correct for dense Transformers (compute ∝ parameters × tokens). However, Llama-2 13B was trained on 2T tokens — the same as Llama-2 7B — so its pretraining compute is approximately 13/7 ≈ 1.86× that of 7B, not 2×. More importantly, the comparison in Figure 2 (left) places Llama-2 13B at an X-axis position corresponding to its total pretraining GPU-days *from scratch*, whereas BTX starts from the already-trained Llama-2 7B. The fair comparison should add Llama-2 7B's pretraining cost to BTX's continued training cost, and then compare that total against Llama-2 13B's total. The paper does this implicitly by starting the X-axis "from the seed model" for BTX and showing Llama-2 13B's position based on its total training cost difference, but the visual presentation makes BTX look substantially more efficient than it actually is because the seed model's pretraining cost (~thousands of GPU-days) is excluded from the X-axis.

A more conservative interpretation: BTX adds ~926 GPU-days of continued training to a model that already cost substantial compute to pretrain. Llama-2 13B cost an additional ~86% more pretraining compute than Llama-2 7B (the difference between 13B and 7B training). If that additional pretraining cost is less than or comparable to 926 GPU-days, then BTX may not be more compute-efficient in total — it just reallocates compute from synchronous pretraining to asynchronous continued training. The paper does not provide enough data to make this comparison precisely, and this is a significant weakness.

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

1. **Single seed model, three domains:** All experiments use Llama-2 7B as the seed and train experts for math, code, and Wikipedia only. There is no evidence that BTX would work with a different base model architecture, different scale (e.g., starting from a 13B or 70B seed), or different domain granularity (what happens with 10 domains? 2 domains?). The paper acknowledges this limitation (Section 6) but the single-configuration testing makes it difficult to assess generalizability.

2. **No multiple training runs or confidence intervals:** All benchmark scores are reported as point estimates from single training runs. With test sets as small as HumanEval (164 problems) and MATH (500 problems for some subsets), individual score differences of 1-2 points could easily arise from sampling variance rather than genuine method differences. The comparison between BTX (47.9) and compute-matched sparse upcycling (47.3) is a 0.6-point difference — without error bars, this should be interpreted as "approximately tied" rather than "BTX wins."

3. **The blending and splitting ablations are underdeveloped:** The blending ablation shows that breaking domain specialization catastrophically hurts performance (22.2 vs. 34.7 average), which is an important finding. But the paper does not explore *why* blending fails — is it because the domain experts' FF representations are incompatible (different activation patterns, different saturation regimes) and averaging them produces incoherent transformations? Or is it simply that the router cannot learn to disentangle the blended representations in only 10B finetuning tokens, and would eventually recover with more training? The frozen-experts result (Table 5, showing expert FF can be frozen without loss) suggests the first explanation is more likely, but no analysis of the blended model's internal representations is provided.

4. **No exploration of the compute allocation ratio between Branch & Train and MiX stages:** The paper identifies that BTX generalizes BTM (100% expert training, 0% MoE finetuning) and sparse upcycling (0% expert training, 100% MoE finetuning), but does not sweep the allocation ratio. What happens if experts are trained for half as many tokens and MoE finetuning is doubled? What if more domains are added but expert training is reduced proportionally? The paper acknowledges this gap (Section 6: "Future work could perform a thorough sweep of the compute allocation ratio between expert training and MoE training") but does not address it.

5. **The load balancing tradeoff is presented as a choice rather than optimized:** The paper observes that load balancing improves code at the cost of math, and chooses α=0.01 as default. But there is no sweep over α values to find a Pareto-optimal setting, no attempt to use domain-conditional load balancing (different α per domain), and no exploration of whether the tradeoff changes with more finetuning tokens. The choice of α seems arbitrary, and the performance with load balancing (average 34.7) equals that without it (34.6) — suggesting the default setting may not be optimal for any particular domain emphasis.

6. **No comparison with ensemble methods that combine domain experts at inference time with learned weights:** BTM uses tf-idf similarity to determine expert weights, which is a weak baseline for inference-time ensembling. A stronger baseline would train a lightweight classifier (e.g., a linear model on top of the seed model's representations) to predict per-domain weights for each input, then ensemble the domain experts' output probabilities using those learned weights. This would provide a middle ground between BTM's heuristic routing and BTX's full MoE integration, and would help isolate whether the benefit of BTX comes from learned routing or from the structural integration of parameters.

7. **The generalist expert (original seed model) is included by default but never ablated:** The paper includes the original Llama-2 7B as a fourth expert alongside the math, code, and Wikipedia experts. The stated reason is to prevent catastrophic forgetting of general capabilities. However, the paper never reports an ablation where the generalist expert is excluded (only math, code, and Wikipedia experts in the MoE). It is unclear how much of BTX's generalist capability retention is due to the generalist expert versus the self-attention averaging. Given that the Wikipedia expert was trained on only 42B tokens (much less than math/code) and had minimal forgetting on knowledge tasks (Table 1), it is possible that simply including the Wikipedia expert — which retained most general knowledge — would suffice without the explicit generalist expert. This ablation would address whether BTX genuinely prevents forgetting through architecture or simply through retaining a frozen copy of the seed model.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Scaling the number of domains:** What happens with 6 domains? 10 domains? Does the router continue to learn meaningful domain-specific routing, or does it collapse to a few experts? Does training throughput scale linearly with more domains?

- **Starting from a different seed model:** Would BTX work with a non-Llama architecture (e.g., MPT, Falcon, or a encoder-decoder model)? Does the approach depend on specific properties of Llama-2's training?

- **Instruction finetuning or RLHF after BTX:** The paper's primary motivation for BTX over BTM is that BTX produces a unified model that can be finetuned. But the paper never actually demonstrates SFT or RLHF on the BTX model, leaving this as a promissory note.

- **Training sparse upcycling for long enough to process 533B tokens:** The compute-matched comparison gives sparse upcycling the same GPU-days, but because throughput is lower, it processes fewer tokens. The missing experiment is to train sparse upcycling for enough GPU-days to process the same 533B tokens as BTX, and compare performance. If data-matched sparse upcycling (46.3) is any guide, BTX would still win (47.9 vs. ~46.3), but this would provide a cleaner isolation of the throughput advantage versus the architectural advantage.

- **Expert training for different durations:** The paper matches expert training tokens to prior work (Llemma, CodeLlama) but does not show how performance scales with expert training tokens. Would training each expert for 400B tokens instead of 200B improve downstream BTX performance, or is there a saturation point?

Overall, the experiments convincingly demonstrate that BTX is a viable and effective method for continued pretraining of Llama-2 7B on three domains, and that it outperforms the specific baselines tested (dense continued training, BTM, sparse upcycling DM). The evidence for BTX being more compute-efficient than training a larger model from scratch (Llama-2 13B) is suggestive but weakened by imprecise compute accounting. The evidence for BTX's generalizability beyond the tested configuration (one model family, three domains, one dataset scale) is nonexistent, and the paper appropriately acknowledges this as a limitation. The paper would benefit most from additional experiments showing (a) BTX working at different scales or with different base models, (b) the SFT/RLHF integration it promises as a key advantage over BTM, and (c) robustness of the results across multiple training runs with variance estimates.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Computation Spent on Expert Training Cannot Be Recovered for Different Domain Combinations

**The assumption or constraint:**
BTX ties expert training to specific domains chosen upfront. The embarrassingly parallel training stage produces domain experts — math, code, Wikipedia, plus the retained generalist — each consuming substantial compute (201B, 210B, and 42B tokens respectively). If a practitioner later wants to add a new domain (say, biology or legal text) or remove one, the existing expert training compute is partially stranded. The paper does not explore incremental expert addition — all experts are trained, then all are merged in a single MoE finetuning stage.

**The consequence:**
BTX's training paradigm assumes a fixed, known set of target domains from the start. In real deployment, the set of desired capabilities evolves: a team might initially need code + math, then later want multilingual support or domain-specific knowledge (medicine, finance). Under BTX as described, adding a new expert requires training that expert from scratch (no amortization of prior experts' training), and then re-running the MoE finetuning stage so the router learns to incorporate the new expert alongside the existing ones. The paper provides no evidence on whether adding an expert to an already-finetuned BTX model works (can you "hot-plug" a new FF expert and finetune only briefly?), or whether the original MoE finetuning is specific to the exact set of experts present. The sparse upcycling baseline (Section 4.1.2) already demonstrates that training an MoE from replicated dense experts works, but that is a *from-scratch* initialization of all experts — not an incremental addition to a trained router. Without experiments on incremental expert addition, BTX presents as a *batch* methodology: choose your domains, train experts, merge once.

**What evidence exists in the paper:**
None directly. The paper trains three domain experts plus the generalist, and that exact set is used in the MoE finetuning stage. Section 6 (Limitations & Future Work) states:

> "Due to compute limitations, we only experimented with three domains and four experts in this paper. Training on more domains such as using unsupervised domain discovery (Gururangan et al., 2023) should amplify the benefit of the parallelization of experts training."

This acknowledges that more domains are desirable, but frames it as a scale issue (we didn't test with more domains) rather than a *modularity* issue (we didn't test whether experts can be added after the fact). The paper also does not discuss whether the MoE finetuning stage's 80B tokens would need to scale with the number of experts — with 10 experts, does the router need proportionally more finetuning data to learn routing among a larger set of options?

**Mitigation status:**
Not addressed. The paper suggests future work on more domains and on sweeping the compute allocation ratio between expert training and MoE finetuning (Section 6), but does not frame incremental expert addition as a problem to solve. A practitioner wanting to deploy BTX in an evolving domain landscape would need to either (a) retrain the full pipeline when domains change, (b) guess a superset of domains upfront and hope it covers future needs, or (c) develop their own incremental addition procedure without guidance from this paper.

### Self-Attention Averaging Assumes Domain-Agnostic Attention Patterns — An Empirical Claim Tested Only on Three Related Domains

**The assumption or constraint:**
The structural merge operation in BTX (Section 3.2) averages the self-attention parameters across all domain experts, based on the stated assumption that "the self-attention layers are less domain specialized than the feedforward layers." This assumption is the architectural linchpin of BTX: if self-attention layers *did* specialize significantly to domains, averaging them would produce degraded representations that even 80B tokens of finetuning might not fully recover. The paper validates this assumption only for the specific case of Llama-2 7B continued-trained on math, code, and Wikipedia — three domains that are all English-language, text-based, and within the distribution of the original pretraining data.

**The consequence:**
If BTX were applied to domains that differ more radically — different languages (where attention patterns over syntax and word order differ), different modalities (where attention over visual patches vs. text tokens involves fundamentally different learned patterns), or domains with specialized document structures (legal contracts, scientific papers with heavy mathematical notation) — the attention-averaging assumption may break. Attention layers that have learned domain-specific patterns (e.g., code indentation structure, mathematical formula parsing, Wikipedia infobox formatting) would, when averaged, produce a suboptimal initialization. The MoE finetuning stage can adjust these averaged parameters, but there is no guarantee that 80B tokens of finetuning suffices to recover from a poor initialization — the paper does not test this boundary.

More subtly, the paper's own data hints at attention specialization that the averaging may obscure. The routing analysis (Figure 3, Appendix C) shows that different domains route to different experts at different layers — and these routing decisions are based on the token representations produced by the preceding self-attention layers. If the self-attention layers were truly domain-agnostic, we might expect more uniform routing patterns across layers. The fact that routing varies by layer and by domain suggests that attention outputs carry domain-specific information that the router exploits.

**What evidence exists in the paper:**
Only indirect. The success of BTX (Table 2: 47.9 average) demonstrates that averaging attention parameters works for the tested domains, but this is existence proof, not systematic analysis. The ablation showing that freezing expert FF parameters during MoE finetuning does not hurt performance (Table 5) suggests that the finetuning stage primarily trains the router and adjusts attention parameters — which *does* indicate that the averaged attention parameters benefit from further training, but not that they start from a good initialization. The failure of expert blending (Table 5: 22.2 vs. 34.7 average) shows that FF parameters are domain-specialized and cannot be homogenized, but the paper never runs the complementary experiment: keeping domain-specialized FF parameters intact while also keeping domain-specialized attention parameters (i.e., not averaging attention). That experiment would directly measure the cost of the attention-averaging assumption.

**Mitigation status:**
Not addressed. The paper treats the attention-averaging assumption as a design choice justified by prior MoE literature and validated by the downstream BTX results, but does not ablate it or test its boundaries. A practitioner applying BTX to substantially different domains would need to either (a) trust that the assumption transfers, (b) run their own ablation comparing averaged vs. non-averaged attention, or (c) accept the risk of degraded performance without a diagnostic for whether attention specialization is occurring.

### The Generalist Expert Is Critical for Preventing Forgetting, but Its Contribution Is Never Isolated

**The assumption or constraint:**
BTX includes the original seed model (Llama-2 7B) as a fourth "generalist" expert alongside the three domain experts. The paper states this is done "so that its general knowledge is transferred to the final model" (Section 4.1.1). This means the BTX MoE model has access to the exact parameters of the original seed model as one of its FF expert options at every layer. When the router needs general knowledge — commonsense reasoning, broad world knowledge, factual recall not covered by the domain experts — it can simply route tokens to the generalist expert, which has not been modified by any domain-specific training.

**The consequence:**
The presence of the generalist expert makes it impossible to determine how much of BTX's catastrophic forgetting resistance is due to the **architecture** (MoE routing + averaged attention) versus the **trivial strategy of keeping an unmodified copy of the seed model around**. If the generalist expert were removed — leaving only the math, code, and Wikipedia experts — would the MoE finetuning stage still prevent catastrophic forgetting on non-specialist domains? The reasoning benchmark results (Table 2) are suggestive: BTX achieves 63.5 on reasoning, essentially identical to the seed model's 63.3 and far above the domain experts (math expert: 56.2 from Table 8 individual reasoning tasks? Actually the reasoning domain averages aren't in Table 1 for individual experts, but the domain experts' catastrophic forgetting on knowledge tasks — TriviaQA drops to 37.1 for math expert, 29.9 for code expert — makes clear that they lost general capabilities). Without a generalist-expert ablation, we cannot distinguish between two hypotheses:

- **Hypothesis A (BTX architecture genuinely prevents forgetting):** The averaged self-attention parameters retain general capabilities, and the router learns to route general-knowledge tokens to the most appropriate domain expert even when none is a perfect match, with the MoE combination producing reasonable outputs.

- **Hypothesis B (the generalist expert is doing all the work):** The router routes all non-math/code/Wikipedia tokens to the unmodified generalist expert, which produces exactly the seed model's output. The domain experts are only used for in-domain tokens. In this case, BTX's "catastrophic forgetting resistance" is not a property of the architecture but of retaining the original model.

If Hypothesis B is true, BTX is effectively a learned routing system over the seed model (for general tasks) and domain experts (for specialized tasks) — closer to BTM with learned token-level routing than to a genuinely integrated multi-domain model. This would not invalidate BTX's practical utility, but it would significantly weaken the claim that BTX "mixes" expert knowledge into a unified whole. If Hypothesis A is true, BTX genuinely blends capabilities in a way BTM cannot.

**What evidence exists in the paper:**
None directly. The generalist expert is included in all BTX configurations by default, and there is no ablation removing it. The paper does provide some suggestive evidence against Hypothesis B (pure generalist reliance). The routing analysis (Figure 3, top panel) shows that reasoning tasks route to both Math and Llama-2 experts roughly equally, and that even within code tasks, tokens are not routed exclusively to the Code expert — the generalist is also used. This indicates the router does not simply shunt all non-domain tokens to the generalist. But without the ablation, the magnitude of the generalist's contribution to non-specialist performance is unknown.

**Mitigation status:**
Not addressed. The paper never discusses the generalist expert as a potential confounding factor, treats its inclusion as obvious, and does not include a generalist-free ablation in any of the reported experiments. A practitioner adopting BTX would need to determine whether they have a suitable "generalist" model to retain — if the seed model is already weak on certain capabilities, retaining it as an expert does not help, and the paper provides no guidance on what happens when the generalist is omitted or when the domain experts collectively cover all desired capabilities.

### Training Compute Accounting Excludes the Seed Model's Pretraining Cost, Overstating Efficiency Gains Relative to Larger Models

**The assumption or constraint:**
The paper's headline efficiency comparison (Figure 2, left; Section 4.2.2) shows BTX at approximately 926 GPU-days on the X-axis, positioned as substantially more compute-efficient than Llama-2 13B. The paper states that the X-axis measures "the total training compute starting from the seed model" and that Llama-2 13B's GPU-days are "an approximate measurement, calculated by doubling the training compute of a 7B model trained with the same amount of pretraining data" (Section 4.2.2 footnote). This accounting omits the pretraining cost of the seed model itself from BTX's total, while showing Llama-2 13B's approximate *total* pretraining cost difference.

**The consequence:**
BTX starts from a fully pretrained Llama-2 7B, which required approximately 184,320 GPU-hours (per Touvron et al., 2023, Table 2) or roughly 7,680 GPU-days of pretraining on 2T tokens using 2,048 A100-80GB GPUs for ~90 days (these are approximate external figures — the paper does not provide the exact number). Adding BTX's 926 GPU-days of continued training, the total compute to produce the BTX model is ~8,600+ GPU-days (seed pretraining + continued training). Llama-2 13B's total pretraining cost is approximately 13/7 × 7,680 ≈ 14,260 GPU-days if it also trained on 2T tokens (the paper's "doubling" approximation).

Under this full accounting, BTX's total compute is roughly 60% of Llama-2 13B's, not the visually implied "less than half" from Figure 2's X-axis starting point. The efficiency advantage is real but substantially smaller than the figure suggests. More importantly, the fair baseline for BTX is not "Llama-2 13B trained from scratch" but "what could be achieved by spending BTX's total compute (~8,600 GPU-days) on a single larger model trained with a compute-optimal token-to-parameter ratio?" The Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) suggest compute-optimal training allocates more tokens per parameter than Llama-2's fixed 2T tokens. A model trained with 8,600 GPU-days allocated optimally between parameters and tokens might outperform Llama-2 13B (which is arguably *under-trained* at 2T tokens for its parameter count), and the paper provides no comparison against such a baseline.

**What evidence exists in the paper:**
The paper explicitly notes the approximation:

> "The GPU days of Llama-2 13B is an approximate measurement, calculated by doubling the training compute of a 7B model trained with the same amount of pretraining data (according to Touvron et al. (2023) Table 2). Since Llama-2 13B is not trained from the seed model, we simply report their difference in GPU days."

This is honest but insufficient. The footnote acknowledges the Llama-2 13B comparison is approximate, but does not acknowledge the more fundamental issue of excluding the seed model's pretraining cost from BTX's total while including it (implicitly) in Llama-2 13B's position. The X-axis label "total training compute starting from the seed model" is precise for the methods that branch from Llama-2 7B, but the Llama-2 13B data point violates this definition.

**Mitigation status:**
Partially addressed through transparency about the approximation, but the visual presentation in Figure 2 (left) is misleading because it visually anchors BTX at ~926 GPU-days and Llama-2 13B much further right without clarifying that the seed model's pretraining thousands of GPU-days are excluded from the former. A practitioner evaluating whether to adopt BTX versus simply training a larger dense model needs the full-cost comparison — including seed model pretraining — to make an informed decision. The paper does not provide this.

### Load Balancing Introduces a Domain-Level Performance Tradeoff with No Guidance for Navigating It

**The assumption or constraint:**
The load balancing auxiliary loss (Section 3.3) with `α = 0.01` is used by default in BTX to prevent dead experts. However, the paper's own ablation (Table 5) reveals that load balancing introduces a direct tradeoff: it improves performance on domains whose experts would otherwise be underutilized (code: HumanEval 19.5 → 27.4, +7.9 points) while degrading performance on domains whose experts would otherwise dominate (math: GSM8K 34.6 → 29.8, −4.8 points). The overall average score is unchanged (34.7 vs. 34.6), but the per-domain distribution shifts substantially.

**The consequence:**
A practitioner deploying BTX faces an implicit priority decision that the paper neither surfaces nor provides guidance on. If the deployment scenario values math performance over code performance (e.g., an educational math tutor), the no-load-balancing variant might be preferable despite its dead expert pathology. If balanced capabilities matter more (e.g., a general-purpose coding assistant that also solves math problems), the default load-balanced variant is better. The paper selects `α = 0.01` without reporting a sweep over `α` values, so we do not know:
- Whether intermediate `α` values produce a more favorable tradeoff (e.g., α = 0.001 might preserve most of the math gain while reviving the code expert partially).
- Whether the tradeoff changes with more MoE finetuning tokens — does the code expert eventually receive sufficient gradient signal even without load balancing if training continues for 160B tokens instead of 10B? The paper's Table 4 only reports the load-balanced variant at higher token budgets, not the no-load-balancing variant.
- Whether the optimal `α` depends on the number of experts or the degree of domain imbalance in the training data.

A related unexamined issue: the load balancing loss treats all experts equally, but the domains are not equally represented in the finetuning data (math: 30.16%, code: 40.31%, Wikipedia: 10.30%, Llama-2: 19.23%). The load balancing loss pushes toward uniform routing regardless of data frequency, which may force the router to overuse the Wikipedia expert (trained on only 42B tokens, much less than math/code) relative to its actual utility.

**What evidence exists in the paper:**
Table 5 and Figure 3 provide the core evidence. Table 5 shows the per-task tradeoff at 10B finetuning tokens. Figure 3 shows the routing distribution difference: without load balancing, the Code expert becomes dead (routing probability near zero across layers), while the Math expert dominates; with load balancing, routing is more uniform across experts. The paper does not report any sweep over `α`, any per-domain analysis of the tradeoff at higher finetuning budgets, or any investigation of whether alternative formulations of load balancing (e.g., domain-conditional load balancing that respects data frequencies) would improve the tradeoff.

**Mitigation status:**
Partially addressed through the load balancing ablation in Table 5, which at least surfaces the tradeoff. But the paper treats load balancing as a binary choice (on/off) rather than a continuous hyperparameter to be optimized, and does not provide practitioners with a methodology for choosing `α` based on their domain priorities. The paper's conclusion that load balancing is beneficial "on average" masks a real deployment decision that could shift performance substantially depending on the application's domain emphasis.

---

### The Method Is Validated on Only One Model Family and Three Domains, with No Evidence of Scaling Behavior

**The assumption or constraint:**
All experiments use exactly one seed model (Llama-2 7B), one model scale (7B parameters), one architectural family (Llama-2's specific Transformer variant), three expert domains (math, code, Wikipedia), and a fixed expert training duration matched to prior work rather than systematically varied. The paper acknowledges this constraint in Section 6:

> "Due to compute limitations, we only experimented with three domains and four experts in this paper."

But the limitation runs deeper than just domain count. We have no evidence on whether BTX's benefits scale with model size (would a BTX built from Llama-2 70B show similar relative gains?), with number of domains (does the router learn effectively with 10 or 20 experts?), with domain diversity (do the findings hold for languages other than English, or modalities other than text?), or with expert training duration (is there a saturation point beyond which more expert training yields no BTX improvement?).

**The consequence:**
The paper's conclusions are, strictly speaking, valid only for "continued pretraining of Llama-2 7B on math, code, and Wikipedia." A practitioner considering BTX for a different setup — a different base model (e.g., Mistral, Falcon, Gemma), a different scale (1B or 70B parameters), a larger or more diverse set of domains (medicine, law, finance, multiple languages), or a different training budget — must extrapolate from a single data point. Several specific concerns:

- **Router learning with more experts:** With 4 experts and Top-2 routing, the router chooses 2 of 4, which is a relatively easy classification problem. With 20 experts and Top-2 routing, the router chooses 2 of 20 — a much sparser selection — and the load balancing loss becomes more critical (more opportunities for dead experts). The paper provides no evidence on whether the router architecture and training procedure scale to larger expert counts.

- **Model scale and expert quality:** Larger seed models (70B) may have less room for domain-specific improvement through continued pretraining (they already possess substantial math/code/knowledge capabilities). The relative benefit of BTX over dense continued training might shrink as the seed model becomes more capable, or it might grow because larger models have more capacity to develop specialized FF representations.

- **Interaction with the base model's pretraining data:** Llama-2 was pretrained on a corpus that already includes substantial math, code, and Wikipedia data. The domain experts are doing *continued* training on data distributions they have partially seen. BTX might behave differently if applied to genuinely novel domains (e.g., a specialized scientific corpus, a new programming language) that the seed model has minimal exposure to. In that case, the Branch & Train stage might need more tokens to achieve specialization, and the attention-averaging assumption might be more strained.

**What evidence exists in the paper:**
None beyond the single configuration. The paper does not vary the seed model, the model scale, the number of domains, or the expert training duration. The sparse upcycling baseline comparisons (Tables 2, 3) use the same MoE configuration (4 experts replicated from Llama-2 7B), so they also do not provide scaling evidence. The paper does not cite or compare against any prior work applying similar two-stage approaches at different scales.

**Mitigation status:**
Acknowledged as a limitation but not addressed experimentally. The paper states (Section 6):

> "Training on more domains such as using unsupervised domain discovery (Gururangan et al., 2023) should amplify the benefit of the parallelization of experts training. Having more experts will also make the final MoE model more efficient because the number of active experts can remain the same while its overall capacity increases."

This is speculation, not evidence. A practitioner looking for guidance on whether BTX will work for their specific model scale, domain count, or domain type must either run their own experiments or assume that the Llama-2 7B / 3-domain results generalize — a significant risk given the many degrees of freedom involved. This is the most fundamental limitation of the paper: it demonstrates that BTX works in one specific configuration, but provides no framework for predicting whether it will work in others.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

BTX is not a paradigm shift in the sense of introducing a fundamentally new architecture or loss function — MoE layers, continued pretraining, and embarrassingly parallel training all predate this work. Rather, it is a **methodological reframing with economic implications**: it reconceptualizes the continued pretraining phase of LLM development not as a monolithic synchronized process, but as a two-stage pipeline where compute allocation (how much to spend on asynchronous specialization versus synchronized integration) is itself a hyperparameter to be optimized. The paper does not fully optimize this allocation — it tests only the endpoints (100/0 for BTM, 0/100 for sparse upcycling) and one interior point (~77/23 for BTX) — but the framework it establishes makes the optimization problem visible and empirically meaningful.

This reframing matters for how organizations plan their training roadmaps. Prior to BTX, the dominant mental model for adding capabilities to an LLM was sequential: pretrain a generalist model, then optionally continue training on domain-specific data, accepting that each domain addition risks interfering with previous ones. The paper's demonstration that a single MoE finetuning stage (costing only 23% of total continued-training compute) can integrate three independently-trained domain experts into a model that matches or approaches specialized models on their target domains while retaining generalist performance — and that this integrated model is a standard architecture supporting further SFT/RLHF — changes the calculus. It suggests that the **late-pretraining phase should be structured as a branching-and-merging operation rather than a linear sequence of domain adaptations**, and that the integration cost (MoE finetuning) is surprisingly low relative to the specialization cost (expert training).

The paper also resolves a tension that has been growing in the MoE literature. Recent work (Jiang et al., 2024; Dai et al., 2024) demonstrated that MoE experts trained from scratch in a synchronized manner do not naturally specialize to domains — they activate roughly uniformly across input types, functioning more as a distributed computation mechanism than as modular specialists. This finding created an apparent conflict with the Branch-Train-Merge approach (Li et al., 2022a; Gururangan et al., 2023), where domain specialization is the entire premise. BTX resolves this tension by demonstrating that **explicitly constructed domain specialization (via asynchronous, data-segregated training) persists through MoE integration and is functionally useful for learned routing**, even though standard synchronized MoE training does not produce it. The partial domain specialization observed in BTX's routing patterns (Figure 3: Code expert dominates code tasks, Math expert dominates college-level math, but GSM8K routes heavily to Code and generalist experts) represents a previously undocumented operational regime for MoE models — one where experts have genuine domain-specific capabilities but the router has the flexibility to mix them on a per-token basis. This is a substantive finding that should shift the conversation from "MoE experts don't specialize" to "MoE experts can be made to specialize through asynchronous pretraining, and this specialization improves downstream performance."

Several research directions become more attractive in light of this paper:

- **Asynchronous training with learned reintegration** becomes a legitimate alternative to both fully synchronized training and purely fragmented expert ensembles. The paper demonstrates that the integration cost is manageable (80B tokens of MoE finetuning to unify experts trained on 453B+ tokens of domain data), which lowers the barrier for exploring other asynchronous-then-integrate pipelines — different expert architectures, different merge strategies, different routing granularities.

- **Domain specialization as a deliberate design choice** rather than an emergent property gains credibility. Prior to this paper, the observation that standard MoE experts don't specialize could be interpreted as evidence that domain specialization is either impossible or unnecessary. BTX's blending failure (Table 5: +12.5 point drop from homogenizing experts) provides strong counter-evidence: when experts are deliberately specialized, the router exploits that specialization, and destroying it catastrophically degrades performance. This suggests that future MoE training recipes might benefit from an explicit specialization phase, even if standard recipes don't produce it naturally.

- **The feedforward-vs-attention specialization hypothesis** moves from speculation to a testable, actionable claim. The paper's structural merge (FF layers become MoE experts, attention layers are averaged) implicitly assumes that domain knowledge is primarily stored in FF parameters. The success of BTX validates this assumption for the tested domains, but more importantly, it provides a template for testing it in other settings: train domain experts, structurally merge by averaging some parameters and treating others as MoE experts, and measure whether the merge degrades performance. This is a clean experimental paradigm for probing where domain knowledge resides in Transformer architectures — a question with implications beyond BTX.

Conversely, some research directions become less attractive:

- **Pure inference-time ensembling of domain experts without architectural integration** (the BTM approach) looks increasingly limited. The paper shows BTM substantially underperforming BTX (43.4 vs. 47.9 average, a 4.5-point gap), with the largest deficits in domains requiring broad knowledge integration (Knowledge: 26.9 vs. 41.0, MMLU: 44.3 vs. 52.5). BTM's coarse document-level routing and output-level ensembling cannot match what learned token-level routing within a unified model achieves. Unless a breakthrough dramatically improves inference-time ensembling, BTX-style architectural integration should be preferred when a unified model is desired.

- **Sparse upcycling as a standalone strategy** appears to be a special case of a more general framework rather than an independently optimal method. The paper shows sparse upcycling (0% async, 100% MoE finetuning) achieves competitive performance (46.3–47.3) but at lower throughput than BTX's mixed allocation. The conceptual implication is that sparse upcycling should be viewed as one endpoint of a continuum — appropriate when domain-specific data is not cleanly separable or when the target domains all overlap with the seed model's pretraining distribution — rather than as the default approach to initializing MoE models.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterize the compute-optimal allocation ratio between expert training and MoE finetuning as a function of the number of domains, domain diversity, and seed model scale.** The paper sweeps only the endpoints (0/100 and 100/0) and one interior point (~77/23 for three domains + generalist). The natural extension is a systematic sweep: for a fixed total GPU-day budget, vary the fraction spent on asynchronous expert training from 0% to 100% in increments of 10–20%, and measure downstream BTX performance. This would produce a curve analogous to the Chinchilla scaling laws but for continued pretraining compute allocation. The experiment should be repeated for different numbers of domains (2, 3, 5, 10), different domain diversities (all code-related vs. code + knowledge + multilingual), and different seed model scales (1B, 7B, 13B) to determine whether the optimal ratio is a function of these variables. A strong result would be a simple functional form (e.g., the optimal fraction spent on MoE finetuning scales as `1/√(number of domains)`) that practitioners could use without running their own sweep.

**Test whether BTX's benefits hold when the seed model has minimal exposure to the target domains — i.e., when continued pretraining teaches genuinely novel capabilities rather than refining existing ones.** Llama-2 7B was pretrained on a corpus that includes substantial math, code, and Wikipedia data; the domain experts are doing continued training on distributions the seed model has partially seen. A more stringent test would use a seed model pretrained exclusively on one distribution (e.g., English text with no code, or pre-2022 documents with no recent events) and then train BTX experts on out-of-distribution domains (code, post-2022 knowledge, a different language). This experiment would reveal whether BTX's attention-averaging assumption breaks when attention patterns need to adapt to structurally different inputs (code syntax, non-English word order), and whether the MoE finetuning stage can compensate even when the averaged attention initialization is poor. The paper's own data hints at this boundary: the Wikipedia expert, trained on only 42B tokens (much less than math/code), showed minimal catastrophic forgetting, suggesting the seed model already had strong Wikipedia-like representations. A domain where the seed model has near-zero capability would provide a cleaner test of BTX's ability to add genuinely new skills.

**Combine BTX with instruction finetuning or RLHF and measure whether the unified MoE architecture actually enables these downstream procedures, as the paper claims as a key advantage over BTM.** The paper's primary stated motivation for BTX over BTM is that BTM "makes it impossible to do further supervised finetuning (SFT) or reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)" (Section 1). This claim is entirely promissory — the paper never actually applies SFT or RLHF to the BTX model. A direct follow-up would take the BTX model (trained through 80B tokens of MoE finetuning), apply a standard instruction tuning procedure (e.g., the Llama-2 chat finetuning recipe), and evaluate on instruction-following benchmarks (AlpacaEval, MT-Bench, etc.). The key comparison is against: (a) applying the same SFT to the seed Llama-2 7B (does BTX's added domain expertise survive instruction tuning?), (b) applying SFT to the individual domain experts then using BTM-style inference-time ensembling (does BTX's unified architecture produce better instruction-following than fragmented experts?), and (c) applying SFT to a sparse-upcycled MoE model (does BTX's explicit domain specialization improve instruction-following in domain-specific contexts?). A negative result — e.g., instruction tuning erases the domain specialization that BTX's Branch & Train stage created — would significantly weaken the paper's central motivation.

**Investigate whether BTX experts can be added incrementally to an already-finetuned BTX model without full retraining.** The paper's current setup is a batch process: train all experts, merge once, finetune the unified model. In practice, new domains emerge after initial deployment. A follow-up experiment would take a finished BTX model (math + code + Wikipedia + generalist experts, already MoE-finetuned for 80B tokens), train a new domain expert (e.g., on medical text), add it as a fifth FF expert at each layer (expanding the router from 4-output to 5-output with the new output dimension randomly initialized), and then continue MoE finetuning on a data mixture that includes the new domain. The key measurements: (1) how many finetuning tokens are needed for the router to learn to use the new expert without forgetting its previous routing behavior? (2) does adding an expert degrade performance on previously-learned domains (catastrophic interference at the routing level)? (3) can the new expert be added without replaying the original expert training data (only training on the new domain's data during the incremental finetuning phase)? If incremental expert addition works with modest finetuning (e.g., 10–20B tokens), BTX becomes substantially more practical for evolving domain requirements. If it fails — e.g., the router collapses to only using the new expert, or the new expert degrades existing domain performance — then BTX is effectively limited to scenarios where the full set of target domains is known upfront.

**Determine whether BTX's routing behavior can be improved by initializing the router with domain-relevant priors rather than random weights.** The paper initializes all router weights `W_l` randomly before MoE finetuning. Given that the domain experts are explicitly trained on known domains, a natural alternative is to initialize the router such that it has a weak but non-random preference for the appropriate expert given domain-indicative features. For example, one could train a lightweight domain classifier on the seed model's hidden representations, and use its weights to initialize the router such that tokens containing math keywords (numbers, LaTeX, "theorem") initially route to the math expert, code keywords route to the code expert, and so on. This would test whether the router's learning problem is primarily one of refinement (starting from a good prior and adjusting) or exploration (needing to discover domain-expert associations from scratch). The paper's load balancing results (Table 5) show that the router easily falls into bad local optima (dead experts) without load balancing, suggesting the exploration problem is real. A good prior initialization might reduce or eliminate the need for load balancing, potentially preserving the math performance that load balancing sacrificed (−4.8 on GSM8K, Table 5) while still preventing dead experts.

**Stress-test the attention-averaging assumption by measuring the divergence of attention parameters across domain experts before averaging, and correlating this divergence with downstream BTX performance.** The paper assumes attention layers are domain-agnostic and can be safely averaged, but never quantifies how much attention parameters actually diverge during domain-specific training. A follow-up analysis would compute layer-wise parameter distance metrics (L2 distance, CKA similarity, angular distance) between the attention parameters of each domain expert and the seed model, and between pairs of domain experts, after the Branch & Train stage but before averaging. If attention parameters show substantial divergence (comparable to FF parameter divergence), it would challenge the paper's assumption and motivate ablations that keep attention domain-specific (e.g., making attention layers also MoE-routed, or using a shared attention with domain-specific adapters). If attention parameters show minimal divergence across all domains, the assumption is validated for the tested case. More interestingly, one could then test a set of deliberately diverse domains (different languages, different modalities via vision-language models) and measure whether attention divergence increases — this would establish a diagnostic for when the attention-averaging assumption breaks and BTX would need modification.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient domain adaptation for LLM deployment platforms:** An organization maintaining a general-purpose LLM API (e.g., for code generation, question answering, and text analysis) faces a tension between serving all use cases from a single large model (expensive at inference time, may not excel at any single domain) and maintaining separate specialized models (complex inference routing, models can't share context across domains). BTX offers a middle path: train domain experts on the organization's key verticals (code, math, knowledge, etc.) using embarrassingly parallel training on separate GPU clusters (potentially cheaper, lower-interconnect hardware), then merge them into a single MoE model with learned routing. The merged model has higher total parameters but lower active parameters per token than a dense model of equivalent capability — the paper shows BTX's 11.1B active parameters outperforming Llama-2 13B's 13B active parameters (47.9 vs. 45.4 average, Table 2) while using less total training compute (though the full-cost caveats from Section 6 apply). For an API platform, this means better performance at lower inference cost per token compared to scaling up a dense model. The key practical enabler is that the expert training phase can use cheaper, potentially preemptible compute without high-speed interconnects, while only the shorter MoE finetuning phase requires the expensive synchronized training infrastructure.

**Modular model development for organizations with siloed data or expertise:** Large organizations often have different teams with access to different proprietary datasets — a code team with internal repositories, a legal team with contract databases, a research team with scientific literature — that cannot be commingled due to access restrictions or data governance policies. BTX's Branch & Train phase allows each team to independently train a domain expert on their siloed data without sharing it across teams, using their own compute budgets. Only the final trained expert parameters need to be shared (not the training data), and the MiX phase integrates them into a single model. This is a concrete scenario where BTM fails: BTM can train experts on siloed data, but produces a fragmented system where the legal expert cannot benefit from the code expert's reasoning capabilities for contract analysis tasks. BTX's token-level routing allows the model to route a contract-analysis token to the legal expert and a following logical-reasoning token to the code or math expert within the same sequence. The paper's routing analysis (Table 6) shows exactly this cross-domain routing behavior — GSM8K math problems route many tokens to the Code and Llama-2 experts, not just the Math expert — demonstrating that the learned router already does cross-domain token mixing when beneficial.

**Specialized model compression via expert pruning for edge deployment:** The paper's finding (Table 5, "freeze experts" ablation) that domain expert FF parameters can be frozen during MoE finetuning with no performance loss suggests an intriguing deployment optimization. After BTX training, one could analyze the routing statistics for a target deployment domain (e.g., a mobile code assistant that only handles Python questions). If the router rarely or never selects certain experts for that domain, those experts' FF parameters can be pruned from the deployed model, reducing memory footprint and load time. The paper's routing analysis (Figure 3, top panel) shows that for code tasks, the Code expert dominates and the Wikipedia expert is rarely used (low routing probability in the later layers). For a code-only deployment, the Wikipedia expert could potentially be removed entirely, the Math expert might be pruned to half capacity, and the model would retain most of its code performance with substantially lower memory requirements. This is a direct consequence of BTX's explicit domain-to-expert mapping — unlike standard MoE where experts are not domain-aligned and cannot be pruned by domain, BTX experts have predictable domain associations that enable domain-conditional compression.

**Data mixture curation for continued pretraining at scale:** Organizations planning large-scale continued pretraining runs face a difficult data mixture design problem: how much of each domain's data should be included, and in what order, to maximize downstream performance without catastrophic forgetting? The paper's expert training results (Table 1) provide clean measurements of what happens when each domain is trained in isolation — the Math expert gains +24.8 on GSM8K but loses −21.4 on TriviaQA, the Code expert gains +18.9 on HumanEval but loses −28.6 on TriviaQA. These per-domain gain/loss curves could be used to forecast the outcome of different data mixture strategies. For example, if an organization knows that their deployment requires math performance above 30 on GSM8K and code performance above 25 on HumanEval, but can tolerate knowledge performance as low as 40 on TriviaQA, the expert training data tells them approximately how many tokens of math and code training are needed, and the BTX results tell them that the MoE integration stage can recover much of the knowledge loss (the final BTX achieves 57.1 on TriviaQA, close to the seed's 58.5, despite the domain experts' collapses). This turns BTX from a training recipe into a planning tool: the expert training stage provides clean per-domain scaling curves that are not confounded by multi-domain interference, and the MoE finetuning stage provides a predictable recovery of general capabilities.
