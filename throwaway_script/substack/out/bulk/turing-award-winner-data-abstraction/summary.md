Here are the takeaways from this Barbara Liskov interview (Turing Award winner for programming languages and distributed systems, pioneered data abstraction and Viewstamped Replication):

**Getting rejected from Princeton and the "happy accident" into computing**

Barbara Liskov finished her bachelor's in math at Berkeley and applied to several graduate programs — including Princeton, which sent back "a little card," a postcard that read, in effect, "we do not admit women." She'd known Princeton didn't take women undergraduates, but hadn't realized the exclusion extended to graduate school. Berkeley itself was co-ed; few women majored in math, but "the idea that a door was shut and women couldn't do things, that was not what went on at Berkeley." She did get into Berkeley for grad school but decided she wasn't ready for a math PhD and took a job instead — and the best offer she got was as a programmer. That pivot she calls "a happy accident." When academia finally opened to women years later (Title IX was about to pass), she was ready, but she didn't get there by waiting — she "just kept on marching along," taking a Mitre research job and using those four years to retool from AI into systems without the pressure of students and teaching.

**The software crisis and inventing data abstraction**

The problem of the early 1970s was that "people did not know how to build big programs that worked." Newspapers carried stories of companies spending millions of dollars and hundreds of man-years on software systems that had to be thrown away. The root cause: no practical modularity mechanism beyond procedures, and procedures "didn't match the kinds of modules you needed" — a file system or database isn't just a function you put something into and get something out of. People were talking about modularity but couldn't define what a module was. Papers debated how many lines a module should contain. "Just a chunk of code — that's not going to work."

At Mitre, Liskov had already built a small-team discipline to keep her programmers out of trouble: a module was a bunch of code with an interface of what she called **operations**, with everything inside hidden and its data inaccessible from outside. At some point the insight crystallized: this wasn't just a module pattern; it was a **data abstraction**. It could be a set, a sequence, whatever — "a new type of module." She says when she looks back at the papers of the era, "it's almost there, except people hadn't managed to pick it out." She describes it as "a time when an idea is ready and I happened to see it."

She and Steve Zilles wrote a paper sketching abstract data types and how a language could support them. It was "very impactful" in the research community. Then came **CLU**, her programming language, which embodied the ideas. CLU had clusters (what we'd later call classes) without inheritance — each cluster stood independently. Liskov decided not to productize CLU ("there wasn't an Internet yet" for distribution; companies in those days wanted a company behind a language). But the government put out a call for a language, which produced Ada — a language "explicitly being designed to have data abstraction in it." And in the 1990s, Java came along.

**What's wrong with Python (and why encapsulation mattered)**

Ryan asks about her remark that something is wrong with Python. The issue: Python has modules but lacks true encapsulation — "it allows code on the outside to muck around with what's going on on the inside." Encapsulation is crucial for making modularity work on large programs with many programmers, because "your team is really only as strong as your weakest programmer." Having the compiler enforce boundaries makes certain kinds of bad behavior impossible. She's careful to note Python was designed for other purposes (helping naive programmers learn quickly), and that she stopped working in programming languages long ago.

**The Liskov Substitution Principle — and the West Coast / East Coast divergence**

When she gave a keynote at the second OOPSLA conference (1986), she decided to actually read all the Smalltalk papers. What she found: Smalltalk had inheritance, where a subclass borrows from and tweaks an implementation. CLU didn't have that. More importantly, the object-oriented community was trying to define a "type hierarchy" but couldn't figure out what it meant — because they were focused on *implementations*, sometimes describing a class's behavior by explaining how its implementation differed from its superclass's. Liskov, meanwhile, had been teaching students to think in terms of specifications and meaning first ("the implementation is hidden inside and you don't worry about it very much"), in a class she co-developed with John Guttag at MIT. So when she read the OO papers, she saw the missing piece: **it has to do with behavior**. A subclass must behave like the superclass when used where the superclass is expected. That became the **Liskov Substitution Principle**. She didn't name it herself; in the 1990s, well after the fact, an email arrived asking "is this the correct interpretation of the Liskov substitution principle?" — and that's when she learned the name existed.

**Stepping into distributed computing (Argus and Viewstamped Replication)**

She read a paper by Bob Kahn (co-founder of the Internet with Vint Cerf) describing his "dream of distributed computing" — programs composed of pieces on different computers connected by a network — and noting that nobody knew how to build them. "I just thought, great problem." Her first distributed project was a programming language called **Argus**, strongly influenced by CLU. It had a special kind of object called a **guardian** — a module residing at a single computer — and guardians communicated through remote procedure calls. A key problem: a computation that starts at one guardian and fans out across the network must either complete entirely or have no effect at all. She borrowed the notion of **transactions** from the database world and made Argus run computations as atomic, distributed transactions.

That work led directly to **Viewstamped Replication** — the beginning of cloud storage. She was thinking of a file system, but the abstraction was general: how do you store data across multiple sites with correct behavior and continuous availability as long as enough nodes are up? The "viewstamp" was an incrementing number (view 25, then 26, etc.) for noticing when nodes failed and determining which ones held the most recent state. It had nothing to do with clocks — just counters passed around.

**Paxos vs. Viewstamped Replication: same idea, different explanations**

Leslie Lamport independently developed **Paxos**, and the two protocols are "essentially the same idea." The big advance they both captured: in transactions, there's a leader that commits and polls everyone; if the leader fails, you have a problem. Both Viewstamped Replication and Paxos solved how to move to a new leader when the old one fails. The protocols only handled crashes, not malice. The first real-world use Liskov is aware of was the Google file system — a former student working as a Google consultant told her, "that's Viewstamped Replication." The Google team called it Paxos; "in fact they are the same system." Why did Paxos become more famous? Lamport himself said, "I went around giving all the talks and she implemented it." Lamport gave many talks, wrote multiple papers, and "Paxos" is a catchier name. Liskov took it in stride.

Later, a DARPA request for proposals raised the problem of **Byzantine attacks** — nodes that look legitimate but have been compromised. Her student Miguel Castro asked: "why don't we see whether we can come up with a protocol that works in the presence of Byzantine attacks?" That became **Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance**, Castro's PhD thesis. (Lamport coined the term "Byzantine" as well.)

**Dijkstra, "Go To Statement Considered Harmful," and why it was controversial**

Ryan asks about Dijkstra and the famous "Go To Statement Considered Harmful." Liskov clarifies: it wasn't a paper — it was a letter to the editor of *Communications of the ACM* — but it was enormously impactful. Dijkstra's real point was about how hard it is to reason about code correctness, at a time when computer science was "kind of dismissed as nothing much" and "anybody can write code." Go Tos, he argued, made reasoning harder. The controversy stemmed from several factors: people writing in assembler had no choice; programming languages lacked modern control-flow constructs; compilers didn't optimize the way they do now, so there was a fear that avoiding Go Tos would hurt efficiency; and "people who used Go Tos and wrote really good code were offended that Dijkstra was saying your code is bad." Dijkstra "was not the most diplomatic person," she says, and he didn't write it gently. But "clearly Dijkstra won the day." She met him many times: "not always as tactful as he might be," but "a very distinguished researcher."

**Why academia, and how teaching and research connect**

She stayed in academia because she loves research and working with students. Teaching was never her favorite, but she felt **teaching and research were very closely connected**: when you teach, you must teach from first principles, and that's the same deep understanding of assumptions and mechanisms that good research demands. Being a professor was "a gift and a curse": the gift is you can do whatever you want; the curse is you have to figure out what to do. That freedom let her career zigzag — "I would switch to something else. I had the freedom to do that." What stops you from going in a useless direction? "You won't get tenure." The ultimate judge is the research community's view of your work. She did try a startup briefly in the late 1990s and didn't like it. In her era, professors forming companies wasn't the norm.

Her advice for picking research directions: don't do incremental work. Find a problem that's amenable to a solution and matches your skill set, and recognize when you're going in a good direction. But she also emphasizes luck and not letting setbacks stop you. When she finished her PhD, she got no good faculty offers and went back to Mitre — a move that gave her four years to transition from AI to systems. "I just kept on marching along." Many people's careers, she notes, follow a pattern of doors opening and closing; you decide whether to step through.

**"Why did she get the Turing Award?" — the backlash as a backhanded compliment**

When she won the Turing Award, her husband went online and found people asking, essentially, "why her?" She suspects other recipients faced similar noise if they'd looked — "the Internet is not necessarily nice." But she also thinks the reaction stemmed from how foundational her work had become. Her own graduate students didn't realize data abstraction hadn't always existed; the whole mindset of modular design felt so baked into the practice of programming that people forgot there was a "before." Byzantine fault tolerance, by contrast, is a protocol — you can point to it and say "somebody invented that protocol." The work on data abstraction was more fundamental: it was "the whole mindset you had about how to develop programs." The criticism, she says, is actually "a huge compliment" — an unwitting statement about how completely the world had absorbed the ideas she and her contemporaries built. Programmers today have never known a world without modularity, and that's the point.

---

Her career traces a through-line of **seeing what's almost there but not yet named** — data abstraction was implicit in the code-organizing habits she'd developed at Mitre, and the Liskov Substitution Principle emerged only because she approached type hierarchies from specifications rather than implementations. That same pattern of extracting a clean concept from a messy practice repeats in her distributed-systems work, where the leader-election mechanism at the heart of Viewstamped Replication came from noticing what transactions *didn't* solve. Her **"gift and a curse" framing** captures the engine underneath all these shifts: the freedom to zigzag — from AI to systems to languages to distributed computing — required constantly finding the next problem worth solving, a discipline she sharpened by **teaching from first principles** and by submitting everything to the research community's judgment. The postcard from Princeton — a door shut — becomes, in retrospect, the first of many moments where she simply "kept on marching along" through a door that was open instead, and then built the concepts that made the next door visible.
