Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay on the tech lead role (a detailed breakdown of the playbook and mindset for the tech lead archetype in software engineering):

**Setting the direction**

The first responsibility of a tech lead is figuring out *where the team should go and why*. Sometimes the problem is handed down by the management chain, and other times the tech lead identifies the opportunity themselves—what Ryan calls **"creating scope."** The core task is to take an impactful problem and formulate a high-level plan to address it. A hallmark of a tech lead who excels at this phase is having "many more project ideas than they have hands for," which creates a healthy pipeline of work and leads naturally to the next step of staffing. This surplus of direction is a deliberate strategy: it ensures the team never stalls waiting for the next thing to build.

**Getting alignment and staffing projects**

With a direction set, the tech lead must get other people involved to actually land the projects. This requires aligning with others on the importance of the work and influencing them to take it on. Ryan identifies two primary channels for finding staffing. The first is working with engineering managers (EMs): if the proposed work impacts something an EM already cares about, they can help allocate engineers. The second is talking to engineers directly: if the work represents an interesting technical challenge or a good career-development opportunity for an individual contributor, they might volunteer to take on the project. In this phase, a good tech lead operates as a peer to the surrounding EMs and actively helps navigate staffing and prioritization discussions rather than sitting back and waiting to be assigned resources. The dynamic is collaborative, not hierarchical—the tech lead is a partner to management, not a subordinate awaiting instructions.

**Executing the roadmap**

Ryan describes this as the “easy” part—with air quotes, because of course the projects are hard and there is an immense amount of work required to get the job done. But the nature of the work shifts: there is "much less ambiguity" at this stage. It becomes a matter of execution. A good tech lead does whatever is necessary for the overall initiative to succeed, which means their activities span a wide variety of modes. They write code, focusing on completing the highest-leverage or riskiest changes. They resolve technical ambiguity by scoping out tasks and designs so that other engineers can easily pick up the work. They uplift others by unblocking stuck work, spreading knowledge, building tooling, or mentoring. They handle project management—leading workstream syncs, tracking progress, and shifting resources toward at-risk areas. And they manage workstream communications, keeping stakeholders informed on progress and increasing the visibility of the workstream itself. The unifying thread is **whatever it takes**: the tech lead fills every gap the project demands rather than clinging to a narrow job description.

**The core reminders: code and communication**

As an engineer transitions into a tech lead role, Ryan flags two things to keep in mind that will determine success. First, "tech leads should still write code." It is okay to write *less* code, but they must never lose touch with the codebase and the existing system. That deep technical context is "what sets a good tech lead apart from an engineering manager." Staying hands-on preserves the credibility and intuition needed to make sound technical decisions under pressure. Second, "good communication is a superpower." Tech leads rely on their writing and speaking to have influence; the better they are at communication, the easier it is to get the ambiguous, directional work done that defines the first phase of the playbook. Influence flows through clarity, and clarity is a product of deliberate communication practice.

**The visibility pitfall**

There is a common pitfall that comes from a piece of viral-tweet advice Ryan quotes: "Bad tech leads make a ton of noise and try to claim credit regardless of the impact that they have." While visibility is genuinely good, self-promotion is never a substitute for high-quality work. The tech lead role should emerge naturally from the needs of the workstream, not from a personal desire to assert yourself over others. The north star remains impact. Ryan warns against mistaking noise for leadership—the goal is not to be seen leading, but to lead in a way that produces undeniable results.

---

Ryan’s entire playbook orbits a single tension: **creating scope** versus simply occupying it. The tech lead who stockpiles more ideas than hands and then navigates the peer-level negotiations with EMs and ICs to staff them is practicing a form of leadership that is earned, not appointed. His insistence that tech leads must still write code—paired with the elevation of communication to a **superpower**—draws a sharp line between the tech lead archetype and both the pure manager and the isolated senior IC. The warning against the "bad tech lead" who makes noise without impact closes the loop: the playbook works only when the visibility is a side effect of the work, never its purpose.
