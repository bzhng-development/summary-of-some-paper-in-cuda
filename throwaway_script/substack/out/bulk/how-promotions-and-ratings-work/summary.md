Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (explains the behind-closed-doors calibration process that decides your performance rating and promotion in big tech):

**The performance review and calibration process**

In big tech, your rating is not decided by your manager alone. It is decided collectively by the managers and top engineers (Staff+) who are the recursive reports of your senior director. The process has four stages: first, you write a self-review, an exhaustive list of all the impact you’ve had in the last 6–12 months. Second, the peers you requested write and submit their feedback. Third, your manager aggregates everything and proposes a rating for you. Fourth, and most critically, comes the **performance discussion** (or “calibrations”), where your manager presents your review to the group of managers who make the final call. Your rating can change from what your manager proposed during that session. The promotion process is similar but uses longer packets that look across all your past work, not just the recent half, and the discussion centers on whether you have demonstrated the behaviors of the next level.

**Your manager is the messenger, not the decider**

The first major takeaway is that your manager doesn’t have full control. Ryan remembers being confused when his manager couldn’t tell him what rating he was on track for. “Now I understand it’s because they don’t know for sure.” A manager can give an “uncalibrated” opinion, but without hearing what the forum of managers thinks, it’s difficult to say. In practice, the more experienced your manager is, the more accurate their uncalibrated opinions will be, and they should be able to give you a general sense of your performance in advance. After calibrations, the real work with your manager is understanding the feedback from the forum—their pushback reveals the gaps you have to the next level.

**The power of high-level vouching**

It’s enormously helpful to have higher-up engineers and managers vouching for you before you ever enter the room. If the people in the calibration sessions already think highly of your work, your manager will face less pushback when presenting it, and “the higher up the people vouching for you are, the more influence their opinion will have.” This is the mechanism behind the evergreen advice to be visible. Aside from visibility to managers, it’s also great if you work with top engineers; if they can vouch for your technical skills, that’s a powerful supporting signal during promotion conversations.

**Writing self-reviews for the calibration audience**

Understanding the process changes how you write. Knowing that your “brag list” will be read by a forum of managers—not just your own manager—should make your writing more effective. Ryan didn’t know any of this until a few years in, and he was less calculated with his career growth early on, “just worked a lot of hours without a lot of focus.” Once you know the process, everything is smoother because it helps you work *with* your manager on your career growth rather than guessing what matters.

---

Ryan’s framing centers on one controlling idea: treat the calibration room—not your 1:1—as the true audience for your impact. His **manager-as-messenger** stance and emphasis on **high-level vouching** both flow from that insight; they recast “being visible” not as self-promotion but as pre-seeding consensus in the only forum that actually decides your rating. That same logic extends to the self-review: once you know a group of managers will read it cold, the writing tightens from a personal journal into a case built for strangers.
