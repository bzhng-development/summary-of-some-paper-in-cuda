Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay (a reflection on learning through apprenticeship, late-night incident response, and the debt owed to exceptional teammates):

**Learning from the engineers around you**

Ryan opens with a scene from a late-night production incident. The team was debugging with no clear cause, chasing theories that all led to dead ends. Then someone in the chat linked an unexpected query that traced the breakage to a seemingly unrelated service no one had considered. That same engineer provided a quick fix that resolved the incident. The moment stuck with him not just because the day was saved, but because it crystallized a pattern of learning that only happens in close collaboration: "There are so many things you pick up by working with strong engineers that you can't learn anywhere else."

From that specific incident, he absorbed two things directly—an unintuitive relationship between services, and a diagnostic tool he'd use for future incidents—but the deeper lesson was about the transmission of **tribal knowledge** that no documentation or course can provide.

**Behaviors you absorb by proximity**

Knowledge of systems is one thing; ways of working are another. Ryan notes that when you work closely with other engineers, "you see what actions they take and why." You absorb not just their conclusions but the process that led to them—how they narrow a search space, when they decide to escalate vs. dig deeper, what questions they ask first. These behaviors, he argues, are "also difficult to learn anywhere else." The medium of learning is apprenticeship, not instruction.

**Responsiveness as a force multiplier**

Skill alone isn't what makes the collaboration productive—it's skill paired with availability and work ethic. Ryan reflects that he "got lucky" on this front: "When I worked late into the night, my top teammates were right there with me discussing things in real time." That responsiveness collapsed the feedback loop from what "could have been days down to minutes," which dramatically accelerated his learning. He frames collaborations with such engineers as a privilege: "Work hard and never let these opportunities go to waste. If you do it right, you'll learn skills fast that will serve you for the rest of your career."

He closes by naming two engineers directly—Haixia Shi and Runshen Zhu—calling them "two of the strongest engineers I've worked with who have taught me much of what I know," and expressing lasting gratitude for "the late-night collaborations and fun I had building systems together." The essay is, in effect, a dedication to the mentors and peers whose proximity shaped his growth.
