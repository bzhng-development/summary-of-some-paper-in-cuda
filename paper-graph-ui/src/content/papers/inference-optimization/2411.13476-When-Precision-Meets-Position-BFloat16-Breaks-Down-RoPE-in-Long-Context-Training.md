# When Precision Meets Position: BFloat16 Breaks Down RoPE in Long-Context Training

**ArXiv:** [2411.13476](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.13476)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper identifies and analyzes a critical numerical issue where **Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE)** combined with BFloat16 precision breaks RoPE's intended relative positional encoding properties, particularly in long-context training scenarios—with the first token contributing most significantly to this deviation and the problem worsening as context length increases.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper identifies and analyzes a critical numerical issue where **Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE)** combined with BFloat16 precision breaks RoPE's intended relative positional encoding properties, particularly in long-context training scenarios—with the first token contributing most significantly to this deviation and the problem worsening as context length increases. To address this, the paper proposes **AnchorAttention**, a plug-and-play attention method that treats the first token as a shared anchor with a consistent position ID visible to all documents within the training context (while keeping tokens from different documents invisible to each other), thereby eliminating positional inconsistencies, reducing unnecessary attention computations, and preventing the rolling accumulation of numerical errors. Experiments on LLaMA-2-7B, LLaMA-3-8B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, and Qwen-1.5-1.8B demonstrate that AnchorAttention consistently outperforms both full attention and standard intra-document attention on the RULER long-context benchmark across lengths from 8K to 128K tokens, while reducing training time by over 50% compared to standard full attention and largely preserving general capabilities on MMLU and HellaSwag, establishing that long-context training can be made both more accurate and more efficient when the first token's positional role is explicitly anchored rather than left to accumulate precision errors.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RoPE's Relative Positional Properties Break Under BFloat16

The fundamental problem this paper tackles sits at the intersection of two ubiquitous choices in modern LLM training: **Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE)** as the positional encoding scheme, and **BFloat16** as the numerical precision format. Individually, each is well-justified and widely adopted. RoPE (Su et al., 2021) has become the de facto standard for positional encoding in LLMs—used in LLaMA, LLaMA-2, LLaMA-3, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma, and Phi-3—because its rotational formulation naturally encodes *relative* positional information (the attention between tokens at positions $i$ and $j$ depends only on $j - i$, not on absolute positions). This relative property is what enables long-context extension techniques like NTK-aware scaling (LocalLLaMA, 2023), YaRN (Peng et al., 2023), and positional interpolation (Chen et al., 2023a) to work: by adjusting rotary frequencies, out-of-distribution position IDs in extended contexts can be mapped back to in-distribution ranges the model has seen during pretraining, allowing adaptation to longer sequences with minimal additional training.

BFloat16 (Wang & Kanwar, 2019) is equally pervasive because it halves memory bandwidth requirements compared to Float32 while maintaining sufficient dynamic range for most deep learning operations (Kalamkar et al., 2019). For long-context training—where self-attention's quadratic memory cost is the primary bottleneck—BFloat16 is not merely a convenience but a practical necessity to fit sequences of 64K to 128K tokens on current GPU hardware (Xiong et al., 2023).

The paper's starting observation is that **these two choices interact destructively**: when RoPE's rotation matrices are applied in Float32 (outside FlashAttention2, as Dao, 2024 requires) but the key-query inner product is computed in BFloat16 (inside FlashAttention2), the precision loss breaks RoPE's theoretical guarantee that adding a constant positional shift $\Delta$ to every position index should leave attention scores unchanged. Formally, RoPE promises:

$$A_{(i+\Delta)(j+\Delta)} = (R_{i+\Delta,\theta} q_i)^\top (R_{j+\Delta,\theta} k_j) = q_i^\top R_{j-i,\theta} k_j = A_{ij}$$

This invariance is not just a mathematical curiosity—it is the mechanism by which RoPE enables models to recognize the same relative distances regardless of where they appear in the sequence. If it breaks, then the same pair of tokens at relative distance $m$ produce different attention scores depending on their absolute positions in the context window, undermining the very property that makes long-context extension feasible.

The paper's empirical measurement of this breakdown is striking: Figure 1 (left) shows that under BFloat16, applying a positional shift of $\Delta_1 = 0$ versus $\Delta_1 = 50$ (with $\Delta_2 = 16$ fixed) produces a substantial cumulative attention difference $D$ across all layers and heads in a pretrained LLaMA-2-7B model. This difference is **absent under Float32** and **amplified by pretraining** (the pretrained model shows larger discrepancies than randomly initialized weights, suggesting that training reinforces patterns that exploit or exacerbate the precision artifacts). Figure 1 (middle) pinpoints the source: **the first token accounts for the vast majority of the attention discrepancy**—when the first token is excluded, the remaining tokens largely preserve the relative positional property. Figure 1 (right) shows that this effect **worsens with sequence length**: as context length grows from 64 to 8192 tokens, the attention logit difference for the first token increases steadily, indicating cumulative numerical error.

### Why This Problem Matters: The Long-Context Training Bottleneck

The practical significance of this finding cannot be overstated. The field is in the midst of a rapid push toward longer context windows—from 4K to 32K, 128K, and even 1M+ tokens—driven by applications that demand processing entire codebases (Jimenez et al., 2024), multi-document question answering (Wang et al., 2024a), many-shot in-context learning (Agarwal et al., 2024), and coherent long-form generation (Mazumder & Liu, 2022). However, extending context windows is not a free lunch. The quadratic computational cost of self-attention means that training on 128K tokens costs approximately $(128K/4K)^2 = 1024\times$ more attention FLOPs per sequence than training on the original 4K window. This has led to a bifurcation in how long-context capabilities are achieved:

1. **Training-free methods** (Xiao et al., 2023; Han et al., 2023; Ruoss et al., 2023) attempt to extend context at inference time without additional training, but recent work (Fu et al., 2024) shows these approaches significantly underperform dedicated long-context training.

2. **Continued pretraining on long contexts** (Fu et al., 2024; Xiong et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2024) yields much better results but is computationally prohibitive if done naïvely with full attention.

The paper's discovery that BFloat16 degrades RoPE's relative properties introduces a compounding problem: **not only is long-context training expensive, but the precision format required to make it affordable actively undermines the positional encoding mechanism that makes it work**. This creates a tension between computational efficiency and model quality that the field has not explicitly recognized. Practitioners training long-context models may be unknowingly fighting against their own numerical precision, producing models whose positional understanding is corrupted in ways that worsen with the very context lengths they are trying to support.

The identification of the **first token** as the primary source of this breakdown is particularly significant because it connects to a broader observation in the literature about "attention sinks" and "massive activations." Xiao et al. (2023) and subsequent work (Han et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024a) have documented that LLMs allocate disproportionate attention to initial tokens (especially the BOS token), which serve as a bias term or "sink" for excess attention scores. The paper's finding that the first token is also the locus of RoPE precision breakdown suggests these phenomena may be mechanistically related—or at minimum, that the first token plays a dual role (positional anchor and attention sink) that makes it uniquely vulnerable to numerical issues.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations in existing approaches along several axes:

**Training-free extension methods address length generalization, not precision-induced corruption.** Methods like Position Interpolation (Chen et al., 2023a), NTK-aware scaling (LocalLLaMA, 2023), and YaRN (Peng et al., 2023) modify RoPE's frequency basis to map extended position IDs into ranges the model has seen during pretraining. These approaches assume RoPE's relative positional encoding is functioning correctly—they adjust *which* rotation angles the model sees, not whether those rotations accurately encode relative distances. If BFloat16 breaks the relative property itself, then frequency scaling is solving a different problem (out-of-distribution position IDs) while leaving the precision problem unaddressed. This explains why even well-tuned RoPE frequency scaling may yield suboptimal long-context performance: the foundation it builds on is numerically unstable.

**Full attention training on long contexts is accurate but prohibitively expensive.** Training with full bidirectional (or causal) attention on long sequences preserves all positional relationships but at quadratic cost. With BFloat16 precision, each additional token not only increases the attention computation but also amplifies the cumulative numerical error on the first token (as shown in Figure 1, right). This means full attention with BFloat16 on very long sequences is simultaneously the most expensive approach *and* the one most vulnerable to precision degradation—a worst-of-both-worlds scenario.

**Intra-document attention (Zhao et al., 2024b; Gao et al., 2024) reduces computation but introduces positional inconsistencies.** Intra-document attention masks out cross-document attention, effectively partitioning a long sequence into multiple shorter ones. This reduces the effective sequence length for attention computation, which should mitigate the precision problem (since shorter sequences accumulate less error). However, the paper identifies a subtle flaw: in the conventional implementation (Figure 2, Left), position IDs are assigned continuously from the start to the end of the concatenated sequence. This means the first token of the *second* document receives a non-zero position ID (e.g., position 512 if the first document is 512 tokens long), and the first token of the *third* document receives yet another position ID. Since the first token's position ID is the primary source of precision-induced deviation, these **inconsistent first-token positions across documents** confuse the model. The model learns during pretraining that "position 0" (the BOS token) has specific properties, but under intra-document attention with continuous position IDs, documents after the first one have their initial tokens at arbitrary positions, breaking this learned association.

The paper's experiment (Figure 3) validates this diagnosis: simply **resetting position IDs to 1 at the start of each document** within intra-document attention consistently improves long-context performance on the RULER benchmark. This is a minimal change that should theoretically make no difference under perfect RoPE (since relative distances are preserved regardless of absolute position reset), yet it produces measurable gains. This result provides strong evidence that BFloat16 precision issues are not merely an analytical curiosity but have **concrete, performance-limiting effects** on long-context capabilities.

**Resetting position IDs creates a new problem: incomplete rotational frequency coverage.** The paper identifies a significant drawback of the reset approach: the model can only learn the full spectrum of RoPE rotation angles when processing sequences that reach or exceed the maximum context length. With position IDs reset per document, the model never sees position IDs spanning the full 128K range unless individual documents are themselves very long—which is rare in natural datasets (the paper notes the scarcity of such lengthy data). This means the model never encounters the high-frequency rotations corresponding to large absolute positions, potentially limiting its ability to generalize to arbitrarily long contexts. This creates a tension between positional consistency (which favors resetting) and full rotational coverage (which favors continuous IDs).

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions AnchorAttention as a resolution to this tension. Rather than choosing between continuous position IDs (which provide full rotational coverage but inconsistent first-token positions) and reset position IDs (which provide consistency but limit rotational coverage), AnchorAttention introduces a third option: **treat the first token as a shared anchor with a fixed position ID visible to all documents, while keeping inter-document attention masked and using continuous position IDs for all other tokens** (Figure 2, Right).

This design directly addresses each identified problem:

- **First-token precision error**: By making a single shared anchor token (the BOS token) serve as the first token for all documents, there is only one "position 0" in the entire sequence, eliminating the inconsistency that arises when different documents' first tokens occupy different absolute positions. Any precision errors that accumulate on this anchor are concentrated on a token that carries minimal semantic content (BOS tokens are typically not meaningful).

- **Cumulative error with sequence length**: By maintaining cross-document masking, the effective attention computation per document does not grow with the total concatenated sequence length. Each document only attends to itself and the shared anchor, so the numerical error doesn't accumulate across documents. The anchor token itself is a single position regardless of total context length.

- **Full rotational coverage**: Because position IDs are continuous from the anchor through all documents (without resetting), the full range of rotation angles up to the maximum context length is exercised during training, even if individual documents are short. A 128K sequence might contain many short documents, but their cumulative position IDs span the full range, ensuring all frequencies are trained.

- **Computational efficiency**: The cross-document masking means AnchorAttention computes attention over $O(\sum L_d^2)$ rather than $O((\sum L_d)^2)$, where $L_d$ are individual document lengths. For a sequence composed of many short documents, this is substantially less than full attention over the entire sequence, yielding the >50% training time reduction reported in Figure 6.

The paper also makes a broader methodological contribution by identifying the **evaluation gap** in long-context research. Existing benchmarks like LongBench (Bai et al., 2023) can be adequately addressed with 16K context—not long enough to stress-test 64K or 128K models. Perplexity, the traditional metric for language model quality, is shown to be poorly correlated with long-context capability: Figure 4 demonstrates that PPL plateaus early in training while RULER performance continues to improve. The paper recommends (1) using the RULER benchmark (Hsieh et al., 2024) which specifically targets long-range dependencies up to 128K tokens, and (2) averaging performance over the last several training checkpoints rather than reporting a single best checkpoint, to avoid cherry-picking and to account for training variance.

### Connection to the Executive Summary

The executive summary establishes that AnchorAttention reduces training time by over 50% and improves long-context performance across multiple model architectures. The context above explains *why* these improvements are possible: AnchorAttention is not merely a sparse attention pattern but a principled response to a specific numerical pathology at the intersection of RoPE and BFloat16. The design choices—shared anchor, continuous IDs, cross-document masking—each follow directly from the diagnosis that the first token's position ID inconsistency is the root cause of RoPE's breakdown under limited precision. Understanding this chain of reasoning is essential because it reveals that AnchorAttention's effectiveness is not coincidental or task-specific; it addresses a fundamental precision issue that affects all RoPE-based models trained in BFloat16, regardless of architecture or dataset.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper builds a **long-context training system** centered on AnchorAttention, a modified attention mechanism that replaces full self-attention during continued pretraining. AnchorAttention solves the problem that BFloat16 precision breaks RoPE's relative positional encoding—particularly through inconsistent first-token position IDs across documents—by introducing a shared anchor token with a fixed position ID visible to all documents, while keeping tokens from different documents masked from each other and using continuous position IDs throughout the full sequence. The "shape" of the solution is a sparse attention pattern that creates a star topology: one central anchor node connected to every document node, with no edges between different document nodes, yielding both correctness (consistent positional references) and efficiency (fewer attention computations than full attention).

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components:

1. **Base Pretrained LLM** (LLaMA-2-7B, LLaMA-3-8B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, or Qwen-1.5-1.8B) — a decoder-only Transformer using RoPE with BFloat16 precision. This model serves as the starting point before long-context continued training.

2. **Data Pipeline with Long-Context Sequences** — raw documents from SlimPajama (or an upsampled variant) are concatenated into sequences of 64K or 128K tokens. Each document maintains its original internal token order, and a shared BOS token is placed at the sequence start. In AnchorAttention, this BOS token becomes the shared anchor.

3. **AnchorAttention Mechanism** — a drop-in replacement for standard causal self-attention that implements three rules: (a) the anchor token (position 0) is visible to every token in every document, (b) tokens within the same document attend to each other causally, (c) tokens in different documents cannot attend to each other. Position IDs are assigned continuously from the anchor through all documents without resetting, so a 128K sequence exercises the full rotational frequency spectrum.

4. **Training Loop with RoPE Base Adjustment** — standard continued pretraining using the AdamW optimizer for 2000 steps on approximately 2 billion tokens. The RoPE base frequency (θ_base) is scaled with context length: 1M for 32K, 5M for 64K, 10M for 128K, and 50M for 256K contexts.

Information flows as follows: pretrained checkpoint → long-context data concatenation with anchor → AnchorAttention computes sparse attention pattern → standard autoregressive language modeling loss → updated model weights. At evaluation time, the trained model is tested on RULER (up to 128K tokens), LongBench (medium context), MMLU, and HellaSwag (short context) to verify that long-context capability improves without degrading general abilities.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the root cause analysis: the formal definition of RoPE and the precision breakdown when BFloat16 is used inside FlashAttention2, including the attention difference metric `$D$` and the per-token logit difference metric `$D_{\text{logit}}$`. This establishes *why* AnchorAttention's design choices are motivated.

- **Second**, the observation that the first token is the primary source of deviation and that resetting position IDs per document improves performance. This motivates the anchor concept and explains why alternative approaches (full attention, standard intra-document attention) are insufficient.

- **Third**, AnchorAttention itself: the attention mask pattern, the shared anchor token with fixed position ID, the continuous position ID assignment strategy, and how these three design elements together resolve the precision problem while maintaining full rotational frequency coverage.

- **Fourth**, the training protocol: base model selection, dataset construction (including upsampling details), RoPE base frequency optimization, and hyperparameter choices. This is where the paper's practical recommendations for long-context training are grounded.

- **Fifth**, the evaluation methodology: why RULER is chosen over perplexity and LongBench, the recommendation to average over multiple checkpoints, and which tasks are excluded (NIAH Multikey 3 and Common Word Extraction for LLaMA-2-7B) and why.

- **Sixth**, the infrastructure implementation: AnchorContext, the two computational engine options (FlexAttention and FlashAttention2), numerical accuracy verification, and the sequence parallelism strategy that enables the >50% training speedup.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **diagnosis-and-fix paper** whose core idea is that BFloat16 precision corrupts RoPE's relative positional encoding through the first token's position ID, and that a simple architectural intervention—making the first token a shared anchor with cross-document masking—eliminates this corruption while simultaneously reducing computational cost and preserving full rotational frequency coverage.

#### The Precision Problem: How BFloat16 Breaks RoPE

**RoPE's theoretical invariance.** RoPE encodes positional information by rotating query and key vectors according to their absolute positions before computing their inner product. For a token at position `$i$`, the rotation matrix `$R_{i,\theta}$` is a block-diagonal matrix where each `$2 \times 2$` block rotates the corresponding pair of dimensions by an angle `$i \cdot \theta_k$`, with `$\theta_k = \text{base}^{-2k/d}$` for the `$k$`-th frequency and hidden dimension `$d$`. The attention logit between a query at position `$i$` and a key at position `$j$` is:

$$A_{ij} = (R_{i,\theta} q_i)^\top (R_{j,\theta} k_j)$$

where `$q_i = W_Q x_i$` is the query vector for the `$i$`-th token, `$k_j = W_K x_j$` is the key vector for the `$j$`-th token, `$W_Q, W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` are learned projection matrices, and `$x_i, x_j$` are the token embeddings at positions `$i$` and `$j$`.

**What this computes:** the unnormalized attention score between tokens `$i$` and `$j$`, with positional information injected by rotating each vector before taking the dot product. The rotation angles depend on the absolute positions `$i$` and `$j$`.

**Why this form:** RoPE is designed so that the product `$R_{i,\theta}^\top R_{j,\theta}$` simplifies to `$R_{j-i,\theta}$`, a rotation matrix that depends only on the relative distance `$m = j - i$`. This is the key property:

$$A_{ij} = q_i^\top R_{i,\theta}^\top R_{j,\theta} k_j = q_i^\top R_{j-i,\theta} k_j$$

This means the attention mechanism sees only relative distances, not absolute positions—exactly what is needed for length generalization, since the model can recognize that tokens 100 positions apart have the same relationship regardless of where that gap occurs in the sequence.

**The invariance under positional shifts.** A direct consequence of relative encoding is that adding a constant shift `$\Delta$` to every position index should leave all attention scores unchanged:

$$A_{(i+\Delta)(j+\Delta)} = (R_{i+\Delta,\theta} q_i)^\top (R_{j+\Delta,\theta} k_j) = q_i^\top R_{(j+\Delta)-(i+\Delta),\theta} k_j = q_i^\top R_{j-i,\theta} k_j = A_{ij}$$

If position IDs were `$[0, 1, 2, ..., L-1]$` and we shift them to `$[\Delta, \Delta+1, \Delta+2, ..., \Delta+L-1]$`, the attention scores should be identical.

**Where the precision breakdown occurs.** The rotation matrices are applied *outside* FlashAttention2 using Float32 precision. However, the inner product `$(R_{i,\theta} q_i)^\top (R_{j,\theta} k_j)$` is computed *inside* FlashAttention2, which requires BFloat16. The BFloat16 format allocates 16 bits: 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, and 7 mantissa bits. This provides only about 2-3 decimal digits of precision in the mantissa. When computing `$q_i^\top R_{j-i,\theta} k_j$`, the rotation matrix multiplication introduces small floating-point errors that depend on the absolute magnitude of the rotation angles. Since the rotation angles `$i \cdot \theta_k$` grow with the absolute position `$i$`, the errors are position-dependent, violating the invariance:

$$A_{(i+\Delta)(j+\Delta)} \neq A_{ij} \quad \text{under BFloat16}$$

The paper formalizes this deviation measurement with an **attention difference metric**:

$$D(X, \Delta_1, \Delta_2) = \sum_{l,h} \sum_{j=1}^{L} \left[ n \odot \left( \sum_{i=1}^{L} \left| \text{ATTN}_{i,j}^{l,h}(X, \Delta_1) - \text{ATTN}_{i,j}^{l,h}(X, \Delta_2) \right| \right) \right]$$

where `$\text{ATTN}_{i,j}^{l,h}(X, \Delta)$` is the post-softmax attention score from token `$j$` to token `$i$` in the `$h$`-th head of the `$l$`-th layer when a global positional shift `$\Delta$` is applied to all position indices of input sequence `$X$`, `$L$` is the sequence length, and `$n = [1/L, 1/(L-1), ..., 1]$` is a normalization vector that accounts for the varying number of valid attention positions due to causal masking (token `$j$` can only attend to tokens `$i \leq j$`). The summation runs over all `$l$` layers, all `$h$` heads per layer, and all `$j$` key positions.

**What it computes:** the total deviation in the attention pattern across the entire model when the same input sequence is processed with two different global positional shifts. If RoPE's relative property holds perfectly, `$D = 0$` for any `$\Delta_1$` and `$\Delta_2$`. Any non-zero value indicates that absolute position is leaking into the attention computation. The normalization vector `$n$` ensures that each row `$j$` of the attention matrix contributes equally on average, compensating for the triangular shape of causal attention.

**Why this form:** summing absolute differences across all attention scores captures both the magnitude and the pervasiveness of the deviation. The layer-and-head summation aggregates effects that may be distributed across the model. The normalization vector prevents early tokens (which have fewer valid attention targets due to causal masking) from being under-weighted relative to later tokens.

**The per-token attention logit difference.** To identify *which* tokens contribute most to the deviation, the paper defines a second metric that isolates individual token positions without the confound of the softmax nonlinearity:

$$D_{\text{logit}} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{l,h} \sum_{i=1}^{T} \left| A_{i,j=1}^{l,h}(\Delta_1) - A_{i,j=1}^{l,h}(\Delta_2) \right|$$

where `$A_{i,j=1}^{l,h}(\Delta)$` is the raw attention logit (pre-softmax) between query position `$i$` and key position `$j=1$` (the first token) in layer `$l$`, head `$h$`, under positional shift `$\Delta$`, and `$T$` is the sequence length.

**What it computes:** the average absolute difference in the unnormalized attention score that every token assigns to the first token, comparing two different positional shifts. The logit (rather than post-softmax score) is used to make measurements comparable across different sequence lengths—softmax scores would be diluted by the increasing denominator as `$T$` grows, masking the true effect.

**Why this form:** examining attention to the first token specifically is motivated by the observation in Figure 1 (middle) that the first token dominates the overall attention difference. Using the logit avoids the softmax normalization confound, allowing comparison across sequence lengths where the partition function `$\sum_k \exp(A_{ik})$` changes.

**Empirical findings from these metrics.** Figure 1 (left) shows that `$D$` with `$\Delta_2 = 16$` fixed and `$\Delta_1$` varying produces a U-shaped curve: the difference is zero when `$\Delta_1 = \Delta_2 = 16$` (trivially), small for nearby shifts, and grows substantially for larger shifts. The key comparisons are:

- **BFloat16 pretrained model (blue line):** large non-zero differences for shifts `$\Delta_1 \neq \Delta_2$`, confirming the invariance is broken.
- **Float32 pretrained model (yellow line):** near-zero differences regardless of shift, confirming Float32 preserves the relative property.
- **BFloat16 random initialization (green line):** smaller but non-zero differences, showing that the precision issue exists even without training but is **amplified by pretraining**—the model learns to rely on position-corrupted attention patterns.

Figure 1 (middle) decomposes the `$\Delta_1 = 0$` vs `$\Delta_2 = 16$` difference token-by-token, revealing that **the first token (position 0) accounts for the overwhelming majority of the discrepancy**. When the first token is excluded, the remaining tokens show near-zero difference, indicating that the relative property is largely preserved for non-initial tokens even under BFloat16.

Figure 1 (right) shows `$D_{\text{logit}}$` for the first token as sequence length varies from 64 to 8192: the difference increases monotonically with sequence length. This establishes that the precision error is **cumulative**—longer sequences expose the first token to larger absolute rotation angles, amplifying the floating-point error.

#### The Mechanism: Why the First Token Is the Locus of Breakdown

The paper does not provide an exhaustive theoretical analysis of *why* the first token specifically suffers from precision errors, but the empirical evidence supports a mechanistic hypothesis that can be reconstructed from the results.

**The first token has the smallest rotation angles.** At position `$i = 0$`, the rotation angles are `$0 \cdot \theta_k = 0$` for all frequency components. The rotation matrix `$R_{0,\theta}$` is therefore the identity matrix `$I$`. When position IDs are shifted to `$\Delta$`, the first token's rotation becomes `$R_{\Delta,\theta}$`, introducing non-zero rotation angles that grow with `$\Delta$`. For subsequent tokens at positions `$j > 0$`, the relative rotation `$R_{j,\theta}$` vs `$R_{j+\Delta,\theta}$` may involve smaller *fractional* changes in the rotation angles (relative to the already-large base angles), making the precision error proportionally smaller.

**The first token's attention logit has no competing normalization.** In a causal attention mechanism, the first token can only attend to itself (position 0 attending to itself). For query position `$i > 0$`, the attention distribution is normalized over `$i+1$` key positions. Small errors in the logit to any single key position are partially absorbed by the softmax normalization across all keys. For the first token (as a query), there is only one key target (itself), so any precision error in the self-attention logit directly translates to an error in the attention score. More importantly, when the first token serves as a *key* for later queries, the cumulative effect of errors in later positions' attention distributions may be disproportionately influenced by errors in the first token's representation.

**Pretraining amplifies the deviation.** The green line (random initialization) vs. blue line (pretrained) in Figure 1 (left) shows that training increases the attention sensitivity to positional shifts. This suggests that during pretraining, the model learns to use the first token's absolute position as a distinguishing feature—perhaps as an "attention sink" (Xiao et al., 2023) or as a bias term that accumulates attention mass. Once the model has learned this dependency, shifting the first token's position ID changes its rotation representation, disrupting the learned pattern. Under Float32, the rotation change would be near-exact (the relative property would hold), and the model could adjust. Under BFloat16, the rotation change is corrupted by precision errors, so the model's learned dependence on the first token's absolute position is broken.

#### The Intra-Document Attention Analysis: Resetting Position IDs Improves Performance

**Standard intra-document attention.** The conventional approach (Zhao et al., 2024b; Gao et al., 2024) concatenates multiple documents into a long sequence, applies a causal attention mask that additionally blocks attention between tokens belonging to different documents, and assigns position IDs continuously from 0 through the full sequence length. Formally, for a sequence containing documents `$d_1, d_2, ..., d_K$` with lengths `$L_1, L_2, ..., L_K$`, the position of the first token of document `$d_k$` is `$P_k = \sum_{m=1}^{k-1} L_m$` (with `$P_1 = 0$`).

**Why this causes a problem.** Under BFloat16, the first token of each document—which should have the same "role" as a sequence start—receives a different absolute position ID (`$P_k \neq 0$` for `$k > 1$`). The precision-induced deviation in attention that depends on this absolute position therefore differs across documents. The model sees document boundaries at varying positions during training, yet the BFloat16 corruption makes the attention pattern position-dependent, so the model receives inconsistent signals about what "the start of a document" means. This inconsistency degrades long-context performance.

**The reset-position-ID experiment (Figure 2, Middle).** The paper tests a modified intra-document attention where position IDs are reset to 0 at the start of each document. For document `$d_k$`, its tokens receive positions `$0, 1, ..., L_k - 1$` regardless of `$k$`. This is a minimal change: under perfect RoPE, the relative distances between any pair of tokens within the same document are identical whether positions are reset or continuous (since subtracting a constant offset from both positions yields the same difference). Therefore, if RoPE's relative property held perfectly, resetting position IDs should have **no effect** on model behavior.

**Results (Figure 3).** The experiment compares LLaMA-2-7B trained on SlimPajama-128K with intra-document attention using continuous position IDs vs. reset position IDs, evaluated on RULER at context lengths from 4K to 128K. Resetting position IDs **consistently improves performance** at all context lengths. For example, at 128K tokens, the reset variant achieves approximately 65.75 vs. 64.31 for the continuous variant (Table 5, SlimPajama-128K).

**What this proves.** The performance difference demonstrates that BFloat16's corruption of RoPE is not merely measurable in attention patterns—it has **downstream effects on task performance**. The minimal intervention of resetting position IDs would be a no-op under perfect precision, so the observed improvement is direct evidence that the first token's position ID inconsistency is a practically significant limitation of standard intra-document attention under BFloat16.

**The drawback of resetting position IDs.** With position IDs reset per document, the maximum absolute position seen during training is `$\max_k L_k$` (the length of the longest individual document), not the total concatenated sequence length. If training sequences are 128K tokens but individual documents are typically much shorter (e.g., a few thousand tokens), the model never sees rotation angles corresponding to large absolute positions (e.g., position 100,000). The RoPE rotation frequencies `$\theta_k = \text{base}^{-2k/d}$` with base values like 10M produce distinct rotation patterns at positions 100K that the model cannot learn if it only sees positions up to, say, 8K. This limits the model's ability to generalize to sequences longer than the maximum document length, because the high-frequency components of RoPE for large positions are untrained (out-of-distribution).

#### AnchorAttention: Design and Mechanism

AnchorAttention resolves the tension between positional consistency (requiring reset IDs) and rotational coverage (requiring continuous IDs) by introducing a shared anchor token that serves as position 0 for all documents, while maintaining continuous position IDs for the remaining tokens.

**The attention mask pattern.** AnchorAttention implements a sparse attention pattern defined by three rules, visualized in Figure 2 (Right):

1. **Anchor-to-all connectivity:** The anchor token (position 0) serves as a key for every query token in every document. Every token can attend to the anchor. The anchor can attend to itself (standard causal self-attention for position 0).

2. **Within-document causal attention:** Tokens within the same document attend to each other following standard causal masking (token `$i$` in document `$d$` can attend to tokens `$j \leq i$` in the same document `$d$`).

3. **Cross-document masking:** Tokens in different documents cannot attend to each other. If token `$i$` belongs to document `$d_a$` and token `$j$` belongs to document `$d_b$` with `$a \neq b$`, the attention logit is masked to `$-\infty$` (or equivalently, the attention score is set to 0 after softmax).

Formally, the attention logit matrix `$A$` for AnchorAttention is:

$$A_{ij} = \begin{cases} (R_{i,\theta} q_i)^\top (R_{j,\theta} k_j) & \text{if } i \text{ and } j \text{ are in the same document, or } j = 0 \text{ (anchor)} \\ -\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where position IDs are assigned continuously: anchor at position 0, document 1 tokens at positions 1 through `$L_1$`, document 2 tokens at positions `$L_1 + 1$` through `$L_1 + L_2$`, etc.

**What this computes:** for each query token, the attention distribution is restricted to (a) other tokens in its own document (up to and including itself, causally) plus (b) the shared anchor token. The attention is normalized by softmax only over this restricted set of keys, not over all preceding tokens in the concatenated sequence.

**Why this form:** the three design rules each address a specific problem:
- **Rule 1 (anchor-to-all):** By making every document share the same position-0 token as its effective "first token," there is no inconsistency in what "the start of a sequence" means. The model learns that position 0 (the anchor) is the universal reference point. Any BFloat16 precision errors that concentrate on position 0 affect all documents equally through the shared anchor, so there is no cross-document variation.
- **Rule 2 (within-document causal attention):** Standard causal attention within documents preserves the model's ability to process each document's content normally. The relative positional relationships within a document are maintained exactly as in pretraining.
- **Rule 3 (cross-document masking):** This is the key efficiency driver. By preventing cross-document attention, the total number of attention computations is `$O(\sum_{d} L_d^2) + O(L_{\text{total}})$` (each document's internal attention plus the anchor connections) rather than `$O((\sum_d L_d)^2)$` (full attention over the entire sequence). For sequences composed of many relatively short documents, this is a dramatic reduction.

**The shared anchor, continuous IDs, and full rotational coverage.** Because position IDs are assigned continuously from the anchor through all documents without resetting, a 128K sequence exercises rotation angles from `$0 \cdot \theta_k$` (the anchor) through `$128K \cdot \theta_k$` (the last token of the last document). Even though individual documents may be short, the cumulative position IDs span the full context length. This means:
- **All RoPE frequencies are trained:** Position 100,000 produces rotation angles `$100000 \cdot \theta_k$` that are distinct from position 1000's rotation angles `$1000 \cdot \theta_k$`. By seeing tokens at all positions up to the maximum context length during training, the model learns to process the full spectrum of rotations. This is crucial for generalization to long sequences at inference time.
- **The anchor absorbs precision errors:** Any BFloat16 precision errors that are position-dependent (worse at larger absolute positions) accumulate on tokens within individual documents, but since all documents are processed independently (cross-document masking), errors from one document do not propagate to another. The anchor token itself is at a fixed position (0), so its precision error is constant regardless of sequence length—unlike the first token in full attention, which acquires progressively larger errors as the sequence grows (Figure 1, right).

**Why the BOS token as anchor.** The paper designates the beginning-of-sequence (`<bos>`) token as the shared anchor. This choice is motivated by two properties:
1. **Semantic neutrality:** The `<bos>` token typically carries no explicit semantic content—it is a delimiter, not a word. Therefore, any precision errors concentrated on this token have minimal impact on the model's understanding of document content.
2. **Existing presence in pretraining:** All pretrained LLaMA-family models already use a `<bos>` token as the first token of every sequence. By reusing this token as the anchor, AnchorAttention requires no changes to the tokenizer or vocabulary, only to the attention mask pattern.

For models without a `<bos>` token (e.g., Qwen series), the paper uses the `<eos>` (end-of-sequence) token as the shared anchor, demonstrating flexibility in the anchor choice.

**Computational complexity comparison.** For a sequence containing `$K$` documents with lengths `$L_1, ..., L_K$` and total length `$L_{\text{total}} = 1 + \sum_{d=1}^K L_d$` (including the anchor), the number of attention computations (key-query dot products) is:

- **Full attention:** `$O(L_{\text{total}}^2)$` — every query attends to every previous key.
- **Intra-document attention (standard):** `$O(\sum_{d=1}^K L_d^2)$` — queries in document `$d$` attend only to keys in document `$d$`.
- **AnchorAttention:** `$O(\sum_{d=1}^K (L_d^2 + L_d))$` — queries in document `$d$` attend to keys in document `$d$` plus the anchor token, giving `$O(L_d^2 + L_d)$` per document. The `$+L_d$` term represents each token's attention to the anchor.

For a typical training configuration with `$L_{\text{total}} = 128\text{K}$` and many documents of average length `$\bar{L} \ll L_{\text{total}}$`, AnchorAttention's complexity is dominated by `$\sum_d L_d^2$`, which is substantially smaller than `$L_{\text{total}}^2$` because the sum of squares is much less than the square of the sum (by Jensen's inequality, since the square function is convex). This is the mathematical basis for the >50% training speedup reported in Figure 6.

**Why not simpler alternatives?** The paper implicitly rules out several simpler approaches:

- **Just use Float32 for everything:** This would solve the precision problem but eliminate the memory savings of BFloat16, making long-context training infeasible on current hardware. The paper acknowledges BFloat16 as a practical necessity.

- **Just reset position IDs per document (without anchor):** This provides consistency but loses full rotational frequency coverage (the drawback discussed above). AnchorAttention gets both consistency and coverage.

- **Just use the first document's first token as anchor for all documents (without cross-document masking):** If all documents could attend to each other but shared the same first token, the precision error on that token would still be position-0 (good), but the cross-document attention would reintroduce the quadratic computational cost that AnchorAttention avoids. Additionally, cross-document attention may be semantically undesirable—documents concatenated in a training batch are typically unrelated, and attending across them may introduce noise.

- **Use learned position embeddings instead of RoPE:** This would avoid the RoPE-BFloat16 interaction entirely, but learned absolute position embeddings (as in the original Transformer) do not generalize to unseen positions, making length extension impossible without architectural changes. Relative position embeddings (Shaw et al., 2018) are an alternative but have not achieved the same widespread adoption or empirical success as RoPE.

**Connection to the attention sink phenomenon.** The paper speculates in Section 8 about a relationship between AnchorAttention and the "attention sink" observation (Xiao et al., 2023), where initial tokens receive disproportionately high attention scores and serve as a bias term. In AnchorAttention, the shared anchor at position 0 may function as an explicit, controlled attention sink—a designated token for absorbing attention mass that would otherwise be distributed (and potentially corrupted by precision errors) across multiple "first tokens" in the concatenated sequence. By concentrating this sink on a single, semantically neutral token, the model may learn more stable attention patterns. However, the paper does not experimentally verify this hypothesis.

#### RoPE Base Frequency Selection

The paper conducts a systematic study of RoPE base frequency (`rope_theta`) selection, recognizing that the optimal base value depends on the target context length and that this choice interacts with AnchorAttention's mechanism.

**The role of the base frequency.** RoPE rotation angles are computed as `$\theta_k = \text{base}^{-2k/d}$` for `$k = 0, 1, ..., d/2 - 1$`. The base value controls the "wavelength" of the rotations: a larger base means that the lowest-frequency components rotate more slowly, allowing the model to distinguish positions at larger distances without the rotation angles aliasing (wrapping around due to the periodicity of sine and cosine). For a model with hidden dimension `$d$` and context length `$L$`, the lowest frequency completes `$L / (\text{base}^{2/d})$` cycles over the full context. If this number is too small (base too small), the rotation at the lowest frequency doesn't traverse enough of the unit circle to provide distinctive positional signals at long range. If it's too large, the highest frequencies rotate too fast, potentially causing aliasing at short ranges.

**Experiment design (Table 2, Table 3, Table 4).** The paper evaluates three positional encoding methods—vanilla RoPE, NTK-aware scaling (LocalLLaMA, 2023), and YaRN (Peng et al., 2023)—at various base values. For NTK-aware and YaRN, the relative scale `$s = T_{\text{new}} / T_{\text{origin}}$` is set to 16 for extending from 4K to 64K context. Each configuration is trained on 1 billion tokens from SlimPajama-64K with full attention (not AnchorAttention—this is a separate study to determine the best positional encoding parameters before applying AnchorAttention).

**Results for within-training-length evaluation (Table 2).** Vanilla RoPE with appropriately chosen base values (1M, 5M, 10M) consistently outperforms NTK-aware and YaRN at the trained context length (64K) and shorter lengths. For example, at 64K tokens, RoPE with base 10M achieves 69.43 on RULER, compared to YaRN with base 10M at 58.91 and NTK with base 10M at 58.75. The pattern holds across base values: vanilla RoPE > NTK-aware > YaRN within the training context.

**Results for generalization beyond training length (Table 4).** When evaluated at 128K tokens (double the 64K training length), YaRN shows better generalization. RoPE with base 1M drops from 66.88 at 64K to 35.93 at 128K (a 30.95-point drop), while YaRN with base 1M drops from 64.22 to 57.07 (only a 7.15-point drop). This confirms that YaRN's frequency interpolation is specifically designed to handle out-of-distribution position IDs better than vanilla RoPE.

**Base value sweep (Table 3).** To isolate the effect of base value, the paper trains models with 32K context length (to save compute) and sweeps base values from 500 to 1B. Performance (averaged over 32K, 16K, 8K, and 4K RULER scores) peaks at base = 900K (average 82.64), with marginal gains beyond base = 600K (average 82.70). Performance degrades for base values that are too low (500: 20.80) or too high (1B: 78.47).

**Recommended base values by context length.** Based on these experiments, the paper recommends scaling the RoPE base with context length:
- 32K context: base = 1M
- 64K context: base = 5M
- 128K context: base = 10M
- 256K context: base = 50M

These values are larger than the default base = 10,000 used in LLaMA pretraining, reflecting the need for slower-rotating frequencies to encode positional distinctions over longer ranges without aliasing. The paper notes that these findings are "aligned with observations from (Liu et al., 2023b; Men et al., 2024)," which also find that larger base values benefit long-context performance.

**Why this matters for AnchorAttention.** AnchorAttention uses continuous position IDs spanning the full context length, so the base frequency choice directly affects the rotation angles seen at positions up to 128K. A base value that is too small would cause the lowest-frequency components to cycle multiple times over the 128K range, making distant positions indistinguishable (since `$\sin(x) = \sin(x + 2\pi)$` and `$\cos(x) = \cos(x + 2\pi)$`). Conversely, a base value that is too large would make even the lowest-frequency components rotate very slowly, potentially failing to provide sufficient positional resolution at short ranges. The recommended values represent empirical optima that balance these considerations.

#### Training Protocol and Data Pipeline

**Base models.** The primary experiments use **LLaMA-2-7B** (Touvron et al., 2023) as the base model, with original pretraining context length of 4,096 tokens. Cross-architecture evaluation extends to **LLaMA-3-8B** (Dubey et al., 2024), **Mistral-7B-v0.3** (Jiang et al., 2023), and **Qwen-1.5-1.8B** (Yang et al., 2024). These models represent different pretraining paradigms (LLaMA-2 vs. LLaMA-3), architectures (Mistral's sliding window attention was pretrained differently but is evaluated with AnchorAttention replacing standard attention), and scales (1.8B vs. 7B/8B). For Qwen models, which lack a `<bos>` token, the paper adapts AnchorAttention to use the `<eos>` token as the shared anchor.

**Dataset.** The primary training data is **SlimPajama** (Soboleva et al., 2023), an open-source replication of the LLaMA pretraining data mixture. The original SlimPajama token mixture is: 67% CommonCrawl, 15% C4, 4.5% GitHub, 4.5% Wikipedia, 4.5% Books, 2.5% ArXiv, and 2.0% StackExchange. The paper uses three data configurations:

1. **SlimPajama-64K:** Original SlimPajama with sequences chunked to 64K tokens. Token mixture: 54% CC, 25% C4, 5% ArXiv, 5% GitHub, 4% Books, 3% Wiki, 3% StackExchange.

2. **SlimPajama-128K:** Original SlimPajama with sequences chunked to 128K tokens. Token mixture: 53% CC, 27% C4, 5% GitHub, 5% ArXiv, 4% Books, 3% Wiki, 3% StackExchange.

3. **UpSampledMix-128K:** A modified mixture that upsamples long sequences within each source while maintaining overall domain distribution. Token mixture: 58% CC, 20% C4, 7% GitHub, 6% ArXiv, 5% Books, 4% Wiki, 2% StackExchange. Compared to the original, this mixture increases the proportion of CommonCrawl (which tends to have longer documents) and scholarly content (ArXiv, Books) while reducing C4 (which tends to have shorter, noisier documents). The sequence length distribution is shown in Appendix C (Figure 8), confirming that UpSampledMix contains a higher proportion of long sequences.

The paper samples **2 billion tokens** from each dataset configuration, corresponding to approximately 1 epoch for a 2B token dataset (the exact number of tokens in the full SlimPajama is 627B, but the paper uses a 2B-token subset for continued training, not the full dataset).

**Training hyperparameters.** The configuration is based on Zhang (2023) with the following settings (Table 1):

- **Optimizer:** AdamW with weight decay = 0.1, `$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`
- **Learning rate:** `$2 \times 10^{-5}$`
- **Training steps:** 2000
- **Batch size:** 8 sequences, equivalent to 0.5 million tokens per batch for 64K context or 1 million tokens per batch for 128K context
- **Hardware:** 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs

With 2000 steps and 1M tokens per batch at 128K context, the total tokens processed are `$2000 \times 1\text{M} = 2\text{B}$` tokens, matching the dataset size (approximately one epoch).

**Why only 2B tokens and 2000 steps?** The paper cites Fu et al. (2024), which suggests that approximately 1B tokens suffice to learn long-context abilities. Using a relatively small amount of continued training (compared to the trillions of tokens in full pretraining) is consistent with the understanding that long-context extension is primarily about adapting positional encodings and attention patterns, not learning new factual knowledge or language capabilities. The short training also makes it feasible to compare multiple attention mechanisms, datasets, and RoPE configurations within a reasonable compute budget.

**Why upsampling long sequences?** The UpSampledMix-128K dataset is designed following Fu et al. (2024)'s data engineering approach. The intuition is that to learn long-range dependencies, the model must be exposed to sufficiently many long documents during training. In the original SlimPajama, most documents are short: a 128K sequence may contain many short documents concatenated together. While AnchorAttention's continuous position IDs ensure that rotation angles for large positions are seen, the model also needs to practice *attending across long distances* within a single document. Upsampling long documents increases the proportion of training examples where individual documents span a significant fraction of the context window, giving the model more practice at long-range attention.

#### Evaluation Protocol

**Why not perplexity?** The paper explicitly argues against using perplexity (PPL) to evaluate long-context models, citing three lines of evidence:

1. **Poor correlation with long-range understanding:** Hu et al. (2024) show that PPL primarily measures local information capture (e.g., predicting the next word given the immediate preceding context) and correlates poorly with tasks requiring reasoning over long distances.

2. **Insensitivity to key tokens:** Fang et al. (2024) demonstrate that PPL overlooks tokens that are crucial for understanding long-context inputs, leading to unreliable assessments.

3. **Empirical plateau during training:** Figure 4 shows that PPL remains essentially unchanged after the first several hundred training steps, while RULER performance continues to improve throughout the 2000-step training run. This demonstrates that PPL fails to capture the learning dynamics of long-context capabilities.

**Why not LongBench alone?** While LongBench (Bai et al., 2023) is a widely used long-context benchmark, the paper notes that its tasks can be adequately addressed with a context length of 16K tokens. Since the paper's models are trained for 64K and 128K contexts, LongBench does not fully stress-test the extended context capability. However, LongBench is still used for evaluating medium-context performance and few-shot in-context learning (ICL) tasks, which are appropriate for base models.

**RULER as the primary benchmark.** The paper adopts the RULER benchmark (Hsieh et al., 2024), which is specifically designed to evaluate long-context models at lengths up to 128K+ tokens. RULER includes tasks across four categories:
- **Needle-in-a-Haystack (NIAH):** Locating specific information within vast content, including single-key, multi-key, multi-value, and multi-query variants.
- **Variable Tracing (VT):** Tracing relationships and dependencies across broad contexts.
- **Aggregation (Common/Frequent Words):** Aggregating and quantifying dispersed information.
- **Question Answering (QA):** Distinguishing relevant details from extraneous information in complex queries.

**Task exclusion for LLaMA-2-7B.** The paper excludes two RULER tasks when evaluating LLaMA-2-7B: NIAH Multikey 3 and Common Word Extraction (CWE). The justification is based on the assumption that "long-context training primarily extends a model's existing abilities from its original context window to longer sequences." If a model cannot perform a task within its original 4K context window, continued long-context training should not be expected to teach it that task. Table 10 (Appendix D) confirms that LLaMA-2-7B struggles with CWE even at 4K context (24.46 accuracy, dropping to 0.8 for the chat model with YaRN 64K). The paper also documents an instability in CWE evaluation: with a 4,096-token context (matching pretraining), LLaMA-2-7B achieves 76.8 on CWE, but dropping to 4,000 tokens causes the score to fall to 24.46, indicating high sensitivity to exact context formatting. For advanced models (LLaMA-3-8B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, Qwen-1.5-1.8B) in the cross-architecture evaluation, all 13 RULER tasks are used.

**Averaging over multiple checkpoints.** The paper recommends reporting the **average RULER performance across the last 50 training steps, saving a checkpoint every 10 steps** (yielding 5 checkpoints). Figure 4 demonstrates significant variance in RULER scores during training—performance fluctuates rather than monotonically improving. Reporting a single best checkpoint risks cherry-picking and overstating the model's capability. The paper notes that retraining with multiple random seeds would be another way to average performance but would significantly increase experimental cost. Checkpoint averaging is proposed as a practical compromise.

**General ability evaluation.** To ensure that continued pretraining for long contexts does not degrade the model's original capabilities, the paper evaluates on:
- **MMLU** (Hendrycks et al., 2021): 57 diverse subjects testing knowledge and reasoning.
- **HellaSwag** (Zellers et al., 2019): Commonsense reasoning via choosing the most plausible scenario continuation.

The original LLaMA-2-7B achieves 71.39 on HellaSwag and 46.66 on MMLU (Table 7). The goal is for AnchorAttention-trained models to remain close to these baselines.

#### Infrastructure Implementation: AnchorContext

The paper provides AnchorContext, a codebase implementing AnchorAttention with two backend options to maximize compatibility across frameworks.

**FlexAttention backend.** This uses FlexAttention, which is natively supported in PyTorch 2.5.0. FlexAttention provides a flexible API for defining custom attention mask patterns by specifying a `BlockMask` that determines which key-query pairs are computed. For AnchorAttention, the mask enforces: anchor-visible-to-all, within-document causal, and cross-document blocked. The advantage of FlexAttention is that it does not require modifying the underlying attention kernel—it leverages PyTorch's built-in optimizations.

**FlashAttention2 backend.** This integrates with FlashAttention2 (Dao, 2024), which is currently the most commonly used efficient attention implementation. The paper modifies the attention mask to implement AnchorAttention's sparse pattern. Since FlashAttention2 does not natively support arbitrary sparse masks, the AnchorContext implementation presumably segments the attention computation into per-document blocks plus anchor connections, executing each block with FlashAttention2 and combining the results. The key advantage is the proven speed and memory efficiency of FlashAttention2's fused kernel.

**Numerical accuracy in distributed training (Table 8).** The paper evaluates whether the distributed training implementation of AnchorAttention introduces numerical discrepancies compared to single-GPU FlashAttention2. The metric is the maximum absolute difference in output logits when processing the same 32K-sequence on 8 A100 GPUs. Two distributed implementations are compared:

- **Zigzag-Ring attention** (from EasyContext, Zhang, 2023): Exhibits a maximum logit difference of 0.75 compared to the single-GPU baseline. This indicates that the ring-based sequence parallelism in EasyContext introduces non-trivial numerical errors, likely due to the order of floating-point operations varying across devices.
- **AnchorContext** (based on DeepSpeed-Ulysses sequence parallelism, Jacobs et al., 2023): Exhibits **zero logit difference** (0.00) compared to the single-GPU baseline. This is achieved because DeepSpeed-Ulysses partitions the sequence along the attention head dimension rather than the sequence dimension, ensuring that each head's computation is performed entirely on a single device with the same arithmetic order as the non-distributed case. For AnchorAttention specifically, the anchor token is replicated across all devices (since every head needs access to it), and the attention mask ensures that each document's computation is self-contained.

**Training speedup (Figure 6).** The paper estimates the number of days required to process 1 billion tokens at context lengths of 8K, 16K, 32K, 64K, 128K, and 256K, comparing Full Attention with DeepSpeed-Ulysses against AnchorAttention with DeepSpeed-Ulysses. The results show that AnchorAttention reduces training time by over 50% compared to Full Attention at all context lengths, with the gap widening at longer contexts. For example, at 128K tokens, Full Attention requires approximately 22 days per billion tokens, while AnchorAttention requires approximately 10 days. This speedup comes from the reduced number of attention computations: for a 128K sequence composed of many short documents, AnchorAttention's complexity `$O(\sum L_d^2)$` is substantially less than full attention's `$O((\sum L_d)^2)$`.

**Flexibility for advanced experiments.** The AnchorContext implementation with FlexAttention supports more complex attention mask patterns, such as the interleaved chunks experiment (Section 5.2), where documents are split into shuffled chunks that must attend to each other in a specific order while maintaining document boundaries. This flexibility enables researchers to explore variations beyond the basic AnchorAttention pattern.

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications: A Summary

- **Shared anchor (BOS token at position 0):** Eliminates inconsistent first-token positions across documents, the identified root cause of BFloat16 precision degradation. Chosen over resetting position IDs per document because it preserves continuous position IDs for full rotational frequency coverage.

- **Cross-document masking:** Reduces computational complexity from `$O(L_{\text{total}}^2)$` to `$O(\sum L_d^2)$`, providing the >50% training speedup. Prevents precision errors from propagating across document boundaries. Chosen over full attention because full attention compounds both computational cost and precision error with sequence length.

- **Continuous position IDs (no reset):** Ensures that the full spectrum of RoPE rotation angles up to the maximum context length is exercised during training, even when individual documents are short. Chosen over per-document reset IDs because resetting limits the maximum position seen to the longest single document, leaving high-frequency rotations for large positions untrained.

- **Anchor-to-all connectivity:** Provides a consistent positional reference (position 0) for every document, allowing the model to learn a stable association between "sequence start" and the anchor token. Minimizes the impact of any BFloat16 errors on the anchor because the anchor's position is fixed (no shifting) and its semantic content is minimal.

- **Vanilla RoPE with scaled base frequency (not NTK or YaRN):** Empirically outperforms interpolation-based methods within the training context length (Table 2). Base frequency is scaled with context length (1M for 32K, 5M for 64K, 10M for 128K) to prevent aliasing at long range while maintaining short-range resolution.

- **RULER with checkpoint averaging for evaluation:** Chosen over perplexity (poor correlation with long-context ability, Figure 4) and LongBench alone (inadequate context length for 64K+ models). Checkpoint averaging (over last 50 steps) chosen over single-checkpoint reporting to avoid cherry-picking and account for training variance.

- **DeepSpeed-Ulysses for distributed training:** Chosen over ring attention (EasyContext/Zigzag-Ring) because it achieves zero numerical discrepancy compared to single-GPU inference (Table 8), ensuring that distributed training does not introduce additional precision errors beyond the BFloat16 RoPE issue being studied.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Diagnosing BFloat16 as a Correctness-Adverse Precision Format for Positional Encoding

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is not proposing a new attention mechanism but **identifying and rigorously characterizing a previously unrecognized failure mode** at the intersection of two universally adopted components: RoPE and BFloat16. This is a *diagnostic* innovation rather than a methodological one—the paper teaches the field that a widely trusted precision format is not merely lossy in the usual sense (adding noise to gradients or activations) but is **correctness-adverse**: it systematically breaks a mathematical invariant (positional shift invariance) that is central to why RoPE works for length generalization.

What makes this diagnosis intellectually distinctive is that it reframes BFloat16 from a *performance optimization* (trading precision for speed/memory, with acceptable accuracy degradation) to a *correctness hazard* (silently corrupting a structural property of the model). The standard view in the field—inherited from Kalamkar et al. (2019) and reinforced by widespread adoption—is that BFloat16's reduced mantissa precision (7 bits vs. Float32's 23 bits) is sufficient because deep learning is robust to low-precision computation. The paper contradicts this consensus for a specific, critical subsystem: the key-query inner product inside FlashAttention2 where RoPE's rotation matrices have already been applied. The precision loss here does not manifest as generic noise but as a **position-dependent bias** that accumulates with sequence length, precisely the regime where RoPE's relative encoding is supposed to provide its greatest benefit.

The diagnostic framework itself—measuring attention pattern deviation under positional shifts and decomposing it token-by-token to isolate the first token as the primary locus—is a methodological template for studying numerical precision issues in Transformers. Prior work on low-precision training (e.g., Kalamkar et al., 2019; Micikevicius et al., 2022) evaluated aggregate metrics like final accuracy or perplexity. The paper's structured perturbation analysis (varying ∆1 independently of ∆2, comparing pretrained vs. random weights, isolating per-token contributions) reveals that aggregate metrics would *miss* this problem: the model's overall perplexity may be fine while its positional understanding is silently corrupted in ways that only manifest under distribution shift (e.g., at longer context lengths than seen during perplexity evaluation). This is a significant methodological contribution in its own right—a blueprint for auditing whether precision choices are breaking structural properties of model components.

The amplification-by-pretraining finding (green vs. blue lines in Figure 1, left) is a rare and valuable example of a **training-induced vulnerability**: the model does not merely tolerate the precision error but learns to depend on the corrupted signal, making the problem worse over the course of pretraining. This is fundamentally different from standard overfitting or distribution shift—it is the model adapting to a *hardware artifact* in ways that create fragility when the artifact's manifestation changes (e.g., at longer unseen sequence lengths). This finding should make practitioners reconsider the common practice of using BFloat16 for long-context continued pretraining of models originally trained in Float32: the distribution shift in numerical precision may be as consequential as the shift in sequence length.

### Innovation 2: Identifying the First Token as the Locus of Positional Encoding Fragility Under Limited Precision

The paper's second diagnostic contribution is the discovery that **the first token is the dominant source of RoPE's precision-induced breakdown**, with other tokens largely preserving relative positional encoding even under BFloat16. This is not an obvious or predictable finding from the structure of RoPE. One might have expected errors to be distributed across all positions or to scale with absolute position magnitude (since larger rotation angles accumulate larger floating-point rounding errors). Instead, the per-token decomposition in Figure 1 (middle) reveals a sharp concentration: the first token accounts for nearly all the attention difference, and when it is excluded, the remaining tokens exhibit near-perfect shift invariance.

This finding transforms the problem from a diffuse numerical issue (requiring higher precision everywhere) into a localized one (requiring careful handling of one specific token's positional role). This localization is what makes AnchorAttention possible as a lightweight intervention: rather than abandoning BFloat16 or redesigning RoPE, the paper can target the first token specifically. The conceptual move is from "BFloat16 breaks RoPE" to "BFloat16 breaks RoPE *through the first token's position ID*," which is a more precise and actionable diagnosis.

The finding also connects to—and potentially reinterprets—a separate line of work on "attention sinks" and "massive activations." Xiao et al. (2023) observed that LLMs allocate disproportionate attention to initial tokens, serving as a bias term. Han et al. (2023), Gu et al. (2024), Sun et al. (2024), and Guo et al. (2024a) further characterized this phenomenon. The paper's discovery suggests a possible mechanistic unification: if BFloat16 precision errors cause the first token's positional encoding to deviate from the relative encoding property, the model may learn to treat it differently (as an absolute position reference or attention sink) during pretraining as an *adaptation* to this numerical artifact. Under this hypothesis, attention sinks are not merely a learned architectural bias but a consequence of—or compensation for—the precision format. The paper does not prove this connection but establishes the empirical foundation for investigating it, which is a significant conceptual contribution even without full mechanistic validation.

A subtler insight is that the first token's special vulnerability may arise because it has the smallest rotation angles at its default position (0 × θ_k = 0 for all frequencies). When a positional shift is applied, the first token's rotation matrix changes from the identity to R_Δ,θ—a maximal relative change in its rotational representation. For later tokens, shifting from position j to j+Δ changes the rotation from R_j,θ to R_j+Δ,θ, which may involve a proportionally smaller perturbation relative to the already-large rotation angles. This creates an asymmetry where the first token's positional encoding is maximally sensitive to absolute position shifts, while later tokens' encodings are more stable. If this interpretation is correct, it implies that any positional encoding based on absolute rotation angles applied in limited precision will have a "first-token fragility," not just RoPE—a broader theoretical implication the paper hints at but does not fully explore.

### Innovation 3: AnchorAttention as a Principled Architectural Intervention Motivated by Precision Diagnostics

The paper's methodological contribution—AnchorAttention itself—is distinctive not because it is a complex new attention mechanism (it is notably simple) but because **its design is directly derived from the diagnostic findings rather than from independent architectural intuitions**. This is a departure from most attention variants in the literature, which are typically motivated by computational efficiency (sparse attention, Linformer, BigBird), by cognitive or linguistic priors (local attention windows, global tokens), or by length generalization (ALiBi, positional interpolation). AnchorAttention's design is motivated by a specific numerical pathology diagnosed in the same paper: the first token's position ID inconsistency across documents under BFloat16.

The conceptual move is: instead of treating the BOS token as a vestigial artifact of tokenization (as most work does), **elevate it to a structural role as a shared positional anchor**. This reframes the BOS token from "the token that happens to be at position 0" to "the designated position-0 reference point that all content tokens orient themselves relative to." This is a small change in mechanism (one additional attention connection per token) but a significant shift in how to think about positional encoding in concatenated-document training: the BOS token becomes part of the positional encoding infrastructure, not just the sequence delimiter.

The paper's empirical validation that this design is *both* more accurate *and* faster is important but secondary to the conceptual contribution. What makes AnchorAttention novel as an idea is that it **solves three problems simultaneously through one structural change**:
- The precision problem (consistent first-token position ID)
- The rotational coverage problem (continuous IDs for full frequency spectrum)
- The efficiency problem (cross-document masking reduces computation)

This is not a case of a method that was designed for one goal and happened to help others—the design explicitly targets all three constraints, and the paper's diagnostic analysis explains why a single intervention can address them all. If the first token were not the locus of precision error, AnchorAttention's shared anchor would be an unnecessary complication. If continuous position IDs were not needed for rotational coverage, the simpler reset-position-IDs approach would suffice. AnchorAttention's elegance as a solution comes from the fact that it is the *minimal* intervention that satisfies all constraints simultaneously, and the paper's diagnostic work establishes what those constraints are.

The cross-architecture validation (LLaMA-2, LLaMA-3, Mistral, Qwen in Table 6) is methodologically significant because it demonstrates that AnchorAttention's effectiveness is not tied to a specific pretraining recipe. BFloat16 and RoPE are universal enough that the precision issue should affect any RoPE-based model trained in BFloat16, and the results confirm that AnchorAttention provides consistent gains across architectures. For Qwen, which lacks a BOS token, the adaptation to use the EOS token as anchor (and still see gains) further supports that the mechanism is about providing a stable position-0 reference, not about any specific property of the BOS token's learned embedding.

### Innovation 4: Recontextualizing Test-Time Compute vs. Architectural Sparsity in Long-Context Training

This innovation is a **meta-contribution about how to think about long-context training efficiency**. The dominant narrative in the long-context literature frames the challenge as primarily computational: attention is quadratic, so we need sparse approximations, linear attention variants, or system-level optimizations (FlashAttention, Ring Attention, sequence parallelism). The paper introduces a competing—or rather, complementary—narrative: **some of what we attribute to computational cost is actually precision cost in disguise**. The reason full attention performs poorly at long contexts under BFloat16 may not be (only) because it is expensive but because it is *numerically corrupted* in ways that sparse patterns incidentally avoid.

Consider the evidence: Full attention under BFloat16 at 128K context (Table 5, SlimPajama-128K) achieves 62.75 on RULER. AnchorAttention with the same model and data achieves 66.15—a gain of over 3 points *while doing less computation*. If the only constraint were computational cost, sparser attention should be at best equal to full attention (since full attention has strictly more information). The fact that AnchorAttention *outperforms* full attention indicates that full attention's additional computation is not just unnecessary but actively harmful under BFloat16—it introduces precision errors that degrade the learned positional representations.

This reframes the efficiency-accuracy tradeoff in long-context training. The standard view is: if you can afford full attention, use it (it's the "gold standard"); if you can't, use sparse approximations and accept some accuracy loss. The paper's results suggest that under BFloat16, **sparse attention can be the gold standard**—more accurate *and* more efficient—because it avoids the precision pathology. This is a significant conceptual shift that should influence how practitioners allocate their hardware and training design choices. If you are training in BFloat16 (which nearly everyone is), you should *prefer* attention patterns that limit the propagation of first-token precision errors, not just patterns that reduce FLOPs.

The comparison between intra-document attention with reset IDs and AnchorAttention (Table 5) further sharpens this point. Both methods use cross-document masking (eliminating the quadratic cost), but AnchorAttention additionally provides a stable position-0 reference via the shared anchor while maintaining continuous position IDs. The consistent advantage of AnchorAttention over intra-document attention with reset IDs shows that avoiding precision errors is not just about reducing sequence length—the *structure* of positional references matters independently of computational cost. This is a non-obvious finding that would not emerge from a purely FLOPs-minimization perspective: it required diagnosing the precision pathology first, then designing an attention pattern that specifically mitigates it.

### Innovation 5: Establishing the First Token's Position ID as a Critical Hyperparameter in Long-Context Training

The paper's final contribution is **elevating a previously invisible design choice—how position IDs are assigned to the first tokens of concatenated documents—to the status of a critical hyperparameter** with measurable effects on downstream performance. Prior to this work, position ID assignment in long-context training was treated as an implementation detail: you concatenate documents, assign position IDs sequentially from 0, and move on. The paper's experiment comparing continuous vs. reset position IDs within intra-document attention (Figure 3) demonstrates that this "implementation detail" has a substantial impact on long-context benchmark scores.

This is a form of **implicit knowledge surfacing**: the field has been making this choice unconsciously, and many implementations may differ in ways that contribute to irreproducible or inconsistent results across long-context training papers. The paper brings this choice into explicit focus and provides guidance (continuous IDs with a shared anchor at position 0) grounded in both empirical results and a mechanistic explanation (first-token precision errors under BFloat16). This is similar in spirit to how the RoPE base frequency was initially treated as a fixed constant (10,000) and later recognized as a tunable parameter that critically affects length generalization (Liu et al., 2023b; Men et al., 2024)—the paper extends this "hyperparameter awakening" to position ID assignment patterns.

The practical significance of this insight extends beyond AnchorAttention itself. Even practitioners who do not adopt AnchorAttention can benefit from understanding that **position ID consistency for the first token matters under BFloat16**. A team using standard full attention for long-context training might improve results simply by ensuring that the first token of every training sequence consistently receives position ID 0 (e.g., by always starting sequences with a BOS token at position 0 rather than beginning in the middle of a document). The paper's diagnostic framework (measuring attention differences under positional shifts) provides a tool for auditing whether a given position ID assignment strategy is introducing unintended variability.

The negative results on interleaved chunks (Section 5.2) reinforce this insight from the opposite direction: a data augmentation technique that improves performance with full attention (Zhao et al., 2024a) *degrades* performance when combined with cross-document masking. The paper hypothesizes that interleaved chunks and cross-document masking are incompatible because the chunking breaks the within-document coherence that sparse attention relies on. This demonstrates that data preparation strategies and attention mask designs are coupled in non-obvious ways, and that the position ID assignment is part of a larger design space that includes both data ordering and attention sparsity patterns. The paper's systematic ablation of these design choices (domain tagging, interleaved chunks, position ID resetting) provides a template for how future work should evaluate long-context training recipes: not as independent knobs but as interacting components whose joint behavior depends on the underlying numerical precision constraints.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset (training).** Continued pretraining uses the **SlimPajama** dataset (Soboleva et al., 2023), an open-source replication of the LLaMA pretraining data mixture. The paper samples 2 billion tokens from three configurations: SlimPajama-64K (sequences chunked to 64K tokens), SlimPajama-128K (sequences chunked to 128K tokens), and UpSampledMix-128K (a modified mixture that upsamples long sequences within each source while maintaining overall domain distribution). Domain mixtures differ slightly across configurations (see Table 9 in Appendix C); for example, UpSampledMix-128K uses 58% CommonCrawl, 20% C4, 7% GitHub, 6% ArXiv, 5% Books, 4% Wiki, and 2% StackExchange, compared to SlimPajama-128K's 53% CC and 27% C4—the upsampling shifts weight toward sources with longer documents (CommonCrawl, ArXiv, Books) and away from shorter C4 documents.

- **Dataset (evaluation).** Long-context capability is primarily evaluated on the **RULER** benchmark (Hsieh et al., 2024), which tests context lengths from 4K to 128K tokens across four task categories: Needle-in-a-Haystack (NIAH) variants, Variable Tracing (VT), Aggregation (Common/Frequent Words), and Question Answering (QA). For LLaMA-2-7B experiments, two tasks are excluded (NIAH Multikey 3 and Common Word Extraction) because the base model cannot perform them even at its original 4K context length—the paper argues long-context training extends existing abilities rather than creating new ones. Advanced models (LLaMA-3-8B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, Qwen-1.5-1.8B) use all 13 RULER tasks. Medium-context performance is assessed on **LongBench** (Bai et al., 2023) using few-shot in-context learning (ICL) tasks appropriate for base models. General capability preservation is measured on **MMLU** (Hendrycks et al., 2021) and **HellaSwag** (Zellers et al., 2019).

- **Base model(s).** Primary experiments use **LLaMA-2-7B** (Touvron et al., 2023) with original pretraining context of 4,096 tokens. Cross-architecture evaluation extends to **LLaMA-3-8B** (Dubey et al., 2024), **Mistral-7B-v0.3** (Jiang et al., 2023), and **Qwen-1.5-1.8B** (Yang et al., 2024). These span different pretraining paradigms (LLaMA-2 vs. LLaMA-3), architectural choices (Mistral's sliding window attention), scales (1.8B to 8B), and tokenizer designs (Qwen lacks a `<bos>` token, requiring adaptation to use `<eos>` as anchor). All models use RoPE with BFloat16 precision as their default configuration.

- **Metrics.** For RULER, the primary metric is **accuracy (%)** averaged across all included tasks, reported separately at each context length (4K, 8K, 16K, 32K, 64K, 128K). The paper recommends averaging over five checkpoints saved every 10 steps during the last 50 training steps, rather than reporting a single best checkpoint, to mitigate training variance (Figure 4). For LongBench, performance is reported as average score across task categories (Code Completion, ICL, Multi-Doc QA, Single-Doc QA, Summarization, Synthetic). For MMLU and HellaSwag, standard accuracy metrics are used. The paper explicitly argues against using perplexity, demonstrating in Figure 4 that PPL plateaus early in training while RULER performance continues to improve, and citing prior work (Hu et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2024) showing PPL correlates poorly with long-range understanding.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against the following attention mechanisms, all implemented within the same continued pretraining framework:
  - **Full Attention**: Standard causal self-attention over the entire concatenated sequence of length L_total, with O(L_total²) complexity. This is the default in most long-context training pipelines.
  - **Intra-Document Attention** (Zhao et al., 2024b; Gao et al., 2024): Cross-document masking where tokens within the same document attend to each other causally but cannot attend to tokens in other documents. Position IDs are assigned continuously from the start of the concatenated sequence (the "non-resetting" variant in Figure 2, Left).
  - **Intra-Document Attention + Reset**: Identical to intra-document attention but with position IDs reset to 1 at the start of each document (Figure 2, Middle). This serves as a direct test of whether first-token position ID consistency matters.
  - **Intra-Document Attention + Interleaved Chunks**: Intra-document attention applied to sequences where documents are split into randomly shuffled chunks (preserving within-document chunk order). This tests compatibility with the data augmentation from Zhao et al. (2024a).
  - The original pretrained model (without continued long-context training) is also reported for general ability benchmarks (MMLU, HellaSwag) as a reference point.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures computational cost in two ways. For **accuracy comparisons**, all methods are compared under identical training regimens: 2000 steps on 2 billion tokens, batch size 8, same optimizer and learning rate. The main variation is the attention mechanism, not the amount of computation. For **training speed**, Figure 6 reports estimated days to process 1 billion tokens at various context lengths using 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with DeepSpeed-Ulysses sequence parallelism—this accounts for both the reduced FLOPs from sparse attention and any overhead from the attention mask implementation. The ~50% training time reduction for AnchorAttention vs. Full Attention is computed under identical hardware and parallelism configurations.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For RULER evaluation, the paper averages performance across the last five checkpoints (saved every 10 steps over the final 50 training steps) of a single training run, rather than retraining with multiple random seeds. This is explicitly presented as a practical compromise between statistical rigor and computational cost. The paper does not report confidence intervals or standard deviations for RULER scores. No cross-validation over data splits is performed—all models are trained once on the full 2B-token sample and evaluated on the standard RULER test set. For LongBench, MMLU, and HellaSwag, single-checkpoint evaluation is used.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### AnchorAttention vs. Baselines on RULER (Table 5)

**Headline result.** AnchorAttention consistently achieves the highest RULER scores across all three datasets and all context lengths, with particularly large advantages at the longest contexts. The pattern holds whether training on 64K or 128K sequences, and whether using the original SlimPajama or the upsampled mixture.

**SlimPajama-64K results (Table 5, top section).** Trained on 64K-context sequences and evaluated from 4K to 64K tokens:

- At 64K tokens: AnchorAttention achieves 73.25, compared to Full Attention at 66.40, Intra-Doc Attention at 69.97, Intra-Doc + Reset at 70.03, and Intra-Doc + Interleaved Chunks at 60.59. AnchorAttention's advantage over Full Attention is +6.85 points, and over the best Intra-Doc variant (Reset) is +3.22 points.
- At 32K tokens: AnchorAttention achieves 75.97 vs. Full Attention 71.78 (+4.19), Intra-Doc 74.70 (+1.27), Intra-Doc + Reset 74.18 (+1.79).
- At 16K tokens: AnchorAttention achieves 82.91 vs. Full Attention 77.63 (+5.28), Intra-Doc 79.15 (+3.76).
- At 8K and 4K tokens: AnchorAttention maintains advantages of +1.62 and +0.85 over Full Attention respectively, showing that gains exist even at shorter contexts but are most pronounced at long ones.

A critical pattern emerges within the Intra-Doc Attention variants: **resetting position IDs (+Reset) provides modest improvements** over standard Intra-Doc at 64K (70.03 vs. 69.97) and 16K (80.27 vs. 79.15), but the gains are small (0.06–1.12 points) compared to AnchorAttention's jump (73.25). This confirms that (a) first-token position ID inconsistency is a real problem (reset helps), but (b) simply resetting is insufficient—AnchorAttention's shared anchor with continuous IDs provides substantially larger gains by also enabling full rotational frequency coverage.

**Interleaved chunks consistently hurt performance.** Intra-Doc + Interleaved Chunks achieves 60.59 at 64K—far below Full Attention (66.40) and the worst of all variants. AnchorAttention + Interleaved Chunks achieves 66.77 at 64K, partially recovering but still well below base AnchorAttention (73.25). The degradation is present at all context lengths (e.g., at 32K: Intra-Doc + Interleaved 66.52 vs. Intra-Doc 74.70; AnchorAttention + Interleaved 69.73 vs. AnchorAttention 75.97). This is an important negative result: a data augmentation that helps full attention (per Zhao et al., 2024a) is **incompatible with cross-document masking**, likely because chunking breaks the within-document coherence that sparse attention relies on.

**SlimPajama-128K results (Table 5, middle section).** Trained on 128K-context sequences and evaluated up to 128K tokens:

- At 128K tokens: AnchorAttention achieves 66.15, compared to Full Attention at 62.75 (+3.40), Intra-Doc at 64.31 (+1.84), and Intra-Doc + Reset at 65.75 (+0.40). The advantage over Intra-Doc + Reset narrows substantially compared to the 64K-trained models—only 0.40 points at 128K vs. 3.22 at 64K—suggesting that at the training context length, resetting position IDs recovers much of the benefit, and AnchorAttention's additional advantage from continuous IDs is smaller but still positive.
- At 64K tokens: AnchorAttention achieves 77.69, a substantial lead over Full Attention (70.56, +7.13), Intra-Doc (70.87, +6.82), and Intra-Doc + Reset (73.34, +4.35).
- At 32K and below: AnchorAttention maintains advantages of 2–4 points over Full Attention, though the gap narrows at shorter contexts (e.g., 4K: 90.60 vs. 88.85, +1.75).

**UpSampledMix-128K results (Table 5, bottom section).** The upsampled data mixture (which includes more long documents) generally improves Full Attention and Intra-Doc performance but **narrows the gap to AnchorAttention**:

- At 128K tokens: Full Attention achieves 63.70 (vs. 62.75 on SlimPajama-128K, a +0.95 gain from upsampling), Intra-Doc achieves 63.96 (vs. 64.31 on SlimPajama-128K, roughly flat), Intra-Doc + Reset achieves 64.10 (vs. 65.75, a -1.65 drop). AnchorAttention achieves 65.24 (vs. 66.15 on SlimPajama-128K, a -0.91 drop). The gap between AnchorAttention and the best non-Anchor variant shrinks to 65.24 - 64.10 = 1.14 points, compared to 66.15 - 65.75 = 0.40 on SlimPajama-128K.
- At 64K tokens: AnchorAttention achieves 76.11 vs. Full Attention 71.45 (+4.66) and Intra-Doc + Reset 74.55 (+1.56).
- The key insight from comparing SlimPajama-128K and UpSampledMix-128K: **AnchorAttention reduces dependency on carefully upsampled data**. On SlimPajama-128K, AnchorAttention at 128K achieves 66.15; on UpSampledMix-128K, it achieves 65.24—essentially identical performance despite the upsampled mixture's higher proportion of long sequences. In contrast, Full Attention gains from upsampling (62.75 → 63.70). This suggests that AnchorAttention's design (continuous position IDs + shared anchor) already provides sufficient exposure to long-range positional signals, making aggressive data upsampling less necessary.

**Domain tagging (AnchorAttention + Tag) shows inconsistent effects.** On SlimPajama-64K, AnchorAttention + Tag achieves 73.88 at 64K (slightly above AnchorAttention's 73.25) but 74.21 at 32K (below AnchorAttention's 75.97). On SlimPajama-128K, it achieves 65.46 at 128K (below AnchorAttention's 66.15) and 74.67 at 64K (below 77.69). On UpSampledMix-128K, 66.85 at 128K (above 65.24) but 73.52 at 64K (below 76.11). The pattern is clear: domain tagging sometimes helps and sometimes hurts, with no consistent directional effect. The paper concludes that domain tagging is not a reliable improvement over base AnchorAttention, though the AnchorAttention + Tag variant does help preserve MMLU performance (discussed in the general ability results below).

#### Cross-Model Evaluation (Table 6)

**Headline result.** AnchorAttention generalizes across model architectures, consistently outperforming Full Attention on RULER across LLaMA-3-8B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, and Qwen-1.5-1.8B, with the largest gains at the longest context lengths.

**LLaMA-3-8B (Table 6, top section).** Trained on SlimPajama-128K (or equivalent configuration):

- At 128K tokens: AnchorAttention achieves 51.49 vs. Full Attention 34.02, a massive +17.47-point advantage. AnchorAttention + Tag achieves 49.67, slightly below base AnchorAttention.
- At 64K tokens: AnchorAttention 70.99 vs. Full Attention 61.80 (+9.19).
- The gains are present at all context lengths: at 4K, AnchorAttention 88.72 vs. Full Attention 83.68 (+5.04).
- Notably, LLaMA-3-8B shows the largest absolute improvements of any model tested, with a 17.47-point gap at 128K. This may reflect LLaMA-3's larger pretraining scale (8B parameters, 15T+ tokens) making its positional encoding representations more vulnerable to BFloat16 precision corruption—or more reliant on the first-token position ID that AnchorAttention stabilizes.

**Mistral-7B-v0.3 (Table 6, middle section).**

- At 128K tokens: AnchorAttention achieves 47.46 vs. Full Attention 45.64 (+1.82). Domain tagging helps: AnchorAttention + Tag achieves 49.61 (+3.97 over Full Attention).
- At 64K tokens: AnchorAttention 61.26 vs. Full Attention 49.05 (+12.21). The gap is much larger at 64K than at 128K, an unusual pattern that may reflect Mistral's pretraining on shorter contexts (Mistral-7B's original context is 8K, with sliding window attention of 4K) affecting how it adapts to different extended lengths.
- At shorter contexts (4K–16K), the gap narrows but AnchorAttention maintains a consistent 3–6 point advantage.

**Qwen-1.5-1.8B (Table 6, bottom section).**

- At 128K tokens: AnchorAttention achieves 34.32 vs. Full Attention 33.56 (+0.76). Domain tagging: 35.84 (+2.28).
- At 64K tokens: AnchorAttention 44.31 vs. Full Attention 41.77 (+2.54).
- The improvements are more modest than for larger models, possibly because the 1.8B scale has fewer attention heads and dimensions, reducing the degrees of freedom through which BFloat16 precision errors can propagate. Alternatively, Qwen's different pretraining recipe and tokenizer (no BOS token, requiring EOS as anchor) may affect how AnchorAttention interacts with its positional representations.
- The paper notes that for Qwen, the `<eos>` token serves as the shared anchor, demonstrating that AnchorAttention's mechanism does not depend on any special property of the `<bos>` token specifically—any consistently positioned token can serve the anchoring role.

#### General Performance on Medium- and Short-Context Tasks (Table 7)

**Headline result.** AnchorAttention largely preserves the original model's general capabilities (MMLU, HellaSwag) and medium-context ICL performance (LongBench), while Full Attention and standard Intra-Doc Attention show varying degrees of degradation—particularly on MMLU.

**LongBench ICL (medium context, Table 7).** Evaluated on few-shot in-context learning tasks:

- SlimPajama-64K training: AnchorAttention achieves 65.38, compared to Full Attention 62.51 (+2.87), Intra-Doc 62.79 (+2.59), Intra-Doc + Reset 63.76 (+1.62). Adding domain tags further improves to 66.02. All attention mechanisms dramatically outperform the original LLaMA-2-7B's 6.22 on ICL—but this is expected since the original model was not trained on long contexts and struggles with ICL tasks requiring processing of long prompts.
- SlimPajama-128K training: AnchorAttention 51.85 vs. Full Attention 50.72 (+1.13), Intra-Doc 51.22 (+0.63). Performance drops across all methods compared to 64K training, likely because the 128K data mixture shifts domain proportions.
- UpSampledMix-128K training: AnchorAttention 50.17 vs. Full Attention 48.96 (+1.21). Similar to the SlimPajama-128K trend, with slightly lower absolute scores.

**HellaSwag (commonsense reasoning, Table 7).** The original LLaMA-2-7B achieves 71.39:

- SlimPajama-64K: AnchorAttention 70.78 (-0.61 from original), Full Attention 68.50 (-2.89), Intra-Doc 71.01 (-0.38). AnchorAttention's degradation is minimal and better than Full Attention's.
- SlimPajama-128K: AnchorAttention 70.51 (-0.88), Full Attention 69.46 (-1.93).
- UpSampledMix-128K: AnchorAttention 70.11 (-1.28), Full Attention 67.64 (-3.75). The upsampled mixture causes more degradation on HellaSwag for both methods, but AnchorAttention retains a clear advantage.

**MMLU (knowledge and reasoning, Table 7).** The original LLaMA-2-7B achieves 46.66. This is where the largest degradations occur:

- SlimPajama-64K: AnchorAttention 40.32 (-6.34 from original), Full Attention 33.93 (-12.73), Intra-Doc 36.94 (-9.72). All methods lose substantial MMLU performance, but AnchorAttention loses the least—roughly half the degradation of Full Attention.
- SlimPajama-128K: AnchorAttention 41.63 (-5.03), AnchorAttention + Tag 42.85 (-3.81), Full Attention 37.93 (-8.73). Domain tagging consistently helps preserve MMLU: +1.22 points over base AnchorAttention on this dataset.
- UpSampledMix-128K: AnchorAttention 41.15 (-5.51), AnchorAttention + Tag 42.03 (-4.63), Full Attention 40.58 (-6.08). Note that Full Attention actually performs relatively well on MMLU here (40.58), narrowing the gap to AnchorAttention to only 0.57 points.

**The tradeoff pattern.** The MMLU degradation is significant across all methods—even AnchorAttention loses 5–6 points from the original 46.66. This reflects a known tradeoff in continued pretraining: adapting to long contexts tends to degrade short-context knowledge and reasoning, as the model's parameters shift to accommodate the new positional and attention patterns. AnchorAttention's advantage is not that it eliminates this tradeoff but that it **minimizes the degradation** compared to Full Attention. The +Tag variant further helps, suggesting that domain identifiers provide a useful signal for preserving domain-specific knowledge (consistent with findings by Allen-Zhu & Li, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024b on domain tagging aiding knowledge retention).

On LongBench and HellaSwag, the degradation is much smaller—AnchorAttention-trained models remain within 1–2 points of the original on HellaSwag, and actually *improve* dramatically on LongBench ICL (from 6.22 to 50–65) since the original model could not handle the long prompts at all. This pattern suggests that MMLU's multiple-choice factual knowledge format is more sensitive to parameter shifts during continued training than HellaSwag's commonsense completion format, which may rely on more robust linguistic patterns.

#### RoPE Base Frequency Selection Experiments (Tables 2–4)

**Headline result.** Within the training context length, vanilla RoPE with an appropriately scaled base frequency consistently outperforms NTK-aware scaling and YaRN. Generalization beyond the training length favors YaRN. Optimal base values depend on target context length.

Within-training-length comparison (Table 2). Models trained on SlimPajama-64K with full attention, evaluated at their training length (64K) and shorter:

- Vanilla RoPE with base 10M achieves 69.43 at 64K, outperforming YaRN with base 10M (58.91, a gap of 10.52 points) and NTK with base 10M (58.75, gap of 10.68 points).
- The pattern holds across base values: at base 5M, RoPE 68.81 vs. YaRN 63.48 vs. NTK 61.45; at base 1M, RoPE 66.89 vs. YaRN 64.22 vs. NTK 62.47. Vanilla RoPE dominates at every base setting.
- At shorter context lengths (8K, 4K), vanilla RoPE also leads: at 4K, RoPE with base 5M achieves 89.28 vs. YaRN 84.01 and NTK 85.17.

**Generalization beyond training length (Table 4).** Models trained at 64K, evaluated at 128K:

- Vanilla RoPE with base 1M drops from 66.88 (at 64K) to 35.93 (at 128K), a catastrophic 30.95-point decline. RoPE with base 5M drops 14.83 points (68.81 → 53.98).
- YaRN with base 1M drops only 7.15 points (64.22 → 57.07), and with base 5M drops 11.18 points (64.48 → 53.30). YaRN's interpolation provides substantially better length generalization.
- NTK with base 10K drops 23.01 points (47.32 → 24.31), intermediate between RoPE and YaRN.

**Base value sweep at 32K (Table 3).** To isolate the effect of base value, models are trained at 32K context and evaluated at 32K, 16K, 8K, and 4K, with the average across all four lengths reported:

- Base = 500: average 20.80 (catastrophic failure—far too small for 32K)
- Base = 10K (default LLaMA): average 42.66
- Base = 200K: average 79.81 (large jump from 10K)
- Base = 600K: average 82.70 (near-peak)
- Base = 900K: average 82.64 (peak)
- Base = 5M: average 81.48 (slight decline from peak)
- Base = 1B: average 78.47 (clear overshoot—too large)

The sweet spot is broad: bases from 200K to 5M all achieve averages above 79, with the peak at 600K–900K. The paper's recommended scaling (1M for 32K, 5M for 64K, 10M for 128K) is somewhat more aggressive than the 32K sweep optimum (which suggests ~600K at 32K), but the paper notes these values are aligned with Liu et al. (2023b) and Men et al. (2024).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Position ID resetting within intra-document attention (Figure 3).** Resetting position IDs to 1 at the start of each document within intra-document attention consistently improves RULER performance compared to the continuous-ID convention, despite being theoretically equivalent under perfect precision. This directly demonstrates that BFloat16-induced positional encoding corruption has measurable downstream effects on task performance. The improvement is most pronounced at longer context lengths, consistent with the cumulative nature of the precision error. However, the improvement from resetting is substantially smaller than the gain from AnchorAttention, indicating that position ID consistency alone is insufficient without the shared anchor's continuous coverage.

**Interleaved chunks with cross-document masking (Table 5).** Applying interleaved chunks to either intra-document attention or AnchorAttention consistently degrades performance compared to non-interleaved versions. Intra-Doc + Interleaved Chunks on SlimPajama-64K achieves 60.59 at 64K (vs. 69.97 for standard Intra-Doc, a 9.38-point drop). On SlimPajama-128K, it achieves 53.74 at 128K (vs. 64.31, a 10.57-point drop). AnchorAttention + Interleaved Chunks partially recovers (66.77 at 64K vs. 73.25, a 6.48-point drop), but remains well below base AnchorAttention. This negative result establishes that interleaved chunks—a technique shown to help full-attention training (Zhao et al., 2024a)—is incompatible with cross-document attention masking. The paper hypothesizes that chunking breaks within-document coherence, and when cross-document attention is already masked, the resulting attention patterns are too fragmented for effective learning.

**Domain tagging (Table 5, Table 6, Table 7).** Adding domain identifier tokens (e.g., "Wikipedia", "CommonCrawl") prepended to each document produces inconsistent effects on long-context performance. On RULER at 128K: AnchorAttention + Tag achieves 65.46 on SlimPajama-128K (below 66.15 for base AnchorAttention) but 66.85 on UpSampledMix-128K (above 65.24). At shorter contexts, the pattern is similarly mixed. However, domain tagging **consistently helps preserve MMLU performance**: on SlimPajama-128K, AnchorAttention + Tag achieves 42.85 vs. 41.63 for base AnchorAttention (+1.22); on UpSampledMix-128K, 42.03 vs. 41.15 (+0.88). This suggests domain tagging aids in knowledge retention during continued pretraining, even if it does not improve long-context capabilities per se.

**Cross-model architecture robustness (Table 6).** AnchorAttention is evaluated on four model families (LLaMA-2-7B, LLaMA-3-8B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, Qwen-1.5-1.8B) spanning different pretraining recipes, tokenizers, and original context lengths. AnchorAttention outperforms Full Attention for all models at all context lengths, with particularly large gains for LLaMA-3-8B at 128K (51.49 vs. 34.02, +17.47). The Qwen-1.5-1.8B adaptation (using `<eos>` as anchor since Qwen lacks `<bos>`) achieves gains of +0.76 to +2.54 points, demonstrating that the anchor mechanism is not tied to a specific token type. The consistent cross-architecture improvement supports the claim that the BFloat16-RoPE precision issue is universal across RoPE-based models.

**Numerical accuracy of distributed implementation (Table 8).** AnchorContext using DeepSpeed-Ulysses sequence parallelism achieves zero logit difference compared to single-GPU FlashAttention2 baseline when processing the same 32K-sequence on 8 A100 GPUs. In contrast, Zigzag-Ring attention (from EasyContext, Zhang, 2023) exhibits a maximum logit difference of 0.75. This verifies that the distributed training implementation does not introduce additional numerical errors beyond those inherent to BFloat16—an important control given that the paper's central claim is about precision issues.

**Dataset configuration comparison (Table 5, across the three dataset sections).** Training on SlimPajama-64K vs. SlimPajama-128K vs. UpSampledMix-128K reveals that (1) 128K training generally outperforms 64K training at evaluation lengths above 64K (as expected, since the model sees longer contexts during training), (2) upsampling long sequences helps Full Attention and Intra-Doc Attention but provides minimal additional benefit for AnchorAttention, and (3) AnchorAttention achieves competitive performance on SlimPajama-128K without the need for careful data upsampling (e.g., AnchorAttention on SlimPajama-128K: 66.15 at 128K vs. on UpSampledMix-128K: 65.24). This suggests AnchorAttention reduces data engineering requirements for long-context training.

**RULER checkpoint averaging vs. single checkpoint (Figure 4).** RULER performance exhibits significant variance during training, with scores at 16K context fluctuating by several points across the final training steps. Perplexity, in contrast, plateaus early and provides no signal of this variance. The paper's recommendation to average over five checkpoints (last 50 steps, saving every 10) is an empirical response to this variance; reporting a single best checkpoint would overstate performance and reduce reproducibility.

**Task exclusion justification (Table 10, Appendix D).** The exclusion of NIAH Multikey 3 and Common Word Extraction for LLaMA-2-7B is supported by baseline evaluation at the original 4K context: the base model's CWE performance is unstable (76.8 at 4096 tokens vs. 24.46 at 4000 tokens, Table 11), and Multikey 3 accuracy is low across all 4K-context model variants (44.0 for base, 70.2 for chat). Since continued training cannot be expected to teach new capabilities not present within the original context window, excluding these tasks avoids penalizing models for failing tasks they could never perform. For the more capable models in Section 5.3, all 13 tasks are included.

**Training speed ablation (Figure 6).** The estimated training time for processing 1 billion tokens scales differently across attention mechanisms. At 128K context with DeepSpeed-Ulysses parallelism: Full Attention requires approximately 22 days per billion tokens; AnchorAttention requires approximately 10 days—a reduction of over 50%. At 256K context, the gap widens further: Full Attention approximately 60 days vs. AnchorAttention approximately 25 days. These estimates are based on the actual GPU utilization achieved by the AnchorContext implementation and account for the reduced attention computations from cross-document masking.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "RoPE's relative properties are compromised under BFloat16 precision"

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figure 1 (left) shows that attention patterns of a pretrained LLaMA-2-7B differ significantly when the same input is processed with different global positional shifts (Δ1 vs. Δ2) under BFloat16. This effect is absent under Float32 and is amplified by pretraining compared to random initialization. Figure 1 (middle) localizes the effect to the first token. Figure 1 (right) shows the effect grows with sequence length.

**Assessment:** The experiments provide strong, internally consistent evidence for the existence of a precision-induced deviation from RoPE's theoretical shift invariance. The experimental design is careful: using three precision conditions (BFloat16 pretrained, Float32 pretrained, BFloat16 random), controlling for sequence content by averaging over 50 sequences, and decomposing the effect both by token position and by sequence length. The claim that "RoPE's relative properties are compromised" is justified at the level of attention score deviations.

**Limitations not addressed:**
- **Causal mechanism not fully established.** The paper demonstrates *that* the first token's attention differs under positional shifts, but does not rigorously explain *why* the first token specifically. The hypothesis that position 0's identity rotation matrix is maximally sensitive to shift is plausible but not formally validated. An experiment measuring the precision error as a function of absolute position would strengthen this: do tokens at position 1000 show smaller attention differences under the same Δ shift than tokens at position 0? If so, does the magnitude scale with rotation angle?
- **Connection to downstream performance is correlational, not causal.** The paper shows that (a) BFloat16 causes attention deviations, and (b) position ID resetting (which eliminates inconsistency in first-token positions) improves RULER scores. But these are separate experiments. The paper does not directly demonstrate that the attention deviations measured in Figure 1 are the mechanism causing the performance differences in Figure 3—there could be other BFloat16-induced artifacts that position resetting incidentally addresses. A causal experiment would intervene on the attention deviation (e.g., by training a model where the first token's attention is explicitly stabilized) and show that this reproduces the performance gains.
- **Single model family for the detailed analysis.** The attention deviation analysis (Figure 1) is conducted only on LLaMA-2-7B. The paper does not replicate this analysis on LLaMA-3-8B, Mistral-7B, or Qwen-1.5—models that show AnchorAttention gains in Table 6. It is therefore possible that different models have different first-token deviation patterns, and the gains from AnchorAttention may arise from somewhat different mechanisms across architectures.

#### Claim 2: "The first token of a sequence contributes to the deviation of RoPE's relative properties, and this deviation becomes more pronounced with larger training window sizes"

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figure 1 (middle) strongly supports the first-token localization. Figure 1 (right) supports the scaling with sequence length for attention logits. The intra-document attention with reset IDs experiment (Figure 3) provides indirect support by showing that fixing first-token position IDs (via resetting) improves performance.

**Assessment:** The evidence for the first token's central role is strong. The per-token decomposition in Figure 1 (middle) is visually unambiguous: nearly all the attention difference between Δ1=0 and Δ2=16 comes from the first token. The sequence-length scaling is clear and monotonic. The reset-position-ID experiment triangulates the finding from a different angle (performance rather than attention metrics), with consistent results.

**Limitations:**
- **First token definition ambiguity.** In the attention difference analysis (Figure 1, middle), "first token" refers to position index 0 in a monolithic sequence of length 4096. In the long-context training experiments (Figure 3, Table 5), "first token" refers to the first token of each document within a concatenated sequence. These are not the same thing—the analysis shows that position 0 (absolute) is special, while the training experiments manipulate the position IDs of each document's initial token (which may be at varying absolute positions). The paper maps between these concepts implicitly (both are "first tokens" that should have position 0), but the mapping assumes that any document-initial token is functioning analogously to the BOS token of a monolithic sequence. This assumption is not directly validated.
- **The logit measurement (Figure 1, right) uses pre-softmax values.** This is a reasonable choice for comparability across sequence lengths, but it means the measured effect is on the *scale* of the logits, not on the attention distribution itself. With longer sequences, the softmax denominator grows, so a fixed logit difference produces a smaller attention score difference. The paper does not discuss whether the logit difference translates to behaviorally significant attention differences at very long sequences, or whether the softmax normalization partially compensates.

#### Claim 3: "AnchorAttention improves long-context performance and reduces training time by over 50%"

**Long-context performance (Table 5, Table 6):**
- **Supported with strong evidence across datasets and models.** AnchorAttention consistently outperforms Full Attention on RULER at all context lengths across three datasets (SlimPajama-64K, SlimPajama-128K, UpSampledMix-128K) and four model architectures. The margins are largest at long contexts (e.g., +6.85 at 64K on SlimPajama-64K, +3.40 at 128K on SlimPajama-128K) and hold even when compared to the improved Intra-Doc + Reset baseline.
- **The claim is specific to RULER.** Performance on LongBench ICL shows smaller gains (+1–3 points) and MMLU shows degradation for all methods (though AnchorAttention degrades less). The "improves long-context performance" claim is accurate for the RULER benchmark specifically. For real-world long-context tasks (LongBench), the improvement is positive but modest, and for short-context knowledge (MMLU), continued training itself causes degradation.

**Training time reduction (Figure 6):**
- **Supported under the reported configuration.** The estimated days to process 1B tokens show AnchorAttention approximately 2× faster than Full Attention at 128K context with DeepSpeed-Ulysses. The comparison is fair: same hardware (8× A100), same parallelism strategy, same sequence length.
- **The claim applies to the specific sequence composition of SlimPajama.** The speedup depends on documents being substantially shorter than the total context length. If most documents were near the full 128K length, the cross-document masking would provide minimal reduction (since there would be few document boundaries to mask). The paper does not report the speedup as a function of document length distribution—it is possible that on datasets with very different length distributions (e.g., mostly very long documents), the speedup would be smaller.
- **The speedup is estimated, not always directly measured.** Figure 6 presents "estimated number of days," not wall-clock measurements from actual training runs. The estimation methodology is not detailed, making it unclear whether overhead from the attention mask construction and sequence parallelism communication is fully accounted for.

**Limitations:**
- **No direct comparison to other sparse attention methods.** The paper compares AnchorAttention to Full Attention and Intra-Doc Attention, but not to other long-context attention mechanisms like LongLoRA's shift short attention (Chen et al., 2023b), StreamingLLM's window attention (Xiao et al., 2023), or InfLLM's memory-based approach (Xiao et al., 2024). It is possible that other sparse attention methods also avoid the BFloat16 precision issue (by limiting effective sequence length) while providing different accuracy-efficiency tradeoffs.
- **No evaluation at the full 256K context with RoPE base 50M.** The paper recommends base 50M for 256K but does not report RULER results at 256K. The 256K data point appears only in the training time estimation (Figure 6). This is an important missing experiment: does AnchorAttention continue to provide gains at 256K, or does the precision issue eventually overcome the anchor mechanism?
- **No comparison to Float32 training.** The paper argues BFloat16 is a practical necessity, which is reasonable—training models at 128K in Float32 would require approximately double the GPU memory, likely exceeding hardware limits. However, an experiment training a smaller model (e.g., Qwen-1.5-1.8B) in Float32 with Full Attention would directly test whether the performance gap between Full Attention and AnchorAttention is entirely attributable to precision, or whether AnchorAttention's sparse pattern provides benefits beyond precision mitigation.

#### Claim 4: "AnchorAttention reduces dependency on carefully upsampled data"

**What the experiments demonstrate:** In Table 5, AnchorAttention achieves 66.15 at 128K on SlimPajama-128K (without upsampling) and 65.24 at 128K on UpSampledMix-128K (with upsampling)—essentially identical performance. Full Attention, in contrast, gains from upsampling (62.75 → 63.70), and Intra-Doc + Reset does as well (from SlimPajama-128K to UpSampledMix-128K, noting that Intra-Doc + Reset on UpSampledMix is 64.10 vs. 65.75 on SlimPajama-128K—actually a decline, but Full Attention does improve).

**Assessment:** The evidence supports the claim for the specific upsampling strategy tested. AnchorAttention's performance is indeed more stable across dataset configurations than Full Attention's. The mechanism is plausible: AnchorAttention's continuous position IDs across the full 128K sequence ensure that the model sees all rotation frequencies regardless of individual document lengths, making explicit upsampling of long documents less critical.

**Limitations:**
- **Single upsampling strategy.** The paper tests only one upsampling recipe (based on Fu et al., 2024). It is possible that other data engineering approaches (e.g., different domain mixtures, targeted long-document inclusion rates, synthetic long-context data) would close the gap for Full Attention without AnchorAttention. The claim that AnchorAttention "reduces dependency on carefully upsampled data" is supported for the specific case studied, but the space of possible data engineering approaches is vast.
- **The claim conflates two benefits of upsampling.** Upsampling long documents serves two purposes: (1) exposing the model to a wider range of absolute positions (for RoPE frequency coverage), and (2) providing practice at attending over long distances within a single document. AnchorAttention's continuous position IDs solve (1), but they do not provide more practice at long-range within-document attention—the cross-document masking means long-distance attention only occurs within individual documents, whose lengths are not changed by AnchorAttention. If (2) were a limiting factor, AnchorAttention would still benefit from upsampling. The fact that AnchorAttention does *not* benefit from upsampling suggests that (1) is the dominant factor, but the paper does not test this directly (e.g., by comparing AnchorAttention with upsampled long documents to AnchorAttention with the same number of tokens but more, shorter documents).

#### Claim 5: "AnchorAttention preserves the original LLM's capabilities on general tasks"

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Table 7 shows HellaSwag and MMLU scores for all attention mechanisms trained on three datasets, compared to the original LLaMA-2-7B. AnchorAttention's HellaSwag scores (70.11–70.78) are close to the original (71.39), with degradations of 0.61–1.28 points. MMLU shows larger degradations (40.32–42.85 vs. original 46.66, losses of 3.81–6.34 points).

**Assessment:** The claim is supported for HellaSwag but qualified for MMLU. "Preserves" is too strong for MMLU—a 6-point drop on a 46.66 baseline is a ~13% relative degradation, which is substantively meaningful. The paper is transparent about this (Table 7 reports the absolute numbers), but the executive summary's "largely preserving the original LLM's capabilities on general tasks" requires the reader to accept a ~5-point MMLU drop as "largely preserved." Compared to Full Attention's ~8–12 point MMLU drop, AnchorAttention does preserve *more* capability, but it does not preserve the original level.

**Limitations:**
- **No evaluation of generation quality or instruction following.** MMLU and HellaSwag are multiple-choice benchmarks. They do not assess whether the model's long-form generation quality, factual accuracy, or instruction-following ability are preserved after continued training. Real-world "general capabilities" include these dimensions.
- **No comparison to the original model with extended context but no additional training.** The original LLaMA-2-7B with a training-free extension method (e.g., NTK-aware scaling at inference) would provide a baseline for MMLU/HellaSwag that doesn't involve any continued training at all. If such a baseline maintained 46.66 MMLU and 71.39 HellaSwag, it would clarify how much of the degradation is due to continued training itself vs. the specific attention mechanism used during training.

#### Experiments That Would Have Strengthened the Paper

**Direct intervention on first-token precision.** An experiment that artificially increases the precision of the first token's attention computation (e.g., computing only the first token's key-query products in Float32 while keeping the rest in BFloat16) and showing that this reproduces AnchorAttention's performance gains would provide strong causal evidence for the paper's central mechanism.

**Scaling curves.** The paper evaluates at one training budget (2B tokens) and one model scale per architecture (1.8B, 7B, 8B). How does the advantage of AnchorAttention scale with training tokens? With model size? Does the BFloat16 precision issue become more or less severe as models grow larger (more heads → more dimensions for error to propagate) or as they are trained longer (more steps for the model to learn reliance on corrupted positional signals)?

**Precision error measurement at the rotation matrix level.** The paper measures the downstream effect (attention differences) but not the source (rotation matrix precision). Measuring the element-wise difference between R_i,θ computed in Float32 vs. BFloat16, or between the product q_i^⊤ R_{j-i,θ} k_j in both precisions, would quantify exactly how much error is introduced at each stage and whether it accumulates multiplicatively.

**AnchorAttention at pretraining scale.** All experiments are continued pretraining from existing checkpoints. Would AnchorAttention work for training a model from scratch? The paper's analysis suggests the precision issue is present even in randomly initialized models (Figure 1, left, green line, though amplified by pretraining). Testing AnchorAttention in a full pretraining run (or even a medium-scale one, e.g., 100B tokens) would determine whether the mechanism is specific to the continued-training setting or fundamental to RoPE+BFloat16 more broadly.

**Comparison to simpler fixes.** If the first token is the problem, what about simply computing the first token's attention in Float32 (a one-line precision override in the attention kernel) while keeping the rest in BFloat16? Or skipping the first token in attention computation entirely (as StreamingLLM does for different reasons)? These simpler interventions might capture most of the gain without the full AnchorAttention mechanism, and comparing them would isolate how much of AnchorAttention's benefit comes from precision stabilization vs. from the sparse attention pattern's other properties (reduced computation, consistent positional references, etc.).

**Failure mode analysis.** The paper attributes performance improvements to mitigating BFloat16 precision errors, but does not present examples or qualitative analysis of *how* the precision errors manifest in model behavior. For instance: does the model make different types of errors on RULER tasks under Full Attention vs. AnchorAttention? Are there specific attention patterns or positional biases that AnchorAttention corrects? Such analysis would bridge the gap between the low-level precision measurements (Figure 1) and the high-level benchmark scores (Tables 5–7).

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for and Potentially Dominant

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper diagnoses the BFloat16-RoPE precision issue by measuring attention deviations and identifies that resetting position IDs per document improves performance over continuous IDs in intra-document attention (Figure 3). This diagnostic process—generating 50 sequences of length 4096, running them through a pretrained model at multiple positional shifts, and computing per-token attention differences (Figure 1)—is a one-time analysis cost not needed for deployment. However, the paper's evaluation of AnchorAttention itself assumes that the training data's document boundaries are known (to apply cross-document masking) and that a `<bos>` token (or `<eos>` for Qwen) is available as the shared anchor. These are reasonable assumptions for continued pretraining on curated datasets like SlimPajama, but the paper does not discuss the cost or feasibility of ensuring clean document boundaries and consistent anchor tokens in other settings (e.g., web-scraped data with no document delimiters, or models pretrained without `<bos>` tokens).

**The consequence.** For practitioners working with raw or minimally processed corpora, implementing AnchorAttention may require a document segmentation preprocessing step that the paper's headline results do not account for. If document boundaries are noisy or missing, the cross-document masking could incorrectly cut within-document contexts or fail to block cross-document attention where it should, degrading the very positional consistency AnchorAttention aims to provide. The paper does not evaluate sensitivity to document boundary quality—all experiments use SlimPajama, which has clean, known document boundaries inherited from its data curation pipeline (Soboleva et al., 2023).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None directly. Section 3.3 explicitly states the design assumes the `<bos>` token as the shared anchor and that "the `<bos>` token typically lacks explicit semantic meaning," but there is no ablation on what happens when document boundaries are ambiguous (e.g., overlapping documents, missing separators, or synthetic sequences without natural document structure). The interleaved chunks experiment (Table 5) provides indirect evidence that breaking document coherence harms performance—Intra-Doc + Interleaved Chunks drops from 69.97 to 60.59 at 64K on SlimPajama-64K—but this tests a deliberate, uniform chunking strategy, not the realistic scenario of noisy or imperfect boundaries.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss document boundary detection as a requirement for AnchorAttention, does not provide guidance on minimum document boundary quality, and does not evaluate robustness to boundary noise. This is a deployment concern left entirely to future work.

---

### No Evaluation of AnchorAttention During Full Pretraining from Scratch

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper are **continued pretraining** from existing checkpoints (LLaMA-2-7B, LLaMA-3-8B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, Qwen-1.5-1.8B), trained for only 2000 steps on 2 billion tokens. The paper states in Section 4.1 that this follows Fu et al. (2024)'s finding that "1B tokens suffice for learning long-context abilities." This is sufficient for the paper's scope—diagnosing and mitigating the BFloat16-RoPE interaction in long-context extension—but it leaves open the question of whether AnchorAttention would be effective (or even necessary) if used from the beginning of pretraining on a fresh model.

**The consequence.** There are two distinct failure modes that cannot be ruled out without pretraining-scale experiments:

1. **AnchorAttention may be unnecessary at pretraining scale.** The paper shows (Figure 1, left, green line) that randomly initialized models exhibit smaller attention deviations under BFloat16 than pretrained models. This suggests the precision issue is amplified by pretraining—the model *learns* to depend on the corrupted positional signal. If AnchorAttention were used from the start of pretraining, the model might never develop this dependency, making AnchorAttention's specific mechanism (shared anchor + continuous IDs) less critical. A simpler approach (e.g., consistently using position 0 for BOS across all sequences) might suffice.

2. **AnchorAttention's cross-document masking may limit pretraining quality.** During full pretraining on trillions of tokens, cross-document attention might serve a useful purpose—exposing the model to relationships between documents concatenated in the same batch, even if those documents are nominally unrelated. Removing this signal with AnchorAttention's masking could affect the model's ability to learn certain linguistic patterns, discourse structures, or long-range dependencies that span document boundaries in pretraining data. The paper's 2B-token continued training experiments cannot detect such effects because the model's fundamental language capabilities are already established from the original pretraining.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Only the continued-training setting. Section 8 (Limitations) explicitly acknowledges: "we cannot exhaust all aspects, such as study its effectiveness for pretraining, optimization hyperparameters and additional data mixtures. We also limit ourselves to the 10B-scale model size regime with 2B tokens, which may limit the generalizability of our findings." This is transparent but does not reduce the uncertainty for practitioners considering AnchorAttention for full pretraining runs.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation in Section 8 but provides no experimental evidence or theoretical argument about what would happen at pretraining scale. This is flagged as future work only in the most general terms.

---

### MMLU Degradation of 5–6 Points Remains Unresolved Despite AnchorAttention

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper claims that AnchorAttention "largely preserves the original LLM's capabilities on general tasks" (abstract) and that "models trained with AnchorAttention better preserve general abilities from the pretraining stage compared to those using full attention or intra-document attention" (Section 5.4). The "largely preserves" framing implicitly treats a ~5-point MMLU drop as acceptable, given that Full Attention loses 8–12 points.

**The consequence.** For production deployments where MMLU scores correlate with downstream task performance (e.g., factual QA, knowledge-intensive applications), a 5–6 point degradation from 46.66 to ~41 is substantively meaningful—it represents roughly 11–13% relative decline on a benchmark that measures broad knowledge and reasoning. The paper's own numbers in Table 7 show:

- SlimPajama-64K AnchorAttention: 40.32 (vs. original 46.66, −6.34)
- SlimPajama-128K AnchorAttention: 41.63 (−5.03)
- UpSampledMix-128K AnchorAttention: 41.15 (−5.51)

Domain tagging partially mitigates this (e.g., 42.85 on SlimPajama-128K, −3.81), but does not eliminate it. The persistent gap means that a practitioner adopting AnchorAttention for long-context extension must accept a non-trivial tradeoff in short-context knowledge and reasoning capability, even if the tradeoff is smaller than with alternative attention mechanisms.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 7 reports the raw MMLU and HellaSwag scores. The paper is transparent with the numbers but does not discuss the practical significance of a 5–6 point MMLU drop. The executive summary's "largely preserving" characterization is not quantitatively qualified—readers unfamiliar with MMLU score scales may not recognize that a 5-point gap is consequential. For reference, the gap between LLaMA-2-7B (46.66) and LLaMA-2-13B (approximately 55 on MMLU) is about 8 points—so a 5-point drop from continued training represents undoing a substantial fraction of the improvement one would get from scaling to the next model size.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies domain tagging as a partial mitigation (+1–2 MMLU points in most configurations), but this is presented as a secondary ablation rather than a core component of the method. No other mitigation strategies are explored. The paper does not investigate whether the MMLU degradation is due to catastrophic forgetting from the continued training data distribution, from the change in attention patterns (sparse attention disrupting learned representations), or from the positional encoding adaptations (RoPE base frequency changes). Without this diagnosis, it is unclear whether the degradation is addressable through better training recipes (e.g., data mixing with short-context data, learning rate schedules, progressive context extension) or is an inherent cost of sparse attention.

---

### Single Long-Context Benchmark (RULER) Limits Generality of the Performance Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline long-context performance claims rest primarily on the RULER benchmark (Hsieh et al., 2024). While RULER is specifically designed to stress-test long-context capabilities up to 128K tokens—addressing a genuine gap in prior benchmarks like LongBench that saturate at 16K (Section 4.2)—the paper's exclusive reliance on RULER for long-context evaluation means that the measured improvements may not generalize to other types of long-context tasks.

**The consequence.** RULER's task suite emphasizes needle-in-a-haystack retrieval, variable tracing, word frequency aggregation, and question answering over synthetic long documents. These tasks primarily test the model's ability to **locate, track, and aggregate information** across long contexts. They do not test other long-context capabilities that matter in practice:

- **Multi-hop reasoning over long documents:** Combining information from multiple, dispersed parts of a long text to answer a question that requires synthesis, not just retrieval.
- **Long-form generation conditioned on long context:** Producing coherent, contextually grounded outputs that require maintaining consistency with details from throughout a long input.
- **Cross-document reasoning:** Tasks like multi-document summarization or comparative analysis, where relationships *between* documents matter—precisely the cross-document attention that AnchorAttention masks out during training (though this masking applies only during training; at inference, the model could theoretically attend across documents if the attention mask is removed, but the model was never trained to do so).

An AnchorAttention-trained model that excels at RULER might perform poorly on tasks requiring cross-document attention (since it was never trained to do it) or on generation tasks that require maintaining long-range coherence (since RULER tasks are primarily discriminative or extractive). Without evaluation on a more diverse set of long-context tasks, the claim that AnchorAttention "improves long-context performance" is accurate for RULER specifically but not established for long-context capabilities broadly.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does include LongBench results (Table 7), but LongBench's tasks cap at ~16K effective context, making them a measure of medium-context rather than true long-context capability. LongBench ICL scores show AnchorAttention improving over Full Attention (+1–3 points across datasets), suggesting that benefits extend to medium-context ICL tasks. However, LongBench does not test cross-document reasoning at scale (its multi-doc QA tasks are relatively short) or long-form generation conditioned on long contexts. The paper acknowledges this limitation implicitly in Section 4.2 when justifying why RULER is the primary benchmark:

> "tasks in LongBench (Bai et al., 2023) can be adequately addressed with a context length of 16K tokens. While 16K tokens represent a medium-length context, this does not fully challenge models like ours that can handle 64K or even 128K tokens."

But the corollary—that RULER's task types do not cover all important long-context capabilities—is not discussed.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not evaluate on other long-context benchmarks that test complementary capabilities, such as InfiniteBench (Zhang et al., 2024c, which includes long-context retrieval, summarization, and QA tasks at 100K+ tokens), NoCha (Karpinska et al., 2024, testing long-context coherence in narrative understanding), or LongCite (Zhang et al., 2024a, testing fine-grained citation across long contexts). The paper's recommendation to use RULER as the primary benchmark is well-justified for the specific capabilities it tests, but the exclusive reliance on it means the generality of AnchorAttention's benefits remains uncertain.

### Cross-Document Masking at Training Time Precludes Learning Cross-Document Relationships

**The assumption or constraint.** AnchorAttention's design enforces cross-document masking during training: tokens in document A cannot attend to tokens in document B (Section 3.3, Rule 3). This is explicitly a *training-time* constraint—the paper does not claim that the model cannot attend across documents at inference time, only that it was never trained to do so effectively within the long-context continued training phase. The shared anchor token (position 0) is the only connection point between documents.

**The consequence.** Any downstream task requiring the model to reason across multiple documents—comparing claims, resolving contradictions, synthesizing information, tracking entities across documents—relies on attention mechanisms that were explicitly blocked during the 2000-step continued training phase. While the base model (e.g., LLaMA-2-7B) was originally pretrained with full attention (including across documents in its 4K-context pretraining), the continued training on long contexts with AnchorAttention means the model has:

1. Not practiced cross-document attention at scale (64K–128K contexts with many documents).
2. Adapted its attention patterns, positional representations, and parameter values under a regime where cross-document attention is impossible.
3. Potentially overwritten or degraded the cross-document attention capabilities it acquired during original pretraining, since those capabilities are never exercised during the 2000-step long-context phase (a form of catastrophic forgetting specific to cross-document attention).

At inference time, even if the cross-document mask is removed, the model's ability to effectively use cross-document attention may be compromised because the parameters have been optimized under the AnchorAttention regime. The paper provides no evidence about how AnchorAttention-trained models perform when evaluated with cross-document attention enabled at inference.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The LongBench results (Table 7) include a "Multi-Doc QA" category (shown in the full LongBench breakdown in Table 12, Appendix E), which tests precisely this capability. On SlimPajama-64K: Full Attention achieves 9.68, AnchorAttention achieves 9.72 (essentially identical). On SlimPajama-128K: Full Attention 6.36, AnchorAttention 6.32 (again identical). On UpSampledMix-128K: Full Attention 6.12, AnchorAttention 6.30 (within noise). These results suggest that AnchorAttention does not *degrade* cross-document QA performance relative to Full Attention, despite blocking cross-document attention during training. However, LongBench's multi-doc QA tasks use relatively short documents and contexts—it is unclear whether this pattern would hold at 64K–128K contexts with many more documents, where cross-document attention becomes more critical for finding and synthesizing information across documents.

**Mitigation status.** Not directly addressed. The paper does not discuss the tradeoff between the efficiency benefits of cross-document masking and the potential cost to cross-document reasoning capabilities. The LongBench Multi-Doc QA results provide some reassurance that cross-document capabilities are not catastrophically degraded at medium context lengths, but this is an incidental finding, not a systematic investigation. Section 8 (Future Work) does not mention cross-document attention restoration or fine-tuning strategies to recover it.

### Training Speedup Depends on Document Length Distribution and Is Not Validated at 256K+

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper reports that AnchorAttention "reduces training time by more than 50% compared to standard full attention mechanisms" (abstract, Section 5.5). Figure 6 presents estimated training time in days to process 1 billion tokens at context lengths from 8K to 256K, showing AnchorAttention consistently taking less than half the time of Full Attention. However, these estimates depend critically on the assumption that the concatenated training sequences are composed of many documents whose individual lengths are substantially shorter than the total context length—the regime where AnchorAttention's complexity O(Σ L_d^2) is significantly less than full attention's O((Σ L_d)²).

**The consequence.** In deployment scenarios where the data distribution differs from SlimPajama's document length profile, the speedup may be dramatically smaller:

- **Long-document regimes:** If training on corpora dominated by very long documents (e.g., books, legal documents, scientific papers where individual documents approach the full 128K context length), the number of documents per sequence shrinks. In the limit where each 128K sequence contains only one document plus the anchor, AnchorAttention computes attention over the full 128K tokens (the single document's internal attention) plus O(L) anchor connections—essentially the same as full attention, with no speedup.

- **Mixed length distributions:** The paper's UpSampledMix-128K dataset deliberately increases the proportion of long sequences (Appendix C, Figure 8). While Table 5 shows that AnchorAttention's accuracy is robust to this change, the training time on UpSampledMix may be higher than on SlimPajama-128K because longer documents mean fewer document boundaries and thus less reduction from cross-document masking. The paper does not report training time separately per dataset configuration.

- **256K+ contexts:** Figure 6 includes estimates at 256K, showing AnchorAttention at approximately 25 days per billion tokens vs. Full Attention at approximately 60 days. However, the paper does not report any 256K training runs or RULER evaluations at 256K (the recommended RoPE base of 50M for 256K is given in Section 4.3 but never used in experiments). The 256K speedup estimate is therefore a pure extrapolation, not validated by actual training. At 256K, sequence parallelism communication overhead, memory constraints, and potential numerical issues (the very precision problems AnchorAttention mitigates) may behave differently than at 128K.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 5.5 provides numerical accuracy validation at 32K context (Table 8) and training time estimates at multiple context lengths (Figure 6). The paper does not report actual wall-clock training times for the experimental runs—only the "estimated number of days" in Figure 6, whose estimation methodology is not detailed. There is no ablation on how the speedup varies with document length distribution. The 50%+ reduction is presented as a single number without sensitivity analysis.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss how document length distribution affects training throughput, does not provide throughput measurements on the actual training runs, and does not validate the speedup at 256K. For a practitioner with a specific data distribution, the paper provides no tools or guidance to estimate the expected speedup for their use case. The 50%+ claim should be understood as an upper bound achievable under favorable document length distributions (many short documents per sequence), not as a guaranteed speedup independent of data characteristics.
