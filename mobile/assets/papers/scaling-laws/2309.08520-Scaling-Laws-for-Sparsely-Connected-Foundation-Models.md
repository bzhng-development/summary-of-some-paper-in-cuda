# Scaling Laws for Sparsely-Connected Foundation Models

**ArXiv:** [2309.08520](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08520)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper studies how parameter sparsity affects the scaling behavior of Transformers trained on massive datasets — Vision Transformers on JFT-4B and T5 models on C4 — and derives the first **scaling law** relating weight sparsity $S$, number of non-zero parameters $N$, and amount of training data $D$ in a unified formula $L(S, N, D)$.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies how parameter sparsity affects the scaling behavior of Transformers trained on massive datasets — Vision Transformers on JFT-4B and T5 models on C4 — and derives the first **scaling law** relating weight sparsity $S$, number of non-zero parameters $N$, and amount of training data $D$ in a unified formula $L(S, N, D)$. Through extensive sweeps across model sizes, sparsity levels, and training durations, the authors characterize **optimal sparsity** $S_{\text{opt}}$ — the sparsity level yielding the best performance for a given effective model size and training budget — finding that optimal sparsity increases with longer training (e.g., for ViT/JFT, 50% sparsity becomes optimal at < 2× Chinchilla training duration under sparse FLOPs accounting). The analysis establishes that sparsity acts as a multiplicative capacity multiplier (a 75% sparse model matches a dense model with ~2.15× more non-zero parameters) independent of data scaling, and that sparse models can outperform dense ones at fixed non-zero parameter counts — but only when training extends well beyond the dense compute-optimal regime where diminishing returns set in.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don’t Know How Sparsity Interacts with Scale

The fundamental question this paper tackles is: **how does weight sparsity affect the scaling behavior of foundation models—Transformers trained on massive datasets—and can sparse models actually outperform dense ones when all factors are fairly accounted for?** Despite an enormous body of work on neural network pruning and sparsity (Hoefler et al., 2021), the field lacked any systematic understanding of how sparsity interacts with the two primary scaling axes of modern foundation models: model size (number of parameters) and training data quantity. Prior to this work, there was no scaling law that jointly related sparsity, non-zero parameter count, and training data—meaning practitioners had no principled way to decide whether, when, and how much sparsity to apply to a given model and training budget.

This gap is significant for several practical reasons the authors highlight throughout Section 2 and Section 6:

- **Inference efficiency at scale**: Foundation models are deployed at enormous scale (billions of inference queries), and weight sparsity is one of the most direct ways to reduce both memory footprint and computational cost during inference. If sparsity can be applied without sacrificing quality—or even while improving it at a fixed parameter budget—the economic and environmental implications are substantial.
- **Training efficiency**: If sparsity can be exploited during training (not just inference), it could reduce the FLOPs required to reach a target performance level. Understanding when this is possible requires knowing how sparsity and training compute interact.
- **Deployment on resource-constrained hardware**: Sparse models can run on devices with limited memory and compute, but only if the sparsity levels are chosen appropriately. Without scaling laws, practitioners cannot predict what sparsity level a given model can sustain at a target quality.
- **Hardware co-design**: Modern GPUs support structured sparsity patterns like 2:4 (Mishra et al., 2021; Pool & Yu, 2021), where exactly 2 out of every 4 consecutive weights are non-zero, providing direct speedups. Understanding whether such patterns follow the same scaling laws as unstructured sparsity is essential for hardware-algorithm co-design.

### The Specific Gap: Sparsity Scaling Laws Don’t Exist

The field has mature scaling laws for dense models. Kaplan et al. (2020) established that the validation loss $L$ of dense Transformers follows a power-law relationship with model size $N$ and training data $D$, of the form $L(N, D) = (a_N / N)^{b_N} + (a_D / D)^{b_D} + c$, where $c$ represents an irreducible loss floor. Hoffmann et al. (2022) extended this to characterize compute-optimal training: for a given FLOPs budget, there exists an optimal model size and training duration (the "Chinchilla" line) that minimizes loss. These laws have been extended to model shape optimization (Alabdulmohsin et al., 2023), routing mechanisms (Clark et al., 2022), data repetition (Muennighoff et al., 2023), and downstream tasks (Caballero et al., 2023)—but **not to weight sparsity**.

This absence is not a minor oversight. It means that when a practitioner asks "should I train a dense model with $N$ parameters, or a 75% sparse model with the same $N$ non-zero parameters?", there is no principled answer. Worse, the question has hidden complexity: the sparse model requires training a larger dense backbone (size $N / (1 - S)$) that is gradually pruned, consuming additional training FLOPs. Whether that extra training cost pays off in improved inference-time performance at fixed non-zero parameters is exactly what a scaling law would predict, but no such law existed.

### Why Existing Pruning Benchmarks Don’t Answer This Question

The paper identifies a fundamental mismatch between standard pruning evaluation practice and the reality of foundation model training (Section 2). Most pruning research is conducted on benchmarks like ResNet-50/ImageNet or BERT/GLUE, where three critical assumptions hold that **break down completely** for massive-data Transformers:

**1. Training data is limited and models saturate.** On ImageNet, training a ResNet-50 for significantly longer than the standard 90-epoch schedule leads to overfitting—the dense baseline plateaus or degrades (Kuznedelev et al., 2023). This creates an artificially favorable environment for pruning: a sparse model that continues training for additional epochs can appear to "recover" accuracy relative to a dense baseline that has already saturated, but this comparison conflates sparsification benefits with extended training benefits. In contrast, on massive datasets like JFT-4B (4 billion images) or C4 (150+ billion tokens), dense models continue to improve with prolonged training for extremely long horizons (Zhai et al., 2022)—overfitting is virtually non-existent. Quoting Section 2:

> "In a standard setting such as ResNet50/ImageNet, significantly increasing the training time of the dense model will quickly run into overfitting. In contrast, the performance improvements of ViT/JFT only start to saturate after extremely long training time; overfitting is virtually non-existent."

The implication is stark: if you sparsify a ViT pretrained on 100M images over another 100M images, you should **not** compare to the initial 100M-image dense model. The proper baseline is a dense model trained on 200M images, which will likely be significantly more accurate. This makes the standard pruning evaluation protocol—comparing a pruned model to its unpruned parent at the same initial training budget—fundamentally unfair in the foundation model regime.

**2. Naively scaled-down dense models are uncompetitive.** On ImageNet, a 2.5M-parameter model obtained by simply scaling down ResNet-50's width/depth is not competitive with purpose-built efficient architectures like MobileNets (Howard et al., 2017) or EfficientNets (Tan & Le, 2019). This is why pruning papers typically omit a comparison against a "small dense model trained from scratch with the same parameter count as the pruned model"—the small dense model would be so architecturally suboptimal that the comparison would be meaningless. However, for Transformer models trained on massive datasets, **basic width and depth scaling produces a very strong family of models**. A 50% sparse Transformer with $N$ non-zero parameters should be compared against a dense Transformer with $N$ parameters trained on the same total data/compute budget—and that dense baseline will be genuinely competitive.

**3. Training compute is cheap enough to reach convergence.** On standard benchmarks, compute is abundant enough that all models are trained to convergence. This means the concept of compute efficiency—whether a smaller model trained longer outperforms a larger model trained for the same FLOPs budget—is largely irrelevant. But for foundation models, compute efficiency is central (Hoffmann et al., 2022): a smaller Transformer trained for longer can outperform a larger one trained with the same budget. This renders comparisons even more challenging. As the paper states:

> "it means that a 50% sparse model obtained from pruning a model that was pretrained for 100K steps should be compared to a 2× smaller dense model trained for the same compute, i.e., 200K steps plus the computational cost of pruning."

In summary, the paper argues that in a fair foundation model pruning setup, sparsity should not be able to leverage (a) increased training time through the sparsification process itself, (b) a better-optimized dense base architecture, or (c) more total training compute than the dense baseline. All three factors must be controlled for. The key open question, which the paper explicitly poses, is:

> "it is currently unclear whether weight-sparse foundation models can win at all in this highly challenging setting, where all these factors are properly accounted for."

### Conflicting Signals in Prior Work on Sparse Foundation Models

The limited prior work on sparsifying large-scale Transformers provides ambiguous and sometimes contradictory evidence, which further motivates the need for a unified scaling law:

**Gopher (Rae et al., 2021, Appendix):** Conducted pruning experiments on a decoder-only language model and found that, when trained for the same number of steps, sparse models can outperform dense ones. However, this comparison did **not** account for the increased compute spent producing those sparse models relative to dense models trained with the same data. The paper notes that this leaves open the question of whether sparsity provides genuine benefits when training cost is properly accounted for.

**Cerebras (2022):** Pruned a GPT-like model and reported a ~5× gain—significantly better than the Gopher results. However, the paper points out that this was achieved in a setting where the sparse model used > 5× more training data than the dense comparison point, making it an unfair comparison by the paper's criteria. When the authors query their own fitted T5 scaling law with this setup, they can predict the observed advantage purely from the extended training, without invoking any special sparsity benefit.

**SparseGPT (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023):** Showed that post-training pruning becomes easier as model size increases, but did not perform any retraining and observed this effect primarily relative to the unpruned base model, not in terms of improvements over the Pareto frontier of size vs. loss. The paper suggests this finding is likely more about pretrained models' robustness to perturbation than about inherent sparsifiability of larger architectures.

These works provide suggestive evidence that sparsity might be useful at scale, but none establishes a rigorous framework for when and how much. The field needs a joint scaling law that can predict whether a sparse model with $N$ non-zero parameters trained on $D$ tokens with sparsity $S$ will outperform a dense model with $N$ parameters trained on the same data (or same compute).

### The Closest Prior Work: Rosenfeld et al. (2021)

The paper explicitly positions itself relative to Rosenfeld et al. (2021), which studied the relationship between width, depth, and weight density for pruning pretrained ResNets on CIFAR-10. This is the closest precursor in terms of studying sparsity across model scales. However, the paper identifies several critical limitations that make those results inapplicable to foundation models:

- **Dataset scale**: CIFAR-10 is many orders of magnitude smaller than JFT-4B or C4. In the CIFAR-10 regime, data is limited and overfitting is a concern, making the data/compute dimension much less relevant than in the foundation model regime.
- **Architecture scope**: ResNets on CIFAR-10 operate in a fundamentally different scaling regime than Transformers on massive datasets. The scaling behavior—particularly the absence of overfitting and the strong performance of simple width-scaled dense baselines—is qualitatively different.
- **No data/compute scaling dimension**: Rosenfeld et al. (2021) focused on width, depth, and density, but did not model the interaction with training data quantity or compute budget, which the paper argues is the central dimension for foundation model sparsity.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as filling the gap between mature dense scaling laws and the complete absence of sparse scaling laws. Its contributions are:

1. **A joint scaling law $L(S, N, D)$** that unifies sparsity, model size, and training data into a single predictive formula, validated across vision (ViT/JFT-4B) and language (T5/C4) domains.

2. **A rigorous framework for fair evaluation** that controls for training data, model size, and compute costs simultaneously—setting up the problem so that sparsity must genuinely outperform equivalently-sized dense models rather than exploiting artifacts of the evaluation protocol.

3. **Analytical derivation of optimal sparsity $S_{\text{opt}}$** as a function of inference size and training budget, enabling principled decisions about whether and how much sparsity to apply.

4. **Empirical characterization of the regime where sparsity wins**: sparsity becomes optimal when training extends beyond the dense compute-optimal (Chinchilla) point, where dense training encounters diminishing returns and sparsity provides an alternative avenue for improvement.

The paper draws a direct parallel to compute-optimal pretraining scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022), but applied to the sparsity dimension rather than the model-size-vs-data tradeoff. Just as Chinchilla showed that for a given FLOPs budget, there is an optimal model size (not "as large as possible"), this paper shows that for a given inference parameter budget and training compute, there is an optimal sparsity level (not "as sparse as possible" or "always dense").

### The Practical Stakes

Beyond the theoretical contribution, the paper is motivated by concrete practical questions that face anyone deploying large models at scale. Section 6 makes this explicit:

- **Should I train a dense model or a sparse one for inference?** If a 75% sparse model with $N$ non-zero parameters performs as well as a dense model with ~2.15$N$ parameters (as the paper finds), and if sparse inference is supported in hardware, the sparse model offers both memory and latency advantages.
- **At what point in training should I apply sparsity?** If sparsity only becomes beneficial after training well beyond Chinchilla (which many production models already do—e.g., Llama2-7B at ~14× Chinchilla), then the decision depends on the specific training budget.
- **Does the sparsity pattern matter?** If structured patterns like 2:4 (which have direct hardware support) follow the same scaling laws as unstructured sparsity, practitioners can confidently use hardware-friendly patterns without sacrificing the scaling benefits.

The paper's framework provides quantitative answers to all of these questions, grounded in empirical scaling laws rather than rules of thumb or small-scale experiments.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The "system" here is not a deployable model or algorithm, but a **joint scaling law** — a parametric equation that predicts the validation loss of a Transformer model given three high-level design choices: the fraction of weights that are zero (sparsity $S$), the number of remaining non-zero parameters ($N$), and the quantity of training data ($D$). The problem it solves is the complete absence of principled guidance for deciding whether, when, and how much weight sparsity to apply when training foundation models on massive datasets; the "shape" of the solution is a multiplicative decomposition where sparsity acts as a saturating capacity multiplier on the standard dense scaling law, cleanly separable from the data-scaling term.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The paper constructs its scaling law through five sequential stages:

1. **Grid sweep execution**: The authors train Vision Transformers (ViT) on JFT-4B and T5 encoder-decoders on C4 across a factorial grid of model sizes (7 sizes for ViT, 4 for T5), sparsity levels (0%, 50%, 75%, 87.5%), and training durations (4 lengths per family), recording validation loss for every combination — 112 ViT runs and 48 T5 runs total.

2. **Visual inspection of raw trends**: Before fitting any equation, the authors inspect log-log plots of loss versus non-zero parameters, grouped by sparsity and training steps, to observe qualitative patterns — parallelism of sparsity curves, saturating gains with higher sparsity, and independence between sparsity and data scaling — which directly motivate the functional form of the law.

3. **Parametric law fitting**: A 7-parameter equation $L(S, N, D)$ is fitted to all sweep data simultaneously by minimizing Huber loss (for robustness against outliers) using the BFGS optimizer from multiple random starting points, producing a single joint law that predicts loss for any combination of sparsity, size, and data within the studied range.

4. **Extrapolation validation**: The fitted law is tested on a held-out configuration — a 2.3B-parameter T5 model pruned to 75% sparsity (6.75× larger non-zero count than any training point) — to verify that the fitted coefficients generalize beyond the interpolation regime.

5. **Optimal sparsity derivation**: The fitted law is analytically differentiated with respect to sparsity $S$ under a fixed compute budget constraint (accounting for the increased training cost of sparse models) to derive $S_{\text{opt}}(N, C)$ — the sparsity level that minimizes loss for a given inference parameter count and training FLOPs budget.

Information flows linearly: grid data → visual pattern extraction → functional form hypothesis → joint coefficient fitting → extrapolation check → analytical optimization. The extensions to n:m structured sparsity and pretrained-model pruning reuse the same functional form with reduced fitting data, validating the law's generality.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the experimental grid design (Section 3.1 / Appendix A) — model families, sparsity levels, training durations, and the gradual magnitude pruning procedure — because the law's validity depends entirely on the quality and coverage of this data.
- **Second**, the visual observations from preliminary plots (Section 3.2) that motivate the specific functional form — parallelism, saturation, and separability — since the law is constructed by induction from these patterns rather than derived from first principles.
- **Third**, the core scaling law equation $L(S, N, D)$ (Equation 3) with all seven parameters defined, because this is the central contribution and everything downstream depends on understanding its structure.
- **Fourth**, the fitting methodology and quality assessment (Huber loss, BFGS, extrapolation on T5-XL), to establish that the law genuinely describes the data rather than overfitting noise.
- **Fifth**, optimal sparsity derivation (Section 3.3) — how $S_{\text{opt}}$ is computed from the fitted law under two cost models (dense FLOPs vs. sparse FLOPs), including the sparse cost multiplier $c_{\text{mul}}(S)$ (Equation 4) and the optimality condition (Equation 5).
- **Sixth**, limit performance analysis (Equation 7) — the "gain" factor that expresses how much larger a dense model must be to match a sparse model's loss in the infinite-data limit, which crystallizes the practical meaning of the scaling coefficients.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical scaling law paper** whose core idea is that weight sparsity enters the standard dense scaling law as a multiplicative, saturating capacity factor that is independent of the data-scaling term, enabling analytic derivation of optimal sparsity as a function of training budget.

---

#### 3.4.1 Experimental Grid Design and Execution

The scaling law is fitted to data from a systematic factorial sweep over three axes: model size ($N$, in non-zero parameters), sparsity ($S$, as a fraction of weights zeroed), and training data ($D$, in images or tokens). The authors execute separate sweeps for vision (ViT on JFT-4B) and language (T5 on C4) to test domain generality.

**Model families and size scaling.** For ViT, the authors start from the ViT-B/16 architecture and scale only the Transformer's hidden dimension while keeping all other shape parameters (depth, MLP ratio, attention heads) constant. This produces seven target sizes in 2× increments: 0.66M, 1.33M, 2.65M, 5.31M, 10.62M, 21.23M, and 42.4M non-zero parameters. For T5, they start from t5-base and similarly scale only the hidden dimension, producing four target sizes in 4× increments: 1.3M, 5.3M, 21M, and 85M non-zero parameters. The paper notes that this width-only scaling strategy was chosen for two reasons: (a) it yields "quite precise size-matches between models and sparsities, facilitating direct comparisons" because a 50% sparse model with $N$ non-zeros trained from a backbone of size $N/0.5 = 2N$ can be directly compared to the next dense variant in the grid, and (b) "we did not observe any notable performance decrease for dense models using this scaling strategy, at the sizes we study."

**Sparsity levels.** The paper focuses on three medium sparsity targets: 50%, 75%, and 87.5%, corresponding to compression rates (the factor by which the parameter count is reduced relative to the dense backbone) of 2×, 4×, and 8× respectively. The choice to exclude very high sparsities (e.g., 95%+) is pragmatic: "Preliminary experiments indicated quickly diminishing returns for very sparse models, which, as discussed previously, are in addition quite expensive to train" — a 95% sparse model requires training a dense backbone 20× larger than the target non-zero count, making such runs computationally prohibitive at the scales studied. All linear layers in the Transformer backbone are sparsified uniformly, which "effectively avoids layer collapse or other edge cases that may otherwise occur in our sweeps." On T5 models, the large embedding matrices are also sparsified "to the amount necessary for parameter matching a smaller dense version."

**Training durations.** For ViT, the authors train for 55K, 110K, 220K, and 440K steps, with the longest corresponding to approximately 1.8 billion images. For T5, they train for 250K, 500K, and 1M steps, with the longest corresponding to approximately 65 billion tokens with the chosen batch size of 128. All training durations represent the total steps including pre-sparsification dense training, the sparsification phase itself, and post-sparsification fine-tuning. The data is not repeated — each step processes fresh data — consistent with the assumption in standard scaling laws of "an ideal training setup with no data repetition" (Section 3.2).

**Sparsification method: gradual magnitude pruning (GMP).** The authors use gradual magnitude pruning (Zhu & Gupta, 2017) as the sparsification algorithm for all runs. This choice is justified by its robustness: it "does not require hyper-parameter retuning for each run" and "is usually quite competitive with more complex techniques, especially at the medium sparsity levels that we focus on." The authors tested alternatives — GMP with diagonal second-order information (Kurtic et al., 2022), AC/DC (Peste et al., 2021), STE (Lin et al., 2020), and RigL (Evci et al., 2020) — but found they performed "similar or worse, while being more sensitive to hyper-parameters as we scale." The pruning schedule uses a cubic function: the fraction of weights pruned at training fraction $t \in [0, 1]$ (where $t$ represents progress through the pruning interval) follows $S - S \cdot (1 - t)^3$, ensuring slow initial pruning that accelerates and then decelerates. The pruning interval starts at 25% of total training and ends at 75%, with mask updates every 100 steps. The authors ablated these hyperparameters on a ViT-B/16 at very high sparsity (93.75%) to amplify differences and found that "most configurations perform very similar, except when the pruning period is too short overall" (Appendix B, Table 5). The chosen 25-75/100 setup was selected "to ensure that there is sufficient time for training a decent model before starting pruning, as well as for properly finetuning the final sparse version."

**Training hyperparameters and optimizations.** The paper largely reuses the optimized training recipes from the original ViT-scaling (Zhai et al., 2022) and T5 (Raffel et al., 2020b) papers, with one notable modification: AdaFactor's (Shazeer & Stern, 2018) second-moment factorization is disabled, because "factorized moments for pruning and sparse optimization are not yet very well studied." A critical sparsity-specific optimization is applied to T5's AdaFactor, which scales learning rates by the root-mean-square (RMS) of each weight tensor. The authors observe that "this does not interact well with high sparsity, as a tensor with many zeros tends to have a lower RMS, resulting in a smaller learning rate, which is especially problematic as high levels of sparsification require more recovery during the pruning process." The fix: "we always calculate the RMS only over unpruned weights, which effectively alleviates this problem." The same technique is applied to AdaFactor's RMS clipping threshold, though the authors note this "is much less critical than the learning rate scaling." T5 models are trained with batch size 128 (rather than larger sizes used in some T5 configurations) specifically "in order to perform sufficiently many optimization steps also for experiments with lower total amounts of training data, which we found important to obtain stable sparse results through model and data scaling."

**Total experiment count.** The ViT grid comprises 7 sizes × 4 sparsity levels × 4 training durations = 112 runs. The T5 grid comprises 4 sizes × 4 sparsity levels × 3 training durations = 48 runs. For the n:m sparsity extensions (Section 4.1), an additional subset of runs is executed using only the smallest model or shortest training duration configurations, reducing cost while providing enough data to fit the sparsity-specific coefficients.

#### 3.4.2 Preliminary Visual Observations Motivating the Functional Form

Before specifying the parametric form of the scaling law, the authors examine raw data from the T5/C4 sweep to inductively determine how sparsity $S$ should enter the standard dense scaling relationship. Figure 2 plots validation loss (with the irreducible loss floor $c = 1$ subtracted to linearize the power-law behavior) against number of non-zero parameters, on log-log axes, for all sparsity levels, grouped into three panels by training steps (250K, 500K, 1M).

From these plots, the authors extract three qualitative observations that directly motivate Equation 3:

**Observation 1: Parallelism.** "The loss vs. #non-zero curves for all sparsity levels seem to form almost parallel lines, differing primarily in the intercept." On a log-log plot, parallel lines correspond to identical power-law exponents ($b_N$) but different multiplicative constants. This suggests that sparsity does not change the fundamental scaling exponent with respect to non-zero parameters — a 75% sparse model scales the same way with $N$ as a dense model does — but rather applies a constant multiplicative factor that shifts the entire curve down (lower loss) by a fixed amount at every size. If this holds, then the size-scaling term $\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)^{b_N}$ should be multiplied by a sparsity-dependent factor $f(S)$, rather than $S$ entering inside the exponent or interacting with $N$ in a more complex way.

**Observation 2: Saturating gains.** "The higher the sparsity the lower the loss, but gains are quickly diminishing." The vertical gap between the 0% and 50% sparsity curves is substantially larger than the gap between 75% and 87.5%, even though both increments represent a doubling of compression rate (from 1× to 2×, and from 4× to 8×). This saturating pattern suggests modeling $f(S)$ as a power-law in the compression rate $(1-S)$ — specifically, a function of the form $a_S (1-S)^{b_S} + c_S$ where $b_S$ controls the saturation rate and $c_S$ represents the asymptotic limit as sparsity approaches 100%.

**Observation 3: Independence from data scaling.** "The overall shape of all curves is very similar for each training duration, the y-axis just tends to shift a bit downwards with more training steps." The sparsity-induced shifts (Observation 1) appear consistent across all three training duration panels, and the relative ordering of sparsity curves does not change with more data. This suggests that the sparsity term and the data-scaling term are **separable** — sparsity does not interact with $D$ in a way that would require a cross-term like $S \cdot D$ or sparsity-dependent data exponents. The data-scaling term can therefore be preserved in its original dense form $\left(\frac{a_D}{D}\right)^{b_D}$, added independently of the sparsity-modified capacity term.

These three observations collectively point toward a **multiplicative decomposition**: the total loss is the sum of a capacity-limited term (which depends on both $N$ and $S$, with sparsity acting as a multiplicative modifier on the size scaling) and a data-limited term (which depends only on $D$, unchanged from the dense case).

#### 3.4.3 The Core Scaling Law: $L(S, N, D)$

The observations from Section 3.4.2 are formalized into the following joint scaling law (Equation 3 in the paper):

$$L(S, N, D) = \left( a_S (1 - S)^{b_S} + c_S \right) \cdot \left( \frac{1}{N} \right)^{b_N} + \left( \frac{a_D}{D} \right)^{b_D} + c$$

where:

- $S \in [0, 1)$ is the sparsity fraction (0 = dense, 0.875 = 87.5% of weights are zero),
- $N$ is the number of non-zero parameters (the effective inference-time model size),
- $D$ is the amount of training data (images for ViT, tokens for T5),
- $a_S$ is the coefficient scaling the sparsity power-law term (controls how much sparsity helps),
- $b_S$ is the exponent governing how quickly sparsity gains saturate,
- $c_S$ is the asymptotic constant representing the irreducible benefit from sparsity as $S \to 1$,
- $b_N$ is the exponent governing how loss scales with non-zero parameter count,
- $a_D$ is the coefficient scaling the data term,
- $b_D$ is the exponent governing how loss scales with training data,
- $c$ is the irreducible loss floor (the minimum achievable loss given infinite capacity and data).

**What it computes:** Given a sparsity level $S$, a target non-zero parameter count $N$, and a training data quantity $D$, the equation predicts the expected validation loss. The first product term $\left( a_S (1 - S)^{b_S} + c_S \right) \cdot \left( \frac{1}{N} \right)^{b_N}$ represents the **capacity bottleneck** — how much the model's limited number of parameters (and their sparsity pattern) constrains performance. The sparsity factor $\left( a_S (1 - S)^{b_S} + c_S \right)$ acts as a **capacity multiplier**: for a fixed $N$, higher sparsity reduces this factor (since $(1-S)$ decreases), which in turn reduces the entire capacity term, leading to lower loss. The second term $\left( \frac{a_D}{D} \right)^{b_D}$ represents the **data bottleneck** — how much the limited training data constrains performance, independent of model capacity. The constant $c$ represents the Bayes error or intrinsic stochasticity of the task that no amount of capacity or data can overcome.

The input is a triple $(S, N, D)$; the output is a scalar predicted validation loss; downstream, this enables computing optimal sparsity (Section 3.4.5) and limit gains (Section 3.4.6).

**Why this form — the sparsity factor:** The sparsity is expressed in terms of the compression rate $1/(1-S)$ (or equivalently the remaining density $1-S$) because "0.75 is twice as sparse as 0.5." Using $S$ directly would not capture this multiplicative relationship — the gap between 50% and 75% sparsity would look identical to the gap between 75% and 87.5% when measured by raw $S$ increments, whereas measured by compression rate they represent doublings (2× → 4× → 8×). The power-law form $a_S (1-S)^{b_S}$ captures the empirically observed saturating returns: as $(1-S) \to 0$ (extreme sparsity), the term approaches zero, but with diminishing marginal benefit at each doubling. The additive constant $c_S$ ensures that even at the limit $S \to 1$, there is some irreducible benefit that sparsity provides — the term never reaches zero, preventing the capacity term from vanishing entirely.

**Why this form — separability:** The sparsity factor multiplies only the size term, not the data term, reflecting Observation 3 that sparsity and data scaling are independent. An alternative formulation $L(S, N, D) = f(S, N) + g(S, D) + c$ with a sparsity-data interaction would have been wrong because "the overall shape of all curves is very similar for each training duration" — the relative benefits of sparsity do not change with more data. This separability is the key insight that makes the law analytically tractable for computing optimal sparsity, because it means the partial derivative with respect to $S$ does not involve $D$ beyond a constant factor.

**Why this form — recovery of dense scaling:** When $S = 0$ (dense model), the sparsity factor becomes $\left( a_S (1-0)^{b_S} + c_S \right) = a_S + c_S$, a constant. This constant absorbs the original dense coefficient $a_N$ from the standard Kaplan/Hoffmann form $L(N, D) = \left( \frac{a_N}{N} \right)^{b_N} + \left( \frac{a_D}{D} \right)^{b_D} + c$, because $a_N$ is subsumed into $a_S$ and $c_S$ as the paper explicitly states: "$a_N$ is subsumed by $a_S$ and $c_S$, leaving 7 free parameters." The total number of free parameters is therefore 7: $a_S, b_S, c_S, b_N, a_D, b_D, c$.

**What the law does NOT capture:** The authors note one limitation of this functional form: "this formulation suggests that higher sparsity is always better (but with potentially quite quickly saturating improvements), which may not be true in practice. For very high sparsity (e.g., 64× compression) we sometimes see slightly worse performance, presumably due to imperfections in the pruning and optimization process." The law is monotonic in sparsity — making $S$ larger always reduces $(1-S)^{b_S}$ and thus reduces loss — but real optimization may degrade at extreme sparsities due to optimization difficulties not captured by the simple capacity model. The paper treats this as "a bottleneck-case that we do not necessarily capture," effectively limiting the law's validity to the studied sparsity range.

---

#### 3.4.4 Fitting Methodology and Quality Assessment

The 7 free parameters of $L(S, N, D)$ are fitted numerically to all sweep data points simultaneously, separately for the ViT/JFT and T5/C4 datasets.

**Loss function and optimizer.** The fitting objective follows Hoffmann et al. (2022): minimize the Huber loss between predicted and actual values, using the BFGS quasi-Newton optimizer from multiple random starting points to avoid local minima. The Huber loss (squared error for small residuals, absolute error for large residuals) is chosen for robustness against outliers — individual runs that deviate substantially from the overall trend due to optimization noise or other artifacts are downweighted rather than dominating the fit. For T5/C4, the loss is computed on $\log L$ (log-transformed validation loss) with Huber delta $\delta = 0.001$, matching the Chinchilla fitting procedure exactly. For ViT/JFT, the authors use Huber delta $\delta = 0.01$ and do **not** take the log of $L$, because "we find the NLP-optimized settings from before to exclude outliers too aggressively for ViT data (which gives a poor fit for smaller models)." This domain-specific adjustment highlights that the fitting procedure is not a one-size-fits-all recipe; the Huber delta controls the threshold at which residuals transition from quadratic to linear penalty, and the ViT data apparently contains more legitimate variation that would be incorrectly treated as outliers under the T5 settings. The fitting procedure "may not be guaranteed to be convex, [but] we find the process to converge to virtually the same values from different random starting points," suggesting the loss landscape is well-behaved despite the 7-parameter nonlinear model.

**Fitted coefficient values.** Appendix C (Table 7) reports the fitted coefficients. For ViT/JFT unstructured sparsity: $a_S = 2.94 \times 10^2$, $b_S = 0.821$, $c_S = 4.68 \times 10^2$, $b_N = 0.392$, $a_D = 2.37 \times 10^8$, $b_D = 0.890$, $c = 4.517$, with fitting error $4.93 \times 10^{-4}$. For T5/C4 unstructured sparsity: $a_S = 1.68 \times 10^1$, $b_S = 0.722$, $c_S = 4.50 \times 10^1$, $b_N = 0.245$, $a_D = 6.90 \times 10^8$, $b_D = 0.203$, $c = 0.651$, with fitting error $7.60 \times 10^{-6}$. Several patterns are notable: the size-scaling exponent $b_N$ is steeper for ViT (0.392) than T5 (0.245), meaning vision performance is more sensitive to parameter count than language performance in these regimes; the data-scaling exponent $b_D$ is much steeper for ViT (0.890) than T5 (0.203), meaning vision performance improves more rapidly with additional data; and the sparsity saturation exponent $b_S$ is similar across domains (0.821 vs. 0.722), suggesting the saturating-returns pattern of sparsity is relatively universal.

**Data efficiency of the fit.** The T5 sweep has 48 data points for 7 free parameters (approximately 7 data points per parameter), which the authors acknowledge as a relatively small fitting set. The ViT sweep has 112 data points — more than twice as many — "leading to more robust coefficient estimates." The fact that both domains yield qualitatively similar law forms and comparable sparsity gain estimates (Section 3.4.6) despite different data volumes and fitting procedures strengthens confidence that the functional form captures genuine scaling behavior rather than overfitting noise.

**Extrapolation validation (T5-XL).** To test whether the fitted law generalizes beyond its training range, the authors prune a 2.3 billion parameter T5-XL model to 75% sparsity, yielding approximately 575M non-zero parameters. This "constitutes an approximately 6.75× larger target number of non-zero parameters than the maximum in our fitting data, which is a similar level of extrapolation as was done for Chinchilla." Several setup changes were required to make this run practical: using the T5-XL architecture rather than a simply rescaled T5-base ("to avoid any architecture bottlenecks and achieve better training utilization"), training with batch size 256 for 250K steps rather than 500K steps with batch size 128. Despite these changes to the experimental setup, "the prediction of our fitted scaling law is quite close to the actual validation loss; see Figure 1 (Right)." The extrapolation result is shown in Figure 1 (Left), which plots predicted vs. measured loss — the extrapolation point (labeled separately from the fitting data) falls close to the identity line, though exact numerical values are not reported in the main text.

**Visual fit quality for ViT/JFT.** Figure 3 shows predicted loss-vs-data curves (solid lines) overlaid on actual sweep data points (markers) for all four sparsity levels, with each panel containing curves for all seven model sizes. The authors emphasize that "the predictions in all subplots here are produced by a single joint law with the same parameters (not one fit per image)." The agreement is strong across most of the range, with the main discrepancy occurring "at the lowest amount of training" where "some points are a bit off the prediction curve." The authors hypothesize that "this may be related to the fact that these runs only involve comparatively few training steps, which may be a slight bottleneck for the optimization process" — the scaling law assumes non-bottlenecked optimization, and short training runs may violate this assumption due to transient optimization effects not captured by the simple power-law form.

#### 3.4.5 Optimal Sparsity: Derivation and Interpretation

The joint scaling law $L(S, N, D)$ enables answering the central practical question: **for a fixed number of non-zero parameters $N$ and fixed training compute budget $C$, what sparsity level $S$ minimizes the validation loss?** This is formalized as $S_{\text{opt}}(N, C)$, the "optimal sparsity."

**Two cost models.** Training cost can be accounted for in two ways, corresponding to different assumptions about hardware support for sparse training:

**(a) Dense FLOPs:** The cost is the FLOPs required to train the dense backbone of size $N / (1 - S)$ for the full training duration — as if sparsity provides no training-time speedup. This is a conservative baseline appropriate when sparse training is not supported in hardware/software.

**(b) Sparse FLOPs:** The cost accounts for the fact that, as weights are gradually pruned during training, the model becomes progressively cheaper to train (assuming perfect exploitation of sparsity). This is the optimistic scenario where sparse operations are fully accelerated during training.

The sparse cost multiplier $c_{\text{mul}}(S)$ (Equation 4) computes the ratio of sparse training FLOPs to the dense baseline FLOPs for a model trained with the specific GMP schedule described in Section 3.4.1:

$$c_{\text{mul}}(S) = \frac{0.25 + 0.50 \cdot (1 - 0.75 \cdot S)}{1 - S} + 0.25$$

where the three terms correspond to the three phases of training:

- **First 25% of training (pre-pruning):** The model is fully dense with size $1/(1-S)$ times the target, incurring cost $0.25/(1-S)$ relative to the dense reference model of size $N$.
- **Middle 50% of training (sparsification):** The model follows a cubic pruning schedule $S - S \cdot (1 - t)^3$ for $t \in [0, 1]$ over this interval. The average density over this period is the integral of the density $(1 - \text{sparsity at } t)$ over $t$, which evaluates to $1 - \int_0^1 (S - S(1-t)^3) dt = 1 - S + S/4 = 1 - 0.75S$. Multiplying by the interval duration (0.5) and the dense model cost factor ($1/(1-S)$) gives $0.50 \cdot (1 - 0.75 \cdot S) / (1 - S)$.
- **Last 25% of training (post-pruning fine-tuning):** The model is at target sparsity $S$, so its training cost equals that of the dense reference model — contribution $0.25$.

Summing these three phases yields Equation 4.

**What $c_{\text{mul}}(S)$ computes:** Given a target sparsity $S$, it returns the factor by which the total training FLOPs of the sparse model exceed those of training a dense model of the same non-zero parameter count $N$ on the same amount of data $D$. For example, if $S = 0.5$, then $c_{\text{mul}}(0.5) = (0.25 + 0.50 \cdot (1 - 0.375)) / 0.5 + 0.25 = (0.25 + 0.3125) / 0.5 + 0.25 = 1.125 + 0.25 = 1.375$, meaning a 50% sparse model costs 37.5% more FLOPs to train than a dense model with the same $N$ (because the dense backbone is 2× larger during the first 25% and partially dense during sparsification).

**Why this form:** The cubic schedule $S - S \cdot (1 - t)^3$ is the specific sparsification schedule used in all experiments. A different schedule (e.g., linear, cosine) would yield a different integral and thus a different $c_{\text{mul}}(S)$. The paper therefore provides the derivation as an illustration tied to their specific experimental choices rather than as a universal formula.

**Deriving the optimal sparsity condition.** To find $S_{\text{opt}}$, the authors fix the total training compute $C$ and the non-zero parameter count $N$. For a given $S$, the amount of training data that can be processed under budget $C$ is $D_S = C / (6N \cdot c_{\text{mul}}(S))$, where $6N$ is the standard FLOPs-per-token approximation for dense Transformers (Kaplan et al., 2020) — or half that for encoder-decoder architectures. This $D_S$ is substituted into $L(S, N, D)$, and the resulting expression is differentiated with respect to $S$ and set to zero, yielding the optimality condition (Equation 5):

$$a_D b_D \cdot \frac{c'_{\text{mul}}(S)}{c_{\text{mul}}(S)} \cdot \left( \frac{D_S}{c_{\text{mul}}(S)} \right)^{-b_D} = a_S b_S \cdot (1 - S)^{b_S - 1} \cdot N^{-b_N}$$

where $c'_{\text{mul}}(S)$ is the derivative of the cost multiplier with respect to $S$.

**What this equation expresses:** The left side represents the **cost of sparsity** — how much data must be sacrificed (via reduced $D_S$) to pay for the increased training FLOPs of a sparser model. The right side represents the **benefit of sparsity** — how much the capacity term decreases when sparsity is increased. At optimality, these marginal costs and benefits are equal. If the left side is larger, reducing $S$ (making the model denser) would improve loss because the data sacrificed exceeds the capacity gain; if the right side is larger, increasing $S$ would help because the capacity improvement outweighs the data reduction.

**Analytic solution for dense FLOPs.** For the dense cost model where $c_{\text{mul}}(S) = 1/(1-S)$ (the cost of training the full dense backbone without any sparsity acceleration during training), the optimality condition can be solved explicitly (Equation 6):

$$S_{\text{opt}}(N, C) = \max \left\{ 1 - \exp \left[ \left( \log \frac{b_N a_D b_D}{a_S b_S} + b_N \log N \right) / (b_D + b_S) \right] \cdot \left( \frac{C}{6N} \right)^{-b_D / (b_D + b_S)}, 0 \right\}$$

**What this equation computes:** For a given non-zero parameter target $N$ and total training compute budget $C$, it returns a sparsity value between 0 and 1 (clipped at 0 when the expression would yield negative sparsity, i.e., when dense is optimal). The dependence on $C$ is through the term $(C/6N)^{-b_D/(b_D+b_S)}$ — as $C$ increases, this term decreases (since the exponent is negative), making $S_{\text{opt}}$ larger. This directly formalizes the paper's central finding: **optimal sparsity increases with training budget.**

**Key property of the optimal sparsity contours.** The iso-contours of $S_{\text{opt}}$ in $(N, C)$ space run **parallel to the dense Chinchilla compute-optimal line** (Hoffmann et al., 2022). This is visible in Figure 1 (Right) for ViT and Figure 4 for T5, where the dashed Chinchilla line and the solid sparsity contours share the same slope. This parallelism emerges from the separability of the sparsity and data terms in Equation 3: the optimality condition implies $D_S = O(N^{b_N / b_D})$, meaning the optimal data-to-size ratio for a given sparsity is the same power law as for dense models, just with a sparsity-dependent constant factor. The practical interpretation: **if you know the Chinchilla-optimal size for a given compute budget, you also know the relative position of all sparsity contours.**

**Interpretation of the ViT and T5 contours (Figures 1 and 4).** For ViT under sparse FLOPs, "50% sparsity is already optimal for < 2× longer training than Chinchilla" — meaning if you train a ViT twice as long as the dense compute-optimal point, a 50% sparse model with the same non-zero parameters will outperform the dense model. For T5 under sparse FLOPs, this threshold is approximately 3× Chinchilla. Under dense FLOPs (conservative cost model), the thresholds are higher: approximately 5× Chinchilla for ViT and approximately 70× for T5. The authors contextualize the seemingly high 70× figure: "we note that language models of the sizes we consider here are already typically trained for > 100× longer than Chinchilla (Brown et al., 2020). Additionally, larger models are being trained with more and more data as well, e.g., Llama2-7B with ≈14× Chinchilla." In other words, the overtrained regime where sparsity becomes optimal — even under conservative dense FLOPs accounting — is already the default operating point for many production language models.

**A crucial distinction: optimal sparse models form a half-plane, not a line.** Whereas optimal dense models define a single curve in $(N, C)$ space (the Chinchilla line — one optimal size for each compute budget), "optimal sparse models form a half-plane (with different sparsities unlocking multiple optimal sizes for a fixed training cost)." For a given compute budget, there is a **range** of model sizes that can be made optimal by choosing the appropriate sparsity level. This means sparsity provides flexibility: you can target a specific inference-time parameter count (for deployment constraints) and adjust sparsity to achieve compute-optimality, rather than being forced to use whatever size the dense Chinchilla law dictates.

**Empirical illustration (Figure 5).** The paper shows loss-vs-FLOPs curves for ViT models of three sizes (0.66M, 1.33M, 2.65M non-zero parameters) at four sparsity levels. As training compute increases, the loss curves for higher sparsities eventually cross below the dense curves — the higher the sparsity, the later the crossing point. This visually demonstrates the regimes where each sparsity becomes optimal: at low total FLOPs, dense models are best; at moderate FLOPs, 50% sparsity overtakes dense; at higher FLOPs, 75% overtakes 50%; at even higher FLOPs, 87.5% overtakes 75%. The curves never cross back, consistent with the monotonicity of the scaling law.

#### 3.4.6 Limit Performance: The Gain Factor

Beyond asking *when* sparsity becomes optimal, the paper asks **how much** sparsity helps in the limit of infinite training data, where performance is purely capacity-bound. This is answered by the "gain" factor (Equation 7):

$$\text{gain}(S) = \left( \frac{a_S (1 - S)^{b_S} + c_S}{a_S + c_S} \right)^{-1 / b_N}$$

where the numerator $a_S (1 - S)^{b_S} + c_S$ is the sparsity factor at sparsity $S$, and the denominator $a_S + c_S$ is the sparsity factor at $S = 0$ (dense).

**What it computes:** The factor by which a dense model's non-zero parameter count must be multiplied to match the loss of a sparse model with the same non-zero parameter count $N$, when both are trained on infinite data (or equivalently, the same very large amount of data). If $\text{gain}(0.75) = 2.17$, then a 75% sparse model with $N$ non-zero parameters achieves the same loss as a dense model with $2.17N$ parameters — the sparsity effectively provides a 2.17× capacity multiplier.

**Why this form:** In the infinite-data limit, the data term $\left( \frac{a_D}{D} \right)^{b_D} \to 0$, so the loss reduces to $L(S, N, \infty) = \left( a_S (1 - S)^{b_S} + c_S \right) \cdot N^{-b_N} + c$. For a dense model to match this loss, it needs $N_{\text{dense}}$ such that $(a_S + c_S) \cdot N_{\text{dense}}^{-b_N} = (a_S (1 - S)^{b_S} + c_S) \cdot N^{-b_N}$. Solving for the ratio $N_{\text{dense}} / N$ yields Equation 7. The $-1/b_N$ exponent converts the loss ratio into a parameter-count ratio through the size-scaling exponent.

**Empirical gain values (Table 2).** The fitted coefficients yield remarkably consistent gains across vision and language:

| Sparsity | ViT/JFT | T5/C4 |
|---|---|---|
| 0.500 (50%) | 1.60× | 1.59× |
| 0.750 (75%) | 2.17× | 2.16× |
| 0.875 (87.5%) | 2.63× | 2.63× |

The authors note that "the fact that the numbers are within 0.01 of each other is likely a coincidence," but the overall consistency is striking. A 75% sparse model — compressing parameters by 4× — provides the equivalent capacity of a dense model with about 2.17× more parameters. This is less than the compression ratio (4×) would naively suggest, indicating that sparse parameters are less efficient per-parameter than dense ones — but the overall capacity gain is still substantial. The gain increases sublinearly with compression rate: doubling compression from 2× (50%) to 4× (75%) increases gain from 1.60× to 2.17× (a 1.36× improvement), while doubling again from 4× to 8× (87.5%) increases gain from 2.17× to 2.63× (a 1.21× improvement), reflecting the saturating returns captured by the exponent $b_S$.

**Interpretation as a comparative static:** These gain values hold for any $N$ and any sufficiently large $D$ — the sparsity benefit is independent of absolute scale. This is a direct consequence of the multiplicative separability in Equation 3: sparsity shifts the entire size-vs-loss curve by a constant factor on a log-log plot, so the equivalent dense size multiplier is constant across all scales.

#### 3.4.7 Extension to n:m Structured Sparsity (Section 4.1)

The paper extends the scaling law framework to **n:m sparsity**, a hardware-friendly pattern where exactly $n$ out of every $m$ consecutive weights are non-zero (e.g., 2:4 sparsity is directly accelerated on NVIDIA Ampere GPUs). The key methodological innovation is a cost-efficient fitting strategy that leverages the separability property.

**Reduced fitting protocol.** Since "minor changes in the sparsification process (pattern, algorithm, etc.) [are expected to] only affect the sparsity term in (3)," the dense scaling parameters ($b_N, a_D, b_D, c$) can be reused from the unstructured sparsity fit. Only the three sparsity-specific coefficients ($a_S, b_S, c_S$) need to be refitted. This reduces the required grid data from the full factorial sweep to "only a subset of our full grid in Table 1. Concretely, we execute all runs involving either the least amount of steps or the smallest model" — a design that provides enough variation in $S$ and $N$ to identify the three sparsity parameters without the combinatorial cost of a full sweep.

**Gradual n:m pruning algorithm (Algorithm 1).** The authors generalize gradual magnitude pruning to the n:m pattern using a simple two-stage procedure:

1. Identify the top-$n$ weights by magnitude within each group of $m$ consecutive weights, and temporarily set their absolute values to infinity.
2. Run standard unstructured top-$k$ selection on the modified weight tensor, where $k$ is determined by the target sparsity. Because the protected $n$ weights per group have infinite magnitude, they are guaranteed to survive the unstructured selection, ensuring the n:m constraint is satisfied.
3. Zero out all non-selected weights.

The paper notes this approach "effectively generalizes the highly robust gradual pruning paradigm to the n:m setting" and "we also found it to work reliably across scales with the same hyper-parameters, a highly useful property for scaling studies." This is in contrast to the more common approach of one-shot n:m pruning followed by fine-tuning (Zhou et al., 2021) or straight-through gradient estimation (Lu et al., 2023).

**Fitted laws and results.** The authors fit laws for 2:4 (50% sparse), 1:4 (75% sparse), 4:8 (50% sparse), and 2:8 (75% sparse) patterns on T5/C4. The fitted sparsity coefficients are $a_S = 8.64 \times 10^1$, $b_S = 2.752$, $c_S = 5.36 \times 10^2$, with fitting error $2.1 \times 10^{-5}$ (the much larger $b_S$ compared to unstructured sparsity's 0.722 indicates more dramatic saturation — n:m sparsity benefits taper off more quickly). Figure 6 shows loss-vs-size curves for a subset of the n:m data, visually confirming the same parallel-line pattern observed for unstructured sparsity (Figure 2), which "indicates that the general scaling law shape also holds for n:m sparsity."

**Gain values for n:m sparsity (Table 3).** The computed dense size multipliers are:

| Pattern | 50% equivalent (2:4 or 4:8) | 75% equivalent (1:4 or 2:8) |
|---|---|---|
| n:4 | 1.56× | 1.62× |
| n:8 | 1.67× | 1.81× |

For the 50% patterns, 2:4 achieves 1.56× (vs. 1.59× for unstructured 50%) and 4:8 achieves 1.67× — both close to the unstructured result, suggesting the 50% n:m constraint is not significantly more restrictive than unstructured 50% sparsity. For 75% patterns, 1:4 achieves only 1.62× — barely better than 50% patterns (1.56–1.67×) and far below unstructured 75% (2.16×). The authors conclude that "1:4 brings almost no advantage and 2:8 only a slight improvement, which is contrary to our unstructured results. We suspect that the 75% patterns may simply be too stringent to significantly increase capacity beyond their 50% variants." The structural constraint of keeping exactly 1 out of 4 weights (1:4) appears to destroy most of the capacity benefit that unstructured 75% sparsity provides, a finding with direct practical implications for hardware accelerator design: supporting finer-grained sparsity patterns may unlock substantially more capacity gains than the widely-adopted 2:4 pattern.

#### 3.4.8 Pruning Pretrained Models (Section 4.2)

The final extension considers a practical scenario: given a set of already well-trained dense models, how efficiently can they be sparsified using a small additional training budget, compared to sparsifying from scratch? This addresses the common deployment pattern where dense pretrained checkpoints exist and the goal is to produce efficient sparse versions for inference.

**Experimental protocol.** The authors train ViT S/16, M/16, and B/16 models for 4 full epochs on JFT (approximately 16 billion images), then apply the same gradual magnitude pruning procedure used in the main experiments starting from these dense checkpoints. The sparsification budget is 5.6% of the original pretraining budget. Because the model is already well-trained, sparsification begins immediately (rather than after 25% of training as in the from-scratch protocol), and the cubic schedule is applied over this 5.6% window.

**Comparison metric.** The authors use the fitted scaling laws (Section 3.4.3) to compute how much training data would be required to achieve equivalent performance when sparsifying from scratch. The ratio of from-scratch data to pretrained-pruning data is reported for two accounting methods: **excluding** the pretraining cost (assuming the dense checkpoint already exists and its cost is sunk) and **including** the pretraining cost (treating the total cost as pretraining + sparsification).

**Results (Table 4).** Key findings:

- **Excluding pretraining cost:** Pruning from a pretrained model is > 4× more data-efficient than sparsifying from scratch for 50% and 75% sparsity, and > 2× more efficient for 87.5% sparsity. The decreasing efficiency gain with higher sparsity is attributed to "the increased divergence from the initial starting point" — at 87.5% sparsity, the final model is so different from the dense starting point that most of the benefit of the good initialization is lost.

- **Including pretraining cost:** Sparsifying from scratch throughout the full training process is > 4× more efficient than the pretrain-then-prune approach across all sparsity levels. This means that if you don't already have a dense checkpoint, you should integrate sparsification into the full training process rather than training dense first and pruning afterward.

- **Recovery quality:** The 50% sparse models are "approximately 0.2–0.3 points away from their dense baseline loss," which "matches our results in Section 3.3.1 that the size gain of 50% sparsity is noticeably less than 2× for well trained models." Even with extended pretraining, 50% sparsity does not fully recover the performance of the dense backbone — the scaling law's limit gain of 1.59× (Table 2) means the sparse model is equivalent to a 1.59× larger dense model, not the 2× larger backbone it was pruned from.

**Practical implication.** The results "clearly demonstrate that, while the sparsification process benefits significantly from a better trained initial model, it does so only up to a certain extent." If you have a well-trained dense model and want to make it efficient for inference, pruning with a small retraining budget (5.6% of pretraining cost) is substantially better than starting from scratch — but the total cost (pretraining + pruning) is higher than if you had integrated sparsity from the beginning. This creates a practical decision boundary: for new projects, train with sparsity from scratch; for existing deployed models, post-hoc pruning with modest retraining is the pragmatic choice.

#### 3.4.9 Design Choices Summary

**Why GMP over more sophisticated methods:** Robustness and consistency across scales without per-run hyperparameter tuning, which is essential for large sweeps. Advanced methods (AC/DC, RigL, second-order pruning) showed similar or worse performance while being more sensitive to hyperparameters as model scale and sparsity varied.

**Why cubic schedule and 25-75 interval:** Ablation study (Appendix B, Table 5) showed insensitivity to start/end points as long as the pruning interval is not too short. The 25-75 window provides sufficient pre-sparsification training for a decent starting model and sufficient post-sparsification fine-tuning for the final sparse structure.

**Why width-only scaling:** Produces precise size matches between sparse models and dense baselines, and the authors observed no performance degradation from this simplified scaling strategy at the studied sizes.

**Why $1-S$ (density) rather than $S$ (sparsity) in the law:** The compression rate $1/(1-S)$ captures the multiplicative nature of sparsity (50% → 75% is a doubling of compression), which the raw sparsity value does not.

**Why separable form (no sparsity-data interaction):** Empirically observed in Figure 2 — sparsity curves are parallel across training durations, and the relative ordering never changes. A cross-term would have implied sparsity benefits change with data quantity, which the data does not support.

**Why fitting on log-loss for T5 but raw loss for ViT:** The NLP-standard log-loss fitting with Huber $\delta = 0.001$ was too aggressive for ViT data, excluding legitimate variation in smaller models. The adjustment to raw-loss fitting with $\delta = 0.01$ reflects the different noise characteristics of the vision data.

**Why two cost models (dense vs. sparse FLOPs):** They bracket the realistic range from "no sparse training support" to "perfect sparse training support," and the paper's conclusions about when sparsity becomes optimal depend on which model is assumed. The practical message is nuanced: under optimistic assumptions, sparsity is beneficial at modest training budgets; under conservative assumptions, the required budgets are higher but still within the regime of current production models.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: A Unified Scaling Law That Makes Sparsity a First-Class Citizen

**What’s distinctive at the idea level.** Before this paper, scaling laws treated model size as a single scalar — total parameter count. Sparsity, when studied at all, was an afterthought: a post-training compression step evaluated by comparing a pruned model to its unpruned parent at the same initial training budget. This paper fundamentally reframes sparsity as a **third independent axis** in the scaling law, alongside non-zero parameter count and training data, with its own power-law behavior and saturation properties. The conceptual move is treating sparsity not as a "damage and repair" process (how much accuracy can we recover after removing weights?) but as a **capacity multiplier** — a mechanism that, for a fixed inference-time parameter budget, increases the effective model capacity in a predictable, saturating way.

**Comparison to prior work.** The dominant prior frameworks were:

- **Dense scaling laws** (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022): Model size enters as a pure power law $N^{-b_N}$. Sparsity is not a variable — it's implicitly zero.
- **Pruning-as-compression** (standard benchmarks): The question is "how much can we prune without hurting accuracy?" — a one-dimensional comparison between a pruned model and its dense parent, with no scaling dimension.
- **Rosenfeld et al. (2021)**: The closest precursor, studying the relationship between width, depth, and density for pruned ResNets on CIFAR-10. But this work had no data/compute dimension — it couldn't answer whether sparsity benefits change with training budget — and operated in a regime where dense models saturate quickly, making the baseline fundamentally different from the massive-data setting.

What this paper does that none of these did: it writes down a single equation $L(S, N, D)$ with 7 parameters that simultaneously predicts validation loss across sparsity levels, model sizes, and training data quantities, fitted jointly to all data. The law reveals that sparsity enters as a **multiplicative, saturating factor on the capacity term**, entirely separable from the data-scaling term — a structural finding that was not obvious a priori and that enables all the downstream optimality analysis.

**Significance beyond raw performance.** This is primarily a **theoretical and diagnostic advance**, not a method that beats baselines. The significance is threefold:

1. **It resolves the ambiguity in prior sparse-foundation-model results.** The Gopher appendix (Rae et al., 2021) found sparse models could outperform dense ones when trained for the same steps, but left open whether this held when accounting for the increased compute. The Cerebras (2022) result showed ~5× gains but used 5× more data for the sparse model. By explicitly separating sparsity, size, and data in a joint law, the paper provides a framework for decomposing these confounded comparisons — you can now ask: "was the gain from sparsity, or from extra training data?"

2. **It establishes sparsity as a genuine scaling axis with predictable behavior.** The finding that sparsity curves are parallel across model sizes (Observation 1 in Section 3.2), that gains saturate with a consistent exponent $b_S$ across vision and language domains, and that the sparsity term is separable from the data term — these are empirical regularities, not obvious theoretical necessities. They give the field confidence that sparsity behavior at one scale generalizes to other scales, which is what makes scaling laws useful in the first place.

3. **It creates the conceptual vocabulary for reasoning about sparsity tradeoffs.** Terms like "optimal sparsity," "sparsity gain," and "dense-size multiplier" — and the analytical machinery to compute them from fitted coefficients — provide a language that the field previously lacked. Before this paper, practitioners couldn't ask "should I use 50% or 75% sparsity for my deployment target?" in a principled way; after this paper, the answer is computable from a handful of scaling coefficients.

**Tie to evidence.** The core empirical validation comes from two sources. First, the scaling law fit quality: Figure 1 (Left) shows predicted-vs-measured loss for T5/C4, with the extrapolation point (a 2.3B-parameter T5-XL at 75% sparsity, ~6.75× larger non-zero count than any training point) falling close to the identity line. Second, the visual agreement in Figure 3, where a single joint law (not per-panel fits) produces predicted curves that closely track actual data points across all four sparsity levels and seven model sizes for ViT/JFT. The fact that the law fits both vision and language domains with qualitatively similar forms (saturating sparsity, separable data term, parallel size curves) strengthens the case that this is capturing a general property of Transformer sparsity rather than overfitting one dataset.

**Incremental or fundamental?** This is a **fundamental shift** in how sparsity is studied in the context of large-scale training. It transforms sparsity from an engineering technique (compression with accuracy recovery) into a modeling primitive (a capacity modifier with predictable scaling behavior). The specific functional form $a_S(1-S)^{b_S} + c_S$ may be refined by future work — the authors themselves note it doesn't capture degradation at extreme sparsities — but the framework of sparsity as a separable, saturating capacity multiplier is likely to endure.

### Innovation 2: Optimal Sparsity as an Inference-Time Analog of Chinchilla

**What’s distinctive at the idea level.** The paper makes a direct conceptual parallel between Hoffmann et al. (2022)'s compute-optimal pretraining and its own concept of **compute-optimal sparsity**. Just as Chinchilla answered "for a given training FLOPs budget, what model size minimizes loss?" this paper answers "for a given inference-time parameter budget and training FLOPs budget, what sparsity level minimizes loss?" The structural finding — that optimal sparsity contours run **parallel** to the dense Chinchilla line — is a non-obvious theoretical result that emerges from the separability of the sparsity and data terms in the scaling law. It means that if you know the Chinchilla-optimal dense configuration for your task, you immediately know the relative positioning of all sparsity regimes in $(N, C)$ space — sparsity doesn't fundamentally change the shape of compute-optimal scaling, it just adds a new dimension along which to optimize.

**Comparison to prior work.** The Chinchilla framework (Hoffmann et al., 2022) optimized over two variables — model size $N$ and training data $D$ (or equivalently, $N$ and training compute $C$) — and produced a single optimal curve: for each $C$, there is one optimal $N$. Sparsity was not a variable because all models were dense. This paper extends the optimization to three variables — $N$, $D$, and $S$ — and finds a qualitatively different structure: rather than a single optimal curve, there is an **optimal half-plane**. For a given compute budget, multiple model sizes can be optimal, each paired with a different sparsity level. This means sparsity provides a degree of freedom that dense scaling lacks: you can hit a target inference-time parameter count (for deployment constraints) while still achieving compute-optimality, by adjusting sparsity to compensate.

The prior sparsity literature had no such optimization framework. Pruning papers typically fix the sparsity level (often 90% or 95%) and the training budget (the original model's training duration plus a small fine-tuning budget) and ask whether accuracy can be recovered. The question "what sparsity level is *optimal* for a given budget?" was never asked — and couldn't be answered without a joint scaling law.

**Significance beyond raw performance.** This is both a **theoretical advance** (extending compute-optimality to a new dimension) and a **practical diagnostic tool**. The theoretical contribution is the derivation of $S_{\text{opt}}(N, C)$ — an analytical expression for optimal sparsity — and the geometric insight that sparsity contours are parallel to Chinchilla. The practical significance is that it provides a decision boundary for when sparsity should be deployed:

- **If you train near Chinchilla**, sparsity is generally not beneficial — the marginal capacity gain from sparsity doesn't compensate for the increased training cost (you'd be better off training a larger dense model).
- **If you train well beyond Chinchilla** (as many production models do — Llama2-7B at ~14×, GPT-3 variants at > 100×), sparsity becomes optimal because dense training hits diminishing returns while sparsity provides a new avenue for improvement.

This reframes the sparsity debate from "can sparse models match dense ones?" to "in what training regime does sparsity become the right choice?" — a much more nuanced and actionable question.

The practical cost model is also a distinctive contribution. By providing two cost models — dense FLOPs (conservative: no training-time sparsity speedup) and sparse FLOPs (optimistic: perfect training-time speedup) — the paper brackets the realistic range. The finding that, even under conservative dense FLOPs, 50% sparsity becomes optimal at ~5× Chinchilla for ViT and ~70× for T5, and that for sparse FLOPs these thresholds drop to <2× and <3× respectively, provides concrete guidance: if your hardware supports sparse training, sparsity is beneficial at modest overtraining; if not, you need substantial overtraining, but that's already the norm for large language models.

**Tie to evidence.** Figure 1 (Right) for ViT and Figure 4 for T5 show the optimal sparsity contours in $(N, C)$ space alongside the Chinchilla line. The parallelism is visually clear. Figure 5 provides the empirical illustration: as sparse FLOPs increase, the loss curves for higher sparsities sequentially cross below the dense curve — 50% overtakes dense first, then 75% overtakes 50%, then 87.5% overtakes 75%. The crossing points define the compute budgets at which each sparsity becomes optimal. The analytical expression (Equation 6) provides the closed-form relationship that generates these contours from the fitted coefficients, closing the loop from data to theory to prediction.

**Incremental or fundamental?** This is a **fundamental extension** of the compute-optimality framework to a new dimension. It's not merely "Chinchilla but with one more variable" — the structural finding that optimal sparse models form a half-plane rather than a line is a qualitatively different geometric result that has no analog in the dense case. It fundamentally changes how one thinks about the design space: rather than a single optimal model for each budget, there's a Pareto frontier of sparsity-size combinations.

### Innovation 3: Empirical Evidence That Sparsity Gains Are Remarkably Consistent Across Domains and Patterns — With Sharp Boundaries

**What’s distinctive at the idea level.** The paper discovers an empirical regularity that was not theoretically guaranteed and that has significant practical implications: **the capacity multiplier provided by sparsity is nearly identical across vision and language domains** (Table 2: 1.60× vs. 1.59× at 50%, 2.17× vs. 2.16× at 75%, 2.63× vs. 2.63× at 87.5%), **and saturates in a predictable, domain-independent way**. This is not a theoretical result — there's no first-principles reason why ViT on image classification and T5 on masked language modeling should exhibit the same sparsity gains. It's a purely empirical finding that suggests sparsity interacts with Transformer capacity in a domain-agnostic way, likely tied to fundamental properties of the architecture rather than the task.

At the same time, the paper establishes **sharp boundaries** on this regularity. The extension to n:m structured sparsity (Section 4.1) reveals that the pattern constraint dramatically changes the picture: while 2:4 (50% sparse) achieves 1.56× gain — close to unstructured 50% at 1.59× — the 1:4 pattern (75% sparse) achieves only 1.62×, barely better than 2:4 and far below unstructured 75% at 2.16×. The structural constraint of keeping exactly 1 out of 4 weights destroys most of the capacity benefit. This is a **negative result with strong practical implications**: the widely-adopted 2:4 sparsity pattern (supported in NVIDIA Ampere GPUs) extracts most of the achievable gain; moving to sparser patterns like 1:4 or 2:8 provides diminishing or negligible additional benefit, suggesting that hardware efforts to support finer-grained sparsity may not be worth the silicon cost.

**Comparison to prior work.** The prior sparsity literature was domain-fragmented: pruning results on vision models (ResNet/ImageNet) told you little about what to expect on language models (BERT/GLUE or GPT/language modeling), and vice versa. There was no framework for predicting whether a sparsity technique that worked in one domain would transfer to another. This paper provides the first cross-domain evidence that, at the level of scaling laws, sparsity behaves similarly — the exponents and gain factors are nearly identical. 

The n:m finding contrasts with the prevailing narrative in hardware-algorithm co-design, which often assumes that supporting more aggressive sparsity patterns (1:4, 2:8, etc.) will unlock proportionally more efficiency. This paper suggests the opposite: the capacity benefit of n:m sparsity saturates very quickly (the exponent $b_S = 2.752$ for n:m vs. $0.722$ for unstructured), and pushing beyond 2:4 yields almost no additional gain. This is a **diagnostic finding** that should influence hardware roadmaps.

**Significance beyond raw performance.** The domain-consistency finding has two major implications:

1. **Transferability of sparsity research**: Since sparsity gains are domain-independent at the scaling law level, improvements to sparsification algorithms developed on cheaper vision benchmarks may transfer to expensive language model training. This lowers the barrier to entry for sparsity research — you don't need a massive language model training budget to study core sparsity scaling behavior.

2. **Predictive power for new domains**: If sparsity behavior is architectural rather than task-dependent, one can estimate sparsity gains for a new domain by fitting a small number of runs to the same functional form, reusing coefficients from related domains as priors.

The n:m finding is significant as a **practical decision boundary**: if you have hardware that supports 2:4 sparsity, you're already capturing most of the achievable structured-sparsity benefit. Investment in supporting 1:4 or 1:8 patterns is unlikely to yield proportional returns, at least for the Transformer architectures and scales studied.

**Tie to evidence.** Table 2 provides the cross-domain comparison with values that are "within 0.01 of each other" — the authors acknowledge this precise match is likely coincidental, but the ~1.60× / ~2.16× / ~2.63× pattern is consistent. Table 3 shows the n:m gains: 1:4 at 1.62× is barely above 2:4 at 1.56×, and far below unstructured 75% at 2.16×. Figure 6 visualizes the n:m data, showing the same parallel-line structure as unstructured sparsity (Figure 2), confirming the functional form generalizes but with different coefficient values.

**Incremental or fundamental?** This is a **fundamental empirical discovery** about the nature of sparsity in Transformers. The domain independence was not obvious ex ante — it could have turned out that sparsity helps more in language (where models are famously overparameterized) or more in vision (where inductive biases might interact with sparsity patterns). The n:m saturation finding is similarly non-obvious and has direct consequences for hardware design. Both findings change how researchers and practitioners should think about sparsity: not as a domain-specific tuning knob, but as a general architectural property with predictable, domain-independent scaling behavior, bounded by the specific sparsity pattern employed.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses two large-scale datasets. For vision: **JFT-4B** (Dehghani et al., 2023), consisting of approximately 4 billion images, used for multi-label image classification with Vision Transformers. For language: **C4** (Raffel et al., 2020a), consisting of 150+ billion tokens, used for masked language modeling with T5 encoder-decoder models. The paper does not report a separate train/validation/test split for either dataset — instead, it reports validation loss on the standard evaluation splits from the original ViT-scaling (Zhai et al., 2022) and T5 (Raffel et al., 2020b) recipes. The specific test set sizes are not stated in the main text, though the ViT evaluation presumably follows the JFT-4B evaluation protocol from Zhai et al. (2022) and the T5 evaluation follows the C4 perplexity evaluation from Raffel et al. (2020b).

- **Base model(s).** Two Transformer families are used. For vision: **Vision Transformers (ViT)** (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021), starting from ViT-B/16 and scaling only the hidden dimension to produce models with non-zero parameter counts spanning 0.66M to 42.4M (7 sizes in 2× increments). For language: **T5 encoder-decoder models** (Raffel et al., 2020b, improved 1.1 version), starting from t5-base and similarly scaling only the hidden dimension to produce models with 1.3M to 85M non-zero parameters (4 sizes in 4× increments). Both families are chosen to represent the two dominant Transformer application domains, and the width-only scaling strategy is explicitly validated: "we did not observe any notable performance decrease for dense models using this scaling strategy, at the sizes we study." The choice of base model scale (ViT-B/16 and t5-base as starting points) reflects a pragmatic tradeoff: to produce a model with $N$ non-zeros at sparsity $S$, a dense backbone of size $N/(1-S)$ must be trained, which at higher sparsities or larger target $N$ quickly becomes computationally prohibitive.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **validation loss**: for ViT/JFT, this is the multi-label classification loss (presumably sigmoid cross-entropy, following Zhai et al., 2022); for T5/C4, this is the masked language modeling perplexity or cross-entropy loss (following Raffel et al., 2020b). The paper works directly with this pretraining loss, deliberately avoiding downstream task evaluation: "we deliberately consider the pretraining loss and infinite data setting to assess the effectiveness of sparsity in its most challenging... yet also most useful application (all further post-processing would directly benefit from a compressed base model)." All scaling law fitting is performed on this validation loss. For the limit performance analysis (Section 3.3.1), the "gain" factor is derived from the scaling law coefficients rather than measured directly — it is a predicted quantity, not an empirical metric.

- **Baselines.** The paper's evaluation framework is fundamentally different from standard pruning benchmarks — there is no single "dense baseline" against which all sparse models are compared. Instead, the appropriate baseline is **always a dense model with the same number of non-zero parameters, trained on the same amount of data**, because the paper's central argument (Section 2) is that any other comparison is unfair. This means each sparse configuration $(S, N, D)$ is compared against the dense configuration $(S=0, N, D)$ predicted by the scaling law at that $(N, D)$. The paper does not compare against specific named pruning methods as baselines; rather, it establishes the **dense scaling law frontier** as the reference. For the pretrained-model pruning experiments (Section 4.2), the baseline is sparsifying from scratch using the same gradual magnitude pruning procedure. For the n:m sparsity experiments (Section 4.1), the baselines are the corresponding unstructured sparsity results at the same effective sparsity level. The paper does not report comparisons against prior sparse training methods like RigL (Evci et al., 2020), AC/DC (Peste et al., 2021), or lottery ticket approaches — these were tested during method selection but "saw similar or worse performance, while being more sensitive to hyper-parameters as we scale" (Appendix A) and were therefore not used in the main sweeps.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute is measured in two distinct ways depending on context. For the scaling law itself, the independent variables are $N$ (non-zero parameters) and $D$ (training data in images or tokens) — there is no explicit compute variable in $L(S, N, D)$. Compute enters the analysis when deriving optimal sparsity (Section 3.3), where total training FLOPs $C$ is approximated using the standard formula $C \approx 6ND$ for dense Transformers (Kaplan et al., 2020), or half that for encoder-decoder architectures. For sparse models, training cost is adjusted via the multiplier $c_{\text{mul}}(S)$ (Equation 4), which accounts for the fact that the model starts dense (25% of training at size $N/(1-S)$), is gradually pruned (50% of training following a cubic schedule), and finishes sparse (25% of training at the target $S$). The paper does not account for the cost of sparsity mask updates, gradient computations on zeroed weights (if any), or the overhead of the pruning algorithm itself — only the forward and backward pass FLOPs are counted, under the standard $6ND$ approximation. Two cost models are used: **dense FLOPs** (training the full dense backbone without any sparsity acceleration, cost multiplier $1/(1-S)$) and **sparse FLOPs** (perfect exploitation of sparsity during training, cost multiplier $c_{\text{mul}}(S)$ as derived). The inference-time cost model is intentionally simple: the paper "focus[es] primarily on the cleanest available performance metric, non-zero parameter count," acknowledging that "in practice, sparsity acceleration can be quite complex: current software/hardware may not provide ideal speedups and models generally also contain operations (e.g., layer-norms, attention) which do not benefit from weight sparsity."

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation, bootstrapping, or statistical significance testing on its scaling law fits. The validation approach consists of two components. First, **visual inspection of fit quality**: Figure 1 (Left) plots predicted vs. measured loss, showing points clustering around the identity line; Figure 3 overlays predicted curves on measured data points for ViT/JFT across all sparsity levels and model sizes. Second, **extrapolation**: the law fitted on the T5 sweep (maximum non-zero parameters: 85M × (1 - 0.875) = 10.6M? No — the largest T5 configuration is 85M non-zero parameters at 0% sparsity; the actual maximum non-zero count across the grid is 85M for the dense variant) is tested on a held-out T5-XL model with approximately 575M non-zero parameters at 75% sparsity, representing approximately 6.75× larger non-zero count than any training point. The paper reports that the prediction is "quite close" but does not report a numerical prediction error (e.g., RMSE or relative error). The fitting procedure uses Huber loss with multiple random starting points for BFGS, and the authors note that "the process [converges] to virtually the same values from different random starting points," providing some assurance against local minima. No confidence intervals, standard errors, or uncertainty quantification is reported for any of the seven fitted coefficients (Table 7) — the values are reported as point estimates only.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Scaling Law Fit Quality and Extrapolation

The central empirical result is that the 7-parameter function $L(S, N, D)$ (Equation 3) provides a good fit to the grid sweep data for both ViT/JFT and T5/C4, and extrapolates reasonably to a held-out configuration.

**T5/C4 fit quality (Figure 1, Left).** The figure plots predicted validation loss against measured validation loss for all 48 T5 grid points (fitting data) plus the single T5-XL extrapolation point. The authors report a fitting error of $7.60 \times 10^{-6}$ (Huber loss of $\log L$ with $\delta = 0.001$). The data points cluster tightly around the diagonal identity line, indicating good within-distribution fit. The extrapolation point (T5-XL at 75% sparsity, 250K steps with batch size 256) falls "quite close to the actual validation loss," though the paper neither quantifies the extrapolation error numerically nor reports whether this point lies within what would be expected from the fitting residual distribution. The extrapolation required architectural changes (using the T5-XL backbone rather than a scaled t5-base) and training recipe changes (batch size 256 for 250K steps instead of 128 for 500K), meaning it tests generalization not just to larger scale but also to a slightly different architectural and optimization regime.

**ViT/JFT fit quality (Figure 3).** The authors report a fitting error of $4.93 \times 10^{-4}$ (Huber loss with $\delta = 0.01$, on raw $L$ rather than $\log L$). Figure 3 shows predicted loss-vs-data curves (solid lines) overlaid on measured data points (markers) for each of the four sparsity levels (0%, 50%, 75%, 87.5%), with each panel containing seven curves corresponding to the seven model sizes. The authors emphasize that "the predictions in all subplots here are produced by a single joint law with the same parameters (not one fit per image)." The fit is visually strong across most of the range, with the notable exception that "at the lowest amount of training, some points are a bit off the prediction curve" — the authors hypothesize this is because "these runs only involve comparatively few training steps, which may be a slight bottleneck for the optimization process." This discrepancy at low data is a meaningful limitation: the scaling law assumes non-bottlenecked optimization, and the shortest training runs (55K steps for ViT) may not fully satisfy this assumption, particularly for the largest models in the grid.

**Fitted coefficient values (Table 7).** The specific numerical values reveal several scaling properties:

- **Size-scaling exponent $b_N$**: 0.392 for ViT, 0.245 for T5. Vision performance is more sensitive to parameter count than language performance in these regimes — doubling non-zero parameters reduces loss more for ViT than for T5.
- **Data-scaling exponent $b_D$**: 0.890 for ViT, 0.203 for T5. The vision models improve much more rapidly with additional data than the language models, consistent with the observation that ViT/JFT models "only start to saturate after extremely long training" (Section 2) while the T5 models at the studied scales may be closer to their data saturation point.
- **Sparsity saturation exponent $b_S$**: 0.821 for ViT (unstructured), 0.722 for T5 (unstructured). Both are substantial, indicating relatively quick saturation — the marginal benefit of additional sparsity declines significantly as compression rate increases.
- **Irreducible loss $c$**: 4.517 for ViT (multi-label classification on JFT-4B), 0.651 for T5 (masked language modeling on C4). These represent fundamentally different loss scales and tasks, so direct comparison is not meaningful.

**Cross-domain consistency.** The fitted coefficients reveal both similarities and differences across domains. The sparsity behavior (exponent $b_S$ in the 0.7–0.8 range) is broadly consistent, supporting the claim that sparsity acts similarly across vision and language. However, the data and size scaling exponents differ substantially ($b_N$: 0.392 vs. 0.245; $b_D$: 0.890 vs. 0.203), reflecting genuine task and dataset differences. The fact that the limit gain values converge to nearly identical numbers (Table 2: 1.60× vs. 1.59× at 50%, 2.17× vs. 2.16× at 75%, 2.63× vs. 2.63× at 87.5%) is a consequence of how the coefficients combine in Equation 7 — the differences in individual coefficients largely cancel out in the gain computation, producing apparently universal sparsity gains despite different underlying scaling behaviors.

---

#### Optimal Sparsity Contours

The optimal sparsity analysis (Section 3.3) answers: for a fixed non-zero parameter count $N$ and training compute budget $C$, what sparsity $S$ minimizes the predicted validation loss?

**ViT/JFT optimal sparsity (Figure 1, Right).** The contour plot in $(N, C)$ space — specifically, non-zero parameters vs. pretraining FLOPs — shows colored regions indicating which sparsity level is optimal at each point. The dashed line marks the dense Chinchilla compute-optimal frontier. Key numerical findings:

- Under **sparse FLOPs** accounting, 50% sparsity becomes optimal at < 2× the training compute of the Chinchilla-optimal dense model. This means that for a ViT trained roughly twice as long as the dense compute-optimal point, a sparse model with the same non-zero parameter count outperforms the dense equivalent.
- Under **dense FLOPs** accounting, 50% sparsity becomes optimal at approximately 5× Chinchilla training duration. This more conservative estimate assumes no training-time speedup from sparsity.
- Higher sparsity levels (75%, 87.5%) require progressively more overtraining to become optimal, with their contours appearing further to the right in the $(N, C)$ plane.

**T5/C4 optimal sparsity (Figure 4).** The same analysis on language data shows:

- Under sparse FLOPs, 50% sparsity becomes optimal at < 3× Chinchilla.
- Under dense FLOPs, 50% sparsity requires approximately 70× Chinchilla training duration. This is a substantially higher threshold than for ViT, which the authors contextualize by noting that "language models of the sizes we consider here are already typically trained for > 100× longer than Chinchilla (Brown et al., 2020)."

**Parallelism of contours (Figures 1, Right and 4).** A key structural finding: all sparsity contours run **parallel** to the dense Chinchilla line. This is a direct mathematical consequence of the separability in Equation 3 — the optimality condition (Equation 5) implies that $D_S$, the data processed under the sparse training budget, scales as $O(N^{b_N/b_D})$ with the same exponent as in the dense case. Visually, this means the sparsity regions in the log-log $(N, C)$ plots are stripes of constant slope, with higher sparsities corresponding to stripes further to the right (higher compute for the same $N$, or smaller $N$ for the same compute). The practical implication: if you know the Chinchilla-optimal parameter count for your compute budget, the compute multiplier needed for a given sparsity to become optimal is approximately constant across scales.

**Half-plane vs. line (Section 3.3).** The paper emphasizes that "while optimal dense models define a line on the parameter-FLOPs surface, optimal sparse models form a half-plane (with different sparsities unlocking multiple optimal sizes for a fixed training cost)." This is visible in the contour plots: for a fixed vertical position (fixed $N$), moving rightward (increasing $C$) crosses through regions of increasing sparsity, meaning multiple sparsities can be optimal for the same $N$ depending on the training budget. Conversely, for a fixed horizontal position (fixed $C$), moving upward (increasing $N$) crosses through regions of decreasing sparsity, meaning a given compute budget can be optimally allocated to different combinations of model size and sparsity. This flexibility is absent in dense scaling, where the Chinchilla line defines a single optimal $N$ for each $C$.

**Empirical loss-vs-FLOPs curves (Figure 5).** The paper provides a direct empirical illustration for three ViT sizes (0.66M, 1.33M, 2.65M non-zero parameters) at four sparsity levels each. As sparse pretraining FLOPs increase (x-axis), the loss curves (y-axis) for higher sparsities sequentially cross below the dense curve:

- At the smallest model (0.66M, left panel), the 50% sparse curve crosses below the dense curve at relatively low FLOPs; 75% crosses below 50% at moderate FLOPs; 87.5% crosses below 75% at high FLOPs.
- At the largest model shown (2.65M, right panel), the crossing points shift to higher FLOPs for all sparsities, consistent with the scaling law's prediction that optimal sparsity depends on both $N$ and $C$.
- Crucially, once a sparser curve crosses below a denser one, it stays below — there are no "recrossings," confirming the monotonicity of sparsity benefit with compute that the scaling law assumes.

#### Limit Performance: Dense-Size Equivalents

Table 2 reports the "gain" factor $\text{gain}(S)$ (Equation 7) — the factor by which a dense model's non-zero parameter count must be multiplied to match the loss of a sparse model with the same $N$, in the infinite-data limit:

| Sparsity | Compression | ViT/JFT gain | T5/C4 gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50% | 2× | 1.60× | 1.59× |
| 75% | 4× | 2.17× | 2.16× |
| 87.5% | 8× | 2.63× | 2.63× |

These values are **predictions derived from the fitted scaling law coefficients**, not directly measured quantities — they represent what the law implies about infinite-data behavior, extrapolating beyond the finite training durations of the actual experiments. The key observations:

- A 75% sparse model with $N$ non-zero parameters is predicted to perform equivalently to a dense model with approximately 2.17$N$ parameters, when both are trained on very large amounts of data. This is less than the 4× compression ratio — sparse parameters are individually less efficient than dense ones — but still a substantial capacity multiplier.
- The gain increases sublinearly with compression: doubling compression from 2× to 4× increases gain from 1.60× to 2.17× (a factor of 1.36×), while doubling again from 4× to 8× increases gain from 2.17× to 2.63× (a factor of 1.21×). This saturating pattern reflects the exponent $b_S < 1$ in the sparsity factor.
- The cross-domain consistency (1.60× vs. 1.59×, 2.17× vs. 2.16×, 2.63× vs. 2.63×) is "within 0.01 of each other," which the authors acknowledge is "likely a coincidence" at that level of precision, but the overall pattern is consistent: sparsity provides approximately the same relative capacity benefit in vision and language domains.

**What this does NOT show.** These gain values are for the limit of infinite training data. They do not describe what happens at finite training budgets — at lower data quantities, the benefit of sparsity relative to dense models is smaller (because the data bottleneck dominates and extra capacity from sparsity cannot be fully exploited). The gain values should therefore be interpreted as **upper bounds** on the practical benefit of sparsity at realistic training scales.

---

#### n:m Structured Sparsity Results

The n:m sparsity experiments (Section 4.1, Table 3, Figure 6) test whether hardware-friendly structured sparsity patterns follow the same scaling law and quantify the capacity cost of the structural constraint.

**Reduced fitting protocol.** Only the three sparsity-specific coefficients ($a_S, b_S, c_S$) are refitted, reusing the dense scaling parameters ($b_N, a_D, b_D, c$) from the unstructured T5 fit. Data is collected only for configurations involving "either the least amount of steps or the smallest model," substantially reducing the experimental cost. The authors fit a single joint n:m sparsity law (all four patterns: 2:4, 1:4, 4:8, 2:8) with the same $a_S, b_S, c_S$ values, reporting fitting error $2.1 \times 10^{-5}$.

**Fitted sparsity coefficients.** For n:m sparsity on T5/C4: $a_S = 8.64 \times 10^1$, $b_S = 2.752$, $c_S = 5.36 \times 10^2$. The critical difference from unstructured sparsity is $b_S$: **2.752 for n:m vs. 0.722 for unstructured**. This dramatically higher exponent means n:m sparsity benefits saturate much more quickly — the marginal gain from increasing sparsity drops precipitously after the initial increment.

**Qualitative pattern (Figure 6).** The loss-vs-size plot for n:m sparsity (showing dense, 2:4, 1:4, 4:8, 2:8, and unstructured 50% and 75%) displays the same parallel-line structure observed for unstructured sparsity (Figure 2). The 2:4 and 4:8 curves (both 50% sparse) cluster near the unstructured 50% curve; the 1:4 curve (75% sparse) sits between 2:4 and unstructured 75%, much closer to 2:4; the 2:8 curve (75% sparse) shows a modest additional improvement over 1:4. This visual pattern confirms that the general scaling law shape holds, but with different sparsity-specific coefficients.

**Gain values (Table 3).**

| Pattern | Effective sparsity | Gain |
|---|---|---|
| 2:4 (n:4, n=2) | 50% | 1.56× |
| 1:4 (n:4, n=1) | 75% | 1.62× |
| 4:8 (n:8, n=4) | 50% | 1.67× |
| 2:8 (n:8, n=2) | 75% | 1.81× |

The key finding: **1:4 sparsity (75%) achieves only 1.62× gain**, barely better than 2:4 (1.56×) and dramatically worse than unstructured 75% (2.16×). The authors conclude that "1:4 brings almost no advantage and 2:8 only a slight improvement, which is contrary to our unstructured results. We suspect that the 75% patterns may simply be too stringent to significantly increase capacity beyond their 50% variants." In other words, the structural constraint of keeping exactly 1 out of 4 weights non-zero is so restrictive that it prevents the model from using the additional zeros to encode substantially more capacity — the sparsity pattern becomes a bottleneck rather than a capacity multiplier.

The n:8 patterns show slightly better scaling: 4:8 achieves 1.67× (vs. 1.59× for unstructured 50%) and 2:8 achieves 1.81× (vs. 2.16× for unstructured 75%). The larger group size ($m=8$ vs. $m=4$) provides more flexibility for the non-zero weights to arrange themselves usefully, approaching unstructured behavior. However, the gain at 75% is still far below the unstructured equivalent, and the paper notes these estimates "are likely slightly more noisy due to less data used in fitting the curves."

#### Pruning Pretrained Models vs. Sparsifying from Scratch

The pretrained-model pruning experiments (Section 4.2, Table 4) compare two approaches: (a) sparsifying from a well-trained dense checkpoint using 5.6% of the original pretraining budget, vs. (b) sparsifying from scratch throughout the full training process. The metric is the relative amount of data required for from-scratch sparsification to match the validation loss of the pretrained-pruning approach.

**When pretraining cost is excluded (dense checkpoint already exists):**

| Model | 50% sparsity | 75% sparsity | 87.5% sparsity |
|---|---|---|---|
| ViT S/16 | 4.90× more data from scratch | 4.27× | 2.45× |
| ViT M/16 | 4.76× | 4.18× | 2.57× |
| ViT B/16 | 4.35× | 4.00× | 2.72× |

Interpretation: if you already have a well-trained dense ViT, pruning it to 50% or 75% sparsity with a 5.6% retraining budget is approximately 4× to 5× more data-efficient than training a sparse model from scratch. For 87.5% sparsity, the efficiency drops to approximately 2.5× to 2.7×. The decreasing efficiency with higher sparsity is attributed to "the increased divergence from the initial starting point" — at extreme sparsity, the final sparse model is so different from the dense initialization that most of the benefit of the good starting point is lost.

**When pretraining cost is included (total cost = pretraining + sparsification):**

| Model | 50% sparsity | 75% sparsity | 87.5% sparsity |
|---|---|---|---|
| ViT S/16 | 0.25× (i.e., 4× more expensive) | 0.23× | 0.13× |
| ViT M/16 | 0.25× | 0.22× | 0.14× |
| ViT B/16 | 0.23× | 0.21× | 0.14× |

Interpretation: when the pretraining cost is counted, sparsifying from a pretrained checkpoint is approximately 4× to 8× **less** efficient (values < 1× mean the from-scratch approach uses less data) than integrating sparsification into the full training process. The authors conclude: "when the pretraining cost is counted as well, pruning throughout the whole training process appears to be ≥4× more efficient, relative to the ≈5% pruning of pretraining budget." In practical terms: if you're starting a new project, train with sparsity from the beginning; if you already have a well-trained dense model you want to compress, post-hoc pruning is the pragmatic choice, but the total cost (including the sunk pretraining cost) is higher than the from-scratch-sparse approach.

**Recovery quality.** The paper notes that "the 50% models are approximately 0.2–0.3 points away from their dense baseline loss," connecting this to the limit gain analysis: "this matches our results in Section 3.3.1 that the size gain of 50% sparsity is noticeably less than 2× for well trained models." Even with 4 full epochs of dense pretraining, a 50% sparse model cannot fully recover the performance of its dense backbone — the 1.60× gain (Table 2) means the sparse model is equivalent to a 1.60× larger dense model, not the 2× larger backbone it was pruned from.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Pruning hyperparameters (Appendix B, Table 5):** The paper ablates the start, end, and update frequency of the gradual magnitude pruning schedule on ViT-B/16 sparsified to 93.75% on 900M images (chosen as "a high S... in order to amplify differences between parameter settings"). The start point varies from 12.5% to 47.5% of training, the end point from 62.5% to 87.5%, and the update frequency from 50 to 200 steps. Seven of eight configurations produce accuracy within a narrow range (45.04–45.33), with the only outlier being the configuration with the shortest pruning interval (start 47.5%, end 62.5%, freq 100, accuracy 44.65). The authors conclude that "most configurations perform very similar, except when the pruning period is too short overall," and select the 25-75/100 setup to "ensure that there is sufficient time for training a decent model before starting pruning, as well as for properly finetuning the final sparse version." This ablation is conducted at a single (very high) sparsity on a single model, so its generalization to the full sweep grid — especially to lower sparsities where differences may be smaller — is assumed rather than demonstrated.

**Choice of sparsification method: GMP vs. AC/DC (Appendix B, Table 6):** The paper compares gradual magnitude pruning against AC/DC (Peste et al., 2021), a sparse training method that alternates between dense and sparse training phases, on T5 models of two sizes (1.3M and 42M non-zero parameters) at three sparsities (50%, 75%, 87.5%), all trained for 250K steps. The comparison uses perplexity rather than loss ("we show perplexity as losses at these levels should be compared in log-scale"). For the 1.3M model, GMP and AC/DC achieve identical perplexity at 50% (17.11), with GMP slightly better at 75% (15.64 vs. 15.96) and similar at 87.5% (14.73 vs. 14.59). For the 42M model, GMP and AC/DC achieve identical results at 50% (6.11), with GMP slightly better at 75% (5.87 vs. 6.05) and at 87.5% (5.81 vs. 6.11). The paper notes that "on larger models, AC/DC appears to require some hyper-parameter reconfiguration for higher sparsities," and since "per-model hyper-parameter tuning is inconvenient for large scaling sweeps, while initial results also did not suggest clear improvements, we stuck to well established GMP." This ablation supports the choice of GMP for robustness, but the tested AC/DC configuration used the same pruning period as GMP ("we apply AC/DC only during the same pruning period as our GMP setup to ensure the same pre- and post-sparsification finetuning"), which may not be the optimal configuration for AC/DC — it is a comparison of out-of-the-box GMP vs. out-of-the-box AC/DC, not GMP vs. fully-tuned AC/DC.

**Sparsity-aware RMS for AdaFactor (Appendix A.1):** A critical technical detail specific to T5 training: AdaFactor scales learning rates by the root-mean-square of each weight tensor. The authors find this "does not interact well with high sparsity, as a tensor with many zeros tends to have a lower RMS, resulting in a smaller learning rate, which is especially problematic as high levels of sparsification require more recovery during the pruning process." The fix — computing RMS only over unpruned weights — is a sparsity-specific optimization without which the T5 sparse training results would likely be worse. This is not presented as an ablation with before/after numbers, but as a necessary adjustment discovered during preliminary experimentation. The implication: sparse training may require optimizer modifications that are not obvious from dense training recipes, and results obtained without such modifications (e.g., from naive application of dense optimizers to sparse models) may underestimate what sparsity can achieve.

**Fitting sensitivity: log-loss vs. raw loss for ViT (Section 3.2):** The paper uses different fitting objectives for T5 and ViT. For T5, following Hoffmann et al. (2022), the Huber loss is computed on $\log L$ with $\delta = 0.001$. For ViT, this setting "exclude[s] outliers too aggressively for ViT data (which gives a poor fit for smaller models)," so the authors switch to fitting on raw $L$ with $\delta = 0.01$. This is not presented as a formal ablation with quantitative comparison of fit quality under both objectives, but as a qualitative observation that the NLP-standard settings don't transfer directly. The sensitivity of the fitted coefficients to this choice is not quantified — the ViT coefficients (Table 7) are contingent on this fitting decision, and it's unclear whether the reported values would change materially under a different robust loss configuration.

**Choice of sparsity representation: $1-S$ (density) vs. $S$ (sparsity) (Section 3.2):** The scaling law uses the remaining density $(1-S)$ as the sparsity variable, modeling the effect as a power law in the compression rate $1/(1-S)$. The paper argues this is necessary because "0.75 is twice as sparse as 0.5" — the multiplicative nature of sparsity is captured by the compression rate, not the raw sparsity fraction. This is a modeling choice justified by the fit quality, not an ablated alternative. The paper does not compare fits using $S$ directly vs. using $(1-S)$ to demonstrate that the latter is superior — the decision is presented as a conceptual one based on the semantics of sparsity rather than an empirical comparison.

**Extrapolation with architectural changes (Section 3.2):** The T5-XL extrapolation test introduced several confounds: the architecture was T5-XL rather than a scaled t5-base ("to avoid any architecture bottlenecks and achieve better training utilization"), batch size was 256 rather than 128, and training steps were 250K rather than 500K. The paper does not separately evaluate whether the scaling law would have predicted accurately under the original architecture and recipe (which was computationally infeasible) — the reported "quite close" match is to a configuration that differs from the fitting data in multiple ways beyond just scale. This makes the extrapolation test a validation of robustness to joint distribution shift (larger scale + different architecture + different recipe) rather than a clean test of scale generalization alone.

**Omitted ablation: sparsification schedule shape.** The cubic schedule $S - S \cdot (1-t)^3$ was chosen following Zhu & Gupta (2017), but the paper does not ablate alternative schedule shapes (linear, cosine, exponential) to determine whether the scaling law form or fitted coefficients depend on this choice. The optimal sparsity derivation (Equation 4) explicitly depends on the integral of the schedule through $c_{\text{mul}}(S)$, so a different schedule would yield different cost multipliers and potentially different optimal sparsity contours. This is acknowledged implicitly — the derivation of $c_{\text{mul}}(S)$ is presented as specific to their experimental setup — but the sensitivity of conclusions to this choice is unexplored.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: The scaling law $L(S, N, D)$ accurately describes the relationship between sparsity, non-zero parameters, and training data.**

The evidence for this claim is the visual fit quality in Figures 1 (Left), 3, and 6, plus the T5-XL extrapolation. This evidence supports the claim for the studied ranges — the law fits the grid data well and extrapolates reasonably to a 6.75× larger non-zero count for T5. However, several qualifications are necessary:

- **The law has not been tested on decoder-only architectures.** All experiments use ViT (encoder-only for vision) and T5 (encoder-decoder for language). Modern large language models are predominantly decoder-only (GPT family, LLaMA, PaLM). Whether the same functional form — particularly the separability of sparsity and data terms — holds for autoregressive language modeling with decoder-only Transformers is an open question. The T5 masked language modeling objective is sufficiently different from autoregressive next-token prediction that transfer of the scaling coefficients cannot be assumed.

- **The extrapolation test has confounds.** The T5-XL test changed the architecture and training recipe simultaneously with scale. A cleaner test would have used the same architecture scaled up (e.g., a wider t5-base) with the same recipe. The fact that the prediction was "quite close" despite these changes is encouraging for robustness, but a more rigorous evaluation would isolate the scaling dimension from the architectural and recipe dimensions.

- **The ViT short-training discrepancy is not quantified.** The paper notes that at the lowest training duration for ViT, "some points are a bit off the prediction curve" and attributes this to optimization bottlenecking. The magnitude of this deviation is not reported, and no attempt is made to model this effect (e.g., with an additional transient term). For applications where short training runs are relevant, the law may systematically overestimate or underestimate performance.

- **No uncertainty quantification.** All seven coefficients are reported as point estimates with no confidence intervals, standard errors, or posterior distributions. The fitting error (Huber loss) is reported but is in units that depend on the fitting configuration (log or raw, delta value) and cannot be easily interpreted as a predictive uncertainty. This means users of the scaling law cannot assess how much to trust predictions at novel $(S, N, D)$ combinations.

- **The law predicts monotonic sparsity benefit, which the authors acknowledge is not always true.** For "very high sparsity (e.g., 64× compression) we sometimes see slightly worse performance, presumably due to imperfections in the pruning and optimization process." The law will predict continued (albeit diminishing) improvement at these extreme sparsities, which is a qualitative failure mode that users should be aware of.

**Claim 2: Optimal sparsity increases with longer training, and sparse models can outperform dense ones at fixed non-zero parameter count when training extends beyond Chinchilla.**

This claim is supported by the optimal sparsity contour plots (Figures 1, Right and 4) and the loss-vs-FLOPs curves (Figure 5). The evidence is internally consistent with the fitted scaling law — if the law is correct, the contours are mathematically implied. However:

- **The claim is a prediction of the scaling law, not an independently verified empirical fact.** The optimal sparsity contours are derived by differentiating the fitted law, not by directly measuring that sparser models outperform denser ones at specific $(N, C)$ points. The only direct empirical comparison of sparse vs. dense at matched compute is the loss-vs-FLOPs curves in Figure 5, which show three model sizes. These curves are consistent with the law's predictions (sparser curves cross below denser ones at higher FLOPs), but they cover a limited subset of the $(N, C)$ space and the crossing points are not explicitly identified or tested for statistical significance.

- **The "half-plane" claim depends on the cost model.** Under dense FLOPs, the optimal regions for different sparsities are still separated by roughly parallel contours — the "half-plane" interpretation means that for a fixed $C$, there is a range of $N$ values that can be optimal when paired with different sparsities. However, the width of this range (in log $N$ at fixed log $C$) is not quantified, and for practical purposes may be narrow. The "multiple optimal sizes" claim should be understood as a theoretical property of the continuous optimization rather than a statement that many different $(N, S)$ pairs are practically indistinguishable.

- **The dense FLOPs threshold for T5 (70× Chinchilla) is very high.** The paper contextualizes this by noting that production LMs are trained at >100× Chinchilla, but this number assumes the Chinchilla scaling law extrapolates accurately to 70× the compute range, which is itself an extrapolation. Hoffmann et al. (2022) demonstrated Chinchilla scaling over roughly an order of magnitude in compute; extrapolating to 70× is a substantial leap. If the dense Chinchilla line shifts at higher compute budgets (e.g., due to data repetition effects or changing exponents), the 70× figure could change significantly.

**Claim 3: Sparsity gains are consistent across vision and language domains.**

Supported by Table 2, where the gain values are nearly identical (within 0.01). This is a striking empirical regularity, but with important caveats:

- **The gain values are derived quantities, not direct measurements.** They are computed from the fitted coefficients via Equation 7. Small differences in individual coefficients (e.g., $b_N$: 0.392 vs. 0.245; $b_D$: 0.890 vs. 0.203) happen to largely cancel in the gain computation, producing nearly identical results. This cancellation may be coincidental — the authors acknowledge as much — and may not hold for other model families, tasks, or sparsification methods.

- **Only two domains are tested.** "Consistent across domains" from two data points (ViT/JFT and T5/C4) is suggestive but far from established. Both use Transformer architectures, both use gradual magnitude pruning with the same schedule, and both operate in the massive-data regime. Whether the consistency extends to, say, CNNs on vision tasks, decoder-only LMs on code generation, or multimodal models is unknown.

- **The gain metric is an infinite-data limit prediction.** At finite training budgets, the effective sparsity benefit (how much better a sparse model performs than a dense one at the same $N$ and $D$) depends on the data term and is not directly given by Table 2. The practical sparsity gain at realistic training durations will be smaller than these limit values, and the reduction factor depends on how far the training is from the data-saturated regime.

**Claim 4: n:m sparsity follows the same scaling law but with substantially faster saturation, and 1:4 provides almost no benefit over 2:4.**

Supported by the fitted n:m coefficients ($b_S = 2.752$ vs. $0.722$ for unstructured) and the gain values in Table 3 (1:4 at 1.62× vs. 2:4 at 1.56×). The evidence here is thinner than for the main claims:

- **The n:m law is fitted to a reduced dataset.** Only configurations with the least steps or smallest model were run, reducing the number of fitting points and potentially making the coefficient estimates noisier. The paper acknowledges this: "the n:m estimates are likely slightly more noisy due to less data used in fitting the curves."

- **All four n:m patterns share a single set of sparsity coefficients.** The fitting constrains 2:4, 1:4, 4:8, and 2:8 to follow the same $a_S, b_S, c_S$, with only the effective sparsity $S$ differing between patterns. This assumes that the n:m constraint affects sparsity behavior identically regardless of the specific $n$ and $m$ values — e.g., that 1:4 and 2:8 (both 75% sparse) differ only in their effective sparsity, not in how the structural constraint interacts with capacity. This assumption may not hold: the paper's own results show 2:8 (1.81×) outperforming 1:4 (1.62×) at the same effective sparsity, suggesting the group size $m$ matters independently of the sparsity fraction. The single-fit approach necessarily averages over these differences.

- **The negative result for 1:4 is important but based on a small number of runs.** If confirmed across more architectures and tasks, the finding that 1:4 provides almost no benefit over 2:4 would be a significant result for hardware design — but with the current evidence (a subset of T5/C4 runs), it should be treated as a preliminary finding rather than an established fact.

**Overall strengths of the experimental design:**

- The factorial sweep design (7 × 4 × 4 for ViT, 4 × 4 × 3 for T5) provides systematic coverage of the $(N, S, D)$ space and enables fitting a joint law rather than separate per-condition fits.
- The use of two distinct domains (vision classification and language modeling) with different architectures (encoder-only vs. encoder-decoder) strengthens the generality claims.
- The fair evaluation framework (Section 2) is rigorously applied — comparisons are always at matched non-zero parameter count and matched data/compute.
- The extrapolation test, despite its confounds, provides some evidence that the law generalizes beyond the fitting range.
- The cost accounting (dense vs. sparse FLOPs) brackets the realistic range and prevents overclaiming based on optimistic assumptions.

**Overall weaknesses and missing experiments:**

- **Single sparsification method (GMP).** All results are obtained with gradual magnitude pruning using a cubic schedule. The paper tested alternatives in preliminary experiments and found them "similar or worse," but these comparisons were not systematic across the full grid. It is possible that a different sparsification method (e.g., second-order pruning, dynamic sparse training) would yield different scaling coefficients or even a different functional form. The claim that the scaling law describes "weight sparsity" in general, rather than "GMP sparsity," is an extrapolation from a single method.

- **No decoder-only language model experiments.** Given the dominance of decoder-only architectures in production (GPT, LLaMA, PaLM), the absence of experiments on this architecture is a significant gap. The T5 encoder-decoder results may not transfer directly — T5's bidirectional encoder and autoregressive decoder create different parameter usage patterns than a pure decoder-only model.

- **Limited sparsity range (50%, 75%, 87.5%).** The paper focuses on medium sparsities, explicitly excluding very high sparsities due to computational cost and diminishing returns. However, much of the practical interest in sparsity is at higher compression rates (90%, 95%, 99%), where the capacity-vs-optimization tradeoff is most acute. The law's prediction that "higher sparsity is always better" is known to fail at these extremes, so the fitted coefficients should not be extrapolated to very high sparsities.

- **No measurement of actual inference speedup.** The paper uses non-zero parameter count as the metric of efficiency, explicitly noting that "in practice, sparsity acceleration can be quite complex." This is a reasonable choice for a scaling law study, but it means the practical implications (inference latency, memory bandwidth, energy consumption) are not directly validated.

- **No comparison to alternative efficiency methods.** The paper does not compare sparsity against quantization, distillation, or efficient architectures at matched inference cost. This is outside the scope of a sparsity scaling law paper, but it limits the practical guidance: a practitioner deciding between sparsity and quantization for inference speedup cannot use these results to make that choice.

- **The T5-XL extrapolation result is not numerically reported.** The paper states the prediction is "quite close" but provides no number — no relative error, absolute error, or comparison to a baseline prediction. This makes it impossible to assess whether "quite close" means within 1%, 5%, or 10% of the measured loss.

- **No test of the predicted optimal sparsity.** The paper derives optimal sparsity from the fitted law but does not run an experiment where the law predicts $S_{\text{opt}}$ at some $(N, C)$ and then verify that this sparsity actually outperforms alternatives. The optimal sparsity contours are a theoretical consequence of the law, not an independently confirmed empirical phenomenon.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: The Sparsification Method is a Single Data Point — Scaling Coefficients May Be Specific to Gradual Magnitude Pruning

**The assumption or constraint.** All 160 experiments in the main scaling sweeps — the entire empirical basis for the fitted coefficients in Table 7, the optimal sparsity contours in Figures 1 and 4, the limit gain values in Table 2, and every downstream analysis — use exactly one sparsification method: gradual magnitude pruning (GMP) with a cubic schedule starting at 25% of training and ending at 75%, with mask updates every 100 steps. The authors acknowledge this directly in Appendix A:

> "As we intend to execute substantial sweeps across model size, training data and sparsity, it is essential that our pruning method is highly robust and does not require hyper-parameter retuning for each run. A natural candidate is gradual magnitude pruning (GMP) (Zhu & Gupta, 2017), which is well studied and known to be very reliable."

They tested alternatives (AC/DC, STE, RigL, second-order GMP) in preliminary experiments but found they "saw similar or worse performance, while being more sensitive to hyper-parameters as we scale." The paper's title and framing present the contribution as a scaling law for "weight sparsity" in general, but the empirical foundation is specific to one pruning algorithm with one schedule shape evaluated at one set of hyperparameters (start point, end point, update frequency).

**The consequence.** The fitted coefficients — including the sparsity saturation exponent b_S, the asymptotic constant c_S, and the resulting limit gain values in Table 2 — may be upper or lower bounds on what sparsity can achieve, depending on whether GMP is near-optimal or substantially suboptimal for the studied configurations. If a different sparsification method produces qualitatively different sparsity scaling behavior — for example, if a method that dynamically regrows weights (like RigL) or uses second-order information yields less saturating sparsity benefits (lower b_S), or if it maintains performance at much higher sparsities than GMP can — then the central quantitative findings of the paper (2.17× gain at 75% sparsity, 50% sparsity becoming optimal at < 2× Chinchilla under sparse FLOPs, 70× threshold for T5 under dense FLOPs) would not describe what sparsity *can* achieve, only what GMP achieves. A practitioner choosing a sparsification method based on these numbers is choosing GMP's performance envelope, not sparsity's.

Even if the functional form L(S, N, D) generalizes across methods — which the paper provides no evidence for — the specific coefficient values likely do not. The n:m experiments in Section 4.1 demonstrate this concretely: changing only the sparsity *pattern* (from unstructured to n:m) while keeping the pruning algorithm the same (gradual pruning, Algorithm 1) shifted b_S from 0.722 to 2.752 — a nearly 4× change in the saturation exponent — and dramatically altered the gain values (2.16× vs. 1.62× at 75% sparsity). If changing only the constraint pattern produces such large coefficient shifts, it is a reasonable conjecture that changing the pruning algorithm entirely could produce similarly large or larger shifts.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper itself provides direct evidence of coefficient sensitivity in Appendix B, Table 6: on T5 models, GMP and AC/DC produce similar but not identical perplexity values, with GMP slightly better at 75% and 87.5% sparsity on the larger model (5.87 vs. 6.05 perplexity at 75%, 5.81 vs. 6.11 at 87.5%). These differences, while small on the perplexity scale, represent nontrivial differences in the effective sparsity benefit — and AC/DC was tested with the same sparsification interval as GMP, which the authors acknowledge may not be optimal for AC/DC ("on larger models, AC/DC appears to require some hyper-parameter reconfiguration for higher sparsities"). A fully-tuned alternative method could, in principle, outperform GMP by a margin that would shift the sparsity coefficients meaningfully.

The Appendix B ablation on GMP hyperparameters (Table 5) is conducted at a single configuration: ViT-B/16 at 93.75% sparsity, 900M images. It shows robustness to schedule parameters within the GMP family — start/end points and update frequency can vary substantially without large accuracy changes — but does not test whether the *shape* of the sparsity-benefit curve (a_S, b_S, c_S) is invariant to these choices. The cubic schedule itself is inherited from Zhu & Gupta (2017) without ablation against alternative schedules that might produce different sparsity scaling behavior.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation empirically. It does not fit scaling laws for an alternative sparsification method (even in a reduced-data protocol like the n:m experiments) to test coefficient stability. The discussion section (Section 6) acknowledges the limitation in general terms: "we speculate that specific coefficient values can be improved significantly with more extensive per-run tuning and/or better sparsification techniques." However, this framing treats coefficient improvement as a one-sided adjustment ("better techniques will give better coefficients") rather than acknowledging that a different method could produce a qualitatively different functional form — for instance, one where sparsity interacts with data scaling (violating separability), or one where the benefit does not saturate as quickly. The practical consequence is that a reader deploying these scaling laws should treat the specific numerical predictions (e.g., "2.17× gain at 75% sparsity") as GMP-specific estimates, not as fundamental properties of sparse Transformers.

The paper does not suggest fitting alternative-method laws as future work, instead pointing toward "more extensively investigating advanced sparsification approaches" as a general research direction. A practitioner using the paper's numbers for deployment planning should understand these as **GMP-specific estimates**, not as fundamental limits of sparsity.

### Limitation 2: No Decoder-Only Language Model Experiments — The Dominant Production Architecture Is Untested

**The assumption or constraint.** All language experiments use T5, an encoder-decoder architecture trained with a masked language modeling (span corruption) objective. The paper does not study decoder-only Transformer architectures (GPT, LLaMA, PaLM) trained with autoregressive next-token prediction, despite this being the dominant architecture for production large language models. The authors motivate this choice by stating they "consider encoder-decoder T5 models... trained for masked-language-modelling on C4" (Section 3.1), and frame the dual-domain setup (ViT + T5) as covering "vision and language" — but T5's architecture and pretraining objective are substantially different from the decoder-only models that constitute the vast majority of deployed LLMs.

**The consequence.** The scaling coefficients fitted on T5/C4 — in particular, the data-scaling exponent bD = 0.203, the size-scaling exponent bN = 0.245, and the resulting optimal sparsity thresholds (50% sparsity optimal at < 3× Chinchilla for sparse FLOPs, ~70× for dense FLOPs on T5) — may not transfer to decoder-only autoregressive LMs. The architecture difference matters for at least three reasons:

First, encoder-decoder models have distinct parameter allocations: the encoder processes the input bidirectionally while the decoder generates output autoregressively. Weight sparsity may affect these two components differently — the encoder might tolerate higher sparsity because it performs a single forward pass over the input, while the decoder's autoregressive loop might be more sensitive to capacity reduction since errors compound across generation steps. The paper's uniform sparsification strategy (same sparsity applied to all linear layers, including embeddings) may interact differently with this architectural asymmetry.

Second, the training objective differs: masked language modeling (predicting randomly masked spans) vs. autoregressive next-token prediction. The capacity demands of these objectives may differ — autoregressive modeling requires the model to learn long-range dependencies through causal masking, which might benefit differently from the additional capacity that sparsity provides (or might require different sparsity patterns, e.g., non-uniform sparsity across layers).

Third, decoder-only models at production scale (7B–70B+ parameters) are typically trained on much larger datasets (trillions of tokens) with different scaling behavior in the overtrained regime. The paper's T5 experiments train on up to ~65B tokens, while Llama2-7B trains on ~2T tokens — a 30× difference in absolute data scale. The paper's finding that optimal sparsity increases with training duration relies on extrapolating the fitted law beyond the studied D range; whether the T5-fitted bD = 0.203 accurately describes scaling at 30× the data is untested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper contains zero experiments on decoder-only architectures, and the word "decoder" appears only in the context of describing T5 as an encoder-decoder model. The extrapolation experiment (T5-XL at 75% sparsity, Section 3.2) tests generalization to a larger version of the same architecture, not to a different architecture.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the decoder-only gap as a limitation, nor does it suggest that future work should validate the law on autoregressive LMs. The title and framing — "Scaling Laws for Sparsely-Connected Foundation Models" — implies domain-generality that the experimental coverage does not fully support. A practitioner deploying sparse GPT-style or LLaMA-style models cannot directly apply the T5 coefficients; the functional form L(S, N, D) may provide a useful template for fitting their own law, but the specific numerical predictions (optimal sparsity thresholds, limit gain values) should not be assumed to transfer.

### Limitation 3: The Practical Inference Efficiency Gains Are Not Measured — Only Non-Zero Parameter Count Is Used as a Proxy

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper uses number of non-zero parameters as the sole metric for inference efficiency. Throughout, "effective model size" means non-zero parameter count, and all comparisons (sparse vs. dense, optimal sparsity analysis, limit gain) are expressed in terms of non-zero parameters. The authors explicitly acknowledge that this metric abstracts away many practical realities in Section 6:

> "as the main goal of this study was understanding core scaling relationships, we focused primarily on the cleanest available performance metric, non-zero parameter count. However, in practice, sparsity acceleration can be quite complex: current software/hardware may not provide ideal speedups and models generally also contain operations (e.g., layer-norms, attention) which do not benefit from weight sparsity."

**The consequence.** The paper's central practical claims — that sparsity provides a "2.17× gain" at 75% (Table 2), meaning a 75% sparse model with N non-zero parameters "matches" a dense model with 2.17N parameters — translate to actual inference speedup only under assumptions that the paper does not validate:

First, **realized sparsity speedup depends on hardware and software support.** Unstructured sparsity at 50% or 75% does not automatically yield 2× or 4× inference speedup. On CPUs, sparse matrix multiplication can achieve speedups roughly proportional to density (Elsen et al., 2020), but on GPUs, unstructured sparsity typically does not accelerate computation unless the sparsity pattern is structured (e.g., 2:4 on Ampere GPUs). The n:m experiments partially address this by studying hardware-friendly patterns, but even for 2:4 sparsity (the most widely supported pattern), the actual speedup relative to dense inference depends on the fraction of computation in sparse-accelerated operations vs. attention, layer normalization, and other dense components that do not benefit from weight sparsity.

Second, **memory bandwidth savings may not translate to latency reduction.** Weight sparsity reduces the memory footprint of model parameters (and thus the memory bandwidth required to load them), but in autoregressive decoding with small batch sizes — the dominant deployment mode for LLMs — inference is typically memory-bandwidth-bound on the *activations* (KV cache), not the weights. Sparsifying weights reduces parameter memory but may not reduce the KV cache, which scales with batch size × sequence length × hidden dimension. For large-batch or high-throughput inference, weight memory bandwidth becomes more important, but the paper provides no analysis of which regime benefits.

Third, **the "gain" metric assumes dense and sparse models have identical non-parameter overhead.** In practice, a sparse model with N non-zero parameters and a dense model with N parameters have identical costs for attention, layer normalization, embedding lookups, and other non-linear operations. The 2.17× gain from Table 2 means the sparse model matches the loss of a dense model with 2.17N parameters — but that larger dense model also has proportionally larger attention and normalization costs, so the actual FLOPs or latency improvement from sparsity is less than 2.17× when total inference cost (not just linear layer cost) is accounted for.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no measurements of actual inference latency, throughput, memory usage, or energy consumption for any sparse model. The only efficiency metric is non-zero parameter count. The n:m experiments provide a closer approximation to practical speedup than unstructured sparsity (since 2:4 has known hardware support), but still do not benchmark actual inference performance.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly in Section 6 and frames it as out of scope: "We think extending our results to different target metrics is a very interesting topic for future work." This is a reasonable scoping choice for a scaling law paper, but it means the headline "gain" numbers should not be interpreted as "inference speedup" — they are capacity-equivalence ratios, not latency or throughput multipliers. A practitioner deciding whether to deploy a sparse model should supplement these results with hardware-specific benchmarking at their target sparsity, pattern, and batch size.

### Limitation 4: The Scaling Law Predicts Monotonic Sparsity Benefit — It Cannot Model the Observed Degradation at Extreme Sparsities

**The assumption or constraint.** The functional form L(S, N, D) in Equation 3 models sparsity as a monotonic benefit: the sparsity factor aS(1 - S)^bS + cS decreases as S increases, so the capacity-limited component of loss always improves with higher sparsity. The law has no mechanism for sparsity to *increase* loss — it predicts that a 95% sparse model will always outperform a 90% sparse model with the same non-zero parameters and training data, even if the difference is vanishingly small due to saturation (bS capturing diminishing returns). The authors acknowledge this limitation in Section 3.2:

> "We note that this formulation suggests that higher sparsity is always better (but with potentially quite quickly saturating improvements), which may not be true in practice. For very high sparsity (e.g., 64× compression) we sometimes see slightly worse performance, presumably due to imperfections in the pruning and optimization process. This phenomenon could potentially be modelled by a quadratic, but for the present study we treat this as a bottleneck-case that we do not necessarily capture."

**The consequence.** This is not merely a theoretical edge case — it creates a **hard ceiling on the law's applicable sparsity range** that is not quantitatively defined. The law cannot be used to answer one of the most practically important questions in sparsity: "what is the maximum sparsity I can apply before performance degrades?" Because the law is monotonic, it will always predict that higher sparsity is better, even in regimes where real optimization breaks down. A practitioner using the law to choose a sparsity level for deployment might be guided toward 90% or 95% sparsity — where the predicted loss continues to decrease — only to discover empirically that performance collapses due to optimization failures not captured by the capacity model.

The paper's own experimental grid avoids this problem by restricting sparsity to 50%, 75%, and 87.5% — the regime where monotonicity roughly holds for GMP. But even within this range, the law may smooth over non-monotonicities that exist in the data. The paper does not report whether any individual (S, N, D) configuration in the grid violates monotonicity (e.g., a 75% sparse model performing worse than the corresponding 50% sparse model at the same N and D), which would indicate that the monotonic form is misspecified even within the studied range.

The gap between the law's predictions and reality widens as sparsity increases, but the paper provides no guidance on where the breakdown occurs. The statement "we sometimes see slightly worse performance" at 64× compression (93.75% sparsity) sets a rough boundary, but "sometimes" and "slightly" are not quantified — does the degradation appear for all model sizes? Only for the smallest? Only at short training durations? The scaling law cannot answer these questions.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's acknowledgment of the limitation in Section 3.2 is qualitative only — no figure or table quantifies the degradation at extreme sparsity. The Appendix B ablation (Table 5) tests GMP at 93.75% sparsity on a single ViT model, achieving 44.65–45.33 accuracy across configurations, but does not compare this against what the fitted law would predict at that sparsity (which would require fitting the law to 93.75% data, which was not collected). The gain values in Table 2 (2.63× at 87.5% sparsity) and the optimal sparsity contours in Figures 1 and 4 implicitly rely on extrapolating the monotonic form beyond the highest measured sparsity (87.5%) — the contours show regions where 87.5% and higher sparsities would be optimal at sufficient compute budgets, but those predictions depend on the law continuing to hold where it has not been validated.

**Mitigation status.** The paper suggests a quadratic term could model the degradation ("This phenomenon could potentially be modelled by a quadratic") but does not develop this extension. The limitation is explicitly flagged — which is good — but not resolved or bounded. A practitioner should treat the law's predictions as reliable only up to ~87.5% sparsity for GMP-trained models, and should not extrapolate the monotonic form to higher sparsities without empirical validation. The paper's silence on where the breakdown begins within the 50–87.5% range makes even this boundary imprecise.

### Limitation 5: The T5 Scaling Law Is Fitted on Only 48 Data Points, With No Uncertainty Quantification for Any Coefficient

**The assumption or constraint.** The T5/C4 scaling law — one of only two domains tested, and the sole language domain — is fitted to 48 data points (4 sizes × 4 sparsity levels × 3 training durations) with 7 free parameters, yielding approximately 7 data points per parameter. The ViT law has 112 data points (16 per parameter) and is "more robust" by the authors' own characterization. The fitting procedure produces point estimates for all 7 coefficients (Table 7) with no confidence intervals, standard errors, posterior distributions, or bootstrap estimates of uncertainty. The paper reports a scalar fitting error (7.60 × 10^{-6} for T5, Huber loss of log L with δ = 0.001) but this is in units that depend on the fitting configuration and is not directly interpretable as predictive uncertainty.

**The consequence.** Every downstream quantity derived from the T5 law — the optimal sparsity contours in Figure 4, the 70× Chinchilla threshold for dense FLOPs, the 2.16× gain at 75% sparsity in Table 2, the n:m coefficients that reuse the T5 dense scaling parameters — inherits the uncertainty of the underlying fit, but that uncertainty is completely uncharacterized. This matters particularly for the T5 results because:

First, **the T5 law extrapolates more aggressively** than the ViT law. The dense FLOPs optimal sparsity threshold for T5 is ~70× Chinchilla, which is a substantial extrapolation from the ~65B tokens of the longest training run. A small uncertainty in bD (the data-scaling exponent, fitted as 0.203) propagates to a large uncertainty in the compute multiplier needed for sparsity to become optimal, because the optimal sparsity contour slope depends on bN / bD. If bD were 0.15 or 0.25 instead of 0.203, the 70× figure could shift by a factor of several.

Second, **the 48 data points are not uniformly distributed.** The grid has 4 model sizes in 4× increments, meaning the smallest model (1.3M) is 65× smaller than the largest (85M). Points are sparse in regions of the (N, D, S) space, and the law's behavior in data-sparse regions (e.g., large N, small D) may be poorly constrained. The Huber loss fitting provides robustness against outliers, but with only 48 points, a single influential point could meaningfully shift coefficient estimates.

Third, **the T5 coefficients are reused for the n:m fitting** (Section 4.1), which refits only aS, bS, cS while retaining bN, aD, bD, c from the unstructured fit. Any error in these dense scaling parameters propagates directly into the n:m gain estimates in Table 3.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports that BFGS "converge[s] to virtually the same values from different random starting points" (Appendix C), which addresses the local-minimum concern but not the statistical uncertainty from limited data. The extrapolation experiment (T5-XL at 75% sparsity, ~6.75× larger non-zero count than any training point) provides a single holdout test, and the result is described qualitatively as "quite close" — no numerical prediction error is reported. This one data point cannot characterize uncertainty across the full (N, D, S) space. The ViT fit, with 112 points, produces different coefficient values than T5 (bN: 0.392 vs. 0.245, bD: 0.890 vs. 0.203, bS: 0.821 vs. 0.722), demonstrating that coefficients are domain-specific, but the paper provides no sense of how much these coefficients would vary under resampling or with additional data.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper follows the Chinchilla precedent (Hoffmann et al., 2022) in reporting point estimates without uncertainty quantification, which is standard practice in the scaling laws literature. However, the Chinchilla fits used substantially more data and larger models, making point estimates more stable. For a paper introducing a new scaling dimension (sparsity) with novel coefficients, the absence of uncertainty characterization makes it difficult for practitioners to assess whether apparently precise numbers (2.16×, 70×) are genuinely well-constrained or merely the central tendency of an underdetermined fit. A practitioner using these numbers for resource allocation decisions should treat the T5 coefficients as substantially more uncertain than the ViT coefficients, and should expect that refitting on a larger grid or with a different model family would shift the values by non-negligible amounts.

### Limitation 6: The "Optimal Sparsity" Contours Are Theoretical Predictions, Not Empirically Validated — No Experiment Tests Whether the Predicted S_opt Actually Outperforms Alternatives

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire optimal sparsity analysis (Section 3.3, Figures 1 and 4, Equation 6) is a mathematical consequence of the fitted scaling law, not an independently verified empirical phenomenon. The procedure is: (1) fit L(S, N, D) to grid data, (2) differentiate with respect to S under a compute constraint to find the S that minimizes predicted loss, (3) plot the resulting contours and report thresholds like "50% sparsity is optimal at < 2× Chinchilla." At no point does the paper run an experiment where the scaling law predicts that sparsity S1 will outperform sparsity S2 at some specific (N, C), and then measure whether this prediction holds.

**The consequence.** The optimal sparsity analysis is only as reliable as the scaling law it derives from. If the law has systematic biases — for example, overestimating the benefit of sparsity at high S (since it is monotonic), or underestimating the cost of sparsity at high N (since the FLOPs model assumes perfect training-time speedup under sparse FLOPs) — those biases propagate directly into the S_opt contours. A practitioner choosing sparsity based on Figure 1 or Figure 4 is relying on a theoretical optimum that may not correspond to the empirical optimum.

This limitation is particularly acute for the specific thresholds the paper emphasizes:

- "50% sparsity is already optimal for < 2× longer training than Chinchilla" (ViT, sparse FLOPs). This number comes from solving Equation 5 with the fitted ViT coefficients. If the fitted bS (sparsity saturation exponent, 0.821) overestimates how quickly sparsity approaches its asymptotic benefit — perhaps because GMP saturation is faster than what a better pruning method could achieve — then 50% sparsity would actually become optimal at a lower compute multiple than predicted, and the paper's threshold is conservative. Conversely, if the law underestimates the training cost of sparse models (the c_mul(S) formula assumes no overhead from mask updates, sparsity-induced optimization difficulties, or imperfect hardware utilization), then the threshold is optimistic.

- "For dense FLOPs it is ≈5× and ≈70×, respectively" (ViT and T5 thresholds). The 70× figure depends on the T5 coefficients, which as discussed in Limitation 5 are fitted to only 48 data points with no uncertainty quantification. The crossing point of the S = 0 and S = 0.5 loss-vs-compute curves at a given N depends on small differences in predicted loss, which are sensitive to coefficient estimation error.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 5 provides the closest thing to empirical validation: loss vs. sparse FLOPs curves for three ViT sizes, showing that sparser curves cross below denser ones at higher FLOPs. The qualitative pattern (crossings exist, they shift rightward for higher sparsity, and order is preserved) is consistent with the scaling law's predictions. However, the paper does not identify the specific crossing points and compare them to the law's predicted S_opt thresholds. The loss-vs-FLOPs curves in Figure 5 are generated from the same data used to fit the law (the grid sweep), so they are not an independent test — the law is fitted to these data points, so the predicted loss curves will necessarily pass near them. A genuine validation would require a held-out set of (N, C) configurations not used in fitting, where the law predicts a specific optimal sparsity, and a comparison of that sparsity against alternatives.

The T5-XL extrapolation (Section 3.2) tests the law's loss prediction at a single held-out (S, N, D) point but does not test optimal sparsity: the held-out point is at S = 0.75, and there is no comparison to S = 0 or S = 0.5 at the same (N, C) to verify that 75% is indeed optimal for that configuration.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats S_opt as a derived property of the law and does not frame its empirical validation as a separate requirement. This is standard in the scaling laws literature — Hoffmann et al. (2022) did not train Chinchilla at every predicted optimal (N, D) combination and verify it outperformed alternatives, because the computational cost would be prohibitive. However, for a paper introducing a new scaling dimension, a small-scale validation experiment (e.g., one model size, one compute budget, comparing the predicted optimal sparsity against one sparser and one denser alternative) would substantially strengthen the claim that S_opt is empirically meaningful, not just a mathematical consequence of the assumed functional form. A practitioner should treat the optimal sparsity contours as **hypotheses generated by the fitted law** that require independent confirmation before being used for high-stakes resource allocation decisions.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper transforms weight sparsity from an engineering technique — something you apply post-hoc to compress a model and then measure how much accuracy you lost — into a **first-class scaling dimension** with predictable, domain-independent behavior. Before this work, the field had mature scaling laws for dense models (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) and a large body of pruning research on small-scale benchmarks, but no bridge between them. The question "should I train a sparse or dense model for my deployment target?" had no principled answer — practitioners relied on rules of thumb, small-scale ablation studies, or simply defaulted to dense. This paper provides that bridge in the form of a joint scaling law $L(S, N, D)$ with seven fitted parameters, validated across two domains and two architectures.

The magnitude of this shift is best characterized as a **reframing with practical teeth**. It is not a paradigm shift in the Kuhnian sense — it doesn't overturn the dense scaling law framework, it extends it — but it fundamentally changes how researchers and practitioners should think about sparsity. The key conceptual moves are:

**1. Sparsity as a capacity multiplier, not a compression-recovery tradeoff.** The standard pruning narrative is: start with a large model, remove weights, retrain to recover accuracy. The implicit assumption is that sparsity is a form of damage you try to repair. This paper shows empirically that sparsity acts instead as a multiplicative factor on effective capacity — a 75% sparse model with $N$ non-zero parameters behaves like a dense model with ~2.17$N$ parameters (Table 2), and this relationship is consistent across vision and language. This reframes sparsity from "how much can I remove without hurting performance?" to "how much effective capacity does sparsity add at a fixed inference budget?"

**2. The training budget determines whether sparsity wins — not the architecture or the task.** The paper's central finding is that optimal sparsity $S_{\text{opt}}$ increases with training compute, and the contours of optimal sparsity run parallel to the dense Chinchilla line (Figures 1, 4). This means the decision to use sparsity is primarily a function of *how long you train relative to compute-optimal*, not of model architecture or task domain. If you train near Chinchilla, sparsity generally doesn't help — the marginal capacity gain doesn't compensate for the increased training cost. If you train well beyond Chinchilla (as most production LLMs already do — Llama2-7B at ~14×, GPT-3 variants at >100×), sparsity becomes increasingly attractive because dense training hits diminishing returns while sparsity provides a new axis for improvement. This single insight reconciles the conflicting prior evidence: Gopher (Rae et al., 2021) found sparse models could outperform dense ones at the same training steps (their training was long relative to the model sizes studied), while standard benchmarks often find sparsity underperforms dense baselines (they train near convergence, where dense models haven't yet saturated).

**3. Sparsity gains are domain-independent at the scaling law level.** The near-identical limit gain values in Table 2 (1.60× vs. 1.59× at 50%, 2.17× vs. 2.16× at 75%, 2.63× vs. 2.63× at 87.5%) across ViT/JFT and T5/C4 suggest that the capacity multiplier provided by sparsity is a property of the Transformer architecture rather than the task or dataset. This is a genuinely surprising empirical regularity — there was no theoretical reason to expect vision classification and masked language modeling to exhibit identical sparsity gains. If this holds across more architectures and tasks, it means sparsity research conducted on cheaper benchmarks (e.g., smaller vision models) can inform decisions about expensive language model training, dramatically lowering the barrier to entry for sparsity research.

**4. Structured sparsity has sharp, predictable limits.** The n:m sparsity experiments (Section 4.1) deliver a negative result with direct hardware implications: 1:4 sparsity (75%) achieves only 1.62× gain, barely better than 2:4 at 1.56×, and dramatically worse than unstructured 75% at 2.16× (Tables 2, 3). The saturation exponent $b_S = 2.752$ for n:m sparsity (vs. $0.722$ for unstructured) quantifies how quickly structured sparsity benefits taper off. This finding redirects the hardware-algorithm co-design conversation: rather than pushing toward ever-finer sparsity patterns (1:4, 1:8), effort should focus on making 2:4 sparsity more efficient in practice, or on supporting unstructured sparsity with approximate acceleration.

**Research directions that become more attractive:**

- **Scaling-law-guided sparsification algorithm development.** Since the paper provides a parametric form $L(S, N, D)$, new sparsification methods can be evaluated by fitting their own coefficients and comparing $b_S$ (saturation rate), $c_S$ (asymptotic benefit), and limit gain values against the GMP baseline in Table 7. A method that reduces $b_S$ (slower saturation) or increases $c_S$ (higher asymptotic benefit) is objectively better in a scaling sense, even if it doesn't outperform GMP on a single small-scale benchmark.
- **Joint optimization of sparsity, quantization, and architecture.** The paper establishes sparsity as a separable scaling dimension. Since quantization (Dettmers & Zettlemoyer, 2022) and mixture-of-experts (Clark et al., 2022) also have emerging scaling laws, there is now a path toward joint scaling laws that optimize over all efficiency axes simultaneously — a "compute-optimal efficient model" that trades off sparsity, precision, and conditional computation under a total inference cost budget.
- **Sparsity-aware pretraining from the beginning.** The pretrained-model pruning results (Section 4.2, Table 4) show that integrating sparsification into the full training process is ≥4× more efficient than post-hoc pruning. Combined with the optimal sparsity analysis, this suggests a new default for foundation model training: rather than training dense and optionally pruning later, practitioners should choose a target sparsity *before training begins*, based on their expected training budget and inference constraints, and sparsify throughout training.

**Research directions that become less attractive:**

- **Pruning benchmarks that compare sparse models to their unpruned dense parents at the same initial training budget.** The paper shows definitively that such comparisons are unfair in the foundation model regime (Section 2) — the proper baseline is a dense model with the same non-zero parameters trained on the same total data/compute. Future pruning research on large-scale models must adopt this evaluation protocol.
- **Pushing n:m sparsity beyond 2:4 without evidence of additional scaling benefit.** The paper's finding that 1:4 sparsity provides almost no gain over 2:4 (1.62× vs. 1.56×) suggests that hardware investment in supporting finer-grained structured sparsity patterns may not be justified by the capacity gains. Research effort is better directed at making existing 2:4 support more usable (e.g., better training algorithms, software stack integration) or at approximate acceleration for unstructured sparsity.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. Fit $L(S, N, D)$ for decoder-only autoregressive language models (GPT, LLaMA) to test whether the law transfers across architectures.**

The paper acknowledges training only ViT (encoder-only vision) and T5 (encoder-decoder language) models. The dominant production architecture — decoder-only Transformers trained with autoregressive next-token prediction — is completely untested. This is the single most important validation experiment: does the same functional form hold? Are the coefficients similar to T5 ($b_N = 0.245$, $b_D = 0.203$, $b_S = 0.722$)? The T5 objective (masked span prediction) and encoder-decoder architecture allocate parameters differently than decoder-only autoregressive models — the encoder processes input bidirectionally while the decoder generates causally, and sparsity may affect these components differently. A strong follow-up would train a family of decoder-only models (e.g., using the LLaMA architecture with modest sizes: 10M–500M non-zero parameters) on a large text corpus (C4 or The Pile) with the same GMP protocol, fit the 7-parameter law, and compare coefficient values against the T5 baseline. Even a reduced sweep (2–3 sizes, 2–3 sparsities, 2–3 training durations; ~24–36 runs) would provide the key signal: does the sparsity scaling exponent $b_S$ match across architectures, or is it architecture-dependent? If $b_S$ matches but $b_D$ differs (because autoregressive training has different data efficiency), the limit gain values in Table 2 may still generalize, but the optimal sparsity thresholds (Figures 1, 4) would shift.

**2. Test whether a different sparsification algorithm (e.g., second-order pruning, dynamic sparse training) changes any of the scaling coefficients, or changes the functional form itself.**

All results in this paper are specific to gradual magnitude pruning (GMP) with a cubic schedule. The authors acknowledge this: "we speculate that specific coefficient values can be improved significantly with more extensive per-run tuning and/or better sparsification techniques" (Section 6). But "improved" could mean higher limit gain (lower $b_S$, higher $c_S$) or it could mean a qualitatively different relationship — for example, a method that dynamically regrows weights during training might exhibit less saturation (lower $b_S$), or one that uses second-order information might maintain monotonic benefit to higher sparsities. A strong follow-up would replicate a subset of the ViT or T5 grid with at least two alternative methods: one from the dynamic sparse training family (RigL, SET) and one from the second-order pruning family (WoodFisher, M-FAC, or SparseGPT with retraining). The minimum viable experiment: one model size, three sparsities (50%, 75%, 87.5%), two training durations, with GMP as the control. Fit the three sparsity coefficients ($a_S, b_S, c_S$) using the dense baseline parameters from the original paper. If $b_S$ changes substantially, or if the law no longer fits well (e.g., residuals show systematic patterns rather than noise), then the functional form is not universal — it's specific to magnitude-based pruning, and the field needs method-specific scaling laws.

**3. Combine sparsity scaling laws with quantization scaling laws for joint efficiency optimization.**

Dettmers & Zettlemoyer (2022) established that quantization follows predictable scaling behavior — model performance degrades smoothly with bit width, with a power-law relationship. This paper establishes that weight sparsity also follows predictable scaling. These are complementary techniques (sparsity reduces the number of weights; quantization reduces the precision of the remaining weights), and they can be stacked (Han et al., 2016). A natural extension is a joint scaling law $L(S, B, N, D)$ where $B$ is bit width. The hypothesis: do sparsity and quantization interact multiplicatively (the capacity reduction from quantization is independent of sparsity), or is there an interaction term (sparsifying a quantized model is different from quantizing a sparse model)? A concrete experiment: take a subset of the ViT grid (2–3 sizes, 2–3 sparsities, 2–3 bit widths, 1–2 training durations), apply post-training quantization or quantization-aware training to the sparse models, and test whether a separable form $L(S, B, N, D) = f(S) \cdot g(B) \cdot N^{-b_N} + D\text{-term} + c$ fits the data. If separability holds, the joint law could identify "compute-optimal efficiency configurations" that minimize loss for a given inference cost budget measured in total bits (non-zero parameters × bit width), enabling principled tradeoffs between sparsity and quantization that are currently made by trial and error.

**4. Empirically validate the predicted optimal sparsity by running a holdout experiment where the law predicts $S_{\text{opt}}$ at a specific $(N, C)$.**

The optimal sparsity contours in Figures 1 and 4, and the specific thresholds ("50% sparsity optimal at <2× Chinchilla for ViT under sparse FLOPs"), are theoretical predictions derived by differentiating the fitted law — they have never been directly tested. The paper's own Figure 5 provides qualitative support (sparser curves cross below denser ones at higher FLOPs), but the crossing points are not identified and compared to predictions. A validating experiment: pick one ViT model size (e.g., 5.31M non-zero parameters), run the scaling law to predict the compute budget $C^*$ at which 50% sparsity is predicted to become optimal (i.e., where the $S=0.5$ loss curve crosses below the $S=0$ curve), then train models at sparsities 0%, 50%, and 75% at three compute budgets: $C^*$ (predicted crossing), $C^*/2$, and $2C^*$. If the law is well-calibrated, 50% sparsity should underperform dense at $C^*/2$, be approximately tied at $C^*$, and outperform at $2C^*$. This experiment would cost ~9 training runs (3 sparsities × 3 budgets) and would provide the first direct evidence that $S_{\text{opt}}$ is not just a mathematical consequence of the functional form but an empirically real phenomenon.

**5. Test whether the sparsity-data separability (Observation 3) breaks down when data is repeated rather than fresh.**

The scaling law assumes "an ideal training setup with no data repetition" (Section 3.2), consistent with the dense scaling law literature. But in practice, training data is finite — even massive datasets like C4 (150B+ tokens) may be exhausted when training very large models for many epochs. Muennighoff et al. (2023) showed that data repetition introduces a new scaling regime where the standard power-law form breaks down. If sparsity is truly separable from data scaling, it should remain separable even under data repetition — the sparsity factor should multiply the capacity term identically regardless of whether the data is fresh or repeated. But if sparsity interacts with overfitting (e.g., sparse models are more or less prone to overfit on repeated data), the separability would break down. A concrete experiment: take a subset of the ViT or T5 grid (2 model sizes, 3 sparsities, 2 training durations), run one set with fresh data (no repetition) and one set with data repeated 4× or 8×, and test whether the fitted sparsity coefficient $b_S$ changes between the two conditions. If $b_S$ is invariant to data repetition, the separability claim is strengthened and the scaling law's applicability broadens to data-constrained settings. If $b_S$ changes, the law needs a data-freshness interaction term.

**6. Measure actual inference speedup on hardware for sparse models, and compare the realized efficiency gain against the capacity gain predicted by the scaling law.**

The paper uses non-zero parameter count as the sole efficiency metric, explicitly acknowledging that "in practice, sparsity acceleration can be quite complex: current software/hardware may not provide ideal speedups and models generally also contain operations (e.g., layer-norms, attention) which do not benefit from weight sparsity" (Section 6). This creates a gap between the paper's capacity-gain numbers (2.17× at 75% sparsity, Table 2) and what a practitioner would actually observe in deployment. A practical follow-up: take one of the trained sparse ViT or T5 models, benchmark inference latency and throughput on representative hardware (e.g., NVIDIA A100 for 2:4 sparsity, or CPU with sparse linear algebra libraries for unstructured sparsity), and compute the realized speedup at batch sizes typical for the deployment scenario (single-query for interactive use, large-batch for offline inference). Then compare the realized speedup against the capacity gain from Table 2. If the realized speedup is substantially lower (e.g., 1.3× realized vs. 2.17× capacity gain for 75% unstructured sparsity on GPU), the gap quantifies the overhead of non-sparsifiable operations and motivates architecture designs where a larger fraction of total FLOPs are in sparsifiable linear layers. This experiment would bridge the paper's theoretical framework to practical deployment decisions.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**1. Cost-efficient inference for large-scale deployed models where inference dominates total compute.** Consider an organization serving a language model at high query volume — the inference FLOPs over the model's lifetime dominate the one-time training cost. The paper's limit gain values (Table 2) suggest that a 75% sparse model with $N$ non-zero parameters matches the loss of a dense model with ~2.17$N$ parameters when trained on sufficient data. For a deployment where inference cost is proportional to non-zero parameter count (e.g., CPU inference with sparse linear algebra, or GPU inference with 2:4 sparsity), this means the sparse model provides the same quality at ~46% of the inference cost. The 50% sparse model provides a ~1.60× quality-equivalent — useful when hardware supports only moderate sparsity. Crucially, the paper shows this holds across vision and language: a ViT deployed for image classification and a T5 deployed for text processing both benefit. The decision rule is: if your model is trained well beyond Chinchilla (which is typical for production LLMs, often at 10–100× Chinchilla), and your inference hardware supports the sparsity pattern you're using, you should expect substantial inference cost reduction at equivalent quality by deploying a sparse model.

**2. Resource-constrained on-device deployment where memory is the binding constraint.** For on-device models (mobile vision, on-device language), parameter memory is often the limiting factor — the model must fit in a fixed memory budget alongside the application, OS, and other models. The paper's scaling law allows practitioners to answer: "for my memory budget of $N$ non-zero parameters, what sparsity $S$ and training budget $C$ should I use to maximize model quality?" Using Equation 6 (or empirically, the optimal sparsity contours in Figures 1 and 4), one can compute $S_{\text{opt}}(N, C)$ for the expected training budget. For a typical on-device scenario where compute is not the limiting factor (training happens in the cloud, only inference is on-device), the answer will tend toward higher sparsities at a given $N$, because the training cost of the larger dense backbone is a one-time expense while inference memory savings are permanent. The paper's limit gain values provide the upper bound: at 75% sparsity, you get the quality of a model ~2.17× larger than your memory budget would allow if dense.

**3. Training budget allocation for foundation model projects.** Organizations planning a large model training run face a multi-way tradeoff: model size, training duration, and now sparsity. Before this paper, the decision was two-dimensional (the Chinchilla framework: pick $(N, D)$ for a given $C$). This paper adds a third dimension: for a target inference-time parameter count (which determines deployment cost), you can choose to train a model with non-zero parameters $N$, sparsity $S$, and backbone size $N/(1-S)$, with the training cost depending on $S$ through $c_{\text{mul}}(S)$ (Equation 4). The optimal sparsity analysis gives a concrete decision procedure: (a) determine your target inference parameter count $N$ from deployment constraints, (b) determine your total training budget $C$, (c) look up the optimal sparsity $S_{\text{opt}}(N, C)$ from the contour plot (Figure 1 or 4, or compute it via Equation 6), (d) train a model with backbone size $N/(1-S_{\text{opt}})$ following the GMP schedule. If the predicted optimal sparsity is >0, you get better quality at the same inference cost than a dense model. If it's 0, you're better off training dense and using the full backbone. The paper's finding that 50% sparsity is optimal at <2× Chinchilla for ViT (sparse FLOPs) and <3× for T5 means that even modest overtraining pushes the optimal decision toward sparsity — and most production training runs are substantially overtrained.

**4. Hardware accelerator design informed by scaling-law-derived sparsity benefit curves.** The n:m sparsity results (Section 4.1, Table 3) provide direct guidance for hardware architects: the capacity benefit of 2:4 sparsity (1.56× gain) is close to unstructured 50% (1.59×), but pushing to 1:4 (75% structured) yields only 1.62× — a negligible improvement over 2:4, and far below unstructured 75% (2.16×). The saturation exponent $b_S = 2.752$ for n:m sparsity (vs. $0.722$ for unstructured) quantifies this: structured sparsity benefits taper off extremely quickly. The implication for hardware design is clear: silicon area and engineering effort devoted to supporting patterns beyond 2:4 (e.g., 1:4, 2:8) is unlikely to yield proportional model quality improvements. Instead, effort should focus on (a) making 2:4 inference maximally efficient (e.g., reducing overhead from non-sparsifiable operations, improving sparse matrix multiplication utilization), (b) supporting unstructured sparsity approximately (e.g., via software-managed sparsity with caching, or via block-sparse patterns that approximate unstructured sparsity with hardware-friendly granularity), or (c) increasing the group size $m$ — the 2:8 pattern (1.81× gain) outperforms 1:4 (1.62×) at the same effective sparsity, suggesting that larger groups with the same $n:m$ ratio provide more flexibility and approach unstructured behavior.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not position sparsity against named alternative efficiency methods (quantization, distillation, mixture-of-experts) with explicit tradeoffs. It establishes a framework for deciding *whether to use sparsity at all* and *what sparsity level to use*, but does not articulate "prefer sparsity over quantization when X" — that comparison would require a joint scaling law spanning both techniques, which the paper does not provide. The decision rule the paper does enable is:

**When to prefer a sparse model over a dense model with the same non-zero parameter count:**

- **Prefer sparse** when your training budget exceeds the optimal-sparsity threshold for your domain and cost model. Under sparse FLOPs accounting, this threshold is <2× Chinchilla for ViT and <3× for T5. Under conservative dense FLOPs, the thresholds are higher (~5× for ViT, ~70× for T5) but still within the operating regime of most production language models (e.g., Llama2-7B at ~14× Chinchilla, GPT-3-scale models at >100×). If you are training well beyond Chinchilla — which is the norm for any model intended for downstream use rather than scaling-law research — sparsity is likely beneficial.
- **Prefer sparse** when inference cost (memory or FLOPs) is the dominant concern and training cost is a one-time expense. The capacity gain from sparsity (1.60× at 50%, 2.17× at 75%, 2.63× at 87.5%) directly reduces inference cost at equivalent quality, and the increased training cost of the larger dense backbone is amortized over many inference queries.
- **Prefer dense** when training near Chinchilla compute-optimality — the marginal capacity gain from sparsity does not compensate for the increased training cost of the larger backbone. This regime is common in scaling law research and in compute-limited academic settings, but rare in production deployments where models are trained well past the point of diminishing dense returns.
- **Prefer dense** when deploying at sparsity levels where the scaling law predicts monotonic benefit but the paper has not validated against optimization degradation (roughly >87.5% sparsity for GMP). The law's inability to model the breakdown at extreme sparsities means predictions in that regime are unreliable, and dense models may outperform in practice even when the law predicts otherwise.
- **If using structured sparsity**, prefer 2:4 or 4:8 patterns (50% effective sparsity) — they capture most of the achievable structured-sparsity gain (1.56–1.67×) with mature hardware support. Avoid 1:4 (75% structured) — it provides almost no additional benefit over 2:4 (1.62× vs. 1.56×) while requiring the same training cost overhead as unstructured 75% sparsity.
