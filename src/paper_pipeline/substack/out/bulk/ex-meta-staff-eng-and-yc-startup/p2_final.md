Here are the takeaways from this Rahul Pandey interview (went from junior engineer at Pinterest to Staff Engineer at Meta, ultimately left to cofound the YC-backed startup Taro):

**Joining a startup right out of Stanford (Kosei)**

Rahul’s first move after graduating from Stanford was joining a startup called Kosei, founded by one of his professors, as one of the first employees. He already had a return offer from Meta and four other big-tech offers in hand, but the decision was driven by a **two-way door** calculus: he knew that if the startup failed, he could always go back to big tech. “I could just go to Google next year. It’s no big deal.” Within a year, Kosei was acquired by Pinterest, which landed him a compensation package slightly larger than if he’d joined Pinterest as a new grad. Looking back, he regrets the *reasoning* behind the decision—not the outcome. The move was “based on ego,” feeling like he was too good for big tech, and he admits he wasn’t mature enough as an engineer to thrive in that early-stage environment. “The decision wasn’t sound.”

**Junior to mid-level at Pinterest (the twice-rejected promotion)**

At Pinterest, Rahul joined the mobile development team as a junior engineer (L3, internally called IC13), drawn by the chance to learn mobile and because it was a top business priority. His first six months were rocky—he floundered, unsure what to work on and lacking strong mentorship. That changed when a Staff engineer joined his team and took him under his wing. Working side-by-side with a strong peer taught him engineering fundamentals much faster and turned his performance around. He emphasizes that **the quality of your peers** is one of the two top factors in picking a team (the other is how important the team’s work is to the business).

His first two promotion attempts were rejected. The second rejection stung more—he thought he was clearly operating at the next level. The feedback from the promotion committee cited a project that had taken 16 months, interpreting the timeline as a lack of urgency. But Rahul knew the narrative didn’t match reality: the project had been deprioritized multiple times by leadership and he’d been repeatedly pulled onto other urgent work. He went through Pinterest’s formal appeal process and presented his case again, this time making the **narrative behind his work** explicit—showing exactly when and why priorities shifted. The committee reversed their decision and granted the promotion. The lesson burned in: “If people don’t understand the narrative, they will fill in their own blanks, and those blanks are likely not going to be favorable to you.”

**Job-hopping to Senior (IC5) at Meta**

After a few years at Pinterest, Rahul felt his career growth had plateaued and decided to move. He interviewed at Meta and got a job-hop promotion to Senior (IC5) with just about three years of total experience—a fast trajectory. He notes this as an interesting data point but also frames job-hopping as something that follows a specific curve: **“job hopping is productive early in your career and counterproductive when you’re more senior.”** Early on, each hop can level you up and broaden your exposure. But at higher levels, impact comes through influence—and influence requires relationships and organizational trust that you can’t rebuild if you’re constantly leaving. The cultural contrast between Pinterest and Meta was stark. Meta was “much faster-paced,” and people were “not afraid to be direct,” which he found refreshing. Where Pinterest’s culture leaned toward politeness and convolution, Meta’s directness meant problems got surfaced and solved faster.

**Building credibility and creating scope for Staff (IC6)**

Rahul spent substantial time earning trust before the Staff-promo opportunities arrived. He worked on fewer projects, but each was high-visibility and high-importance. “I would do fewer things, but I would do them really well.” That approach built his reputation: when leadership needed someone to drive a large, cross-org migration—a clear IC6-scope initiative—he was the obvious choice because he’d shown he was the best fit. This is his model for **being handed scope**: you don’t get tapped for the big role unless you’ve already proven you can deliver.

The second project that made his IC6 case was scope he **created on his own**. He noticed that engineers on his team and across the org (~100 engineers) were spending disproportionate time on a repetitive, manual build-and-test loop. He built tooling that automated the bottleneck, then personally drove adoption until it became the default across the whole org. This had leveraged impact: a relatively small investment of his own time multiplied across many engineers. Ryan notes the pattern of taking initiative and pushing adoption without being asked—Rahul calls the alternative just “being a good worker bee,” which doesn’t get you to Staff. Creating impact means identifying something nobody owns and making it happen.

**Transition to TLM and the path to leaving ($800k+ and walking away)**

Soon after the IC6 promotion, Rahul transitioned into a Tech Lead Manager (TLM) role. It wasn’t a calculated career move so much as a natural fit—he’d been informally tech-leading already, wrote a lot of the team’s code, and discovered he genuinely enjoyed working through others and watching them grow. When the team needed more people and a manager, he took it on because “it would be weird to hire someone above me.” The formal TLM title just codified what he was already doing. But even with total compensation north of $800,000 per year, he didn’t stay long. He’d reached a point where he felt his growth inside Meta had plateaued, and he felt satisfied with what he’d accomplished in big tech. The pull toward founding a company had been building for years.

**Founding Taro (YC-backed startup)**

The decision to leave Meta and start Taro lined up on three preconditions Rahul now recommends to anyone considering the leap: **enough savings** to not worry about finances for a few years, a **cofounder you’d want to work with** (whom he’d met at Meta), and a clear sense that your growth in your current environment has flatlined. He calls the cofounder choice “potentially the most important part of starting a company.” The third condition was crucial for him personally—he didn’t want to leave out of frustration or restlessness; he wanted to leave because it was the right next step, not an escape.

There’s a skill set you simply don’t build inside big tech: taking in raw user feedback, deciding what to build, and owning the product end-to-end. “These are product management skills that are critical when you’re starting your own company,” and big tech insulates engineers from them. On Y Combinator: he says it’s a **no-brainer for most first-time founders**, though the value depends on how strong your existing network already is. If you’re like most people starting out, the YC network, accountability, and sprinting toward demo day are well worth it. That sprint, in particular, forced a completely different pace of learning—expectations of weekly results compressed months of growth into weeks.

**Reflections on growth, luck, and networking**

Asked when he grew the most, Rahul named three accelerators. The first was co-working with the Staff engineer at Pinterest who pulled him out of his onboarding slump—having someone senior show him “how it’s done” accelerated his fundamentals dramatically. The second was taking initiative at Meta and pushing adoption of his tooling; that taught him how to create impact rather than just execute assigned work. The third was the YC demo-day sprint, where the intensity and unfamiliar problems forced rapid skill acquisition.

On luck, Rahul is unusually direct: he credits “almost all” of his career success to luck. But he emphasizes that you can increase your **luck surface area**—by being visible, by doing interesting things, and by genuinely networking. His networking philosophy isn’t transactional. It’s two ingredients: do interesting work that gives people a reason to want to talk to you, and be genuinely curious about what *other* people are working on. Most opportunities in his career arrived not through formal applications, but through relationships built this way. Every job move he’s made came through someone he knew.

**Advice to his younger self: “Don’t be an NPC.”**

His single line of advice: **“Don’t be an NPC.”** You can do things you aren’t explicitly told to do. You can build things. You can exercise creativity. Looking at his own path—doing well in school and checking boxes—he wishes he’d internalized earlier that he had more agency than he thought. “I wish I had built more things before entering the industry, rather than just doing well at school.”

---

Rahul’s **“Don’t be an NPC”** ethos surfaces across his whole arc: from creating scope at Meta by building tooling nobody asked for, to appealing a rejected promotion by reclaiming his own narrative, to eventually leaving an $800k+ role because he refused to let big tech’s script write his next chapter. His **two-way door** framing made the startup leap low-risk, but his regret about the ego behind it reveals a deeper thread—sound decisions require honesty about your own maturity, not just a safety net. Taken together, his philosophy of expanding **luck surface area** through visibility and genuine curiosity, combined with the belief that you can always just *do things*, forms a quietly unified playbook: treat your career not as a series of assigned quests, but as something you’re allowed to author.
