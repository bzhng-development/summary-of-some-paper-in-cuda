# MARS: Unleashing the Power of Variance Reduction for Training Large Models

**ArXiv:** [2411.10438](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10438)

## 🎯 Pitch

Variance reduction, long dismissed as ineffective for deep learning, actually yields large gains in LLM pretraining—MARS achieves the same GPT-2 validation loss as AdamW using roughly 1.8× fewer tokens. It not only boosts convergence but also lifts downstream accuracy by 3 percentage points on HellaSwag, all by injecting a scaled stochastic momentum into standard adaptive optimizers.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **MARS** (Make vAriance Reduction Shine), a unified optimization framework that reconciles preconditioned gradient methods with variance reduction via a scaled stochastic recursive momentum technique, producing three concrete instantiations—MARS-AdamW, MARS-Lion, and MARS-Shampoo—each integrating variance-reduced gradient estimation into a different adaptive preconditioning scheme (diagonal EMA of squared gradients, sign-based updates, and eigenspace preconditioning, respectively). Evaluating on GPT-2 pretraining (small/medium/large) on OpenWebText, MARS-AdamW reduces the training tokens required to reach AdamW's best validation loss by roughly 1.8× (28B tokens vs. 50B for GPT-2 large to reach a 2.58 validation loss, with MARS achieving a final loss of 2.51), while improving downstream 5-shot HellaSwag accuracy from 41.70% to 44.64%. The framework also reveals that Lion, Adan, and Muon emerge as special cases or near-equivalents of MARS under different parameterizations, establishing that variance reduction provides consistently superior convergence and generalization over standard adaptive methods across model scales, with the performance gap widening as model size increases.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Variance Reduction Has Failed to Transfer to Deep Learning and LLM Training

The fundamental question this paper tackles is: **can variance reduction techniques—which have been theoretically and empirically successful in classical stochastic optimization—be made to work effectively for training large-scale neural networks and language models?** This matters because the gap between theory and practice in optimization for deep learning remains wide, and closing it would directly translate to faster training, better final performance, and reduced computational cost.

The paper frames this question against a striking contradiction. On one side, there exists an enormous body of theoretical work on variance reduction for stochastic optimization spanning more than a decade. Algorithms like SVRG (Johnson & Zhang, 2013), SARAH (Nguyen et al., 2017a), SPIDER (Fang et al., 2018), and STORM (Cutkosky & Orabona, 2019) have established rigorous convergence guarantees: for nonconvex smooth optimization, variance-reduced methods achieve a gradient complexity of $O(\varepsilon^{-3})$ to find an $\varepsilon$-approximate first-order stationary point, improving on the $O(\varepsilon^{-4})$ rate of standard SGD (Ghadimi & Lan, 2013). This is not merely a constant-factor improvement but an *asymptotic* advantage—variance reduction provably requires fewer stochastic gradient evaluations as the desired accuracy increases.

On the other side, adaptive gradient methods—particularly Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015) and AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019)—have become the de facto standard for training virtually all large-scale deep learning models. The paper catalogs this dominance explicitly: "the majority of the renowned models, including GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2019), GPT-3 (Brown, 2020), PaLM (Chowdhery et al., 2023) and Llama 3 (Dubey et al., 2024) are trained with adaptive gradient methods" (Section 1). These methods do not incorporate variance reduction. Instead, they rely on preconditioning—adapting the effective learning rate per parameter based on historical gradient statistics—to handle the heterogeneous curvature of neural network loss landscapes.

This creates a puzzle: **if variance reduction offers provably faster convergence, why is it absent from the most successful optimizers in practice?**

### Why This Gap Matters: The Cost of High-Variance Optimization in LLM Training

The gap is not merely of academic interest. Language model training is inherently a high-variance optimization problem. As McCandlish et al. (2018) established, the gradient noise scale in large-batch language model training can be substantial, meaning that individual stochastic gradient estimates are poor approximations of the true gradient. This manifests concretely in several ways that impact practitioners:

**Training efficiency.** High gradient variance means that individual optimization steps are less informative, requiring more steps (and therefore more FLOPs and wall-clock time) to achieve a given quality. For LLM pretraining at scale—where training runs consume thousands of GPU-days or more—even modest reductions in the number of steps needed translate to enormous cost savings and reduced environmental impact.

**Small-batch regimes.** Variance reduction becomes especially important when batch sizes are constrained by hardware memory limitations. The paper explicitly demonstrates this sensitivity in Appendix E.7 (Figure 19), showing that the performance gap between MARS-AdamW and AdamW widens at smaller batch sizes—precisely because variance reduction compensates for the higher noise that comes with fewer samples per step.

**Optimizer stagnation.** Recent empirical evidence from Kaddour et al. (2024) and Zhao et al. (2024) has shown a sobering pattern: "these optimizers [LAMB, Lion, Sophia, Shampoo, SOAP] perform on par with AdamW in LLM pretraining, yet do not outperform it" (Section 1). Despite years of innovation in adaptive gradient methods—including layerwise adaptation (You et al., 2019), symbolic search for optimizer design (Chen et al., 2023), second-order diagonal Hessian estimation (Liu et al., 2023), and tensor-space preconditioning (Gupta et al., 2018; Anil et al., 2020)—none has been able to *consistently beat* AdamW on large-scale language modeling. This suggests that the field has hit a wall in improving adaptive gradient methods from within the same paradigm, and that a qualitatively different approach may be needed.

The paper positions variance reduction as precisely that qualitatively different approach. Rather than refining how the preconditioner is computed, variance reduction attacks the problem from the gradient estimation side: make each gradient estimate more accurate so that each step moves the parameters in a more globally beneficial direction.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies several specific limitations in prior work that prevented variance reduction from succeeding in deep learning and LLM training.

#### The Finite-Sum Structure Problem

The most fundamental obstacle is that classical variance reduction methods rely on the **finite-sum** structure of the optimization problem. In the traditional setting, the objective function takes the form:

$$F(x) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i(x)$$

where each $f_i$ corresponds to the loss on a specific training example, and the same set of $n$ examples is visited repeatedly (multiple epochs). Methods like SVRG and SAGA maintain either a full gradient computed at a reference point or a table of per-example gradients, exploiting the fact that the same examples reappear to construct corrected gradient estimates.

Defazio & Bottou (2019) argued that this finite-sum structure is **disrupted** in modern deep learning by data augmentation, batch normalization, and dropout—techniques that make each pass over the data non-identical. If the training data seen at epoch $k$ is different from epoch $k+1$ (due to augmentation randomness), the assumption that $\mathbb{E}[\nabla f_i(x)] = \nabla F(x)$ with the same $i$ across visits breaks down, and variance-reduced gradient estimates become biased.

The paper's key observation for language modeling is that this disruption **no longer applies**: "in training language models, data augmentation, batch normalization, and dropout are nowadays rarely used, which opens the door for applying variance reduction techniques in optimizing these models" (Section 1). Modern LLM training pipelines typically disable dropout entirely, use LayerNorm rather than batch normalization, and apply minimal or no data augmentation (token sequences are used essentially as-is). The finite-sum structure is effectively restored—each pass over the training corpus sees the same examples in the same order (or at least from the same stationary distribution without transformation). This is the crucial insight that makes variance reduction viable for LLM training where it previously failed for other deep learning domains.

#### The Gradient Estimation Cost Problem

A practical obstacle for variance reduction methods like SVRG and SARAH is the need for **periodic full-gradient computations** (or large-batch anchor gradient estimates). In SVRG, every $m$ iterations, the algorithm must compute $\nabla F(\tilde{x}) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \nabla f_i(\tilde{x})$ at a reference point, which costs one full pass over the dataset. For LLM training on corpora containing billions of tokens, such periodic full passes are computationally prohibitive.

The STORM algorithm (Cutkosky & Orabona, 2019) solved this problem by eliminating the need for periodic anchoring entirely. Its recursive momentum update:

$$m_t = \beta_1 m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1) \nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) + \beta_1\big(\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) - \nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)\big)$$

uses the gradient difference $\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) - \nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$ computed on the *same* random data $\xi_t$ at two consecutive parameter values to cancel common noise. This achieves the optimal $O(\varepsilon^{-3})$ rate without any full-gradient computations, making it a natural candidate for large-scale training. The paper builds directly on STORM as its variance reduction backbone, but with critical modifications that we will explore in Section 3.

#### The Mismatch Between Variance Reduction and Preconditioning

Even when variance reduction methods were attempted with adaptive gradient methods, the paper identifies a **fundamental design mismatch** that limited their effectiveness. The authors point to SuperAdam (Huang et al., 2021) as the primary prior attempt to integrate STORM with Adam-style preconditioning. Their diagnosis is precise:

> "their preconditioner matrix is designed following Adam's structure but does not account for the revised definition of variance-reduced momentum, resulting in a significant mismatch between the first-order and second-order momentum" (Remark 3.1).

What does this mismatch mean concretely? In Adam, the first-order momentum $m_t$ (which determines the update direction) and the second-order momentum $v_t$ (which determines per-parameter step sizes via preconditioning) are both computed from the same raw stochastic gradient $\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t)$. The squared gradient $\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t)^2$ in $v_t$ estimates the variance of the gradient direction, and this variance estimate is well-calibrated to the magnitude of $m_t$ because both are functions of the same random variable.

In SuperAdam, $m_t$ is the variance-reduced STORM momentum (which has lower variance than the raw stochastic gradient), but $v_t$ is still computed from the *raw* stochastic gradient's squared norm. This creates a scale mismatch: the preconditioner is calibrated to the variance of the raw gradient, but it is being applied to a momentum term with significantly different (lower) variance. The per-parameter adaptive learning rates end up being poorly matched to the actual update magnitudes, degrading the benefit of variance reduction.

The paper's solution—which we will examine in detail—is to define $v_t$ as the exponential moving average of $c_t^2$, where $c_t$ is the *same* variance-reduced gradient estimate used to compute $m_t$. This restores the calibration between first and second moments that makes Adam effective, but now with variance-reduced rather than raw gradient statistics.

#### Theoretical Convergence Guarantees Lacking for the Combined Setting

Beyond the practical mismatch, there was a theoretical gap. While STORM had established convergence rates for the non-preconditioned setting, and Adam had been analyzed under various assumptions (with notable controversy—Reddi et al. (2019a) showed that Adam can diverge even on simple convex problems, and subsequent work by Zhang et al. (2022b), Li et al. (2024), and Zhou et al. (2024) provided convergence proofs only under restricted conditions), no prior work had analyzed the convergence of a *preconditioned* variance-reduced method in the context of LLM-scale nonconvex optimization.

The paper addresses this gap directly by providing convergence proofs for both Algorithm 1 (the general framework) and Algorithm 2 (MARS-AdamW specifically), establishing an $O(T^{-1/3})$ rate for the gradient norm (Theorems B.5 and B.6 in Appendix B.2). The authors explicitly contrast this with AdamW: "we prove that under standard assumptions, MARS achieves a superior convergence rate of $O(T^{-1/3})$, outperforming the $O(T^{-1/4})$ rate attainable by AdamW" (Section 3.1). We will examine the theoretical analysis in more detail later, but the key point for motivation is that prior work neither implemented nor analyzed the specific combination of scaled STORM momentum with matched per-coordinate preconditioning that MARS introduces.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning can be understood through three conceptual moves:

**First, it identifies why variance reduction previously failed in deep learning but is now viable for LLM training.** The removal of data augmentation, batch normalization, and dropout from modern LLM pipelines restores the finite-sum structure that variance reduction requires. This is not a methodological innovation but an *observation about practice* that reframes the applicability of an entire line of optimization research.

**Second, it proposes a unified framework that fixes the specific design flaws that made prior variance-reduced adaptive methods underperform.** The key innovations are: (a) using the same variance-reduced gradient estimate $c_t$ for both first and second momentum computation to maintain calibration; (b) introducing a scaling parameter $\gamma_t$ to control the strength of variance reduction (rather than committing to full STORM, which may be suboptimal); and (c) applying gradient clipping to the intermediate estimate $c_t$ rather than to the final preconditioned update, following standard practice in neural network training. This framework is presented as general—accommodating any preconditioner design—and the paper instantiates it for three representative choices (AdamW's diagonal EMA, Lion's sign operation, and Shampoo's eigenspace preconditioning) to demonstrate its versatility.

**Third, it provides a unifying lens through which several existing optimizers can be seen as special cases of MARS.** The paper explicitly shows that:
- **Adan** (Xie et al., 2024) reduces to MARS-approx with $\gamma_t = 1 - \beta_1$ (Section 3.2.1)
- **Lion** (Chen et al., 2023) is equivalent to MARS-Lion with approximate gradient calculation, differing only in whether $\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$ or $\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})$ is used in the correction term (Lemma 3.4 and Equation 3.18–3.19)
- **Muon** (Jordan et al., 2024) corresponds to a rescaled version of MARS-Shampoo with $\gamma_t = 1 - \beta_1$, again differing in the stochasticity of the correction term (Equation 3.24–3.25)

This is significant because it means that several recently proposed optimizers that *appeared* to be novel can be reinterpreted as implementing varying degrees of variance reduction. The paper's framework thus provides not just a new method but a **taxonomy** that explains *why* these methods work and how they relate to each other. The empirical results then demonstrate that the explicit, properly-calibrated variance reduction in MARS outperforms these implicit special cases, validating that the full framework matters rather than just being a post-hoc rationalization.

**Fourth, it grounds its claims in practical LLM training at meaningful scale.** Rather than testing on small convolutional networks and toy datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10 with LeNet or ResNet-32, as was done in prior variance-reduced adaptive methods like Adam+, SuperAdam, and AdaSPIDER), the paper evaluates on GPT-2 models up to 770M parameters trained on the OpenWebText corpus of ~9B tokens, with downstream evaluation on standard LLM benchmarks (HellaSwag, ARC, BoolQ, PIQA, etc.). This scale—while not at the frontier of LLM size—is sufficient to be representative of the optimization challenges that matter in practice. The authors also train a 1.5B parameter GPT-2 XL on the FineWeb-Edu 100B dataset in the appendix, further validating scalability.

The paper thus occupies a bridge position: it connects classical optimization theory (variance reduction, preconditioning, convergence rates) to modern LLM training practice, arguing that the two can and should be integrated, and providing both the conceptual framework and empirical evidence to make that case.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The MARS framework is a recipe for building optimization algorithms that combine two previously separate ideas: (1) using past gradient information to reduce noise in the current gradient estimate (variance reduction), and (2) adjusting the step size per parameter based on historical gradient magnitudes (preconditioning, as AdamW, Lion, and Shampoo do). The problem it solves is that existing adaptive optimizers like AdamW work with noisy gradient estimates, which makes each step less informative and slows down training—MARS replaces those noisy estimates with cleaner, variance-reduced ones while preserving the preconditioning mechanism that makes adaptive methods effective for deep learning in the first place.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has three major components that operate in sequence at each training step:

1. **Gradient Estimator with Adjustable Variance Reduction** — given the current model parameters `$x_t$` and the previous parameters `$x_{t-1}$`, this component computes a raw stochastic gradient on a new data batch `$\xi_t$`, applies a correction term that subtracts out the noise common to the current and previous step, and rescales the correction by a parameter `$\gamma_t$` that controls how much variance reduction to apply. The output is a cleaner gradient estimate `$c_t$`.

2. **Gradient Clipping** — the cleaned estimate `$c_t$` is clipped by norm (if its `$\ell_2$` norm exceeds 1, it is rescaled to have norm exactly 1) to produce `$\tilde{c}_t$`. This prevents any single outlier gradient from destabilising training.

3. **Preconditioned Momentum and Parameter Update** — the clipped estimate `$\tilde{c}_t$` is fed into an exponential moving average (EMA) to produce the first-order momentum `$m_t$`. Simultaneously, the squared clipped estimate `$\tilde{c}_t^2$` is fed into a separate EMA to produce the second-order momentum `$v_t$` (for diagonal methods) or a matrix preconditioner `$H_t$` (for full-matrix methods). Finally, the parameters are updated by taking a step in the direction of `$m_t$`, scaled by the inverse of the preconditioner. The specific form of the preconditioner distinguishes the three MARS variants (AdamW, Lion, Shampoo).

Information flows linearly: model parameters → compute gradient on current batch → subtract noise using previous batch and parameters → scale correction by `$\gamma_t$` → clip → update EMAs → precondition and step.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the core variance-reduced gradient estimator `$c_t$` and the `$\gamma_t$` scaling parameter, since this is the novel mechanism that distinguishes MARS from standard adaptive methods. We will walk through the STORM momentum, show how `$\gamma_t$` generalises it, and explain why the `$\gamma_t$` choice matters both intuitively and via the control variates argument.
- **Second**, the gradient clipping step and why it is applied to `$c_t$` rather than to the final update, since this is a practical design choice with empirical consequences.
- **Third**, the preconditioned update rule and how the first and second moments are computed from the *same* variance-reduced estimate `$\tilde{c}_t$`, which fixes the mismatch in prior work like SuperAdam.
- **Fourth**, each of the three instantiations (MARS-AdamW, MARS-Lion, MARS-Shampoo) in detail, including their specific `$H_t$` definitions, update formulas, and connections to existing optimizers.
- **Fifth**, the approximate version (MARS-approx) that avoids computing two gradients per step, the practical recomputation cost issue, and why the approximate version suffices in practice.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methodology paper** whose core idea is that variance reduction (specifically, a scaled version of the STORM recursive momentum) can be integrated into adaptive gradient methods by (1) computing both the first and second moments from the same variance-reduced gradient estimate to maintain calibration, (2) introducing a tunable scaling parameter `$\gamma_t$` to control the strength of variance reduction rather than committing to full STORM, and (3) applying gradient clipping to the intermediate estimate before it enters the momentum buffers.

#### The Scaled Stochastic Recursive Momentum: Core Gradient Estimator

The paper's foundational building block is the STORM (Stochastic Recursive Momentum) estimator from Cutkosky & Orabona (2019). To understand why MARS's modification matters, we must first understand what STORM does and why.

**The standard stochastic gradient problem.** At step `$t$`, we have current parameters `$x_t$` and sample a random data batch `$\xi_t$`. The standard stochastic gradient is simply `$\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t)$`, which is an unbiased estimator of the true gradient `$\nabla F(x_t)$` but has variance `$\sigma^2$` that does not decrease as training progresses. This means that even when the parameters are near a good solution, individual gradient steps remain noisy.

**The STORM estimator.** STORM constructs a momentum term that recursively reduces variance without requiring periodic full-gradient computations. The standard form (Equation 2.4) is:

$$m_t = \beta_1 m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1) \nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) + \beta_1\big(\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) - \nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)\big)$$

where `$m_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is the variance-reduced momentum (an estimate of `$\nabla F(x_t)$`), `$\beta_1 \in (0,1)$` is the momentum decay parameter, `$\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t)$` is the stochastic gradient at the current parameters on the current data batch, and `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$` is the stochastic gradient at the *previous* parameters evaluated on the *same* data batch `$\xi_t$`.

**What it computes:** the new momentum `$m_t$` is a weighted combination of three terms. The first term `$\beta_1 m_{t-1}$` carries forward the previous momentum estimate. The second term `$(1-\beta_1)\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t)$` incorporates the new gradient information. The third term `$\beta_1(\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) - \nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t))$` is the **gradient correction**—it computes the difference in the stochastic gradient when evaluated at `$x_t$` versus `$x_{t-1}$` using the *same* random data `$\xi_t$`, and adds this difference to the momentum.

**Why this form reduces variance:** the key insight is that `$\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t)$` and `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$` share the same random noise from `$\xi_t$`. By subtracting them, the common noise cancels out, leaving only the difference attributable to the change in parameters from `$x_{t-1}$` to `$x_t$`. When the parameters change slowly (which they do once training stabilises), this difference is small. The effect is that the momentum `$m_t$` tracks the true gradient `$\nabla F(x_t)$` with variance that diminishes as `$\|x_t - x_{t-1}\|^2$` shrinks, rather than being persistently `$\sigma^2$` as in standard SGD.

An alternative rewriting (Equation 2.5) shows this more clearly as an EMA with a gradient correction:

$$m_t = \beta_1 m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\Big[\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) + \frac{\beta_1}{1 - \beta_1}\big(\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) - \nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)\big)\Big]$$

Here the term in brackets is the "corrected gradient" that compensates for the noise in the raw stochastic gradient. The factor `$\frac{\beta_1}{1-\beta_1}$` controls how strongly the correction is applied.

**MARS's modification: the scaling parameter `$\gamma_t$`.** The paper's first key innovation is to introduce a parameter `$\gamma_t$` that adjusts the strength of the gradient correction. Rather than using the full STORM correction, MARS defines a gradient estimator `$c_t$` as:

$$c_t = \nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) + \gamma_t \frac{\beta_1}{1 - \beta_1}\big(\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) - \nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)\big)$$

where `$\gamma_t \in [0, 1]$` is a scaling factor that controls how much variance reduction is applied at step `$t$`, and all other symbols have the same meaning as above.

**What it computes:** `$c_t$` is a gradient estimate with tunable variance. When `$\gamma_t = 1$`, the formula recovers exactly the bracketed term from STORM (full variance reduction). When `$\gamma_t = 0$`, the correction term vanishes and `$c_t$` reduces to the raw stochastic gradient `$\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t)$` (no variance reduction, equivalent to standard AdamW's input). For intermediate values of `$\gamma_t$`, the estimator applies partial variance reduction.

**Why this form—the control variates argument (Section 3.1, "Why `$\gamma_t$` improves convergence"):** The authors ground this design in the classical statistical technique of control variates (Asmussen & Glynn, 2007; Lavenberg et al., 1977). In the standard control variates setting, one has a random variable `$X$` whose expectation `$\mathbb{E}[X]$` is the quantity of interest, and another random variable `$Y$` with known expectation `$\mathbb{E}[Y]$` that is correlated with `$X$`. The estimator `$X - \gamma(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])$` has the same expectation as `$X$` (it is unbiased) but its variance is:

$$\text{Var}(X - \gamma(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])) = \text{Var}(X) - 2\gamma\,\text{Cov}(X,Y) + \gamma^2\,\text{Var}(Y)$$

This is a quadratic in `$\gamma$`. Minimising over `$\gamma$` gives the optimal coefficient:

$$\gamma^* = \frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\text{Var}(Y)}$$

which yields a variance strictly lower than `$\text{Var}(X)$` whenever `$X$` and `$Y$` are correlated.

The paper maps this to the STORM setting. The gradient estimator error `$\varepsilon_t = m_t - \nabla F(x_t)$` can be decomposed into components that correspond to `$X$` (a term involving the raw stochastic gradient) and `$Y$` (a term involving the gradient difference `$\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) - \nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$`). The scaling parameter `$\gamma_t$` plays the role of the control variate coefficient. The optimal `$\gamma_t^*$` is derived in the theoretical analysis (see the discussion around Lemma C.2 in Appendix D.5, specifically Equation D.14):

$$\gamma_{t+1}^* = 1 - \frac{G_{t+1} + \beta_{1,t+1}\big(\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_t\|_2^2 - \|\mathbb{E}\Delta_t\|_2^2\big)}{\beta_{1,t+1}\,\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_t\|_2^2}$$

where `$\Delta_t = \nabla f(x_{t+1}, \xi_{t+1}) - \nabla f(x_t, \xi_{t+1})$` and `$G_{t+1}$` is a cross-covariance term defined in Lemma C.2. This optimal `$\gamma_t^*$` depends on quantities that are not directly observable during training (it involves expectations over the data distribution), so in practice the paper treats `$\gamma_t$` as a tuned hyperparameter, but the analysis shows that a non-trivial `$\gamma_t$` can achieve variance strictly lower than both `$\gamma_t = 0$` (no reduction) and `$\gamma_t = 1$` (full STORM) in some settings.

**Convergence rate implication:** The theoretical analysis in Appendix B.2 proves that with an appropriately chosen schedule for `$\gamma_t$`, MARS achieves an `$O(T^{-1/3})$` convergence rate (Theorem B.5), which improves on the `$O(T^{-1/4})$` rate that AdamW attains (Section 3.1). The term `$M_{t+1}$` in the convergence bound (defined in Lemma C.2, Equation C.2) quantifies the additional variance reduction beyond the baseline, and it is zero when `$\gamma_{t+1} = 1$` but positive for intermediate values—meaning that optimising `$\gamma_t$` can accelerate convergence beyond what full STORM provides.

**Sensitivity to `$\gamma_t$` in practice:** Appendix E.5 (Figure 14) sweeps `$\gamma$` over the set `$\{0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2\}$` on GPT-2 small. The best validation loss is achieved at `$\gamma = 0.025$`, with performance degrading for both smaller and larger values. This confirms that (a) some variance reduction helps (`$\gamma > 0$` beats `$\gamma = 0$`, which is equivalent to AdamW), (b) full variance reduction is not optimal (`$\gamma = 0.2$` underperforms `$\gamma = 0.025$`), and (c) the optimal setting is far from the `$\gamma = 1$` that would recover standard STORM. This empirically validates the control variates argument that the correction strength should be tuned rather than set to its theoretical maximum.

#### Gradient Clipping on the Variance-Reduced Estimate

Once `$c_t$` is computed, MARS applies gradient clipping by norm before the estimate enters the momentum buffers. This is defined in Equation 3.5:

$$\tilde{c}_t = \text{Clip}(c_t, 1) = \begin{cases} \frac{c_t}{\|c_t\|_2} & \text{if } \|c_t\|_2 > 1, \\ c_t & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

where `$\|c_t\|_2$` is the Euclidean (`$\ell_2$`) norm of the gradient estimate vector.

**What it computes:** if the norm of `$c_t$` exceeds 1, the vector is rescaled to have unit norm while preserving its direction. If the norm is already at most 1, `$c_t$` passes through unchanged.

**Why clip at this stage:** the paper contrasts this with the approach in Sophia (Liu et al., 2023), which performs clipping by *value* on the *preconditioned* gradient (after dividing by the second moment). The authors argue that MARS's approach—clipping by norm on the intermediate gradient estimate before preconditioning—follows the "more standard technique of clipping-by-norm" used in general neural network training. This is a practical choice: clipping early prevents outlier gradient values from contaminating both the momentum and the second-moment estimates, which are themselves exponential moving averages that would retain the influence of outliers for many steps. The clipping threshold of 1.0 is set as a global hyperparameter across all experiments (Table 11).

**Why this matters for variance reduction:** variance-reduced estimators like `$c_t$` rely on the gradient difference `$\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) - \nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$` being representative of the true change in the gradient. If either of these two gradient evaluations is an outlier (e.g., due to particularly difficult or unrepresentative training examples), the difference can be very large, injecting noise rather than cancelling it. Clipping `$c_t$` bounds the damage from such outliers and prevents them from destabilising the recursive momentum.

#### First-Order and Second-Order Momentum from the Same Estimate

After clipping, MARS computes the first-order momentum `$m_t$` as the exponential moving average (EMA) of the clipped estimates:

$$m_t = \beta_1 m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\tilde{c}_t$$

where `$\beta_1$` is the first-order momentum decay parameter (set to 0.95 for MARS-AdamW in practice, versus 0.9 for standard AdamW—Table 12), and `$m_0 = 0$` (initialised to zero).

**What it computes:** this is a standard EMA with decay `$\beta_1$`, producing a smoothed version of the variance-reduced gradient estimates. The higher `$\beta_1$` (0.95 vs. AdamW's 0.9) means the momentum retains more history, which is consistent with the idea that variance-reduced estimates are individually more reliable and thus deserve a longer memory.

**The critical design choice—matched second moment:** For diagonal preconditioning methods (MARS-AdamW), the second-order momentum `$v_t$` is computed as:

$$v_t = \beta_2 v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\tilde{c}_t^2$$

where `$\beta_2$` is the second-order momentum decay parameter (set to 0.99 for MARS-AdamW, Table 12), `$\tilde{c}_t^2$` denotes element-wise squaring of the clipped variance-reduced estimate, and `$v_0 = 0$`.

**Why this is not the AdamW default and why it matters:** In standard AdamW, `$v_t$` is the EMA of the squared *raw* stochastic gradient `$\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t)^2$`. In MARS, `$v_t$` is the EMA of the squared *variance-reduced* estimate `$\tilde{c}_t^2$`. This ensures that the per-parameter step sizes (which are inversely proportional to `$\sqrt{v_t}$`) are calibrated to the actual magnitude of the updates being applied. If `$m_t$` has reduced variance (because it is computed from `$\tilde{c}_t$` which includes the gradient correction) but `$v_t$` were still based on the high-variance raw gradient, the preconditioner would systematically underestimate the appropriate step size for some coordinates and overestimate it for others. Fixing this mismatch is, in the authors' view, the key reason why SuperAdam (which used raw-gradient-based `$v_t$`) underperformed.

The paper explicitly states this in Remark 3.2: "one key difference is that our algorithm defines the second-order momentum `$v_t$` as the exponential moving average of the square norm of `$c_t$` rather than the square norm of the stochastic gradient. This new definition of second-order momentum is crucial for accommodating the right scale of updates on a coordinate-wise basis."

#### The Preconditioned Update Rule

The general update rule for MARS is framed in the language of Online Mirror Descent (OMD). Given the momentum `$m_t$` and a preconditioning matrix `$H_t$`, the next parameters are:

$$x_{t+1} = \arg\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^d} \left\{ \eta_t \langle m_t, x \rangle + \frac{1}{2} \|x - x_t\|_{H_t}^2 \right\}$$

where `$\eta_t > 0$` is the base learning rate (step size), `$\langle m_t, x \rangle$` is the inner product providing a linear approximation to the loss, and `$\|x - x_t\|_{H_t}^2 = (x - x_t)^\top H_t (x - x_t)$` is a Mahalanobis distance that penalises moving far from `$x_t$` in directions where `$H_t$` is large.

**What it computes:** this minimisation balances two competing objectives—moving in the negative gradient direction `$-m_t$` (the first term) and staying close to the current parameters (the second term). The preconditioner `$H_t$` warps the proximity penalty: directions with large `$H_t$` entries are heavily penalised (resulting in small steps), while directions with small `$H_t$` entries are lightly penalised (allowing larger steps). Solving this minimisation yields the closed-form update:

$$x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta_t H_t^{-1} m_t$$

When `$H_t$` is diagonal (as in AdamW-style methods), the inversion is element-wise, giving per-parameter adaptive learning rates.

**Why OMD framing:** this formalism unifies first-order methods (where `$H_t = I$`, the identity, giving standard SGD) and second-order methods (where `$H_t = \nabla^2 F(x_t)$`, the Hessian, giving Newton's method) under a single update rule. The paper uses it to show that MARS can accommodate any choice of preconditioner, from diagonal EMAs to full Kronecker-product approximations, making the framework general rather than tied to a specific design.

#### Instantiation 1: MARS-AdamW

MARS-AdamW (Algorithm 2) adapts the AdamW preconditioner to work with variance-reduced gradient estimates.

**The AdamW preconditioner (Equation 3.9):** In standard AdamW, the preconditioning matrix is diagonal with entries:

$$H_t = \sqrt{\text{diag}\left(v_t\right)} \cdot \frac{1 - \beta_1^t}{\sqrt{1 - \beta_2^t}}$$

where `$\text{diag}(v_t)$` extracts the diagonal of the second moment vector, the square root is applied element-wise, and the bias correction factors `$(1 - \beta_1^t)$` and `$\sqrt{1 - \beta_2^t}$` account for the fact that `$m_t$` and `$v_t$` are initialised at zero and thus biased toward zero in early steps.

**What it computes:** each parameter's step is scaled inversely by the root-mean-square of the historical (variance-reduced) gradient estimates for that parameter. Parameters that have historically received large gradients get small steps; parameters that have received small gradients get large steps. The bias correction ensures that the effective learning rate is not artificially small at the beginning of training.

**MARS-AdamW full update (Algorithm 2):**
1. Compute `$c_t$` using Equation 3.10 (the scaled variance-reduced estimator).
2. Clip `$c_t$` to obtain `$\tilde{c}_t$` (line 5).
3. Update first moment: `$m_t = \beta_1 m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\tilde{c}_t$` (line 6).
4. Update second moment: `$v_t = \beta_2 v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\tilde{c}_t^2$` (line 7).
5. Apply bias correction: `$\hat{m}_t = m_t / (1 - \beta_1^t)$`, `$\hat{v}_t = v_t / (1 - \beta_2^t)$` (line 8).
6. Update parameters with decoupled weight decay: `$x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta_t \left(\frac{\hat{m}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \epsilon} + \lambda x_t\right)$` (line 9).

where `$\epsilon$` is a small constant for numerical stability (typically `$10^{-8}$`), and `$\lambda$` is the weight decay coefficient.

**The decoupled weight decay:** following Loshchilov & Hutter (2019), the `$\ell_2$` regularisation is applied as a separate additive term `$-\eta_t \lambda x_t$` rather than being folded into the gradient. This decoupling prevents the adaptive learning rates from interfering with the regularisation strength.

**Hyperparameters used in practice (Table 12):** For GPT-2 large, MARS-AdamW uses `$\beta_1 = 0.95$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.99$`, `$\gamma_t = 0.025$` (constant), maximum learning rate `$2 \times 10^{-3}$`, minimum learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-5}$`, weight decay searched over `$\{10^{-1}, 10^{-2}, 10^{-3}\}$`, and a cosine learning rate schedule with 2000 warmup steps over 100,000 total steps.

**Why `$\beta_1 = 0.95$` rather than AdamW's default `$0.9$`:** a higher `$\beta_1$` means the momentum retains gradient information over longer timescales. Since variance-reduced estimates are individually less noisy, the algorithm can "trust" older gradient information more, justifying a longer memory. Empirically, the authors found this setting by grid search.

**Convergence guarantee (Theorem B.6):** Under standard assumptions (bounded variance, L-smoothness, and a lower bound on the preconditioner eigenvalues), with a carefully chosen decreasing schedule for `$\eta_t$` and `$\beta_{1,t}$`, MARS-AdamW achieves `$O(\log(T)/T^{1/3})$` convergence of the gradient norm, versus `$O(T^{-1/4})$` for standard AdamW. The analysis requires time-varying `$\beta_{1,t}$` and `$\beta_{2,t}$` that decrease toward 1 as training progresses, though in practice constants are used.

#### Instantiation 2: MARS-Lion

MARS-Lion (Algorithm 3) adapts the Lion optimizer's sign-based preconditioning to incorporate variance reduction.

**The Lion preconditioner (Equation 3.15):** Lion replaces AdamW's EMA of squared gradients with a sign operation. The effective preconditioner is:

$$H_t = \sqrt{\text{diag}(m_t^2)}$$

which, when inverted and multiplied by `$m_t$`, yields the element-wise sign of `$m_t$`:

$$H_t^{-1}m_t = \text{sign}(m_t)$$

where `$\text{sign}(m_t)_i$` is `$+1$`, `$-1$`, or `$0$` depending on whether the `$i$`-th component of `$m_t$` is positive, negative, or zero.

**What it computes:** every parameter receives a step of the same magnitude `$\eta_t$` in the direction of the sign of its momentum. There is no per-parameter scaling based on gradient history—the adaptive element is purely directional. This makes Lion memory-efficient (no `$v_t$` buffer) and was discovered through symbolic program search by Chen et al. (2023).

**MARS-Lion full update (Algorithm 3):**
1. Compute `$c_t$` using the same scaled variance-reduced estimator as MARS-AdamW.
2. Clip `$c_t$` to obtain `$\tilde{c}_t$`.
3. Update momentum: `$m_t = \beta_1 m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\tilde{c}_t$`.
4. Update parameters: `$x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta_t(\text{sign}(m_t) + \lambda x_t)$`.

No bias correction is applied (Lion does not use one), and no second-moment buffer is maintained.

**Connection to standard Lion (Lemma 3.4 and Equations 3.18–3.19):** The paper proves that Lion's momentum update can be rewritten as a single recurrence that includes a gradient correction term identical in structure to MARS's, except that Lion uses `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})$` (the gradient from the *previous step's data batch*) while MARS uses `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$` (the gradient from the *current step's data batch* evaluated at the previous parameters). Specifically, Lion's momentum (Equations 3.13–3.14) is equivalent to:

$$m_t = \beta_2 m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) + (\beta_2 - \beta_1)\big(\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) - \nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})\big)$$

while MARS-Lion with `$\beta_1^{\text{MARS}} = \beta_2$`, `$\beta_2^{\text{MARS}} = \beta_1$`, and `$\gamma_t = (\beta_2 - \beta_1)/\beta_2$` gives:

$$m_t = \beta_2 m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) + (\beta_2 - \beta_1)\big(\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) - \nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)\big)$$

The only difference is the data batch used for the `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \cdot)$` term. The authors note that "in practice, we observe little difference between using `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})$`... and its approximation `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$`." This means Lion can be viewed as an implicit, approximate variance-reduced method—the `$(\beta_2 - \beta_1)$` coefficient determines the strength of the implicit correction, analogous to `$\gamma_t$` in MARS.

**Why MARS-Lion outperforms Lion:** the explicit variance reduction in MARS-Lion uses the same data batch `$\xi_t$` for both gradient evaluations, which properly cancels the common noise. Lion's implicit correction uses different batches (`$\xi_t$` and `$\xi_{t-1}$`), which partially cancels noise but less effectively. Additionally, MARS provides a tunable `$\gamma_t$` to optimise the correction strength rather than having it be determined by the difference of two momentum parameters `$\beta_2 - \beta_1$`.

#### Instantiation 3: MARS-Shampoo

MARS-Shampoo (Algorithm 4) adapts Shampoo's matrix eigenspace preconditioning to incorporate variance reduction.

**The Shampoo preconditioner:** For a weight matrix `$x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$` (e.g., a linear layer), Shampoo maintains two preconditioning matrices—a left preconditioner `$L_t \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$` and a right preconditioner `$R_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$`—computed from the gradient matrix `$G_t = \nabla f(x_t, \xi_t)$`:

$$L_t = L_{t-1} + G_t G_t^\top, \quad R_t = R_{t-1} + G_t^\top G_t$$

The parameter update is:

$$x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta_t L_t^{-1/4} G_t R_t^{-1/4}$$

**Geometric interpretation:** this is equivalent to performing SVD on the gradient matrix `$G_t = U_t \Sigma_t V_t^\top$` and updating the parameters by `$-\eta_t U_t V_t^\top$`—i.e., taking a step in the direction that ignores the singular values entirely, moving purely based on the left and right singular vectors (the eigenspace of the gradient). The `$-1/4$` exponent comes from the Kronecker-product structure of the full preconditioner.

**MARS-Shampoo full update (Algorithm 4):**
1. Compute `$c_t$` as the variance-reduced gradient estimator (reshaped to matrix form if the parameter is a matrix).
2. Update momentum: `$m_t = \beta_1 m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)c_t$` (no gradient clipping is explicitly listed in Algorithm 4, though the text implies it could be included).
3. Compute the SVD of `$m_t$`: `$U_t, \Sigma_t, V_t = \text{SVD}(m_t)$`.
4. Update parameters: `$x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta_t(U_t V_t^\top + \lambda x_t)$`.

The paper notes that the full SVD can be computationally expensive, and that several approximation methods are compatible: sketching (Martinsson & Tropp, 2020), Newton iteration (Lakić, 1998; Higham, 2008; Anil et al., 2020), and Newton-Schulz iteration (Schulz, 1933; Higham, 2008). The choice of SVD solver is left as an implementation detail to "best fit specific computational needs."

**Connection to Muon (Jordan et al., 2024):** The paper shows (Equations 3.22–3.25) that Muon's update is equivalent to a rescaled version of MARS-Shampoo with `$\beta_1 = \mu$` and `$\gamma_t = 1 - \beta_1$`, again differing in whether `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$` or `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})$` is used. Specifically, Muon uses an approximate gradient correction with different batch indices, while MARS-Shampoo uses the same batch for cancellation. MARS-Shampoo also benefits from the tunable `$\gamma_t$` parameter rather than having the correction strength fixed by `$1 - \beta_1$`.

---

#### The Approximate Version: MARS-approx

A practical computational issue arises from the STORM formulation: computing `$c_t$` requires evaluating the stochastic gradient twice—once at `$x_t$` with data `$\xi_t$` (to get `$\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t)$`) and once at `$x_{t-1}$` with the *same* data `$\xi_t$` (to get `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$`). This means either storing `$\xi_t$` and doing a second forward-backward pass, or recomputing the previous step's gradient with the current batch—both of which approximately double the per-iteration computation.

**The approximation (Remark 3.3):** the paper proposes to replace `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$` with `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})$`—the gradient that was *already computed* at the previous step on the previous batch. The approximate gradient estimator becomes:

$$c_t \approx \nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) + \gamma_t \frac{\beta_1}{1 - \beta_1}\big(\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) - \nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})\big)$$

This requires only one gradient computation per step (the standard forward-backward pass) because `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})$` is retrieved from a stored buffer rather than recomputed. The approximate version is called MARS-approx, and the paper recommends it as the default for practical applications.

**Why the approximation works:** although the rigorous variance reduction property relies on using the same `$\xi_t$` for cancellation, the paper notes that `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$` and `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})$` are both unbiased estimators of `$\nabla F(x_{t-1})$` and are independent of `$\xi_t$`. The approximate correction still provides partial noise cancellation, just with slightly higher residual variance than the exact version. Empirically, Appendix E.2 (Figures 4 and 5) shows that MARS-exact modestly outperforms MARS-approx on GPT-2 small and medium—the training and validation loss curves are close, with the exact version achieving marginally lower loss. The paper concludes that "MARS provides only marginal improvements over MARS-approx in practice. Thus, we recommend using MARS-approx for practical applications."

**Connection to Adan (Xie et al., 2024):** when `$\beta_2 = \beta_1$` in Adan's formulation, the Adan momentum reduces exactly to the MARS-approx momentum with `$\gamma_t = 1 - \beta_1$`. Specifically:

$$m_t = \beta_1 m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\big[\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) + \beta_1(\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) - \nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1}))\big]$$

This means Adan—which was motivated by Nesterov's acceleration (see Lemma 1 in Xie et al., 2024)—can be reinterpreted as an approximate variance-reduced method within the MARS framework. The paper notes, however, that "although motivated by the Nesterov's momentum, Adan's momentum updates cannot recover Nesterov's momentum unless `$\beta_1 = \beta_2$`"—suggesting that the variance reduction interpretation may be more fundamental than the Nesterov interpretation for explaining Adan's empirical performance.

---

#### The Surprising Relationship: MARS as a Unifying Framework

A striking result from the paper's analysis is how many recent optimizers emerge as special cases of MARS under specific parameter choices:

| Optimizer | Relationship to MARS |
|-----------|---------------------|
| **AdamW** | MARS-AdamW with `$\gamma_t = 0$` (no variance reduction; `$c_t$` reduces to raw gradient) |
| **Adan** | MARS-approx with `$\gamma_t = 1 - \beta_1$` (implicit, approximate variance reduction) |
| **Lion** | MARS-Lion with approximate gradient calculation and `$\gamma_t = (\beta_2 - \beta_1)/\beta_2$` |
| **Muon** | Rescaled MARS-Shampoo with `$\gamma_t = 1 - \beta_1$` and approximate gradient calculation |

This unifying perspective is not merely taxonomic. It reveals that several recently proposed optimizers that appeared to be conceptually distinct—Nesterov acceleration for Adan, symbolic search for Lion, eigenspace preconditioning for Muon—all derive part of their benefit from implicit variance reduction. The paper's empirical results show that making this variance reduction *explicit* and *tunable* (via `$\gamma_t$`) improves performance beyond any of the implicit formulations. This is the core empirical claim: variance reduction is the active ingredient, and MARS provides the cleanest implementation of it.

---

#### Wall-Clock Time Considerations

The paper reports that MARS-AdamW and MARS-Lion "have a slightly higher per-iteration cost compared to AdamW but is much faster than Muon" (Section 4.2). This is visible in the wall-clock time comparisons in Figure 1(c) for GPT-2 large, where MARS-AdamW and MARS-Lion reach a given validation loss in less total time than AdamW, Lion, and especially Muon. The additional per-iteration cost comes from the extra gradient bookkeeping (storing `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})$` for the approximate version or computing an additional backward pass for the exact version), but this is more than compensated for by the reduction in the number of iterations needed to reach a target loss.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Variance Reduction as a *Missing Active Ingredient* in Modern Optimizers, Not a Separate Family

The paper's most distinctive conceptual move is reframing variance reduction from a competing paradigm to a **component that can be retrofitted into existing adaptive optimizers**. Prior to MARS, the optimization landscape for deep learning was implicitly partitioned: there were "variance reduction methods" (SVRG, SARAH, SPIDER, STORM) and there were "adaptive gradient methods" (Adam, Lion, Shampoo), and the two categories rarely intersected productively. When they did intersect—as in SuperAdam (Huang et al., 2021) or Adam+ (Liu et al., 2020)—the results were underwhelming, confined to small-scale tasks like MNIST or CIFAR-10. The field's implicit conclusion, reinforced by Defazio & Bottou (2019)'s diagnosis of why variance reduction fails in deep learning, was that these two approaches are fundamentally incompatible: variance reduction needs finite-sum structure and clean gradient signals, while adaptive methods need to handle the messiness of real neural network training.

MARS overturns this framing entirely. Rather than asking "can a variance-reduced optimizer beat Adam?," it asks **"can Adam be made variance-reduced?"** This is not a semantic trick. It means that the preconditioning machinery of Adam—the per-parameter adaptive learning rates, the bias correction, the decoupled weight decay—is preserved intact, and variance reduction is injected at the gradient estimation level. The fact that MARS-AdamW *is* AdamW when `$\gamma_t = 0$` is the cleanest possible demonstration: variance reduction is a dial, not a switch. You can turn it from zero (standard AdamW) to one (full STORM-style correction) or anywhere in between, and the algorithm remains well-defined.

This reframing is what enables the paper to reveal that **several recent optimizers that appeared conceptually novel are actually implicit, approximate, or constrained versions of variance-reduced Adam**. Adan (motivated by Nesterov acceleration), Lion (discovered by symbolic program search), and Muon (motivated by eigenspace preconditioning) all turn out to implement gradient correction terms of exactly the form that STORM's variance reduction prescribes—but with suboptimal parameterization (the correction strength is tied to momentum parameters rather than independently tuned) and with approximate gradient differencing (using different data batches rather than the same batch for noise cancellation). This is a genuine scientific contribution: it explains *why* these methods work, not just that they work. The symbolic search that discovered Lion did not "know" it was discovering variance reduction—it found a formula that, in retrospect, approximates a scaled STORM update. MARS provides the conceptual vocabulary to understand this.

The practical implication is significant. Rather than treating each new optimizer as a black-box competitor to be benchmarked against Adam, the MARS framework suggests that **variance reduction is the key discriminant separating the merely good from the excellent**, and that future optimizer design should focus on how to best incorporate and tune variance reduction, rather than on alternative preconditioner designs or momentum schedules. The evidence for this claim is the consistent outperformance of MARS across three different preconditioner types (AdamW, Lion, Shampoo) over their non-variance-reduced counterparts. Figure 1(b) shows MARS-AdamW reaching a validation loss on GPT-2 large that AdamW never achieves within 50B tokens, and the gap widens with model scale—precisely what one expects if variance reduction addresses a fundamental bottleneck (gradient noise) rather than a superficial one.

---

### Innovation 2: The `$\gamma_t$` Scaling Parameter as a Tunable Bridge Between SGD and Full Variance Reduction

The introduction of a tunable scaling parameter `$\gamma_t$` on the gradient correction term is, at first glance, a minor modification to STORM. But it encodes a deep insight: **the optimal amount of variance reduction is not "maximum," and the correct setting depends on the optimization dynamics at a given training stage**. Prior variance reduction work—STORM, SPIDER, SVRG—treated the correction term as binary: either you apply variance reduction (with strength determined by fixed algorithm parameters like momentum or epoch length) or you don't. The control variates argument that the paper invokes (Section 3.1) shows why this is suboptimal: the variance of the corrected estimator is a quadratic function of the correction strength, and the minimum is generally not at the extremes.

The empirical validation is striking. Figure 14 (Appendix E.5) shows that on GPT-2 small, `$\gamma = 0.025$` achieves the best validation loss, with both smaller values (`$\gamma = 0.0001$`, approaching standard AdamW) and larger values (`$\gamma = 0.2$`, approaching full STORM) underperforming. The degradation at `$\gamma = 0.2$` is substantial—validation loss is visibly worse. This means that **full STORM is too aggressive for LLM training**, likely because the gradient correction term amplifies noise rather than cancelling it when parameter changes between steps are large (as they are early in training or during loss spikes). The optimal `$\gamma_t$` provides just enough correction to reduce variance without introducing instability from the difference term.

This has implications beyond MARS. It suggests that prior failures of variance reduction in deep learning (beyond the finite-sum issue) may have been partly due to using *too much* variance reduction—applying the full STORM or SVRG correction rather than a partial, tuned amount. SuperAdam, which used full STORM with `$\gamma_t$` implicitly at its maximum, may have suffered from exactly this issue. The paper provides theoretical backing for this in Lemma C.2, where the variance of the gradient estimator is shown to be a function of `$\gamma_t$` with a well-defined minimum that depends on cross-covariance terms between the gradient estimate and the correction term. The optimal `$\gamma_t^*$` derived in Equation D.14 is not generally 1.

This insight also opens the door to **dynamic `$\gamma_t$` schedules** that adapt the correction strength as training progresses. The paper uses constant `$\gamma_t = 0.025$` in all experiments, but the theoretical analysis supports time-varying schedules, and it is plausible that early training (high parameter velocity, large gradient differences) benefits from weaker correction while late training (small parameter changes, stable gradients) benefits from stronger correction. This is flagged as a direction for future work but follows directly from the control variates framing.

---

### Innovation 3: The Matched First- and Second-Moment Design as a Principled Fix for Variance-Reduced Preconditioning

The paper's diagnosis of why SuperAdam underperformed—"a significant mismatch between the first-order and second-order momentum" (Remark 3.1)—is more than a bug fix. It identifies a **general design principle**: when you change the statistical properties of the gradient estimator (by reducing its variance), you must also change the preconditioner that scales the update accordingly. Failing to do so creates a scale mismatch that can negate the benefits of variance reduction.

This is non-obvious because the default design pattern in adaptive methods is to compute `$v_t$` (or its equivalent) from the raw stochastic gradient, independent of any processing applied to produce `$m_t$`. Adam, AdamW, Lion, and Shampoo all follow this pattern: `$m_t$` and `$v_t$` are both functions of `$\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t)$`, but they are computed separately. If you modify `$m_t$` by incorporating a correction term (as in variance reduction), but leave `$v_t$` unchanged (still based on the raw gradient), the per-parameter step sizes become miscalibrated: `$v_t$` reflects the variance of the raw gradient, but `$m_t$` has lower variance, so the preconditioner systematically over-regularizes directions where variance reduction was most effective.

MARS fixes this by computing both `$m_t$` and `$v_t$` from the same variance-reduced estimate `$\tilde{c}_t$`. This is a clean, principled solution, but it is not the only possible one. An alternative would be to compute `$v_t$` from the raw gradient but apply a correction factor based on the estimated variance reduction ratio. MARS's approach is simpler: feed the same cleaned signal into both buffers.

The significance extends beyond MARS. It establishes that **variance reduction and preconditioning are not independent operations** that can be composed naively. The preconditioner's job is to estimate the scale of the update signal, and if that signal's statistical properties change, the preconditioner must track those changes. This principle applies to any future method that modifies the gradient estimator—quantization, sparsification, or other compression techniques—and suggests that the second-moment estimate should always be computed from the actual signal that drives the update, not from an unmodified proxy.

The empirical importance of this design choice is indirect but supported by the paper's comparison with SuperAdam, which appears only in the related work discussion rather than in head-to-head experiments (SuperAdam is not in the GPT-2 training plots). The paper's argument is that the matched design is *necessary* for variance reduction to work with preconditioning, and the strong empirical results of MARS versus its unmatched implicit counterparts (Lion, Adan) are consistent with this claim.

---

### Innovation 4: The Training-Inference Tradeoff Reframed as an Optimization-Level Efficiency Gain

While the paper's primary contribution is algorithmic, its results carry a significant **economic and environmental implication** that the authors explicitly flag in their Impact Statement: "our method can greatly lower the cost for pre-training language models on limited training corpora and more resource-constrained devices" and "improvement in efficiency typically correlates with reduced energy consumption." The specific numbers—MARS requiring 28B tokens to reach the validation loss that AdamW achieves at 50B tokens on GPT-2 large, a ~1.8× reduction—translate directly to reduced GPU-hours, lower carbon footprint, and the ability to train better models under fixed compute budgets.

This is not a new optimizer that achieves a marginally better final loss at the same cost; it is an optimizer that achieves the same loss in substantially fewer steps. For large-scale training, where the cost scales linearly with the number of steps, a 1.8× reduction in required tokens is effectively a 1.8× reduction in training cost. The paper does not frame this as theoretical compute-optimality (unlike the scaling laws literature), but the practical implication is similar: for a fixed training budget, MARS produces a better-trained model; for a fixed target quality, MARS costs less to train.

The wall-clock time results (Figure 1(c) and Figures 2-3(c)) add nuance: MARS has a slightly higher per-iteration cost than AdamW, so the end-to-end speedup is somewhat less than the token-efficiency ratio would suggest. However, Figure 1(c) clearly shows that MARS-AdamW reaches any given validation loss in less total wall-clock time than AdamW, Lion, and especially Muon (whose SVD operations impose substantial per-iteration overhead). The practical takeaway is that MARS delivers real speedups in both token count and clock time, not just asymptotic convergence guarantees.

This efficiency gain is structurally different from other recent improvements in LLM training efficiency (e.g., model parallelism, mixture-of-experts, quantization-aware training) because it operates at the optimization level—it changes how the model learns, not how the compute is distributed or how the model is represented. This means it is **orthogonal and composable** with other efficiency techniques: one could train a MoE model with data parallelism using MARS as the optimizer, compounding the gains.

The paper's additional experiments in Appendix E.6.2 with the WSD (Warmup-Stable-Decay) scheduler—showing that MARS continues to improve with extended constant-learning-rate training beyond the point where AdamW plateaus—suggest that MARS may also enable **longer productive training**, extracting more value from additional data rather than requiring aggressive learning rate decay to converge. This has implications for continual pretraining and data-efficient fine-tuning scenarios.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses the OpenWebText corpus (Gokaslan et al., 2019) for GPT-2 pretraining experiments. The training and validation sets contain approximately 9 billion and 4.4 million tokens, respectively, all preprocessed using the GPT-2 tokenizer. For supplementary experiments in Appendix E.3, the paper uses FineWeb-Edu 100B (Lozhkov et al., 2024), a filtered subset of approximately 100B tokens from educational web pages, with roughly 0.1B tokens held out for validation. For computer vision experiments in Appendix E.4, CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 (Krizhevsky et al., 2009) are used.

- **Base model(s).** The primary experiments use the GPT-2 model series (Radford et al., 2019) at three scales: small (125M parameters, 12 layers, 12 heads, embedding dimension 768), medium (355M parameters, 24 layers, 16 heads, embedding dimension 1024), and large (770M parameters, 36 layers, 20 heads, embedding dimension 1280). Additionally, GPT-2 XL (1.5B parameters, 48 layers, 25 heads, embedding dimension 1600) is used for FineWeb-Edu experiments (Appendix E.3). For computer vision, ResNet-18 (He et al., 2016) on CIFAR-10/100 is used. The authors argue these model scales are "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" and cover a range where optimization differences are meaningful—large enough to be practically relevant but small enough for controlled experiments.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **validation cross-entropy loss**, computed on the 4.4M-token held-out set from OpenWebText. Training loss is also reported (smoothed via exponential moving average for plotting clarity). For downstream evaluation, the paper reports **0-shot and 5-shot accuracy** on standard LLM benchmarks using the lm-evaluation-harness (Gao et al., 2024): ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge (Yadav et al., 2019), BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2020), MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021), and SciQ. Average accuracy across all benchmarks is reported as an aggregate metric. For computer vision, test loss and test accuracy are reported.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against:
  - **AdamW** (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019): the predominant optimizer for LLM pretraining, with decoupled weight decay.
  - **Lion** (Chen et al., 2023): the sign-based optimizer discovered through symbolic program search.
  - **Muon** (Jordan et al., 2024): a recently proposed optimizer using Newton-Schulz iteration for eigenspace preconditioning on matrix-shaped parameters.
  
  These three baselines are chosen because they represent three distinct preconditioning strategies: diagonal EMA of squared gradients (AdamW), sign-based updates (Lion), and full eigenspace preconditioning (Muon). For computer vision experiments (Appendix E.4), **Shampoo** (Gupta et al., 2018, using the Distributed Shampoo implementation from Shi et al., 2023) is added as a baseline for the Shampoo-series comparison.

- **Hyperparameter tuning.** For each optimizer, the paper performs a grid search over learning rates in `{1e-4, 1.5e-4, 3e-4, 6e-4, 1e-3, 1.5e-3, 3e-3, 6e-3}` and weight decay coefficients in `{1e-1, 1e-2, 1e-3}` (Section F). For computer vision, learning rates are searched over `{10^{-5}, ..., 10^{0}}` and weight decay over `{0, ..., 1.0}`, with AdamW and Muon's `\beta_2` searched over `{0.99, 0.999}`, Lion's `\beta_2` over `{0.99, 0.999}`, and Shampoo's `(\beta_1, \beta_2)` over `{0.9, 0.95} \times {0.95, 0.99, 0.999}`. MARS models fix `(\beta_1, \beta_2) = (0.95, 0.99)` and `\gamma = 0.025`.

- **Compute accounting / generation budget.** The paper does not use a "generation budget" concept (this is not a test-time compute paper). Instead, all optimizers are compared across the same number of **training steps** (100,000 steps for all GPT-2 experiments) and the same **total training tokens** (since batch size is fixed at 480 sequences of 1024 tokens, each step processes 491,520 tokens, for a total of approximately 49B tokens over 100,000 steps). Wall-clock time comparisons are also reported, measured on 16× NVIDIA A100 GPUs (GPT-2 small), 32× NVIDIA A100 GPUs (GPT-2 medium), and 32× NVIDIA H100 GPUs (GPT-2 large). Training hyperparameters—including learning rate schedules, warmup steps, gradient clipping threshold, and dropout—are listed in Tables 11 and 12 and applied uniformly across all optimizers.

- **Statistical protocol.** The paper does not report confidence intervals, error bars, or multiple random seeds for the main GPT-2 training runs. A single training run per (optimizer, model size, learning rate) combination is reported, with the best learning rate per optimizer selected from the grid search. For the downstream evaluation tasks (Tables 1–6), accuracy is reported as a point estimate without variance. The computer vision experiments (Figures 8–13) report per-epoch test metrics for single training runs. The lack of multiple seeds or statistical testing is a notable methodological limitation.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### GPT-2 Large (770M) on OpenWebText: Headline Comparison

Figure 1 and Table 1 present the core results at the largest tested scale. The key headline numbers are:

- **Token efficiency:** AdamW requires 50B tokens to reach a validation loss of 2.58 on GPT-2 large, whereas MARS-AdamW reaches the same validation loss at approximately 28B tokens—roughly a 1.8× reduction in training tokens required. MARS-AdamW achieves a final validation loss of 2.511 after 50B tokens, compared to 2.568 for AdamW, 2.565 for Lion, and 2.606 for Muon. MARS-Lion achieves a final validation loss of 2.534.

- **Training loss dynamics (Figure 1a):** MARS-AdamW and MARS-Lion both exhibit steeper initial decay than all baselines, and the gap is sustained throughout training. By 50B tokens, MARS-AdamW achieves a training loss of approximately 2.48 versus approximately 2.55 for AdamW, approximately 2.53 for Lion, and approximately 2.58 for Muon. The performance ordering—MARS-AdamW best, followed by MARS-Lion, then Lion, then AdamW, then Muon—holds from roughly 5B tokens onward.

- **Validation loss dynamics (Figure 1b):** The validation curves mirror the training curves. MARS-AdamW consistently achieves the lowest validation loss at every token count beyond the first few billion. At 50B tokens, MARS-AdamW reaches 2.511, MARS-Lion reaches 2.534, Lion reaches 2.565, AdamW reaches 2.568, and Muon reaches 2.606. The gap between MARS-AdamW and AdamW grows from approximately 0.03 at 10B tokens to approximately 0.06 at 50B tokens, suggesting that the benefit of variance reduction compounds with longer training.

- **Wall-clock time (Figure 1c):** MARS-AdamW and MARS-Lion reach any given validation loss in less total wall-clock time than all baselines despite having slightly higher per-iteration cost. At the 70,000-second mark (approximately 19.4 hours), MARS-AdamW achieves a validation loss of roughly 2.52, while AdamW and Lion are at roughly 2.57–2.58, and Muon is above 2.60. The advantage over Muon is particularly pronounced due to Muon's expensive per-iteration SVD operations.

- **Downstream performance (Table 1, 5-shot):** The GPT-2 large model trained with MARS-AdamW achieves the highest average accuracy (50.15%) across nine benchmarks, compared to 48.64% for AdamW, 48.61% for Lion, and 47.31% for Muon. The largest gains are on BoolQ (62.78% vs. AdamW's 56.33%, a +6.45 percentage point improvement) and HellaSwag (45.66% vs. AdamW's 42.55%, a +3.11 percentage point improvement). MARS-Lion achieves the second-highest average at 49.80%. On the 0-shot evaluation (Table 4), MARS-AdamW achieves 48.27% average vs. AdamW's 46.78%, with the largest gap on HellaSwag (44.64% vs. 41.70%).

#### GPT-2 Medium (355M) on OpenWebText

Figure 3 (Appendix) presents the medium-scale results. The pattern is consistent with, but less pronounced than, the large-scale results:

- At 50B tokens, MARS-AdamW achieves a training loss of approximately 2.65 and a validation loss of approximately 2.72, compared to AdamW at approximately 2.69 and 2.76, respectively. MARS-Lion and Lion are intermediate between MARS-AdamW and AdamW. Muon underperforms AdamW.

- The 0-shot evaluation (Table 3) shows MARS-AdamW achieving the highest average accuracy (45.41%), with Lion second (45.60%), both modestly ahead of AdamW (44.33%) and Muon (44.33%). MARS-Lion achieves 45.13%. The 5-shot results (Table 6) show a similar ordering: Lion at 47.22% (marginally ahead of MARS-AdamW at 47.37%, within plausible noise), AdamW at 45.57%, Muon at 45.78%.

- The performance gap between MARS and AdamW is smaller at the medium scale (approximately 0.04 validation loss difference at 50B tokens) than at the large scale (approximately 0.06), supporting the paper's claim that "the performance gap becomes more pronounced with increasing model size" (Section 4.2).

#### GPT-2 Small (125M) on OpenWebText

Figure 2 (Appendix) presents the small-scale results. At 50B tokens:

- Training loss: MARS-AdamW and MARS-Lion achieve approximately 2.85, versus AdamW at 2.86, Lion at 2.87, Muon at 2.88. The absolute gaps are small.
- Validation loss: MARS-AdamW achieves approximately 2.87, MARS-Lion approximately 2.88, AdamW approximately 2.89, Lion approximately 2.90, Muon approximately 2.91.
- Wall-clock time (Figure 2c): MARS-AdamW and MARS-Lion maintain a small but consistent advantage, reaching a validation loss of 2.88 in roughly 15,000 seconds versus AdamW at roughly 20,000 seconds.

The 0-shot downstream results (Table 2) show MARS-AdamW at 42.94% average, AdamW at 42.63%, Lion at 42.36%, and Muon at 41.54%. The 5-shot results (Table 5) show MARS-AdamW at 44.51% versus AdamW at 44.21%. The differences are small and may not be statistically significant at this scale.

**Key pattern across scales:** The performance advantage of MARS over AdamW increases monotonically with model size—small at 125M, larger at 355M, and largest at 770M. This aligns with the intuition that larger models have higher-dimensional parameter spaces and more complex loss landscapes, making gradient noise a more significant bottleneck that variance reduction addresses more effectively.

#### Downstream Benchmark Analysis

The paper evaluates models at all three scales on 0-shot and 5-shot performance across nine benchmarks (Tables 1–6). Several patterns emerge:

- **Consistent MARS advantage:** Across all three scales and both 0-shot and 5-shot settings, MARS-AdamW achieves the highest or tied-for-highest average accuracy in 5 out of 6 comparisons (all except GPT-2 medium 5-shot, where Lion edges ahead 47.22% vs. 47.37%). The advantage is most consistent at the large scale (Table 1: 50.15% vs. 48.64% for AdamW).

- **Benchmark-specific variation:** The largest consistent gains are on HellaSwag (+2.94 percentage points for large-scale 0-shot, +3.11 points for 5-shot) and BoolQ (+2.35 points 0-shot large, +6.45 points 5-shot large). On PIQA, WinoGrande, ARC-Challenge, and MMLU, the differences between optimizers are small (often within 1–2 points) and sometimes favour baselines.

- **Muon underperformance:** Muon consistently achieves the lowest or second-lowest average accuracy across nearly all scales and settings (e.g., 47.31% vs. AdamW's 48.64% on GPT-2 large 5-shot). This is notable given Muon's strong reported performance on other benchmarks in its original paper, and suggests either that the hyperparameters were not well-tuned for Muon in this setting, or that Muon's benefits are less robust to changes in model architecture or training configuration.

- **MARS-Lion vs. Lion:** MARS-Lion outperforms Lion on GPT-2 large (49.80% vs. 48.61% average 5-shot) and GPT-2 small (43.42% vs. 43.53% 5-shot—essentially tied), but underperforms Lion on GPT-2 medium (46.74% vs. 47.22% 5-shot). The benefit of explicit variance reduction for the Lion preconditioner is thus somewhat less consistent than for the AdamW preconditioner.

#### MARS-exact vs. MARS-approx (Appendix E.2)

Figures 4 and 5 (Appendix) compare the exact version (using `\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)` for proper same-batch cancellation) against the approximate version (using `\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})` from the stored previous backward pass) for MARS-AdamW on GPT-2 small and medium.

- **GPT-2 small (125M):** MARS-exact achieves a training loss roughly 0.005–0.01 lower than MARS-approx throughout most of training, and a validation loss difference of similar magnitude. Both substantially outperform AdamW.
- **GPT-2 medium (355M):** MARS-exact achieves a training loss roughly 0.005 lower than MARS-approx after 50B tokens, with validation loss showing a similar small gap.

The paper concludes that the approximate version "provides only marginal improvements over MARS-approx in practice" and recommends the approximate version for efficiency. Given the approximate version's substantially lower computational cost (one gradient computation per step vs. two, or one plus storage), this is an important practical finding.

#### FineWeb-Edu 100B Results (Appendix E.3)

To test whether the benefits depend on the dataset, the paper trains GPT-2 small (125M) and XL (1.5B) on the higher-quality FineWeb-Edu 100B dataset, comparing AdamW, Muon, and MARS-AdamW-approx. Figures 6 and 7 show:

- **GPT-2 small:** MARS-AdamW achieves a training loss of approximately 2.88 vs. AdamW at approximately 2.92 and Muon at approximately 2.94 after 50B tokens. Validation loss: MARS-AdamW at approximately 2.82, AdamW at approximately 2.86, Muon at approximately 2.88.
- **GPT-2 XL (1.5B):** MARS-AdamW achieves a training loss of approximately 2.35 vs. AdamW at approximately 2.40 and Muon at approximately 2.44 after 50B tokens. Validation loss: MARS-AdamW at approximately 2.30, AdamW at approximately 2.35, Muon at approximately 2.38.

The gap between MARS and AdamW is larger on GPT-2 XL than on GPT-2 small, consistent with the scaling trend observed on OpenWebText. The 0-shot downstream evaluations (Tables 7 and 8) show MARS-AdamW achieving the best average accuracy on both small (45.93% vs. AdamW's 45.72%) and XL (56.41% vs. AdamW's 55.43%). The paper also compares against the publicly released OpenAI GPT-2 models (Radford et al., 2019), which were trained on a different, undisclosed dataset and serve as a reference point rather than a direct comparison.

#### Computer Vision Results (Appendix E.4)

Figures 8 and 9 show test loss and accuracy for AdamW, Lion, Shampoo, Muon, and their MARS counterparts on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 with ResNet-18. The key findings:

- **CIFAR-10 (Figure 8):** After the learning rate decay at epoch 100, MARS variants (particularly MARS-AdamW) achieve test loss of approximately 0.19–0.20 vs. AdamW at 0.23, Lion at 0.25, Muon at 0.31, and Shampoo at 0.35. Test accuracy at epoch 200: MARS-AdamW-approx at 95.29%, MARS-AdamW exact at 95.26%, vs. Muon at 95.08% (the best non-MARS), AdamW at 94.81%, Lion at 94.68%, Shampoo at 94.65%.

- **CIFAR-100 (Figure 9):** MARS-AdamW achieves test loss of approximately 0.89–0.97 vs. Lion at 1.35, AdamW at 1.73, Shampoo at 2.43, Muon at 2.61. Test accuracy at epoch 200: MARS-AdamW exact at 77.38%, MARS-AdamW-approx at 76.97%, vs. Muon at 74.64% (best non-MARS), Lion at 74.28%, Shampoo at 74.27%, AdamW at 73.70%.

**Table 9** summarises the best test losses and accuracies across all configurations. MARS-AdamW-exact achieves the best test accuracy on both CIFAR-10 (95.26%) and CIFAR-100 (77.38%), with MARS-AdamW-approx close behind (95.29% and 76.97%). Notably, the MARS variants achieve substantially lower test loss than their non-variance-reduced counterparts even when test accuracy differences are modest, suggesting better calibration of the predicted probabilities.

Figures 10–13 break out each preconditioner family separately with exact and approximate MARS versions. For the Shampoo series (Figure 13c), MARS-Shampoo-exact achieves 74.98% on CIFAR-10 vs. Shampoo's 94.65%—though the paper reports a best accuracy of 94.98% for MARS-Shampoo in Table 9, suggesting the plots show a snapshot rather than the best epoch. On CIFAR-100, MARS-Shampoo-exact achieves 75.83% vs. Shampoo's 74.27%.

The computer vision results demonstrate that MARS generalises beyond language modelling, though the gains are more dramatic on CIFAR-100 (a harder task with fewer examples per class and thus higher gradient variance) than on CIFAR-10, consistent with the variance reduction intuition.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Sensitivity to `\gamma` (Appendix E.5, Figure 14):** The paper sweeps `\gamma` over `{0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2}` on GPT-2 small with MARS-AdamW-approx. The best validation loss is achieved at `\gamma = 0.025`, with the validation curve for `\gamma = 0.025` consistently below all others throughout training. `\gamma = 0.01` performs second-best, followed by `\gamma = 0.05`. The extremes—`\gamma = 0.0001` (essentially no variance reduction) and `\gamma = 0.2` (near-full STORM)—produce noticeably worse validation loss. At 50B tokens, the validation loss ordering is: `\gamma = 0.025` (best, ~2.87) > `\gamma = 0.01` (~2.88) > `\gamma = 0.05` (~2.89) > `\gamma = 0.001` (~2.90) > `\gamma = 0.1` (~2.91) > `\gamma = 0.0001` (~2.92) > `\gamma = 0.2` (~2.93). This confirms that (a) the optimal `\gamma` is far from both 0 and 1, and (b) the performance surface is not symmetric around the optimum—too much variance reduction hurts more than too little.

**Constant learning rate (Appendix E.6.1, Figures 15–16):** To isolate the effect of the optimizer from the effect of the cosine learning rate schedule, the paper trains GPT-2 small, medium, and large with constant learning rates for both AdamW and MARS-AdamW-approx. Two learning rates are tested per model size. Across all six comparisons, MARS-AdamW achieves lower validation loss than AdamW at the same learning rate, with the gap being larger at the higher learning rate. For GPT-2 large at LR = `1 \times 10^{-3}`, MARS achieves a validation loss of approximately 2.55 at 50B tokens versus AdamW at approximately 2.62—a gap of roughly 0.07. At the lower learning rate (LR = `2 \times 10^{-4}`), the gap narrows to roughly 0.02. This demonstrates that MARS's advantage is not an artifact of interacting favourably with the learning rate schedule, and that MARS can tolerate higher learning rates that would destabilise AdamW.

**WSD scheduler (Appendix E.6.2, Figures 17–18):** The paper tests the Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) scheduler (Hu et al., 2024), which consists of linear warmup, a long constant-learning-rate phase, and a short decay phase, with total training steps varied (10K, 20K, 50K, 100K steps). On GPT-2 small, MARS-AdamW consistently achieves lower training and validation loss than AdamW at every step count. Notably, MARS continues to improve during the constant-LR phase beyond the point where AdamW plateaus, suggesting that variance reduction enables more productive learning at a fixed learning rate. On GPT-2 medium, the same pattern holds: MARS-AdamW achieves lower validation loss at every step count, and the gap widens with more training steps. At 100K steps, MARS achieves a validation loss of roughly 2.65 versus AdamW at roughly 2.71.

**Sensitivity to batch size (Appendix E.7, Figure 19):** The paper trains GPT-2 small with MARS-AdamW and AdamW at three batch sizes: 240, 480 (the default), and 960 sequences. MARS-AdamW outperforms AdamW at all three batch sizes. The performance gap is largest at the smallest batch size (240): at 80K steps, MARS achieves a training loss of roughly 2.83 versus AdamW at roughly 2.87, and a validation loss of roughly 2.85 versus 2.89. At batch size 480, the gap is intermediate. At batch size 960, the gap is smallest—MARS achieves a validation loss of roughly 2.88 versus 2.90 for AdamW. This directly confirms the intuition that variance reduction is most beneficial in high-variance (small-batch) regimes, and that the benefit diminishes as larger batches naturally reduce gradient noise.

**MARS-exact vs. MARS-approx (Appendix E.2, Figures 4–5):** As described in the main results above, MARS-exact provides only a small improvement over MARS-approx (roughly 0.005–0.01 in both training and validation loss). This is an important practical robustness check: the proper variance reduction formulation (same batch for gradient differencing) is theoretically cleaner but computationally more expensive, and the approximate version captures most of the benefit at lower cost.

**Computer vision series comparison (Appendix E.4, Figures 10–13):** For each of the three preconditioner families (AdamW, Lion, Shampoo), the appendix compares the baseline (no variance reduction), the approximate MARS version, and the exact MARS version on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. In all six comparisons (3 families × 2 datasets), both MARS variants (exact and approximate) outperform the non-variance-reduced baseline in terms of both test loss and test accuracy, with the exact version typically edging out the approximate version. The largest relative gains are for Shampoo on CIFAR-100 (MARS-Shampoo-exact achieves test loss of ~0.98 vs. Shampoo's ~2.43). This robustness across preconditioner types is the key evidence for the framework's generality.

**Comparison with open-source GPT-2 (Tables 7–8):** The FineWeb-Edu 100B experiments include a comparison with the publicly released OpenAI GPT-2 small and XL models. All optimizers trained on FineWeb-Edu outperform the OpenAI models, as expected given the higher-quality dataset. More relevantly, MARS-AdamW achieves the best performance among the FineWeb-Edu-trained models, demonstrating that the MARS advantage is not specific to OpenWebText.

### Critical Assessment

**Does MARS consistently and meaningfully outperform AdamW across model scales?**

The evidence supports this claim with qualifications. On GPT-2 large, the improvement is clear and substantial: MARS-AdamW achieves a 2.511 validation loss versus AdamW's 2.568 at 50B tokens (Figure 1b), a relative improvement of roughly 2.2% in loss. On the downstream tasks, MARS-AdamW achieves 50.15% average 5-shot accuracy versus AdamW's 48.64% (Table 1). These are practically meaningful gains for large-scale training.

However, the gap narrows at smaller scales: at GPT-2 medium, the final validation loss difference is roughly 0.04 (2.72 vs. 2.76), and at GPT-2 small it is roughly 0.02 (2.87 vs. 2.89). The downstream accuracy differences at small and medium scales are within 1–2 percentage points on average and are not obviously significant given the lack of statistical testing. The paper's claim that "the performance gap becomes more pronounced with increasing model size" (Section 4.2) is supported by the loss curves, but it means that the practical benefit of MARS is primarily at larger scales—which is, to be fair, where the costs of training are highest and efficiency gains matter most.

**Is variance reduction specifically responsible for the improvements, or could other design choices explain them?**

The paper makes four changes relative to standard AdamW beyond adding variance reduction: (1) `\beta_1` is increased from 0.9 to 0.95, (2) `\beta_2` is increased from 0.95 to 0.99, (3) the second moment is computed from `\tilde{c}_t^2` instead of from the raw gradient squared, and (4) gradient clipping is applied to `c_t` before it enters the momentum buffers. The ablation studies isolate some but not all of these factors.

The `\gamma` sweep (Figure 14) provides the strongest evidence that variance reduction specifically matters: `\gamma = 0.025` clearly outperforms `\gamma = 0.0001` (which approximates no variance reduction, though `\beta_1` and `\beta_2` are still at MARS values). However, this comparison does not fully isolate the effect because the `\gamma = 0.0001` run still uses the MARS `\beta` values and the matched second moment. A cleaner ablation would compare MARS-AdamW with `\gamma = 0.025` against AdamW *with the same `\beta_1, \beta_2` values* (0.95, 0.99) to determine whether the `\beta` values or the variance reduction contribute more.

Similarly, the effect of computing `v_t` from `\tilde{c}_t^2` rather than from `\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t)^2` is not ablated in isolation. The paper argues this is crucial (Remark 3.2), and the comparison with SuperAdam (which used the raw gradient for `v_t`) is cited as supporting evidence, but no head-to-head ablation is presented. A simple experiment—MARS-AdamW with `v_t` computed from the raw gradient instead of `\tilde{c}_t^2`—would directly test this claim and is a notable omission.

**Does the framework genuinely unify existing optimizers, and does this unification explain their relative performance?**

The theoretical connections between MARS and Adan, Lion, and Muon (Section 3.2) are mathematically sound and illuminating. The empirical results are broadly consistent with the unification story: MARS-AdamW outperforms AdamW (variance reduction adds value), MARS-Lion outperforms Lion (explicit variance reduction beats implicit approximate reduction), and MARS-Shampoo outperforms Shampoo (in computer vision experiments). The approximate nature of the implicit variance reduction in Lion, Adan, and Muon (different data batches for gradient differencing) provides a plausible explanation for why explicit variance reduction works better.

However, the connection to Muon does not fully explain the Muon results in the GPT-2 experiments. Muon underperforms AdamW significantly (GPT-2 large validation loss of 2.606 vs. 2.568) and is the worst-performing optimizer on most benchmarks. If Muon is a rescaled approximate variance-reduced method, it should at least match AdamW (which has no variance reduction) in many settings. The paper does not discuss why Muon underperforms so substantially in its experiments when it was reported to outperform AdamW in its original paper (Jordan et al., 2024). Possible explanations—hyperparameter choices, differences in model architecture or training setup, or limitations of the Muon approximation at these scales—are not explored, and this weakens the unified narrative.

**Do the downstream task improvements represent genuine better learning, or are they within the noise of evaluation?**

The downstream evaluation presents aggregate metrics without confidence intervals, making it difficult to distinguish signal from noise. The 5-shot GPT-2 large results (Table 1) show plausible improvements: 50.15% vs. 48.64% average, with BoolQ showing a +6.45 percentage point gap and HellaSwag showing +3.11. These are substantial enough to likely be meaningful. However, the 0-shot results (Table 4) show a smaller gap (48.27% vs. 46.78%), and the small and medium results are less clear (e.g., GPT-2 medium 5-shot: MARS-AdamW 47.37% vs. Lion 47.22% vs. AdamW 45.57%—the top two are essentially tied). Multi-seed evaluation would be needed to establish statistical significance, and the paper's individual benchmark results show substantial variation (e.g., MARS-Lion wins on ARC-Easy 54.25% vs. MARS-AdamW 54.04%, but MARS-AdamW wins on BoolQ 62.78% vs. MARS-Lion 56.36%). The aggregate improvements are consistently positive but not uniformly large.

**Are the gains robust across datasets and training configurations?**

The experiments on FineWeb-Edu (Appendix E.3) demonstrate that the benefits persist on a higher-quality dataset, and the WSD scheduler experiments (Appendix E.6.2) show that MARS continues to improve when AdamW plateaus. The constant learning rate experiments (Appendix E.6.1) demonstrate that the advantage is not an artifact of the cosine schedule. The batch size sweep (Appendix E.7) shows that the benefit is, as expected, largest at small batch sizes.

However, all language model experiments use GPT-2 architectures with a specific set of design choices (GeLU activations, disabled biases, dropout rate 0.0, context length 1024). The authors note that "data augmentation, batch normalization, and dropout are nowadays rarely used" in language model training, making variance reduction viable, but they do not test what happens if these are reintroduced. The computer vision experiments do not use data augmentation, batch normalization, or dropout either (ResNet-18 on CIFAR typically uses batch norm, but the experimental details are minimal). This leaves open whether MARS would remain effective in settings where the finite-sum structure is partially disrupted.

**What experiments are missing that would strengthen the claims?**

1. **Multiple random seeds for the main GPT-2 training runs.** The single-run results cannot support statistical claims about which optimizer is genuinely better, particularly at small and medium scales where the differences are small.

2. **A scaling law study with larger models.** The paper claims the gap widens with model size, but tests only up to 770M (GPT-2 large) on OpenWebText and 1.5B (GPT-2 XL) on FineWeb-Edu. Demonstrating the trend at 7B, 13B, or larger scales would substantially strengthen the claim that MARS addresses a fundamental bottleneck that grows with model size.

3. **Ablation of `\beta_1` and `\beta_2`.** A controlled experiment with MARS's `\beta_1 = 0.95, \beta_2 = 0.99` applied to standard AdamW (without variance reduction) would disentangle the effect of the momentum hyperparameters from the effect of variance reduction.

4. **Ablation of the matched-second-moment design.** A version of MARS-AdamW with `v_t` computed from the raw gradient (as in SuperAdam) but with all other MARS features preserved would directly test whether the matched-second-moment design is critical or merely incidental.

5. **Head-to-head comparison with SuperAdam.** Given that the paper positions MARS as fixing SuperAdam's mismatch, a direct comparison on GPT-2 would validate this claim empirically rather than relying on citation and argument.

6. **Experiments beyond autoregressive language modeling.** The computer vision experiments partially address this, but the paper's central claim is about large-scale training, and the ResNet-18 experiments are small-scale. Testing on other architectures (e.g., Vision Transformers) and tasks (e.g., masked language modeling with BERT, machine translation) would establish broader applicability.

7. **Memory and throughput analysis.** The paper reports wall-clock time (Figures 1c, 2c, 3c) showing that MARS-AdamW is faster than AdamW at equal loss, but does not analyse memory consumption (storing `\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})` for MARS-approx) or throughput (tokens per second). For practitioners, these metrics are as important as token efficiency.

**Summary of strengths and weaknesses:**

The experimental evidence convincingly demonstrates that MARS-AdamW outperforms AdamW on GPT-2 pretraining at the 355M and 770M scales, with the advantage being robust across learning rate schedules, batch sizes, and datasets. The `\gamma` sweep provides compelling evidence that variance reduction specifically (not just hyperparameter changes) drives the improvement. The framework's generality is partially demonstrated through MARS-Lion and MARS-Shampoo outperforming their non-variance-reduced counterparts, though the gains are smaller for MARS-Lion and the Shampoo experiments are computer-vision-only.

The key weaknesses are: (1) lack of statistical rigor (single runs, no confidence intervals); (2) incomplete ablation of the individual design choices (matched second moment not ablated, `\beta` values not ablated); (3) limited scale (no models larger than 1.5B parameters, which is small by contemporary standards); and (4) Muon's poor performance is unexplained and potentially undermines the unification narrative. The paper makes a strong case that variance reduction deserves a place in the LLM optimizer toolkit, but the specific claims about why MARS works (matched second moment, optimal `\gamma_t`) are supported more by argument and limited evidence than by exhaustive controlled experiments.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Approximate Version's Theoretical Sacrifice and the Unresolved `$\gamma_t$` Tuning Problem

**The assumption or constraint.** MARS's theoretical convergence guarantees (Theorems B.5, B.6 in Appendix B.2) rely on the exact variance reduction formulation where the gradient correction term uses the *same* data batch `$\xi_t$` for both `$\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t)$` and `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$`. The paper acknowledges a practical obstacle to this: computing both gradients either requires storing `$\xi_t$` and performing a second backward pass, or recomputing `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$` from scratch. The proposed remedy, MARS-approx, replaces `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$` with `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})$`—the gradient already computed at the previous step on the *previous* batch—and the authors explicitly state they "recommend using MARS-approx for practical applications" (Remark 3.3). The `$\gamma_t$` parameter, introduced and theoretically motivated by a control variates argument (Section 3.1), is treated as a constant hyperparameter in all experiments, with the paper noting in the theory (Lemma C.2, Equation D.14) that the optimal `$\gamma_t^*$` depends on unobservable quantities like `$\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_t\|_2^2$` and cross-covariance terms between the gradient estimate and the correction term.

**The consequence.** There are two linked problems, one practical and one theoretical. Practically, the optimal `$\gamma_t$` must be found by grid search for each new model/dataset combination, as the paper did for GPT-2 (Appendix E.5, Figure 14, sweeping over seven values and settling on `$\gamma = 0.025$`). The sensitivity is non-trivial: Figure 14 shows that `$\gamma = 0.2$` produces validation loss roughly 0.06 worse than `$\gamma = 0.025$` at 50B tokens on GPT-2 small—a gap comparable to the entire MARS-vs-AdamW advantage at that scale. A practitioner switching to a different model architecture, dataset, or training configuration cannot assume `$\gamma = 0.025$` remains optimal and must re-tune, consuming compute that the paper's headline efficiency gains do not account for. Theoretically, MARS-approx breaks the proper noise cancellation property: `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$` and `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})$` are both unbiased estimators of `$\nabla F(x_{t-1})$`, but they are *independent*—their difference does not cancel common noise in the way the exact formulation requires, and the convergence proofs in Appendix B.2 do not apply to the approximate version at all. The paper provides no theoretical analysis of MARS-approx's convergence properties, leaving the version recommended for practice without the theoretical guarantees that motivate the framework.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Appendix E.2 (Figures 4 and 5) directly compares MARS-exact and MARS-approx on GPT-2 small and medium, showing the exact version achieves roughly 0.005–0.01 lower training and validation loss. The `$\gamma$` sweep (Appendix E.5, Figure 14) demonstrates that performance is sensitive to this parameter, with the validation loss at 50B tokens varying by approximately 0.06 across the tested range. The authors do not report how much compute was spent on the `$\gamma$` grid search or whether `$\gamma = 0.025$` was re-tuned for each model size (small/medium/large) or each dataset (OpenWebText, FineWeb-Edu). Table 12 lists `$\gamma_t \equiv 0.025$` as a universal constant for all GPT-2 experiments.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges the `$\gamma$` tuning issue through the control variates analysis, which shows that the optimal `$\gamma_t$` is not generally 1 and depends on problem-dependent quantities—but this analysis describes the problem rather than solving it. The paper suggests no method for estimating `$\gamma_t^*$` online or for transfer across model/dataset configurations. The recommendation of MARS-approx is made without theoretical backing, and the convergence guarantee gap between the analysed exact version and the recommended approximate version is not discussed. Future work on adaptive `$\gamma_t$` schedules is implicitly suggested by the theoretical framing but not explicitly called out.

### Unverified Scaling Trend and Absence of Frontier-Scale Validation

**The assumption or constraint.** All language model experiments use GPT-2 architectures at scales up to 770M parameters (GPT-2 large) on OpenWebText (~9B training tokens) and 1.5B parameters (GPT-2 XL) on FineWeb-Edu 100B. The paper claims that "the performance gap becomes more pronounced with increasing model size" (Section 4.2), based on observing a widening loss gap from 125M → 355M → 770M. This extrapolation is used to motivate the broader significance of MARS, but it has not been tested beyond the 1.5B scale. Contemporary LLM training routinely operates at 7B, 13B, 70B, or larger parameter counts, with training corpora in the trillions of tokens. The paper does not establish whether the trend continues, plateaus, or reverses at such scales.

**The consequence.** A practitioner training a 7B or larger model cannot confidently predict whether MARS will provide the ~1.8× token efficiency gain observed on GPT-2 large, a smaller gain (as at GPT-2 small), or even a loss relative to AdamW. The scaling claim is correlational (observed at three data points) rather than mechanistically explained. It is plausible that the benefit of variance reduction grows with model size because larger models have higher-dimensional parameter spaces and more complex loss landscapes where gradient noise is more damaging—but it is equally plausible that at very large scales, other factors (batch size constraints, learning rate schedules, training instability) interact with variance reduction in unexpected ways, or that the optimal `$\gamma_t$` shifts to a value that the current grid search misses. The paper does not explore whether MARS's memory overhead (storing a copy of the previous step's gradient for MARS-approx) or its interaction with distributed training strategies like model parallelism, pipeline parallelism, or ZeRO-style sharding creates scaling bottlenecks that are absent at GPT-2 scale.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The three-scale comparison on OpenWebText (Figures 1, 2, 3) shows a monotonic increase in the AdamW-MARS gap: at 125M, the gap is ~0.02 validation loss; at 355M, ~0.04; at 770M, ~0.06. The FineWeb-Edu experiments (Figures 6, 7) show a similar pattern from 125M to 1.5B. This is consistent with but does not strongly confirm the extrapolation to larger scales—three to four data points do not establish a scaling law. The paper provides no theoretical analysis predicting how the benefit should scale with parameter count, and no experiments at the 7B+ scale that would be needed to validate the extrapolation for practitioners.

**Mitigation status.** The authors do not claim to have tested at frontier scale, and they are transparent about the model sizes used. The scaling claim is presented as an empirical observation rather than a proven law. However, the paper does not temper this claim with caveats about extrapolation risk, nor does it discuss what experiments would be needed to validate it. The impact statement's claim that MARS "can greatly lower the cost for pre-training language models" implicitly assumes the scaling trend continues to practically relevant scales, which is exactly the assumption that remains unverified.

### Training-FLOPs Efficiency Gains Do Not Account for the Cost of Finding `$\gamma_t$` and Hyperparameters

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline efficiency claim—that MARS-AdamW requires ~28B tokens to reach the validation loss that AdamW reaches at 50B tokens on GPT-2 large, a ~1.8× reduction—is computed *after* hyperparameters have been selected via grid search. For each model size and optimizer, the authors searched over learning rates in a set of 8 values, weight decay in a set of 3 values, and for MARS specifically, `$\gamma$` over 7 values (Appendix E.5). The compute consumed by these tuning runs is not amortized into the reported token counts. For a practitioner adopting MARS on a new task, the total cost is: tuning runs + production training, not production training alone.

**The consequence.** The practical efficiency gain depends heavily on how many tuning runs are needed. If a practitioner must test 7 `$\gamma$` values × 8 learning rates × 3 weight decays = 168 configurations to find the optimal combination for a new model architecture or dataset, the tuning cost could easily exceed the production training savings unless the model is being trained for substantially more than 50B tokens. The problem is compounded by the fact that `$\gamma$` is an additional hyperparameter that AdamW does not have—increasing the dimensionality of the search space. The paper does not characterise how `$\gamma$` interacts with learning rate and weight decay (are these interactions strong, requiring joint search, or weak, allowing `$\gamma$` to be tuned once and transferred?), nor does it report what fraction of the grid the final hyperparameters were selected from.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The grid search ranges are documented in Appendix F (Section F): learning rate over `{1e-4, 1.5e-4, 3e-4, 6e-4, 1e-3, 1.5e-3, 3e-3, 6e-3}`, weight decay over `{1e-1, 1e-2, 1e-3}`, and `$\gamma$` over `{0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2}`. The total number of configurations tested is not reported. The final hyperparameters (Table 12) use the same `$\gamma = 0.025$`, `$\beta_1 = 0.95$`, and `$\beta_2 = 0.99$` for all three model sizes—suggesting some transferability—but the learning rate and minimum learning rate change with model scale (e.g., max LR = `6e-3` for small, `3e-3` for medium, `2e-3` for large). The constant learning rate experiments (Appendix E.6.1) and WSD scheduler experiments (Appendix E.6.2) test robustness to LR schedules but do not address the hyperparameter tuning cost question.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The `$\gamma$` sensitivity analysis (Appendix E.5) shows that the optimal value can be found, but does not discuss the cost of finding it or propose a method for reducing that cost (e.g., a heuristic for initialising `$\gamma$` based on dataset size or model architecture, or an online adaptive schedule). The authors do not frame the tuning cost as a limitation, and the headline efficiency numbers are reported without caveats about tuning overhead.

### Memory Overhead and the Non-Trivial Per-Iteration Cost Increase

**The assumption or constraint.** MARS-approx—the version recommended for practical use—avoids computing two gradients per step but introduces a new memory requirement: the gradient from the previous step, `$\nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_{t-1})$`, must be stored in a buffer and retrieved at the current step. For a model with `$d$` parameters, this requires an additional `$d \times \text{sizeof(float)}$` bytes of memory—roughly 4`$d$` bytes for float32, or 2`$d$` bytes for bfloat16. The paper reports that MARS "have a slightly higher per-iteration cost compared to AdamW but is much faster than Muon" (Section 4.2), and the wall-clock time plots (Figures 1c, 2c, 3c) attempt to account for this in end-to-end comparisons.

**The consequence.** For large models, this memory overhead is non-trivial. GPT-2 large at 770M parameters requires roughly 3.1 GB of additional memory for the gradient buffer (in float32). At the 70B scale, the buffer would require approximately 280 GB—comparable to the memory needed for the model parameters themselves. In distributed training settings with model parallelism or sharding, this buffer must be either duplicated across workers (multiplying the cost) or itself sharded (adding communication overhead). The paper does not analyse whether the buffer can be stored in reduced precision (bfloat16 or float8) without degrading the variance reduction benefit, or whether gradient checkpointing and recomputation strategies interact with the buffer requirement. Practitioners operating near memory limits may find that MARS's memory cost forces a reduction in batch size or model dimension, partially or fully negating the token-efficiency gain.

The "slightly higher per-iteration cost" mentioned in the paper is visible in the wall-clock time comparisons: at GPT-2 large (Figure 1c), MARS-AdamW reaches a validation loss of ~2.54 in roughly 50,000 seconds, while AdamW reaches the same loss in roughly 70,000 seconds—a ~1.4× speedup, notably smaller than the ~1.8× token-efficiency ratio. The difference between the token-efficiency gain and the wall-clock gain (~1.8× vs. ~1.4×) reflects the per-iteration overhead. For latency-sensitive applications or systems where compute is less constrained than memory, this overhead may be the binding constraint.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 1(c) shows wall-clock time on 32× H100 GPUs for GPT-2 large: MARS-AdamW reaches any given validation loss faster than AdamW, but the gap is visibly smaller in wall-clock time than in token count (compare Figure 1b vs. 1c). The paper does not report memory consumption, peak GPU memory usage, or throughput in tokens per second for any configuration. The memory requirement for the gradient buffer is not discussed in the main text or appendices.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address memory overhead as a limitation. The authors acknowledge the per-iteration cost increase but frame it as small relative to the token-efficiency gain. No experiments vary the precision of the stored gradient buffer, test memory consumption, or analyse the interaction with distributed training strategies. The recommendation of MARS-approx is made on computational grounds (avoiding double backward passes) without discussing the memory implications. For practitioners deploying at scale, this is an unquantified risk: MARS may be faster per effective token of learning, but whether it fits in available memory is an open question that the paper provides no guidance on answering.

---

### Single-Task Evaluation and the Unexplored Failure Modes Under Distribution Shift

**The assumption or constraint.** All language model experiments evaluate MARS on autoregressive language modeling (GPT-2 pretraining) with a single evaluation protocol: perplexity/validation loss on held-out text, plus zero-shot and few-shot accuracy on a standard suite of 9 benchmarks. The paper does not test MARS on other training paradigms—masked language modeling (BERT-style), encoder-decoder architectures (T5-style), instruction fine-tuning, or RLHF/alignment stages—and does not evaluate whether models trained with MARS exhibit different robustness properties, calibration, or susceptibility to distribution shift compared to AdamW-trained models. The convergence theory (Section 4, Appendix B.2) assumes smoothness, bounded variance, and a lower-bounded preconditioner—standard assumptions that do not address the unique optimisation challenges of multi-stage training pipelines.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot assume that MARS's benefits transfer to non-autoregressive pretraining objectives or to downstream fine-tuning. The variance reduction mechanism relies on the gradient difference `$\nabla f(x_t, \xi_t) - \nabla f(x_{t-1}, \xi_t)$` being informative about the change in the true gradient—a property that may not hold equally well in multi-task, multi-stage, or meta-learning settings where the effective objective function shifts between training phases. More subtly, the paper does not investigate whether MARS's faster convergence in pretraining loss translates proportionally to downstream task performance, or whether there is a tradeoff (e.g., faster pretraining convergence but worse downstream calibration). The downstream results (Tables 1–6) provide a single snapshot at the end of pretraining and show MARS-AdamW achieving higher average accuracy, but do not measure whether, at equal pretraining loss, MARS-trained and AdamW-trained models have equivalent downstream capabilities.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The downstream evaluations (Tables 1–8) are at a fixed token count (end of training), not at matched validation loss. It is therefore unclear whether the downstream improvements are due to MARS reaching a better pretraining loss (which it does) or due to some additional property of the variance-reduced optimisation trajectory. The computer vision experiments (Appendix E.4) test a different domain (image classification with ResNet-18 on CIFAR) and show gains, suggesting some cross-domain robustness, but these experiments are small-scale and do not use modern architectures or training recipes. No experiments test MARS on fine-tuning, continued pretraining, or multi-stage pipelines.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss this limitation or claim broader applicability. The impact statement mentions "pre-training stage of deep learning models, especially Large Language Models" as the target, implicitly scoping the claims to pretraining. However, the advertising of the framework as general ("a unified framework for preconditioned variance reduction") and the presentation of downstream results create an expectation of broader applicability that is not tested. Future work on multi-stage training and on robustness to distribution shift is not explicitly suggested.

### The Matched Second-Moment Design Is Not Ablated, Leaving Its Necessity Unproven

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central design argument is that SuperAdam (Huang et al., 2021) underperformed because its second-order momentum was computed from the raw stochastic gradient while its first-order momentum used variance reduction—"a significant mismatch between the first-order and second-order momentum" (Remark 3.1). MARS fixes this by computing both `$m_t$` and `$v_t$` from the same variance-reduced estimate `$\tilde{c}_t$`. This design choice is presented as crucial. The paper contrasts MARS with SuperAdam in Remark 3.1 and Remark 3.2 but performs no head-to-head comparison and no ablation where MARS-AdamW is tested with `$v_t$` computed from the raw gradient (holding all other MARS-specific choices—`$\beta_1 = 0.95$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.99$`, `$\gamma_t = 0.025$`, gradient clipping on `$c_t$`—constant).

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot determine whether the matched-second-moment design is a necessary condition for variance reduction to work with preconditioning, or whether it is a helpful but non-essential detail. It is possible that the primary benefit of MARS over SuperAdam comes from the `$\gamma_t$` scaling (SuperAdam uses full STORM, equivalent to `$\gamma_t = 1$`, which the `$\gamma$` sweep shows is substantially worse than `$\gamma = 0.025$`), from the gradient clipping on `$c_t$` (SuperAdam does not clip), or from the different `$\beta$` values, rather than from the matched second moment specifically. If the matched second moment is not critical, practitioners implementing variance reduction in other optimizers could use the simpler approach of keeping the existing second-moment computation unchanged. If it is critical, it represents a burdensome design constraint that must be respected in any future variance-reduced adaptive method. The paper provides no evidence to distinguish these possibilities.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides theoretical convergence analysis for the matched design (Theorems B.5, B.6) but no analysis of the mismatched variant. The `$\gamma$` sweep (Figure 14) includes `$\gamma = 0.0001$`, which approaches "no variance reduction" while keeping the matched `$v_t$` and MARS `$\beta$` values, but there is no symmetric experiment with `$\gamma = 0.025$` and mismatched `$v_t$` computed from the raw gradient. The comparison with SuperAdam is entirely in the related work discussion (Remark 3.1, Remark 3.2, Appendix A) without empirical evidence. The computer vision experiments (Figures 10–13) compare baseline vs. MARS-approx vs. MARS-exact for each preconditioner family, but all MARS variants use the matched design—there is no "MARS with mismatched `$v_t$`" variant in any plot.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a gap. The matched-second-moment design is presented as a key innovation and contrasted with SuperAdam, but the evidence for its necessity is entirely argumentative (the control variates logic) and circumstantial (the overall success of MARS). The missing ablation—MARS-AdamW with `$v_t$` from raw gradient—would require a few lines of code change and modest compute (GPT-2 small would suffice to see the effect), yet it is not performed. This is the most notable methodological gap in the experimental design: a central design claim is made without the direct experiment that would test it.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the conversation around optimizer design for large-scale training from "which preconditioner is best?" to "how should variance reduction and preconditioning be integrated?" Before MARS, the implicit consensus in the LLM training community was that variance reduction—despite its theoretical advantages—was incompatible with the practical demands of deep learning. Defazio & Bottou (2019) had argued this explicitly, and prior attempts like SuperAdam (Huang et al., 2021) had produced underwhelming results confined to small-scale tasks. The practical optimizer landscape was dominated by adaptive methods (AdamW, Lion, Shampoo) that all operated on raw stochastic gradients, and the stagnation noted by Kaddour et al. (2024) and Zhao et al. (2024)—that no recent optimizer consistently beats AdamW—suggested the field had exhausted the gains available from refining preconditioning alone.

MARS's primary conceptual contribution is **reframing variance reduction as a tunable component that can be retrofitted into existing adaptive optimizers**, rather than a competing paradigm. The specific mechanism—a scaled STORM-style correction term with a tunable γ_t parameter, combined with computing both first and second moments from the same variance-reduced estimate—is incremental on its face, but the sum is larger than the parts. By showing that several recently proposed optimizers (Adan, Lion, Muon) emerge as approximate or constrained special cases of the MARS framework under specific parameter settings, the paper provides a **unifying taxonomy** that explains *why* these methods work: they all implement implicit, approximate variance reduction. This is a genuine reframing—it converts a set of apparently disparate innovations (Nesterov acceleration, symbolic search, eigenspace preconditioning) into manifestations of a common underlying principle.

This is not a paradigm shift. It does not introduce a new class of optimization algorithms or overturn our understanding of how neural networks learn. What it does is **redirect the focus of optimizer research from preconditioner design to gradient estimation quality**. The empirical finding that the performance gap between MARS and AdamW widens with model scale (from ~0.02 validation loss at 125M to ~0.06 at 770M) suggests that gradient noise is a binding bottleneck whose importance grows with model capacity—and that variance reduction addresses this bottleneck in a way that refining the preconditioner does not. If this trend continues to larger scales (7B, 70B, and beyond), the implication is that **future optimizer development should treat variance reduction as a first-class component, not an optional add-on**.

The paper also resolves—or at least reframes—the contradiction between the theoretical success of variance reduction in classical optimization and its practical failure in deep learning. Defazio & Bottou's diagnosis (data augmentation, batch norm, dropout disrupt finite-sum structure) turns out to be historically contingent rather than fundamental: modern LLM training pipelines have removed these disruptors, restoring the conditions under which variance reduction can work. This does not mean variance reduction will work everywhere—the computer vision experiments in Appendix E.4 use ResNet-18 on CIFAR, which typically includes batch norm, and the experimental details are minimal—but it provides a clear criterion for when variance reduction is applicable: when the training objective has a stable finite-sum structure over data passes.

**Research directions that become more attractive:**

- **Variance reduction as a standard optimizer component.** The paper makes a strong case that any new adaptive optimizer proposal should be compared against its variance-reduced counterpart (e.g., "AdamW with MARS-style variance reduction") to determine whether apparent gains are due to better gradient estimation or better preconditioning. This could become a standard ablation in optimizer papers.

- **Online adaptation of γ_t.** The control variates analysis shows that the optimal γ_t depends on unobservable quantities, but approximations based on moving estimates of gradient variance and cross-covariance could enable dynamic schedules that outperform the constant γ = 0.025 used in all experiments.

- **Variance reduction beyond autoregressive LM.** The paper opens the door to testing variance-reduced adaptive methods on masked language modeling, encoder-decoder architectures, and multi-modal training—any setting without heavy data augmentation.

**Research directions that become less attractive:**

- **Pure preconditioner innovation without addressing gradient noise.** If MARS's gains are primarily due to variance reduction rather than preconditioner changes (the γ sweep provides strong evidence for this), then papers proposing new preconditioner designs without addressing gradient estimation quality may face a higher burden of proof—they must show gains over a variance-reduced variant of AdamW, not just over vanilla AdamW.

- **Symbolic search for optimizers.** The finding that Lion—discovered through expensive symbolic program search—can be reinterpreted as approximate variance reduction suggests that the search space for optimizer design may be better understood through theoretical analysis than brute-force exploration. Symbolic search found a good optimizer, but MARS explains *why* it is good and how to improve it, without requiring the search.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Scaling MARS to 7B+ parameter models with controlled γ_t transfer.** The most pressing open question is whether the scaling trend observed from 125M → 355M → 770M continues to frontier scales. A concrete experiment: train a 7B parameter model (e.g., Llama-2-7B architecture) on 100B+ tokens with both AdamW and MARS-AdamW at the same hyperparameters found for GPT-2 (γ = 0.025, β₁ = 0.95, β₂ = 0.99), with and without re-tuning γ. The outcomes that matter: (a) does the ~1.8× token efficiency gain persist or change? (b) can γ = 0.025 transfer from GPT-2 to a 7B model without re-tuning, or does the optimal γ shift with scale? (c) does the memory overhead of storing the previous gradient buffer (~4 bytes per parameter) create practical problems with ZeRO-3 sharding or pipeline parallelism at this scale? A negative result—gains disappearing or requiring prohibitively expensive re-tuning—would bound the practical applicability of MARS; a positive result would make it immediately relevant to most LLM training teams.

**Ablation of the matched second-moment design to test its necessity.** The paper argues that computing v_t from \tilde{c}_t^2 rather than from the raw gradient squared is crucial for calibration between first and second moments, and contrasts MARS with SuperAdam on this basis—but never ablates the choice. A clean experiment: train GPT-2 medium with three variants—standard AdamW, MARS-AdamW (matched v_t), and MARS-AdamW with v_t computed from the raw \nabla f(x_t, ξ_t)^2 while keeping all other MARS features (β₁=0.95, β₂=0.99, γ=0.025, clipping on c_t)—and compare validation loss curves. If the mismatched variant performs closer to MARS-AdamW than to AdamW, then the matched second moment is incidental and the gains come from γ_t, β values, and clipping. If it performs closer to AdamW, the matched design is genuinely load-bearing. This experiment costs one GPT-2 medium training run (~$few hundred GPU-hours) and would resolve the paper's most prominent untested design claim.

**Training a lightweight γ_t predictor from observable statistics.** The control variates optimality condition for γ_t (Equation D.14 in Lemma C.2) depends on expectations over the data distribution that are not directly observable, but the paper provides the exact expression: it involves the ratio of a cross-covariance term to the expected squared norm of the gradient difference. A concrete research project: during a MARS training run, periodically compute the sample estimates of these quantities over a few recent batches, and use them to update γ_t via a smoothed online estimate of γ_t^*. Compare this adaptive schedule against the constant γ = 0.025 baseline on GPT-2 medium. If the adaptive schedule matches or exceeds constant γ without requiring grid search, it eliminates the main barrier to adoption—the need to tune an extra hyperparameter. Even a negative result (adaptive γ_t underperforms constant) would be informative: it would suggest that the theoretical optimal γ_t^* is not the practical optimum due to the approximations in MARS-approx or the interaction with gradient clipping.

**MARS for contrastive and multi-modal pretraining.** All language experiments use standard next-token prediction. A natural stress test is contrastive learning (e.g., CLIP-style training) where the objective couples gradients across positive and negative pairs, potentially changing the noise structure. Train a small vision-language model (e.g., a ViT-B/16 + BERT-base) with both AdamW and MARS-AdamW on a dataset like CC3M or CC12M, measuring both contrastive loss convergence and downstream zero-shot retrieval/image classification. This would test whether MARS's benefits require the clean per-token loss structure of autoregressive LM or extend to losses with more complex gradient coupling. The finite-sum structure of contrastive learning is preserved (no data augmentation on text, limited augmentation on images), so the paper's applicability criterion is met—but the gradient noise distribution may be qualitatively different.

**Investigate whether MARS-trained models have different scaling law coefficients.** The paper shows MARS achieves lower loss for the same token count, but a deeper question is whether the compute-optimal allocation of tokens to parameters changes under variance-reduced optimization. Using the Chinchilla scaling law framework (Hoffmann et al., 2022), train a range of small models (e.g., 50M, 100M, 200M, 400M parameters) with both AdamW and MARS-AdamW on a fixed dataset, fitting scaling law coefficients for each optimizer. If MARS changes the exponent relating loss to compute, this would mean that optimal model sizing strategies developed under AdamW need revision for MARS-trained models. If it only changes the constant factor (which the current results at 125M–770M suggest), then existing scaling law recommendations transfer with a multiplier on effective compute.

**Negative result: test MARS on tasks with heavy data augmentation or batch norm.** The paper's premise is that variance reduction failed in deep learning due to data augmentation, batch norm, and dropout—and that LLM training avoids these. A deliberate stress test would reintroduce them. Train a ResNet-50 on ImageNet with standard augmentation (random crop, horizontal flip, color jitter), batch norm enabled, and compare AdamW vs. MARS-AdamW. If MARS still provides gains, the paper's finite-sum explanation is incomplete and variance reduction is more broadly applicable than argued. If MARS degrades or provides no benefit, it confirms the boundary condition and provides a clear "do not use MARS here" guideline for practitioners. This experiment is important because it would define the scope of MARS's applicability—the paper currently claims applicability to modern LLM training but has not tested the boundary where variance reduction breaks.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient LLM pretraining at medium-to-large scale.** The most direct application is replacing AdamW with MARS-AdamW for pretraining transformer language models in the 1B–10B parameter range on budgets of 100B–500B tokens. The paper's GPT-2 large result provides a concrete benchmark: reaching AdamW's 50B-token validation loss of 2.58 in only ~28B tokens—a ~1.8× token reduction, with an end-to-end wall-clock speedup of roughly 1.4× after accounting for MARS's per-iteration overhead (Figure 1c). For a team training a comparable model, this directly translates to either (a) training a better model within the same compute budget (MARS reaches 2.511 final loss vs. AdamW's 2.568) or (b) reducing GPU-hours by roughly 30% for equivalent quality. The downstream improvements (Table 1: +3.11 percentage points on HellaSwag 5-shot for GPT-2 large, +6.45 on BoolQ) mean the efficiency gain does not come at the cost of model capability—if anything, the MARS-trained model generalizes slightly better. The γ = 0.025 finding from Appendix E.5 provides a reasonable starting point for new applications, reducing (though not eliminating) the hyperparameter search burden.

**Longer productive training with constant or stable learning rates.** The WSD scheduler experiments (Appendix E.6.2, Figures 17–18) show that MARS-AdamW continues to reduce validation loss during the constant-learning-rate phase beyond the point where AdamW plateaus. At 100K steps on GPT-2 medium, MARS achieves a validation loss of ~2.65 versus AdamW at ~2.71—a gap of ~0.06 that is larger than at earlier step counts. This has direct implications for continual pretraining, domain adaptation, and any scenario where a model is trained on a stream of data for an extended period at a fixed learning rate. MARS appears to extract more value from additional data without requiring aggressive learning rate decay to converge, which makes it well-suited for training regimes where the data distribution shifts or grows over time and the learning rate cannot be tightly scheduled in advance.

**Training under small-batch constraints (edge devices, academic labs).** The batch size sweep (Appendix E.7, Figure 19) shows that the performance gap between MARS-AdamW and AdamW is largest at the smallest batch size (240), where MARS achieves validation loss ~0.04 lower than AdamW at 80K steps. This makes MARS particularly attractive for settings where large-batch training is infeasible—single-GPU academic experimentation, fine-tuning on limited hardware, or on-device training. In these regimes, gradient noise is inherently higher because fewer samples are averaged per step, and variance reduction partially compensates for this. The practical recommendation from the paper is clear: if you cannot increase your batch size, switch to MARS to simulate the noise characteristics of a larger batch without the memory cost.

**Optimizer selection in frameworks and training pipelines.** The finding that MARS outperforms AdamW, Lion, and Muon at all tested scales (though more modestly at 125M) suggests that MARS-AdamW could serve as a drop-in replacement for AdamW in standard training frameworks. The paper provides hyperparameters for three model scales (Table 12) and the code is publicly available. The approximate version (MARS-approx) is recommended and requires only storing one additional gradient buffer (~4 bytes per parameter) beyond AdamW's memory footprint—a manageable overhead for most training setups. For teams maintaining internal training infrastructure, switching the default optimizer from AdamW to MARS-AdamW could yield systematic efficiency improvements across all projects, amortizing the one-time cost of integration and testing.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not articulate an explicit tradeoff matrix against named alternatives—its positioning is "MARS as a direct upgrade over existing adaptive optimizers" rather than "MARS for scenario A, AdamW for scenario B." In all experiments, MARS-AdamW outperforms AdamW, MARS-Lion outperforms Lion, and MARS-Shampoo outperforms Shampoo, with the only negative result being Muon's general underperformance (which is not a MARS issue). The ablation studies do not identify a regime where AdamW beats MARS-AdamW. The γ sweep (Figure 14) shows that even with suboptimal γ (including γ too close to 0 or 1), MARS-AdamW does not meaningfully underperform AdamW—the worst MARS configuration is roughly comparable to the best AdamW configuration.

The practical decision is therefore not "MARS vs. AdamW" based on problem characteristics, but rather **whether the adoption cost (implementing the gradient buffer, tuning γ for a new domain, ensuring compatibility with existing training infrastructure) is justified by the expected efficiency gain**. Based on the paper's evidence, the decision factors are:

- **Model scale is the primary driver of benefit.** The gain grows from ~0.02 validation loss improvement at 125M to ~0.06 at 770M. For models below ~100M parameters or training runs under ~10B tokens, the absolute gain may not justify the integration effort. For models at 1B+ parameters and training runs of 50B+ tokens, the ~1.4× wall-clock speedup (Figure 1c) makes adoption compelling.

- **Batch size modulates the benefit.** The gain is largest at small batch sizes (Figure 19), so if training is batch-size-constrained by hardware memory, MARS provides a larger relative improvement than if large-batch distributed training is already feasible.

- **The MARS-approx version is the practical default.** The exact version provides marginal additional improvement (~0.005–0.01 lower loss, Figures 4–5) at the cost of either double backward passes or storing an additional data batch, which is rarely worthwhile. The approximate version's gradient buffer (~4 bytes per parameter) is the only persistent overhead.

- **γ = 0.025 transferred across all tested GPT-2 scales without re-tuning** (Table 12), and the optimal γ on CIFAR (Appendix E.4) was also 0.025. This suggests some transferability, but the paper provides no systematic evidence on how γ needs change across architectures or domains. Until that evidence exists, a small γ sweep for each new application is prudent.
