Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (The Peterman Post founder, on engineering effectiveness and career growth after big-tech layoffs):

**Run towards the fire**

Fewer engineers means more fires and more unsolved problems accumulating across the org. The natural instinct is to avoid them — but Ryan frames these as the highest-leverage opportunities you'll have. "Identifying problems is a good start. Taking the initiative to report bugs or escalate incidents is helpful," but the career-defining move is being the person who *fixes* it. If you consistently do that, "you'll develop a reputation that will grow your career."

The deeper reason this works is that solving ambiguous problems is precisely the skill that separates junior from senior engineers. Juniors thrive on well-scoped tasks; seniors drive resolution for unclear problem areas "without any task-level guidance." Every abandoned fire is therefore a free growth vector toward senior-level scope. You don't need permission to get senior-level reps — the problems are sitting there waiting.

**Be scrappy**

When peers you depended on are no longer at the company, your project's dependencies don't disappear — they just become ownerless. You have two choices: ping the relevant team and ask when they'll staff that work with someone new (adding weeks to your timeline, assuming they ever get to it), or "dive in and do it yourself" if the ask is simple enough.

Ryan's definition of **scrappy**: "Own your project outcomes and do whatever it takes to make progress." If that means running your own data analysis, spelunking unfamiliar codebases, or doing light data engineering — so be it. The counterintuitive insight is that doing it yourself is often *more* efficient than delegation for simple tasks, because "delegation comes with the cost of communicating the work and waiting for others to pick it up." The round-trip tax — explaining the ask, waiting for acknowledgment, waiting for a staffing decision, waiting for execution — can dwarf the cost of just learning enough to ship it.

Over time, this scrappiness compounds into a personal acceleration. You catalog more systems, build more cross-domain muscle, and "can avoid the round trips in waiting for others to do things for you." You become a faster, less bottlenecked engineer.

**Prioritize ruthlessly**

With less bandwidth, the bar for what gets staffed must rise. "We must only take on work with the highest impact and be clear on what we can't staff." For roadmapping, that means determining the return on engineering time *before* committing — a practice that sounds obvious but was easy to skip "when there are plenty of engineers," when teams took on "nice-to-haves without thinking twice."

The harder discipline is *explicitly dropping* work that doesn't clear the bar, rather than leaving it in "the purgatory of 'eventually.'" Ryan gives a concrete example: his team once waited months for something from a partner team. The problem wasn't that the project was deprioritized (it wasn't a high priority, and that was fine). The real cost was the communication waste on both sides — "every update from their side was a non-update," and his team kept checking in and advocating for work that was never going to get staffed. The takeaway: if you're not going to do it, say so. Leaving people invested in a zombie project burns coordination cycles across teams.

He closes on a note of qualified optimism. "Although the cloud of layoffs hangs over us, there is a silver lining. Problems for the company can be opportunities for the individual. Those who solve these problems will have outsized impact and grow a lot."
