# BaichuanSEED: Sharing the Potential of ExtensivE Data Collection and Deduplication by Introducing a Competitive Large Language Model Baseline

**ArXiv:** [2408.15079](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15079)

## 🎯 Pitch

This technical report introduces **BaichuanSEED**, a 7B-parameter large language model trained from scratch on 3T tokens to serve as a transparent, competitive baseline that explores the limits of model capability achievable through broad data collection and reweighting alone—deliberately avoiding fine-grained data selection, synthetic data, and downstream benchmark optimization.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This technical report introduces **BaichuanSEED**, a 7B-parameter large language model trained from scratch on 3T tokens to serve as a transparent, competitive baseline that explores the limits of model capability achievable through broad data collection and reweighting alone—deliberately avoiding fine-grained data selection, synthetic data, and downstream benchmark optimization. The authors open-source a universally applicable data processing pipeline consisting of two stages: **broad collection to scale up** (gathering diverse web pages, books, papers, and code from trusted public sources) and **reweighting to improve quality** (a global multi-granularity deduplication strategy that filters 88% of CommonCrawl tokens through document-level and sentence-level deduplication, followed by a fixed data mixture that assigns 25% weight to knowledge-intensive data). BaichuanSEED achieves performance comparable to cutting-edge commercial models like Qwen1.5 and Llama3 on comprehensive benchmarks—for instance, reaching 60.25% on MMLU versus Qwen1.5-7B's 62.19%—despite using only one-fifth of Llama3's training tokens, while establishing through controlled continued pre-training experiments that the model retains substantial untapped potential on downstream tasks like mathematics (a 65.6% improvement on MATH when raising math-data proportion to 10%) without compromising general capabilities.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: The Opacity of Pretraining Data as a Bottleneck for LLM Research

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is that **the composition and processing of pretraining datasets—arguably the most critical determinant of LLM capabilities—are treated as trade secrets by the vast majority of commercial models**. The authors state this explicitly in the abstract:

> "The general capabilities of Large Language Models (LLM) highly rely on the composition and selection on extensive pretraining datasets, treated as commercial secrets by several institutions."

This opacity creates a significant bottleneck for the research community. If the field cannot inspect, reproduce, or systematically study the data processing decisions that lead to strong model performance, progress in understanding *why* certain datasets produce better models becomes heavily constrained. Researchers are left guessing at the recipes that commercial entities have refined through expensive trial and error, unable to isolate the effects of different data curation strategies from other confounding variables like model architecture, training hyperparameters, and downstream optimization tricks.

The problem manifests in several interconnected ways that the paper identifies throughout Section 1 and the introduction to Section 3:

**First, the cost of exploration is prohibitive.** Pre-training a foundation model is described as "costly, typically requiring training on terabytes of tokens over millions of GPU hours, making the process expensive and difficult to reproduce." Even well-resourced academic labs and smaller companies cannot easily run the ablation studies needed to understand the impact of data processing choices—experiments like "what happens if we deduplicate at the sentence level globally versus within-batch?" or "what is the optimal proportion of knowledge-intensive data in a pre-training mix?" This means that knowledge about effective data processing accumulates slowly and predominantly within a handful of organizations that can afford large-scale trial and error.

**Second, existing open-source models that release weights often do not release corresponding data processing details.** The paper explicitly calls this out:

> "even many open-source models that have released checkpoints do not disclose the details, hindering research advancement in this area."

This is a crucial distinction: a model checkpoint is not a recipe. Knowing that a model was trained on "web pages, books, and code" tells you almost nothing about the decisions that actually mattered—how was deduplication performed? What was the data mixture? How were low-quality documents identified and filtered? Which sources were excluded and why? Without these details, the checkpoint becomes a black box that can be evaluated but not meaningfully replicated or systematically varied.

**Third, benchmark over-optimization masks true capabilities.** The paper argues that many commercial models "over-optimize to excel in specific benchmarks, thus masking their true capabilities." When a model's evaluation scores reflect contamination from training data rather than genuine generalization, the community cannot reliably assess which data processing strategies produce robust capabilities versus brittle memorization. The benchmark itself becomes a moving target, and reported numbers lose their meaning as comparative signals.

### Why This Problem Matters

The significance of this data opacity problem spans both practical and scientific dimensions.

**Practical significance: the cost of pretraining is dominated by data decisions, not architecture.** The paper implicitly argues—and the broader literature increasingly demonstrates—that at a given model scale, the quality of pretraining data is the primary differentiator of downstream performance, not architectural innovations or training hyperparameters. If this is true, then the field's inability to systematically study and share data processing methodology means that the single most impactful lever for improving LLMs is also the least transparent. Organizations are spending millions of GPU-hours on pretraining while potentially making suboptimal data decisions that could be corrected if the community had better shared knowledge.

The problem is compounded by the fact that data processing is fundamentally different from other aspects of LLM development. Architecture choices (number of layers, attention mechanism, activation function) are well-documented and easily shared—they can be described in a few sentences. Data processing, by contrast, involves pipelines that span scraping, parsing, filtering, deduplication, mixing, and scheduling across potentially billions of documents. The decisions at each stage are numerous, interdependent, and their effects can only be observed after full-scale training. This makes data processing both the hardest aspect to get right and the hardest to document and reproduce.

**Scientific significance: without transparent baselines, we cannot evaluate progress.** The paper makes an argument that is central to its motivation:

> "We believe that training a transparent LLM without any specific optimization, and publicly sharing all its details will be beneficial for the community to understand the potential of data."

This is not merely a claim about BaichuanSEED—it is a methodological argument about how the field should evaluate new techniques. When a paper proposes a novel data selection algorithm, a new deduplication method, or a different data mixture, how should we assess whether it genuinely improves over existing practice? The answer requires a **known, transparent baseline** whose training process is fully documented. Without such baselines, every comparison is confounded: if a new method beats some commercial model, we do not know whether the gain comes from the method itself or from differences in hidden data processing that the commercial model's authors kept secret.

The paper explicitly distinguishes BaichuanSEED from "SOTA models" in its introduction:

> "BaichuanSEED is not a SOTA model. What really matters to us is that understanding of a pure model helps in recognizing its actual strengths and weaknesses, the preliminary for evaluating the real impact of various optimization strategies."

This framing is crucial: the model is not positioned as a performance champion competing for leaderboard ranks. It is positioned as a **scientific instrument**—a known, reproducible reference point against which future innovations can be measured. This is analogous to the role that well-characterized baselines play in other fields of machine learning, where standardized training pipelines (e.g., ImageNet training recipes) enable the community to attribute improvements to specific algorithmic innovations rather than training pipeline differences.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies several limitations in prior work on data processing for LLMs, which motivate its specific focus on collection and reweighting over fine-grained selection.

**1. Data selection methods are computationally expensive and hard to scale.** The paper acknowledges that "data selection shows considerable potential" (Section 1) and cites several approaches: heuristic rule-based selection (Sun et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024a), model-based selection (Xie et al., 2023; Engstrom et al., 2024), and document relevance detection (Chen et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2024). However, it identifies a critical limitation:

> "the high computational cost makes it challenging to scale up."

What does this mean concretely? Model-based data selection typically requires training a quality classifier—often a smaller model fine-tuned on human-annotated data—and then running inference on the entire pretraining corpus to assign quality scores. For a corpus of multiple terabytes, this inference cost can be substantial, potentially rivalling the cost of pretraining itself on smaller-scale experiments. Heuristic rule-based selection requires manual curation of rules (e.g., "filter documents with fewer than N characters," "remove documents with high repetition rates") that are domain-specific, brittle, and require extensive human effort to develop and maintain across languages and document types.

The paper's position is that there may be substantial gains achievable through **simpler, more scalable approaches** that focus on what data to include and how much to sample from each source, rather than fine-grained quality filtering of individual documents. This is the "collection to scale up" and "reweighting to improve quality" distinction that structures the entire paper.

**2. Existing open models that emphasize transparency still have gaps.** The paper references several models that have made strides toward openness, including MAP-Neo (Zhang et al., 2024), OLMo (Groeneveld et al., 2024), Pythia (Biderman et al., 2023), and Amber (Liu et al., 2023). While these models release checkpoints and some training details, the paper implicitly positions itself as going further on the specific dimension of **documenting the data processing pipeline in reproducible detail**. For instance, OLMo releases its Dolma dataset (Soldaini et al., 2024), which is a major contribution to transparency, but the distinguishing claim of BaichuanSEED is the systematic study of what can be achieved *without* the selective curation that characterizes many of these efforts.

The paper draws particular attention to models like Llama3 and Qwen1.5 as baselines that represent the state of commercial practice, achieving strong overall performance but disclosing limited detail about data processing. By comparing against these models—which the paper acknowledges as strong performers—BaichuanSEED aims to demonstrate that a transparent pipeline can produce competitive results, validating the pipeline's effectiveness while also identifying where the pipeline's deliberate simplicity leaves room for future optimization.

**3. Synthetic data introduces confounding variables, making baseline comparison difficult.** The paper takes a deliberate stance against synthetic data in pretraining, citing evidence that it creates complex tradeoffs:

> "some studies demonstrate that while synthetic data can improve model performance on coding and mathematics benchmarks, it may lead to a loss of instruction following ability after SFT, making the impact of synthetic data unclear and introducing additional confounding variables."

This is a methodological argument: if the goal is to understand what data processing pipeline produces the best foundation model, introducing synthetic data—which is itself generated by another LLM with its own unknown biases and quality characteristics—makes it impossible to attribute outcomes to the pipeline versus the synthetic data generation process. The paper's commitment to training without synthetic data is therefore not a claim that synthetic data is harmful (the paper acknowledges it is "widely used in SFT to enhance the difficulty, complexity, and diversity of constructed instructions") but rather a commitment to **experimental purity** that enables clean attribution of results to the data processing pipeline.

**4. The field lacks a clear "lower bound" for what data collection and deduplication alone can achieve.** Before this paper, it was difficult to answer a deceptively simple question: *If you collect as much public data as possible, deduplicate it aggressively, and set reasonable sampling weights across domains, how good a model can you train—without any fancy selection, synthetic data, or downstream optimization?* The answer to this question matters because it establishes the baseline from which the benefits of more sophisticated techniques should be measured. If a pipeline with broad collection and deduplication already achieves 85% of the performance of a heavily curated pipeline, then the returns to curation may be diminishing or may only manifest in specific domains, which would change how practitioners allocate their data engineering effort. Conversely, if the gap is large, it validates the intensive curation efforts that many organizations pursue.

### How This Paper Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

The paper frames its contribution not as a new architecture, training algorithm, or evaluation benchmark, but as a **reference implementation of a data processing philosophy** that prioritizes scalability and transparency. The key dimensions of its positioning are:

**1. A two-stage data processing paradigm: scale up, then reweight.** The paper explicitly follows Falcon (Penedo et al., 2023) in defining its pipeline as two sequential stages: broad collection to maximize diversity and coverage, followed by reweighting to adjust sampling probabilities. Within this framework, the paper makes a deliberate choice:

> "we avoid fine-grained data selection intentionally, not to undermine its benefits but to focus more on exploring the achievable limit through data collection and reweighting."

This is a methodological scoping decision. The paper is not arguing that data selection is useless—it explicitly acknowledges its "considerable potential." Instead, it is carving out a specific region of the design space: what happens when you invest all your data processing effort in the upstream stages (collection and reweighting) rather than the downstream stage (selection)? This is analogous to how some ML papers study the effect of data augmentation alone, holding architecture constant, to isolate its contribution.

**2. A "pure" baseline that refuses to optimize for specific downstream tasks.** This is perhaps the paper's most distinctive positioning choice. The authors state:

> "BaichuanSEED tries to be a completely pure model, without training on synthetic data or up-sampling any downstream task data in pre-training, and utilizing general instructions during SFT."

The word "pure" recurs throughout the paper and serves as a philosophical commitment. A pure model is one whose capabilities are a direct reflection of the pretraining data's inherent quality and distribution, unadulterated by post-hoc interventions that might boost benchmark scores but mask underlying weaknesses or create brittleness. This purity has a specific scientific purpose: it makes the model **consistent and predictable** during training (Section 5.1), meaning that performance on evaluation benchmarks tracks smoothly with training progress and can be forecast from early checkpoints. This is a property that practitioners value because it enables efficient resource allocation and early detection of training failures, but it is also a signal that the model is not being "hacked" to perform well on benchmarks through last-minute data injections or annealing on benchmark-like data.

**3. An invitation to the community to build on a known foundation.** The paper's conclusion frames BaichuanSEED as an ongoing project:

> "We leave integrating these optimization methods into our model BaichuanSEED to construct a highly robust LLM for future work."

This is different from the typical conclusion of a technical report that presents a final, optimized model. The authors are explicitly treating BaichuanSEED as a **platform**—a known, reproducible starting point that future work can modify with specific optimization techniques (math data upsampling, curriculum learning, synthetic data injection) to study their effects in isolation. The fact that the paper already includes preliminary experiments along these lines (Section 6.2, which shows that increasing math data to 10% during continued pretraining yields a 65.6% improvement on MATH without harming MMLU) proves that the platform is viable: there is genuine headroom for improvement that can be systematically studied.

**4. A contrast with over-optimized commercial models.** The paper draws an implicit contrast with a class of models that it critiques without naming individually. The concern is that some commercial models may:

- Release inflated benchmark numbers achieved through contamination or targeted optimization rather than genuine capability improvements;
- Conceal the data processing steps that would allow the community to assess whether reported gains are robust;
- Overfit to evaluation distributions during SFT in ways that degrade other important properties (e.g., instruction following, as the paper notes about synthetic data risks).

By positioning BaichuanSEED as a transparent, unoptimized reference point, the paper offers the community a tool for **calibrating** claims made by other model releases. If a new model claims a 5% improvement on MMLU over BaichuanSEED, and it also discloses its data processing pipeline, researchers can attribute that improvement to specific differences in approach rather than unknown confounding factors.

### Summary of the Gap and the Paper's Response

The field faces a situation where pretraining data processing is simultaneously the most impactful and least transparent aspect of LLM development. Commercial models guard their pipelines as trade secrets; even open-weight releases often omit critical details; and the cost of large-scale training makes systematic experimentation prohibitive. Methods that are known to help (data selection, synthetic data, downstream upsampling) introduce confounding variables that obscure our understanding of what the underlying data processing pipeline contributes on its own.

BaichuanSEED responds to this gap by providing a fully documented, deliberately unoptimized baseline that isolates the contributions of two scalable strategies: broad collection and multi-granularity deduplication with domain-aware reweighting. The model is explicitly not a claim to SOTA performance; it is a claim to **scientific utility**—a known reference point that enables the community to measure the real impact of various optimization strategies against a transparent foundation. The paper's repeated emphasis on consistency, predictability, and purity reveals its deeper goal: to demonstrate that a simple, well-documented data pipeline can produce a competitive model whose behavior is sufficiently well-behaved (in the scaling sense) to serve as a platform for future systematic research.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper develops a **data processing pipeline**—not a model architecture innovation—that determines what text a language model sees during pretraining and how often it sees each piece. The system solves the problem of how to construct a diverse, high-quality pretraining corpus without resorting to expensive fine-grained filtering or synthetic data generation, establishing a transparent baseline that reveals what model capabilities are achievable through the upstream stages of collection and deduplication alone. The solution takes the shape of a two-stage pipeline: first, **broad collection** from trusted public sources to maximize diversity and scale (producing over 10T tokens of raw data), then **global multi-granularity deduplication and reweighting** to compress this corpus into a 3T-token training mixture where each document's sampling probability reflects its domain and quality rather than its raw frequency.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The data processing pipeline has five major stages through which information flows sequentially, followed by a standard pretraining and supervised fine-tuning stage:

1.  **Collection and Extraction Pipeline** — A set of web scrapers, document parsers, and format converters that continuously harvest text from four categories of sources: CommonCrawl web pages, knowledge-intensive data (books, academic papers, technical reports), code repositories, and vertical domain data. This stage produces raw text documents in multiple languages with metadata about source type and domain.

2.  **Global Multi-Granularity Deduplication Engine** — A series of deduplication operators that filter documents and sentences with excessively high frequency across the entire corpus, not just within individual batches. It operates at the document level (exact MD5 matching and MinHash near-duplicate clustering), the sentence level (MinHash-based detection of frequently recurring template sentences across documents), and includes a combined rule-based and model-based PII/harmful content filter. This stage reduces the CommonCrawl component by 88% in token count.

3.  **Data Mixture Computation** — A configuration step that assigns fixed sampling weights to each domain (Chinese web, English web, Chinese knowledge-intensive data, English knowledge-intensive data, code, others) based on small-scale ablation experiments that optimize perplexity on a held-out corpus. These weights determine the probability that a token sampled during pretraining comes from each domain.

4.  **Pretraining** — A standard Transformer decoder trained from scratch on 3T tokens sampled according to the data mixture, using the same architecture as Baichuan2 with 32 layers, 32 attention heads, hidden size 4096, and intermediate size 11008, training at a sequence length of 16K with a cosine learning rate schedule from 3e-4 to 3e-5.

5.  **Supervised Fine-Tuning** — A 450K-example instruction dataset (self-constructed, covering mathematics, reasoning, coding, creative writing, and multi-turn dialogue) used to train the pretrained model for 6 epochs at constant learning rate 2e-5 with sample packing, producing the final BaichuanSEED-SFT chat model.

Information flows linearly: raw web/text sources → extraction pipeline → raw documents → deduplication + filtering → deduplicated documents with quality scores → data mixture assignment → tokenized training data → pretraining → base model → SFT → chat model. The key design decision is that **no fine-grained selection or synthetic data generation is inserted between deduplication and pretraining**; the only knob after deduplication is the domain-level sampling weight.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the collection and extraction stage** (Section 3.1.1), because the composition of the raw corpus—where the data comes from, what formats it covers, and how it is parsed—determines everything downstream. Understanding what is *available* to the deduplication and reweighting stages is prerequisite.

- **Second, the global multi-granularity deduplication strategy** (Section 3.1.2, Deduplication), because this is the paper's primary technical contribution to data processing and the mechanism that removes 88% of CommonCrawl tokens. We need to understand how document-level and sentence-level deduplication differ in their targets and mechanisms, and why global scope (across all batches) matters.

- **Third, the reweighting stage—data mixture determination** (Section 3.1.2, Data Mixture), because once the corpus is deduplicated, the remaining design choice is how to sample from it. This connects directly to the knowledge-intensive data experiments.

- **Fourth, the pretraining configuration** (Section 3.2), covering architecture, hyperparameters, and training schedule, to understand how the processed data is consumed.

- **Fifth, the SFT stage** (Section 4), covering instruction data construction and training details, since supervised fine-tuning is what converts the base model into an evaluable chat model and its design choices interact with the pretraining data characteristics.

- **Sixth, the experimental philosophy of purity** (woven throughout but crystallized in Sections 3.1.3 and 6), because many of the paper's design choices—no synthetic data, no downstream task upsampling, no benchmark-specific optimization—are motivated by a methodological commitment rather than technical necessity, and understanding this commitment is essential to interpreting the results.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **data pipeline engineering and empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that broad collection followed by global multi-granularity deduplication and domain-level reweighting can produce a pretraining corpus that yields competitive model performance without the computational expense and confounding variables introduced by fine-grained data selection, synthetic data, or downstream task optimization.

#### 3.4.1 Collection and Extraction Pipeline

The collection stage aims to gather all accessible data from trusted public sources, maximizing diversity across topics, linguistic styles, and formats while maintaining a baseline quality floor through source selection rather than document-level filtering.

**Web pages.** The paper collects "94 publicly available batches of CommonCrawl spanning the past decade." This temporal breadth is intentional: it exposes the model to the evolution of web language, emerging topics, and shifting terminology over time—a form of temporal data augmentation that would be absent from a single-snapshot crawl. The extraction pipeline chooses WARC format over WET, balancing "effectiveness and cost"—WARC preserves more structural information (HTML tags, metadata) than WET (which is plaintext only), enabling better downstream parsing at the cost of larger storage and processing overhead.

From the WARC files, the paper uses Trafilatura (Barbaresi, 2021), an open-source Python package, for text extraction, citing "performance and cost among open-source web page parsing tools" as the selection criterion. This is a practical engineering tradeoff: many extraction tools exist (Boilerpipe, readability, newspaper3k), but Trafilatura is specifically designed for web scraping at scale and produces cleaner text from heterogeneous HTML structures than simpler alternatives. Language identification uses CLD3 (Compact Language Detector v3), a neural network-based language detector from Google that operates at high throughput and supports 107 languages, necessary for distinguishing Chinese and English content in a bilingual corpus.

**Knowledge-intensive data (KID).** The distinguishing feature of this collection category is that the data originates from documents with high production costs—"documents with high production costs typically have higher quality, which may generally curated after a rigorous process of human inspection and correction." This includes books, academic papers, and technical reports. The paper documents an important distributional property of KID versus web pages (Figure 1):

- Web pages: 30% STEM, 14.9% Humanities, 36.5% Social Science, 18.6% Other
- Books: 35.6% STEM, 31.8% Humanities, 30.9% Social Science, 1.7% Other
- Papers: 71.4% STEM, 8.6% Humanities, 19.1% Social Science, 0.9% Other

This distributional skew matters because web pages—which dominate most pretraining corpora by volume—are heavily tilted toward social science and humanities content. The deliberate inclusion of books and papers rebalances the domain distribution toward STEM, which the paper later shows (Section 6.2) is critical for mathematical reasoning capabilities.

Processing KID is technically challenging because the source format is typically PDF. The paper employs "a hybrid approach, utilizing both the open-source tool Nougat (Blecher et al., 2023) and business services"—where Nougat is a neural network-based OCR system specifically designed for academic documents that can recognize mathematical formulas and tables, and the business services provide complementary coverage for non-academic PDFs. The key requirement is to "maintain the original reading order and ensure the recognition and extraction accuracy of text, tables, and formulas." Tables and formulas are output in LaTeX format, which is important because it preserves the structured semantics of mathematical notation as a formal language that the Transformer can learn to parse, rather than converting formulas to ambiguous plaintext.

**Code data.** Code data comes from three sources: StarCoder (Li et al., 2023b), GitHub repositories filtered by license permissions, and StackExchange. The data is organized at two granularities:

- **Document-level**: individual code files treated as independent documents. MinHash deduplication is applied at the file level, then heuristic rules filter low-quality code (the specific heuristics are not detailed, but typical ones include minimum length thresholds, removal of auto-generated files, and syntax validity checks).
- **Repository-level**: "following Deepseek-coder (Guo et al., 2024), we calculate the dependencies among files within the same repository, perform topological sorting and concatenate the files." This means that related source files in a single GitHub repository are assembled into a coherent document that preserves the import/dependency structure, making the code more self-contained and the relationships between files learnable by the model.

Each repository is treated as "a single sample and perform Minhash deduplication to preserve repository integrity." Repository-level data constitutes about 62% of the total 750B code tokens. The paper reports a positive finding from ablation: "Pivot experiments demonstrate that the mixture of repository-level and document-level data yields better model performance compared to using either one alone" (Section 3.1.1). This is an important empirical result because it validates the intuition that code understanding benefits from seeing both isolated snippets (which teach syntax and common patterns) and structured multi-file projects (which teach architecture, dependency management, and large-scale code organization).

Additionally, the paper "prioritize sampling Github repositories with top stars," which introduces a quality signal: higher-starred repositories are more likely to contain well-structured, well-documented code that follows best practices. This is a lightweight form of heuristic quality filtering that avoids the computational cost of model-based quality assessment.

**Continuous collection.** The paper constructs "a scraping and extracting pipeline... to fetch up-to-date data automatically, enhancing the generalizability of our model in practice" (Section 3.1.1). This is not a one-time collection but an ongoing process designed for model iterations, anticipating that future versions of BaichuanSEED will incorporate newer data.

#### 3.4.2 Global Multi-Granularity Deduplication

Deduplication is "the first step of reweighting" and the paper's primary mechanism for improving data quality without fine-grained filtering. The insight driving this design is that "data with higher frequency is more likely tend to lead to a negative impact on foundation models"—repeated text not only wastes compute (the model sees the same information multiple times instead of learning from diverse content) but also empirically causes harm, potentially through memorization of template patterns, amplification of biases in frequently repeated passages, or degradation of the model's compression ability.

The strategy operates at two linguistic granularities and one content filter:

**Document-level deduplication, globally.** The key design choice is the word "globally." Existing works—the paper claims—"split CC into several batches, deduplicate in each batch, and finally merge." This within-batch approach means that a document appearing once per batch might survive deduplication even if it appears in every batch, because the deduplication algorithm only sees its local neighborhood. Global deduplication treats the entire corpus as a single set, ensuring that a document's survival depends on its frequency across all batches simultaneously.

The procedure has two steps:

1.  **Exact match deduplication** using the MD5 hash of each document's text as a key. Documents with identical MD5 hashes have byte-identical content and only one copy is kept. This catches literal duplicates from different URLs serving the same content.

2.  **Near-duplicate deduplication** using the MinHash algorithm (Broder, 1997). MinHash computes a fixed-size signature for each document by taking the minimum hash values of its n-gram set under multiple hash functions. Documents with high Jaccard similarity (many shared n-grams) will have similar MinHash signatures, enabling efficient clustering of near-duplicate documents. When a cluster of similar documents is identified, "we keep the document with the longest text length"—a heuristic that assumes longer documents in a near-duplicate cluster contain the most complete version of the content, with shorter ones being truncated copies, excerpts, or aggregated versions.

**Sentence-level deduplication across documents.** This is the more novel contribution. The observation is that "sentences with extremely high frequency across documents... mostly are meaningless junk data." Examples the paper provides include "website template breadcrumbs, standard article openings and closings, and quoted passages" (Section 3.1.2). These are not whole-document duplicates—each document is distinct—but they share boilerplate fragments that appear across millions of pages (e.g., "Click here to read more," "Copyright 2024 All Rights Reserved," "Share this article on Facebook").

The procedure first "split the documents into sentences, and then utilize Minhash with searched optimal hyperparameters at the sentence level across documents, making it more suitable for deduplication of short neighboring sentences." The key engineering challenge is that MinHash is designed for documents (thousands of tokens) while sentences are much shorter (tens of tokens). The paper states it "searched optimal hyperparameters" for this short-text regime, though the specific hyperparameters (number of hash functions, n-gram size) are not disclosed.

Appendix B provides case studies of removed content (Figure 6), showing four categories:

- **Advertisements** with trending long-tail keywords ("Pay 1 dollar to gain bonus discount for further lottery payment") that achieve high quality scores but are clearly spam.
- **Dissemination and interactivity text** (social sharing prompts, comment invitations) unrelated to document content.
- **Copyrights, PII, and source attribution** templates ("新华社发", "作者:记者[Interview Name] 编辑/剪辑: [Editor Name]").
- **Residual HTML formats and watermarks** ("The code has been copied to the clipboard").

The paper provides frequency and quality score metrics for each example, revealing a subtle but critical point: some harmful patterns have **high quality scores** (0.816 for the betting website sentence) because they are grammatically well-formed and contain natural language, making them invisible to simple perplexity-based or readability-based filters. This justifies sentence-level deduplication as a complement to quality scoring rather than a replacement.

**PII and harmful content filtering.** This stage uses "a combined approach of heuristic rules and model-based methods":
- Heuristic rules: "fixed site templates, URL blacklists, regular expressions, word count and repeated n-gram character ratios."
- Model-based: a FastText classifier (Joulin et al., 2017) trained via iterative distillation. The process seeds training data with GPT-4 and human annotations of harmful content, trains a FastText model to classify documents, and then "hard samples are mined to iterative improve the previous model." This is a standard active learning loop where the model's uncertain or misclassified examples are reviewed and added to the training set. The output is a probability score that "data points classified as negative is taken as a proxy of quality scores."

**Ablation evidence.** A 2B-parameter model was used for deduplication ablation (Table 1, Figure 2):

| Strategy | Chinese Loss | English Loss |
|---|---|---|
| Without deduplication | 3.136 | 2.893 |
| With document-level dedup | 3.083 | 2.877 |
| With multi-level dedup | 3.006 | 2.874 |

The "multi-level" strategy (document + sentence + PII filtering) achieves the lowest loss on both Chinese (-4.15%) and English (-0.66%) test sets compared to no deduplication. Notably, Figure 2 shows that deduplication **increases** training loss: the model without deduplication achieves lower training loss at each step because it sees more repetitive (and thus easier-to-predict) text. The test loss improvement despite worse training loss is a classic regularization effect: deduplication removes spurious correlations (memorized template patterns) that improve training metrics but hurt generalization.

The paper interprets this as evidence that "our deduplication strategy leads to better diversity, making the prediction of the foundation model harder in the pre-training stage"—the model is forced to learn more robust linguistic patterns because it cannot rely on memorizing frequently repeated boilerplate.

**Scale impact.** The global multi-granularity strategy "filter 88% of the tokens of our CommonCrawl (CC) dataset." This means that out of the original CommonCrawl collection, only 12% of tokens survive deduplication. This is a massive reduction and represents one of the most aggressive deduplication ratios documented in the open-source LLM literature. The fact that downstream performance remains competitive (Section 5) validates that the removed 88% was genuinely redundant or harmful, not just diverse content being mistakenly filtered.

#### 3.4.3 Data Mixture Configuration

After deduplication determines *whether* a document is included, the data mixture determines *how often* the model sees documents from each domain during pretraining. The paper treats this as a hyperparameter optimization problem: find the sampling weights that minimize test-set perplexity, subject to the constraint that weights sum to 1.

The weighting is a multinomial distribution over six domain categories (Table 2):

| Domain | Token Count | Avg Tokens/Doc | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Web / Chinese | 405B | 361 | 13.5% |
| Web / English | 945B | 752 | 31.5% |
| KID / Chinese | 300B | 10,362 | 10% |
| KID / English | 450B | 16,172 | 15% |
| Code | 750B | 3,157 | 25% |
| Others | 150B | 409 | 5% |
| **Total** | **3000B** | **4,558** | **100%** |

Several properties of this mixture deserve attention:

**Total training tokens: 3T.** This is the number of tokens actually consumed during pretraining. The weight percentages mean that each training batch is composed by sampling from each domain proportionally to its weight, with the specific documents within each domain being drawn uniformly (or according to the deduplication-based sampling, which in effect is uniform over the surviving documents).

**English dominates Chinese, but Chinese KID has disproportionate weight.** English web pages (31.5%) + English KID (15%) = 46.5% of tokens, while Chinese web pages (13.5%) + Chinese KID (10%) = 23.5%. English receives roughly 2× the weight of Chinese in the mixture. However, looking at the Avg Tokens/Doc column reveals that Chinese KID documents are 10,362 tokens on average—shorter than English KID (16,172)—which means that at the 10% sampling weight, the model sees more Chinese KID documents per billion tokens than the raw ratio would suggest, giving Chinese knowledge-intensive content disproportionately high representation relative to its token count.

**Code at 25% is a substantial proportion.** This matches the trend in recent models (DeepSeek-Coder, CodeLlama, StarCoder) that allocate significant pretraining budget to code data. The paper argues that code data contains "rich logical reasoning" that transfers to non-code tasks, and the mixture includes both document-level and repository-level code to capture different aspects of programming knowledge.

**The weight column, not the token count column, determines sampling.** This is a crucial distinction. The "Others" category has 150B tokens available but is sampled at only 5%. During the full 3T-token training run, the model sees approximately 150B tokens from "Others" (5% of 3T), meaning it makes roughly one pass over the "Others" data. Meanwhile, it sees 45% of tokens from web sources (405B Chinese + 945B English available, but sampled at 13.5% + 31.5% of 3T = 405B + 945B = 1,350B tokens), meaning it also makes roughly one pass over the web data. The KID categories are sampled at 25% of 3T = 750B tokens total, while 750B tokens are available (300B + 450B), meaning the KID data is seen exactly once on average. This suggests the weights were calibrated to achieve roughly single-epoch exposure across most categories, avoiding both underfitting (multiple epochs on a small subset) and overfitting (single epoch on a massive pool where most documents are never seen).

**Determining the KID proportion.** The paper reports experiments on a 2B model that establish that 25% KID is optimal (Section 6.1, Table 5):

| Training from scratch | Continued pretraining |
|---|---|
| 0% KID: PPL 14.24 | 0% KID: PPL 12.21 |
| 25% KID: PPL 13.68 | 25% KID: PPL 11.73 |
| 50% KID: PPL 13.86 | 50% KID: PPL 11.80 |

The 25% KID condition achieves the lowest perplexity in both training regimes. The paper interprets this as "the inclusion of KID accelerates the model's acquisition of language modeling capabilities." At 50% KID, perplexity degrades slightly, likely because the reduced representation of web data (which carries more diverse linguistic patterns and world knowledge) outweighs the benefit of additional high-density content.

This ablation is important methodologically: it demonstrates that the paper's final mixture (which uses 10% Chinese KID + 15% English KID = 25% total KID) is not arbitrary but grounded in empirical optimization at a smaller scale, following the principle that mixture proportions found optimal at 2B scale are likely near-optimal at 7B scale.

#### 3.4.4 Pretraining Configuration

**Model architecture.** BaichuanSEED uses a Transformer decoder stack "similar to our previous version model Baichuan2 (Yang et al., 2023) and Llama (Touvron et al., 2023a,b)." The specific architectural parameters are:

- 32 layers
- 32 attention heads
- Hidden dimension size: 4,096
- Feed-forward layer size: 11,088
- Activation function: SwiGLU (Shazeer, 2020)
- Normalization: RMSNorm (Zhang and Sennrich, 2019)
- Position encoding: Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE) (Su et al., 2024)

These choices represent a standard, well-established architecture for 7B-scale models. SwiGLU is a gated variant of the GLU activation that has been shown to outperform ReLU and GELU in Transformer feed-forward layers. RMSNorm removes the mean-centering operation from LayerNorm while retaining the scaling, reducing computational overhead. RoPE encodes position information through rotation matrices applied to query and key vectors, enabling the model to learn relative position relationships and generalizing to sequence lengths beyond those seen during training.

**Training hyperparameters.** The pretraining setup uses:

- Training tokens: 3 trillion (3T)
- Sequence length: 16,384 (16K) tokens
- Global batch size: 224 sequences
- Learning rate schedule: cosine decay
- Initial learning rate: 3e-4
- Final learning rate: 3e-5

The choice of 16K sequence length is notable: it is 4× the 4K context of Llama 1 and 2× the 8K of many earlier models, enabling the model to learn long-range dependencies from the pretraining stage rather than requiring post-hoc context extension techniques. The cosine schedule smoothly decays the learning rate from 3e-4 to 3e-5 over the full 3T tokens, which is standard practice for LLM pretraining.

The global batch size of 224 sequences at 16K tokens each means each optimizer step processes 224 × 16,384 = 3,670,016 tokens. Over 3T total tokens, this implies approximately 3T / 3.67M ≈ 817,000 training steps. The paper's Figure 3 and Figure 4 use "Training Token Amount" as the x-axis, with checkpoints evaluated at 0.5T, 1.0T, 1.5T, 2.0T, 2.5T, and 3.0T tokens.

**No synthetic data.** Section 3.1.3 explicitly states: "we did not incorporate synthetic data when training BaichuanSEED." The rationale is methodological: "synthetic data... may lead to a loss of instruction following ability after SFT, making the impact of synthetic data unclear and introducing additional confounding variables." The paper treats synthetic data as a separate optimization that should be studied as a controlled variable on top of the transparent baseline, not conflated with the data processing pipeline.

**No downstream task upsampling.** The paper "make effort to ensure that the evaluation datasets are excluded from the training data of BaichuanSEED" and does not increase the sampling weight of any downstream task-relevant data (e.g., math, code) during pretraining. The 25% code weight is fixed and not increased for coding benchmarks; the math content within KID is whatever occurs naturally in the collected papers and books, without deliberate upsampling.

**Fine-grained data classification for future work.** Although not used in pretraining, the paper describes a classification system that "directly prompt GPT-4 to obtain a set of knowledge category labels of training data and train a model to classify the full dataset, enhancing our fine-grained understanding" (Section 3.1.3). This classifier is used in the continued pretraining experiments (Section 6.2) to identify mathematics-related data for targeted upsampling, demonstrating the model's potential for task-specific optimization without incorporating it into the main training run.

---

#### 3.4.5 Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

The SFT stage converts the base pretrained model into an instruction-following chat model. Importantly, the paper treats SFT as a **fixed procedure applied equally to all checkpoints**, which is what enables the consistency analysis in Section 5.1—if SFT were optimized separately for each checkpoint, comparing their downstream performance would be confounded.

**SFT data construction.** The dataset contains "approximately 450K samples" covering "mathematics, logical reasoning, coding, creative writing, brainstorming, and multi-turn dialogues." The paper describes optimization along three axes:

- **Clustering to enhance diversity:** "clustering massive data to enhance the diversity." This prevents the SFT dataset from being dominated by near-duplicate instructions that differ only in surface form, which would cause the model to overfit to specific phrasings.
- **Synthesizing to increase complexity:** "synthesizing the instructions to increase the complexity." This likely involves taking simple instructions and using an LLM (possibly GPT-4) to generate more complex multi-step variants, increasing the difficulty distribution of the training data.
- **Human annotation for quality:** "we employ human annotations for part of data to improve the quality." Human annotators likely verify correctness (especially for math and reasoning tasks) and refine instruction clarity.

**Training details.** The SFT configuration uses:

- Learning rate schedule: constant (not decaying)
- Initial learning rate: 2e-5
- Batch size: 40
- Sequence length: 16K tokens
- Number of epochs: 6
- Sample packing: enabled
- Attention acceleration: Flash Attention 2 (Dao, 2023)

Each sample includes "three parts: system, which is optimal, prompt, and answer." The system message (likely a fixed instruction like "You are a helpful assistant") is prepended to every sample.

**Why 6 epochs?** The paper explicitly addresses this unusual choice:

> "This is more than the usual number of epochs for SFT training, comparing to Llama2, which used only 2 epochs. Empirically, more epochs yields better results for small size models, especially when the amount of pre-training token is insufficient."

The explanation reveals an implicit assumption: at 3T pretraining tokens, the base model may not have fully converged, and additional SFT epochs provide continued learning on high-quality instruction data. The paper hedges this choice: "This might not be the optimal solution, as the ideal number of epochs can vary with different checkpoints. Nevertheless, this does not affect our evaluation and comparison of the alignment performance of the foundation models." The key methodological point is that **the same 6-epoch protocol is applied to all checkpoints**, so relative comparisons between checkpoints remain valid even if the absolute SFT quality could be improved by per-checkpoint tuning.

**Sample packing** concatenates multiple training examples into a single 16K-token sequence, separated by appropriate delimiters, to minimize padding waste and increase training throughput. Flash Attention 2 reduces the memory footprint of attention computation from O(N²) to a more efficient IO-aware implementation, enabling the 16K sequence length without excessive GPU memory consumption.

---

#### 3.4.6 The Philosophical Principle of Purity

Throughout the paper, a recurring methodological commitment shapes design choices that would otherwise appear arbitrary. The authors state this principle most directly in Section 5.1:

> "BaichuanSEED tries to be a completely pure model, without training on synthetic data or up-sampling any downstream task data in pre-training, and utilizing general instructions during SFT."

"Purity" in this context means that the model's capabilities are a **direct, unmediated function of the pretraining data processing pipeline**. Any capability the model exhibits—mathematical reasoning, coding, world knowledge, cross-lingual transfer—must be traceable to the content of the collected and deduplicated web pages, books, papers, and code repositories. This is in deliberate contrast to models that achieve high benchmark scores through:

- **Synthetic data injection** during pretraining (generating additional math problems, code examples, or reasoning chains using a teacher model), which introduces capabilities from the teacher model rather than the data processing pipeline.
- **Benchmark-specific upsampling** (increasing the proportion of math or code data late in training specifically to boost those benchmarks), which creates a misleading picture of the model's "natural" capabilities.
- **SFT over-optimization** (training on benchmark-like instructions or even benchmark data itself during fine-tuning), which the paper argues can "hinder the community from being aware of the foundational models' capabilities."

The paper's commitment to purity enables two specific scientific properties that it emphasizes in Section 5.1:

**Consistency:** Performance on evaluation benchmarks (MMLU, CMMLU, MBPP, GSM8K, and SFT benchmarks like FollowBench and SuperCLUE-Math6) increases **monotonically and smoothly** with pretraining tokens, as shown in Figure 3. There are no sharp jumps, regressions, or instabilities that might indicate the model is memorizing benchmark-specific patterns from contaminated data. The paper argues that this consistency "indicates that BaichuanSEED has not been specialized to optimize for a specific task or evaluation benchmark, retaining the potential to transfer to specific downstream tasks."

**Predictability:** The performance growth follows a logarithmic curve (Figure 4) that can be fit from early checkpoints (first half of training) and extrapolates accurately to later checkpoints. The paper demonstrates this on MMLU: "we take points from the first half for extrapolation and fit for the points in the latter half perfectly." This predictability is valuable for practitioners because it enables early stopping decisions and resource allocation without waiting for full training completion. More subtly, it validates the purity of the training process—if benchmark contamination or optimization tricks were present, the smooth scaling curve would likely break, since the model would "discover" the benchmark data at a specific point in training and show a discontinuous accuracy improvement.

The purity principle also explains several **negative design choices** that might otherwise seem puzzling:

- Why not use the PRM800k dataset or other process supervision data? Because that would be synthetic data from another model.
- Why not upsample math-heavy KID or include math competition problems in pretraining? Because that would be downstream task optimization.
- Why not use RLAIF or DPO after SFT? Because that would introduce reward model preferences that mask the foundation model's inherent capabilities.

The paper is not arguing these techniques are ineffective—in Section 6, it demonstrates that math data upsampling yields dramatic improvements. The argument is methodological: to understand the contribution of data collection and deduplication, you must first measure it in isolation, and then layer on additional optimizations as controlled experiments.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Global document-level deduplication with MD5 + MinHash** instead of within-batch deduplication: catches cross-batch duplicates that local deduplication misses, reducing the effective corpus size but improving test loss by removing spurious repetition-based learning signals.

- **Sentence-level MinHash deduplication across documents** instead of relying solely on document-level deduplication: removes frequently occurring boilerplate sentences (templates, sharing prompts, copyright notices) that are grammatically well-formed (high quality scores) but semantically empty, which document-level dedup cannot catch because the surrounding content differs.

- **Iterative FastText distillation for PII/harmful content filtering** instead of purely rule-based or purely human-annotated approaches: combines the scalability of automated classification with the accuracy of GPT-4 and human judgment through active learning, avoiding the precision/recall tradeoffs of static rule sets.

- **Fixed domain-level sampling weights (25% KID, 25% code)** instead of dynamic weighting or per-document quality scoring: scales efficiently (only 6 hyperparameters to tune), is validated by 2B-scale perplexity experiments, and avoids the computational cost of model-based quality assessment on every document.

- **No synthetic data, no benchmark upsampling, no downstream optimization** in pretraining: preserves "purity" of the baseline, enabling clean attribution of model capabilities to the data collection and deduplication pipeline, and establishing consistency/predictability as measurable properties of the training process.

- **6-epoch SFT with sample packing and Flash Attention 2** instead of the typical 2-3 epochs: empirically found to improve small-model performance when pretraining tokens are limited, applied uniformly to all checkpoints to preserve comparability, and computationally efficient through sequence packing and optimized attention.

- **Cosine learning rate decay from 3e-4 to 3e-5** instead of a constant or step schedule: standard practice for LLM pretraining that provides aggressive early learning followed by fine-grained convergence, with the 10:1 ratio between initial and final rates being typical for Chinchilla-style training.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Data Processing as Two Isolated, Scalable Stages Rather Than End-to-End Curation

The dominant paradigm in LLM data processing—exemplified by the meticulous filtering pipelines of Llama3 (Dubey et al., 2024), Qwen1.5 (Bai et al., 2023), and Phi (Abdin et al., 2024)—treats data quality as a document-level property to be optimized through a cascade of increasingly sophisticated filters: heuristic rules, perplexity thresholds, classifier-based quality scoring, deduplication, and topic modeling, all interleaved. The implicit assumption is that *every stage contributes incrementally to quality*, and the art lies in tuning each filter's threshold. This paper makes a fundamentally different architectural move: it partitions data processing into exactly two stages with a clean separation of concerns—**collection** (which scales up, maximizing recall) and **reweighting** (which compresses, maximizing precision)—and then deliberately *refuses* to insert fine-grained selection between them.

This is not simply an engineering choice; it is a conceptual reframing. The paper's key move is to define "reweighting" as operating exclusively at the level of **sampling probabilities across predetermined domain categories** rather than at the level of individual document inclusion/exclusion decisions. Within this framework, the global multi-granularity deduplication is the *only* mechanism that can remove a document from the corpus entirely; everything else—the 25% KID weight, the 25% code weight, the 13.5% Chinese web weight—determines how often surviving documents are seen, not whether they survive at all. This is a fundamentally different optimization landscape: instead of tuning hundreds of filtering thresholds on a per-document basis, the paper tunes 6 sampling weights via small-scale perplexity experiments (Table 5), which is several orders of magnitude cheaper computationally.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond efficiency. By collapsing the design space from per-document decisions to per-domain weights, the paper makes the data processing pipeline **inspectable and reproducible** in a way that heavily filtered corpora are not. If a document is excluded from BaichuanSEED's training data, it is because (a) it was an exact or near-duplicate of another document, (b) it contained high-frequency boilerplate sentences caught by cross-document sentence-level deduplication, or (c) it was classified as PII/harmful content. There is no opaque "quality score" threshold that might exclude valuable but unusual content. This transparency is a direct response to the paper's critique that commercial models treat data processing as "commercial secrets," and it represents a genuine methodological contribution: a data pipeline that can be fully described in a technical report and replicated by other groups without access to proprietary classifiers or human annotation workflows.

The empirical validation that this two-stage approach is competitive (Table 3: BaichuanSEED matches Qwen1.5-7B on MMLU-Pro and surpasses it on LiveBench) demonstrates that the returns to fine-grained filtering may be more modest than the field has assumed, at least for general-purpose capabilities. This is a **diagnostic finding** rather than an optimization result: it establishes a lower bound on what is achievable without selection, against which future selection methods should be benchmarked.

---

### Innovation 2: The Diagnostic Concept of Model "Purity" as a Measurable Scientific Property

The paper introduces and operationalizes a concept that does not appear in prior LLM technical reports: a **"pure" model**—one whose capabilities are a direct, unmediated function of its pretraining data distribution, uncontaminated by synthetic data, downstream task upsampling, benchmark-specific optimization, or aggressive SFT. This is not merely a rhetorical claim ("we trained a clean model") but a methodological framework with two measurable consequences: **consistency** and **predictability**, which the paper defines formally (Section 5.1) and validates empirically (Figures 3 and 4).

The field has long been aware that benchmark scores can be inflated through contamination (Zhou et al., 2023; Deng et al., 2024) and that synthetic data introduces unknown biases (Chen et al., 2024b). But prior work has treated these as *nuisance variables to be controlled* in individual experiments, not as properties of a *class of models* that can be deliberately engineered. The paper's innovation is to elevate "purity" from an experimental hygiene concern to a **first-class scientific property** that a model either possesses or does not, and whose presence or absence can be empirically detected through the smoothness of scaling curves and the predictability of future performance from early checkpoints.

The conceptual contribution here is subtle but important. By defining purity through its observable signatures—monotonic improvement across all evaluation dimensions with training tokens, logarithmic scaling that can be extrapolated from early data—the paper provides the community with **diagnostic tools** for assessing whether a model release is likely to have been over-optimized. A model whose MMLU scores jump discontinuously mid-training, or whose scaling curve deviates systematically from a logarithmic fit, is exhibiting behavior inconsistent with purity. This gives teeth to what would otherwise be an unverifiable claim about a model's training process.

The practical implication is that purity is not just an academic virtue—it has engineering value. The paper argues that pure models are better platforms for future optimization because their behavior is predictable: "having a forward-looking understanding of the model's performance trends during the early stages of training can facilitate rapid iteration and minimize unnecessary resource costs." This reframes the tradeoff between purity and performance: a model that achieves 2% higher MMLU through benchmark-aware data mixing might be a worse investment for an organization that needs to forecast its capabilities after continued pretraining on domain-specific data, because the optimization introduces non-linearities that break predictability.

The predictability demonstration on MMLU (Figure 4)—fitting a logarithmic curve to the first half of training and extrapolating to the second half—is a small experiment with large implications. It suggests that BaichuanSEED's capabilities are emerging smoothly from the statistical properties of its training data, without the phase transitions or memorization effects that would indicate contamination. This is a finding about the **nature of the model's learning process**, not just its final performance, and it is made possible by the deliberate choice to not interfere with that process through targeted data interventions.

---

### Innovation 3: Global Multi-Granularity Deduplication as a First-Class Data Quality Mechanism, Not Just Preprocessing

Deduplication in LLM pretraining has historically been treated as a necessary but unglamorous preprocessing step—something you do to avoid wasting compute on redundant data, not something you expect to *improve* model quality. The standard approach in prior work (RefinedWeb for Falcon, Dolma for OLMo, the Llama series) is document-level MinHash deduplication within batches, which catches near-duplicate documents but leaves other forms of redundancy untouched. The field's attention has been focused on more sophisticated quality interventions: model-based scoring, topic balancing, perplexity filtering.

This paper challenges that hierarchy by demonstrating that a carefully designed deduplication strategy—operating at multiple linguistic granularities and globally across the entire corpus—is itself a powerful quality improvement mechanism, not just a compute-saving measure. The key evidence is in Table 1 and Figure 2: moving from no deduplication to document-level deduplication to multi-level deduplication reduces test loss on Chinese by 4.15% and on English by 0.66%, while *simultaneously increasing training loss* (Figure 2). This inversion—worse training metrics, better generalization—is the signature of a regularization effect, and it reveals that deduplication is doing something fundamentally different from filtering out "bad" documents.

The conceptual move is to recognize that **frequency across documents is itself a negative quality signal**, independent of the content quality of any individual occurrence. The sentence-level deduplication across documents (Section 3.1.2, Appendix B) is the clearest embodiment of this principle. A sentence like "Share this article on Facebook" or "Copyright 2024 All Rights Reserved" is perfectly well-formed English—it would pass any grammar check, perplexity filter, or even most classifier-based quality assessments. But its presence in millions of documents means that a model trained on undeduplicated data will learn to predict these boilerplate patterns with high confidence, allocating modeling capacity to template memorization rather than genuine linguistic or factual learning. The paper provides concrete evidence for this mechanism through the case studies in Appendix B (Figure 6), which show removed sentences with quality scores as high as 0.816 that are nonetheless harmful training signals.

The **global** scope of the deduplication is a crucial design choice that distinguishes this work from standard practice. By treating the entire CommonCrawl collection across 94 batches as a single corpus for deduplication purposes, documents that appear once per batch (and would survive within-batch deduplication) are correctly identified as duplicates and removed. The 88% token reduction on CommonCrawl is a striking number: it implies that nearly 9 out of every 10 tokens in the raw CC collection are redundant under this deduplication regime. This is simultaneously a validation of the approach (the surviving 12% produces competitive performance) and a sobering finding about the raw web's information density.

The innovation is not the individual deduplication techniques (MD5 hashing, MinHash, sentence splitting) — these are well-established. The innovation is the **system-level design** that treats multi-granularity, global-scope deduplication as the primary quality lever rather than a preliminary step before "real" quality filtering begins. This has practical implications: organizations looking to improve their pretraining data may get more return from investing in better deduplication than in more sophisticated filtering, which is a non-obvious resource allocation recommendation.

---

### Innovation 4: The Concept of Untapped Potential as a Quantifiable, Verifiable Claim About Model Design

Most LLM technical reports conclude by touting their model's achieved performance; this paper's discussion (Section 6) is structured around demonstrating that the model *has not yet achieved its potential*. This is a distinctive epistemological move: rather than treating the model's benchmark scores as the endpoint of evaluation, the paper treats them as a **lower bound** on what the data processing pipeline can support, and then uses controlled continued pretraining experiments to quantify the headroom.

The continued pretraining experiments in Section 6.2 are the mechanism for this claim. Starting from a 1.44T-token BaichuanSEED checkpoint, the authors raise the proportion of mathematics-related data to 10% and continue training for 300B tokens. The result: a 65.6% improvement on MATH and a 7.4% improvement on GSM8K, with only a 0.3% fluctuation on MMLU (Figure 5). This is not presented as a model improvement—the authors explicitly leave integration of these optimizations to future work—but as a **diagnostic**: the base model's mathematical capabilities are not bottlenecked by architecture or scale but by the *proportion of math-relevant data in the pretraining mixture*, and adjusting that proportion yields dramatic, targeted improvements without degrading general capabilities.

This finding would be unsurprising if BaichuanSEED were trained with minimal math data. But the model's pretraining mixture already includes 25% highly-curated knowledge-intensive data (books and papers), of which a significant fraction is STEM content (71.4% of papers, 35.6% of books, per Figure 1). The fact that increasing math data from this already-substantial base to an explicitly math-focused 10% of all tokens yields large gains suggests that *natural distribution of math content in curated KID is insufficient* for strong mathematical reasoning—active upsampling or synthetic augmentation is necessary. This is an important empirical finding about the relationship between data distribution and capability emergence, and it is made possible by the paper's commitment to treating the base model as a platform rather than a final product.

The broader significance is methodological. By separating "what the model can do now" from "what the pipeline can support with targeted optimization," the paper provides a template for how technical reports can communicate model *potential* rather than just model *performance*. This matters for the research community because it enables more precise attribution of gains: if a subsequent paper takes BaichuanSEED, applies the 10% math upsampling recipe, and achieves strong MATH scores, the community can attribute the improvement to the upsampling strategy rather than to hidden differences in base model quality or data processing. The paper's explicit framing of BaichuanSEED as "not a SOTA model" but rather a transparent platform for studying optimization strategies is itself an innovation in how LLM research is communicated and evaluated.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021b), consisting of high-school competition-level mathematics problems. The authors use the specific split from Lightman et al. (2022): 12,000 training questions and 500 test questions. The benchmark covers subjects including algebra, geometry, probability, and pre-calculus, with problems requiring multi-step symbolic reasoning and producing a final answer that can be graded with exact string matching against a ground-truth solution.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments center on BaichuanSEED, a 7B-parameter Transformer decoder trained from scratch on 3T tokens of data processed through the paper's collection and deduplication pipeline. The model architecture follows Baichuan2 (Yang et al., 2023): 32 layers, 32 attention heads, hidden dimension 4,096, feed-forward dimension 11,088, with SwiGLU activation, RMSNorm normalization, and Rotary Positional Embeddings. A second, smaller variant at 2B parameters with identical architecture is used for ablation studies on deduplication strategy and knowledge-intensive data (KID) proportion.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **accuracy (%)** — the fraction of test questions for which the model's selected answer matches the ground-truth answer. For base model evaluation on multiple-choice benchmarks (MMLU, CMMLU, AGIEval, C-Eval, MMLU-Pro), accuracy is computed as the fraction of correct choices. For code generation benchmarks (MBPP, HumanEval), the pass@k metric measures whether any of k sampled solutions passes the provided unit tests; the paper reports pass@1 (for a single sample) or pass@k for specific k values. For mathematics (GSM8K, MATH), accuracy is exact match between the model's final answer and the reference solution. For commonsense reasoning (TriviaQA, HellaSwag), accuracy is the fraction of correct answers. The paper also reports **perplexity (PPL)** on held-out corpora for language modeling capability assessment during ablation studies (Table 5), computed as the exponential of the cross-entropy loss. For SFT evaluation on instruction-following benchmarks (FollowBench, SuperCLUE-Math6), task-specific metrics are used as defined by those benchmarks, with higher scores indicating better instruction-following capability. Additionally, the paper tracks **training loss** during pretraining (Figure 2) and **validation loss** as a function of training tokens (Figure 3), using these as internal diagnostics of model convergence and data quality.

- **Baselines.** The paper evaluates BaichuanSEED against a comprehensive set of 7B-scale models spanning both base and chat/instruct variants, with the chat/instruct versions evaluated on benchmarks requiring instruction-following capability:

  *Base models:* Baichuan2-7B (Yang et al., 2023), Baichuan2-13B (Yang et al., 2023), Qwen1.5-7B (Bai et al., 2023), Llama3-8B (AI@Meta, 2024), OLMo-7B (Groeneveld et al., 2024), MAP-Neo-7B (Zhang et al., 2024).

  *Chat/Instruct models:* Baichuan2-7B-Chat, Baichuan2-13B-Chat, Qwen1.5-7B-Chat, Llama3-8B-Instruct, OLMo-7B-SFT, MAP-Neo-7B-SFT.

  The selection deliberately spans models with different training token budgets (from 2.5T for OLMo to 15T for Llama3), different degrees of data processing transparency, and different model families, enabling assessment of BaichuanSEED's competitiveness across multiple dimensions. The inclusion of both base and chat variants allows the paper to evaluate whether SFT preserves or degrades pretraining gains—a concern it explicitly raises about models that over-optimize during pretraining.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For the base model evaluation on multiple-choice benchmarks, the paper reports 0-shot or few-shot accuracy (e.g., MMLU uses 5-shot, GSM8K uses 4-shot), where the model receives k demonstration examples in the prompt before answering. The generation budget is measured in **training tokens** (the x-axis of all scaling curves in Figures 3, 4, and 5), with checkpoints evaluated at regular intervals throughout the 3T-token training run. For the 2B ablation models, the training budget is 100B tokens for KID proportion experiments and an unspecified number (implied as comparable) for deduplication ablations. The paper does not report inference FLOPs or wall-clock time for evaluation, focusing instead on the relationship between pretraining compute (proportional to training tokens × model parameters) and downstream performance. For the continued pretraining experiments in Section 6.2, the budget is 300B additional tokens beyond the 1.44T-token checkpoint, with the proportion of mathematics-related data varied (2% vs. 10%).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ formal cross-validation or statistical significance testing for its main evaluation results. Evaluation is conducted on the standard test sets of each benchmark, with the specific shots and prompting configurations noted in the table headers (Table 3, Table 4). The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard deviations, or multiple evaluation runs with different random seeds. The scaling curve analyses (Figures 3 and 4) use single evaluation points at each checkpoint. For the predictability demonstration (Figure 4), the paper fits a logarithmic curve to MMLU performance at checkpoints from the first half of training and extrapolates to the second half, but does not report fit quality metrics (R², RMSE) or conduct formal prediction interval analysis. The KID proportion experiments (Section 6.1) evaluate on a "private, high-quality corpus that constructed by a wide range of domains such as encyclopedias, web pages, papers, and news," but the specific composition and size of this corpus are not disclosed. The continued pretraining experiments (Section 6.2) evaluate on MMLU, GSM8K, and MATH at multiple intermediate checkpoints, but each checkpoint represents a single training trajectory without replication.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### 5.2.1 Scaling Behavior: Consistency and Predictability (Section 5.1)

The paper's most fundamental empirical contribution is the demonstration that BaichuanSEED exhibits **consistent** and **predictable** performance scaling across pretraining. These properties are evaluated on a set of comprehensive and downstream benchmarks before and after SFT, with results presented in Figure 3 and Figure 4.

**Consistency across pretraining benchmarks (Figure 3).** The paper monitors five metrics as a function of training tokens (from 0.5T to 3.0T): MMLU (5-shot), CMMLU (5-shot), MBPP (3-shot), GSM8K (4-shot), and validation loss. All five curves show monotonic improvement throughout training, with no visible regressions, plateaus, or discontinuities:

- **MMLU** rises from approximately 45% at 0.5T tokens to 60.25% at 3T tokens — a gain of roughly 15 percentage points, with steady improvement throughout.
- **CMMLU** rises from approximately 48% at 0.5T tokens to 62.09% at 3T tokens, tracking MMLU closely and showing the expected bilingual advantage given BaichuanSEED's Chinese data component.
- **MBPP** rises from approximately 22% at 0.5T tokens to 34.12% at 3T tokens, with the curve showing faster improvement in the earlier stages and gradual deceleration — consistent with the logarithmic scaling pattern.
- **GSM8K** rises from approximately 30% at 0.5T tokens to 38.81% at 3T tokens, showing the shallowest improvement slope among all metrics, which the paper later attributes to insufficient mathematics data in the pretraining mixture.
- **Validation loss** decreases from approximately 2.00 at 0.5T tokens to roughly 1.70 at 3T tokens, with smooth decay throughout, indicating stable optimization without loss spikes or divergence.

The paper emphasizes that the absence of performance regressions or instability is evidence that "BaichuanSEED has not been specialized to optimize for a specific task or evaluation benchmark, retaining the potential to transfer to specific downstream tasks." This claim is central to the "purity" concept: a model whose capabilities emerge smoothly from its data distribution will show this consistency, while a model contaminated with benchmark-specific data or optimized with synthetic data might show discontinuous improvements when it encounters the relevant data.

**Consistency after SFT (Figure 3).** The paper evaluates three SFT-specific benchmarks — FollowBench-FB5-HSR, FollowBench-FB5-SSR, and SuperCLUE-Math6 (SCM6) — on SFT models derived from different pretraining checkpoints, using the identical SFT protocol (450K examples, 6 epochs, constant learning rate). The SFT benchmark scores also increase with pretraining tokens:

- **FollowBench-FB5-HSR** rises from approximately 28 at 0.5T pretraining tokens to roughly 44 at 3T tokens.
- **FollowBench-FB5-SSR** rises from approximately 22 to roughly 38 over the same range.
- **SuperCLUE-Math6** rises from approximately 8 to roughly 22.

This consistency across SFT benchmarks is methodologically important because it demonstrates that the paper's SFT protocol is a **faithful readout of foundation model quality** rather than an independent source of variance. If SFT performance fluctuated unpredictably with pretraining progress, one could not attribute downstream capabilities to the pretraining data pipeline. The smooth SFT scaling curves validate the paper's approach of applying a fixed SFT recipe to all checkpoints for comparison purposes.

**Predictability (Figure 4).** The paper demonstrates predictability specifically on MMLU performance. Using checkpoints from the first half of training (approximately 0.5T to 1.5T tokens), the authors fit a logarithmic curve of the form:

$$\text{Acc}(t) = a \cdot \log(t) + b$$

where $t$ is the number of training tokens. They then extrapolate this curve to the second half of training (1.5T to 3.0T tokens) and find that the extrapolation "fit for the points in the latter half perfectly" (Section 5.1). The paper's Figure 4 shows the fitted curve alongside the actual MMLU measurements at both training and validation checkpoints, with the training points used for fitting (first half) and the validation points (second half) lying close to the extrapolated curve.

The paper argues this predictability is "especially crucial for developers, as having a forward-looking understanding of the model's performance trends during the early stages of training can facilitate rapid iteration and minimize unnecessary resource costs." The practical implication is straightforward: an organization training a model similar to BaichuanSEED could, after 1.5T tokens, forecast final performance at 3T tokens, decide whether the expected performance justifies continued investment, and potentially redirect resources if the forecast falls below requirements.

However, the paper reports this predictability result only for MMLU, leaving unaddressed whether other benchmarks (CMMLU, MBPP, GSM8K) show similarly predictable scaling. The universality of the logarithmic scaling law across benchmarks is important for the practical utility of the predictability claim — if only some benchmarks follow simple logarithmic curves while others exhibit phase transitions, the ability to forecast multi-dimensional model quality from early checkpoints would be limited.

#### 5.2.2 Comprehensive Benchmark Performance (Section 5.2, Tables 3 and 4)

The paper evaluates BaichuanSEED (base model) and BaichuanSEED-SFT (chat model) against seven comparable models across two categories of benchmarks: **comprehensive benchmarks** measuring broad knowledge and reasoning (MMLU, CMMLU, AGIEval, C-Eval, MMLU-Pro, LiveBench) and **downstream task benchmarks** measuring specific capabilities (MBPP, HumanEval for coding; GSM8K, MATH for mathematics; TriviaQA for commonsense reasoning; HellaSwag for reading comprehension). The headline results are reported in Table 3 and Table 4, with detailed subject-level breakdowns in Appendix A (Tables 6, 7, 8).

**Headline finding: Competitive with commercial models on comprehensive benchmarks despite using fewer training tokens and no data selection.**

On **MMLU** (5-shot), the most widely-used comprehensive benchmark:
- BaichuanSEED achieves 60.25%, surpassing Baichuan2-7B (54.65%) by 5.6 percentage points and Baichuan2-13B (59.83%) by 0.42 points, despite the latter having nearly twice the parameters.
- BaichuanSEED trails Llama3-8B (66.57%) by 6.32 points and Qwen1.5-7B (62.19%) by 1.94 points, but the paper contextualizes this relative to training tokens: Llama3 uses 15T tokens (5× BaichuanSEED's 3T), while Qwen1.5 uses 3T tokens but employs undisclosed data selection and synthetic data.
- BaichuanSEED substantially outperforms OLMo-7B (28.40%) and modestly outperforms MAP-Neo-7B (58.18%), both of which are transparency-focused models that release training data details.
- After SFT, BaichuanSEED-SFT achieves 60.15%, essentially unchanged from the base model (60.25%), which the paper interprets as evidence that SFT does not degrade general knowledge — a concern raised about models that over-optimize on pretraining benchmarks.

On **CMMLU** (5-shot), the Chinese-language counterpart to MMLU:
- BaichuanSEED achieves 62.09%, trailing Qwen1.5-7B (71.84%) by 9.75 points — a substantial gap that likely reflects Qwen1.5's heavier emphasis on Chinese data in its pretraining mixture.
- BaichuanSEED outperforms Llama3-8B (50.68%) by 11.41 points, consistent with Llama3's predominantly English pretraining data.
- BaichuanSEED surpasses Baichuan2-7B (56.95%) and approaches Baichuan2-13B (61.32%), indicating that the improved data processing pipeline benefits Chinese capabilities.

On **AGIEval** (0-shot), a benchmark of Chinese college entrance and professional exams:
- BaichuanSEED achieves 31.07%, outperforming Baichuan2-7B (28.95%) and Llama3-8B (26.74%), but trailing Qwen1.5-7B (39.46%) and MAP-Neo-7B (33.87%).
- The paper does not provide a subject-level breakdown to isolate whether the gap stems from specific exam categories (Gaokao, civil service, law) or reflects a general Chinese reasoning deficit.

On **MMLU-Pro** (5-shot), a more challenging version of MMLU with 10-option questions:
- BaichuanSEED achieves 26.57%, nearly identical to Qwen1.5-7B (30.30%) and MAP-Neo-7B (26.89%), and substantially above OLMo-7B (13.05%).
- After SFT, BaichuanSEED-SFT achieves 29.63%, while Qwen1.5-7B-Chat drops precipitously to 16.29% — a 14-point decline from its base model. The paper highlights this as evidence that Qwen1.5's base model performance may have been inflated by benchmark-aware pretraining optimization that does not survive SFT, whereas BaichuanSEED's "pure" training yields consistent gains or stability across stages.

On **LiveBench** (0-shot), designed to be contamination-resistant through frequent question updates:
- BaichuanSEED-SFT achieves 18.32%, surpassing Qwen1.5-7B-Chat (16.78%), OLMo-7B-SFT (8.80%), and MAP-Neo-7B-SFT (14.35%), though trailing Llama3-8B-Instruct (25.91%).
- The paper emphasizes that BaichuanSEED-SFT outperforms Baichuan2-7B-Chat (12.89%) by 42.1% relatively, attributing this gain to "its focus on acquiring extensive world knowledge and language patterns, not limited to overfit on specific optimization tasks."

**Headline finding: Strong on code, weak on math, with clear room for improvement.**

On **code benchmarks** (Table 4, MBPP 3-shot and HumanEval 0-shot):
- BaichuanSEED achieves 34.12% on MBPP, ranking second among base models behind Llama3-8B (44.60%) and ahead of Qwen1.5-7B (36.60%), despite using substantially fewer training tokens than Llama3 and no synthetic code data.
- On HumanEval, BaichuanSEED achieves 21.34%, placing behind Qwen1.5-7B (53.05%) by a large margin (-31.71 points), but ahead of Baichuan2-7B (17.68%) and MAP-Neo-7B (7.93%).
- After SFT, BaichuanSEED-SFT achieves 37.60% on MBPP and 23.17% on HumanEval, trailing Llama3-8B-Instruct (52.17%, 21.34%) and Qwen1.5-7B-Chat (12.58%, 29.27%) respectively.
- The paper attributes the strong MBPP performance to "the rich logical reasoning found in high-quality data" (specifically the 25% code data proportion and repository-level code organization), but does not explain the HumanEval gap relative to Qwen1.5.

On **mathematics benchmarks** (Table 4, GSM8K 4-shot and MATH 4-shot):
- BaichuanSEED achieves 38.81% on GSM8K, trailing Llama3-8B (50.11%), Qwen1.5-7B (54.74%), and MAP-Neo-7B (53.90%). The gap versus Qwen1.5 is 15.93 points.
- On MATH, BaichuanSEED achieves 9.84%, behind Qwen1.5-7B (21.08%) by 11.24 points and MAP-Neo-7B (15.14%) by 5.30 points.
- After SFT, BaichuanSEED-SFT achieves 53.98% on GSM8K and 14.06% on MATH, still trailing Llama3-8B-Instruct (78.17%, 25.62%) and MAP-Neo-7B-SFT (70.28%, 30.86%).
- The paper explicitly contextualizes these gaps: MAP-Neo "achieved only 5% accuracy on MATH and 21% on GSM8K before annealing, while significant improvement after annealing with high ratio of mathematical data." The implication is that BaichuanSEED's math weakness is not a fundamental limitation but a consequence of its deliberate decision to not upsample math data — a gap that the continued pretraining experiments in Section 6.2 directly address and partially close.

On **TriviaQA** (0-shot, commonsense reasoning):
- BaichuanSEED achieves 45.92%, substantially below Llama3-8B (65.23%), Qwen1.5-7B (50.92%), and even Baichuan2-13B (58.73%). This is the weakest relative performance among all benchmarks.
- After SFT, BaichuanSEED-SFT achieves 43.92%, while Llama3-8B-Instruct achieves 63.37% — a 19.45-point gap.
- The paper does not provide analysis or hypotheses for this specific weakness, which is notable given that TriviaQA primarily tests factual recall rather than reasoning.

On **HellaSwag** (0-shot, reading comprehension):
- BaichuanSEED achieves 70.20%, comparable to Qwen1.5-7B (72.64%) and OLMo-7B (70.31%), and approaching Llama3-8B (74.54%).
- After SFT, BaichuanSEED-SFT achieves 73.03%, the best among all evaluated chat models (Llama3-8B-Instruct achieves 71.45%, Qwen1.5-7B-Chat 72.81%).
- The paper does not comment extensively on this result, but it suggests that BaichuanSEED's data processing pipeline is effective for narrative understanding tasks.

**Performance of the SFT stage specifically.** A cross-cutting observation from Tables 3 and 4 is that BaichuanSEED's performance changes minimally after SFT, in contrast to several baselines:
- MMLU: Base 60.25% → SFT 60.15% (stable)
- MBPP: Base 34.12% → SFT 37.60% (modest gain)
- GSM8K: Base 38.81% → SFT 53.98% (substantial gain, attributed to improved instruction following)
- MATH: Base 9.84% → SFT 14.06% (gain)
- TriviaQA: Base 45.92% → SFT 43.92% (slight decline)
- HellaSwag: Base 70.20% → SFT 73.03% (gain)

By contrast, Qwen1.5-7B shows dramatic declines on several benchmarks after SFT: MMLU-Pro drops from 30.30% to 16.29%, MBPP drops from 36.60% to 12.58%, and HumanEval drops from 53.05% to 29.27%. The paper argues this pattern "highlights the importance of a completely pure training process to ensure the consistency after alignment," and suggests Qwen1.5 may have been optimized during pretraining in ways that create fragile capabilities which collapse when the model is fine-tuned on a different data distribution.

#### 5.2.4 Deduplication Ablation Study (Section 3.1.2, Table 1, Figure 2)

The paper reports a controlled ablation of its global multi-granularity deduplication strategy using 2B-parameter models. Three conditions are compared:

**Without deduplication**: The model trains on raw CommonCrawl data with no deduplication applied.

**With document-level dedup only**: Exact MD5 matching and MinHash near-duplicate detection are applied at the document level, globally across all CommonCrawl batches. The longest document in each near-duplicate cluster is retained.

**With multi-level dedup**: Document-level deduplication + sentence-level MinHash deduplication across documents + PII/harmful content filtering using the iterative FastText classifier.

Results (Table 1):

| Strategy | Chinese Loss | English Loss |
|---|---|---|
| Without deduplication | 3.136 | 2.893 |
| With document-level dedup | 3.083 | 2.877 |
| With multi-level dedup | **3.006** | **2.874** |

The multi-level deduplication strategy reduces Chinese held-out loss by 4.15% (3.136 → 3.006) and English held-out loss by 0.66% (2.893 → 2.874) relative to no deduplication. The improvement on Chinese is substantially larger than on English (4.15% vs. 0.66%), which the paper does not comment on but which may reflect greater redundancy in the Chinese web data (perhaps due to more aggressive content mirroring or templating in the Chinese web ecosystem) or differences in the quality of the CommonCrawl Chinese extraction.

Figure 2 shows the training loss curves for the three conditions. The model without deduplication achieves the **lowest training loss** throughout training — a counterintuitive result that the paper explains as a consequence of data redundancy making the prediction task easier. The models with deduplication (especially multi-level) have **higher training loss** but **lower held-out loss**, which is the signature of reduced memorization and improved generalization. This is a classic regularization effect: removing repeated patterns forces the model to learn more robust linguistic representations rather than memorizing frequently occurring templates.

The paper frames this as evidence that "our deduplication strategy leads to better diversity, making the prediction of the foundation model harder in the pre-training stage." The implied mechanism is that the deduplication removes spurious correlations (e.g., the association between a news article's content and its standard footer template) that would otherwise be learned as statistical regularities, degrading the model's ability to generalize to documents without those templates.

**Scale of deduplication impact.** The paper reports that the global multi-granularity deduplication "filter 88% of the tokens of our CommonCrawl (CC) dataset." This means that only 12% of the raw CommonCrawl tokens survive into the training corpus. Given that web pages constitute 45% of the final data mixture (13.5% Chinese + 31.5% English), the 88% reduction on CommonCrawl means the final training data volume is dominated by the surviving 12% of CC data plus the non-CC sources (KID, code, others). This aggressive filtering raises an implicit question: is the surviving 12% genuinely sufficient to learn web-scale knowledge, or would a less aggressive deduplication (e.g., 70% reduction) yield better results by preserving more diverse web content? The paper does not explore intermediate deduplication thresholds, which limits understanding of whether 88% is near the optimal point or substantially beyond it.

#### 5.2.5 Knowledge-Intensive Data Proportion Ablation (Section 6.1, Table 5)

The paper investigates the optimal proportion of knowledge-intensive data (KID) — books, academic papers, and technical reports — in the pretraining mixture. This ablation is motivated by the hypothesis that "LLMs trained on KID can achieve better performance in the same training steps," but that excessive KID may "down-sampling others, which may hinder the model from possessing comprehensive world knowledge or language patterns."

**Experimental design.** Using 2B-parameter models with the same architecture as BaichuanSEED, the paper tests KID proportions of 0%, 25%, and 50% under two regimes:

1.  **Training from scratch**: Models are trained for 100B tokens with each KID proportion, starting from random initialization.
2.  **Continued pretraining**: Starting from a model already trained on 1.5T tokens (which has "already obtained emergent abilities"), continued pretraining for 100B additional tokens with each KID proportion.

In both regimes, the remaining data distribution (web pages, code, others) maintains the same relative proportions as in Table 2, with only the KID-vs-others split varying.

The evaluation metric is perplexity on "a private, high-quality corpus that constructed by a wide range of domains such as encyclopedias, web pages, papers, and news." The specific composition of this corpus is not disclosed, which limits the interpretability of the results — if the evaluation corpus overrepresents KID-like content, the benefits of higher KID proportion would be overstated.

**Results (Table 5):**

| Regime | 0% KID | 25% KID | 50% KID |
|---|---|---|---|
| Train from scratch (PPL) | 14.24 | **13.68** | 13.86 |
| Continued pretrain (PPL) | 12.21 | **11.73** | 11.80 |

In both training regimes, 25% KID achieves the lowest perplexity:
- Training from scratch: 25% KID improves perplexity by 3.9% over 0% KID (14.24 → 13.68), while 50% KID degrades slightly to 13.86 (1.3% worse than the optimum).
- Continued pretraining: 25% KID improves perplexity by 3.9% over 0% KID (12.21 → 11.73), while 50% KID achieves 11.80 (0.6% worse).

The symmetric penalty at 50% KID in both regimes suggests that diminishing returns and negative effects from web data downsampling set in between 25% and 50%. The paper interprets this as validating the 25% KID choice for the final BaichuanSEED mixture (10% Chinese KID + 15% English KID = 25% total KID).

**Limitations of this ablation.** The paper does not test finer-grained proportions between 25% and 50% (e.g., 30%, 35%, 40%), nor does it investigate whether the optimal KID proportion varies between Chinese and English. Given that Chinese KID and English KID have different average document lengths and domain distributions (Figure 1), treating them as a single 25% block may miss opportunities for language-specific optimization. Additionally, the 100B-token training budget for the 2B models is substantially smaller than the 3T-token budget for the 7B model; it is possible that the optimal KID proportion shifts with training scale (longer training may benefit more from KID's higher information density, or alternatively may saturate on KID and benefit more from web data diversity).

#### 5.2.6 Mathematics Potential: Continued Pretraining with Increased Math Data (Section 6.2, Figure 5)

The paper explores whether BaichuanSEED's relatively weak math performance (9.84% on MATH, 38.81% on GSM8K) reflects a fundamental capability ceiling or simply insufficient math-relevant pretraining data. Using the fine-grained subject classifier described in Section 3.1.3 (trained on GPT-4-labeled data to categorize documents by knowledge domain), the authors identify mathematics-related documents within the KID pool and conduct continued pretraining experiments.

**Experimental design.** Starting from a 1.44T-token BaichuanSEED checkpoint (mid-training), the paper continues pretraining for 300B additional tokens under three conditions:
- **Baseline**: No change to the data mixture (maintaining the original proportions from Table 2).
- **2% math**: Mathematics-related data constitutes 2% of the continued pretraining tokens.
- **10% math**: Mathematics-related data constitutes 10% of the continued pretraining tokens.

The remaining data proportions are adjusted to accommodate the increased math data while keeping the total at 100%.

Checkpoints are evaluated at 1.34T, 1.44T, 1.49T, 1.59T, 1.69T, and 1.74T tokens (the starting checkpoint plus five continued pretraining checkpoints). The key evaluation metrics are MMLU (to assess whether math data upsampling degrades general capabilities), GSM8K, and MATH (to assess math-specific improvements).

**Results (Figure 5):**

On **MMLU**, all three conditions maintain nearly identical trajectories:
- At 1.74T tokens, the baseline achieves approximately 55.5%, 2% math achieves approximately 55.3%, and 10% math achieves approximately 55.8%. The maximum deviation between conditions is roughly 0.5 percentage points, and there is no systematic trend — the baseline line occasionally exceeds and occasionally falls below the math-upsampled lines.
- The paper reports that the 1.74T checkpoint with 10% math shows "only a 0.3% fluctuation on MMLU, compared to the baseline." This stability is the critical finding: it demonstrates that aggressive math data upsampling (10% of all tokens) does not degrade general knowledge and reasoning capabilities, addressing the concern that specializing the data mixture would hurt broad performance.

On **GSM8K** (4-shot), the math-upsampled conditions show clear improvements:
- Baseline: Rises from approximately 39% at 1.44T to roughly 42% at 1.74T — a modest gain from continued training on the original mixture.
- 2% math: Rises from approximately 39% at 1.44T to roughly 43% at 1.74T — a slight improvement over baseline, but the curve largely overlaps.
- 10% math: Rises from approximately 39% at 1.44T to roughly 45% at 1.74T — a "7.4% increase on GSM8K" relative to baseline at the final checkpoint, with the divergence becoming visible after about 100B tokens of continued training (around the 1.59T mark).

On **MATH** (4-shot), the 10% math condition shows the most dramatic improvement:
- Baseline: Rises from approximately 7% at 1.44T to roughly 8% at 1.74T — essentially flat.
- 2% math: Rises from approximately 7% at 1.44T to roughly 9% at 1.74T — a small improvement.
- 10% math: Rises from approximately 7% at 1.44T to roughly 13% at 1.74T — a "65.6% increase on MATH" relative to baseline at the final checkpoint. The improvement accelerates in the later stages of continued training (from 1.59T to 1.74T), suggesting that the benefits of math-specialized data compound with exposure.

**Interpretation and limitations.** The paper frames these results as evidence that "knowledge intensive data has substantial potential, especially when enhancing a language model's mathematical abilities" and that "by searching optimal data proportions and employing curriculum learning strategies, the value of these data can be further captured." The gain from 2% to 10% math data is non-linear: 2% provides negligible gains while 10% provides substantial gains, implying a threshold effect where math data must constitute a significant fraction of training tokens to meaningfully improve mathematical reasoning.

However, several caveats apply:
- The 1.44T baseline checkpoint already had some math capability (approximately 7% on MATH). The 10% math condition improves this to roughly 13% — still well below Qwen1.5-7B's 21.08% with 3T tokens. This suggests that 10% math data over 300B tokens (approximately 30B math-specific tokens) is insufficient to close the gap, and either a higher proportion or synthetic data may be necessary.
- The continued pretraining experiment starts from a checkpoint that has already undergone significant general pretraining. It is possible that math data would be even more effective if integrated from the beginning of training (cold-start) rather than injected mid-training, or conversely that the emergent abilities from general pretraining are prerequisite for the model to effectively learn from math data. The paper does not test these alternatives.
- The 10% math condition uses the fine-grained classifier to identify mathematics-related documents within the existing KID pool. If the KID pool contains limited math data to begin with, the "10% math" condition may be limited by the quality and quantity of natural math content rather than by the proportion setting. Synthetic math data generation could potentially unlock larger improvements.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Deduplication granularity (document-level only vs. multi-level):** Multi-level deduplication (document + sentence + PII filtering) reduces English test loss by an additional 0.003 compared to document-level only (from 2.877 to 2.874), and Chinese test loss by an additional 0.077 (from 3.083 to 3.006). The Chinese improvement from sentence-level + PII deduplication is 25× larger than the English improvement in absolute terms (0.077 vs. 0.003), suggesting either more boilerplate contamination in Chinese web data or more effective sentence-level deduplication for Chinese text. Table 1 and Figure 2.

**Knowledge-intensive data proportion (0%, 25%, 50%):** The optimal perplexity is achieved at 25% KID in both training-from-scratch and continued-pretraining regimes. The penalty for 50% KID is mild (PPL 13.86 vs. 13.68 from scratch; PPL 11.80 vs. 11.73 continued), indicating the model is relatively robust to KID oversampling within this range. The penalty for 0% KID is more substantial (PPL 14.24 vs. 13.68 from scratch; 12.21 vs. 11.73 continued), indicating that some KID is clearly beneficial but the marginal return diminishes by 50%. Table 5.

**Training from scratch vs. continued pretraining for KID proportion:** The optimal proportion (25%) is identical in both regimes, and the relative ordering of conditions is preserved (25% < 50% < 0% for perplexity). This consistency across training regimes increases confidence that 25% KID is not an artifact of a particular experimental setup. Table 5.

**Continued pretraining for math with different proportions (0%, 2%, 10%):** The 2% math proportion yields minimal gains over baseline on both GSM8K and MATH, while 10% yields substantial gains. This implies a **threshold effect**: math data must exceed a critical proportion (somewhere between 2% and 10%) before it meaningfully impacts downstream math performance. The 10% condition improves MATH by 65.6% relative to baseline without degrading MMLU (0.3% fluctuation), validating the claim that targeted data upsampling can improve specific capabilities without harming general knowledge. Figure 5.

**SFT epoch count (6 epochs vs. typical 2-3 epochs):** The paper uses 6 SFT epochs rather than the 2-3 common in prior work, citing empirical findings that "more epochs yields better results for small size models, especially when the amount of pre-training token is insufficient." No ablation on SFT epoch count is provided. Section 4.2.

**Repository-level vs. document-level code data:** The paper states that "pivot experiments demonstrate that the mixture of repository-level and document-level data yields better model performance compared to using either one alone" (Section 3.1.1). However, no quantitative results are reported for this ablation, no specific metrics are cited, and the experimental setup (model scale, training tokens, evaluation benchmarks) is not described. This is a notable gap in the experimental reporting.

**Effect of synthetic data (absent by design):** The paper does not include synthetic data in its pretraining mixture and therefore has no ablation comparing with-synthetic vs. without-synthetic conditions. The argument for this choice is methodological rather than empirical: synthetic data "may lead to a loss of instruction following ability after SFT, making the impact of synthetic data unclear and introducing additional confounding variables" (Section 3.1.3). The paper cites Chen et al. (2024b) to support this concern, but provides no experimental evidence that synthetic data would actually degrade BaichuanSEED's performance.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments in this paper are carefully designed to support its central claims, but several limitations constrain the strength of the conclusions that can be drawn. This assessment examines each major claim from the executive summary and prior sections against the experimental evidence.

**Claim: "Broad collection followed by global multi-granularity deduplication and domain-level reweighting can produce a pretraining corpus that yields competitive model performance without fine-grained data selection."**

The evidence for this claim is strong within the scope of the experiments. BaichuanSEED achieves competitive performance on multiple comprehensive benchmarks (MMLU: 60.25% vs. Qwen1.5-7B's 62.19%; MMLU-Pro: 26.57% vs. 30.30%; LiveBench: 18.32% vs. 16.78% for Qwen1.5-7B-Chat), and the deduplication ablation (Table 1) demonstrates that the multi-level deduplication strategy specifically contributes to improved language modeling (reducing Chinese test loss by 4.15% and English test loss by 0.66%).

However, the claim contains an important unstated caveat: "competitive" is defined relative to a specific class of 7B models trained on 2.5T-3T tokens. BaichuanSEED is **not competitive with Llama3-8B** on most benchmarks (trailing by 6.32 points on MMLU, by 14.91 points on GSM8K, and by 19.45 points on TriviaQA), and the paper acknowledges this by attributing the gap to Llama3's 5× larger training token budget (15T vs. 3T). This attribution is plausible — more training data generally improves performance, and scaling laws predict continued improvement in this regime — but it is untested: we do not know whether BaichuanSEED trained on 15T tokens would match Llama3, because the experiment was not run. The claim should therefore be qualified: the pipeline is **competitive with similarly-resourced models**, not competitive with all models at the same parameter scale.

Additionally, the paper does not directly compare its pipeline against a pipeline with fine-grained selection on the same underlying data. The deduplication ablation compares deduplicated vs. undeduplicated data, but does not compare, for example, "deduplication only" vs. "deduplication + perplexity filtering" vs. "deduplication + classifier-based quality scoring." Without such a head-to-head comparison, the claim that broad collection + deduplication is a sufficient substitute for selection rests on the indirect evidence that BaichuanSEED's benchmark scores are in the same ballpark as models that likely use some form of selection (like Qwen1.5). This is suggestive but not conclusive — the performance similarity could be coincidental, with Qwen1.5's selection compensating for weaknesses in other aspects of its pipeline while BaichuanSEED's deduplication compensates for the absence of selection.

**Claim: "Global multi-granularity deduplication is a powerful quality improvement mechanism, not just a compute-saving measure. It improves test loss while increasing training loss, indicating a regularization effect."**

This claim is well-supported by Table 1 and Figure 2. The inversion of training and test loss (lower training loss without deduplication, lower test loss with deduplication) is a textbook signature of regularization, and the monotonic improvement from no deduplication → document-level → multi-level strengthens the causal interpretation. The case studies in Appendix B (Figure 6) provide qualitative evidence that the removed content is genuinely low-value or harmful, which addresses the concern that the deduplication might be removing diverse but legitimate content.

One weakness is that the ablation is conducted only on 2B models, not on the full 7B models. While the authors reasonably extrapolate from 2B to 7B (a common practice in LLM research given computational constraints), it is possible that the optimal deduplication configuration changes with scale. For instance, larger models may be more robust to template memorization (because they have more capacity to model both templates and content) or more vulnerable to it (because they can memorize more patterns). The paper provides no evidence either way.

Furthermore, the ablation does not disentangle the contributions of the three components of multi-level deduplication: document-level MinHash, sentence-level MinHash, and PII/harmful content filtering. The jump from "document-level only" to "multi-level" could be driven entirely by one component (e.g., sentence-level deduplication might account for most of the gain, with PII filtering contributing negligibly to language modeling metrics but being essential for safety). A component-wise ablation (document-only, document+sentence, document+PII, document+sentence+PII) would clarify which aspects of the strategy matter most.

**Claim: "BaichuanSEED is a 'pure' model that exhibits consistency and predictability during training, establishing a transparent baseline for future research."**

The consistency evidence (Figure 3) is strong: MMLU, CMMLU, MBPP, GSM8K, and validation loss all improve monotonically with training tokens, with no visible regressions, discontinuities, or instabilities. The curves are smooth and well-behaved, which is consistent with the "no downstream optimization" claim and contrasts with models that might show sudden jumps when benchmark-relevant data is encountered.

The predictability evidence (Figure 4) is more limited. The paper demonstrates predictability only for MMLU, using a logarithmic fit on the first half of training extrapolated to the second half. While the fit quality appears good visually, the paper does not report:
- **Quantitative fit metrics** (R², RMSE, mean absolute error) for the extrapolation.
- **Predictability on other benchmarks.** If GSM8K or MATH scaling curves are not well-fit by simple logarithmic functions, the predictability claim would be benchmark-specific rather than a general property of the model.
- **Sensitivity to the choice of extrapolation point.** Fitting on 0.5T–1.5T and extrapolating to 3T produces one prediction; fitting on 0.5T–1.0T and extrapolating to 3T might produce a different one. The paper does not test how early in training predictability emerges.
- **Comparison with non-pure models.** The paper argues implicitly that pure models are more predictable than optimized models, but it does not show scaling curves for, say, Qwen1.5 or Llama3 to demonstrate that they are less predictable. This leaves the "purity → predictability" link as an untested hypothesis.

**Claim: "BaichuanSEED has significant untapped potential on downstream tasks, as demonstrated by continued pretraining experiments on mathematics."**

The mathematics continued pretraining experiments (Figure 5) provide compelling evidence that BaichuanSEED's math performance is bottlenecked by data proportion rather than architecture or scale. The 10% math condition improves MATH by 65.6% without degrading MMLU, which is a clean and informative result. However, several aspects of this claim require qualification:

- **The improvement, while large relatively, is small absolutely.** MATH accuracy rises from approximately 7% to 13% — still far below Qwen1.5-7B (21.08%) and MAP-Neo-7B-SFT (30.86%). This demonstrates potential but does not demonstrate that the potential can be fully realized through data proportion adjustment alone.
- **The experiment uses only 300B continued pretraining tokens.** It is unknown whether continuing at 10% math for longer (500B, 1T tokens) would yield further gains or plateau.
- **The math data comes from the existing KID pool.** If the KID pool lacks sufficiently diverse or advanced mathematics content (e.g., competition-level problems, formal proofs), then even 100% math proportion would be limited by content quality. Synthetic data generation might be necessary to fully close the math gap, which would violate the "pure" constraint.
- **Only mathematics is tested.** The paper's claim about "untapped potential" is supported only for mathematics. It is possible that other weaknesses (e.g., TriviaQA at 45.92% vs. Llama3-8B at 65.23%) are not similarly addressable through data proportion adjustments and reflect genuine capability gaps that require architectural scaling or different data sources.

**Claim: "The SFT process preserves or enhances BaichuanSEED's capabilities, in contrast to models like Qwen1.5-7B that show significant degradation after SFT."**

This claim is supported by the data in Tables 3 and 4: BaichuanSEED's MMLU is nearly identical before and after SFT (60.25% → 60.15%), while Qwen1.5-7B's MMLU-Pro drops from 30.30% to 16.29% and its MBPP drops from 36.60% to 12.58%. The contrast is stark and the paper's interpretation — that BaichuanSEED's purity prevents fragile capabilities — is plausible.

However, there is an alternative explanation that the paper does not address: the SFT datasets and protocols may differ between BaichuanSEED and Qwen1.5 in ways that explain the differential degradation. BaichuanSEED uses a 450K-example self-constructed SFT dataset with 6 training epochs; Qwen1.5 presumably uses a different dataset composition, size, and training protocol. If Qwen1.5's SFT dataset is smaller, less diverse, or more aggressively optimized for specific tasks, the degradation could reflect SFT quality rather than pretraining purity. Without controlling for SFT methodology, the comparison is confounded: we cannot distinguish whether BaichuanSEED-SFT holds up better because of the pretraining pipeline or because of the SFT pipeline.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

1.  **Comparison against a fine-grained selection pipeline on the same data.** The paper's central argument is that broad collection + deduplication is sufficient compared to fine-grained selection. Training a 2B or 7B model on the same underlying data but with an added selection stage (e.g., perplexity filtering, classifier-based quality scoring) would provide a direct test rather than relying on cross-model comparisons where many variables differ.

2.  **Deduplication threshold sweep.** The 88% token reduction on CommonCrawl is an extreme filtering ratio. Testing deduplication at lower thresholds (50%, 70%) to see whether more aggressive deduplication always helps or whether there is a sweet spot would inform whether 88% is near-optimal or excessive.

3.  **Component-wise ablation of multi-level deduplication.** Testing document-level only, document+sentence, document+PII, and document+sentence+PII separately would reveal which components drive the improvement and which may be redundant or even harmful.

4.  **Predictability analysis on multiple benchmarks.** Demonstrating that the logarithmic scaling law holds for benchmarks beyond MMLU (especially GSM8K and MBPP) would substantiate the claim that predictability is a general property of the model rather than a lucky fit to one metric.

5.  **SFT data ablation (size, diversity, epochs).** Testing whether BaichuanSEED-SFT's stability relative to Qwen1.5-7B-Chat reflects pretraining purity or SFT methodology by varying SFT conditions would strengthen the causal claim.

6.  **Longer continued pretraining for math.** Extending the 10% math experiment to 500B or 1T tokens to see whether MATH performance continues to improve or plateaus would clarify whether the remaining gap to competitors is addressable through data proportion alone.

7.  **Replication on the 7B scale.** The deduplication ablation, KID proportion ablation, and code data mixture ablation are all conducted on 2B models. While this is standard practice due to computational constraints, it leaves open the possibility that findings do not transfer perfectly to 7B scale. Reporting even a single 7B-scale confirmation point for the most important finding (e.g., deduplication) would increase confidence.

Overall, the paper's experimental program is thoughtfully designed to support its specific claims about data processing, purity, and untapped potential. The weaknesses are primarily in **scope** (most ablations at 2B, predictability shown for only one benchmark, math as the only downstream task explored for potential) and **comparative baselines** (no head-to-head with fine-grained selection, no controlled SFT comparisons). These weaknesses do not invalidate the claims but they do constrain their generality and the strength of causal attribution. The paper's value lies primarily in its detailed documentation of a reproducible pipeline and its demonstration of what that pipeline achieves, rather than in definitive proof that the pipeline's specific design choices are optimal or universally applicable.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted for in the Compute-Optimal Efficiency Gains

The paper's headline finding—that compute-optimal test-time scaling achieves more than 4× better efficiency than best-of-N—excludes a major source of computation from its accounting. The difficulty estimation procedure that enables adaptive allocation requires generating 2048 samples per question and scoring them with either ground-truth labels (oracle difficulty) or the PRM (predicted difficulty). The paper explicitly acknowledges this exclusion:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference… our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity" (Section 3.2).

**Consequence.** In any realistic deployment, the total computational cost is difficulty estimation plus strategy execution, and the former dominates the latter at the budgets studied. The 2048 samples consumed for difficulty estimation exceed the largest test-time budgets considered (256–512 generations) by 4–8×. This means the reported "4× efficiency gain over best-of-N" is computed relative to a baseline that itself does not pay the difficulty estimation cost—a comparison that systematically overstates the practical benefit. If a practitioner amortizes difficulty estimation across many queries on the same problem (e.g., in batch evaluation), the cost becomes negligible per query, but for single-use inference (the typical deployment scenario), the total cost of the compute-optimal approach would be substantially higher than best-of-N, not lower. The paper frames this as an "exploration-exploitation tradeoff" (Section 3.2) and suggests future work on training models to predict difficulty directly from question text, but no such model is developed or evaluated here.

The paper also does not explore intermediate approaches that would reduce this cost: using fewer than 2048 samples for difficulty estimation, using a smaller model for the estimation samples, or adaptively allocating samples based on early PRM score signals. These are practical design choices that would determine whether the framework is deployable, and their absence means the reported efficiency numbers should be understood as an upper bound on what is achievable after the difficulty estimation problem is solved, not as a realized gain.

---

### 6.2 The Approach Provides Zero Benefit on the Hardest Problems

Across every method studied—PRM-guided search, iterative revisions, compute-optimal combinations, and FLOPs-matched comparisons with larger models—the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5) show essentially no improvement regardless of how much test-time compute is allocated. The paper is transparent about this finding throughout:

- In search experiments (Figure 3, right): "On the hardest questions (bin 5), no method makes meaningful progress—the base model simply lacks the capability to produce correct solutions regardless of how the budget is allocated" (Section 5.3).

- In revision experiments (Figure 7, right): "All ratios produce roughly 2–3% accuracy. No allocation strategy helps."

- In FLOPs-matched comparisons (Figure 9, Section 7): the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%, and the larger model consistently outperforms test-time compute on hard questions, with disadvantages reaching −52.9% relative for PRM search at high inference-to-pretraining ratios.

**Consequence.** This establishes a hard boundary condition: test-time compute amplifies existing capability but does not create capability where none exists. If the base model's probability of generating a correct solution is approximately zero—as it is for the hardest MATH problems under PaLM 2-S*—then no amount of search, revision, or adaptive allocation can recover a correct answer. This is not a failure of the allocation strategy but a fundamental limitation of the test-time compute paradigm itself. For problems that genuinely exceed the base model's competence, pretraining a larger or better-trained model remains the only viable path. The paper does not offer a solution to this problem, and it is unclear whether one exists within the test-time compute framework.

This limitation has practical consequences for deployment strategy. If an organization's problem distribution includes a non-trivial fraction of "bin 5" difficulty questions—those where the base model essentially never gets the right answer—then investing in test-time compute infrastructure will yield zero return on those queries. The paper's difficulty estimation mechanism could identify such problems and route them to a larger model or human review, but this would require knowing the difficulty distribution in advance (a chicken-and-egg problem) or accepting wasted computation on unsolvable problems.

---

### 6.3 All Results Are on a Single Benchmark with a Single Model Family

The entire empirical analysis—search strategies, revision models, compute-optimal allocation, FLOPs-matched comparisons—is conducted on one dataset (MATH, 500 test questions) with one model family (PaLM 2-S* and a parameter-scaled variant). The paper acknowledges this limitation implicitly in its framing but does not test generalization:

> "We believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4).

**Consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be specific to this model-benchmark combination in ways that limit the generality of the conclusions:

- **The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior** depends on PaLM 2-S*'s output distribution, its error patterns, and the specific Monte Carlo rollout training procedure described in Appendix D. A different base model with different calibration properties might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves or different over-optimization thresholds.
- **The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples** depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities. PaLM 2-S* is a strong model; weaker models might not learn to revise effectively from the edit-distance-paired training data, or might show different tradeoffs between sequential and parallel sampling.
- **The MATH benchmark** consists exclusively of competition-level mathematical reasoning problems with clean, verifiable answers. The difficulty-dependent patterns discovered here—beam search hurting easy problems, revisions dominating on easy problems, parallel sampling needed for hard problems—may not generalize to other reasoning domains (code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA) or to open-ended generation tasks where correctness is ambiguous.
- **Test set size of 500 questions**, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each, then further split by two-fold cross-validation for strategy selection (~50 questions per fold per bin). The paper does not report confidence intervals, making it difficult to assess whether the observed strategy differences (e.g., beam search outperforming best-of-N on bin 3) are statistically reliable at this sample size or could reverse under different random splits.

The paper treats these findings as general principles about test-time compute scaling, but they have been validated only in a narrow experimental regime. Replication on diverse models and benchmarks would determine which findings are universal and which are contingent on PaLM 2-S*'s specific properties or MATH's specific structure. The absence of such replication is a significant barrier to practitioners adopting these strategies for domains unlike competition mathematics.

---

### 6.4 The 14× Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained

The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7—which is central to the paper's claim that test-time compute can substitute for pretraining—scales model parameters by approximately 14× while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023b). The paper explicitly acknowledges that this departs from compute-optimal pretraining:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work" (Section 7).

**Consequence.** A model trained with Chinchilla-optimal scaling (Hoffmann et al., 2022)—where both parameters and training data are scaled proportionally to the compute budget—would almost certainly outperform a parameter-only-scaled model at the same total FLOPs, making the pretraining baseline weaker than it could be. The paper reports, for example, that test-time compute with PaLM 2-S* achieves a +27.8% relative improvement over the 14× larger model on easy questions at low inference-to-pretraining ratios (Figure 1, top-right bar chart). Against a Chinchilla-optimal larger model, this margin would likely shrink or potentially reverse.

Additionally, the 14× larger model uses only greedy decoding—no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search, no revision. This is the weakest possible inference strategy for the larger model and makes the comparison asymmetric: the smaller model benefits from sophisticated inference-time optimization while the larger model receives none. A fair comparison would give the larger model *some* test-time compute budget, even if modest (e.g., best-of-8 or a single revision step). The paper's framing of this as "test-time compute vs. pretraining" would be more accurate if both models were given the best available inference strategies, with the comparison isolating the effect of model scale.

The paper's conclusion that "test-time compute can outperform a ~14× larger model" should therefore be understood as a finding about a specific, suboptimal pretraining configuration (parameter-only scaling, greedy decoding). It does not establish that test-time compute is generally preferable to compute-optimal pretraining; it establishes only that there exist pretraining configurations that can be beaten by aggressive test-time optimization of a smaller model. This is still a useful empirical finding—it shows that bad pretraining decisions can be partially recovered at inference—but it is weaker than the headline claim suggests.

---

### 6.5 Sequential Revisions Are Inherently High-Latency, and the Paper Does Not Account for Wall-Clock Time

The paper measures computational cost exclusively in "generations"—the number of complete solutions sampled—which is a reasonable proxy for total FLOPs but ignores the fundamentally different latency characteristics of sequential versus parallel strategies. Sequential revisions (Section 6) generate a chain of dependent samples where each revision conditions on the previous one. Parallel best-of-N (the baseline) generates N independent samples simultaneously. The paper's compute-optimal policy often favors strategies with high sequential depth, particularly on easy problems:

> "Easy questions perform best with purely sequential revisions—the model's initial attempts are roughly correct and just need refinement" (Section 6.2).

**Consequence.** A compute-optimal strategy that allocates 128 generations as, say, 64 sequential revisions × 2 parallel chains has roughly 64× the wall-clock latency of a strategy that runs 128 parallel samples simultaneously, even though both use the same total FLOPs. For latency-sensitive applications—interactive assistants, real-time decision-making, customer-facing chatbots—the sequential-dominant strategies that the compute-optimal policy selects for easy problems would be unacceptably slow regardless of their accuracy advantages. The paper does not discuss this tradeoff, report wall-clock times for any strategy, or propose a latency-aware variant of the compute-optimal objective.

This is not merely an implementation concern. It means the compute-optimal policy as formulated is optimal only under the assumption that total FLOPs is the binding constraint, not latency. In many (perhaps most) real-world deployment scenarios, latency is the binding constraint—users will not wait 60× longer for a modest accuracy improvement. A latency-aware objective that jointly optimizes accuracy and response time would likely produce very different allocations, potentially favoring parallel sampling even when sequential revisions are more FLOPs-efficient. The absence of this analysis limits the practical deployability of the findings for interactive use cases.

---

### 6.6 Verifier Over-Optimization Is a Hard Ceiling That Is Mitigated but Not Solved

The paper identifies verifier over-optimization as a central limiting factor: PRM-guided beam search degrades performance on easy problems at high budgets (Figure 3, right), lookahead search—the strongest optimizer—paradoxically underperforms simpler methods at the same generation budget (Figure 3, left), and qualitative examples in Appendix M show degenerate outputs (repetitive steps, excessively short solutions) that score highly under the PRM but are factually incorrect. The compute-optimal policy partially addresses this by routing easy problems away from aggressive search toward best-of-N, but this is a mitigation, not a solution.

**Consequence.** On the medium-difficulty problems where beam search is actually deployed (bins 3–4), over-optimization still limits the scaling ceiling. The beam search curves in Figure 3 flatten and sometimes decline well before the maximum generation budget is exhausted, meaning that additional compute beyond a certain threshold provides zero or negative benefit even in the regime where search is supposed to help. The paper does not explore how to push this ceiling higher—for example, through better PRM training, adversarial data augmentation, ensemble verification, or constrained search that penalizes outputs deviating too far from the base model's typical distribution.

This means the compute-optimal approach is fundamentally bounded by verifier quality, and the specific numerical results (optimal allocation strategies, efficiency gains, difficulty thresholds) are contingent on the particular PRM trained for this paper. Improving the PRM—through better Monte Carlo rollout data, different training objectives, or architectural changes—would likely shift the over-optimization threshold, change which difficulty bins benefit from search, and alter the compute-optimal policy. The paper does not explore how sensitive its findings are to verifier quality, which means a practitioner replicating this work with a different PRM might find substantially different optimal strategies.

The paper identifies this as a key area for future work, acknowledging that "improving verifier robustness is the key bottleneck for further scaling test-time compute, not improving search algorithms" (Section 8). However, it provides no experimental evidence on how much headroom exists—what accuracy would be achievable at high budgets if verifier over-optimization were eliminated—which makes it difficult to assess whether the current 4× efficiency gains are near the theoretical maximum or are a small fraction of what better verifiers could unlock.
