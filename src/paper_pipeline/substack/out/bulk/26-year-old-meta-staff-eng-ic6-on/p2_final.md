Here are the takeaways from this Simon Kindström interview (went from new grad IC3 to Staff IC6 in 3 years, earned two “Redefines Expectations” ratings, and received secret discretionary equity bonuses):

**Choosing a team for legible business impact**

Simon’s trajectory was set before he even started. During recruiting, his future manager explicitly pitched the team by connecting business impact to career growth: the team’s work directly drove revenue, and that visibility would accelerate Simon’s career. He joined because of that pitch—a decision that proved foundational. Once on the team, he landed multiple unexpected revenue wins that became the raw material for high ratings and fast promotions. The deliberate framing was: don’t pick a team purely for technical interest; pick one where your work will be *legible as business impact* to the people who decide your ratings.

**The “Redefines Expectations” engine: chasing failed experiments to the root**

The cornerstone of Simon’s speed was earning the company’s highest rating—“Redefines Expectations”—twice. The pattern behind it is specific and repeatable: experiments that flopped unexpectedly. Most engineers, when an A/B test comes back flat or negative, shrug, document the null result, and move on. Simon refused to abandon a failed experiment until he understood *why* it failed. He would adjust, re-run, re-analyze, and iterate—sometimes through multiple cycles—until he either had a clear causal explanation or, in several cases, actually turned the results around into a significant revenue win. Some of his biggest career-defining outcomes grew from experiments that initially looked like dead ends. The “Redefines” rating wasn’t about flawless execution; it was about the stubbornness to extract signal from noise everyone else had walked away from.

**Writing as leverage: put the punchline first**

Simon identified writing as one of the highest-leverage skills in his career—it amplified the perceived impact of his work because decision-makers could actually see and act on his results. His core tactical rule: put the impactful, interesting part at the *top*. Engineers habitually bury the lede, walking through methodology, data, and edge cases before finally arriving at the conclusion. Most readers, especially senior leaders making resource decisions, don’t care about the journey; they need the destination immediately. By front-loading the revenue impact, the surprising finding, or the key recommendation, you earn the reader’s attention for the details that follow. It’s not just communication hygiene; it directly shapes how leadership perceives the weight of your work.

**Scaling himself: “Delegate, don’t abdicate”**

As he pushed toward Staff, Simon hit a predictable wall: delegation. He was handing off work but disengaging from it—a pattern that produced end-of-half scrambles when delegated work came back incomplete, off-target, or simply not done. The fix was a mindset shift he crystallized as **Delegate, don’t abdicate**: taking ownership of the *results* of delegated work, not just the assignment of tasks. Practically, this meant staying close enough to course-correct early, treating a junior engineer’s output as his own responsibility, and never letting a half-cycle pass with a surprise gap. The phrase captures a specific failure mode—delegation as dumping, followed by discovery at deadline. His correction was to maintain a light but persistent thread of accountability, checking in, unblocking, and ultimately feeling personally on the hook for what shipped.

**The manager multiplier**

A through-line in Simon’s story is his manager, who had himself been promoted fast at the company before transitioning into management. This person coached Simon closely throughout, serving as both career strategist and translator of the company’s unwritten rules. The manager knew what calibrated at each level, what kinds of impact would register in calibration rooms, and how to position Simon’s work so it was legible to leadership. Simon attributes much of his acceleration to having someone who could say “that project won’t count for promotion” or “this is the dimension you’re weak on; go fix it”—unglamorous, tactical guidance that compounds over cycles.

**The management detour, and a transparent return**

At some point Simon tried management—partly to learn a new skill set, partly to help the org. It didn’t go well. He found himself pulled further from the technical work he enjoyed, and the role introduced a level of thrash (reorgs, people issues, shifting priorities) he hadn’t anticipated. Crucially, he was transparent about the whole journey: he communicated openly with his leadership about why he was trying management, what wasn’t working, and his decision to switch back to IC. The episode didn’t derail his career; the transparency turned what could have been a silent struggle into a legible, professionally handled pivot. His takeaway wasn’t anti-management—he frames it as a legitimate learning experience—but a recognition that management operates on a different axis of satisfaction, and the distance from hands-on technical work wasn’t one he wanted.

**Secret discretionary equity bonuses**

Beyond ratings and promotions, Simon also received what he describes as “secret” bonuses—discretionary equity grants outside any published compensation framework. These existed apart from the normal review cycle and weren’t something he knew to expect early on. They were tied directly to the outsized revenue impact he’d generated and the visibility his writing had created. The mechanism isn’t documented in any internal wiki; it’s something managers and directors can tap into when they need to retain someone whose contributions are disproportionate. The insight: the formal comp and promo system is a floor, not a ceiling. Truly exceptional business impact can unlock entirely off-cycle rewards, but only if your work is visible and legible to the people with discretionary budget authority.

---

Simon’s **“Delegate, don’t abdicate”** playbook and his **experiment-chasing engine** share a common root: an almost unnatural refusal to let responsibility diffuse. Where others hand off and move on, or close a failed test and ship something else, Simon stays tethered to the outcome—whether the work is done by him or by someone junior. That same thread runs through his **writing advice**: front-load the punchline because most people won’t dig for your impact; make it impossible to miss. His secret bonuses are the proof point at the end of that chain: impact that was pursued stubbornly, made legible through deliberate communication, and therefore visible to the people with discretionary equity to distribute.
