Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman and Ricky joint talk at UCLA (a Q&A-style event for their old club UPE, focused on industry secrets they wish they’d known before graduating):

**The best engineers aren’t just the best coders**

One of the first truths they wanted the students to absorb is that writing perfect code isn’t enough to be a great engineer. Ryan and Ricky both observed that the most impressive engineers they’ve worked with in industry were rarely the students at the very top of their college programming classes. Instead, the people who excel pair technical competence with the ability to influence others, work effectively in teams, and communicate concisely, especially in writing. “The best engineers usually weren’t the top programmers we met in college,” they told the room, “but rather the students with a balance of technical and soft skills.” The industry filters for a much broader set of skills than a CS curriculum does, and students who only optimize for grades often miss the collaboration and communication muscles that drive real-world impact.

**You have to advocate for yourself**

Because promotions are ultimately decided by people, doing great work in silence is a common trap. Ryan and Ricky stressed that you have to actively advocate for yourself. Visibility matters as much as the quality of your output—you need to make sure the right people know what you’ve accomplished and what you’re capable of. This isn’t about bragging; it’s about deliberately making your impact legible to decision-makers rather than assuming it will speak for itself. In an environment where managers and promo committees are overloaded with information, “the work you don’t surface might as well not have happened” (in spirit, if not in words, from how they framed it).

**Manufacturing luck for promotions**

Luck plays a real role in promotions, but Ryan and Ricky don’t treat it as something completely random. They described a posture of increasing your **luck surface area**: having the initiative to constantly look for new opportunities and staying ready to act when one appears. Doing more projects, meeting more people, and volunteering for stretch work all widen the pool of chances that something career-accelerating will come your way. “Having the initiative to look for new opportunities—and being ready when they come—will increase your luck surface area.” The core insight is that you can manufacture more “luck” by being more active; passivity shrinks your surface area to near zero.

**Big Tech vs. startups after college**

On the classic post-graduation dilemma, both Ryan and Ricky agreed they would choose Big Tech over a startup right out of school. Their reasoning is pragmatic: a well-known company gives you instant credibility on your resume and a solid foundation of engineering skills, processes, and mentorship that are harder to guarantee in a young startup. After a few years, you can pivot to a startup with both brand equity and a stronger skill set, and you’ll be better equipped to evaluate which startup is actually worth joining. It’s a “foundation first, bet later” philosophy.

**Impact is not lines of code**

One of the biggest mindset shifts from college to industry is understanding that impact means concrete, measurable outcomes, not effort or code volume. Ryan and Ricky were explicit: the tech industry rewards results. If anything, writing *less* code to achieve a larger outcome is the more impressive and desirable path. Someone who deletes 10,000 lines of legacy code and simplifies a system can have more impact than someone who ships 20,000 new ones. “Impact is just another way of saying the concrete, measurable outcomes of your work.” It is a proxy for the business or user value your work generates, not how hard you worked or how elegant the abstraction is.

**Big Tech’s financial reality**

Working your way up in a large tech company is a high-confidence path to financial stability and freedom, but Ryan and Ricky were careful to set expectations: Big Tech won’t make you “crazy rich.” If that level of extreme wealth is someone’s goal, taking a bet on an early-stage breakout startup is a more realistic route, albeit one with far higher risk. Big Tech offers a smoother, more predictable wealth-building curve; startups offer a lottery ticket. Neither is wrong, but confusing the two—expecting startup-like upside from a FANG career or Big Tech stability from a seed-stage company—is a costly mistake.

**Job hopping: early vs. late career**

They drew a sharp distinction between job hopping early in your career versus later. Early on, moving companies every couple of years can accelerate your learning, salary growth, and title progression. But after the Senior or Staff level, influence and credibility become the dominant force behind further promotion—and those take time to build within an organization. Hopping too frequently at that stage resets your political and social capital, becoming counterproductive just when you need it most to reach higher levels. “Later in your career, job hopping can be counterproductive” was the blunt summary.

**AI as a tool, not a replacement**

A question from the audience surfaced the anxiety about AI replacing software engineering jobs. Ryan and Ricky’s stance was pragmatic: engineers already use a vast array of non-AI tools to write code (IDEs, linters, frameworks, libraries), and LLMs are simply another productivity tool in that lineage. For the next five years, they wouldn’t worry about AI eliminating the need for software engineers. “Engineers already use many non-AI tools to write code. LLMs are tools that enable engineers to be more productive.” Beyond that horizon, they said, it’s genuinely impossible to say, but in the medium term they see enhancement, not replacement.

**College recruiting realities**

For students still in the job hunt, their advice was hands-on. The bare minimum to get noticed is having some projects on your resume that demonstrate real, applied work beyond coursework—proof you can ship something. After that, persistence matters. Being willing to take a role at a scrappier, less prestigious company early on can build experience faster than holding out for a name-brand job. Early reps compound faster than brand prestige.

**The internship-to-return-offer formula**

For internships specifically, they laid out a dead-simple formula. Most internships come with a well-defined project. The straightforward path to a return offer is to finish that project as quickly as possible with high-quality code. But the students who stand out don’t stop there—they propose meaningful improvements on top of the original scope. “If you can propose improvements afterward, even better.” That extra initiative signals ownership and senior-level thinking, even from an intern.

---

The through-line of the talk is that career growth is active, not passive—a posture captured in their **luck surface area** frame. Whether it’s advocating for your own visibility, choosing Big Tech first to build foundational credibility before chasing startup upside, or treating AI as just the next tool to master rather than a threat, the recurring message is that the students who treat their career as something they *shape* rather than *wait for* will pull ahead.
