Here are the takeaways from this Zach Wilson interview (went from new grad to Staff at Airbnb by age 26 through job hopping, deliberate brand-building, and hard-won behavioral growth):

**Junior (IC3) to IC4 in one half at Meta**

Zach joined Meta as a new grad and immediately focused on what he calls **aggressive expectation-setting** with his manager. In his very first week, he asked his manager point-blank: "What would it take for me to get promoted?" The answer was concrete—ship a certain number of diffs, own a project end-to-end, demonstrate independence. He treated this as a checklist and executed against it relentlessly, getting promoted to IC4 in a single half. The core insight he walked away with: "A lot of early-career engineers just kind of *wait* to be told what to do, and then they're surprised when promo doesn't happen." He was not shy about naming the goal up front and asking for the exact criteria. He also notes that he deliberately chose projects with high visibility and made sure his manager knew he was tracking against the promo rubric explicitly—turning a typically opaque process into a transparent scoreboard.

**Senior (IC5) promo blocked at Meta — the behaviors trap**

Zach's technical trajectory was strong enough that he was rated at the IC5 level on performance, but his actual promotion was repeatedly blocked. The issue wasn't capability—it was behavior. He admits he was abrasive in code review, overly argumentative in design discussions, and had developed a reputation for being hard to collaborate with. "I had the 'brilliant jerk' thing going on," he says, "and I didn't even realize it until a director pulled me aside and told me explicitly: *you are technically ready, but nobody wants to work with you at that level*." That conversation was a wake-up call. He learned that **behaviors gate promos** just as much as technical execution does, and that "high ratings plus low trust equals a permanent ceiling." He started deliberately softening his communication—asking questions instead of making declarations in design reviews, acknowledging when other people had good ideas, and investing in relationships across teams. But the reputation damage took time to undo, and by then, he was already looking elsewhere. The scar tissue from this period became the single biggest teacher of his career.

**Interviewing into IC5 at Netflix — network as leverage**

When Zach decided to leave Meta, he didn't apply through a portal. He reached out to a former coworker who had landed at Netflix and asked for a referral. That connection got his resume in front of the right hiring manager and skipped the initial recruiter screen. He then prepared obsessively for the Netflix culture memo and the technical interviews, but he stresses that **the initial wedge was entirely network-driven**. "Your internal brand doesn't just matter inside your company—it follows you. The people who saw me grinding at Meta were the same people who vouched for me externally." He also notes that Netflix's leveling is flatter, so coming in as a Senior (IC5 equivalent) there meant owning significantly larger scope than at Meta. He explicitly chose Netflix because he believed it would force him to grow faster, even if it was uncomfortable. The lesson: "Every job move I've made has come through someone I worked with before. I've never gotten a job through a cold application."

**Burning out on Staff (IC6) scope at Netflix — behaviors round two**

Netflix gave Zach Staff-level scope almost immediately: he was responsible for a critical payments platform handling billions in transactions, with high reliability requirements and cross-functional stakeholders across product, legal, and finance. At one point, a bug he shipped cost the company roughly $150K in a single day—a searing lesson in the weight of the platform he was now responsible for. The work itself was technically demanding, but what burned him out was the same behavioral pattern resurfacing in a new environment. He defaulted to "hero mode"—working 60-hour weeks, insisting on owning the most critical pieces himself, pushing back hard on any design he disagreed with. A senior leader told him: "You're delivering, but you're also a bottleneck. A Staff engineer's job is to *multiply* the team, not *be* the team." He realized he was failing at delegation and influence. "I was treating Netflix like a bigger Meta, but the behaviors that got me to Senior were actively blocking Staff. At Staff, your primary deliverables are *other engineers*, not just code." The burnout was real—he left Netflix after roughly 18 months, calling it "the most humbling experience of my career." He didn't just leave; he was let go, a fact he's open about as a pivotal inflection point that forced him to reckon with his own patterns.

**Interviewing into IC6 at Airbnb — level negotiation through competing offers**

After Netflix, Zach took a deliberate approach to his next job hunt. He interviewed at multiple companies simultaneously including Stripe, Airbnb, and a couple of startups, and secured multiple offers. Critically, he negotiated on *level*, not just compensation. Airbnb initially came in at IC5, but he used his other IC6-equivalent offers to push back, framing the conversation around scope alignment: "I'm already doing IC6 work and I want to join a team where I can hit the ground running at that level, not spend a year proving myself for a promo." Airbnb's hiring committee relented and brought him in at Staff. He says the lesson is that **companies will give you the level they think they can get away with**, and having competing offers is the strongest leverage to recalibrate that assumption. But he also notes this only works if your interview performance is strong enough that the hiring committee is genuinely afraid of losing you. He was 26 when he started at Airbnb as a Staff engineer—a title that typically takes a decade or more to reach.

**How to build credibility fast — the Zach Wilson playbook**

Zach has a repeatable methodology for ramping up credibility at a new company, which he's now executed at Meta, Netflix, and Airbnb. He calls it **land-and-expand credibility**. The playbook: (1) In the first 30 days, identify the single most painful operational or developer-experience problem on the team and fix it—something tangible like a flaky test suite, a slow build pipeline, or an on-call rotation that's burning people out. "Ship a win in the first month that everyone on the team feels immediately." At Airbnb, he fixed a CI pipeline that was taking 45 minutes, bringing it down to 12, and "suddenly every engineer on the team knew my name and associated it with making their life better." (2) Write a detailed, high-quality design doc for something in your team's upcoming roadmap and circulate it widely—not to push your own agenda, but to demonstrate rigorous thinking and solicit genuine feedback. "People judge your competence by the quality of your artifacts early on. One great design doc buys you six months of trust." (3) Publicize your work internally through whatever channel the company uses—present at an internal tech talk, write a post in the engineering-wide Slack, or send a summary email after a difficult incident. "Your work doesn't speak for itself. You have to narrate it." He stresses that this isn't self-promotion for vanity's sake—it's about making your competence *legible* to people who don't work directly with you, which is essential at Senior+ levels where your influence must extend beyond your immediate team.

**Reflecting on regrets — what he would have changed**

If he could go back, Zach says he would have invested in his behavioral growth *two years earlier* than he did. "The technical skills compound fast early on, but the leadership skills compound even faster and I was late to that game." He regrets that his time at Netflix ended the way it did—he thinks if he had stayed and genuinely worked on the delegation and influence problems instead of falling back into hero mode, he might have broken through to a more mature operating model rather than having to restart his reputation somewhere else. He also regrets not finding a mentor sooner. At Meta, he mostly kept his head down; at Netflix, he operated in isolation; only at Airbnb did he deliberately seek out a Staff+ mentor outside his chain of command who could give him honest, unvarnished feedback. "If I could tell my 22-year-old self one thing, it would be: find someone who'll tell you the truth about yourself, not just about your code." He says the single biggest unlock in his career was realizing that **your technical ceiling is almost never the bottleneck—your relational ceiling is**. The flip side of his unusually fast leveling is that he had to learn this lesson the hard way, in public, across three different companies.

---

His whole arc is an accelerated case study in his own phrase: **behaviors gate promos**. The **aggressive expectation-setting** that propelled him from IC3 to IC4 at Meta was necessary but insufficient—the same intensity, unchecked, became the **"brilliant jerk" behavior** that blocked IC5 and later imploded his Netflix scope. His **land-and-expand credibility** playbook is, in a sense, the counterweight: a deliberate system for rebuilding trust through visible wins, rigorous artifacts, and narrated impact when raw technical force has proven self-limiting. The capstone lesson—**"your relational ceiling, not your technical ceiling"**—is the through-line he had to learn across three companies before it stuck.
