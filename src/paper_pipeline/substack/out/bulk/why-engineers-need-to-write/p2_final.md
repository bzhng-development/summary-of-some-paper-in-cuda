Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (the host of The Peterman Post on technical careers, making the case for writing as a core engineering skill):

**Writing is the job, not a side quest**

Ryan opens with a confession: he hated writing in high school. It wasn’t objective like math and science, and parsing Shakespeare didn’t help. "My perspective on writing changed once I started working full-time as a software engineer." The shift began when he became a mid-level engineer and led a small workstream of a few engineers—he was writing more and more without consciously deciding to. "Soon writing became a significant part of my work outside of coding." COVID accelerated the trend, moving most conversations to async chats and word docs, cementing writing as the primary medium of collaboration.

The argument isn't subtle: "Almost everything software engineers do requires writing." Asking technical questions, commenting on code reviews, creating design docs—all of it is writing. "This is because writing software is collaborative." The corollary is that writing skill isn't a nice-to-have; "the better you are at writing, the more effective you will be at building software." He’s not carving out a separate skill to practice—he’s naming the medium in which engineering already happens.

**Scaling yourself through writing**

Beyond daily necessity, writing is "the most efficient way for you to scale yourself." He gives a concrete example: a friend who takes incredible notes at work, turning them into a living wiki of common terminal commands, code pointers, and diagrams for the whole team. "The best part is that his writing helps others move faster without any work on his part after sharing it." This is leverage in its purest form: effort expended once, value extracted repeatedly.

He frames the career math bluntly: "At the end of the day, you can only ship so much code by yourself. You can have much more impact by influencing others to ship what matters most." And "writing is the most scalable way to influence others." The progression from solo contributor to force multiplier runs directly through writing.

**Writing improves thinking, with or without AI**

Some worry ChatGPT will make writing obsolete. Ryan counters that writing's value persists regardless. "Writing your thoughts down forces them into a coherent, logical narrative. Condensing your writing gives you a deeper understanding. This process improves your thinking." Writing doesn’t just clarify existing ideas—"it generates more of them." When he writes design docs, he often discovers optimizations or edge cases he hadn’t considered initially. "So long as critical thinking is necessary, writing will not be obsolete."

He closes by observing: "It is no coincidence that the strongest engineers I know are all excellent technical writers." The skill is not orthogonal to engineering effectiveness; it is central. That conviction is what prompted his own commitment to writing once a week for his Substack—a practice that is both the medium and the message.

---

His arc from writing-averse student to someone who names writing as **the job itself** mirrors the leverage argument he makes: writing scaled him from individual contributor to influencer, and now from engineer to public voice. His "scaling yourself" frame and the conviction that writing **improves thinking** converge on a single, simple thesis—**the better you are at writing, the more effective you will be at building software**—and he’s betting that committing to it weekly is the most honest way to prove it.
