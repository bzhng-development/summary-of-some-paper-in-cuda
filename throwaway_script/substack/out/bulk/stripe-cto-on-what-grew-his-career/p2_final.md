Here are the takeaways from this David Singleton interview (Stripe CTO for 7 years, went from junior engineer to VP at Google, now building /dev/agents):

**Getting into programming and his first role at Symbian**

David Singleton’s origin story is unusually outcome-focused for a kid in the late eighties. When his family got a computer for the parents’ business, he didn’t write games — he built them bespoke invoicing software. Before that, his parents manually tabulated their quarterly tax returns, which made them “super grumpy.” After his system, “you could press a button and it came out of the printer.” That early taste of software’s real-world impact gave him “the bug” and sent him into computer science. The thread from that moment to Stripe’s mission — “increase the GDP of the internet” — is a straight line, and he’ll reference it decades later.

His first industry job came at Symbian in 2001, the company building smartphone operating systems before smartphones existed. He landed there largely by accident: his planned job in Belfast evaporated in the dot-com bust, and he thought the Symbian folks “were really lovely people.” Once inside, he found himself whiteboarding turn-by-turn GPS navigation years before it shipped, deep in core Bluetooth and PC-connect code. That alone was formative, but the real intellectual spark came from a role-shift near the end: he became a “technical consultant,” effectively a forward-deployed engineer assigned to handset manufacturers (Panasonic, Samsung, Nokia) who were turning Symbian’s “bag of bits” into real products.

The right way to help each team was completely different — setting up source control for one, debugging a graphics issue for another — and he started noticing how engineering culture shaped outcomes. At Samsung, multiple tiny teams were given the same mission; the first to finish shipped. At Nokia, everything was intricately planned, more predictable, but much slower. It was here that he “became a bit of a geek for thinking not only about what software we were writing but how teams were organized to get it done.” That meta-interest in org design seeded everything that followed.

**Joining Google and building agency**

Post-IPO, Google decided to open a London engineering office focused on mobile, recognizing the city’s concentration of Symbian-era talent. Singleton attended a recruiting event, went through a tough process (his first trip to California), and immediately felt the talent density. He expected to stay a couple of years and didn’t leave for nearly 12, because “every 18 months or two years, I’d pick up my head…and realize I learned a tremendous amount.” He’d do roughly six different jobs inside Google with “gradual transitions between them.”

A formative pattern early on: he’d chase problems outside his lane purely from technical curiosity, and that agency drove impact and scope. The canonical story is the mobile redirect. Every mobile user visiting google.com was hit with a 2.5-second round-trip redirect to google.com/m. Everyone told him it was “too hard” and “really complicated.” His stance: “it’s just software, so I must be able to figure it out.” He learned the front-end infrastructure, merged a change in the main Google web server, and eliminated the redirect — saving something like 70 years of user time per year and producing a noticeable usage bump. “You get a real bug for those things — being able to just follow your nose to learn and take agency to do stuff that matters.”

The motivation initially was pure technical curiosity. That changed when a product manager named Gummi (Icelandic) started translating product metrics into population analogies: first Iceland, then Ireland, then Scotland, then Germany. “I found myself picturing the whole population of Iceland using our product in a day.” Google taught him to think about impact at scale, and that’s been his driver ever since.

**Deciding to try management — and a defense of the TLM role**

The fork came surprisingly. He was an IC having a blast, and walked into his manager Dave’s office saying he wanted to be a product manager. His manager countered: “If you really want that, we can figure out how to make it happen, but you should be an engineering manager.” The pitch was that as a TLM (Tech Lead Manager), he’d get the fulfillment of impact plus the ability to keep doing technical work. Singleton took it, and acknowledges he was also solving a staffing problem for his manager.

The TLM period was intense. During the day he managed; after hours he built personal projects to scratch the engineering itch — including the first version of Google Translate for iPhone, which gave him a chance to interact with a whole new group. He’s well aware that many people warn against the TLM role (“you’re doing two jobs; it’s difficult to excel at either one”), and his counter is structural: the IC projects were separate from the teams he managed. When they’re the same team, it’s harder because you can “get sucked into your own personal work and lose track of what every individual on the team also needs.” The key is “a constant prompt to go into this different mode” — something he later formalized with a framework from a management coach at Stripe named Jeff Lawrence: **step onto the balcony**. “You’re on the dance floor doing stuff in the org, but you have to prompt yourself to step onto the balcony and look down at how everything is working.” For a TLM, that means consciously scheduling moments to switch from builder to manager.

**The biggest gap when moving to manager-of-managers**

At Google he won the “Great Manager Award” as a frontline manager — an award voted on by the teams themselves — and now says “I don’t think I deserved it.” The reason crystallizes a key shift in perspective: “Those teams that are perfectly happy are rarely performing at their very best potential.” As a manager-of-managers, you see the portfolio. You’ve got one manager who has a good sense of direction, moves distractions out of the way, and pushes their team a little; you’ve got another who does what Singleton used to do (focusing on making everyone happy). “The answer for me was to help them get more out of the team.”

The toolkit changes. A line manager focuses on one-on-ones, goal-setting, getting into the craft with individuals. A manager-of-managers starts designing the operating system of the org: “How do all the things we do every day, week, month, quarter, and year fit together to help us get the most out of everybody?” Product reviews, hiring processes, the cadence of operational rhythms. Singleton, a distributed-systems geek, found it “quite exciting to think about the organization I was running as if it were a little bit of an operating system.”

He draws a deliberate contrast between surface-level happiness and what turns out to be the best experience in retrospect. At Stripe he encountered the concept of **type two fun**: “the fun that you think of as some of the best times in your life when you look back on it, but it’s kind of hard in the moment.” His goal as a manager is to create a lot of type two fun — “let’s look back on it and think we got a lot done in that time.” When Ryan asks whether a too-happy team is a red flag, he clarifies: it’s not about making people unhappy; it’s that the folks with constructive feedback and a desire to change things tend to have the best retrospective experience. “There should be some balance,” just like teams that aren’t breaking anything are probably moving too slow.

**The VP jump: leading functions beyond engineering**

At Google, the boundary between being a very senior manager and a VP was taking on responsibility for multiple functions, not just engineering. Title was secondary to scope. His mental model shifted. Earlier, he had “tended to think Google was a classically engineering-first company” and that other functions existed to feed engineers. Running a multi-function org (product, engineering, UX, marketing, partnerships on Android Wear) disproved that. If you don’t have PMs, design, and business deals aligned on the right problem, “you’re going to have much less fun and much less impact on the world.”

The personal challenge was confidence. He could jump into any engineering seat; he couldn’t do the same in design or marketing. “I have designers who report to me. I can tell you I am a pretty poor designer.” The adjustment was learning to have high-bandwidth conversations about work he couldn’t himself execute, which “took a real adjustment…a little bit of a personal confidence thing.” He later applied the same logic in reverse at Stripe, doing what he called a **sales-acation** — shadowing salespeople to understand their tools — showing the same deliberate practice of putting himself at the “one foot level” of functions he didn’t personally do.

**Engineering leader communication: writing, speaking, and “always on stage”**

On speaking: it’s pure practice. Early in his career he was nervous presenting to the Android org. A supportive manager gave him “more at bats” — including speaking to auditoria of a thousand Google customers with live demos. “I look back on that and think about how today I probably do a much better job, but getting the reps in is what makes that go well.” Ryan notes that Singleton’s keynote presence (Stripe Sessions 2019) immediately radiates confidence, and Singleton credits the years of deliberate practice, not innate ability: “if you had tried to put me on that Stripe Session stage in 2012, you would’ve had a completely different conclusion.”

One non-obvious lesson: as a senior leader, you’re **always on stage** — walking the office, hallway conversations, random meetings. “Folks pay a lot of attention to how you’re doing. They’ll take a lot of their own confidence in the plans of the company from the body language of the CTO.” That means every interaction is an opportunity to reinforce the overarching message. It also requires “dynamic range” — if everything’s awesome all the time, nobody believes you when it actually is. “I find it valuable to take a moment before I open my mouth in any meeting…and think, what am I actually trying to communicate here?”

On writing: he uses Claude for help and stresses that condensing something important into a short, clear document is incredibly leveraged — people will pass it around, read it more than once. He adopted a practice from Stripe of sending drafts to reviewers and credits that with improving his quality immensely. “As an author of a piece, you always have a mental model of what am I trying to convince someone of or convey…It’s quite common that I would share something I’ve written…and be completely blown away by the feedback.” He’s clear this isn’t for every email — only for the important stuff.

For people running all-hands where everyone is on laptops, his advice is basic: prepare. “They’re all spending 15, 20 minutes of their time listening to you. The least you can do is spend 30 minutes of your time preparing.” He also cites a leader at Google named Alan Ti who modeled a powerful technique: when asked a question, always take one step back and explain the context behind it so that people unfamiliar with the problem can follow and learn something broader. “I aspire to be like Alan. I’m certainly not that good.”

**Scaling yourself through organizational design**

His framework for high-leverage scaling as a senior leader: start thinking about the rituals, processes, and systems of the org — not just people. As an early-career engineer at Symbian, he had thought “process” was a dirty word (they had a literal process library tied to ISO 9001 certification that nobody followed but retroactively filled out). When someone later told him to “really think about the processes in your organization,” he initially recoiled, then realized they meant “the rituals and how we work and how we get stuff done.”

The concrete example is the **ops review** — a nested set of weekly operational-review meetings that he later built at Stripe (modeled explicitly on Amazon’s version, after a lunch with Charlie Bell who walked him through it). It cascaded through all engineering teams and became the primary tool for systematically improving reliability, efficiency, and performance. That’s a completely different lever than coaching direct reports — it’s “very hard to impact through just talking to your directs and having it trickle down.”

The trap he identifies: in many companies, the person running a process isn’t the person who needed it, so they have no incentive to ask “is this still fit for purpose?” As a leader, your job is to make sure a process “only exists as long as it is clearly a net positive for everyone responsible for operating within it.” If you delegate it with context on the problem rather than just the instruction, outcomes change: “Here’s the problem we’re trying to solve and I think that we should probably do this, you go do it and please take agency in making sure that we’re really solving the real problem.”

**The playbook for building a remote engineering site**

Ryan invokes a previous guest, Philip Su (Facebook London site lead), who credited Singleton with the best advice on launching a remote office. Singleton and Su were direct competitors poaching the same London talent pool, and eventually decided to talk. That speaks to a Silicon Valley norm Singleton loves: “even some of the fiercest competitors will help each other in the craft.”

The advice itself, forged at Google London (eight-plus time zones from Mountain View):

1. **Give every team a clear, complete mission and full autonomy.** For eight hours when HQ isn’t awake, everyone’s cranking independently. The few cross-site dependencies fit into the narrow overlap window.
2. **Never split a team across a wide time-zone gap.** “The worst way to set up a team like that would be to have half the team in Mountain View and half the team in London” — you generate so many cross-site unblocks daily that progress grinds to a halt inside the tiny overlap window.
3. **The site lead’s highest-leverage activity is being at HQ.** His predecessor Shannon spent little time in London because the best thing he could do was be in Mountain View building relationships, making the team visible, and bringing context back. Singleton traveled heavily so his engineers didn’t have to.
4. **Create a “clan” identity rooted in the broader company culture.** London had its own t-shirts, laptop stickers, and discussions about what kind of work the site excelled at. That identity motivates people to fix local friction that might otherwise feel too small. But the identity can’t diverge into “everyone on the other side of the ocean is a bozo” — it has to be “rooted in the culture of the whole company because that’s what everyone is there for.”

Ryan notes that the time-zone rule implies: the further apart, the more autonomous each site must be. Singleton concurs. On the post-COVID remote question, he distinguishes between offices and true remote, and lands on a nuanced position: remote can be almost as effective as co-located if the company has a document-based decision-making culture and periodic in-person bonding (every six months can be enough). “There is no substitute for spending time together in person to build up personal rapport, but you don’t have to spend every single day in person.” That said, for his current tiny startup, “the very best is if you’re actually all jamming in one spot together.”

**What kept him at Google — and why he left**

After shipping Android Wear and being promoted to VP, he stayed another ~2.5 years because of “unfinished business” — plans with watch manufacturers already in motion, and he was still learning. The decision to leave arrived as a specific sensation: “if I think about the last year at this company, what I had done was exactly the same as the year before. You could change the code names of all the projects, but it was essentially like turning the crank on the same thing again.” He realized he hadn’t learned much, and since “I’m here to learn,” that was the wake-up call. This aligns with Ryan’s observation that people who succeed at a single company for a long time aren’t staying out of complacency — they’re continuously re-evaluating whether the company is still growing them.

The off-ramp moment intersected with a plan to start a company with Philip Su. Their idea: a platform for fractional professional workers (lawyers, designers) in the gig economy, providing stability, benefits, and career development — think Fiverr but with proper employment and professional growth. He still thinks it’s a good idea (“if someone out there wants to build this company, give me a call”). Complicated personal circumstances paused the startup, which led to seeking advice from Stripe.

**Joining Stripe: from advice-seeker to CTO**

Singleton reached out to Stripe for advice on using Stripe Connect for his startup idea. He’d heard the adage: “anytime you want money, ask for advice, and anytime you want advice, ask for money.” In classic fashion, the advice conversation turned into a recruitment. Patrick Collison, Stripe’s co-founder and CEO, followed up in person, and Singleton “immediately” felt drawn to the mission — “increase the GDP of the internet” — which resonated with his origin story of building invoicing software for his parents. He also realized “to my surprise, a lot of what they needed back then to scale the company was things that I’d done before” (running remote engineering sites, building new products).

He joined the leadership team; the CTO title came later and explicitly wasn’t something he cared about. “I was joining the leadership team, the core leadership team of the company. And that was important to me.” The title was adopted mostly because it helped with external signaling — customers, partners, speaking engagements. “I really like that kind of culture where we’re not necessarily listened to because we have a title. And that’s certainly the culture at Stripe.” But “when you think about interfacing with the rest of the world, this can matter.”

**Comparing cultures: Stripe’s operating principles and Amazon’s influence**

Singleton’s comparison frame is shaped by having gone deep at only one big company (Google), but having studied others deliberately. Google’s culture was phenomenal — high talent density, huge agency, the real 20% time — but “it was not very deliberately designed.” It grew organically. By contrast, Stripe’s leadership was intentional. They distilled **operating principles** from the behaviors of the most effective Stripes. These principles are concrete, observable, and periodically reviewed. They’re different from abstract values.

Two principles stood out for him:
- **Users first** (the Amazon “customer obsession” parallel).
- **Be meticulous in your craft.** “Taking pride in how well things are done for the sake of how well they’re done.” This shows up in Stripe’s API error messages — instead of a bare 404, Stripe runs extra code to say, “Not found, but an object with that ID exists in the other mode.” Singleton heard CTOs of huge companies say they were inspired by those error messages and brought the energy into their own culture. “It ends up being incredibly valuable.”

When Stripe needed to scale reliability, they intentionally looked to Amazon’s systematic org design for inspiration, and Singleton explicitly modeled the ops review on Amazon’s version after a lunch with Charlie Bell. “Amazon is definitely the company with the very systematic org and operational design that over time I’ve taken a lot of inspiration from.”

**Setting and enforcing culture**

Singleton says you should care from day one (his co-founders literally wrote down values on the day of incorporation), but the mechanisms scale with the company. “Running an early stage company is an exercise in being smacked in the face quite often.” Early values help you know where to compromise and where not. Later, culture becomes something you enforce by **being an exemplar** and remembering that **“culture is the behaviors you accept.”** He tells the story of a senior leader who stopped to clean up a spilled drink on the stairs — “they clearly had way more important things to do that day” — but the signal was that walking past a mess is not okay. The story went around the company.

For enforcement, he used a **Walk the Store** practice: dogfooding the product publicly in all-hands, not to call people out but to convey the aspiration. He also wrote **friction logs**: deliberately putting on a user hat and documenting every little frustrating thing, which became input for team priorities.

**Reliability at Stripe — and why it didn’t cost speed**

The famous Stripe reliability story (multiple nines, now reportedly six nines) started with a cultural inflection point in 2020. The pandemic drove a surge of offline-to-online businesses, systems scaled faster than ever imagined, and reliability became existential. “It was mostly a cultural change that was important to realize.” The tools were intentionality, the nested ops reviews, and giving every team the data to make trade-off decisions scientifically.

Ryan raises the intuitive objection: reliability isn’t free; it should slow you down. Singleton disagrees — at least in Stripe’s case, they achieved massive reliability gains while also making the system more efficient. His framework splits unreliability into two causes: (1) capacity shortfalls (you need over-provisioning), and (2) changes breaking things (you need guardrails). Stripe’s investment went heavily into the second bucket: automated testing, gradual deployment of changes, detecting failures before they’re critical — for code, data, and feature flags. “If you start paying attention to what’s causing problems and then asking five whys behind them to see what changes we can make, I think you’ll find that you can cover enormous ground.”

The payoff was an environment where every engineer’s merged change went to production the same day through automated gradual release processes — a stark contrast to his Google experience where it took a month from merge to full production. “It’s so much more fun when you can make a change and the same day you can show it to your friends.” He recommends the book *Accelerate* by Nicole Forsgren.

**Hiring at scale without Leetcode**

Stripe’s funnel started with its product: ship great developer documentation and a beautiful API, and great engineers reach out because they want to work on what they’ve already loved using. “We considered building the docs part of building the product.”

On evaluation: they rejected whiteboard interviews and instead designed an interview where candidates pair-programmed on a laptop with all their usual tools, working through exercises “very deliberately designed to ensure that we were selecting for folks that wanted to be meticulous in their craft.” Maintaining this required constant question-refresh because questions inevitably leaked. It was a volunteer effort from passionate engineers, and Singleton as a leader made sure to recognize them publicly at all-hands and make it count toward advancement. The lesson: it’s hard work, but “if you tell me that Meta or Google don’t have the resources to build good engineering interviews, I don’t believe you. They could totally do this if they wanted to. It’s really just a case of a revealed preference of how much they care about it.”

**Lessons from Stripe’s leadership: first-principles thinking**

Singleton’s biggest learning from the leadership team: **think from first principles.** At Google, his reflex was “how did we do it last time?” and replicating that. At Stripe, the constant refrain was “from first principles, what is actually going on here? Can we deeply understand the dynamics?” Part of that was talking to four or five people at other companies who’d faced similar problems, then triangulating. That can sound slow, but “if you’re applying that in the most consequential places, it’s amazing how much you can benefit from multiple perspectives.” He estimates 80% of the time the pattern is familiar and you just go; 20% of the time it’s subtly different and the deliberate step-back changes the answer.

**Why leave Stripe — and what /dev/agents is building**

Singleton left to found /dev/agents, building what he calls “a next-generation operating system” for AI agents and agentic apps to work for everyone — a consumer platform, not enterprise. The intellectual thread runs straight back to Symbian and Android. He worked on smartphones “before they were a thing” and spent years inventing permissions models, UI paradigms, and application affordances. Hugo (his co-founder) was early product on Android. Nicholas (the other co-founder) designed core mobile interfaces and worked on Chrome. At Stripe, he used early LLMs (GPT-4 era) to build what they later realized were the world’s first AI agents, and he got “really obsessed with the idea that this is as breakthrough a technology as the early days of mobile.”

The analogy shapes the strategy: just as the iPhone and Android had to invent new paradigms beyond what Nokia and Sony Ericsson did in the early days, he doesn’t think the first movers in AI agents will necessarily win. “It is not a foregone conclusion that the folks that are applying technology in any space at the very beginning are the ones that end up making the biggest impact.” His approach is differentiated (he won’t yet say how), driven by asking what consumers actually need — compounding value when multiple agents work together, context on the user, privacy and security solved in a way that creates real value.

**Career reflections: what went well, what he’d change**

His entire career can be seen as “focusing on always making sure that I was learning.” He deliberately chose roles outside his comfort zone — most notably joining the ads team at Google when the obvious path was Android. “I had no idea how Google made money.” He was reasonably senior in an org full of domain experts and had to learn at “a tremendous rate,” but “man, it was awesome. I understand how all of those auction systems work…I wouldn’t know that if I hadn’t taken that leap.”

His biggest regret: as an early engineer, he didn’t speak up in meetings because he assumed his manager and their manager knew it all. “What you realize as you go through your career is the people who have the most context for everything going on are the folks that are actually doing the building at the one-foot level.” He was sometimes sitting there thinking “I think they’re wrong, but they must know” — and he actually did know because he was doing the work. The advice he’d give his younger self: “Assume that they’re wrong and speak up.” In good companies, this is always rewarded. “If you’re in an organization that does not reward that, maybe go work somewhere else.”

**Books and habits**

The book: *High Output Management* by Andy Grove. “Almost every way of working that I ran into at Google, and assumed that Google had invented, actually came from Andy Grove” — OKRs, efficient one-to-ones, the whole lineage. It’s short enough to read on a transatlantic flight.

The habit: every Sunday night, he writes a to-do list for the week in a doc titled with a rocket emoji — his **rocket doc**. “If I have a great week this week, what did I get done?” During the week, that’s where he starts, not email or calendar. “Don’t go to your inbox, don’t go to your calendar. Go to the rocket doc.” Later, his executive assistants got read access and would ping him midweek: “how are we doing against these things?” They started chasing him to do the stuff he wanted to do. “It was amazing.”

One more habit: exercise. “Every time I was finding myself being pretty grumpy at work and a bit unhappy, it was because I’d forgotten that you have to exercise.”

---

Singleton’s career can be read as a long proof of his own advice: **focus on always learning**. His **first-principles thinking** mantra from Stripe’s leadership, his deliberate **friction logs** and **engineer-acations** to stay at the “one-foot level,” and his **step onto the balcony** framework for managers together paint a picture of a leader who treats self-correction as a core skill. Even his belief that “culture is the behaviors you accept” circles back to learning — from the spilled-drink story to the **Walk the Store** practice, he built systems that forced himself and his orgs to keep seeing reality. The through-line from building invoicing software for his parents to chasing a consumer AI-agent platform is simply a learner’s refusal to “turn the crank on the same thing again.”
