Here are the takeaways from this Michael Novati interview (went from intern to Senior Staff / IC7 by 27 at Meta, known as the #1 code committer):

**Joining Facebook as an intern in 2009**

Michael Novati joined Facebook as an intern in May 2009, the summer between undergrad and starting a PhD in HCI at the University of Washington. He already had housing and a research assistantship lined up, but wanted hands-on work experience at a top company first. He only applied to Facebook and Google and got interviews easily by just applying online—"nowadays it seems a lot harder." The stars aligned when he randomly interviewed with an engineer working on internal collaboration tools (diff review, tasks, discussion tools), exactly what he wanted to build. The company had about 220 people then, give or take depending on whether production engineers were counted. What drew him in was both technical and social. On day one, he opened his dev environment to find it running Apache locally, hitting a MySQL database just like a side project—"it just felt like a really good fit. I just sat down and felt I was at home." Socially, Facebook was just really cool at the time, a product everyone knew and talked about, and getting constant feedback from friends was motivating.

Ryan notes PHP scared off many engineers who joined later, but Novati explains that Facebook's approach was to customize the language at a compiler level to make it better than anything off-the-shelf. The evolution to Hack—with loose typing added early—was an optimization path rather than a "rewrite in Java or Python" panic. On culture, he was the quintessential **move fast and break things** person, but clarifies that "break things" was never about recklessly causing havoc. It was about **breaking norms**, a spirit closer to first-principles thinking than to shattering glass. Later, when the company matured and the smallest bug could have tens of millions of dollars of impact, he understood why "break things" was dropped from the motto, even if he still feels the original framing had merit.

**The IPO moment and the stock that tanked**

The 2012 IPO was a turning point. Mark Zuckerberg was grounded and kept it humble—"IPOing is a very rational thing to do to raise funding for the company. It's not a party." But the Nasdaq brought the bell-opening button to campus, and Zuck's parents stood right beside Novati in the crowd, tears in their eyes. He later told Zuck about that moment. Financially, nobody's stock vested until six months after the IPO, so no one was suddenly rich on the day. When it did vest, the stock had tanked—he remembers sitting in a movie theater with about twenty coworkers when everyone's phones lit up with "your vested stock has been deposited." He held all his IPO shares and still has them, but eventually began selling on vest to diversify. Facebook provided financial-literacy classes taught by "the old guard" who'd gone through the Google and Yahoo IPOs, which he found invaluable as someone from Canada who had never dealt with stock-based compensation. His advice to engineers comparing offers today: single-digit percentage differences feel huge, but stocks swing wildly for reasons beyond your control—prioritize a company where you'll fit and perform, because your performance over time is more within your control than guessing a stock price.

**The Weekly NTI newsletter and the cost of being unfiltered**

Novati ran an internal newsletter called the *Weekly NTI* on Facebook's Notes platform. He'd worked on Notes extensively at hackathons—he added rich text editing in a single, infamous 11,000-line diff that required an in-person meeting to review because nobody could handle it asynchronously. His posts were often off-the-cuff and sometimes controversial. One argued Facebook needed a San Francisco office (Stripe and Uber were poaching engineers), and eventually a South San Francisco office did open. Another post openly called out the disparity between canonical software engineers who could change teams easily and network/data engineers who couldn't. He didn't realize that was already a simmering tension behind the scenes, and his post forced executives into damage control—"Michael, I'm working on this problem. It didn't really help us to call this out." HR gave him feedback when he wrote about which competitors were poaching Facebook talent with large stock grants, warning it might inadvertently incentivize people to explore other opportunities. Concerned, he messaged Zuck directly. Zuck was totally fine with it and encouraged him to keep posting.

In retrospect, though, Novati would not do the newsletter again. He had no strategic goal—no performance-review angle, no influence-building. He just believed in being open and direct. But the friction it created with executives, the unintended consequences of certain posts that sounded like "damage control" triggers, made him realize that "that off-the-cuff style does have consequences." He still posts on Reddit and LinkedIn with a similarly unfiltered voice, but he's more aware of the trade-offs now.

**Working with Zuck and the Lockdown bet**

Novati had a working relationship with Mark Zuckerberg that began when the company was small. He worked with Zuck on a 2009 hackathon to let anyone put any emoji on any post as a reaction—an idea that sounded wild then but is now the norm everywhere. The code was "just so bad" it never shipped, but Zuck never gave up on it; when the Reactions launch finally happened years later under PM Sammy Krug, Novati thought it was so much better than Zuck's original code. During the 2010 Lockdown period—a company-wide sprint to ship features fast in response to the threat of Google Plus—Novati made a public bet at a company Q&A with Zuck. If Zuck committed code by the end of Lockdown, Novati would stop ripsticking (riding a two-wheeled skateboard that required hip-wiggling) around the office. Zuck did merge a PR, and the ripstick ban followed—though the ban's enactment had its own absurd twist: the head of HR emailed the five most prominent ripstickers for feedback on the draft ban email before announcing it, a classic buy-in technique Novati only recognized years later.

**The engineers who impressed him and the coding-machine archetype**

His role models when he joined were engineers whose names were "all over the code base"—Evan Priestley, Putnam, and the product-infrastructure team building what would become the React framework. Priestley, the #1 committer before Novati, single-handedly wrote Facebook's entire diff-review tool suite (DiffCamp, later Phabricator). Novati calls him the canonical **coding machine**: doing something people didn't think was possible and making it actually happen. The framework architects like Nick Shrock and Ola impressed him differently—their specialty was creating elegant, performant abstractions that reduced cognitive overhead for every engineer. He respects that making things simpler is sometimes harder work than writing a lot of code. If he could pick between Evan Priestley and four senior engineers for a project in a comfortable codebase, he'd take Priestley. But for something brand-new, diverse backgrounds might matter more; a coding machine needs time to absorb a codebase before the magic activates. The canonical example of what a coding machine uniquely delivers: he manually refactored thousands of classes to remove a legacy data-fetching framework called "Preferables" until the parent class itself could be deleted. A team wouldn't have prioritized it; one person with relentless grit could.

**Will LLMs kill the coding machine?**

When Novati was doing these massive refactors, he used Vim, a proprietary indexed code-search tool called TBGS, and a tab-completion plugin. No VS Code, no Copilot. So moving to modern tools is an extension of going from Vim to VS Code, and the current wave of LLM tools is yet another step-function in productivity. In his current role (working in a ~500,000-line codebase), he's producing roughly **five times more code** than he was six months ago, purely through prompt-optimization intuition. He already knows the code he wants to write; the LLM just gets it out faster. When agentic flows mature and AI can independently maintain its own services, he thinks code as we know it will change fundamentally—an AI might not write JavaScript, it might manage its own opaque services behind an API contract, and nobody will read the code. He doesn't see a dystopian overnight shift. Instead, he urges engineers to focus on making the AI world "the best that it can be instead of pushing back." The transitional phase, though, will be one where experienced engineers with strong taste get dramatically more productive, while junior engineers find it harder to build that taste.

**Operating as an IC7: the 30/70 split and the judgment layer**

At his peak, Novati spent about 30% of his mental space on his team's actual roadmap and 70% on codebase-wide initiatives, cleanup refactorings, and one-off cross-org problems. The split wasn't a time-sheet; it was a focus allocation. He often reported to more-junior frontline managers while really working under his skip's guidance—"I would almost help them manage the team and then really report to the skip." A VP-level leader he met with monthly. The raw output never really changed from his first week, when he merged two competing task-tool UIs in a single, unannounced sprint and posted to the whole company, "The tools are merged. It's done. Deal with it." What grew was **judgment and taste**—knowing which code areas were sensitive, how to manage the push engineers whose trust he needed not to ship broken code to production, and when to say "if this breaks the site, I will resign." He said that more than once, meaning it—the pressure made him triple-check every line and think through every edge case. He defines taste as like building a patina on a cast-iron pan: layers of experience burned in over years. You can't read a book on speed-skating and then enter the Olympics; you need thousands of hours feeling the ice under different temperatures, the sharpness of blades used once versus twice. The same applies to knowing what to touch and what to leave alone.

Ryan presses on whether teams ever pushed back on him touching their code. Yes—once a team was in the middle of a complex migration and his refactor created cascading merge conflicts. He learned to look for in-progress migrations and stay clear. Ownership was never a territorial fight because the philosophy was absolute: every line you write, you're responsible for. If you're okay being woken up at 3 a.m. to fix it, skip the tests if you want. He'd get auto-assigned bugs two years later on code he'd barely remembered touching, and he'd fix them because "that is now my code."

He aggressively guarded his focus time. He pushed back on calendar invites with no context, skipped standups that had become social chitchat, and—in moments he now regrets—walked out of meetings mid-session. "I don't think this meeting's useful for me anymore. I'm leaving." He cringes at it now, recognizing it was arrogant. But the PMs didn't hate him for it, because he fixed their bugs so fast and built things so quickly; they'd often preemptively release him from the portion of a meeting he didn't need.

**The IC7+ only group and what all Staff+ engineers shared**

When he got promoted to IC7, he was added to an IC7+-only group. Everyone in it—whether a coding machine, a specialist, or a fixer—shared certain traits: extremely strong conscientiousness, due diligence, and a **sharpness** that let them move through complex concepts at speed. "Instead of having an hour-long presentation... everyone would sharply be, 'What about A, B, C, D?' And the follow-up questions would happen very quickly." Some had arrived at these traits through personality (he suspects his own OCD-leveraging obsession with every detail helped), while others built them over 25 years of experience, but the bar was consistent. Junior conversations, by contrast, tended to have more missing context and slower follow-up cadences, which naturally limited project scope.

**A weakness: prioritizing for impact over for "I can see the answer"**

He has a clear self-diagnosed weakness. He picks tasks where he can already see the full solution in his head—"it's just how fast can I type." This is also why LLMs supercharge him: he already knows what he wants to do and just needs to get it out. But this means he sometimes ignored his manager's top priority ("Can you please work on A?") because he didn't see the answer to A in his head, and instead cranked out B, C, D, E, F, G, H. If you crank out enough, the total impact adds up, and he passed every review. But "if A really, really was so important and it did not get done... overall impact would probably be higher by doing A." He frames it as a genuine limitation—a tendency to optimize for what he can execute instantly rather than what carries the highest business value.

**Landing code faster: the compound cycle**

His practical framework for accelerating productivity is simple but nuanced. **Step one: do something.** Most people who ask for advice are thinking too much and not writing enough code. His manager early on was frustrated spending all day reviewing Novati's massive, sloppy diffs—but that was the necessary first step. **Step two: get feedback from people who have the taste and judgment you aspire to**, not from peers who are only slightly ahead of you. A bootcamp grad getting feedback from another recent bootcamp grad who became the instructor is missing the deep-experience layer. **Step three, where most fail: actually internalize the feedback as improvement rather than as approval.** If you treat feedback as a grade to pass, you'll change the wrong things or fail to reflect; you need to let it reshape your approach. The three common mistakes map exactly to missing any of these steps.

For someone already productive who wants the next level, his advice is to push scope—take on something ambitious that sits just outside your current comfort zone within the org. If you're the coding machine of your department, push to be the coding machine across the broader org. And if you're stuck, reach out to senior leaders. He regularly set up 15-minute one-on-ones with the C-suite, including Chris Cox, to surface wider-scoped projects.

**Leaving Meta and the "all in or not all in" threshold**

Around the six-year mark, the relationship shifted from "Facebook had my back a hundred percent; I had their back a hundred percent"—a little culty, he admits—to feeling like a business relationship. Something broke. Coinciding with that, a vesting anomaly meant his take-home pay was going to drop 80% in a single month. He gave three months' notice, took a farewell tour building Messenger for Kids, and slowly emptied his desk. He's now a founder at Formation, where his all-in drive has somewhere to go. The principle is binary for him: he's either all in or not all in.

**Talent vs. hard work vs. luck**

He believes talent is real, whether biological or shaped by upbringing and undiscovered affinities, and finding what you're naturally better at than most people is a huge unlock. But hard work is the one thing fully within your control—you can outwork comparable peers and get recognized. As for luck, he puts it at about 50% of his growth. Growing up in Canada, he had no context for the red-carpet treatment tech engineers receive. Landing at Facebook exactly when the culture and code perfectly matched his personality was pure luck, and he's seen people with the luck but without the same drive still do very well.

**Advice to his younger self**

He'd tell his younger self—and the many interview candidates he now coaches at Formation—to stop treating feedback as a grade or an approval score. "I wanted to get an A+ or a hundred percent, and I didn't really know what I was learning." He was putting pressure on himself to get a perfect score next time, not pressure to improve. The shift from seeking approval to genuinely reflecting on feedback and pushing your comfort zone as a result is the change he wishes he'd made earlier.

---

Michael Novati’s arc is a story of **taste** built through relentless, feedback-fueled **compound cycles**—a patina earned not by planning but by doing, breaking norms, and absorbing the consequences. His **coding machine** archetype was less about raw keystrokes than about a willingness to touch everything, own everything, and stake his job on every line, a stance crystallized in his "if this breaks the site, I will resign" credo. The same OCD-leveraging intensity that powered thousand-class refactors also created friction—in his **Weekly NTI** posts, in meetings he walked out of—revealing a tension he still navigates between being unfiltered and understanding that "that off-the-cuff style does have consequences." His philosophy of being **all in or not all in** ultimately governed both his decade at Meta and his departure: once the relationship became transactional and a vesting cliff loomed, the binary switch flipped, and he moved on.
