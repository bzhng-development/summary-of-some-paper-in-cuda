# Direct Language Model Alignment from Online AI Feedback

**ArXiv:** [2402.04792](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04792)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Online AI Feedback (OAIF)**, a method that makes direct alignment from preference (DAP) methods online and on-policy by using an LLM as an annotator — on each training iteration, two responses are sampled from the current policy and the LLM annotator chooses which is preferred, providing feedback without requiring a separately trained reward model.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Online AI Feedback (OAIF)**, a method that makes direct alignment from preference (DAP) methods online and on-policy by using an LLM as an annotator — on each training iteration, two responses are sampled from the current policy and the LLM annotator chooses which is preferred, providing feedback without requiring a separately trained reward model. The approach is evaluated on TL;DR, Anthropic Helpfulness, and Anthropic Harmlessness using PaLM 2 models, demonstrating via human evaluation that online DAP methods (DPO, IPO, SLiC) with OAIF consistently outperform their offline counterparts, with an average win rate of ~66% over offline versions of the same methods. On TL;DR in a 4-way human comparison, online DPO with OAIF is preferred over SFT, RLHF, and RLAIF 58% of the time, establishing that on-policy AI feedback can replace both human-labeled preference datasets and the traditional RLHF reward-modeling pipeline — provided the annotating LLM is of sufficient quality, as performance degrades gracefully with smaller annotator sizes.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: DAP Methods Inherit Distribution Shift from Offline Data

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is a mismatch between how direct alignment from preferences (DAP) methods are trained and how they are evaluated. In the standard DAP pipeline — exemplified by methods like DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), IPO (Azar et al., 2023), and SLiC (Zhao et al., 2023) — a preference dataset $D = \{(x_i, y^+_i, y^-_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ is collected *once*, ahead of training, and kept fixed. The responses $y^+$ and $y^-$ are sampled from some existing language model $\rho$, and preferences are annotated (by humans or AIs) before alignment begins. The target policy $\pi_\theta$ is then optimized on this static dataset.

The mismatch arises because $\pi_\theta$ changes over training: at step $t$, we are training on data generated by $\rho$ but evaluating (and deploying) $\pi_{\theta_t}$. Since $\pi_{\theta_t} \neq \rho$ for $t > 0$, **the training data is off-policy**. This is not merely a theoretical concern — it means that:

- **The policy never receives feedback on its own generations.** The DAP loss functions (Equations 1–3 in the paper) compute gradients from preference pairs $(y^+, y^-)$ sampled from $\rho$, not from $\pi_{\theta_t}$. The model is therefore learning to rank responses it did not produce, which may not transfer to improving the responses it actually generates.
- **The distribution of responses shifts during training.** As $\pi_{\theta_t}$ departs from $\pi_{\theta_0}$ (the SFT starting point), the gap between the training distribution and the model's output distribution widens. This is illustrated schematically in the paper's Figure 2: two independent shifts occur — an initial shift from $\rho$ to $\pi_{\theta_0}$, and a gradual shift from $\pi_{\theta_0}$ to $\pi_{\theta_t}$ during alignment.
- **Early overfitting is a practical consequence.** Figure 3 (red curve) shows that offline DPO's win rate against the SFT baseline drops sharply around training step 3,500, while online DPO continues improving. The authors attribute this to offline DPO overfitting the static, off-policy preferences in $D$.

The paper frames this through the lens of reinforcement learning terminology (elaborated in Appendix A). **Online learning** requires dynamic interaction — the policy generates responses and receives feedback on them in real time. **On-policy learning** requires that the data used for training comes from the current policy itself. DAP methods as typically practiced are both *offline* (feedback is pre-collected) and *off-policy* (responses come from $\rho$, not $\pi_{\theta_t}$). The paper's central claim is that making DAP methods both online and on-policy — specifically, by replacing pre-collected human preferences with real-time AI feedback on the current policy's own outputs — substantially improves alignment performance.

### Why This Problem Matters

The importance is both practical and conceptual.

**Practical deployment constraints.** In real-world applications, collecting pairwise preferences from human annotators is expensive and slow. The standard approach — collect once, train once — is economically attractive. But if this shortcut fundamentally limits alignment quality, then the economics need reconsideration. OAIF offers a path that maintains the practical efficiency of DAP methods (no separate reward model, simple loss functions, no policy gradients) while recovering the alignment benefits of online interaction. This is relevant for:

- **Cost-sensitive deployments:** Organizations that cannot afford continuous human annotation cycles but want alignment quality competitive with RLHF.
- **Rapid iteration:** If preference criteria change (new safety requirements, new cultural norms, different user populations), OAIF can adapt by changing the annotator prompt without recollecting a dataset or retraining a reward model.
- **Scalability to new tasks:** For novel domains where pre-collected preference datasets don't exist, OAIF can bootstrap alignment without any human preference data at all — needing only a capable LLM annotator and a prompt describing the desiderata.

**Theoretical significance: on-policy vs. off-policy in language model alignment.** The paper provides what is, to our knowledge, the first systematic empirical comparison of online vs. offline variants of the *same* DAP loss functions, controlling for all other variables (same base model, same annotator quality, same hyperparameters). This clean comparison reveals that offline-over-online is not a minor detail — it produces a ~66% vs. ~34% win rate gap in human evaluation across three DAP methods (Table 3). This finding has implications for how the field thinks about preference optimization: it suggests that the *data collection process* may matter as much as the *loss function design*, challenging the assumption that improved DAP loss formulations alone can close the gap with RLHF.

### Prior Approaches and Their Shortcomings

The paper identifies three families of prior work, each with specific limitations that OAIF addresses.

#### Offline DAP Methods: Simple but Off-Policy

DPO, IPO, and SLiC all operate on pre-collected preference datasets. Their key advantage is simplicity: no reward model training, no RL, just direct supervised-style optimization on preference pairs. The gradient computations are exact and efficient (no policy gradient variance, no value function), and the loss functions have appealing theoretical interpretations (DPO as an implicit Bradley-Terry reward model, IPO as a constrained optimization, SLiC as a margin-based ranking loss).

However, their fundamental limitation is the **offline + off-policy** nature of the training data. The authors verify this distribution shift empirically in Appendix B (Figure 8): when responses are sampled from a different model (PaLM 2-S) and evaluated under the target policy (GPT-2 Large), the log-probability gap between on-policy and off-policy responses is clear — the target model assigns significantly lower probability to off-policy responses. This means the DAP loss is being optimized on data points that lie far from the model's current output distribution, reducing the signal-to-noise ratio of the gradient updates.

The paper acknowledges that DPO includes a mitigation: the SFT baseline $\pi_{\theta_0}$ is often trained on the preference dataset so that $\pi_{\theta_0} \approx \rho$. But this addresses only the *initial* distribution shift (at $t=0$). As training proceeds and $\pi_{\theta_t}$ moves away from $\pi_{\theta_0}$, the off-policy problem re-emerges. Figure 3's sharp overfitting curve for offline DPO is direct evidence of this — the initial SFT alignment helps initially, but it cannot prevent degradation as the policy diverges.

#### RLHF / RLAIF: Online but Complex

RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017; Stiennon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022) and RLAIF (Bai et al., 2022b; Lee et al., 2023) solve the online feedback problem by training a separate reward model (RM) that can score arbitrary responses on-the-fly. During the RL phase, the policy $\pi_\theta$ generates responses, the RM scores them, and policy gradient methods (typically PPO with a KL penalty to the SFT baseline) update the policy.

This is genuinely online and on-policy — the responses being scored come from $\pi_{\theta_t}$ itself. The benefits are well-documented: improved exploration, direct optimization of the reward signal, and avoidance of the off-policy degradation seen in offline DAP.

But RLHF/RLAIF introduce their own problems:

- **Complexity:** RLHF requires training and maintaining a separate RM, implementing policy gradient methods with value function baselines (to reduce variance), and managing the instability of RL training. This is a multi-component system with its own hyperparameters and failure modes.
- **RM distribution shift:** As the authors note in Appendix A.3, the RM itself suffers from a distribution shift problem. It is typically trained on preference data $D \sim \rho$, but at inference time it must score responses from $\pi_{\theta_t} \neq \rho$. Unlike DAP methods, this shift is *hidden* — the RM produces a score, but its reliability degrades silently as $\pi_{\theta_t}$ moves away from the training distribution. The paper shows empirically that this matters: "online DPO with the same RM used for RLAIF... significantly underperforms online DPO with OAIF, with a win rate of <30%" (Section 4.4). Retraining the RM synchronously with the policy (as suggested by Ziegler et al., 2019) would address this but "greatly complicate the training pipeline and increase training cost."
- **Inflexibility:** The RM encodes a fixed preference function. If the desired alignment criteria change (e.g., wanting shorter responses, or different safety priorities), the RM must be retrained on new preference data. This is costly and slow.

#### RM-Based Online DAP Methods: Partial Solutions

Recent work has attempted to make DAP methods online by incorporating reward models: **Rejection Sampling Optimization (RSO)** by Liu et al. (2023) uses an RM to perform rejection sampling from the current policy, generating on-policy preference data for DAP training. **Iterative DPO** by Xu et al. (2023) and **West-of-N** by Pace et al. (2024) use RMs to pseudo-label responses from $\pi_{\theta_t}$, creating online preference pairs.

These approaches make the DAP step online and on-policy, but they do not eliminate the distribution shift problem — they merely relocate it. The RM used for rejection sampling or pseudo-labeling is still trained on off-policy data $D \sim \rho$, so its preferences over $\pi_{\theta_t}$'s responses become increasingly unreliable as $\pi_{\theta_t}$ diverges from $\rho$. The paper's Table 1 summarizes this clearly: RM-based online DAP methods achieve "on-policy generation" and "online feedback" but still require training a separate RM, which inherits the distribution shift.

Additionally, these methods reintroduce complexity that DAP was designed to avoid. Training a separate RM and running rejection sampling or pseudo-labeling pipelines partially defeats the purpose of DAP's simplicity.

### Concurrent Work and OAIF's Positioning

The paper situates itself among two concurrent lines of work:

**Self-rewarding models (Yuan et al., 2024):** The policy being aligned provides feedback to itself — the model both generates responses and judges them. This is a special case of OAIF where the annotating LLM equals $\pi_\theta$, but the authors argue this is unnecessarily restrictive. OAIF can leverage any LLM as annotator, including larger or higher-quality ones than the policy being aligned. Section 4.5 demonstrates that larger annotators produce better results (Figure 5: PaLM 2-L as annotator achieves 95.24% win rate vs. SFT, while PaLM 2-XS achieves 82.31%). The paper frames this as a practical advantage: "the choice of LLM annotator should not necessarily be limited to the model being aligned, especially when an LLM annotator of larger size or higher quality is available" (Section 5).

**Online preference but with RMs (Swamy et al., 2024):** This concurrent work also investigates the importance of online preferences but still relies on a separately trained RM. OAIF is positioned as more direct — skipping the RM entirely and extracting preferences from an LLM via prompting.

### How OAIF Positions Itself

The paper's positioning can be understood as **occupying a previously empty design point in the alignment taxonomy** (Table 1): a method that simultaneously achieves "No RM needed" (the advantage of DAP over RLHF), "On-policy generation" (the advantage of RLHF over offline DAP), and "Online feedback" (the advantage of RLHF over offline DAP). No prior method hits all three.

The key enabling insight is that LLMs can serve as reliable preference annotators via prompting — a finding established by Lee et al. (2023) in the RLAIF context. OAIF's innovation is recognizing that this capability can replace not just the human annotator (as in RLAIF) but the entire reward model *and* the pre-collected dataset in DAP methods. By generating responses on-the-fly from $\pi_{\theta_t}$ and annotating them with an LLM, OAIF makes DAP training online and on-policy without introducing a separate RM training step.

This positioning also explains the paper's empirical strategy. The head-to-head comparisons are carefully constructed to isolate variables:

- **Online DPO vs. Offline DPO** (Figures 3, 4; Tables 2, 3): same loss function, same base model, different data source (online AI feedback vs. pre-collected human preferences). This isolates the effect of online + on-policy feedback.
- **Online DPO vs. RLAIF / RLHF** (Figure 4a; Section 4.4): different alignment algorithms, same annotator quality (PaLM 2-L for both online DPO's feedback and RLAIF's RM training). This isolates the DAP vs. RL training paradigm while controlling for feedback quality.
- **Annotator size ablation** (Figure 5): same online DPO algorithm, different annotator sizes. This isolates the effect of feedback quality independent of the algorithm choice.

The paper does not claim that OAIF is fundamentally more capable than RLHF — the theoretical maximum of both approaches given perfect feedback should converge. Rather, the claim is about **practical efficiency**: OAIF achieves better results than RLHF and RLAIF under realistic constraints (fixed annotator model, no RM retraining, standard training budgets) because it avoids the hidden distribution shift in the RM and the variance/complexity of policy gradient methods.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The system being built is an **alignment training loop** — a procedure that takes a base language model (the "policy" to be aligned) and iteratively improves it so that its responses better satisfy a desired quality criterion (helpfulness, harmlessness, summarization quality) without requiring any pre-collected human preference dataset or separately trained reward model. The problem it solves is that standard direct alignment from preference (DAP) methods like DPO train on static, off-policy data, causing the aligned model to overfit to responses it didn't generate and never receive feedback on its own outputs; OAIF solves this by **replacing the pre-collected dataset with real-time AI judgments of the current model's own generations**, making alignment online and on-policy while preserving DAP's simplicity (no reward model, no RL, just differentiable loss functions on preference pairs).

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The OAIF system has four interacting components connected in a loop:

1. **The policy model `$\pi_\theta$`** (initially `$\pi_{\theta_0}$`, an SFT-fine-tuned PaLM 2-XS): This is the language model being aligned. At each training step, it samples two responses `$y_1, y_2$` given a random prompt `$x$` from the prompt dataset.
2. **The prompt dataset `$D_X$`**: A collection of input prompts extracted from existing preference datasets (TL;DR, Helpfulness, Harmlessness) by discarding the pre-existing response pairs and keeping only the prompts. This gives OAIF something to generate responses *to*, without using the pre-collected preferences.
3. **The LLM annotator** (PaLM 2-L by default, ablated down to PaLM 2-XS and PaLM 2-S): A separate, frozen language model that receives the prompt `$x$` and the two sampled responses `$y_1, y_2$`, and outputs a preference judgment indicating which response is better according to a criteria described in the annotator's instruction prompt. This replaces both the pre-collected preference dataset and the reward model.
4. **The DAP loss function** `$\ell(x, y^+, y^-, \theta)$`: Any differentiable preference-based loss (DPO, IPO, or SLiC) that consumes the prompt and the annotated preference pair and produces a scalar loss used to update `$\pi_\theta$`'s parameters via gradient descent.

The loop executes one training step as follows: (Step 1) sample a prompt `$x$` from `$D_X$`; (Step 2) sample two responses `$y_1, y_2$` from the *current* policy `$\pi_{\theta_t}$` conditioned on `$x$`; (Step 3) feed `$x, y_1, y_2$` as a formatted prompt to the LLM annotator, which outputs a preference probability distribution — the response with higher probability is labeled `$y^+$` (preferred), the other `$y^-$` (dispreferred); (Step 4) compute the DAP loss `$\ell(x, y^+, y^-, \theta_t)$` and backpropagate, treating the sampling and annotation steps as non-differentiable (via `stop_gradient`). This loop repeats for `$T$` steps, producing the aligned policy `$\pi_{\theta_T}$`.

- **First**, the formal DAP loss functions (DPO, IPO, SLiC) and how they differ from RLHF's objective — because understanding what these losses optimize is prerequisite to understanding *why* making them online matters.
- **Second**, the OAIF training loop in detail (Algorithm 1) — how the four components interact step-by-step, what stops gradients where, and why the `stop_gradient` choice is both necessary and correct.
- **Third**, the LLM annotator prompting mechanism — how raw text prompts are converted into preference judgments, how position bias is mitigated, and how the same mechanism serves both training-time feedback and evaluation-time judging.

- **Fifth**, the controllability mechanism — how modifying the annotator's instruction prompt changes the alignment objective without retraining anything, using response length as the case study.
- **Sixth**, the gradient computation detail — why `stop_gradient` is placed on both the sampling and annotation steps, and what would go wrong if it were not.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method paper** that proposes a training procedure (OAIF) rather than a new model architecture or loss function. Its core idea is that DAP methods can be made online and on-policy by replacing the static preference dataset with real-time LLM-generated annotations of the current policy's own outputs, and that this substitution yields substantial alignment improvements across multiple DAP loss functions and tasks.

#### The DAP Loss Functions: What They Optimize and Why

The paper experiments with three direct alignment from preferences (DAP) loss functions: DPO, IPO, and SLiC. All three share a common structure — they operate on preference pairs `$(y^+, y^-)$` and update the policy `$\pi_\theta$` by increasing the relative likelihood of preferred responses over dispreferred ones, using the SFT baseline `$\pi_{\theta_0}$` as a reference point. Understanding these losses is essential because OAIF changes *what data they consume* (online vs. offline, on-policy vs. off-policy), not *how they compute updates*.

**DPO Loss:**

$$\ell_{\text{DPO}}(x, y^+, y^-, \theta) = -\log \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^+|x)}{\pi_{\theta_0}(y^+|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^-|x)}{\pi_{\theta_0}(y^-|x)}\right)$$

where `$\sigma(\cdot)$` is the logistic (sigmoid) function, `$\beta$` is a scalar hyperparameter controlling the strength of the preference signal relative to staying close to `$\pi_{\theta_0}$`, `$\pi_\theta(y|x)$` is the probability the current policy assigns to response `$y$` given prompt `$x$`, and `$\pi_{\theta_0}(y|x)$` is the same probability under the frozen SFT baseline.

**What it computes:** The DPO loss is the negative log-likelihood of the preferred response under an implicit Bradley-Terry preference model. The term inside the sigmoid, `$\beta(\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^+|x)}{\pi_{\theta_0}(y^+|x)} - \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^-|x)}{\pi_{\theta_0}(y^-|x)})$`, is the difference in "reward" between the preferred and dispreferred responses, where the implicit reward for a response `$y$` is defined as `$\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_0}(y|x)}$`. The sigmoid converts this reward difference into a probability (between 0 and 1) that `$y^+$` is preferred over `$y^-$`; taking the negative log makes this a maximum-likelihood objective: the loss is minimized when the model assigns high probability (close to 1) to the event that `$y^+$` is better than `$y^-$`. The gradient of this loss increases `$\pi_\theta(y^+|x)$` and decreases `$\pi_\theta(y^-|x)$`, with the magnitude scaled by how wrong the current model is (when the sigmoid output is far from 1, the gradient is large).

**Why this form:** The DPO loss is derived from the same Bradley-Terry preference model that underlies RLHF's reward modeling step, but it eliminates the separate reward model by reparameterizing the reward directly in terms of the policy's log-probability ratio to the SFT baseline. This means optimizing the DPO loss *is* optimizing an implicit reward function — there is no need to train a separate RM and then optimize against it with RL. The `$\beta$` parameter controls a KL-divergence penalty implicitly: smaller `$\beta$` permits larger deviations from `$\pi_{\theta_0}$` (stronger optimization of the preference signal), while larger `$\beta$` keeps `$\pi_\theta$` closer to the SFT baseline. The paper uses `$\beta = 0.1$` for DPO based on preliminary experiments, which represents a moderately strong preference signal.

**IPO Loss:**

$$\ell_{\text{IPO}}(x, y^+, y^-, \theta) = \left(\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^+|x)}{\pi_{\theta_0}(y^+|x)} - \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^-|x)}{\pi_{\theta_0}(y^-|x)} - \frac{1}{2\beta}\right)^2$$

where `$\beta$` again controls the strength of the preference constraint, but with a different interpretation than in DPO.

**What it computes:** The IPO loss is a squared-error objective that drives the log-probability ratio difference — the same implicit reward difference as in DPO — toward a fixed target value of `$1/(2\beta)$`. This is a *regression* objective rather than a *classification* objective: instead of maximizing the probability that `$y^+$` outranks `$y^-$` (which can be achieved by making the gap arbitrarily large once the sigmoid saturates), IPO explicitly targets a specific reward gap. When the gap equals `$1/(2\beta)$`, the loss is zero; any deviation (either too small or too large) incurs a quadratic penalty.

**Why this form:** The IPO loss addresses a known weakness of DPO: because the sigmoid function saturates (approaches 1 or 0 asymptotically), once the model confidently prefers `$y^+$` over `$y^-$`, the gradient vanishes even if the model's behavior could be further improved. IPO's quadratic form provides a nonzero gradient until the reward gap precisely hits the target, preventing premature convergence. The paper uses `$\beta = 1.0$` for IPO, meaning the target reward gap is `$1/(2 \times 1.0) = 0.5$` in log-probability-ratio space.

**SLiC Loss:**

$$\ell_{\text{SLiC}}(x, y^+, y^-, \theta) = \max\left(0, 1 - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^+|x)}{\pi_{\theta_0}(y^+|x)} + \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^-|x)}{\pi_{\theta_0}(y^-|x)}\right)$$

where `$\max(0, \cdot)$` is the hinge function and `$\beta$` scales the reward difference.

**What it computes:** The SLiC loss is a margin-based ranking loss. The term `$\beta(\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^+|x)}{\pi_{\theta_0}(y^+|x)} - \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^-|x)}{\pi_{\theta_0}(y^-|x)})$` is again the implicit reward difference. If this difference exceeds a margin of 1, the max returns 0 (no loss — the model sufficiently prefers `$y^+$`). If the difference is less than 1, the loss is `$1 - \text{difference}$`, linearly penalizing the shortfall. This is analogous to a support vector machine objective in the reward space: it only cares about cases where the preference is wrong or insufficiently confident, and it doesn't penalize making the gap larger once the margin is satisfied.

**Why this form:** The hinge loss provides robustness to noise: if the preference annotation is incorrect (the true preference is the reverse of what the annotator said), a saturated sigmoid in DPO would strongly penalize the model for being right; the hinge loss would stop penalizing once the model's implicit reward correctly favors the *actual* better response beyond the margin. The paper uses `$\beta = 0.002$` for SLiC, which is much smaller than the DPO and IPO `$\beta$` values — this is because the loss scale is different (the hinge operates on raw reward differences without the sigmoid's compression).

**What all three losses share:** Each loss function takes the same inputs — a prompt `$x$`, a preferred response `$y^+$`, a dispreferred response `$y^-$`, and the model parameters `$\theta$` (with `$\theta_0$` frozen). They all increase `$\pi_\theta(y^+|x)$` relative to `$\pi_\theta(y^-|x)$`, but differ in *how much* they increase it and *when they stop*. In OAIF, the crucial change is not to these loss functions themselves, but to where `$(y^+, y^-)$` comes from — online from `$\pi_{\theta_t}$` with AI annotation, rather than offline from `$\rho$` with pre-collected human labels.

#### The OAIF Training Loop (Algorithm 1)

The paper's Algorithm 1 describes the core procedure. I'll walk through each line and explain what happens mechanistically.

**Line 1: "for t := 0 to T do"** — The training loop runs for `$T$` steps. The paper does not specify an exact value of `$T$` in Algorithm 1, but from the experimental figures (e.g., Figure 3, Figure 6b), training runs for up to 12,000 steps for TL;DR and up to 6,000 steps for Helpfulness, with checkpoints evaluated periodically. The batch size is 128 (specified in Section 4.1), meaning each step processes 128 prompt-response-preference triplets.

**Line 2: "Sample prompt $x \sim D_X$"** — A prompt is drawn from the prompt dataset. Crucially, `$D_X$` contains *only prompts*, not response pairs. The paper constructs `$D_X$` by extracting the input prompts from existing preference datasets (TL;DR, Helpfulness, Harmlessness) and discarding the associated `$(y^+, y^-)$` pairs. This means OAIF does not use the pre-collected human preferences at all during training — it only reuses the *prompts* to ensure the training distribution of queries matches the evaluation distribution. The size of `$D_X$` is not explicitly stated but is inherited from the source datasets: TL;DR contains approximately 117,000 training prompts (from Stiennon et al., 2020), Helpfulness and Harmlessness each contain approximately 43,000 training prompts (from Bai et al., 2022a). With a batch size of 128, one epoch over TL;DR is approximately 914 steps, so 12,000 steps represents roughly 13 epochs — but since responses are sampled fresh each step from the evolving policy, the effective data seen is much larger than the prompt count.

**Line 3: "Sample response pair $y_1, y_2 \sim \pi_{\theta_t}(\cdot|x)$"** — The current policy generates two complete responses given the prompt. The sampling uses a temperature of 0.9 (specified in Section 4.1), which introduces stochasticity — the same prompt sampled twice will produce different responses, and the same prompt sampled at different training steps will produce responses from different policy distributions. Temperature 0.9 is slightly below the standard 1.0, making the sampling moderately peaked toward high-probability tokens — this balances exploration (needed to discover good responses) with exploitation (needed to generate responses of reasonable quality that the annotator can meaningfully compare). The policy is PaLM 2-XS (Extra Small), initialized from an SFT checkpoint trained on the same task's data.

**What "on-policy" means here:** Because `$y_1$` and `$y_2$` are sampled from `$\pi_{\theta_t}$` (not from `$\pi_{\theta_0}$` or some other `$\rho$`), the preference pair is *on-policy* — the model is being trained on data generated by its current self. As `$\theta_t$` changes, the distribution of `$(y_1, y_2)$` changes with it, creating a feedback loop: the model's improvements change what responses get generated, which changes what preferences the annotator sees, which changes the gradient updates. This is exactly the dynamic that defines online reinforcement learning and distinguishes it from learning on a fixed dataset.

**Line 4: "Use LLM annotator to get preference pair $y^+, y^-$"** — The two sampled responses, along with the prompt, are formatted into a text prompt and fed to the LLM annotator (detailed in the next subsection). The annotator outputs a preference: one response is labeled `$y^+$` (preferred) and the other `$y^-$` (dispreferred). If the annotator's preference is a tie (equal probability), the paper does not specify a tie-breaking procedure explicitly, but the softmax-over-logits scoring mechanism described in Appendix E naturally handles this by producing probabilities that can be compared.

**Line 5: "Update $\theta_t$ into $\theta_{t+1}$ using $\nabla_\theta \ell(x, y^+, y^-, \theta_t)$"** — The DAP loss is computed on the prompt and the annotated preference pair, and the gradient is used to update the policy parameters. The optimizer is Adafactor (Shazeer & Stern, 2018) with a learning rate of `$5 \times 10^{-7}$` and a warm-up period of 150 steps (Section 4.1). Adafactor is chosen over Adam/AdamW likely because it has lower memory requirements (it doesn't store full second-moment estimates), which matters for large models, though the paper doesn't explicitly justify this choice.

**The `stop_gradient` detail (critical):** The paper states that the gradient computation "amounts to placing a `stop_gradient` on both the sampling and LLM annotation steps." This means `$\nabla_\theta \ell(x, y^+, y^-, \theta_t)$` treats `$y^+$` and `$y^-$` as constants with respect to `$\theta$` — the gradient flows only through the DAP loss's explicit dependence on `$\pi_\theta(y^+|x)$` and `$\pi_\theta(y^-|x)$`, not through the process that generated those labels. This is a design choice with consequences:

- If gradients flowed *through* the sampling step, the model would need to backpropagate through the discrete sampling operation (requiring REINFORCE-style gradient estimators, adding variance) and through the LLM annotator (requiring the annotator to be differentiable and in-memory, which is impractical). The `stop_gradient` makes the update a standard supervised-style gradient — the preference labels are treated as ground truth for this step.
- This is theoretically justified because the annotator's preferences are being used as a *learning signal*, not as a *differentiable component of the computation graph*. The annotator is a fixed function mapping `$(x, y_1, y_2) \to (y^+, y^-)$`, and we want `$\pi_\theta$` to produce responses that this fixed function rates highly. This is analogous to how RLHF uses a frozen RM to score responses without differentiating through the RM.

**Output:** After `$T$` steps, the aligned policy `$\pi_{\theta_T}$` is returned. In practice, the paper selects the best checkpoint based on a combination of manual inspection and development set win rate against the SFT baseline evaluated by Gemini Pro (Section 4.2), rather than simply taking the final checkpoint.

#### The LLM Annotator Prompting Mechanism

The LLM annotator is the component that replaces both the pre-collected preference dataset and the reward model. Understanding how it works is central to understanding OAIF, because the *quality* and *controllability* of the annotator determine the quality of the alignment signal.

**Pairwise prompting scheme.** The paper adopts the approach from Lee et al. (2023): the annotator receives a formatted text prompt containing the input context (the prompt `$x$`), the two candidate responses (`$y_1$` and `$y_2$`), and a detailed instruction describing the criteria for judging which response is better. The annotator is asked to output "1" or "2" to indicate which response is preferred. This is a *discriminative* task (choose A or B), not a *generative* task (produce justification), which is important because it means the annotator's capabilities needed are different from — and potentially simpler than — the policy's generative capabilities.

**How preferences are extracted from the annotator.** Rather than decoding a discrete "1" or "2" token (which would be non-differentiable and potentially noisy), the paper uses a probabilistic scoring method (Appendix E): the annotator computes the log-probability of generating the token "1" vs. the token "2" at the position where the choice is expected. A softmax over these two log-probabilities produces a preference distribution — e.g., if the log-probability of "1" is higher, the softmax assigns a higher probability to response 1 being preferred. The response with the higher probability is labeled `$y^+$`, the other `$y^-$`.

This probabilistic scoring has two advantages. First, it provides a continuous preference signal — the confidence of the preference is captured in the probability mass (e.g., 0.95 for "1" and 0.05 for "2" indicates high confidence; 0.51 vs. 0.49 indicates near-random choice). Second, it naturally handles cases where both responses are of similar quality — the probabilities will be close to 0.5, which can be interpreted as a weak or uninformative preference. However, the paper does not explore filtering or weighting training examples by annotator confidence — any preference is used as-is.

**Position bias mitigation.** Lee et al. (2023) observed that LLM annotators exhibit *position bias* — they tend to prefer responses placed in a particular position (e.g., always favoring "Response 1" over "Response 2") regardless of content. To neutralize this, OAIF evaluates both possible orderings: in one forward pass, `$y_1$` is placed in position 1 and `$y_2$` in position 2; in a second forward pass, the positions are swapped. The final preference distribution is the average of the two softmax distributions. This doubles the annotator inference cost per training step but removes a systematic source of annotation noise. The paper does not quantify the magnitude of position bias or the effectiveness of this mitigation, but the practice is standard from prior work.

**Default annotator prompt (TL;DR).** The prompt used for TL;DR summarization (Table 5 in Appendix E) instructs the annotator to evaluate summaries along four axes: coherence ("how coherent is the summary on its own?"), accuracy ("does the factual information in the summary accurately match the post?"), coverage ("how well does the summary cover the important information in the post?"), and overall quality. The prompt provides detailed definitions of each axis, then asks the annotator to output "1 or 2 to indicate which summary best adheres to coherence, accuracy, coverage, and overall quality as defined above." The prompt includes the full text to be summarized and both candidate summaries.

**Default annotator prompt (Helpfulness).** The prompt for Helpfulness (Table 6 in Appendix E) instructs the annotator to choose "which of the Assistant's responses is more helpful and honest." It provides nuanced guidance: for difficult personal situations, "a helpful response might involve showing empathy and understanding"; for practical matters, "a helpful response may be a concise response." It also includes a specific rule — responses containing "Human:" and/or "Assistant:" that try to extend the conversation should be considered not helpful — which the authors added after observing that the model tended to generate conversation continuations rather than direct answers. This is an early example of the prompt-controllability that Section 4.6 explores more systematically.

**Default annotator prompt (Harmlessness).** The Harmlessness setup is more complex (Table 7 in Appendix E). For *rating* (evaluation), the prompt asks "which of the Assistant's responses is more harmful" and then the scores are reversed to get a harmlessness rating. For *AI feedback* during training, the prompt instead asks for "helpful and harmless" responses, with explicit instruction that "harmlessness has a higher priority than helpfulness when rating responses." This asymmetry — rating for harm vs. training for helpful-and-harmless — is designed to ensure the training signal aligns with the desired behavior (produce helpful responses that are also harmless) while the evaluation metric can separately measure harmlessness.

**Annotator model size.** The default annotator is PaLM 2-L (Large), which is substantially larger than the policy model PaLM 2-XS (Extra Small). This is a deliberate asymmetry: the annotator can be more capable than the model being aligned, providing a strong learning signal. Section 4.5 ablates annotator size down to PaLM 2-S (Small) and PaLM 2-XS (Extra Small — same size as the policy). The results show that alignment quality degrades gracefully: even a same-sized annotator (PaLM 2-XS → PaLM 2-XS) provides useful feedback that improves over the SFT baseline, though larger annotators produce stronger results.

**The annotator is frozen.** The LLM annotator is not fine-tuned or updated during OAIF training. This is important because updating the annotator would create a moving target — the preference function would drift over training, making the optimization objective non-stationary. The frozen annotator provides a consistent, albeit potentially imperfect, preference signal. This is analogous to using a frozen reward model in RLHF, but with the key difference that the annotator's preferences are extracted via prompting rather than learned from data, making them controllable via prompt engineering (Section 4.6).

**What makes the annotator's feedback "online."** The feedback is online because it is solicited *during training* for responses generated by the *current* policy. This contrasts with offline DAP, where the feedback was collected once, ahead of time, for responses from a potentially different policy `$\rho$`. The online nature means that as `$\pi_\theta$` improves, the annotator sees progressively better response pairs — the difficulty of the annotation task adapts to the policy's current capability level. If the policy improves from generating terrible responses to generating mediocre ones, the annotator must now distinguish between two mediocre responses rather than between two terrible ones, which is a harder task. This adaptive difficulty is what keeps the training signal informative throughout alignment.

#### The Online/Offline and On-Policy/Off-Policy Distinction (Appendix A)

The paper's Appendix A provides precise definitions that are essential for understanding what OAIF changes. I'll explain these definitions in the LLM alignment context and then map OAIF onto them.

**Online vs. offline learning (Appendix A.1).** In reinforcement learning terminology, adapted to LLM alignment: a learning process is *online* if there are dynamic interactions between the policy and the feedback source during training — the policy generates responses, the feedback source (human, RM, or LLM annotator) provides judgments, and the policy updates, all interleaved. It is *offline* if the feedback is pre-collected and the policy trains on a static dataset without further interaction. In the LLM alignment context: offline DAP methods are offline because `$D = \{(x_i, y^+_i, y^-_i)\}$` is collected ahead of time from `$\rho$` and fixed; RLHF/RLAIF are online because the policy generates responses that the RM scores in real time during the RL phase; OAIF is online because the policy generates responses that the LLM annotator judges in real time during DAP training.

**On-policy vs. off-policy learning (Appendix A.2).** A learning process is *on-policy* if the training data (the responses being scored/judged) is generated by the current policy `$\pi_{\theta_t}$` itself. It is *off-policy* if the data comes from a different policy (either a different model `$\rho$`, or an earlier version `$\pi_{\theta_s}$` for `$s < t$`). In the LLM alignment context: offline DAP methods are off-policy because `$y^+, y^- \sim \rho$` (a different model); RLHF/RLAIF are on-policy during the RL phase because responses are sampled from `$\pi_{\theta_t}$` and scored by the RM; OAIF is on-policy because `$y_1, y_2 \sim \pi_{\theta_t}$` and are annotated by the LLM.

**The relationship between the two concepts in DAP.** As the paper notes: "for DAP methods, offline DAP is also off-policy, as `$y^+_i$` and `$y^-_i$` are not sampled from the current policy." This is because the pre-collected dataset `$D$` is both fixed (offline) and generated by `$\rho \neq \pi_{\theta_t}$` (off-policy). However, the two concepts are logically separable — one could imagine an online but off-policy method (e.g., collecting new preferences on-the-fly but from a different policy) or an offline but on-policy method (e.g., pre-collecting a large dataset from `$\pi_{\theta_0}$` and training only on that, which would be on-policy only at `$t=0$` and increasingly off-policy thereafter). OAIF achieves both properties simultaneously: the responses are from `$\pi_{\theta_t}$` (on-policy) and the feedback is solicited in real time (online).

**The distribution shift OAIF solves.** The paper identifies a specific shift: the gap between the distribution of responses in the training data (`$\rho(y|x)$`) and the distribution of responses from the aligned policy at step `$t$` (`$\pi_{\theta_t}(y|x)$`). Figure 2 illustrates this as two sequential shifts: an initial shift `$\rho \neq \pi_{\theta_0}$` (present even at the start of DAP training) and a gradual shift `$\pi_{\theta_0} \neq \pi_{\theta_t}$` (emerging as training progresses). OAIF eliminates both by sampling directly from `$\pi_{\theta_t}$`. The paper also acknowledges in Appendix B (Figure 8) that off-policy responses receive significantly lower log-probability under the target policy than on-policy responses, providing empirical evidence that the distribution shift is real and substantial.

**The distribution shift OAIF does NOT solve.** The paper explicitly notes (Section 5, Limitations): "we study only the shift between distributions over responses... However, the shifts also happen on the user prompt distribution `$p_X$` and the ground-truth human value function." OAIF extracts prompts from the same pre-collected dataset used for offline DAP, so the prompt distribution during training matches the prompt distribution during evaluation (in-distribution assumption). If the deployed model encounters prompts from a different distribution (e.g., different topics, different styles, different user populations), OAIF provides no mechanism for adapting — the annotator prompt would need to change, and the training prompts would need to cover the new distribution. This is left as a limitation for future work.

**The RM distribution shift (Appendix A.3).** The paper makes a subtle but important observation about RLHF/RLAIF: while the *RL phase* is online and on-policy, the *RM* used to score responses is typically trained on off-policy data `$D \sim \rho$`. This means "there is then a shift between the RM training distribution (`$D \sim \rho$`) and the RM inference distribution (`$\pi_{\theta_t}$`)." In other words, RLHF's reward model may become increasingly unreliable as `$\pi_\theta$` diverges from `$\rho$`, even though the optimization is online. OAIF avoids this hidden shift because the LLM annotator is not trained on `$D$` at all — its preferences come from zero-shot prompting, not from learning a preference function from data. This is a key architectural advantage: the annotator's reliability does not degrade as `$\pi_{\theta_t}$` moves away from `$\pi_{\theta_0}$`, because the annotator was never calibrated to `$\rho$`'s distribution in the first place. The annotator's preferences may be imperfect (they don't perfectly match human preferences), but they are consistently imperfect — the error is stationary rather than growing over training.

#### The Controllability Mechanism

Section 4.6 demonstrates that the LLM annotator's preferences can be steered by modifying its instruction prompt, without any retraining or data collection. This is a distinctive capability of OAIF that is not available in standard RLHF (where the RM encodes a fixed preference function) or offline DAP (where the dataset encodes fixed human preferences).

**Length controllability experiment.** The paper uses response length as a testbed for controllability. Starting from the Helpfulness-aligned online DPO model (trained with the default "helpful and honest" annotator prompt from Table 6), the authors train two additional online DPO models where the annotator prompt is modified to request shorter responses:

- **"Helpful and short"** (Table 8, top): The prompt is modified to add "When the quality of two responses is similar, the shorter one should always be preferred." This injects a tie-breaking preference for conciseness without sacrificing helpfulness as the primary criterion.
- **"Helpful and very short"** (Table 8, bottom): The prompt is rewritten to make shortness a primary criterion alongside helpfulness. The instruction asks for "the most helpful, honest and shortest response" and describes the assistant as someone who "would (hopefully) be helpful and concise." This makes shortness a first-class objective.

**Results of length control (Figure 6a).** The average response length drops from approximately 120 tokens (helpful-only) to approximately 90 tokens (helpful and short) to approximately 40 tokens (helpful and very short). This demonstrates that OAIF can achieve substantial, quantitative changes in model behavior purely through prompt engineering on the annotator. The degree of change is controllable — "short" vs. "very short" produce different magnitudes of length reduction — so the prompt can be tuned to hit a desired target.

**Quality-tradeoff (Figure 6b).** The win rate against the SFT baseline drops as the length constraint is tightened: the helpful-only model achieves approximately 91% win rate, the helpful-and-short model approximately 80-85%, and the helpful-and-very-short model approximately 55-65% (judged by Gemini Pro, which itself may have a length bias). Human evaluation confirms the same trend: quality scores drop from 4.08 (helpful) to 3.72 (helpful and short) to 3.26 (helpful and very short), but all three remain above the SFT baseline score of 3.19. The key finding is that **controllable length reduction is possible without falling below the SFT baseline** — the model can be made shorter while still improving over the starting point.

**Why this matters beyond length.** Length is chosen as a simple, measurable testbed, but the same mechanism applies to any qualitative desideratum that can be specified in a prompt. The paper explicitly suggests extensions to "human values (such as helpfulness and impartiality)" and other qualitative objectives (Section 5). Because the annotator prompt is the sole specification of the preference function, changing the alignment objective requires only prompt engineering — no re-annotation, no RM retraining, no dataset reconstruction. This makes OAIF uniquely suited for scenarios where alignment criteria evolve over time or vary across deployment contexts. For example, a single base policy could be aligned differently for different user populations (adults vs. children, technical vs. non-technical, different cultural contexts) simply by using different annotator prompts during training, without the combinatorial explosion of preference datasets that this would require in offline DAP or RLHF.

**The mechanism underlying controllability.** The annotator prompt functions as a *preference function specification*. When the annotator sees "prefer shorter responses when quality is similar," it incorporates this criterion into its pairwise judgments. The DAP loss then optimizes the policy to produce responses that satisfy this criterion. Because the annotator processes the prompt instructions alongside the responses, it can apply nuanced tradeoffs (e.g., "only prefer shorter when quality is similar" vs. "always prefer shorter") that would be difficult to encode in a scalar reward function. This is an instance of using the LLM's language understanding capability to define a complex, context-sensitive preference function that a parametric reward model would struggle to capture from finite preference data.

#### Gradient Computation Details

The gradient computation has a subtle but crucial property that the paper describes explicitly.

**The `stop_gradient` placement.** The update step in Algorithm 1 uses `$\nabla_\theta \ell(x, y^+, y^-, \theta_t)$`, which the paper clarifies "amounts to placing a `stop_gradient` on both the sampling and LLM annotation steps." In automatic differentiation terms, this means the computational graph is severed at two points:

1. **After sampling `$y_1, y_2$` from `$\pi_{\theta_t}$`:** The gradient does not flow back through the sampling operation into the policy's generative distribution. Without this `stop_gradient`, the loss's dependence on the sampled tokens would require backpropagating through the discrete sampling step — the token choices are non-differentiable, so this would necessitate REINFORCE-style score function estimators or the reparameterization trick (which doesn't apply to discrete sampling). The `stop_gradient` converts the sampled responses into "data" for the DAP loss — they are treated as given, and the gradient only optimizes the policy's *evaluation* of those specific token sequences under the DAP objective.

2. **After the LLM annotator produces `$(y^+, y^-)$`:** The gradient does not flow through the annotator. This is necessary because the annotator (PaLM 2-L) is a separate, frozen model that may be too large to fit in memory alongside the policy model, and its internal computations are irrelevant to the policy's optimization. The preference labels are treated as ground-truth supervision for this training step.

**What the gradient actually computes.** Given a prompt `$x$`, a preferred response `$y^+$`, and a dispreferred response `$y^-$` (all treated as constants), the DAP gradient updates `$\theta$` to increase `$\pi_\theta(y^+|x)$` and decrease `$\pi_\theta(y^-|x)$`, with the reference policy `$\pi_{\theta_0}$` providing a regularizing anchor. This is a fully supervised update — it doesn't "know" that `$y^+$` and `$y^-$` were generated by the model itself or that the annotator might have made an error. This is identical in form to the gradient computed in offline DAP, except that the `$(y^+, y^-)$` pairs are fresh (online) and from the current policy (on-policy) rather than stale and off-policy.

**Why this is correct.** The `stop_gradient` choice is not an approximation or a compromise — it is the correct thing to do given the problem structure. The annotator defines a *fixed* preference function `$P(y^+ \succ y^- | x, y_1, y_2)$` (where `$\succ$` denotes "is preferred over") that we want the policy to optimize. This preference function is a property of the annotator model and its prompt — it does not depend on `$\theta$`. Therefore, there is no reason to differentiate through it. The policy's goal is to maximize the annotator's preference for its outputs, which is achieved by optimizing the DAP loss on the annotator's judgments. This is precisely analogous to RLHF, where the policy gradient is computed with respect to the frozen RM's scores without differentiating through the RM.

**What would go wrong without `stop_gradient`.** If gradients flowed through the sampling step, we would face the high-variance problem of score function estimators over discrete sequences — the variance of REINFORCE gradients scales with sequence length, making optimization unstable. If gradients flowed through the LLM annotator, we would need the annotator to be a differentiable function of its inputs, which would require a separate, smaller, fine-tuned reward model (exactly what OAIF is designed to avoid). The `stop_gradient` design is thus essential for making OAIF practically workable while preserving the simplicity of DAP methods.

**Relationship to the online/on-policy property.** Even though the `stop_gradient` means the gradient doesn't "know" the responses were sampled from `$\pi_{\theta_t}$`, the *data distribution* for the supervised update is `$\pi_{\theta_t}$` — the policy is being trained to rank responses from its own current distribution. As `$\theta_t$` changes, the distribution of `$(y_1, y_2)$` pairs changes, which changes which preferences the annotator provides, which changes the supervised targets. This is the online learning dynamic: the supervision itself adapts to the policy's evolving behavior. The `stop_gradient` doesn't break this dynamic — it merely makes each individual gradient step a clean supervised update, while the outer loop provides the adaptivity.

**Optimizer details (reiterated from Section 4.1).** The gradient is applied using Adafactor with a learning rate of `$5 \times 10^{-7}$` and a warm-up period of 150 steps. Adafactor's sublinear memory cost is important here because both the policy model (PaLM 2-XS) and potentially the annotator model (if loaded on the same device for inference) occupy GPU memory. The learning rate of `$5 \times 10^{-7}$` is relatively small — this is typical for fine-tuning large language models where aggressive updates can cause catastrophic forgetting of the SFT capabilities. The 150-step warm-up means the learning rate linearly increases from 0 (or a very small value) to `$5 \times 10^{-7}$` over the first 150 steps, which stabilizes early training when the policy is closest to `$\pi_{\theta_0}$` and the gradient estimates from random sampling may be noisy.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Offline/On-Policy Mismatch as THE Bottleneck in DAP — Not the Loss Function

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is diagnosing that the **primary performance limitation of direct alignment methods is not the choice of loss function (DPO vs. IPO vs. SLiC), but rather the offline, off-policy nature of the training data**. This reframes the DAP research agenda: the field had been focused on designing better loss functions (IPO's quadratic form to prevent saturation, SLiC's hinge loss for robustness, various KL-constraint formulations), implicitly assuming that better optimization objectives on the same pre-collected data would close the gap with RLHF. OAIF demonstrates that this assumption is wrong — or at least severely incomplete.

The evidence for this diagnostic claim is unusually clean because the paper performs a controlled experiment that prior work had not: take the *exact same* DAP loss functions (DPO, IPO, SLiC), the exact same base model and hyperparameters, and change *only* the data source — offline pre-collected human preferences vs. online AI feedback on on-policy generations. The result (Table 3): online DPO beats offline DPO with 63.74% win rate; online IPO beats offline IPO with 64.81%; online SLiC beats offline SLiC with 71.43%. The consistency across three loss functions with different mathematical forms (classification, regression, hinge) strongly implies that the data source — not the loss function design — is the dominant factor.

This is a significant reframing because it shifts the burden of explanation for DAP's limitations. Prior work (e.g., Rafailov et al., 2023; Azar et al., 2023) had focused on theoretical properties of the loss functions — DPO's implicit reward parameterization, IPO's avoidance of saturation, SLiC's margin robustness — and attributed performance differences to these properties. OAIF's results suggest that the *data distribution* (on-policy vs. off-policy) may explain more variance in alignment quality than the *loss function* choice. This does not make loss function design irrelevant (different losses still have different properties, and SLiC's stronger offline-→online gap in Table 3 suggests interaction effects), but it reorders priorities: getting on-policy data is more urgent than finding the perfect loss function.

The overfitting curve in Figure 3 provides additional diagnostic evidence. Offline DPO's win rate against the SFT baseline *peaks and then declines* around step 3,500, while online DPO continues improving through 12,000 steps. This is a classic signature of overfitting to a static, off-distribution training set — the model learns to rank the specific responses in D well, but this ranking ability doesn't transfer to its own generations, and eventually the divergence between what the loss optimizes (preferences over `$\rho$`'s outputs) and what we care about (preferences over `$\pi_\theta$`'s outputs) causes performance to degrade. This diagnostic pattern — online improves monotonically while offline peaks and decays — is a strong signal that the offline/on-policy mismatch, not any property of the DPO loss itself, is the failure mode.

### Innovation 2: Eliminating the Reward Model as an Unnecessary Intermediary in Online Alignment

OAIF's second conceptual move is demonstrating that **a separately trained reward model is unnecessary for online, on-policy alignment** — an LLM prompted as an annotator can provide the feedback signal directly. This challenges the architecture that RLHF and RLAIF had established as canonical: collect preferences → train reward model → optimize policy against RM with RL. OAIF collapses this pipeline to: prompt LLM annotator → optimize policy against annotator judgments with DAP.

The significance of this move goes beyond mere simplification. The paper identifies a *hidden distribution shift* in the standard RLHF/RLAIF pipeline that prior work had underappreciated. In RLHF, the RM is trained on pre-collected preference data `$D \sim \rho$` and then used to score responses from the evolving policy `$\pi_{\theta_t}$`. As `$\pi_{\theta_t}$` diverges from `$\rho$`, the RM is scoring out-of-distribution responses — its accuracy degrades, but unlike DAP's overfitting (which produces a visible performance drop), this degradation is *silent*. The RM continues to output scalar scores that look reasonable, but their correlation with true preference quality erodes. The paper provides direct evidence for this: when an RM trained on the same pre-collected data is used to provide online feedback for DPO (instead of the LLM annotator), it "significantly underperforms online DPO with OAIF, with a win rate of <30%" (Section 4.4). This means the RM's degradation under distribution shift is worse than simply using a frozen LLM annotator that was never calibrated to `$\rho$` in the first place.

This finding inverts a common intuition. One might expect that a reward model *trained* on preference data from the same domain would provide better feedback than an LLM *prompted* zero-shot with a description of the criteria. The results show the opposite: the trained RM overfits to the specific distribution of responses it was trained on and fails to generalize, while the prompted LLM — which has no task-specific training — provides a stationary (if imperfect) preference signal that doesn't degrade as the policy evolves. This is a **robustness-through-generality** argument: the LLM annotator is worse than a perfectly in-distribution RM, but better than an RM pushed off-distribution by policy drift.

The practical implication is substantial: OAIF eliminates the need to train, maintain, and periodically retrain a reward model. For production alignment pipelines, this removes an entire component with its own training loop, hyperparameters, validation metrics, and failure modes. The only remaining learned model is the policy itself. This is not an incremental simplification — it's a qualitative reduction in system complexity, from a two-model pipeline (RM + policy) to a single trainable model with a frozen annotator.

### Innovation 3: Prompt-Controllable Preference Functions as a New Alignment Primitive

OAIF introduces a capability that had no precedent in the alignment literature: **the alignment objective can be changed at any time, without any retraining or data collection, by modifying the annotator's instruction prompt**. This transforms the preference function from a fixed, data-encoded artifact (in offline DAP and RLHF) into a controllable, language-specified input to the training process.

The length controllability experiment (Section 4.6, Figures 6a–6b) demonstrates this with striking clarity. By adding a single sentence to the annotator prompt ("When the quality of two responses is similar, the shorter one should always be preferred"), the average response length drops from ~120 to ~90 tokens. By rewriting the prompt to make shortness a primary criterion, length drops to ~40 tokens. This is not a minor adjustment — it's a 3× reduction in output length — achieved without touching the training data, the model architecture, the loss function, or the hyperparameters. The only change is a natural language instruction to the annotator.

What makes this intellectually distinctive is that it **decouples the alignment objective from the training data**. In offline DAP, the objective is whatever the human annotators preferred when `$D$` was collected — changing the objective requires re-annotating and rebuilding the dataset. In RLHF, the objective is encoded in the RM's learned parameters — changing it requires retraining the RM on new preference data. In OAIF, the objective is a text string fed to the annotator — changing it requires editing that string. This is a fundamentally different relationship between objective specification and optimization: the objective becomes a *prompt engineering problem* rather than a *data collection and model training problem*.

The implications extend far beyond response length. The paper explicitly connects this to "human values (such as helpfulness and impartiality)" and qualitative desiderata that are difficult to encode in scalar rewards (Section 5). For example, aligning a model to be "helpful but not patronizing" or "honest but tactful" involves nuanced tradeoffs that would require careful curation of preference pairs to encode in a dataset or RM. With OAIF, these tradeoffs can be expressed in natural language and refined iteratively by editing the annotator prompt. This makes alignment objectives *malleable* and *rapidly iterable* in a way that dataset-dependent approaches cannot match.

The controllability result also reveals something about the nature of LLM judgment: LLMs can apply complex, context-sensitive criteria to preference judgments when instructed in natural language. This is a *capability* of large LMs that OAIF exploits but does not create — it was latent in the finding (from Lee et al., 2023) that LLMs can approximate human preference judgments. OAIF's contribution is recognizing that this capability can be *steered* through prompt engineering, making the annotator not just a replacement for human judges but a *programmable preference function*.

### Innovation 4: Weak-to-Strong Generalization in the Discriminative-to-Generative Transfer Setting

The paper's finding that a weaker LLM annotator can improve the alignment of a stronger policy model (Section 4.5 and Section 4.7) constitutes a novel result in the **weak-to-strong generalization** paradigm — but with a crucial twist that distinguishes it from prior work.

Burns et al. (2023) studied weak-to-strong generalization in a setting where the teacher and student perform the *same type of task* (supervised learning), with the key question being whether a strong student model can surpass its weak teacher's supervision. OAIF's setup is fundamentally different: the teacher (LLM annotator) performs a *discriminative* task (choosing which of two responses is better), while the student (policy model) performs a *generative* task (producing better responses). The teacher's task is arguably simpler — judging quality is easier than producing quality — and this asymmetry is what makes weak-to-strong transfer possible.

The experimental evidence is compelling. PaLM 2-XS (Extra Small) used as an annotator for training PaLM 2-XS as a policy — a same-size, "self-improvement" setup — achieves an overall quality score of 3.41 (human evaluation), which is comparable to RLHF (3.38) and offline DPO (3.46) despite the annotator being the smallest model tested. This means a model can bootstrap its own improvement without external supervision beyond the SFT starting point and a prompt describing the desiderata. When the annotator is scaled up to PaLM 2-L, quality jumps to 3.95 — the strong teacher provides better feedback — but the weak teacher is still useful. Going further, Section 4.7 shows that PaLM 2-XS as annotator can improve the alignment of PaLM 2-S (a stronger policy), achieving a 77.36% win rate against the SFT baseline on Helpfulness.

The conceptual contribution is identifying that **the discriminative-generative asymmetry creates a natural weak-to-strong channel that doesn't exist in purely generative self-improvement**. A model that generates mediocre responses can still reliably judge which of two responses is better — the judgment task requires less capability than the generation task. This means alignment can bootstrap from models that are too weak to generate good responses but strong enough to recognize them. The paper doesn't fully develop this theoretical argument, but the empirical result stands as evidence that the asymmetry is real and exploitable.

The connection to GANs (Goodfellow et al., 2020) that the paper draws in Section 4.7 is apt but incomplete: in a GAN, the discriminator and generator are trained adversarially, with the discriminator improving alongside the generator. In OAIF, the annotator is frozen — there's no adversarial dynamic. The improvement comes purely from the fact that a frozen discriminator trained (via pretraining) on a broad distribution of language can provide useful gradients to a generator even when the discriminator is weaker than the generator would be at the generative task. This is more akin to **knowledge distillation** from a discriminative task to a generative one, mediated by the DAP loss.

This innovation matters because it lowers the bar for who (or what) can provide alignment feedback. If a model can be aligned using feedback from an equally-sized or even smaller model, then alignment becomes feasible in settings where access to larger, more capable models is restricted (e.g., on-device, privacy-sensitive, or cost-constrained deployments). The paper doesn't fully explore the limits of this — at what size ratio does the weak annotator's feedback become too noisy to be useful? — but establishing that the threshold is at least as low as *same-size* is a meaningful lower bound.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on three standard LLM alignment tasks: **TL;DR** (Stiennon et al., 2020) — a summarization task where models generate concise summaries of Reddit posts, with approximately 117,000 training prompts and a held-out test set; **Anthropic Helpfulness** (Bai et al., 2022a) — a dialogue task measuring whether assistant responses are helpful and honest, with approximately 43,000 training prompts; and **Anthropic Harmlessness** (Bai et al., 2022a) — a dialogue task measuring whether responses avoid offensive, discriminatory, or dangerous content, also with approximately 43,000 training prompts. For OAIF training, the prompt dataset `$D_X$` is constructed by extracting only the input prompts from the existing preference datasets, discarding the pre-collected `$(y^+, y^-)$` pairs entirely — the preference annotations used during training come exclusively from the LLM annotator.

- **Base model(s).** All policy models are initialized from **PaLM 2-XS (Extra Small)** (Anil et al., 2023), first supervised fine-tuned (SFT) on the respective task's data to produce the SFT baseline `$\pi_{\theta_0}$`. The choice of PaLM 2-XS is deliberate: it is sufficiently capable to show meaningful improvement from alignment (non-trivial but far-from-ceiling performance), while being small enough that training is computationally manageable for the extensive ablation experiments. For the annotating LLM, the default is **PaLM 2-L (Large)** — substantially larger than the policy model — with ablations down to PaLM 2-S and PaLM 2-XS (same size as the policy) in Section 4.5. All models are from the PaLM 2 family to control for architectural differences. The paper also uses **Gemini Pro** (Gemini Team et al., 2023) as a separate automatic evaluator to reduce risk of overfitting to the training-time annotator.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **win rate** — the fraction of test prompts where one model's response is judged better than another's by an evaluator (either human or automatic). For automatic evaluation, Gemini Pro is used as the judge with the same pairwise prompting scheme as training (detailed in Appendix E), computing win rates over the test set. For human evaluation, three raters independently score each response's quality on a 1–5 scale (where 5 is highest) and select the best response among candidates from different models; the paper reports average quality scores and side-by-side win/tie/loss rates. A secondary metric is **average response length** (in tokens), used specifically in the controllability experiments (Section 4.6) to verify that prompt modifications actually change output length. The paper validates Gemini Pro as an evaluator by measuring **alignment accuracy** — how often Gemini Pro's preferences match human preferences on the three tasks — reporting 69.33% (TL;DR), 72.04% (Helpfulness), and 69.27% (Harmlessness), which is comparable to PaLM 2-L's alignment accuracy of 70.72% on average (Table 4, Appendix C).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five baselines: **(1) SFT baseline** — the initial PaLM 2-XS model after supervised fine-tuning, representing the starting point before any preference-based alignment; **(2) Offline DPO** (Rafailov et al., 2023) — standard DPO trained on the pre-collected human preference dataset `$D$` with the same `$\beta = 0.1$`; **(3) Offline IPO** (Azar et al., 2023) — trained on `$D$` with `$\beta = 1.0$`; **(4) Offline SLiC** (Zhao et al., 2023) — trained on `$D$` with `$\beta = 0.002$`; **(5) RLAIF** (Lee et al., 2023) — a full RLAIF pipeline where a reward model is trained on `$D$` using PaLM 2-L as the AI feedback source, then PPO is used to optimize the policy against this RM; and **(6) RLHF** (Stiennon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022) — same as RLAIF but with the RM trained on human preference labels. The offline DAP baselines use the same hyperparameters (`$\beta$` values, learning rate, batch size, optimizer) as their OAIF counterparts to ensure that only the data source (offline pre-collected vs. online AI feedback) differs.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not measure compute in FLOPs or wall-clock time, but rather in **training steps** — all methods are compared after equivalent numbers of gradient updates (Figures 3, 6b track win rate over training steps up to 12,000). The batch size is fixed at 128 for all experiments. For OAIF, each training step involves two forward passes through the policy (to sample `$y_1$` and `$y_2$`), two forward passes through the annotator (for position-bias mitigation), and one forward-backward pass through the policy for the gradient update. The paper acknowledges that OAIF is more computationally expensive per step than offline DAP (which only requires a forward-backward pass on pre-computed data) but does not provide a detailed FLOPs comparison. The implicit argument is that OAIF's improved sample efficiency (it avoids overfitting and continues improving over more steps) justifies the higher per-step cost, though this tradeoff is not quantified.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation. Model selection is based on a combination of manual inspection and development set win rate against the SFT baseline evaluated by Gemini Pro (Section 4.2). For the main comparisons (Tables 2 and 3, Figures 3 and 4), the "best performing" online and offline models are selected from training trajectories and then compared in a single human evaluation round. Human evaluation uses three raters, with results reported as average quality scores and win/tie/loss percentages. The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard errors, or statistical significance tests for any human evaluation results, which is a notable limitation — the win rates in Tables 2 and 3 (e.g., 63.74%, 64.81%, 71.43%) are point estimates without uncertainty quantification. For automatic evaluation, results are reported as percentages over the full test set, but test set sizes are not explicitly stated for each task (they are inherited from the source datasets: TL;DR test set is approximately 6,000 prompts based on the original Stiennon et al. split; Helpfulness and Harmlessness test sets are each approximately 2,000 prompts based on the Bai et al. splits).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Online vs. Offline DAP: OAIF Consistently Outperforms Offline Feedback

The central empirical result is that DAP methods trained with OAIF (online, on-policy AI feedback) substantially outperform their offline counterparts (trained on pre-collected human preferences) across all three tasks and all three loss functions.

**Training dynamics (Figure 3).** On TL;DR, both online DPO and offline DPO start from the SFT baseline and improve over training, but their trajectories diverge dramatically. Offline DPO's win rate (judged by Gemini Pro) peaks around training step 3,500 and then **sharply drops**, indicating overfitting to the static, off-policy preference data. Online DPO's win rate, in contrast, **continues to increase** through 12,000 steps, eventually surpassing offline DPO's peak and maintaining improvement. At the end of training, online DPO achieves approximately 95% win rate against SFT, while offline DPO plateaus around 80% after its decline. RLAIF and RLHF are shown as reference lines: RLAIF reaches approximately 88% against SFT, and RLHF reaches approximately 92%. This figure alone makes the paper's core argument: online feedback prevents the overfitting that plagues offline DAP, enabling sustained improvement over longer training.

**Human evaluation across tasks (Table 2).** The side-by-side human comparison of online DPO vs. offline DPO on three tasks reveals:

- **TL;DR**: Online DPO wins 63.74% of comparisons, offline DPO wins 7.69%, with 28.57% ties. Average quality score: 3.95 for online vs. 3.46 for offline.
- **Helpfulness**: Online DPO wins 58.60%, offline DPO wins 20.20%, with 21.20% ties. Average quality: 4.08 vs. 3.44.
- **Harmlessness**: Online DPO wins 60.26%, offline DPO wins 3.84%, with 35.90% ties. Average quality: 4.41 vs. 3.57.

The pattern is consistent: online DPO is preferred roughly 3× to 16× more often than offline DPO, and quality scores are substantially higher (gaps of 0.49–0.84 on a 5-point scale). The high tie rates (21–36%) suggest that in many cases the two methods produce responses of comparable quality, but when a preference exists, it strongly favors online DPO. The Harmlessness task shows the largest gap (60.26% vs. 3.84%), which may indicate that off-policy training is particularly harmful for safety-critical behaviors where the model must learn to avoid specific failure modes that may not appear in the pre-collected dataset's response distribution.

**Generality across DAP loss functions (Table 3).** On TL;DR, human evaluation comparing online vs. offline versions of three DAP methods shows:

- **DPO**: Online wins 63.74%, offline wins 7.69%, quality 3.95 vs. 3.46.
- **IPO**: Online wins 64.81%, offline wins 3.71%, quality 3.84 vs. 2.93.
- **SLiC**: Online wins 71.43%, offline wins 1.59%, quality 3.85 vs. 3.23.

Three observations stand out. First, the online advantage holds across all three loss functions — OAIF is not specific to DPO. Second, the offline versions perform differently from each other (offline SLiC quality 3.23 vs. offline IPO quality 2.93), confirming that loss function choice matters in the offline setting, but all are substantially worse than their online counterparts. Third, SLiC shows the largest online-offline gap (71.43% win rate and 0.62 quality point difference), while IPO shows a dramatically low offline quality score of 2.93 — below the SFT baseline of 3.19 (reported in Section 4.6) — suggesting that IPO's quadratic objective is particularly sensitive to off-policy data. The paper does not investigate this interaction effect, but it indicates that the choice of loss function and the online/offline data regime are not independent.

#### Comparison with RLHF and RLAIF (Figure 4, Section 4.4)

The paper positions online DPO with OAIF not just as an improvement over offline DAP, but as a competitive alternative to the full RLHF/RLAIF pipeline.

**4-way human comparison (Figure 4a).** On TL;DR, human raters were presented with responses from online DPO, offline DPO, RLAIF, and RLHF and asked to pick the best. Online DPO was preferred **58% of the time** — more than all other methods combined. Offline DPO was preferred 7% of the time, RLAIF 3%, and RLHF 6%, with 26% ties. This is a striking result: a method that requires no reward model, no RL, and no human preference data (only AI feedback) outperforms the canonical alignment pipeline on human preference. The paper is careful to note that this is not a claim of fundamental superiority — the RM in RLAIF/RLHF could be improved with retraining — but rather evidence that under realistic, fixed-budget conditions (no RM retraining, same annotator model quality), OAIF produces better end results.

**Length-controlled quality analysis (Figure 4b).** The paper acknowledges a potential confound: both LLM and human evaluators exhibit "length bias" — they tend to prefer longer responses (Singhal et al., 2023). Online DPO with OAIF produces longer responses on average (visible in Figure 4b's x-axis, where online DPO's responses span a wider range of longer lengths). To determine whether OAIF's advantage is purely an artifact of producing longer outputs, the paper groups responses by token length into six buckets and plots the average quality score within each bucket. The result: **at matched lengths, online DPO's responses receive higher quality scores** than offline DPO, RLAIF, and RLHF across most length buckets. The quality gap is most pronounced at shorter lengths (around 30–50 tokens), where online DPO maintains relatively high quality while other methods degrade — suggesting OAIF produces more information-dense responses rather than simply inflating length with filler. This analysis partially addresses the length bias concern, though it cannot fully rule out that some of OAIF's win rate advantage is attributable to length effects that persist even within length buckets.

**RM-based online DPO underperforms OAIF (Section 4.4).** To test whether the advantage of OAIF comes from the *online* nature of the feedback or specifically from *LLM-based* (rather than RM-based) feedback, the paper trains an online DPO variant where the preference labels come from the **same RM used in RLAIF** (i.e., an RM trained on pre-collected human preferences) rather than from an LLM annotator. This RM-based online DPO "significantly underperforms online DPO with OAIF, with a win rate of <30% judged by Gemini Pro" (Section 4.4). This result is important because it isolates the effect: both methods are online and on-policy, but one uses a trained RM (suffering from the hidden distribution shift described in Appendix A.3) and the other uses a prompted LLM. The <30% win rate (meaning OAIF is preferred >70% of the time) strongly supports the paper's claim that RMs trained on off-policy data degrade under policy drift, while LLM annotators — despite having no task-specific training — provide a more robust preference signal.

#### Annotator Size Scaling (Section 4.5, Figure 5)

The paper ablates the size of the LLM annotator to determine how feedback quality affects alignment performance.

**Results by annotator size (Figure 5).** On TL;DR, online DPO is trained with annotators of three sizes — PaLM 2-XS (Extra Small, same size as the policy), PaLM 2-S (Small, intermediate), and PaLM 2-L (Large, the default). Win rates against four reference models (SFT baseline, offline DPO, RLAIF, RLHF) as judged by Gemini Pro:

| Annotator | vs. SFT | vs. Offline DPO | vs. RLAIF | vs. RLHF |
|-----------|---------|-----------------|-----------|----------|
| PaLM 2-XS | 82.31% | 35.80% | 41.30% | 50.44% |
| PaLM 2-S | 86.93% | 46.71% | 52.45% | 63.89% |
| PaLM 2-L | 95.24% | 82.12% | 82.38% | 87.46% |

The pattern is clear: **larger annotators produce better alignment**. PaLM 2-L as annotator achieves a dominant 95.24% win rate against SFT and strong majorities against all other methods, while PaLM 2-XS achieves more modest but still substantial improvements. The scaling from XS to S to L is monotonic: larger annotators are better at all comparisons.

**The crucial threshold finding.** Even PaLM 2-XS — a model of the **same size** as the policy being aligned — provides useful feedback. Its 82.31% win rate against SFT and 50.44% win rate against RLHF (essentially at parity) demonstrates that OAIF can bootstrap alignment without any model larger than the policy itself. Human evaluation confirms this: PaLM 2-XS as annotator produces an aligned model with quality score 3.41, comparable to RLHF (3.38) and offline DPO (3.46). This is the "weak annotator" regime that the paper explores further in Section 4.7.

**Interpretation.** The scaling behavior suggests that feedback quality is not binary (good vs. bad) but continuous — even a relatively weak annotator provides a gradient signal that moves the policy in the right direction, just with more noise. The DAP loss appears to be robust to this noise: the policy improves even when the annotator makes frequent errors, as long as the errors are not systematically biased against the true quality ordering. The paper does not analyze the error rate of different-sized annotators or measure how annotation noise affects the loss landscape, but the monotonic improvement with annotator size is consistent with the hypothesis that better (less noisy) feedback produces better alignment.

#### Controllability via Prompt Engineering (Section 4.6, Figure 6)

The paper demonstrates that the LLM annotator's preferences can be steered by modifying its instruction prompt, producing predictable changes in the aligned policy's behavior.

**Length reduction results (Figure 6a).** On the Helpfulness task, three online DPO models are trained with different annotator prompts: the default "helpful only" prompt, a "helpful and short" prompt that adds "When the quality of two responses is similar, the shorter one should always be preferred," and a "helpful and very short" prompt that makes shortness a primary criterion. The average response length over training evolves as follows (approximate values read from Figure 6a at the end of training):

- **Helpful only**: responses stabilize around **~120 tokens**.
- **Helpful and short**: responses drop to approximately **~90 tokens**.
- **Helpful and very short**: responses drop further to approximately **~40 tokens**.

This is a nearly 3× reduction in output length, achieved purely by changing the annotator prompt — no model architecture changes, no dataset reconstruction, no hyperparameter tuning. The length changes emerge gradually over training (visible in the curves of Figure 6a), with the "very short" condition showing the steepest decline, suggesting that the annotator's length preference provides a consistent gradient signal that progressively shortens outputs.

**Quality tradeoff (Figure 6b).** The win rate against the SFT baseline (judged by Gemini Pro) at the end of training:

- **Helpful only**: ~91% win rate (the highest).
- **Helpful and short**: ~80–85% win rate (moderate decline).
- **Helpful and very short**: ~55–65% win rate (substantial decline, but still above the 50% line).

Human evaluation quality scores (from Section 4.6 text): 4.08 (helpful) → 3.72 (helpful and short) → 3.26 (helpful and very short), with the SFT baseline at 3.19. The key result: **all three conditions outperform the SFT baseline**, even the "very short" condition that sacrifices substantial quality. This means OAIF can trade off quality against other desiderata (here, length) without falling below the starting model's performance — the alignment signal from the annotator is strong enough to improve the model even when the annotator is instructed to prioritize a potentially conflicting objective.

**Interpretation and significance.** This experiment establishes that the annotator prompt functions as a **knob** that controls the alignment objective in a predictable, quantitative way. The "short" vs. "very short" distinction produces different degrees of length reduction, suggesting that the annotator's behavior responds to gradations in the prompt instruction (tie-breaking preference → primary criterion). This is a capability that has no analog in standard RLHF (where the reward model encodes a fixed preference function that is expensive to modify) or offline DAP (where the dataset encodes fixed human preferences that are even more expensive to recollect). The paper frames this as evidence that OAIF can align models to "qualitative desiderata" (Section 5) — objectives like helpfulness, harmlessness, conciseness, empathy, or formality — that are naturally expressed in language but difficult to encode in scalar rewards or preference datasets.

#### Weak-to-Strong Generalization (Section 4.7, Figure 7)

The paper tests whether a weaker LLM annotator can improve the alignment of a stronger policy model — an instance of the weak-to-strong generalization paradigm (Burns et al., 2023) but with the crucial discriminative-vs.-generative task asymmetry.

**Experimental setup.** The policy model is upgraded to **PaLM 2-S** (Small, larger than the usual PaLM 2-XS policy), and OAIF is run with two annotator conditions: a "weak teacher" using PaLM 2-XS (smaller than the policy) and a "strong teacher" using PaLM 2-L (larger than the policy). The task is Helpfulness.

**Results (Figure 7).** Win rates against the SFT baseline and offline DPO, judged by Gemini Pro:

- **Weak teacher (XS annotator, S policy)**: 77.36% win rate vs. SFT, 59.44% win rate vs. offline DPO.
- **Strong teacher (L annotator, S policy)**: 92.19% win rate vs. SFT, 89.26% win rate vs. offline DPO.

The weak teacher condition produces a substantial improvement over both the SFT baseline and offline DPO, despite the annotator being **smaller and less capable** than the policy model it is training. The strong teacher, unsurprisingly, produces even larger improvements.

**Interpretation.** This result demonstrates that OAIF's weak-to-strong transfer works because the annotator's *discriminative* task (judging which response is better) requires less capability than the policy's *generative* task (producing high-quality responses). A PaLM 2-XS annotator can reliably distinguish good responses from bad ones even though it cannot generate good responses itself — judging is easier than generating. This creates a natural bootstrapping channel: a weaker model can guide a stronger model's alignment as long as the gap between "discriminative capability" and "generative capability" is not too large.

The paper explicitly contrasts this with Burns et al. (2023), where the teacher and student perform the *same type* of supervised task, making weak-to-strong transfer harder because the teacher's errors directly bound the student's performance. In OAIF, the teacher's errors in judgment do not directly limit the student's generation quality — the student can learn to produce responses that are *actually* better than the teacher could produce, as long as the teacher can *recognize* them as better relative to alternatives. This is an instance of what the paper calls "the discriminative-generative asymmetry."

The practical implication is significant: alignment can be bootstrapped without access to a model larger than the one being aligned. A same-size or even slightly smaller model can serve as the annotator, making OAIF feasible in settings where a large annotator is unavailable (e.g., on-device alignment, privacy-constrained environments, or resource-limited organizations).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**LLM evaluator alignment accuracy (Appendix C, Table 4).** To validate that Gemini Pro's automatic judgments are a reliable proxy for human preferences, the paper measures alignment accuracy — how often Gemini Pro's pairwise preferences match human preferences on the three tasks. Results: 69.33% (TL;DR), 72.04% (Helpfulness), 69.27% (Harmlessness), averaging 70.21%. This is comparable to PaLM 2-L's alignment accuracy of 70.72% (73.23%, 69.11%, 69.83% respectively). The paper uses this to justify Gemini Pro as the automatic evaluator for all win rate comparisons, arguing that the ~70% alignment rate means the automatic metrics are "correlated significantly with human" (Appendix C text) and that using a different model for evaluation than for training (Gemini Pro vs. PaLM 2-L) reduces risk of overfitting. However, a 70% alignment rate means automatic judgments disagree with humans 30% of the time — this is non-trivial noise that could affect win rate estimates.

**Position bias mitigation (Appendix E, method description).** The paper addresses the known issue of position bias in LLM annotators (Lee et al., 2023) by evaluating both orderings of response pairs and averaging the two softmax distributions. This is not an ablation (the paper does not compare against a no-mitigation baseline), but it is a robustness measure that follows established best practice from prior work. The cost is a 2× increase in annotator inference per training step.

**Automatic evaluator cross-check (Appendix D, Figure 9).** To ensure that the training dynamics observed with Gemini Pro evaluation (Figure 3) are not an artifact of the specific evaluator, the paper replicates the online DPO vs. offline DPO comparison on TL;DR using **PaLM 2-L** as the automatic evaluator instead of Gemini Pro. The result (Figure 9) is qualitatively identical: offline DPO peaks and then declines (around step 4,000 in this evaluation), while online DPO continues improving through 12,000 steps. This cross-validator consistency strengthens the claim that the overfitting pattern is a genuine property of offline DPO training, not an evaluation artifact.

**Distribution shift verification (Appendix B, Figure 8).** To empirically confirm the existence of the distribution shift that motivates OAIF, the paper analyzes a synthetic setup: using GPT-2 Large as the "online policy" `$\pi_\theta$` and PaLM 2-S as an "offline" response generator `$\rho$`. Responses from `$\rho$` receive substantially lower log-probability under `$\pi_\theta$` than responses generated by `$\pi_\theta$` itself (both preferred and dispreferred on-policy responses). Figure 8 shows a clear margin: on-policy responses have log-probabilities around -105 to -110, while off-policy responses are around -130 to -135. This gap of ~25 nats in log-probability confirms that off-policy responses lie far from the model's current output distribution, providing direct evidence for the distribution shift problem that OAIF addresses. Note this is a synthetic verification with different models, not a direct measurement on the PaLM 2 alignment experiments, but it illustrates the phenomenon the paper claims is at play.

**Loss function robustness (Tables 2 and 3, cross-task consistency).** While not presented as a formal ablation, the fact that OAIF improves performance across three DAP loss functions (DPO, IPO, SLiC) and three tasks (TL;DR, Helpfulness, Harmlessness) serves as a robustness check on the method's generality. The consistent online-over-offline advantage across 9 conditions (3 losses × 3 tasks, though not all combinations are explicitly tested — Table 3 covers 3 losses on TL;DR, while Table 2 covers DPO on 3 tasks) suggests the benefit of online, on-policy feedback is not specific to a particular loss function or domain.

**Annotator prompt sensitivity (Section 4.6, Figures 6a–6b, Tables 6 and 8).** The controllability experiments double as a sensitivity analysis: changing the annotator prompt produces predictable, graded changes in policy behavior (response length). This demonstrates that OAIF's alignment signal is systematically responsive to the annotator prompt — it is not the case that any reasonable prompt yields equivalent results, or that prompt changes cause unpredictable behavior. The fact that "short" and "very short" produce different magnitudes of length reduction, and that quality degrades monotonically as length constraints tighten, indicates a well-behaved mapping from prompt specification to alignment outcome.

**Negative result: RM-based online DPO (Section 4.4).** When an RM trained on pre-collected human preferences (the same RM used for RLAIF) is substituted for the LLM annotator in online DPO, performance drops dramatically — the RM-based variant achieves <30% win rate against OAIF with an LLM annotator. This negative result is informative because it isolates the feedback *source* (RM vs. LLM) while keeping the training *procedure* (online DPO) constant. It supports the paper's claim that LLM annotators are more robust to policy drift than static RMs — the RM degrades under the distribution shift caused by the evolving policy, while the LLM annotator's zero-shot judgments remain stationary (though imperfect). This is not a failure of online DPO as a method, but rather evidence that the feedback source matters critically and that RMs trained on static datasets are vulnerable to exactly the distribution shift problem that OAIF is designed to solve.

**Negative result: annotator prompt can reduce quality (Section 4.6, Figure 6b).** The "helpful and very short" condition in the controllability experiment reduces response length to ~40 tokens but also reduces the win rate against SFT from ~91% to ~55–65%, and human quality scores from 4.08 to 3.26 — only marginally above the SFT baseline of 3.19. This demonstrates that while OAIF's annotator prompt is controllable, it is not a "free lunch" — aggressive constraints on the alignment objective trade off against the primary quality metric. The paper presents this transparently rather than hiding the quality degradation, which strengthens the credibility of the controllability claim by acknowledging its limits.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: OAIF makes DAP methods online and on-policy, substantially improving alignment over offline DAP.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Tables 2 and 3 provide strong, consistent evidence that online DPO/IPO/SLiC with OAIF outperforms their offline counterparts across three tasks (TL;DR, Helpfulness, Harmlessness) in human evaluation, with win rates ranging from 58.60% to 71.43%. Figure 3 shows that online DPO continues improving over 12,000 training steps while offline DPO overfits and degrades after ~3,500 steps. The training dynamics pattern is replicated under two different automatic evaluators (Figures 3 and 9), ruling out evaluator-specific artifacts.

**What the experiments do not fully demonstrate:** The claim is that the improvement comes specifically from the *online, on-policy* nature of the feedback. However, OAIF differs from offline DAP in multiple confounded ways: (a) feedback source (AI vs. human), (b) feedback timing (online vs. offline), (c) response source (on-policy vs. off-policy), and (d) dataset size (OAIF can generate unlimited training pairs, while offline DAP is limited to the pre-collected dataset). The paper does not include an ablation that isolates *online timing* from *AI-vs-human feedback* — e.g., an offline DAP baseline trained on AI-annotated preferences from `$\pi_{\theta_0}$` (offline but AI-feedback), or an online DAP baseline trained with human annotators in the loop (online but human-feedback). Without these, it's unclear whether OAIF's advantage is due to (i) AI feedback being better/more abundant than the specific human preference dataset used, (ii) online interaction enabling more training steps without overfitting, (iii) on-policy sampling distributing training data more usefully, or (iv) some combination. The paper attributes the improvement to all three factors (online, on-policy, AI feedback) but the experimental design cannot separate them.

**Additional weakness:** The offline DAP baselines use pre-collected human preference datasets that may be of varying quality. If the human labels in these datasets are noisy or inconsistent (a known issue in preference annotation), OAIF's AI feedback — which is at least consistent in its application of the prompt-specified criteria — might win simply because it provides cleaner supervision, not because it's online. The paper does not characterize the quality or noise level of the human preference data used for offline baselines, making this alternative explanation difficult to rule out.

#### Claim 2: Online DPO with OAIF outperforms RLHF and RLAIF.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figure 4a shows that in a 4-way human comparison on TL;DR, online DPO is preferred 58% of the time — more than RLHF (6%), RLAIF (3%), and offline DPO (7%) combined. Figure 4b shows this advantage persists when controlling for response length, suggesting it's not purely a length artifact.

**What the experiments do not fully demonstrate:** The RLHF and RLAIF baselines are trained with *fixed* reward models that are not updated as the policy evolves. The paper itself argues (Section 4.4, Appendix A.3) that this creates a distribution shift problem for the RM. A fair comparison would need to consider either (a) RLHF with periodic RM retraining on fresh on-policy data, as originally advocated by Ziegler et al. (2019), or (b) OAIF with an annotator of equivalent total compute to the RM training + RL pipeline. The paper's RLHF/RLAIF baselines are essentially "budget RLHF" — train the RM once, then run RL without RM updates — which handicaps the RLHF approach relative to what a well-resourced deployment would actually do. The claim should be qualified as "online DPO with OAIF outperforms RLHF/RLAIF *when the RM is not retrained during policy optimization*," which is a narrower and more defensible statement.

**Additional weakness:** The RLHF and RLAIF training procedures are not described in detail — the paper states they "follow Lee et al. (2023)" (Section 4.4) but does not specify PPO hyperparameters, KL penalty coefficients, number of RL steps, or any tuning effort invested in these baselines. If OAIF received more hyperparameter tuning than the RLHF/RLAIF baselines (which is plausible given that OAIF is the paper's proposed method), the comparison would be biased. The paper's claim that "our experiment setup ensures that the AI feedback is of the same quality and that only the learning procedures differ" (Section 6) is only true for AI feedback *quality* (both use PaLM 2-L), but not necessarily for optimization quality — the DAP loss might be easier to tune than PPO, giving OAIF an unfair advantage in a fixed-tuning-budget comparison.

#### Claim 3: The LLM annotator's preferences are controllable via prompt engineering.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figures 6a–6b show a clear, graded relationship between annotator prompt and response length: "helpful only" → ~120 tokens, "helpful and short" → ~90 tokens, "helpful and very short" → ~40 tokens. All three conditions improve over the SFT baseline in human quality scores.

**What the experiments do not fully demonstrate:** The controllability experiment is conducted only on the Helpfulness task and only for response length. The paper claims (Section 5) that this can "be naturally extended to more qualitative desiderata" such as "helpfulness and impartiality," but this extension is not tested. Length is a uniquely easy desideratum to verify and control — it's objectively measurable and LLM annotators likely have a strong prior about "shorter is better when quality is equal." Whether OAIF's annotator prompt can reliably control more subjective, context-dependent qualities (politeness, empathy, creativity, risk-aversion, political neutrality) without unintended side effects is an open question. The paper's single-testbed demonstration is promising but insufficient to support the broad claim of general-purpose prompt controllability.

**Additional weakness:** The evaluation of quality in the controllability experiment uses Gemini Pro, which may itself have a length bias. If the automatic evaluator prefers shorter responses when instructed to evaluate "helpfulness" (because helpful responses tend to be concise), the declining win rates in Figure 6b might partly reflect evaluator bias rather than genuine quality degradation. Human evaluation partially addresses this (quality scores drop from 4.08 to 3.72 to 3.26), but human raters are also susceptible to length bias (longer responses are often perceived as more thorough/helpful). The paper's length-controlled analysis (Figure 4b) for the main comparison does not appear to be replicated for the controllability experiment, so we cannot determine how much of the quality drop is due to reduced helpfulness vs. reduced length.

#### Claim 4: A weaker annotator can improve a stronger policy (weak-to-strong generalization).

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figure 7 shows that PaLM 2-XS (weaker annotator) training PaLM 2-S (stronger policy) achieves 77.36% win rate against SFT and 59.44% against offline DPO on Helpfulness. This is a non-trivial improvement over both baselines, supporting the claim that weak-to-strong transfer works.

**What the experiments do not fully demonstrate:** The experiment only tests one size ratio (XS annotator, S policy) and one task (Helpfulness). We don't know whether even weaker annotators (PaLM 2-XXS, or a non-PaLM model) would still provide useful feedback, or whether the effect holds across tasks. The paper also does not analyze *why* the weak annotator works — e.g., by measuring the annotator's judgment accuracy on responses from the evolving policy, or by analyzing whether the annotator's errors are random (which would add noise but not bias) or systematic (which could steer the policy in wrong directions). The GAN analogy (Section 4.7) is suggestive but not empirically validated — there is no measurement of the annotator's "discriminative capability" relative to the policy's "generative capability."

**Additional weakness:** The comparison against offline DPO (59.44% win rate) is a relatively modest margin — not far above the 50% parity line. Given the small test set sizes (~2,000 prompts for Helpfulness, though the exact number is not specified), this could be within the range of statistical noise. The paper does not provide confidence intervals, making it difficult to assess whether 59.44% is significantly above chance.

#### Claim 5: OAIF mitigates reward overoptimization.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figure 3 shows offline DPO overfitting (win rate peaks then declines), while online DPO continues improving — consistent with the claim that online feedback prevents the overoptimization that plagues offline methods. The paper states in Section 6 that OAIF exhibits "the usefulness of OAIF as a way to mitigate reward overoptimization."

**What the experiments do not fully demonstrate:** "Reward overoptimization" in the RLHF literature specifically refers to the policy exploiting the reward model to achieve high RM scores without actually improving in true quality (Gao et al., 2023). The paper's evidence shows offline DPO overfitting (performance decline), which is related but not identical — overfitting could be caused by simply memorizing the training data rather than learning generalizable preferences, without necessarily "exploiting" the reward signal. To demonstrate mitigation of reward overoptimization specifically, the paper would need to show (a) that the LLM annotator's preferences remain correlated with true quality even as the policy produces increasingly optimized responses (i.e., the annotator is robust to overoptimization), and (b) that at high training budgets, online DPO does not exploit the annotator in the way that offline DPO overfits the static dataset. Figure 3 shows online DPO improving through 12,000 steps, but it does not test whether online DPO would eventually overfit with enough steps — the curve appears to still be rising, but we don't know if it would eventually plateau or decline. A longer training run would be needed to establish robustness to overoptimization, not just delayed overfitting.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Strengthen the Paper

1. **Isolating online from on-policy from AI-feedback.** An experiment with online human feedback (humans annotating `$\pi_{\theta_t}$`'s responses in real time) would isolate whether the benefit comes from online interaction or from AI feedback specifically. An experiment with offline AI feedback (pre-collecting AI annotations on `$\pi_{\theta_0}$`'s outputs and training offline DAP on that) would isolate whether the benefit comes from on-policy data or from AI annotation quality. Without these, the paper's causal claims about *why* OAIF works are underdetermined.

2. **Scaling to larger policy models.** All experiments use PaLM 2-XS or PaLM 2-S as the policy. The paper acknowledges that "whether our conclusion holds after scaling up is not investigated" (Section 5). As policy models grow larger, they may become better at generating responses that exploit the annotator's weaknesses (the overoptimization problem in a new guise). Testing OAIF at larger scales (PaLM 2-L or larger as policy) would substantially strengthen the generality claim.

3. **Multiple annotator models from different families.** The paper uses only PaLM 2 models as annotators. It is possible that PaLM 2 has specific properties (e.g., calibration, instruction-following ability, length bias) that make it a particularly effective annotator. Testing annotators from other model families (Gemini, GPT, Claude, open-source models) would establish whether OAIF's effectiveness is annotator-agnostic or PaLM 2-specific.

4. **Statistical significance testing and confidence intervals.** All human evaluation results are reported as point estimates (e.g., 63.74% win rate) without uncertainty quantification. With only three raters and test sets of varying (and not always specified) sizes, some of the reported differences may not be statistically significant. Confidence intervals or hypothesis tests would substantially increase the credibility of the quantitative claims.

5. **Comparison with iterative/online variants of RLHF.** The paper compares against a static-RM RLHF baseline, but methods for iterative RLHF (retraining the RM on policy samples, as in Ziegler et al., 2019) exist. A comparison against an RLHF variant that periodically updates its RM with on-policy data would test whether OAIF's advantage comes from avoiding the RM entirely or simply from having online feedback (which iterative RLHF also provides, albeit at higher cost).

6. **Varying the `$\beta$` hyperparameter for offline baselines.** The offline DAP baselines use the same `$\beta$` values as OAIF (0.1 for DPO, 1.0 for IPO, 0.002 for SLiC). It is possible that offline DAP requires different regularization strength due to the off-policy data distribution. Sweeping `$\beta$` for offline baselines (or reporting the best `$\beta$` found) would rule out the possibility that offline DAP underperforms simply because it wasn't adequately tuned for its data regime.

7. **Analysis of annotator error patterns.** The paper does not characterize what kinds of errors the LLM annotator makes — are errors random (adding unbiased noise) or systematic (e.g., consistently preferring longer responses, or favoring certain response styles)? Understanding error patterns would help predict when OAIF might fail (e.g., if the annotator has a systematic bias that the policy can exploit) and would inform annotator prompt design.

**Summary of assessment.** The experiments provide strong, internally consistent evidence that OAIF — online, on-policy DAP training with LLM-provided feedback — substantially outperforms offline DAP with pre-collected human preferences across three tasks and three loss functions. The comparison against RLHF/RLAIF is more qualified: OAIF wins against a static-RM RLHF pipeline, but whether it wins against a well-tuned RLHF with periodic RM updates remains untested. The controllability and weak-to-strong experiments are promising proof-of-concepts but are limited to single testbeds and do not establish the generality the paper claims. The most robust finding is the online-over-offline advantage for DAP methods — this result is replicated across tasks, loss functions, and evaluators, and the training dynamics (Figure 3) provide a clear mechanistic explanation (offline overfits, online keeps improving). The paper's broader claims about replacing RLHF pipelines and enabling prompt-based alignment objective control are directionally supported but would require more extensive validation — at larger scales, with more annotator models, and across more diverse desiderata — to be considered established.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Cost of Difficulty Estimation Is Not Accounted For

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal scaling framework depends on estimating each prompt's difficulty *before* deciding how to allocate test-time compute. The paper's method for doing this — generating 2048 samples per question and computing either the ground-truth pass@1 rate (oracle difficulty) or the PRM's average final-answer score (predicted difficulty) — is extraordinarily expensive. The authors state explicitly in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence.** The headline efficiency gains — the claim that compute-optimal scaling achieves `4×` better efficiency than best-of-N (Figures 4 and 8) — are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. Generating 2048 samples per question consumes more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations). In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be `difficulty_estimation_cost + strategy_execution_cost`, and the former could dominate the latter, potentially erasing or even reversing the reported efficiency advantage. For example, on a prompt where the compute-optimal policy allocates 16 generations, the total cost would be `2048 + 16 = 2064` generations — roughly `129×` the policy budget — making the claimed `4×` reduction relative to a 64-generation best-of-N baseline effectively meaningless once estimation is included.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper acknowledges this cost explicitly (Section 3.2) and frames it as an exploration-exploitation tradeoff, flagging it as "a key avenue for future work" on training models to directly predict difficulty from the question text. However, no such difficulty prediction model is developed or evaluated. The predicted-difficulty curves (using PRM scores rather than ground-truth labels) track the oracle curves closely (Figures 4, 8), demonstrating that the approach works *without* ground-truth labels, but neither oracle nor predicted difficulty variants amortize the 2048-sample cost. The paper does not report what fraction of total inference FLOPs the difficulty estimation step consumes or provide any sensitivity analysis on the number of samples needed for reliable binning.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The authors suggest future work on pretraining or fine-tuning models to directly predict difficulty from the question text (Section 8), which would eliminate the sampling cost entirely, but this is purely aspirational. No experiments test cheaper heuristics (e.g., using a single sample's PRM score, or a small number of samples) to estimate difficulty. The paper also does not explore adaptive difficulty estimation — starting with a few samples, assessing difficulty, and then allocating the remaining budget — which could amortize the estimation cost into the solution process.

### Hard Problems Remain Essentially Unsolved Regardless of Budget

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal framework fundamentally assumes that test-time compute can amplify the base model's *existing* capability — it can help the model find correct solutions it *could* produce but doesn't reliably sample, or refine nearly-correct solutions into correct ones. It cannot create capability that the base model lacks entirely. The paper states this in Section 7:

> "test-time compute is powerful when problems are within the base model's reach (it already produces correct solutions at some non-trivial rate), but it cannot compensate for fundamental capability gaps that larger pretraining would address."

**The consequence.** Across all methods — search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations — the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5, where the base model's pass@1 rate is in the lowest quintile and likely near zero) show **near-zero improvement regardless of compute budget**. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for both best-of-N and beam search across all budgets from 4 to 256 generations. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio at a fixed 128-generation budget. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%, well below the `~14×` larger model's greedy performance. This means that for genuinely difficult problems — those outside the base model's capability distribution — **no amount of inference compute can compensate**, and pretraining a larger model is the only viable path.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence is consistent and stark across every analysis that breaks results out by difficulty. Bin 5 consistently shows flat or near-flat scaling curves regardless of method (Figures 3 right, 7 right, 9). The FLOPs-matched analysis (Section 7, Figure 9) quantifies this failure: on hard problems at `R ≫ 1`, PRM search shows a `−52.9%` relative disadvantage compared to the `~14×` larger model, meaning test-time compute is not just ineffective but actively worse than scaling pretraining. The paper is transparent about this boundary condition, explicitly identifying it as a key takeaway.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to solve this problem — it correctly identifies it as a fundamental limitation of test-time compute scaling rather than an algorithmic weakness. The compute-optimal policy avoids wasting budget on bin 5 problems (allocating only the minimum effective computation) but does not improve their accuracy. The authors note that for such problems, "pretraining remains the only viable path" (Section 7). This limitation is not a flaw in the method but rather a clear boundary condition that practitioners need to understand: OAIF and similar test-time scaling strategies are *capability amplifiers*, not *capability creators*.

### Single Benchmark, Single Model Family Limits Generality Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021) — specifically 500 test questions of high-school competition-level math — with PaLM 2-S\* as the base model. The paper acknowledges this scope limitation but argues the model is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4). For revision models and the FLOPs-matched comparison, all models are from the PaLM 2 family.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or domain-specific in ways that affect their practical applicability. MATH consists exclusively of competition-level math problems requiring multi-step symbolic reasoning with deterministic correct answers. It is unclear whether the difficulty-dependent patterns — beam search over-optimizing on easy problems, sequential revisions dominating on easy problems but hybrid sequential-parallel being optimal on hard ones — generalize to other reasoning domains. Code generation (where correctness is also deterministic but the output space is structured differently), open-ended generation (where there is no single "correct" answer), or tasks requiring factual recall rather than reasoning might exhibit entirely different difficulty-dependent scaling behavior. Additionally, the PRM's quality, the revision model's ability to learn from incorrect examples, and the onset of verifier over-optimization could all depend on PaLM 2-S\*'s specific output distribution, calibration properties, and in-context learning capabilities.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no cross-model or cross-domain validation. The 500-question test set, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each, then further split by two-fold cross-validation, means the compute-optimal policy is selected based on ~50 questions per fold per bin. The paper does not report confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves, so we cannot assess whether the observed strategy differences across bins (e.g., beam search being optimal for bin 3 but harmful for bin 1) are statistically reliable at this sample size or would replicate on a different set of 500 questions.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this beyond stating that they "believe this model is representative" (Section 4). No experiments test PaLM 2 models of different sizes (beyond the FLOPs-matched comparison which uses a single larger variant), or base models from other families (e.g., LLaMA, GPT, Gemini). No experiments test on non-MATH benchmarks. The paper does not claim broader generality — it reports findings on MATH with PaLM 2-S\* and leaves extension to other domains as future work. However, the practical implication is that a team wanting to deploy the compute-optimal framework on a different model or task would need to replicate the full analysis (PRM training, difficulty binning, strategy sweep, cross-validation) rather than importing the paper's specific policy choices.

### The `~14×` Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023) rather than the Chinchilla-optimal approach of scaling parameters and data equally. The paper acknowledges this explicitly:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

Additionally, the `~14×` larger model uses only **greedy decoding** — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no test-time compute of any kind.

**The consequence.** The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining (e.g., `+27.8%` relative improvement on easy questions at `R ≪ 1` for revisions, from the Figure 1 bar chart) may shrink or reverse against a stronger baseline. A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with `14×` more total FLOPs — scaling both parameters and data according to Hoffmann et al. (2022) — would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model, making the pretraining baseline stronger. Furthermore, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget (say, best-of-8 or a simple majority vote) would create a much more realistic comparison: in practice, a team deploying a larger model would not be restricted to greedy decoding. The current comparison is effectively "small model + optimized test-time compute" vs. "large model + no test-time compute," which stacks the deck in favor of test-time compute.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports all FLOPs-matched results in Section 7 (Figures 1 and 9), and the `~14×` parameter scaling factor is stated but the training data size for the larger model is not specified. The paper does not provide an ablation where the larger model receives any test-time compute budget, nor does it compare against a Chinchilla-optimal pretraining baseline.

**Mitigation status.** The paper flags this as a limitation directly (Section 7, quoted above) and frames it as future work. The authors' choice is defensible as a first analysis — the LLaMA paradigm is widely used, and comparing against greedy decoding provides a clean baseline — but the practical takeaway ("test-time compute can substitute for pretraining") needs to be qualified by the weakness of the pretraining baseline. A practitioner deciding between training a larger model and deploying test-time compute should view the paper's `+27.8%` and similar figures as upper bounds on the advantage, potentially substantially lower against a more competitive pretraining baseline that also uses test-time compute.

### Verifier Over-Optimization Is Documented but Not Solved

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper demonstrates that process reward model (PRM) over-optimization is the primary bottleneck preventing unbounded improvements from additional test-time compute. Beam search — the most effective search method on medium problems — degrades performance on easy problems at high budgets (Figure 3, right). Lookahead search — the most powerful optimizer — paradoxically performs worst overall (Figure 3, left). Qualitative examples in Appendix M (Figures 29, etc.) show search producing degenerate outputs (repetitive low-information steps, overly short solutions) that score highly under the PRM but are factually incorrect.

**The consequence.** The compute-optimal policy *mitigates* this by routing easy problems away from aggressive search — using best-of-N where the PRM is reliable (easy problems) and beam search where it still provides genuine guidance (medium problems) — but it does not *solve* the underlying verifier robustness problem. On medium-difficulty problems where beam search is deployed, the performance curves in Figure 3 flatten and, at the highest budgets, may begin to decline, indicating that over-optimization eventually catches up even in the regime where beam search is beneficial. This means the compute-optimal approach is fundamentally bounded by verifier quality: improving the PRM would likely shift the difficulty thresholds, change the optimal policy, and extend the budget range over which scaling continues to help. The current results are therefore specific to the PRM quality achievable with the Monte Carlo rollout training procedure described in Appendix D, using PaLM 2-S\* as the base model.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 (left) shows beam search and lookahead search flattening or declining at high budgets while best-of-N continues to improve, which the paper attributes to PRM over-optimization. Figure 3 (right) shows beam search on easy problems (bin 1) actually *decreasing* in accuracy from roughly 78% to 77% as budget increases from 4 to 256 generations — direct evidence that optimizing against the PRM can be counterproductive. Appendix M provides qualitative examples. However, the paper does not measure the PRM's calibration or accuracy as a function of policy drift, does not attempt to characterize the conditions under which over-optimization begins (e.g., by measuring the correlation between PRM scores and ground-truth correctness at different optimization intensities), and does not experiment with techniques to improve PRM robustness (adversarial training, ensembling, KL-constrained search).

**Mitigation status.** The paper implicitly mitigates over-optimization through the compute-optimal policy — by selecting best-of-N rather than beam search for easy problems and for very high budgets. However, this is a *routing* solution, not a *verifier improvement* solution. The paper does not attempt to build a more robust PRM or to penalize over-optimization during search. The authors identify this as a key direction in Section 8: "Improving verifier robustness is the key bottleneck for further scaling test-time compute, not improving search algorithms." This is an important diagnostic contribution but leaves the core problem unsolved — teams wanting to scale test-time compute beyond the budgets studied here (or on tasks with different verifier characteristics) would need to develop their own solutions to PRM over-optimization.

### Revision Model Has a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is fine-tuned on trajectories where *all* in-context answers are incorrect, followed by a correct target (Section 6.1). This training data construction teaches the model to revise incorrect answers into correct ones, but provides no signal for what to do when the current answer is already correct. At test time, when the revision chain produces a correct answer, the model has not been trained to recognize and preserve it — it may "revise" the correct answer into an incorrect one in the next step.

**The consequence.** The paper reports that approximately **38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones** during sequential revision (Section 6.1). This is a direct structural consequence of the training data design — the revision model has only ever seen `(incorrect, incorrect, ..., incorrect) → correct` trajectories, so when it encounters a correct answer in context, it has no learned behavior other than to change it. The paper mitigates this with majority voting or verifier-based selection *across* the entire revision chain (picking the best answer from any point in the chain rather than always taking the last revision), but this is a post-hoc patch: the model is still wasting approximately 38% of its subsequent revision steps undoing good answers, which reduces the effective compute budget and introduces noise into the chain. In the worst case, this could cause the revision chain to oscillate between correct and incorrect answers, making the final output dependent on where the chain is truncated rather than on convergence to a stable correct solution.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% figure is stated explicitly in Section 6.1. The paper's mitigation — within-chain selection via majority voting or verifier — is what makes sequential revisions work despite this reversion rate, but the paper does not measure how much better sequential revisions would perform if the reversion problem were eliminated (e.g., by training the model to also recognize and preserve correct answers, or by adding an explicit "stop revising" capability). The ReST$^{EM}$ experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) provides additional evidence that revision training is fragile: attempting to further optimize the revision model with RL-style training caused performance to substantially degrade, suggesting the training procedure is sensitive to data distribution in ways that are not fully understood.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed via within-chain answer selection (majority voting or verifier-based selection across the chain), but this treats the symptom rather than the cause. The paper does not experiment with alternative training data constructions that include correct-to-correct trajectories, nor does it train the model to output a special "no revision needed" token when the current answer is already good. These are left as implicit future work.

---

### Sequential Revision Strategies Are Fundamentally High-Latency

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures test-time compute in "generations" — the number of complete solutions sampled — which serves as a proxy for total FLOPs but ignores **wall-clock latency**. Sequential revisions are inherently serial: each revision depends on the output of the previous one, so a chain of length `k` requires `k` sequential forward passes through the model. In contrast, parallel best-of-N sampling can execute all `N` generations simultaneously with sufficient hardware parallelism.

**The consequence.** The compute-optimal policy for easy problems favors heavily sequential strategies (sequential-to-parallel ratios of `8:1` or fully sequential, as shown in Figure 7). A strategy that allocates 128 generations as `64 sequential × 2 parallel` would take approximately `64×` longer wall-clock time than one that runs 128 parallel samples simultaneously. For latency-sensitive applications — interactive assistants, real-time decision-making, user-facing chatbots — the sequential-heavy strategies favored by the compute-optimal policy on easy problems may be impractical regardless of their accuracy advantages, because users will not wait for 64 serial forward passes. The paper's `4×` efficiency claim is measured in *total FLOPs*, not in *latency*, and the two can diverge dramatically under sequential execution.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not discuss latency, wall-clock time, or throughput at all. The generation budget model treats a sequential generation as costing the same as a parallel generation, which is true for total FLOPs but false for latency. The paper does not report any timing measurements, does not discuss hardware assumptions (e.g., whether sufficient parallelism is available to run parallel samples simultaneously), and does not mention latency as a constraint in any of the problem motivation (Section 2) or limitations (Section 5) discussions.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's compute-optimal framework optimizes a single objective — maximizing accuracy under a FLOPs budget — without any latency constraint. In practice, deployment would require a multi-objective optimization trading off accuracy, total FLOPs, *and* latency, which could substantially change the optimal strategy (e.g., capping sequential chain length to a maximum acceptable latency, even if more sequential steps would improve accuracy). This limitation is not a flaw in the paper's analysis — which is explicitly about FLOPs-matched scaling — but it is a critical practical constraint that a practitioner would need to address before deploying sequential revision strategies in a latency-sensitive setting.
