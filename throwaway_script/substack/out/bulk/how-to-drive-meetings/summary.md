Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (wrote about the tactical skills for driving meetings to grow to Senior Engineer):

**Meetings as a "necessary evil"**

Ryan opens by acknowledging that meetings are widely loathed among engineers for disrupting focus time and forcing costly context switches. Many meetings, he argues, can and should be replaced with async discussions. But meetings have their place: they become more efficient when you need rapid back-and-forth—imagine trying to hash out an engineering design by waiting for every reply. They also serve as a **forcing function**. “People are often less responsive to messages than face-to-face,” so he'll sometimes book meetings explicitly to force progress or compel participation in group activities like brainstorming. One of his favorite tactics is to **“threaten” with a meeting**—booking it while offering to cancel if everything gets figured out async beforehand. Most of the time, the work gets done before the meeting because people don’t want to attend it. Finally, meetings are irreplaceable when sharing sensitive information, since there’s no substitute for in-person non-verbal communication.

**Before the meeting: lock down who and what**

Given meetings’ high cost, preparation is the only way to ensure the time is well spent. “If you don’t, your meeting is doomed before it starts.” Ryan says you must lock down two things. First, **the who**: invite the smallest group necessary to achieve the meeting’s goal—every person attending costs the company. Second, **the what**: make the goal clear and set an agenda to achieve it, often sending this to attendees in advance. He also stresses doing all the necessary pre-work beforehand, like pulling data or outlining options, rather than wasting everyone’s time by doing things on the fly.

**During the meeting: the driver’s extra responsibilities**

Driving a meeting means you’re not just a normal attendee; you have extra responsibilities to steer things smoothly. Ryan lays out a typical flow from start to finish. It begins with a **greeting**—welcoming people and setting expectations on when you’ll start so “people should sense you’re driving the meeting.” That flows into a brief **preamble** explaining the goal and agenda. Then comes what he flags as the most important and stressful part: **moderation**. This means guiding the group toward the goal, managing the floor, and keeping track of time. “If someone is speaking too long or getting off track, kindly guide the conversation back.” Finally, the **conclusion**—summarizing the discussion, agreeing on follow-ups, and assigning ownership. Those follow-ups are the meeting’s “impact.” One realization that relieved stress for him was understanding that driving a meeting doesn’t mean you must have all the answers. Knowing you can ask others or look something up afterward “takes a lot of pressure off.” He notes that moderating is the most stressful part and takes time to get right, but after running hundreds of meetings, this reframe helped significantly.

**After the meeting: polish follow-ups and share context**

Afterward, take the rough follow-ups and polish them to share so they don’t get lost. Ryan’s rule: every follow-up must have exactly **one owner**. “If you spread the responsibility among more than one person then it’s easy for people to think someone else will handle it.” If there are people who didn’t attend but should know what happened, summarize the discussion and share it with them. He acknowledges that driving meetings can feel uncomfortable at first, but it’s a baseline expectation for anyone aiming for Senior to have team-level influence and lead projects of multiple engineers. And there’s a payout: “After running tons of meetings, I don’t get nervous anymore.”

---

Ryan’s playbook turns meetings from a productivity tax into a leadership lever. His **“threaten with a meeting”** tactic exploits the same human nature that makes his **forcing function** rationale work—people will self-organize async to avoid the synchronous cost. The through-line is intentionality: locking down the **who** and **what** beforehand, moderating toward the goal during, and assigning exactly **one owner** per follow-up after. It’s a system designed not for comfort, but for reliability, which is exactly why he frames it as a baseline expectation for Senior—not taught anywhere, but learnable through deliberate repetition.
