Here are the takeaways from this Bjarne Stroustrup interview (creator of C++, from a Bell Labs distributed-systems dream to stewarding a language used by millions):

**The origin of C++ (and the PhD that set the stage)**

Stroustrup’s C++ journey began with a very specific, impossible problem. At Bell Labs, he wanted to build a distributed Unix, but no language could do what he needed: low-level hardware access for kernels and device drivers, *plus* high-level abstractions for modules and communication protocols across machines. C was the obvious choice for the low-level stuff—“Dennis Ritchie and Brian Kernighan were down the hall”—but the high-level languages of the time were too slow and couldn’t manipulate hardware. He needed to merge two worlds, and his PhD had already taught him what happens when you don’t.

At Cambridge, he’d built a distributed-systems simulator in Simula (a language he admired for its class concept, and whose inventors, Kristen Nygaard and Ole-Johan Dahl, he knew). It worked beautifully on small tests, but the first full-scale run hogged the department mainframe and got him kicked off. He rewrote the whole simulator in BCPL—a language that “makes C look like a high-level language” with only one data type, the word—on a little-used experimental CAP computer. It ran an estimated 50 times faster and got him his PhD, but writing it was so painful he “lost half his hair debugging.” He vowed never again to tackle a problem with tools that inadequate, and kept a list of what his ideal language needed. C wasn’t perfect, but it came closer than anything else, and merging Simula’s class concept into C became the core idea: “take the class concept from Simula and stick it into C so that it could run much faster and be used for systems programming.” Overloading—treating built-in and user-defined types with a single set of rules—came later and unlocked generic programming.

**What Bell Labs was really like**

Bell Labs wasn’t just legendary in retrospect; “it was the place to go if you wanted to do practical engineering at a large scale, at sort of world class.” Stroustrup estimates the Computer Science Research Center had roughly twice as many computer scientists as MIT. He got his foot in the door when someone from Bell Labs, during a pub chat at Cambridge, said “when you need a job, give us a buzz.” Stroustrup flew to New Jersey on his own dime—no formal job offer. His future boss, Sandy Fraser, told him he’d come at the wrong time; there were no jobs. The next day Stroustrup gave a talk to a development group, they changed their minds, and he got routed to the research group. The “interview” was just talking with people like Dennis Ritchie; Bell Labs hadn’t hired anybody new for five years and simply did it “by the seat of the pants.”

Project-selection philosophy at Bell Labs was radical: **“hire the best people you can find and don’t tell them what to do.”** Stroustrup’s job was described as *do something interesting in a year’s time; tell us what you did on one sheet of paper using more than a nine-point font; if we like it, we’ll give you the same deal next year.* He contrasts this with the alternative model of a well-funded, management-directed project with 20–30 people. “On average, this fairly anarchic organization did better than the well-organized one.” Most of Bell Labs’ famous outputs—Unix, fiber optics, wireless tech, charge-coupled devices—came from that building. The density was humbling: “when I came to Bell Labs and saw the names on the doors and I’d read the papers, they had created the fields I like to work in… I thought, I have to up my game.”

He and Dennis Ritchie had lunch together roughly once a week for 16 years—not as language rivals, but as colleagues. Ritchie never said anything negative about C++; in his paper, he called C++ the obvious successor to C. “All of these C versus C++ language wars are ridiculous. They should never have happened.” Ritchie helped Stroustrup design `const`; he was initially uneasy about overloading because you had to look at function declarations to know what a call meant, but accepted it. Stroustrup notes that the modern function-declaration and call syntax C programmers use today actually came from his early C-with-Classes work.

**When to build a language—and the early technical decisions**

When people ask Stroustrup how to build a programming language, he says they’re asking the wrong question. **“What you need is a problem that needs a solution.”** Too many people want to build a language that is better at what they personally are doing now; that usually results in a special-purpose language, which is fine, but a general-purpose language has to serve many users and domains. For him, the problem was needing both high- and low-level facilities in one language so he wouldn’t have to use two and suffer the performance tax of their interfaces (often requiring garbage collection, unsuitable for device drivers). Once the problem is clear, the engineering cycle is: “do the best you can, then see what works and what doesn’t work and try to fix the problems. Then you repeat.” That’s how C++ grew.

On the implementation side, no single piece was overwhelmingly hard; the challenge was the *many* pieces. He made two early pragmatic decisions that shaped C++ forever. First, **don’t mess with the linker.** Bell Labs alone had roughly 25 different linkers in use, and nobody wants you touching their linker because if you break it, everything breaks. Second, **use C as the assembler.** Every computer had its own optimizer; he couldn’t write a dozen. But all of them had a common interface—C—so he compiled C++ to C. That gave him C compatibility, and he rationalized it this way: “we can have Dennis Ritchie’s mistakes, which we know, and we can have my mistakes, which we don’t know yet, so we’ll take Dennis’s.” Bootstrapping the compiler was a known technique: start with a C preprocessor for the early C++ features, write a simple compiler for a subset in that, then use operator overloading and classes to build a scope class for name lookup, iterating into the next version. “I didn’t invent this… I think I was taught it as an undergrad.”

**C++ is not object-oriented (and the type-system philosophy)**

Stroustrup never called C++ an object-oriented language; in the first edition of *The C++ Programming Language*, the closest he came was noting that some people call these techniques “object-based.” He sees it as type-oriented, class-oriented. He supports Simula-style class hierarchies for groups of related classes, but he emphatically does not want object-oriented complex numbers; he wants `2 + z` to mean addition without dots and arrows. “Math has developed a notation over the last 300 years or so… and it is very good.” He wanted Fortran compatibility for numerics, C compatibility for systems software, and Simula compatibility where class hierarchies made sense; **the overriding instinct was to reuse what already existed**, not to build a single-paradigm green field.

He strengthened C’s type system because weak typing was a major source of errors and endless debugging. Overloading was essential for generic programming: if you write a `vector<T>`, you need the type system to resolve operations on `T`s at compile time. A Bell Labs study in Chicago found that teams switching from C to C++ compiled roughly twice as often, but consumed roughly the same total compile time because C++ compilers were about twice as slow; “I wanted to move towards using more compile time resolution.” On static vs. dynamic typing: in a telephone switch, you can’t drop into a Smalltalk-style debugger when a runtime error hits; you need guarantees, you need performance, and you need small memory footprints (even today, “99% of all computers are embedded systems,” and battery-constrained devices favor statically typed languages). Raw Python runs about 70× slower than raw C++; it’s viable largely because key libraries are written in C or C++. “The errors found by the type system in a statically typed language are discovered at runtime later” in dynamic languages, and as systems grow, reliability requires much more unit testing to compensate.

**Memory safety and the profile approach**

Stroustrup is visibly tired of the “C++ = memory-unsafe foot gun” narrative: “I haven’t had those problems for years.” A study by Herb Sutter found more than 90% of buffer-overflow and hacking incidents came from people writing C-style code—raw pointers without the number of elements, no fat pointers or spans. C++ has had vectors, spans, and hardened libraries from Apple, Google, and Microsoft for years; it just wasn’t standardized. C++26 will have a hardened standard option, and Stroustrup’s current work on **profiles** aims to give a way of guaranteeing that “you don’t do the stupid things.” “Fundamentally, theoretically, the problem was solved many years ago, and people just do what they’ve always done and get the problems they’ve always had.” He wants compilers to prevent risky practices by default, ideally in C++29, but concedes some committee members are “very devoted to their old code and their old ways.”

**The standards committee: arm-twisting, shuttle diplomacy, and the Vasa ship**

C++ was never a dictatorship—“once you have some users, you gain some responsibility.” Two representatives from IBM, HP, and a third major vendor came into his office at Bell Labs and told him their organizations could not use a language that wasn’t standardized under ISO rules and wasn’t owned by a single corporation they might compete with. “They twist my arm. Ow, ow, ow… I said okay, I will standardize C++ under ANSI rules.” He believes that if he’d refused, C++ would have faded into an academic curiosity. Sun spent far more marketing Java (which was explicitly billed as a C++ killer) than the $5,000 over three years that constituted C++’s near-zero marketing budget; today there are 10–12× more C++ developers than when those “kill C++” ads ran.

The committee works by consensus, not a simple numeric vote. The convener determines what consensus means, and a 95%–5% vote where all the major compiler implementers are in the 5% is not consensus. Stroustrup wants to see 90% approval; 80% makes him worry. One of the most vivid committee stories is his **“shuttle diplomacy”** between IBM (PowerPC) and Intel (x86), who were deadlocked over cache-coordination models, each with massive, irreplaceable legacy code. They’d been arguing for days across the room. Stroustrup spent a couple of hours walking back and forth, listening to each side’s constraints, translating, and asking questions, until they reached an agreement that made it into the standard. A couple of years later, both sides were using a cross-pollinated combination.

He still tells the committee the cautionary tale of the **Vasa**, the 17th-century Swedish battleship that sank in Stockholm harbor because the king kept ordering more features (a second gun deck, more ornate statues) without strengthening the foundation, and because they stopped the stability test after seven of the required fourteen runs when it started to look too dangerous. “Always improve the foundation… do not compromise your testing… sometimes the professional thing is to say, no, we are not doing this.” The ship is now a museum piece and well worth seeing.

**Garbage collection, Turing-complete templates, and zero-overhead abstraction**

In 1995 the committee pushed for standard support for garbage collection, and Stroustrup listened. They found an interface and put it in. Over the next decade, usage declined as RAII (which he’d had from the beginning, though he regrets the name almost as much as anything) became better understood and people who still used GC found better, non-standard interfaces. When Erwin Unruh demonstrated that C++ template instantiation was Turing-complete—calculating prime numbers at compile time via error messages—and thought Stroustrup should *ban* it, Stroustrup’s reaction was **“This looks useful. Great.”** A real Turing machine needs an infinite tape; real compilers run out of resources before that becomes a problem. But people *misused* templates for factorial calculations, producing bloated, expensive compiles, which led him and Gabriel Dos Reis to build `constexpr`—perfectly ordinary code evaluated at compile time, much simpler and faster.

On the persistent belief that C is “closer to the machine” and therefore faster than C++, he’s blunt: “No, it’s not the case.” Both share the same machine model—C borrowed the C11 model. C++ compilers have *more* information at compile time and can therefore generate code that runs faster than C. The right framework is **negative overhead abstraction**: “It’s compiled away.” Abstraction gives you zero overhead, but often the compiler can use the extra information to do better than a lower-level equivalent. His talk title to a group of performance-oriented finance engineers was *“Don’t Be (Too) Clever”*—he got out alive. Clever 1990s-style manual optimizations (rat’s nests of pointers, many allocations) tend to pessimize code on modern hardware and compilers. His approach: “throw away the clever stuff and then see if you run faster or slower. Usually, you run faster.” He and a colleague did this on a fluid-dynamics performance suite and got a 20% improvement while shrinking the codebase 20%. His rule echoes Knuth: “First, build the stuff using high-level facilities, see if it’s good enough,” and only then time-and-optimize the critical 2–3%.

**AI writing code (and why he’s skeptical for his domain)**

Stroustrup is not anti-AI, but he is deeply skeptical about LLMs for safety-critical, performance-critical code. In the domains he cares about—CERN, JPL Mars rovers, telephone switches—AI-generated code he’s seen has more bugs, more security holes, and is bloated. The senior developers needed to validate it are starting to retire because they don’t want to spend their time re-validating reams of generated code that changes non-locally every time the prompt or model shifts. Regulatory bodies demand traceable, localized changes; AI makes that difficult. LLMs are “imitating old code and getting old performance and old bugs again”; his job is to help people write *new* things with improved techniques. He also flags the junior-developer pipeline problem: if organizations eliminate junior roles, where do seniors come from? And he notes the escalating cost—$100 million data centers and enormous electricity bills—asking, “how many junior developers does it take to be cheaper?” His assessment: “I’m in a subfield where it is probably more problematic than most.” He cites Dijkstra’s less-polite view that natural-language programming is idiotic because natural languages are ambiguous; “we need a programming language that’s precise, that’s engineering, that’s math; it’s not English.”

**What keeps him going, and the advice he’d give**

His motivation boils down to two things: the fun of building the future, and an obligation to the community. He knew from the start that C++ would have to evolve over decades because he couldn’t build the perfect language in one go—limited resources, limited input, and the engineering reality of “build what you can, see what works, improve it.” He deliberately designed it *not* to be a single-paradigm language because he could already see problems that didn’t fit pure object orientation. The other half is the joy of seeing applications: visiting the JPL Mars rover team or CERN, where a programmer’s job was software for stopping a multi-ton lead radiation door (“you have engines, but then you have to make sure you stop it”). That’s fun. “I still travel around and talk to people and see what C++ is being used for, what it can be used for, and what it can’t be used for. Just learning is fun.”

The famous “shoot yourself in the foot” quote was an offhand remark at a Boston talk in the 80s. Nobel laureate Arno Penzias later gave him the refined framing: **“You can’t have a power tool without knowing how to use it.”** A power saw bounces and hurts you if you try to use it like a hand saw; a faster car can wrap you around a tree in a way a slower car can’t. It’s fundamental to power tools, and C++ is one. On learning multiple languages, he says it’s not about which ones specifically, but about absorbing a *set of ideas*; learn a scripting language like Python, a functional language like Haskell, and “don’t be a monoglot.”

Three regrets from the time-machine question. First, he should have fought harder against implicit narrowing conversions between built-in types; he tried at Bell Labs and was stopped by more senior people (including Ritchie’s Unix team, who found explicit casts too clunky for early resource-starved, no-runtime-checking hardware). Second, he should have delayed the release of C++ until he could include something like templates to deliver a proper standard library; “everybody was building standard libraries, and we got saved by Alex Stepanov with the STL—that was real luck.” Third, with what he now knows about standards committees and their ballooned bureaucracy (more subgroups today than total members at the start), he would have tried to set up a small steering group of five or six people with vast experience making decisions based on input from the wider community, rather than a 500-person vote. He adds a footnote to that exercise: “don’t give advice that couldn’t possibly be followed.” Languages designed for future computers and future programmers usually die, because the world changes while they wait.

---

His **negative overhead abstraction** insight—that giving the compiler *more* information lets it produce *better* code than a lower-level equivalent—runs as a thread from his earliest compiler decisions through his modern “Don’t Be (Too) Clever” optimization talks. It’s the engineering flip side of his **power-tool** philosophy: the same tool that can outperform hand-crafted assembly if wielded with modern discipline will also blow your leg off if you insist on 1990s C-style pointer tricks. His “identify the problem first” language-design rule and his Vasa story are the same lesson at different scales—both warn against piling features onto a foundation you haven’t stress-tested. Even his arm-twisting into standardization reflects his core instinct to **reuse and serve existing communities** rather than chase green-field purity, an instinct that kept C++ from becoming “an academic cube language” and instead turned it into a tool that outlived every “C++ killer” with 10–12× more developers.
