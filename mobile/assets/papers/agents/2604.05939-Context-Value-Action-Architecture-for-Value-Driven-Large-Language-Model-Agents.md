# Context-Value-Action Architecture for Value-Driven Large Language Model Agents

**ArXiv:** [2604.05939](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.05939)

## 🎯 Pitch

Telling LLM agents to think harder about their personality backfires spectacularly—it makes them more stereotypical, not more human. This paper introduces a value-verification architecture that fixes the problem by grounding decisions in dynamic human values rather than chain-of-thought, restoring population-level behavioral diversity to match real-world data within ~1%.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes the **Context-Value-Action (CVA) Architecture**, a novel framework for building LLM agents that decouples action generation from cognitive reasoning to improve behavioral fidelity by explicitly modeling dynamic value activation grounded in Schwartz's Theory of Basic Human Values. Evaluated on CVABench—a custom benchmark comprising over 1.1 million real-world interaction traces from 15,571 users across social media, conversation, and mobility domains—the CVA architecture introduces a Value Verifier (a trained discriminator scoring candidate actions against value profiles) and a Value-Action Mapping Calibration pipeline (SFT followed by DPO). The system reduces population-level value polarization to a variance deviation of only +1.06% from ground truth, while prompt-driven reasoning baselines exhibit worsening polarization with increased inference intensity—collapsing variance by up to 40.74%—establishing that explicit psychological prompting in standard agents exacerbates behavioral rigidity rather than capturing authentic human diversity.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLM Agents Don't Behave Like Real People

The fundamental question this paper tackles is straightforward but surprising in its answer: **when we use LLMs to simulate human behavior, why do they produce caricatures rather than authentic representations?**

The context is that LLM-based agents are increasingly deployed as human simulacra across a wide range of applications—game NPCs (Gallotta et al., 2024), social simulation platforms (Park et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025), embodied robotic systems (Driess et al., 2023), and task-oriented assistants (Anthis et al., 2025). In all these settings, a basic requirement is that the agent faithfully captures what the paper calls the "inherent complexity, diversity, and stochasticity of human behavior" (Fuchs et al., 2023). An NPC that always responds the same way, or a social simulacrum that collapses every personality into a single stereotype, fails at its core purpose.

Yet the paper identifies a persistent gap: **existing LLM-based agents exhibit behavioral rigidity and stereotyping** (Li et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025). Rather than producing the full spectrum of human responses—subtle, contradictory, context-dependent—they gravitate toward simplified, extreme versions of whatever persona they're assigned. The paper's key observation is that this problem is not just about accuracy at the individual level. When you simulate an entire *population* of agents, these individual biases compound into a systemic failure: the simulated population loses the diversity and distributional nuance of real human communities, converging instead on a few rigid behavioral modes.

Why does this matter beyond academic curiosity? The paper implies (though doesn't exhaustively enumerate) several practical stakes:

- **Invalid social science:** If researchers use LLM agents as proxies for human participants in social simulations (a growing trend; Anthis et al., 2025), behavioral rigidity means the simulations produce artifacts rather than insights. A simulation where every "irritable" agent responds identically won't reveal how real populations with diverse personality profiles might behave.
- **Broken user experiences:** In gaming, virtual assistants, or interactive storytelling, characters that lack nuance feel artificial and break immersion. The difference between a believable NPC and a caricature is precisely the difference between rich, context-sensitive behavior and rigid stereotype-driven responses.
- **Amplified bias at scale:** When these agents interact with real users, their stereotyping tendencies can reinforce harmful simplifications about how certain "types" of people behave, creating feedback loops that narrow rather than expand representations of human diversity.

### The Evaluation Problem: We've Been Grading Our Own Homework

A substantial portion of the paper's motivation is methodological. The authors argue that the severity of behavioral rigidity has been **masked by a problematic evaluation paradigm**.

The dominant evaluation approach in human-like agent research is what the paper calls **"LLM-as-a-judge"** (Wang et al., 2025, 2023). You prompt an LLM (often the same model family as the agent itself) to rate how well the agent's behavior matches its assigned persona. The problem—and the paper articulates this clearly—is that this creates a **self-referential validation loop**. The judge model shares the same pretraining biases as the agent model. When the agent produces a stereotypically aggressive response because it's playing an "irritable" character, the judge model—trained on the same internet text that associates irritability with aggression—rates this as a *high-quality* portrayal. It endorses the caricature rather than penalizing it for lacking realistic subtlety.

This is a subtle but damning critique. It means the field's evaluation infrastructure is structurally incapable of detecting the very problem the paper identifies. The metrics are biased toward confirming that existing approaches work, because both the generator and the evaluator are drawn from the same biased distribution. The paper's introduction puts this pointedly:

> "the 'judge' model shares similar pre-training biases with the agent, [so] it is prone to endorsing polarized or stereotypical behaviors (e.g., rating an overly aggressive response as a high-quality depiction of an 'irritable' persona) rather than penalizing them for lacking realistic subtlety."

The solution the paper proposes—and builds its entire evaluation around—is to **anchor evaluation in empirical ground truth**. CVABench is constructed from over 1.1 million authentic human interaction traces from 15,571 real users. Rather than asking an LLM judge "does this look like a good simulation?", the evaluation compares simulated behavior distributions directly to observed human behavior distributions using quantitative metrics (accuracy, MSE for temporal predictions, distributional variance for value profiles). This is a significant methodological shift: ground truth replaces consensus bias.

### Prior Approaches and Why They Fall Short

The paper organizes existing work into three paradigms and diagnoses specific failure modes for each.

**Paradigm 1: Prompt-Driven Role-Play Agents.** These agents use In-Context Learning (ICL) to map context directly to action: $A \sim P_{LLM}(A|C)$. The LLM is given a persona description and historical behavior examples, then asked to produce contextually appropriate responses. This is the most common approach (Chang et al., 2024; Sahoo et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025).

The paper's diagnosis: these agents produce **inconsistent, surface-level imitation**. They can mimic surface patterns (vocabulary, sentiment) but don't capture the underlying psychological mechanisms that generate those patterns. Why? Because they have no explicit model of *why* a person with certain traits acts differently in different situations. They learn correlations between contexts and actions from their pretraining data, which already contains the stereotyping biases the paper identifies. The result is behavior that looks approximately right at a glance but lacks the context-sensitivity and internal consistency of real human decision-making.

**Paradigm 2: Prompt-Driven Reasoning Agents.** These agents attempt to go deeper by simulating the cognitive process through which values guide actions (Park et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025, 2023; Colas et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2025; Piao et al., 2025). They employ "Chain-of-Thought" style prompting to make the agent explicitly reason about its values before acting, ostensibly modeling $P(A|C, V)$—the conditional probability of an action given both context and internal values.

The paper's diagnosis is the core empirical finding of Section 3.1 and the most counterintuitive result in the paper: **increasing the intensity of this reasoning does not improve fidelity—it makes things worse.** The authors call this **"cognitive distortion."** The mechanism they propose is that the LLM's inherent biases cause it to simplify the authentic value profile $V$ into a caricatured archetype $V'$. When forced to reason explicitly about "what would someone with high Self-Direction and moderate Hedonism do?", the model doesn't produce a nuanced answer. It latches onto the most stereotypical associations from its training data—Self-Direction means gym-going, Hedonism means seeking pleasure—and produces actions from a distorted distribution $P(A|C, V')$ that exaggerates these traits. The more reasoning steps you add (the paper tests 0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 rounds), the more opportunities the model has to drift toward these extremes.

The quantitative evidence (Table 5, Appendix C) is striking: as reasoning strength increases from 0 to 8, the standard deviation of simulated value distributions *monotonically decreases* (indicating collapse toward a single mode) while the absolute mean *monotonically drifts away from ground truth* (indicating polarization toward extreme values). The average variance deviation goes from +107.06% (already too much variance with zero reasoning) to +50.84% (far too little variance with 8 reasoning rounds), while the absolute mean difference increases from 0.0071 to 0.0914. This is not a problem of insufficient reasoning; it's a problem of **reasoning itself amplifying biases**.

The lead-in case study (Appendix A, Figure 7) crystallizes this. An agent roleplaying an IT professional with high Self-Direction (0.9) and varying Hedonism (0.2 to 0.6) is asked to choose an evening activity after an exhausting workday. Real humans in this situation often choose rest over exercise, regardless of their trait levels—context overrides disposition. But the LLM agent chooses "go to the gym" with near-absolute probability for all Hedonism levels below 0.5, and with 93% probability even at Hedonism = 0.6. It over-indexes on the dominant value (Self-Direction) and under-weights contextual factors (exhaustion, the hedonic pull toward rest). This is a miniature version of the systematic failure the paper documents at scale.

**Paradigm 3: Training-Required Agents.** These approaches use supervised fine-tuning or reinforcement learning to directly parameterize a behavioral policy $P_{trained}(A|C)$ from human data (Shao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2025b). The motivation is that if prompt-driven methods are insufficient, perhaps the model needs to be explicitly trained on behavioral examples.

The paper identifies two problems with existing training-based approaches. First, they typically don't model values explicitly—they learn a direct mapping from context to action, which means they may still learn spurious correlations rather than the underlying value structure that generates consistent behavior across different contexts. Second, training on behavioral data without explicit value conditioning can itself amplify biases present in the training distribution. The baseline SFT+DPO agent in the paper's experiments (Table 3, second-to-last row) actually *increases* value variance deviation to +27.98% compared to ground truth, meaning it produces populations that are *less* diverse than real humans despite being trained on human data. Training alone, without the architectural innovations the paper introduces, is insufficient.

### The Psychological Foundation: Why Values Matter

The paper's proposed solution doesn't come from ML architecture innovation for its own sake. It is grounded in two established psychological frameworks that provide a principled decomposition of human decision-making.

**The Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) Model** (Mehrabian and Russell, 1974). This classic psychological framework posits that environmental stimuli (S) don't directly cause behavioral responses (R). Instead, they activate internal organismic states (O)—cognitive and affective processes—which then produce behavior. The paper adapts this directly: Context (C) is the stimulus, Values (V) are the organismic state, and Action (A) is the response. The goal becomes modeling $P(A|C, V)$ rather than the simpler $P(A|C)$.

This is more than a relabeling. It means the agent needs an explicit, dynamic internal state that mediates between situation and action—a state that can be inspected, verified, and steered. A prompt-driven agent that maps "exhausted IT professional after work" directly to "probably goes to gym" has no such mediating state. A CVA agent reasons: the context activates certain values (exhaustion activates Hedonism for rest, Self-Direction might activate for exercise), and the action is chosen based on which values are most strongly activated *in this specific context*.

**Schwartz's Theory of Basic Human Values** (Schwartz, 1992a, 1994). This theory posits that human values fall into 10 universal dimensions organized in a circular structure (the "circumplex"): Self-Direction, Stimulation, Hedonism, Achievement, Power, Security, Conformity, Tradition, Benevolence, and Universalism. Adjacent values in the circle are compatible (e.g., Self-Direction and Stimulation), while opposing values are in tension (e.g., Self-Direction and Conformity). Importantly, values are not static personality traits—they are **dynamically activated by context**. The same person may prioritize Hedonism when exhausted and Achievement when facing a work deadline.

The paper uses this framework to operationalize the "Organism" state $V$ not as a fixed personality vector but as a **dynamic activation vector** across the 10 Schwartz dimensions. At any given moment, the agent has a value profile, but *which subset of these values drives behavior* depends on the context. The paper's key innovation—the Value Verifier—is explicitly designed to model this context-dependent activation rather than treating values as static traits.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution as a **fundamental architectural shift** from "generate-evaluate internally" (where the same LLM both produces and judges its own behavior, creating the self-referential loop) to "generate-then-verify externally" (where a separately trained verifier assesses alignment against a value model learned from real human data).

The diagnostic contribution is as important as the proposed solution. By building CVABench with ground-truth human distributions and systematically testing prompt-driven reasoning agents at varying intensities, the paper provides evidence for a specific failure mode—**value polarization under explicit reasoning**—that the field had not previously characterized. This changes the conversation from "how do we get LLMs to reason better about their assigned personas?" to "is prompting for explicit persona reasoning actually harmful, and do we need fundamentally different architectures that decouple value alignment from generation?"

The paper also positions itself within the training-based agent camp but with a critical distinction. It doesn't just train an agent to imitate human behavior (Paradigm 3). It trains a **verifier** to recognize value-aligned behavior, keeping the generator and evaluator as separate components with different training objectives. This is the architectural instantiation of the paper's core insight: to avoid self-referential bias, the mechanism that judges behavioral quality must be independent of the mechanism that produces behavior. The generator is trained via SFT+DPO to produce diverse, realistic candidates. The verifier is trained via pairwise ranking on ground-truth preferences to select among them based on value alignment. Neither operates in a self-referential loop with itself.

The connection to the "LLM-as-a-judge" critique is direct and deliberate. Standard self-verification approaches (where the LLM generates candidates and then evaluates them itself, as in the prompt-driven reasoning baselines) are a microcosm of the macro-level evaluation problem: the same biases that produce caricatured behavior also produce evaluations that endorse those caricatures. The CVA architecture breaks this loop by training the verifier on **empirical preferences** (which candidate action is closer to what a real human with this value profile actually did) rather than on the model's own judgments of alignment.

Finally, while the paper's experiments are on behavioral simulation, the architectural principle—decouple generation from evaluation, ground evaluation in empirical preferences rather than model introspection—has broader implications for any LLM system where self-consistency or self-critique is currently relied upon. The paper doesn't make this claim explicitly, but the logic extends naturally to areas like self-improving code generation, automated red-teaming, or any loop where an LLM evaluates its own outputs.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper builds a system for making LLM agents behave like real humans rather than caricatures, by separating the job of *generating* possible actions from the job of *selecting* the right action, and training the selection mechanism on authentic human behavioral data. The core idea is that when a single LLM both produces behavior and reasons about whether that behavior matches an assigned persona, its pre-existing biases cause it to drift toward stereotypical extremes—what the paper calls "value polarization"—and the solution is to introduce an independently trained Value Verifier that explicitly models how context activates specific human values, breaking the self-referential loop.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The CVA system has three major components, each trained separately with distinct objectives and data:

1. **Generator (Calibrated Base Model)**: A fine-tuned LLM that takes context `$C$` and a value profile `$V$` as input and produces `$N$` diverse candidate actions at inference time. It is trained via a two-stage pipeline (SFT followed by DPO) on CVABench trajectory data to approximate the true conditional distribution `$P(A \mid C, V)$` while suppressing the base model's intrinsic stereotyping biases.

2. **Value Verifier**: A separate discriminator model—architecturally distinct from the generator—that scores how well a candidate action `$a_i$` aligns with the target value profile `$V$` given the context `$C$`. It employs a multi-encoder design with a cross-attention mechanism that explicitly models context-dependent value activation. It is trained on pairwise preference data derived from ground-truth human behavior, not on the model's own judgments.

3. **Inference Protocol (Value-Driven Reasoning)**: At deployment, the generator samples `$N$` candidate actions conditioned on context and values; the Value Verifier scores each candidate for value alignment; and the highest-scoring candidate is selected as the final output. This "Generate-then-Select" protocol decouples production from evaluation.

Information flows: context `$C$` and value profile `$V$` enter the system → the generator produces candidate set `$\mathcal{A}$` → the Value Verifier processes each candidate through its text encoder, value encoder, and cross-attention mechanism to produce alignment scores `$s_i$` → the candidate with maximum `$s_i$` is output. The generator never sees its own evaluations, and the verifier never generates actions.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal problem statement: how the paper frames behavioral simulation as modeling `$P(A \mid C, V)$` using the S-O-R psychological model, and why existing paradigms fail at this specific distribution.
- **Second**, the Generator training pipeline (VMC): how SFT and DPO are applied to shift the base model's output distribution away from stereotypes and toward authentic value-action mappings, including the data construction and loss functions.
- **Third**, the Value Verifier architecture and training: its dual-encoder design, the cross-attention mechanism for context-dependent value activation, the pairwise ranking objective, and why this separate model breaks the self-referential loop.
- **Fourth**, the inference-time Value-Driven Reasoning protocol: how candidates are generated, scored, and selected, and the empirical finding that behavioral fidelity peaks at intermediate compute budgets rather than improving monotonically.
- **Fifth**, CVABench construction: the datasets, domains, metrics, and psychometric measurement via GPV, since the evaluation infrastructure is essential to understanding what the architecture is designed to optimize.

This is primarily a **systems and empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that behavioral fidelity in LLM agents requires explicitly decoupling value alignment verification from action generation, grounding the verifier in empirical human preferences rather than model introspection.

#### Formal Problem Statement and Psychological Grounding

The paper adapts the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) model (Mehrabian and Russell, 1974) into a probabilistic framework with three variables. The goal is to model the conditional probability `$P(A \mid C, V)$`—the distribution over actions `$A$` given context `$C$` and internally activated values `$V$`.

**Context `$C$`** (the Stimulus) represents the aggregate of environmental and historical stimuli. It comprises two components: **working memory** `$C_w$`, which captures immediate situational factors (current platform, conversation history, geo-temporal context), and **long-term memory** `$C_l$`, which stores the agent's historical preferences and past behaviors. For computational feasibility, long-term memory is constructed by applying a retrieval function `$f(\cdot)$` to the full memory set `$C_m$`: `$C_l = f(C_m)$`, where `$f$` selects the most relevant historical content (the paper uses BM25 retrieval for the baselines; implementation details for CVA are in Appendix B).

**Values `$V$`** (the Organism) is conceptualized using Schwartz's Theory of Basic Human Values (Schwartz, 1992a, 1994) as a dynamic activation vector spanning 10 dimensions: Self-Direction, Stimulation, Hedonism, Achievement, Power, Security, Conformity, Tradition, Benevolence, and Universalism. Crucially, `$V$` is not a static personality profile but a **context-dependent activation**—the same individual may have Hedonism strongly activated when exhausted and Achievement activated when facing a deadline. The paper uses GPV (Ye et al., 2025), a generative psychometric tool, to measure these value profiles from behavioral logs. Each dimension is normalized to `$[-1, 1]$`, where `$1$` represents strong consistency with that value and `$-1$` represents strong opposition. Appendix E (Table 7) provides full conceptual definitions for all ten dimensions.

**Action `$A$`** (the Response) is the final behavioral output—a review text and rating in the social media domain, a comment in the conversation domain, or a next-location and stay-duration prediction in the mobility domain.

The paper's central diagnosis is that existing prompt-driven approaches fail to faithfully model `$P(A \mid C, V)$` because of **cognitive distortion**. When an LLM is prompted to simulate how values guide actions (via Chain-of-Thought or iterative reasoning), its inherent biases cause it to simplify the authentic value profile `$V$` into a caricatured archetype `$V'$`. Actions are then sampled from a distorted distribution `$P(A \mid C, V')$` that exaggerates dominant value dimensions and suppresses nuance. Section 3.1 provides quantitative evidence: increasing reasoning rounds from 0 to 8 monotonically reduces population variance while shifting means toward extremes. This motivates the architectural solution: rather than asking the LLM to introspect about values internally—which amplifies biases—the CVA architecture uses an external, independently trained verifier to assess value alignment, breaking the self-referential loop.

#### Generator Training: Value-Action Mapping Calibration (VMC)

The VMC pipeline is a two-stage training procedure applied to a base LLM (Qwen2.5-7B in the reported experiments) that shifts its output distribution from `$P_{\text{base}}(A \mid C)$` (biased toward stereotypes) to approximately `$P(A \mid C, V)$` (conditioned on authentic value-behavior mappings). Unlike the baseline training approach (Appendix B.3), the CVA generator is explicitly conditioned on value labels `$V$` alongside context `$C$` during both training stages.

**Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).** The base model is fine-tuned on CVABench trajectories using the role-playing system prompts as input context `$x = (C, V)$` and the ground-truth human behavior as the target response `$y_{\text{gt}}$`. This establishes a basic mapping from context-and-values to realistic actions. The training minimizes standard cross-entropy loss between generated and ground-truth tokens:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}} = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P_\theta(y_{\text{gt}, t} \mid y_{\text{gt}, <t}, x)$$

where `$T$` is the sequence length, `$y_{\text{gt}, t}$` is the `$t$`-th ground-truth token, and `$\theta$` are the model parameters.

**What it computes:** the negative log-likelihood of the ground-truth human behavior under the model's predicted token distribution, summed over all tokens in the sequence. Minimizing this encourages the model to assign high probability to tokens that appear in real human responses given the context and value conditioning.

**Why this form:** cross-entropy is the standard maximum-likelihood objective for autoregressive language models. It directly incentivizes the model to reproduce the target distribution. The key design choice is **what the input `$x$` includes**. By injecting value labels `$V$` explicitly into the input (unlike the baseline role-play agent, which receives value information but does not structurally condition on it), the model learns to distinguish between responses that are generically plausible and responses that are *value-aligned*—that is, appropriate for a person with this specific value profile in this specific context.

**Stage 2: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).** The SFT-trained model is further refined using preference pairs `$(y_w, y_l)$` that explicitly favor nuanced value consistency over caricatured responses. The construction process is:

1. Sample `$K = 10$` candidate actions from the SFT-trained model `$\pi_{\text{SFT}}(\cdot \mid x)$` at temperature `$0.8$`.
2. Evaluate each candidate's similarity to the ground-truth behavior using a linguistic metric `$S(y_i, y_{\text{gt}})$`.
3. Select the preferred action `$y_w$` as the candidate with maximum similarity and the rejected action `$y_l$` as the candidate with minimum similarity.

The DPO training objective combines three loss terms:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = 1.0 \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} + 0.2 \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCO}} + 1.2 \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}$$

where `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}$` is the standard sigmoid DPO loss (Azar et al., 2023) that increases the relative probability of `$y_w$` over `$y_l$`, `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCO}}$` is a BCO pair loss (detailed in the code repository), and `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}$` is an SFT regularization term that prevents the model from drifting too far from the ground-truth distribution learned in Stage 1.

**What it computes:** a weighted sum of three objectives. The DPO term directly optimizes the preference ordering—the model should prefer `$y_w$` to `$y_l$`. The BCO term (weight 0.2) provides additional preference signal. The SFT term (weight 1.2) acts as a regularizer, keeping the model anchored to the empirical distribution so that preference optimization doesn't cause it to overfit to the specific preference pairs at the expense of general behavioral realism.

**Why this form:** standard SFT alone maps context to a single ground-truth action, but human behavior is stochastic—the same person in the same situation may do different things on different days. SFT tends to produce a peaked distribution, collapsing behavioral diversity. DPO with carefully constructed preferences (based on similarity to real behavior) pushes the model to prefer nuanced, realistic actions over caricatured ones while the SFT regularization preserves the breadth of the learned distribution. The specific weights (1.0, 0.2, 1.2) are empirically determined through the ablation studies; the SFT term has the highest weight, reflecting the paper's finding that ground-truth anchoring is more important than preference optimization for preventing polarization.

**Memory-efficient implementation.** Both SFT and DPO stages employ QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) for parameter-efficient fine-tuning and Flash Attention (Dao, 2023) for memory-efficient attention computation. Training details and hyperparameters are in the code repository. Both stages are trained for a single epoch.

**Why SFT+DPO rather than RLHF?** The paper's approach avoids the complexity of reward model training and online RL, instead using offline preference pairs constructed from ground-truth similarity. This is computationally simpler and avoids the exploration-instability issues of RL. The key insight is that for behavioral simulation, we have access to ground-truth human actions as targets—this makes offline preference construction from the SFT model's own distribution a natural fit.

#### Value Verifier Architecture

The Value Verifier is the architectural centerpiece of CVA. It is a separately trained discriminator model that scores candidate actions for value alignment, designed explicitly to break the self-referential loop that causes polarization in prompt-driven reasoning agents. Unlike standard self-verification approaches where the same LLM evaluates its own outputs, the Value Verifier is architecturally distinct from the generator and trained on a different objective with different data.

**Multi-Encoder Design.** The verifier employs three encoders that process different input modalities in parallel:

- **Action and Context Encoder (`$E_a$`, `$E_c$`)**: A text encoder (decoder-only language model backbone) processes the candidate action `$A$` and the context `$C$` to produce embeddings `$\mathbf{e}_a$` and `$\mathbf{e}_c$`, respectively. These capture the semantic content of what the agent is doing and what situation it's in.

- **Value Structure Encoder (`$E_v$`)**: A separate encoder maps the value profile `$V$` (the 10-dimensional Schwartz vector) to a value embedding `$\mathbf{e}_v$`. This encoder is designed to capture the **internal structure** of value preferences—the relationships between different value dimensions as specified by the Schwartz circumplex theory. For example, it should represent that Self-Direction and Stimulation are adjacent (compatible) values while Self-Direction and Conformity are opposed.

**Context-Dependent Value Activation via Cross-Attention.** The critical architectural innovation is the cross-attention mechanism that models *which* values are activated by the current context. The context embedding `$\mathbf{e}_c$` serves as the query, attending over the value embedding `$\mathbf{e}_v$` as keys and values:

$$\mathbf{e}'_v = \text{CrossAttn}(Q = \mathbf{e}_c, K = \mathbf{e}_v, V = \mathbf{e}_v)$$

where `$\mathbf{e}_c$` is the query (representing "what is happening right now"), `$\mathbf{e}_v$` serves as both keys and values (representing "what values are potentially relevant"), and `$\mathbf{e}'_v$` is the context-refined value representation.

**What it computes:** a weighted combination of value dimensions where the weights are determined by how relevant each value dimension is to the current context. If the context is "exhausted after a long workday," the cross-attention mechanism should assign higher weight to Hedonism (seeking rest/comfort) and lower weight to Achievement (striving for success). The resulting vector `$\mathbf{e}'_v$` represents not the person's abstract value profile but their **contextually activated values**—the subset of their values that should actually drive behavior in this situation.

**Why this form:** a simple alternative—concatenating context and value embeddings and feeding them through a feedforward network—would learn fixed relationships between contexts and values, unable to capture that *the same value profile should produce different activations in different contexts*. Cross-attention makes the value weighting explicitly context-dependent. The context embedding acts as a dynamic query that "asks" which values are relevant right now, and the attention weights provide interpretable evidence of which values the model is using (exploited in the interpretability analysis of Section 3.4).

**Final Scoring.** The context-refined value embedding `$\mathbf{e}'_v$` is concatenated with the action embedding `$\mathbf{e}_a$`, and the combined representation is fed through a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to produce a single scalar consistency score:

$$s(A, C, V) = \text{MLP}([\mathbf{e}'_v; \mathbf{e}_a])$$

where `$[\cdot; \cdot]$` denotes concatenation and `$s \in \mathbb{R}$` represents the predicted alignment fidelity between the action and the contextually activated values.

**What it computes:** a single number representing how well the candidate action aligns with the values that *should be active* given the current context. A high score means "this action is what someone with these activated values would do"; a low score means "this action conflicts with the activated values."

**Why this form:** the concatenation `$[\mathbf{e}'_v; \mathbf{e}_a]$` gives the MLP access to both the value representation (what should matter) and the action representation (what was actually generated). The MLP can learn complex nonlinear interactions—for example, that a "go to gym" action aligns well with high Self-Direction activation only when the context doesn't simultaneously activate Hedonism for rest.

#### Value Verifier Training

The verifier is trained on **pairwise preference data** derived from ground-truth human behavior, ensuring it learns to recognize value alignment from empirical evidence rather than from its own judgments.

**Training Data Construction.** For each training example `$(C, V)$` with ground-truth action `$A_{\text{gt}}$`:

1. Sample `$K = 5$` candidate actions from the DPO-trained generator model at temperature `$0.8$`. This temperature balances diversity (exploring a range of possible actions) with quality (not producing random noise).
2. Rank all candidates by linguistic similarity to the ground-truth action using a domain-specific similarity metric `$S(A_i, A_{\text{gt}})$` (described in Appendix D).
3. Select the preferred action `$A_w$` as the one with maximum similarity and the rejected action `$A_l$` as the one with minimum similarity.

The training dataset `$\mathcal{D}$` consists of tuples `$(C, V, A_w, A_l)$`.

**Pairwise Ranking Objective.** The verifier is trained to assign strictly higher consistency scores to preferred actions than to rejected actions by minimizing the pairwise ranking loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{ver}} = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma \left( s(A_w, C, V) - s(A_l, C, V) \right) \right]$$

where `$\sigma(\cdot)$` is the sigmoid function and `$s(\cdot)$` is the verifier's scoring function defined above.

**What it computes:** for each training pair, the loss penalizes the model when it assigns a higher (or equal) score to the rejected action than to the preferred action. The sigmoid `$\sigma(z)$` maps the score difference `$z = s(A_w) - s(A_l)$` to `$(0, 1)$`, and the negative log encourages this difference to be large and positive. When `$s(A_w) \gg s(A_l)$`, `$\sigma(z) \approx 1$` and the loss is near zero; when `$s(A_w) \leq s(A_l)$`, `$\sigma(z) \leq 0.5$` and the loss is large.

**Why this form:** this is the standard Bradley-Terry preference model objective, which assumes that the probability of preferring `$A_w$` over `$A_l$` is proportional to the exponential of their score difference. It's the same objective used to train reward models in RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022). The key design choice is **what defines "preferred."** The paper defines it by similarity to ground-truth human behavior, not by the model's own judgments. This means the verifier learns: "actions that look like what real humans actually did in this situation are better aligned with the specified values" rather than "actions that the model thinks are value-aligned are good." This is the mechanism by which the verifier escapes the self-referential bias loop: its training signal comes from **empirical behavioral similarity**, not from introspective value reasoning.

**Why not train the verifier to directly predict the ground-truth action?** A regression approach (predicting the exact ground-truth action) would be inappropriate because human behavior is stochastic—multiple actions can be equally value-aligned. The pairwise ranking objective only requires that actions more similar to ground truth score higher than actions less similar, which is a weaker and more appropriate signal for the verifier's role as a discriminator rather than a generator.

**Why 5 candidates?** The paper does not provide an explicit ablation over `$K$` for verifier training, but the choice mirrors the limited cognitive capacity framing: humans can typically evaluate only a handful of options at once, and the verifier's role is to simulate this bounded evaluation process. The number 5 appears to be an empirical choice balancing training signal diversity (more candidates = more preference pairs) with computational cost.

---

#### Value-Driven Reasoning (VDR) at Inference

At inference time, the CVA agent follows a "Generate-then-Select" protocol that cleanly separates production from evaluation:

**Step 1: Candidate Generation.** The calibrated generator model (post-SFT+DPO) is conditioned on the context `$C$` and the target value profile `$V$`. It samples `$N$` candidate actions at temperature `$0.8$` (the same temperature used during training, maintaining consistency):

$$\mathcal{A} = \{a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_N\} \sim P_{\text{generator}}(A \mid C, V)$$

The paper's primary experiments use a single-generation setting (implicitly `$N = 1$` for the main results in Table 3, with the verifier selecting from a larger candidate pool in the reasoning-intensity sweep of Figure 4). The generator's role is to produce diverse, contextually plausible candidates—not to self-evaluate or filter.

**Step 2: Value Alignment Scoring.** The Value Verifier evaluates each candidate `$a_i$` against the context `$C$` and value profile `$V$`, producing a consistency score:

$$s_i = f_{\text{ver}}(a_i, C, V)$$

where `$f_{\text{ver}}$` is the trained verifier function.

**Step 3: Optimal Selection.** The candidate maximizing the consistency score is selected as the final output:

$$a^* = \arg\max_{a_i \in \mathcal{A}} s_i$$

This protocol ensures that the final action is the one the verifier—trained on ground-truth human preferences—judges as most value-aligned, rather than the one the generator happens to produce with highest probability or the one the generator itself would rate highest.

**Why separate generation from selection?** If the generator also evaluated its own outputs (as in prompt-driven reasoning agents), the same biases that distorted the generation would also distort the evaluation, compounding errors. By training the verifier on a different objective (pairwise ranking against ground truth) with different data (preference pairs from human behavior), the CVA architecture ensures that the selection mechanism does not inherit the generator's stereotyping tendencies. The generator can produce diverse candidates—some realistic, some caricatured—and the verifier filters out the caricatured ones based on empirical alignment evidence.

---

#### Inference Intensity and the U-Shaped Optimality Curve

The paper investigates how the number of reasoning rounds (verifier evaluations) affects behavioral fidelity. This is the "inference intensity" parameter that the Value-Driven Reasoning module sweeps.

For a given context, the protocol can be repeated with different candidate sets or iterative refinement. The paper tests intensity levels of 1, 3, 5, and 8 reasoning rounds (Figure 4). At each round, the generator produces new candidates and the verifier selects among them.

The key empirical finding is that **performance does not improve monotonically with inference intensity**. Sentiment accuracy (in the media domain) initially increases with more reasoning rounds—going from approximately 0.35 at intensity 1 to a peak around 0.50-0.53 at intensity 4-5—but then **saturates or slightly declines** beyond 4 rounds. The paper explicitly notes:

> "performance gains saturate when the number of inference rounds exceeds four; beyond this threshold, additional reasoning steps yield diminishing returns in behavioral modeling accuracy"

This is interpreted as an analog of bounded rationality in human cognition: humans don't exhaustively evaluate all possible actions against all values; they satisfice with a limited evaluation scope. The verifier's performance exhibits the same pattern—beyond a moderate number of evaluations, additional computation doesn't help and may even hurt (possibly due to the verifier itself having calibration imperfections that compound when forced to make increasingly fine-grained distinctions between good candidates).

**Why this matters for the architecture:** it validates the "Generate-then-Select" design over a "Generate-then-Refine" or iterative self-improvement design. If more evaluation rounds always helped, you'd want an architecture that iteratively refines actions based on verifier feedback. The saturation suggests instead that a moderate fixed evaluation budget is both computationally efficient and behaviorally optimal—matching the paper's framing of the verifier as simulating bounded human evaluation rather than exhaustive optimization.

---

#### CVABench: Construction and Evaluation Infrastructure

CVABench is both the training data source and the evaluation framework for the CVA architecture. It aggregates over 1.1 million authentic interaction traces from 15,571 unique users across three behavioral domains.

**Domain 1: Social Media Reviews (Yelp).** 4,924 users, 54K entries. Each entry includes a user's review text, numerical rating (1-5), and the business being reviewed. The task is to predict both the review content and rating given the user's history and the target business. Metrics: Rating Accuracy (discrete 1-5 prediction), Sentiment Accuracy (binary positive/negative), and linguistic fidelity measured via Type-Token Ratio (TTR) Wasserstein distance between generated and ground-truth corpora.

**Domain 2: Conversational Discourse (Reddit).** 5,640 users, 155K entries. Each entry is a Reddit comment with its conversational context (parent comments, subreddit). The task is to generate a comment that matches the user's historical style and stance. Metrics: Attitude Accuracy (predicting the user's stance/subreddit-specific behavior) and linguistic fidelity via TTR Wasserstein distance.

**Domain 3: Spatio-Temporal Mobility (Foursquare).** 5,007 users, 871K entries. Each entry records a user's check-in at a Point-of-Interest (POI) with timestamp. The task is to predict the next POI category and stay duration given the day's movement history. Metrics: Category Accuracy (correct POI category) and Mean Squared Error (MSE) for stay duration in minutes.

**Data split.** To prevent data leakage, 10% of users are held out from all training (SFT, DPO, verifier training) and used exclusively as the final evaluation simulation environment. Training methods have absolutely no access to these users' data.

**Psychometric Measurement via GPV.** Rather than using self-reported questionnaires (prone to response bias) or LLM-based inference (prone to the self-referential bias the paper criticizes), the paper uses GPV (Ye et al., 2025), a Generative Psychometrics for Values tool. GPV infers value profiles directly from behavioral logs—given a user's generated or historical actions, it produces a 10-dimensional Schwartz value profile normalized to `$[-1, 1]$`. The paper reports that GPV has been "extensively tested with real human subjects and is considered to accurately and reliably report the value measurements of natural persons," achieving "superior stability and construct validity compared to human self-reports and other LLM-based measurement tools" (Section 6).

**Why GPV over questionnaires?** Self-report questionnaires introduce reactivity bias: people answer based on how they want to be perceived rather than how they actually behave. LLM-based inference (asking GPT-4 to estimate someone's values from their text) introduces the exact self-referential bias the paper identifies—the evaluator shares the same pretraining biases as the agent. GPV, by inferring values from behavioral patterns, provides a measurement that is both objective (derived from actions, not self-descriptions) and independent of the agent's own value judgments.

**Evaluation Metrics.** The paper uses domain-specific metrics for individual-level fidelity and a cross-domain metric for group-level value alignment:

- **Individual Level:** Rating Accuracy and Sentiment Accuracy (social media), Attitude Accuracy (conversation), Category Accuracy and MSE (mobility). Linguistic fidelity is measured by the 1-Wasserstein distance between the TTR distributions of generated and ground-truth text:

$$W_1(P_{\text{gen}}, P_{\text{real}}) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Gamma(P_{\text{gen}}, P_{\text{real}})} \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \gamma} [|x - y|]$$

where `$\Gamma(P_{\text{gen}}, P_{\text{real}})$` is the set of all joint distributions with marginals `$P_{\text{gen}}$` and `$P_{\text{real}}$`, and `$x, y$` are TTR values from simulated and real distributions. Lower Wasserstein distance means the generated text has lexical richness patterns more similar to real human text.

- **Group Level (Value Distribution Variance):** The key metric for detecting polarization is the relative variance deviation:

$$\text{Var}\% = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{sim}} - \sigma^2_{\text{gt}}}{\sigma^2_{\text{gt}}} \times 100\%$$

where `$\sigma^2_{\text{sim}}$` is the variance of the simulated population's value distribution and `$\sigma^2_{\text{gt}}$` is the variance of the ground-truth human population. Positive values indicate the simulated population is too spread out (excessive variance); negative values indicate the population has collapsed to a narrow mode (polarization/rigidity); values closer to zero indicate better alignment. This metric is computed across all three domains and aggregated into the "Overall Val." column in Table 3.

**Why variance ratio rather than distribution divergence (e.g., KL)?** The paper's primary concern is with polarization—the systematic loss of population diversity. Variance ratio directly captures this: a collapsing distribution will have `$\sigma^2_{\text{sim}} \ll \sigma^2_{\text{gt}}$`, producing large negative values. This is simpler and more interpretable than full distribution divergences while capturing the specific failure mode the paper identifies. The complementary mean-deviation analysis in Table 5, Panel B confirms that the reduced variance is accompanied by mean-shift toward extremes—together, these constitute the full polarization picture.

---

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Decoupled generator and verifier** over integrated self-evaluation: breaks the self-referential bias loop that causes prompt-driven reasoning agents to amplify stereotypes. The generator produces diverse candidates; the verifier selects based on empirical preferences, not model introspection.

- **SFT+DPO for generator training** over RLHF or pure SFT: SFT anchors the model to ground-truth behavior; DPO with preference pairs explicitly penalizes caricatured responses. The SFT regularization term in the combined loss (weight 1.2) prevents DPO from drifting the model away from the empirical distribution.

- **Cross-attention for context-dependent value activation** over simple concatenation or fixed weighting: models the psychological insight that the same value profile produces different activations in different contexts. Enables interpretability by exposing which values the model is attending to.

- **Pairwise ranking objective for verifier training** over regression to ground truth: human behavior is stochastic—multiple actions can be equally value-aligned. The ranking objective only requires the verifier to prefer actions more similar to ground truth, which is a weaker and more appropriate signal.

- **GPV for psychometric measurement** over questionnaires or LLM inference: avoids reactivity bias (questionnaires) and self-referential bias (LLM inference). Provides objective value profiles derived from behavioral patterns.

- **Multi-domain CVABench** (social media, conversation, mobility) over a single-domain evaluation: tests whether value-driven behavioral fidelity generalizes across qualitatively different decision types—opinion expression (reviews), social interaction (comments), and spatial planning (mobility). The 10% held-out user split ensures no contamination between training and evaluation.

- **Limited inference intensity (4-5 rounds optimal)** over exhaustive search: matches the psychological principle of bounded rationality—humans don't optimize exhaustively, and the verifier shows diminishing returns beyond moderate evaluation depth. This is both computationally efficient and behaviorally authentic.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Explicit Reasoning About Persona Traits Amplifies Stereotypes Rather Than Improving Fidelity

This is the paper's most counterintuitive and potentially field-reorienting diagnostic finding: **making an LLM think harder about its assigned psychological traits does not make its behavior more realistic — it makes it more caricatured.** This is not a failure of insufficient reasoning depth; it is a failure caused by reasoning depth itself.

The dominant assumption in human-like agent research has been that if simple persona prompting (Paradigm 1) produces shallow imitation, the solution is to add cognitive structure — Chain-of-Thought reasoning about how values guide actions, iterative self-evaluation of candidate behaviors against value profiles (Paradigm 2). This assumption is baked into architectures like Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023) and reasoning-augmented role-play systems (Wang et al., 2025, 2023; Colas et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2025; Piao et al., 2025). The implicit logic is: more explicit cognitive modeling → more faithful simulation.

The paper demonstrates this logic is **backward** for LLM agents. The evidence from Section 3.1 and Appendix C is systematic rather than anecdotal. As reasoning rounds increase from 0 to 8 (Algorithm 1 in Appendix B), population-level value distributions undergo two simultaneous deformations: (1) variance monotonically decreases, collapsing the rich spread of human diversity into a narrow mode, and (2) the mean monotonically shifts away from ground truth toward extreme values. At 8 reasoning rounds, the average standard deviation falls to 50.84% of ground truth (meaning the simulated population is roughly half as diverse as real humans) while the absolute mean deviation reaches 0.0914 on the [-1, 1] scale. The violin plots in Figure 8 visualize this: the broad, nuanced distributions of real human value profiles shrink into sharp, rigid spikes as reasoning depth increases.

The mechanism the paper proposes — **cognitive distortion**, where the LLM simplifies the authentic value profile `V` into a caricatured archetype `V'` — is significant not because it is a new psychological concept (stereotyping is well-studied) but because it identifies a specific *architectural pathology* in LLM agents. The problem is not that LLMs lack psychological knowledge; it is that their knowledge is *too stereotypical* when accessed through explicit reasoning prompts. The model knows the cultural script for "Self-Directed person" (independent, gym-going, achievement-oriented) but doesn't know — or can't access under reasoning pressure — the subtler script of "Self-Directed person who is also exhausted and chooses rest." The lead-in case study (Appendix A, Figure 7) crystallizes this: an agent with Self-Direction = 0.9 and Hedonism varying from 0.2 to 0.6 chooses "go to the gym" with 93-100% probability after an exhausting workday, despite real humans in this situation frequently prioritizing rest regardless of trait levels. The reasoning chain "a Self-Directed person would push through fatigue to exercise" overrides the contextual signal "I am exhausted."

What makes this a fundamental rather than incremental contribution is that it **reframes the problem**. Prior work asked: "How can we get LLMs to reason better about personas?" This paper's evidence suggests the right question is: "Should LLMs be reasoning explicitly about personas at all, or does the act of explicit reasoning about psychological traits inherently amplify the model's stereotyping biases?" This is a diagnostic contribution that changes the research agenda — from improving reasoning prompts to developing architectures that avoid introspective persona reasoning entirely, which is exactly what the CVA architecture does by outsourcing value alignment to an independently trained verifier.

---

### Innovation 2: Decoupling Generation from Evaluation as an Architectural Principle to Escape Self-Referential Bias

The paper's proposed solution — the CVA architecture's "Generate-then-Select" protocol — is more than a specific model design. It represents a **architectural design principle** for any LLM system where behavioral quality depends on alignment with criteria that the LLM itself tends to stereotype.

The field's default approach to value-aligned generation — both in role-play agents and more broadly in self-improvement, self-critique, and constitutional AI paradigms — is what the paper calls "generate-and-verify internally." The same model (or a model from the same family) both produces candidate outputs and evaluates their quality. Self-verification, self-consistency, and Chain-of-Thought self-evaluation all follow this pattern. The paper's critique — articulated through the "LLM-as-a-judge" analysis in Section 1 and the prompt-driven reasoning failure in Section 3.1 — is that this creates a **self-referential bias loop**. The evaluator shares the generator's pretraining biases, so it endorses the same stereotypes the generator produces. An overly aggressive response to an "irritable" persona prompt gets rated as high-quality by a judge model that also associates irritability with aggression.

The CVA architecture breaks this loop through two design moves that are individually simple but collectively transformative:

**First, the generator and verifier are architecturally distinct and trained on different objectives.** The generator is trained via SFT+DPO to produce diverse, realistic candidates conditioned on context and values. The verifier is trained via pairwise ranking to prefer actions with higher similarity to ground-truth human behavior. The verifier never generates; the generator never self-evaluates. This separation means the verifier's judgments are anchored in **empirical preferences** (what real humans actually did) rather than **model introspection** (what the model thinks a person with these traits would do).

**Second, the verifier's training signal comes from behavioral similarity to ground truth, not from value-alignment judgments.** This is a subtle but crucial distinction. A naive verifier trained to predict "how value-aligned is this action?" would use the same stereotyping heuristics as the generator — it would learn that gym-going is "Self-Direction-aligned" and rate it highly regardless of context. The CVA verifier is instead trained to predict "is this action more similar to what a real human actually did than this other action?" The difference matters because the ground-truth data contains the contextual nuance that explicit value reasoning strips away — real Self-Directed people do sometimes choose rest when exhausted, and the verifier learns this from the preference pairs because the ground-truth action in that context reflects it.

The significance of this principle extends beyond behavioral simulation. Any LLM system where self-evaluation is used — self-improving code generation, automated red-teaming, constitutional AI alignment, self-consistency voting — inherits the self-referential bias problem. If the generator's biases cause it to produce certain kinds of errors, and the evaluator shares those biases, the evaluation will be blind to those errors. The CVA principle — train an independent evaluator on empirical ground truth rather than model judgment — provides a template for addressing this in other domains. The paper doesn't make this generalization claim explicitly, but the architectural logic is transferable.

This is a **fundamental shift** from the dominant "better prompting" paradigm because it changes the nature of the solution from *improving the model's introspection* to *eliminating the need for introspection*. The CVA architecture doesn't try to make the LLM better at reasoning about values; it removes value reasoning from the LLM's responsibilities entirely and assigns it to a component trained specifically for that purpose on empirical data.

---

### Innovation 3: Value Polarization as a Quantifiable, Diagnosable Failure Mode with a Ground-Truth Metric

The paper introduces **value polarization** not just as a qualitative observation but as a precisely measurable phenomenon with a specific metric — the relative variance deviation `Var%` — anchored in empirical population distributions. This transforms what was previously an anecdotal concern ("LLMs produce stereotypical behavior") into a diagnostic criterion that can be computed, compared across methods, and used to guide architectural decisions.

Prior work had noted that LLM agents exhibit stereotyping and reduced diversity (Li et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025), but these observations were largely qualitative or based on ad-hoc similarity metrics. The paper makes three contributions that together constitute a significant methodological advance:

**First, it operationalizes the Schwartz Value System as a measurement space for behavioral diversity.** By using GPV to infer 10-dimensional value profiles from behavioral logs, the paper creates a quantitative representation of *how diverse* a simulated population is and *in what ways* it deviates from real human populations. The Schwartz circumplex is well-suited to this because it captures not just individual trait levels but the *structure* of relationships between traits — whether the population exhibits realistic trade-offs between opposing values (e.g., Self-Direction vs. Conformity) or collapses them into a single mode.

**Second, it proposes a two-dimensional characterization of polarization: variance collapse and mean shift.** The paper reports both `Var%` (how much the distribution has narrowed) and absolute mean deviation (how far the center has shifted toward extremes). Together, these capture the specific pathology identified in prompt-driven reasoning: the population doesn't just become less diverse (which could happen if the model was merely conservative); it becomes less diverse *and* more extreme, converging on caricatured versions of the target traits. Table 5 shows both effects clearly: at 8 reasoning rounds, standard deviation drops to 50.84% of ground truth (Panel A) while absolute mean deviation rises to 0.0914 (Panel B).

**Third, it provides a ground-truth baseline for what "correct" diversity looks like.** This is the critical move that escapes the self-referential evaluation problem. Rather than asking "does this population look diverse?" (which an LLM judge would answer based on its own stereotypes of diversity), the paper asks "does this population's variance match the variance of real human populations measured from empirical behavior?" The ground truth is the distribution of 15,571 real users' GPV-inferred value profiles. A Var% of 0 means the simulated population is exactly as diverse as real humans; negative Var% means polarization; positive Var% means excessive (unrealistic) variance.

The diagnostic power of this framework is evident in the results. The prompt-driven reasoning agents show progressively worsening Var% with more reasoning rounds (from +107.06% at 0 rounds to +50.84% at 8 rounds), meaning they start with too much variance (inconsistent behavior) and end with too little (rigid stereotypes). The CVA architecture achieves +1.06% — nearly perfect alignment. This quantitative evidence makes the case for decoupled architectures far more compelling than qualitative observations alone could.

This is an **incremental-to-fundamental** contribution: individual components (Schwartz values, GPV, variance metrics) exist in prior work, but their combination into a diagnostic framework for agent behavioral fidelity — and the demonstration that this framework reveals a systematic failure mode invisible to LLM-as-judge evaluation — represents a significant methodological advance for the field.

---

### Innovation 4: The Value Verifier's Learned Embeddings Recover the Theoretical Structure of Human Values Without Explicit Structural Supervision

One of the paper's most intriguing findings is that the Value Verifier's learned value embeddings spontaneously organize into the **circular circumplex structure** predicted by Schwartz's theory (Schwartz, 1992a, 1994), despite the verifier receiving no explicit training signal about this structure. The embeddings are trained only to support pairwise action ranking; the circular topology emerges from the data.

The paper quantifies this via the Circular Inversion Score (CIS), a custom metric that measures how well the angular ordering of value embeddings in PCA-reduced 2D space matches the theoretical Schwartz circumplex ordering (Appendix E). The trained verifier achieves a CIS of 0.75, compared to 0.48 for the untrained verifier embeddings and 1.00 for the theoretical ground truth. Specific structural features are preserved: the adjacency within the Self-Transcendence cluster (Universalism and Benevolence) and the Conservation cluster (Tradition and Conformity) emerges naturally. The primary deviation is the displacement of Achievement from the Self-Enhancement quadrant to a position adjacent to Self-Direction, which the paper interprets as the model conflating "individual success" (Achievement) with "intellectual autonomy" (Self-Direction) — a plausible semantic confusion given the behavioral data distribution.

This finding is significant for three reasons beyond being aesthetically pleasing:

**First, it provides validation that the verifier has learned something genuinely structured about human values, not just surface correlations.** If the embeddings had been arbitrary — if the verifier was just memorizing which actions tend to get high scores without forming a coherent value representation — the CIS would be near chance level. The 0.75 score indicates that the internal representation captures genuine psychometric structure, which in turn suggests the verifier's judgments are based on meaningful value reasoning rather than shallow pattern matching.

**Second, it demonstrates that value structure can be learned from behavioral preferences alone, without explicit psychological annotation.** The verifier was trained on pairwise preferences derived from linguistic similarity to ground-truth actions — not on value labels or circumplex structure. The fact that the Schwartz circular structure emerges suggests that this structure is latent in real human behavioral data: people's choices naturally reflect the compatibility and tension relationships between values, and a model trained to predict those choices will internalize those relationships. This is a form of unsupervised structure discovery with implications beyond this specific architecture — it suggests that value-aligned AI systems might not need explicit value taxonomies if they can learn value structure from behavioral preferences.

**Third, it enables interpretability and potential steerability.** The cross-attention mechanism's weights can be projected back to the vocabulary space (the word-to-value analysis in Appendix E, Figures 6 and 10), revealing which lexical features trigger which value activations. Figure 10 shows a clear diagonal structure: words like "creativity" and "technological" strongly activate Self-Direction; "comfort" and "beauty" activate Hedonism; "protestors" and "ukraine" activate Universalism. This means the verifier is not a black box — its value judgments can be inspected and understood at the token level. Furthermore, the latent value structure opens the possibility of **probe-based value manipulation**: steering agent behavior by intervening on the value embeddings along specific circumplex dimensions, which the paper flags as a future direction.

This is an **emergent property finding** rather than a designed innovation — the paper did not set out to recover the Schwartz circumplex; it discovered post-hoc that the verifier had learned it. But it significantly strengthens the paper's theoretical grounding by showing that the architecture doesn't just work empirically — it works for psychologically meaningful reasons.

---

### Innovation 5: Bounded Rationality as an Emergent Property of Value Verification — More Compute Is Not Always Better

The paper's finding that behavioral fidelity peaks at 4-5 verifier reasoning rounds and saturates or declines thereafter (Figure 4) is not just a practical observation about hyperparameter tuning. It represents an **emergent alignment between the computational properties of the verifier and the cognitive constraints of human decision-making**, and it carries implications for how we design value-aligned agent architectures.

The standard assumption in test-time compute scaling is that more evaluation rounds → better decisions. This assumption underlies best-of-N sampling, majority voting, and iterative refinement approaches. The CVA verifier violates this monotonicity. Sentiment accuracy in the media domain increases from approximately 0.35 at intensity 1 to a peak of 0.50-0.53 at intensity 4-5, then plateaus or slightly declines. The paper interprets this through the lens of **bounded rationality** (Simon, 1957): real humans don't exhaustively evaluate all options against all values; they satisfice with a limited evaluation scope. The verifier's performance curve mirrors this cognitive constraint.

Why does performance decline beyond the optimal intensity? The paper does not fully explain the mechanism, but the implications section suggests a plausible account: the verifier itself has calibration imperfections (it is, after all, a trained model with finite data). When forced to make increasingly fine-grained distinctions between already-good candidates beyond 4-5 rounds, these imperfections compound. The verifier may start to prefer candidates that overfit to its specific training biases rather than candidates that are genuinely more value-aligned — essentially, a form of **verifier over-optimization** analogous to reward hacking in RLHF.

This finding has two significant implications for the architecture's design philosophy:

**First, it validates the "Generate-then-Select" design over iterative refinement.** If more evaluation always helped, you would want an architecture where the verifier provides feedback to the generator in a loop, iteratively improving actions. The saturation suggests instead that a fixed, moderate evaluation budget is both computationally optimal and behaviorally authentic. The architecture doesn't need to be a loop; it needs to be a single-pass selection with a well-chosen evaluation budget.

**Second, it suggests a deeper connection between computational and cognitive constraints that the paper doesn't fully develop but that is provocative for future work.** The fact that the verifier's optimal performance occurs at an evaluation depth roughly analogous to human cognitive limits (evaluating a handful of options, not dozens) might not be a coincidence. If the verifier is trained on human behavioral data, and humans are bounded reasoners, the verifier may implicitly learn the evaluation budget that matches human decision processes. More evaluation rounds push the verifier into a regime that has no counterpart in the training data — the data reflects what humans chose after bounded deliberation, not after exhaustive optimization — and performance degrades as the verifier is forced to extrapolate beyond its training distribution. If this account is correct, it means that **value-aligned systems may inherently have optimal evaluation budgets determined by the cognitive constraints of the agents whose values they model**, not by the computational limits of the verification system.

This is an **emergent finding** rather than a designed feature, but it contributes to the paper's broader narrative: the CVA architecture doesn't just outperform baselines on behavioral fidelity metrics; it exhibits structural properties (value embedding topology, bounded optimality) that align with psychological theory in ways that were not explicitly programmed. This alignment strengthens the case that value-driven architectures grounded in empirical human data are the right direction for human-like agents.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use CVABench (Section 2.4), a custom benchmark aggregating over 1.1 million authentic interaction traces from 15,571 unique users across three behavioral domains: Social Media Reviews (Yelp, 4,924 users, 54K entries), Conversational Discourse (Reddit, 5,640 users, 155K entries), and Spatio-Temporal Mobility (Foursquare, 5,007 users, 871K entries). For training, 90% of users are used; the remaining 10% are held out as an unseen evaluation simulation environment to prevent data leakage. No training method has access to these held-out users' data.

- **Base model(s).** The primary base model for the CVA architecture and training-required baselines is Qwen2.5-7B, a 7-billion-parameter open-source LLM. The prompt-driven baselines (role-play and reasoning agents) use GPT-4o-mini as the underlying LLM. The paper does not explicitly justify the model scale choice beyond noting that Qwen2.5-7B is used as the backbone for all trainable methods, but the asymmetry in base model capability (GPT-4o-mini for training-free baselines vs. Qwen2.5-7B for trained methods) is a potential confounding factor in direct comparisons that the paper does not address.

- **Metrics.** The evaluation framework uses domain-specific individual-level metrics and a cross-domain group-level metric:
  - **Social Media:** Rating Accuracy (discrete 1–5 prediction accuracy computed as `Acc = (1/N) ∑ I(ŷ_i = y_i)` ) and Sentiment Accuracy (binary positive/negative classification accuracy with the same formula). Linguistic fidelity is measured via the 1-Wasserstein distance between the Type-Token Ratio (TTR) distributions of generated and ground-truth text: `W₁(P_gen, P_real) = inf_{γ∈Γ} E_{(x,y)~γ}[|x − y|]`. Lower Wasserstein distance indicates lexical richness patterns closer to real human text.
  - **Conversation:** Attitude Accuracy (predicting user stance/subreddit-specific behavior; same accuracy formula as above) and TTR Wasserstein distance.
  - **Mobility:** Category Accuracy (correct POI category prediction) and Mean Squared Error for stay duration: `MSE = (1/N) ∑ (t̂_i − t_i)²`, where t̂_i and t_i are predicted and actual stay durations in minutes.
  - **Group-Level Value Alignment:** The relative variance deviation between simulated and ground-truth population value distributions: `Var% = (σ²_sim − σ²_gt) / σ²_gt × 100%`. Values closer to 0 indicate better alignment; negative values indicate variance collapse (polarization/rigidity); positive values indicate excessive variance. This metric is aggregated across all three domains into the "Overall Val." (Var%) column in Table 3. Value profiles are measured using GPV (Ye et al., 2025), which infers 10-dimensional Schwartz value vectors (normalized to [−1, 1]) from behavioral logs.

- **Baselines.** The paper evaluates against three categories of baselines:
  - **Prompt-Driven Role-Play Agent:** A training-free agent that uses In-Context Learning with historical user data retrieved via BM25 to condition action generation on working memory and long-term memory, without explicit value conditioning. Uses GPT-4o-mini.
  - **Prompt-Driven Reasoning Agents:** Variants of the role-play agent augmented with iterative value-driven reasoning (Algorithm 1, Appendix B), tested at reasoning strengths T ∈ {0, 1, 2, 4, 8}. At each reasoning round, the agent generates K = 3 candidate actions (2 new + the previous best), evaluates each for value alignment via prompt-driven self-evaluation, and selects the highest-scoring candidate. The T = 0 variant is explicitly conditioned on value information (unlike the pure role-play baseline); T > 0 adds iterative refinement. Uses GPT-4o-mini.
  - **Training-Required Agents:** Fine-tuned variants of Qwen2.5-7B using the CVABench training data. Two variants are reported: Qwen2.5-7B-SFT (supervised fine-tuning only) and Qwen2.5-7B-SFT+DPO (SFT followed by Direct Preference Optimization with preference pairs constructed via linguistic similarity to ground truth; see Appendix B.3 for the DPO loss formula and BCO component). These serve as ablations for the CVA generator training pipeline.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The primary experiments in Table 3 use single-generation outputs (one action per context, selected either directly or via verifier). The inference intensity sweep in Figure 4 and the ablation in Table 4 vary the number of verifier reasoning rounds (1, 3, 5, 8), where each round involves generating new candidates and scoring them. For the prompt-driven reasoning baselines, the generation budget at strength T is (2T + 1) generations per context (one initial action plus 2 new candidates per reasoning round). For the SFT+DPO baseline, K = 10 candidates are generated for DPO preference pair construction; at inference, single-generation is used. The candidate count for verifier training is K = 5 per context. The paper does not report total FLOPs or wall-clock time for any method, making compute comparisons across training-free and training-required approaches qualitative rather than quantitative, though the focus is on behavioral fidelity rather than compute efficiency.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report cross-validation for the main results in Table 3. The held-out 10% user split serves as a fixed test set, with no indication that multiple splits or statistical significance tests were performed. For the inference intensity sweep (Figure 4), the paper reports accuracy at 4 discrete intensity levels, but does not report error bars, confidence intervals, or standard deviations for any metric in the main results tables or figures. The absence of statistical rigor is a limitation, particularly given the relatively small per-domain user counts (500–600 held-out users per domain) and the stochasticity inherent in LLM generation.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Population-Level Value Polarization in Prompt-Driven Agents (Section 3.1)

The central diagnostic result appears in Figure 3 (main text) and Figure 8 (Appendix C, showing all 10 Schwartz dimensions), with quantitative backing in Table 5. The headline finding: **increasing the intensity of prompt-driven psychological reasoning causes population-level value distributions to simultaneously collapse in variance and shift in mean toward extreme values.**

At reasoning strength 0 (value-conditioned generation without iterative reasoning), the simulated population already shows distortion relative to ground truth: the average standard deviation across the 10 value dimensions is 107.06% of ground truth (Table 5, Panel A), indicating **excessive variance** — the model produces overly random behavior. The absolute mean deviation is 0.0071 on the [−1, 1] scale (Table 5, Panel B).

As reasoning strength increases to 2, 4, and 8 rounds, two things happen simultaneously:

- **Variance monotonically collapses.** The average standard deviation drops from 107.06% (T = 0) to 75.67% (T = 1) to 64.69% (T = 2) to 59.26% (T = 4) to 50.84% (T = 8). By T = 8, the simulated population is roughly **half as diverse** as real humans. Individual dimensions show even more extreme collapse: Hedonism drops to 10.57% of ground-truth variance at T = 4; Benevolence drops to 1.30% at T = 8 — nearly a point mass. The role-play baseline (without explicit value conditioning) shows the opposite extreme: average variance of 110.29%, indicating that value-unaware role-play produces overly erratic behavior that is also misaligned with real population distributions.

- **Mean shifts toward extremes.** The absolute mean difference from ground truth increases from 0.0071 (T = 0) to 0.0583 (T = 1) to 0.0844 (T = 2) to 0.0785 (T = 4) to 0.0914 (T = 8). By T = 8, dimensions like Self-Direction (mean deviation 0.1547), Power (0.1486), and Conformity (0.1723) show substantial drift toward the −1 or +1 extremes. These are not small shifts: on a [−1, 1] scale, a mean deviation of 0.15–0.17 represents a substantial fraction of the available range.

The violin plots in Figure 8 visualize this trajectory. At T = 0, distributions are broad but often centered away from ground truth. As T increases, the violins narrow and migrate toward one or both extremes (visible in the "pinching" of the violin shapes and the shift of the black mean-dash toward +1 or −1). The Stimulation dimension (shown in Figure 3 of the main text) is representative: at T = 0, the distribution is spread roughly from −0.5 to +1.0; by T = 8, it has collapsed to a narrow mode near +0.8, with almost no density below +0.5. The role-play baseline shows a similar but less extreme pattern, with the mean shifted toward positive values but the variance remaining somewhat broader.

This result directly supports the paper's central diagnostic claim: **explicit reasoning about values amplifies stereotyping rather than improving fidelity.** It also reveals a previously undescribed failure mode: the transition from excessive variance (inconsistent, random-seeming behavior at low reasoning strength) to insufficient variance (rigid, caricatured behavior at high reasoning strength), with no intermediate point that achieves ground-truth alignment. Simply adding value conditioning (T = 0 vs. role-play) reduces the excessive-variance problem but does not solve the mean-shift problem; adding reasoning depth then creates the opposite problem (variance collapse) while worsening the mean shift. The paper's interpretation — that prompt-driven reasoning introduces "cognitive distortion" where the LLM simplifies authentic value profiles into caricatures — is consistent with these observations.

#### Main Benchmark Results: CVA vs. Baselines (Section 3.2, Table 3)

Table 3 reports the head-to-head comparison of the full CVA architecture (Qwen2.5-7B backbone with VMC generator training and VDR verifier-based selection) against five baselines across all three domains, plus an aggregated value alignment score.

**Individual-Level Behavioral Fidelity.** The CVA architecture achieves the best or second-best result on 6 of 7 domain-specific metrics:

- **Social Media Reviews:** CVA achieves Rating Accuracy of 0.47 (best, vs. 0.43 for the best prompt-driven reasoning agent at T = 2 and 0.43 for SFT+DPO). Sentiment Accuracy is 0.36 (best, vs. 0.38 for the best T = 2 and T = 4 reasoning agents, which is one of only two metrics where CVA is not strictly best — and the gap is 0.02). Linguistic TTR Wasserstein distance is 0.04 (best, tied with the T = 2 and T = 4 reasoning agents at 0.06 — CVA is marginally better by this metric as well).

- **Conversation:** CVA achieves Sentiment Accuracy of 0.53 (best, vs. 0.52 for SFT and SFT+DPO baselines) and Positive-stance Accuracy of 0.32 (best by a large margin — the next-best is SFT and SFT+DPO at 0.23; all prompt-driven baselines are at 0.02–0.05). Linguistic TTR distance is 0.03 (best, vs. 0.06 for the best prompt-driven baseline and 0.04 for SFT and SFT+DPO).

- **Mobility:** CVA achieves MSE of 2.77 for stay duration prediction (best, vs. 2.87–2.89 for prompt-driven baselines and 2.96–3.04 for training-required baselines). Notably, the SFT and SFT+DPO baselines **degrade** mobility MSE relative to the prompt-driven approaches (3.04 and 2.96 vs. 2.87–2.89), suggesting that naive fine-tuning on behavioral data can harm performance in some domains while CVA's value-conditioned training avoids this.

**Group-Level Value Alignment.** This is where CVA's advantage is most dramatic. The Overall Val. (Var%) metric is +1.06% for CVA — meaning the simulated population's value variance is within 1.06% of the ground-truth human population variance. Every baseline shows substantially worse alignment:

- Role-Play Agent: +10.29% (positive, indicating excessive variance — the population is too spread out)
- Reasoning Agent (T = 0): +7.06% (excessive variance)
- Reasoning Agent (T = 2): −35.31% (variance collapse — the population is far too narrow)
- Reasoning Agent (T = 4): −40.74% (even more extreme variance collapse)
- Qwen2.5-7B SFT: +18.68% (excessive variance)
- Qwen2.5-7B SFT+DPO: +27.98% (even more excessive variance — training on behavioral data without value conditioning actually makes the diversity problem worse, not better)

This result is the paper's strongest evidence for its central claim. The CVA architecture doesn't just outperform baselines on accuracy metrics (which could be achieved through overfitting to surface patterns); it achieves near-perfect recovery of population-level value diversity. The contrast between CVA (+1.06%) and SFT+DPO (+27.98%) is particularly instructive: both are trained on the same data, but the SFT+DPO baseline — which lacks explicit value conditioning and a separate verifier — produces a population that is substantially more spread out than real humans, while CVA's value-conditioned generation plus verifier-grounded selection recovers the correct distribution.

**A notable pattern in the training-required baselines.** The SFT and SFT+DPO baselines show competitive or strong individual-level accuracy (Rating = 0.43, Sentiment = 0.52–0.53) but poor group-level value alignment (+18.68% and +27.98%). This suggests they are learning to reproduce average or surface-level behavioral patterns without capturing the psychological structure that generates population diversity. They produce accurate individual predictions but the wrong population distribution — a classic sign of mode collapse toward common patterns with insufficient modeling of individual differences. CVA resolves this by explicitly conditioning on value profiles.

**The prompt-driven reasoning agents show a consistent accuracy pattern across difficulty.** Rating Accuracy peaks at T = 2 (0.43) and T = 4 (0.42), then presumably declines (though T = 8 is not reported for individual-level metrics). Sentiment Accuracy peaks at T = 2–4 (0.38) and T = 0 (0.35), with no clear monotonic trend. Attitude Accuracy is poor across all reasoning strengths (0.31–0.40) — the conversation domain appears to be particularly challenging for prompt-driven methods. The key takeaway is that even at their best, prompt-driven reasoning agents underperform CVA on most metrics while simultaneously producing dramatically worse value alignment.

**GPT-4o-mini vs. Qwen2.5-7B confound.** The prompt-driven baselines use GPT-4o-mini (a more capable base model than Qwen2.5-7B), while CVA and the training-required baselines use Qwen2.5-7B. This means the prompt-driven baselines have an **unfair advantage** in base model capability — yet they still underperform CVA on most metrics. The paper does not explicitly discuss this asymmetry, but it strengthens the case for the CVA architecture: a 7B model with value-driven architecture outperforms a (presumably) larger, more capable model with prompt-driven reasoning.

#### Inference Intensity Sweep: Verifier Reasoning Rounds (Section 3.3, Figure 4)

Figure 4 reports the CVA architecture's sentiment accuracy in the media domain as a function of inference intensity (number of verifier reasoning rounds, tested at 1, 3, 5, and 8 rounds). The results are presented as "top 1/2/4 accuracy" across four discrete intensity levels, though the exact numerical values are not quoted in the text — only the trend is described.

The headline finding: **sentiment accuracy initially increases with more verifier reasoning rounds but saturates and plateaus or slightly declines beyond 4–5 rounds.** The text states: "increasing inference depth initially improves decision-making quality. However, performance gains saturate when the number of inference rounds exceeds four; beyond this threshold, additional reasoning steps yield diminishing returns in behavioral modeling accuracy."

The trend lines for "Reason 1," "Reason 3," "Reason 5," and "Reason 8" in Figure 4 show the overall pattern, with the "Trend" line confirming the saturating curve. The paper interprets this through the lens of bounded rationality (Section 3.3 and the Discussion): just as humans don't exhaustively evaluate all options, the verifier's optimal evaluation depth is limited. Beyond 4–5 rounds, additional computation may cause the verifier to make increasingly fine-grained distinctions that exceed the reliability of its training signal, possibly introducing verifier over-optimization.

This finding is significant for the architecture's design philosophy — it validates that the "Generate-then-Select" protocol with a fixed, moderate evaluation budget is both computationally efficient and behaviorally optimal, rather than requiring iterative refinement loops. However, the paper's reporting of this result is notably thin: no exact numerical values are quoted, no statistical significance is reported, and the finding is only demonstrated for the sentiment accuracy metric in the media domain. It is unclear whether the same saturation pattern holds for other metrics (rating accuracy, attitude accuracy, mobility MSE) or other domains. This limits the strength of the bounded-rationality interpretation to a suggestive observation rather than a robust empirical result.

#### Ablation Study: CVA Component Contributions (Section 3.3, Table 4)

Table 4 reports an incremental ablation of the CVA architecture in what appears to be the social media domain (Rating and Sentiment Accuracy, plus linguistic TTR). The progression is:

- **Raw Model (Qwen2.5-7B, no fine-tuning):** Rating = 0.22, Sentiment = 0.29, Linguistic TTR = 0.07.
- **+ SFT:** Rating improves to 0.43 (+0.21), Sentiment to 0.33 (+0.04), TTR to 0.06 (−0.01, slight improvement).
- **+ DPO:** Rating unchanged at 0.43, Sentiment unchanged at 0.33, TTR moves to 0.07 (back to raw model level).
- **+ Verifier Reasoning (full CVA):** Rating improves to 0.47 (+0.04), Sentiment to 0.36 (+0.03), TTR improves to 0.04 (−0.03).

The key takeaways:

- **SFT provides the largest single gain** across all metrics. The jump from raw model (0.22 Rating) to SFT (0.43) accounts for the majority of the total improvement to 0.47. This is expected — the model needs to learn the basic mapping from context to domain-appropriate actions.

- **DPO alone provides no measurable benefit** on Rating or Sentiment Accuracy when added to SFT. The Rating stays at 0.43, Sentiment at 0.33. The TTR distance actually increases slightly (0.06 → 0.07), suggesting DPO may trade off some linguistic diversity for preference alignment. This is a notable and somewhat surprising result given the paper's emphasis on DPO for suppressing caricatured responses. The paper does not discuss this null result explicitly. Possible interpretations: (a) DPO's benefits manifest primarily through interaction with the verifier, not standalone metrics; (b) DPO improves group-level value alignment rather than individual-level accuracy (not shown in this table); or (c) the preference pairs constructed via linguistic similarity are not sufficiently informative to improve accuracy beyond what SFT already achieves.

- **The Verifier Reasoning module provides a consistent incremental gain** across all three metrics: Rating +0.04, Sentiment +0.03, TTR −0.03. This is the contribution of decoupled value-verification on top of a well-calibrated generator. The magnitude is modest (4–9% relative improvement) but consistent across metrics, and importantly, does not come at the cost of any other metric — the verifier improves behavioral fidelity without introducing new failure modes.

The ablation provides clean evidence for the paper's architectural claims: (1) training on ground-truth data (SFT) is essential and provides most of the gain; (2) the verifier adds incremental value above what training alone achieves; (3) the components are complementary rather than redundant. However, the ablation is incomplete in two respects: (a) it does not report group-level Var% for each ablation stage, which would be the most direct test of the claim that the verifier specifically reduces polarization; and (b) it does not include a "SFT+Verifier (without DPO)" configuration, which would test whether DPO is actually necessary when the verifier is present. The paper's architecture claims that DPO suppresses caricatured responses; if the verifier already selects against caricatured responses, DPO might be redundant. This missing ablation is a gap.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper's ablation and robustness analysis is primarily concentrated in Appendix C (value polarization analysis across all 10 Schwartz dimensions), Appendix E (circular embedding structure and word-to-value projection), and the incremental component ablation in Table 4. Several important ablations and robustness checks that would strengthen the paper are absent.

**Full 10-dimension value polarization analysis (Appendix C, Table 5, Figure 8):** This extends the Stimulation-only result shown in Figure 3 to all ten Schwartz value dimensions. The pattern of variance collapse and mean polarization under increased reasoning strength is **consistent across all ten dimensions**, though with quantitative variation. Some dimensions show more extreme collapse than others: Benevolence drops to 1.30% of ground-truth variance at T = 8 (nearly a point mass), while Power drops to 9.04% and Tradition to 114.71% (actually showing *higher* variance than ground truth at T = 4–8, an interesting exception). The "Average" row confirms the monotonic trend: variance decreases from 107.06% (T = 0) to 50.84% (T = 8), and mean deviation increases from 0.0071 to 0.0914. This cross-dimensional consistency is important: it rules out the possibility that the polarization effect is specific to one or two value dimensions and establishes it as a systematic property of prompt-driven value reasoning. The exceptions (Tradition maintaining high variance, Security showing initial excessive variance then moderate collapse) suggest that some value dimensions are more resistant to polarization than others, possibly because they are less strongly associated with stereotypical behaviors in the model's training data — but the paper does not explore this heterogeneity.

**Circular embedding structure analysis (Appendix E, Figure 5, Table 8):** The learned value embeddings from the Value Verifier exhibit a spatial arrangement that matches the theoretical Schwartz circumplex. The Circular Inversion Score (CIS) quantifies this: trained verifier achieves CIS = 0.75, compared to untrained verifier CIS = 0.48 and ground-truth CIS = 1.00. The MDS visualization in Figure 5 (left) shows the embeddings in 2D PCA-projected space; Figure 5 (right) shows the reconstructed circular structure with labeled positions. The primary deviation is the displacement of Achievement from the Self-Enhancement quadrant to adjacency with Self-Direction — the paper interprets this as the model conflating "individual success" with "intellectual autonomy." The preservation of the Self-Transcendence cluster (Universalism and Benevolence) and Conservation cluster (Tradition and Conformity) indicates that the verifier has learned the coarse structure of value compatibilities. This is a robustness check on the **psychological validity** of the verifier's representations: it demonstrates that the verifier's internal value model is not arbitrary but captures genuine psychometric structure. However, the paper does not report CIS scores for intermediate training stages or ablated architectures, so it's unclear whether this emergent structure is specific to the verifier or would also appear in the generator's value representations.

**Word-to-value projection analysis (Appendix E, Figures 6, 10, 11):** The cross-attention weights from the Value Verifier can be projected back to the vocabulary space to identify which words trigger which value activations. Figure 6 shows a word cloud for the Universalism dimension, where larger terms represent stronger activation. Figure 10 shows a heatmap of relative activation strengths across words and values, with a clear diagonal structure: words like "creativity" and "technological" activate Self-Direction; "comfort" and "beauty" activate Hedonism; "protestors" and "ukraine" activate Universalism; "chatgpt" and "sell" activate Achievement. Figure 11 provides per-dimension word clouds. This analysis serves as a **qualitative interpretability check**: it demonstrates that the verifier's attention patterns are semantically sensible and align with psychological theory. Words triggering Universalism (Figure 6) include "protestors," "ukraine," "suicide," "banning," and "suffering" — concepts related to concern for welfare of all people, consistent with the Schwartz definition. However, this analysis is purely post-hoc and qualitative; no quantitative metric is proposed for evaluating interpretation quality, and no comparison is made against what a "correct" word-to-value mapping should look like. The diagonal structure in Figure 10 is visually compelling but no numerical summary (e.g., average diagonal-to-off-diagonal ratio) is reported.

**Coarse-grained value activation by community (Appendix E, Figure 9):** Average cross-attention weights are reported across three Reddit communities (changemyview, unpopularopinion, explainlikeimfive) for all ten value dimensions. The paper notes that "the relatively consistent activation patterns across diverse subreddits suggest that community-level metadata is insufficient to fully disentangle value triggers, motivating the need for the fine-grained, token-level projection analysis." This is a negative result that justifies the more detailed word-level analysis, but the interpretation is questionable: the three subreddits chosen are fairly similar (all are discussion/debate-oriented communities), so finding similar value activation patterns is not surprising and does not demonstrate that subreddit-level analysis is insufficient in general. A stronger test would compare politically left-leaning vs. right-leaning subreddits, or hobby-focused vs. news-focused communities, where community-level value differences would be expected.

**Missing domain-level breakdown for the main CVA results.** Table 3 reports domain-specific metrics but maintains the same model across all domains. The paper does not report whether the CVA architecture's advantage varies systematically by domain — are the gains concentrated in one domain (e.g., conversation, where CVA's positive-stance accuracy of 0.32 dwarfs all baselines at 0.02–0.05) or consistent across domains? The mobility domain shows the narrowest advantage (MSE 2.77 vs. 2.87 for the best prompt-driven baseline), suggesting domain-specific variation, but this is not analyzed.

**Missing comparison to a separately trained ORM/PRM-style verifier.** The Value Verifier is compared to no-verifier ablations but not to simpler verifier architectures — e.g., an outcome-based verifier that scores complete actions without the cross-attention value-activation mechanism, or a PRM-style step-by-step verifier. This makes it impossible to attribute the verifier's benefits specifically to the value-activation mechanism rather than to having any trained discriminator at all. A "same generator + simple verifier" baseline would disentangle the contribution of the architecture's value-modeling sophistication from the mere presence of a separate evaluation component.

**Missing temperature sweep for generator sampling.** The generator uses temperature 0.8 for both training candidate generation (SFT+DPO) and inference candidate generation. No ablation over temperature is reported. Given that behavioral diversity is a central concern, temperature is a critical hyperparameter — too low, and the generator may produce insufficient candidate diversity for the verifier to select from; too high, and candidate quality may degrade. The paper's claim that CVA "effectively mitigates polarization" depends partly on the generator producing diverse enough candidates that the verifier can restore population-level variance. Without a temperature sweep, we don't know how sensitive this result is to this hyperparameter.

**Missing statistical significance.** No error bars, confidence intervals, or significance tests are reported for any metric in Table 3, Table 4, Figure 3, Figure 4, or any other result. The test set is 10% of users (approximately 1,557 users across all domains, with roughly 500 per domain), and individual-level metrics are averaged over the per-user predictions. Without variance estimates, we cannot assess whether the differences between methods (e.g., CVA Rating 0.47 vs. SFT+DPO Rating 0.43) are statistically reliable or within the range of sampling noise. This is a significant methodological weakness, particularly for a paper whose central contribution is demonstrating that a specific architectural choice improves behavioral fidelity.

**Missing training data ablation.** The CVA generator is trained on the full CVABench training set (90% of users). No ablation over training data quantity is reported. Given the relatively modest size of the social media and conversation domains (54K and 155K entries, respectively), it is unclear whether the SFT gains would hold with less data (important for domains where large-scale behavioral traces are unavailable) or whether more data would continue to improve performance (indicating the approach is data-limited).

### Critical Assessment

**Central Claim 1: Increasing prompt-driven reasoning intensity exacerbates value polarization and collapses population diversity.** This claim is **well-supported by the experimental evidence**, with minor caveats about domain coverage. The quantitative evidence in Table 5 (variance collapse from 107.06% to 50.84%, mean deviation increase from 0.0071 to 0.0914 as reasoning strength goes from 0 to 8) and the visual evidence in Figure 8 (all 10 dimensions) is systematic and internally consistent. The trend is monotonic across reasoning strengths, rules out the possibility of an optimal intermediate reasoning depth, and holds across all value dimensions (with understandable quantitative variation). The lead-in case study (Appendix A, Figure 7) provides a qualitative illustration that aligns with the quantitative results.

**What holds:** The relationship "more reasoning → worse value alignment at the population level" is demonstrated for GPT-4o-mini on the Reddit conversation domain of CVABench.

**What is not tested:** The paper does not demonstrate this phenomenon with other base models (GPT-4, Claude, Llama), other domains within CVABench (the analysis is primarily from the Reddit domain), or other tasks (only conversational behavior, not review generation or mobility prediction). The paper's framing implies this is a general property of prompt-driven reasoning in LLM agents, but the evidence is from a single model on a single domain. This is a significant limitation: if GPT-4o-mini has particularly strong stereotyping biases (plausible given its training), the effect might be less severe or even absent in models with different alignment characteristics. The paper would be stronger with at least one additional base model for the polarization analysis.

**Central Claim 2: The CVA architecture effectively mitigates polarization while achieving superior behavioral fidelity.** This claim is **supported with important boundary conditions.** The group-level Var% result (+1.06% for CVA vs. −35% to −41% for prompt-driven reasoning, +19% to +28% for training-required baselines) is a strong quantitative demonstration that CVA recovers population-level value diversity that other methods systematically distort. The individual-level accuracy results (Table 3) show CVA as best or competitive on most metrics.

**What holds:** CVA with Qwen2.5-7B backbone, trained on CVABench data, evaluated on held-out CVABench users, achieves near-ground-truth population value variance while maintaining or exceeding baseline individual-level accuracy.

**What is not tested:**
- **Generalization to other models.** Would CVA work with a different base model (e.g., Llama-3, Mistral)? The paper uses only Qwen2.5-7B for trainable methods. The value-conditioned SFT+DPO pipeline may be sensitive to the base model's pretraining biases — a model with different stereotyping tendencies might require different DPO preference construction or different hyperparameters.
- **Generalization to other domains.** The three CVABench domains (Yelp reviews, Reddit comments, Foursquare check-ins) are all relatively constrained behavioral domains with clear action spaces. It's unclear whether the architecture would transfer to open-ended dialogue, creative writing, or multi-step planning tasks where the action space is less structured and ground-truth similarity is harder to define.
- **Sensitivity to value measurement quality.** The entire architecture depends on GPV for value profile inference. GPV's measurement accuracy directly bounds CVA's achievable fidelity. The paper acknowledges that GPV "may still contain encoded biases" (Section 6) but argues the framework avoids self-reinforcing bias loops because ground truth provides the ultimate supervision signal. This is a reasonable argument, but it means CVA can only be as good as GPV's value measurements. If GPV systematically misattributes certain behaviors to certain values, the verifier will learn those misattributions. The paper does not report GPV's test-retest reliability or construct validity on the CVABench domains specifically, only citing prior validation from Ye et al. (2025).
- **The confounded base model comparison.** CVA (Qwen2.5-7B) is compared against prompt-driven baselines (GPT-4o-mini) — different model families with different scales and capabilities. The paper's claim that CVA "significantly outperforms baselines" conflates architectural advantage with potential base model differences. The training-required baselines (Qwen2.5-7B SFT and SFT+DPO) provide a fairer comparison and do show CVA's advantage, but the prompt-driven baselines are not on equal footing.

**Central Claim 3: The CVA architecture decouples action generation from cognitive reasoning, and this decoupling is what breaks the self-referential bias loop.** This claim is **partially supported by the component ablation** but is not directly tested. Table 4 shows that adding the Verifier Reasoning module to an SFT+DPO-trained generator improves all metrics. This demonstrates that decoupling helps, but does not demonstrate *why* it helps — is it because the verifier is trained on ground-truth preferences (the empirical grounding argument), because it is architecturally separate (the decoupling argument), or because it uses cross-attention for context-dependent value activation (the psychological modeling argument)?

The paper's theoretical framing attributes the improvement specifically to breaking the self-referential loop, but this mechanism is not isolated experimentally. To test the decoupling claim directly, one would need a baseline that uses the same generator for both generation and self-evaluation (comparing "generator + separate verifier" vs. "generator + self-evaluation"), holding the evaluation architecture constant. The SFT+DPO baseline without verifier (second-to-last row in Table 3) is a partial comparison — it has no explicit evaluation step at all — but does not isolate the value of *architectural separation* from the value of *having any evaluation mechanism*. A "generator with integrated value reasoning head" baseline would be a more direct test.

**Central Claim 4: The Value Verifier's learned embeddings recover the theoretical structure of human values.** This claim is **supported by the CIS analysis** (CIS = 0.75 for trained verifier vs. 0.48 for untrained, Table 8) and the qualitative visualizations in Figures 5, 10, and 11. However, the support is largely post-hoc and qualitative. The CIS metric is custom-defined for this paper and has not been validated against established circumplex structure measures. The threshold for "good" CIS is not established — is 0.75 meaningfully better than 0.48 in a way that translates to behavioral differences? The paper doesn't report whether CIS correlates with downstream behavioral fidelity: does a verifier with higher CIS produce better agent behavior, or is the embedding structure an epiphenomenon?

Additionally, the CIS analysis excludes Power and Security dimensions due to unstable representation (noun probability exceeding 10%). This is a reasonable exclusion with justification, but it means the "recovered circular structure" is demonstrated for 8 of 10 dimensions — an important caveat that the main text's claim of circular structure recovery glosses over.

**Central Claim 5: Behavioral fidelity shows an inverted-U relationship with inference intensity, mirroring bounded rationality.** This claim is **suggestively supported but weakly evidenced.** Figure 4 shows the saturation pattern for sentiment accuracy in the media domain, but no numerical values are quoted, no other metrics or domains are shown, and no statistical analysis is provided. The claim that performance "declines" beyond 4–5 rounds is stronger than what Figure 4 visibly shows — the figure appears to show plateauing rather than clear decline. The paper's interpretation through bounded rationality is interesting but speculative without stronger evidence that the effect is robust across domains and metrics. This claim would need a dedicated experiment varying inference intensity across multiple domains, reporting confidence intervals, and ideally comparing against a computational model of bounded rationality to move from observation to demonstrated alignment.

**What experiments would have strengthened the paper:**

1. **A "same generator, different verifiers" experiment** comparing the CVA Value Verifier against (a) a simple cosine-similarity verifier between action and value embeddings, (b) an LLM-as-judge verifier (GPT-4o evaluating value alignment), and (c) no verifier. This would isolate the specific contribution of the cross-attention value-activation mechanism vs. simpler value-scoring approaches and vs. the self-referential LLM-as-judge approach the paper criticizes.

2. **A base model ablation** running the same prompt-driven reasoning baselines with Qwen2.5-7B (instead of GPT-4o-mini) to establish whether the polarization effect is model-specific or general. If Qwen2.5-7B shows the same pattern, the architectural critique is stronger; if it doesn't, the critique may be specific to GPT-4o-mini's training.

3. **A data quantity sweep for verifier training** to establish how much ground-truth preference data is needed for the verifier to be effective. The paper reports using 5 candidates per context for verifier training — is this sufficient, or would performance improve with more preference pairs? This is practically important for domains where behavioral data is scarce.

4. **An out-of-distribution evaluation** testing CVA agents on users from an entirely different dataset not used in training, to assess whether the value-conditioned policy generalizes to new populations or overfits to CVABench's specific user distribution.

5. **Statistical significance reporting** for all main results, with confidence intervals or standard errors. Without these, we cannot assess whether the numerical differences between methods (often 0.02–0.05 on accuracy metrics with sample sizes of ~500 per domain) are reliable or noise.

**Summary of experimental rigor:**

The paper's strongest experimental contribution is the systematic documentation of value polarization under prompt-driven reasoning (Section 3.1, Appendix C), which is thorough, multi-dimensional, and quantified with clear metrics. The CVA architecture's performance (Table 3) provides consistent evidence of improvement over baselines, with the Var% metric particularly compelling as a direct measure of the polarization problem the paper identifies.

The weakest aspects are: (a) absence of statistical rigor throughout, (b) the confounded base model comparison (GPT-4o-mini vs. Qwen2.5-7B), (c) incomplete ablation design (no "SFT+Verifier without DPO" condition, no simpler verifier baselines), (d) thin reporting of the inference intensity result (one metric, one domain, no numerical values), and (e) no out-of-distribution or cross-model generalization tests. The paper's theoretical claims about *why* the architecture works (decoupling breaking self-referential loops, emergent value structure, bounded rationality) are supported more by post-hoc interpretation than by targeted experimental isolation of mechanisms.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Overhead Makes the `4×` Efficiency Claim an Upper Bound, Not a Realized Gain

The CVA architecture's headline advantage—near-perfect population-level value alignment (Var% of +1.06%, Table 3)—is achieved only after the system has access to value profiles `V` for every user. These value profiles are not free: they come from GPV (Ye et al., 2025), which requires processing each user's behavioral logs to infer a 10-dimensional Schwartz value vector. The paper does not account for this cost in any of its efficiency or performance calculations. Section 2.1 defines `V` as a "dynamic activation vector" provided to the agent; Section 2.4 notes that GPV is used "to profile these values" from behavioral logs—but the cost of running GPV on the full behavioral history of every user to be simulated is never quantified, amortized, or even discussed as an overhead.

**The consequence** is that the paper's central quantitative claim—that CVA achieves Var% of +1.06% while baselines range from –40.74% to +27.98%—represents performance *conditional on having accurate value profiles*. In a realistic deployment where value profiles must be inferred from limited data (or from no data at all for new users), the actual alignment could be substantially worse. This is directly analogous to the difficulty estimation problem in the reference paper, where generating 2048 samples per question to estimate difficulty consumed more compute than the test-time strategies being optimized. The reference paper authors explicitly flagged this: "our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity." This paper does not even flag it.

Furthermore, GPV's inference quality depends on having sufficient behavioral data. For a new user with sparse history, GPV's value estimates may be noisy or biased. The paper does not evaluate CVA performance as a function of value profile accuracy—no experiment perturbs `V` with noise or uses degraded value estimates from shorter behavioral histories. This means we do not know the robustness curve: how quickly does performance degrade as value estimates become less reliable?

**Evidence in the paper:** The cost of value profiling is entirely unmentioned. GPV is cited (Ye et al., 2025) for its measurement validity, not for its computational cost or data requirements. The training pipeline assumes access to GPV-inferred values for all 15,571 users (Section 2.4). The evaluation uses GPV-inferred values for the held-out 10% of users. No experiment tests performance with alternative, cheaper value measurement methods or with fewer behavioral samples per user.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, propose cheaper value estimation methods, or analyze the sensitivity of results to value measurement quality. The interpretability analysis in Section 3.4 and Appendix E provides qualitative evidence that the verifier's value representations are psychologically meaningful, but this does not address the practical question of how to obtain those value profiles at scale for new users. The paper's Limitations section (Section 6 of the paper itself) mentions that "characterizing complex internal values... is inherently challenging and rarely free of bias," but frames this as a measurement validity concern rather than a cost concern, and argues that GPV is sufficiently reliable—without addressing the overhead of deploying GPV in a production agent system.

### The Architectural Separation Claim Is Not Isolated Experimentally—We Don't Know Whether Decoupling or Empirical Grounding Drives the Gains

The paper's central architectural thesis is that decoupling action generation from cognitive reasoning "breaks the self-referential bias loop" that causes polarization in prompt-driven agents. The CVA architecture instantiates this decoupling in two ways simultaneously: (1) the verifier is a *separate model* from the generator (architectural decoupling), and (2) the verifier is trained on *ground-truth behavioral preferences* rather than on the generator's own value judgments (empirical grounding). The paper treats these as a package—the Value Verifier is both separate and empirically trained—but they are distinct mechanisms, and experiments do not disentangle which one matters.

**The consequence** is that we cannot attribute CVA's improvements to the specific mechanism the paper emphasizes. It is possible that *empirical grounding alone* would suffice: an integrated model trained with a value-alignment head on ground-truth preferences might achieve similar performance without architectural separation. Conversely, it is possible that *architectural separation alone* would suffice: a separate verifier trained to predict value alignment via self-supervision (without ground-truth preferences) might also reduce polarization. The paper's theoretical framing strongly emphasizes the self-referential loop as the core problem, but the experiments do not test this causal claim directly. A practitioner wanting to apply the CVA principle in a different domain would not know which component to prioritize—build a separate verifier, or focus on collecting ground-truth preference data?

**Evidence in the paper:** Table 4 shows the incremental contribution of each component: Raw Model → +SFT → +DPO → +Verifier Reasoning. The verifier adds 0.04 to Rating Accuracy and 0.03 to Sentiment Accuracy above the SFT+DPO baseline. This demonstrates that the verifier *helps*, but does not distinguish between the two mechanisms of decoupling. No ablation tests a "generator with integrated value-evaluation head trained on ground-truth preferences" (empirical grounding without architectural separation) or a "separate verifier trained via LLM-as-judge self-supervision" (architectural separation without empirical grounding). The SFT+DPO baseline (without any verifier) is as close as the paper comes to testing decoupling, but this removes evaluation entirely rather than comparing different evaluation architectures.

**Mitigation status:** Partially addressed through the paper's theoretical argument (Section 2.2, the "cognitive distortion" analysis), but not experimentally. The prompt-driven reasoning baselines (Section 3.1) demonstrate that integrated generation-and-evaluation *without* empirical grounding fails badly—the same LLM both proposes actions and judges their value alignment, and polarization results. This provides evidence that the integrated, non-empirical approach fails, but does not constitute a controlled comparison between the two decoupling mechanisms.

A more complete test would require baselines like: (a) generator with a value-classification head trained on GPV labels (integrated, empirically grounded), (b) separate verifier trained on LLM-as-judge value alignment scores (decoupled, not empirically grounded), and (c) the existing CVA (decoupled, empirically grounded). Without these, the paper's claims about *why* the architecture works are primarily theoretical rather than empirically demonstrated.

---

### Results Are Demonstrated for a Single Base Model Family on a Single Benchmark—Generalization to Other Models and Domains Is Unverified

All trainable experiments use Qwen2.5-7B as the backbone, and all evaluations use CVABench (Yelp, Reddit, Foursquare). The paper does not report results with any other base model (e.g., Llama-3, Mistral, Gemma) or on any other behavioral domain. This is significant because the paper's diagnostic finding—that prompt-driven reasoning causes value polarization—is demonstrated with GPT-4o-mini (a different model family), suggesting some level of cross-model consistency. But the positive claim—that CVA resolves this polarization—is demonstrated only for Qwen2.5-7B.

**The consequence** is uncertainty about whether CVA's benefits are specific to Qwen2.5-7B's pretraining biases or would generalize. The generator training pipeline (SFT+DPO) is designed to "rectify intrinsic value distortion" (Section 2.3.1) from the base model. Different base models have different pretraining biases—some may stereotype certain value dimensions more than others, some may have better calibration for nuanced behavioral generation out of the box. A base model that already produces reasonably diverse behavior might benefit less from CVA (the SFT+DPO correction would be smaller). Conversely, a base model with more extreme biases might need more aggressive correction than the current pipeline provides. The paper provides no evidence about this sensitivity curve.

Domain generalization is equally uncertain. The three CVABench domains (reviews, comments, check-ins) are all relatively constrained behavioral tasks with structured action spaces (star ratings, text responses, POI categories). It is unclear whether the CVA architecture would transfer to open-ended dialogue, negotiation, creative collaboration, or other domains where actions are less structured and ground-truth similarity is harder to define. The Value Verifier's pairwise ranking training depends on a similarity metric `S(A_i, A_gt)` that compares candidate actions to ground-truth behavior—in domains without clean similarity metrics (e.g., open-ended conversation where many responses could be equally appropriate), constructing preference pairs becomes substantially harder.

**Evidence in the paper:** Section 6 acknowledges this limitation explicitly: "CVABench is currently limited to approximately 15,000 users across three primary domains. We plan to expand this coverage to validate agent generalizability across broader and more nuanced contexts." The paper mentions future domains (consumer behavior, cultural consumption) but does not provide any cross-domain or cross-model evidence in the current work. The prompt-driven reasoning baselines use GPT-4o-mini while CVA uses Qwen2.5-7B (Table 3), which is a confound rather than cross-model validation. The paper does not report whether CVA-trained Qwen2.5-7B would outperform CVA-trained GPT-4o-mini, or whether the polarization effect in prompt-driven reasoning (Section 3.1) would replicate with Qwen2.5-7B as the base.

**Mitigation status:** Partially addressed as acknowledged future work in Section 6. The paper does not provide any within-paper cross-model or cross-domain evidence. The single-model, single-benchmark scope is understandable for a paper introducing both a new architecture and a new evaluation framework—building CVABench and training CVA on it is substantial engineering. However, the strength of the paper's general claims ("the CVA architecture effectively mitigates polarization") would require at minimum a second base model or a second behavioral domain to establish that the effect is not specific to the Qwen2.5-7B + CVABench combination.

---

### The Verifier's Training Depends on Ground-Truth Behavioral Data—The Approach Does Not Extend to Domains Without Clean Correctness Signals

The CVA architecture's core mechanism—the Value Verifier—is trained on pairwise preference data where "preferred" is defined by similarity to ground-truth human behavior (Equation 13, Appendix F.2). For each training context, candidate actions are ranked by linguistic similarity to what a real human actually did, and the verifier learns to prefer actions closer to ground truth. This requires clean, observable ground-truth actions.

**The consequence** is that the CVA architecture, as presented, cannot be directly applied to domains where "correct" behavior is ambiguous, multi-dimensional, or unobservable. Consider an agent designed to provide emotional support in a mental health context: there is no single ground-truth "correct" response, and the quality of a response depends on long-term outcomes that are not captured in behavioral logs. Or consider a creative writing agent: what is the "ground truth" action that a verifier should prefer? The paper's approach implicitly assumes that behavioral fidelity—matching what a specific real person did—is the right objective. This assumption holds for the three CVABench domains (where the task is to simulate specific users' historical behavior patterns) but would not hold for applications where the goal is to produce *appropriate* rather than *historically accurate* behavior, or where the agent should improve upon rather than replicate human decisions.

This is a fundamental scope limitation, not an implementation oversight. It means the CVA architecture is specifically an architecture for **behavioral simulation** (reproducing the distribution of human behavior) rather than for **value-aligned decision-making** (making decisions that reflect human values, which may differ from replicating human behavioral patterns). The paper's framing sometimes blurs this distinction—terms like "human-like agents" and "value-driven" suggest broader applicability than the method actually supports.

**Evidence in the paper:** Section 6 acknowledges that CVABench domains are limited to three specific behavioral contexts. The paper does not discuss what would be required to extend CVA to domains without ground-truth actions. The Value Verifier's training objective (Equation 14) is explicitly a preference-based ranking loss that requires a ground-truth similarity function. The paper does not propose or evaluate alternative verifier training signals (e.g., human preference judgments, LLM-as-judge evaluations, learned reward models) that could substitute when behavioral ground truth is unavailable.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as a fundamental scope constraint—that the method is inherently tied to simulation of observed behavior rather than optimization of value-aligned behavior more broadly. The Limitations section (Section 6 of the paper) focuses on data scale and value measurement bias, not on the architectural assumption of available ground-truth actions. A practitioner wanting to use CVA for an application where the goal is to make *good* decisions rather than *historically accurate* decisions would need to redesign the verifier training pipeline substantially, and the paper provides no guidance for doing so.

### The Population-Level Value Alignment Result (+1.06% Var%) May Be Partially Circular—GPV Is Used for Both Training Signal and Evaluation Metric

The CVA architecture uses GPV-inferred value profiles as input conditioning for the generator (via SFT and DPO training) and the verifier. The evaluation metric—the Var% score that the paper uses to demonstrate CVA's near-perfect value alignment—is also computed using GPV-inferred value profiles (from generated behavioral logs). This creates a potential circularity: GPV is part of both the system being evaluated and the evaluation framework. If GPV has systematic biases in how it maps behavior to value profiles, the CVA architecture might learn to produce behaviors that *GPV rates as value-aligned* rather than behaviors that are *genuinely value-aligned*, and the evaluation would be blind to this distortion because it uses the same GPV tool.

**The consequence** is that the headline +1.06% Var% result could partially reflect CVA learning to satisfy GPV's measurement model rather than achieving genuine population-level value alignment. This is a subtler version of the self-referential bias problem the paper critiques in LLM-as-judge evaluation: the evaluator (GPV) shares structure with the system being evaluated (CVA, which was trained using GPV outputs). The paper argues in Section 6 that this is not the same as the LLM-as-judge problem because "our approach utilizes Ground Truth (GT) as the ultimate supervision signal for the main model"—the verifier is trained on behavioral similarity, not on GPV value consistency. However, the generator's SFT and DPO training *does* use GPV-inferred value profiles as input conditioning, so the generator learns to associate specific value profiles with specific behaviors as measured by GPV. The verifier's cross-attention mechanism learns value representations from data labeled with GPV values. The evaluation then measures whether the generated population's GPV-inferred values match the ground-truth population's GPV-inferred values. While this is not as direct a self-referential loop as LLM-as-judge (where the same model generates and evaluates), it is a form of **measurement-mediated circularity** where GPV serves as both training feature and evaluation rubric.

**Evidence in the paper:** Section 6 of the paper discusses this concern partially: "while we acknowledge that GPV may still contain encoded biases, our framework avoids the 'self-reinforcing' bias loop often observed in 'LLM-as-a-judge' paradigms. In those paradigms, the evaluator's bias can compound the generator's bias. In contrast, our approach utilizes Ground Truth (GT) as the ultimate supervision signal for the main model." This defense applies to the verifier (trained on behavioral similarity to ground truth) but not fully to the generator (trained with GPV values as input features). The paper does not report an evaluation using an alternative value measurement tool (e.g., traditional Schwartz Value Survey questionnaires, or a different LLM-based psychometric tool) to validate that CVA's Var% advantage persists when measured by a different instrument. The original Schwartz values for CVABench users are GPV-inferred, so there is no independent ground-truth value measurement to compare against.

**Mitigation status:** Partially addressed through the verifier's training design—the verifier's pairwise ranking objective uses behavioral similarity, not GPV value consistency, as the preference signal. This means the verifier is not directly optimizing for GPV scores. However, the generator is trained on GPV-labeled data, and the end-to-end evaluation uses GPV. The paper's argument that ground-truth behavior serves as the ultimate supervision signal is valid for the verifier but does not eliminate the measurement circularity in the evaluation framework. An independent value measurement tool or a behavioral metric that does not depend on GPV (e.g., direct comparison of action distributions without value mediation) would strengthen confidence that the Var% result reflects genuine alignment rather than GPV-consistency.

### There Are No Statistical Significance Estimates for Any Reported Result, and the Test Set Is Modest

The paper reports point estimates for all metrics (accuracy, MSE, Var%, TTR Wasserstein distance) without confidence intervals, standard errors, or significance tests. The held-out evaluation set consists of 10% of CVABench users—approximately 1,557 users across all domains, with roughly 500 per domain. Per-user predictions are then aggregated into the metrics reported in Table 3, Table 4, and Figure 4. Without variance estimates, we cannot assess whether the numerical differences between methods—which are often in the range of 0.01–0.05 on accuracy metrics—are statistically reliable or within sampling noise.

**The consequence** is uncertainty about the robustness of every comparative claim in the paper. For example, Table 3 reports CVA Rating Accuracy as 0.47 vs. SFT+DPO at 0.43, a difference of 0.04. With approximately 500 held-out users in the social media domain, and binary/discrete accuracy metrics, a 0.04 difference could be within one standard error depending on per-user variance. The Var% metric of +1.06% for CVA vs. +27.98% for SFT+DPO is a larger effect and more likely to be robust, but still lacks formal quantification of uncertainty. The inference intensity result (Figure 4) is particularly vulnerable—the paper claims a saturation effect beyond 4–5 rounds, but without error bars, the apparent plateau could be consistent with a continuing (noisy) upward trend.

This is not merely a reporting omission—it affects the paper's central contribution. The paper claims to have identified a systematic failure mode (value polarization) and demonstrated that a specific architecture resolves it. If the resolution is +1.06% Var% with a confidence interval of [−5%, +7%], the claim that CVA achieves "near-perfect recovery of population-level value diversity" is weaker than if the interval is [−0.5%, +2.5%]. We simply don't know which is the case.

**Evidence in the paper:** No error bars are present in any figure or table. No mention of standard error, confidence intervals, bootstrap estimates, or significance tests appears anywhere in the paper. The test set size (10% of 15,571 users) is stated in Section 3.2 and Appendix B, but no power analysis or minimum detectable effect calculation is provided.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. Given that LLM generation is inherently stochastic (temperature 0.8 is used for both generator and verifier training), and that per-user behavioral predictions have high variance (real humans are inconsistent), statistical quantification of uncertainty is essential for interpreting the results. The absence is a significant methodological weakness, particularly for a paper whose primary contribution is empirical demonstration of a specific failure mode and its architectural resolution.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper's most significant contribution to the field is **diagnostic rather than architectural**: it provides systematic, quantitative evidence that the dominant paradigm for human-like LLM agents—prompt-driven psychological reasoning—does not just underperform but is **actively counterproductive**, amplifying the very stereotyping it aims to reduce. This is not a claim that current methods could be improved with better prompts; it is a demonstration that adding more of the thing the field has been adding—explicit cognitive reasoning about personas—makes behavioral fidelity strictly worse.

The field's default assumption prior to this work was that if simple persona prompting produces shallow imitation, the solution is deeper cognitive modeling: Chain-of-Thought reasoning about values, iterative self-evaluation, and explicit deliberation over action choices. This assumption is encoded in influential architectures like Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023) and numerous reasoning-augmented role-play systems. The paper's central empirical result—that increasing reasoning rounds from 0 to 8 monotonically collapses population value variance from 107% to 51% of ground truth while shifting means toward extremes (Table 5, Appendix C)—directly contradicts this assumption. The relationship is not "more reasoning → diminishing returns"; it is **"more reasoning → worse fidelity."**

This reframes the research agenda from **improving introspection** to **eliminating the need for introspection**. The paper's proposed solution—decoupling generation from evaluation via a separately trained verifier grounded in empirical preferences—is one architectural instantiation of this principle, but the diagnostic finding is broader than the specific method. It suggests that any architecture where the same model (or same model family) both produces behavior and judges its quality will inherit the self-referential bias loop the paper identifies. This has implications beyond behavioral simulation: self-improving code generation, self-critique pipelines, constitutional AI alignment, and any system relying on LLM self-evaluation are potentially subject to the same pathology. The paper doesn't make this generalization explicitly, but the logic extends naturally.

The paper also resolves a latent tension in the literature. On one side, researchers have reported successful persona-consistent behavior from prompt-driven agents (Park et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). On the other, recent work has documented stereotyping and reduced diversity in LLM-generated personas (Li et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025). The paper's framework reconciles these findings: success has been measured primarily through LLM-as-judge evaluations, which share the generator's biases and endorse stereotypical behavior as high-quality. When evaluation is instead anchored in ground-truth population distributions, the same methods that appeared successful reveal systematic polarization. The conflict was not about whether prompt-driven agents work—it was about what "works" means, and the LLM-as-judge paradigm was structurally incapable of detecting the failure mode.

This diagnostic contribution changes which research directions look promising:

- **More attractive:** training separate verifier models on empirical human preference data; developing value-measurement tools that avoid self-referential bias; studying how pretraining biases manifest in persona-conditioned generation; building evaluation frameworks grounded in observable human behavior rather than model judgment.
- **Less attractive:** incremental improvements to persona prompting templates; adding more reasoning steps to prompt-driven agents; using LLMs to evaluate the behavioral fidelity of other LLMs without empirical ground truth.

The paper's methodological contribution—CVABench as an evaluation framework with ground-truth human distributions and quantitative distributional metrics—is potentially field-shifting in its own right. It provides a template for how to escape the self-referential evaluation trap: anchor evaluation in empirical population data, measure distributional alignment rather than point-estimate accuracy, and use psychometric instruments (like GPV) that are independent of the agent's own value judgments. If adopted broadly, this could raise the evidentiary standard for human-like agent research substantially.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Cross-model replication of the polarization finding.** The paper demonstrates value polarization under prompt-driven reasoning exclusively with GPT-4o-mini on the Reddit conversation domain (Section 3.1). A natural and important follow-up would replicate this experiment across diverse base models (GPT-4, Claude-3.5, Llama-3-70B, Mixtral) and across all three CVABench domains. The critical question: is polarization a universal property of prompt-driven value reasoning in LLMs, or does it vary with model scale, alignment training, or architecture? A finding that larger models polarize *more* (because they have more stereotyped knowledge to draw on) versus *less* (because they have more nuanced representations) would have fundamentally different implications for whether the problem worsens or improves as models scale. The paper's methodology—GPV-based value measurement from behavioral logs, Var% and mean-deviation as distributional metrics—provides a ready-made evaluation protocol. A researcher could run the exact same prompt templates (Appendix B) on new models, generate population-level simulations on the held-out CVABench users, and produce directly comparable polarization curves.

**Integrated-vs-separated verifier ablation to isolate the decoupling mechanism.** The paper attributes CVA's success to breaking the self-referential bias loop, but this claim conflates two distinct mechanisms: architectural separation (different models for generation and evaluation) and empirical grounding (training the verifier on ground-truth behavioral preferences rather than model introspection). A targeted ablation would train three variants: (a) a generator with an integrated value-alignment classification head trained on GPV value labels (empirically grounded, not decoupled), (b) a CVA-style separate verifier trained on LLM-as-judge value assessments rather than ground-truth similarity (decoupled, not empirically grounded), and (c) the existing CVA (both decoupled and empirically grounded). Comparing Var% across these conditions would reveal whether decoupling alone helps (b vs. prompt-driven baselines), whether empirical grounding alone helps (a vs. prompt-driven baselines), and whether the combination is additive or multiplicative (c vs. a and b). The paper's existing infrastructure—the generator training pipeline, the verifier architecture, and the CVABench evaluation—makes this a straightforward, high-impact experiment that would substantially strengthen the paper's theoretical claims.

**Value profile perturbation to characterize robustness to measurement noise.** The CVA architecture assumes access to accurate GPV-inferred value profiles `V` for every user, but in practice, value measurements will be noisy—especially for new users with sparse behavioral history. A critical robustness experiment would systematically perturb the value profiles fed to CVA during inference: add Gaussian noise at varying magnitudes to each Schwartz dimension, use value profiles inferred from progressively smaller subsets of each user's behavioral history (e.g., 10%, 25%, 50% of their data), or replace GPV-inferred values with simpler alternatives (e.g., bag-of-words personality estimates, or random assignment within the user's ground-truth distribution). The dependent variable is the Var% degradation as a function of value profile accuracy. This would produce a **robustness curve** that tells practitioners how much behavioral history they need to collect before CVA becomes useful, and whether the architecture gracefully degrades or catastrophically fails with noisy values. The paper's current results (Var% of +1.06% with full GPV profiles) represent the ceiling; the robustness curve would reveal the floor.

**Out-of-distribution generalization to new user populations.** All CVA training and evaluation uses CVABench users from three specific domains (Yelp reviewers, Reddit commenters, Foursquare users). Would a CVA agent trained on this data generalize to simulating users from a different platform, demographic, or behavioral domain? A strong test would train CVA on CVABench, then evaluate on an entirely held-out dataset—for example, Twitter users, Amazon reviewers, or participants in a lab-based behavioral study with ground-truth action logs. This tests whether the architecture learns general value-action mappings or overfits to CVABench-specific correlations. The key metric would be whether Var% remains near zero on the new population, or whether the value alignment degrades (indicating that the verifier's value-action knowledge is domain-specific). This is particularly important given the paper's claim that the Value Verifier recovers the "theoretical circular structure" of human values (Appendix E)—if that structure is truly universal, it should transfer across domains. If it doesn't, the recovered structure may be an artifact of CVABench's specific user distribution rather than genuine psychometric knowledge.

**Comparisons against simpler verifier architectures to isolate the cross-attention value-activation mechanism.** The Value Verifier's cross-attention mechanism (Equation 12) is designed to model context-dependent value activation—the psychological insight that the same value profile should produce different behavioral implications in different situations. But does this mechanism actually improve behavioral fidelity over simpler alternatives? A targeted architecture ablation would compare the CVA verifier against: (a) a verifier that concatenates context and value embeddings and feeds them through an MLP without cross-attention (no context-dependent activation), (b) a verifier that uses a fixed value weighting (learned once, not context-dependent), and (c) a baseline using cosine similarity between action and value embeddings as the alignment score. If the cross-attention variant substantially outperforms these simplifications, it validates the paper's psychological motivation. If the simpler architectures perform comparably, the verifier's benefit comes primarily from having *some* trained discriminator rather than from the specific value-activation modeling, which would redirect research attention toward verifier training data quality rather than verifier architecture.

**Verifier over-optimization characterization across domains and metrics.** The paper's inference intensity result (Figure 4) shows that performance saturates at 4–5 verifier reasoning rounds for sentiment accuracy in the media domain, but the finding is reported for only one metric in one domain, without numerical values or error bars. A systematic characterization of verifier over-optimization would sweep inference intensity (1 to perhaps 16 rounds) across all three CVABench domains and all metrics (Rating Accuracy, Sentiment Accuracy, Attitude Accuracy, Category Accuracy, MSE), and also report the Var% at each intensity level. This would reveal: (a) whether the saturation point is consistent across domains (suggesting a fundamental property of the verifier) or domain-specific (suggesting it depends on task difficulty or action-space complexity), (b) whether Var% degrades at high intensities even if point-estimate accuracy plateaus (which would indicate that over-optimization causes the verifier to select less diverse candidates, collapsing population variance), and (c) whether the bounded-rationality interpretation—that the optimal evaluation budget matches human cognitive limits—holds up under rigorous measurement. The paper's discussion of bounded rationality is currently more interpretive than empirical; this experiment would provide the evidence to support or refute it.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Population-scale social simulation with calibrated diversity.** The most direct application of CVA is to improve the fidelity of LLM-based social simulations—a growing methodology in computational social science where researchers use agent populations to model phenomena like information spread, opinion dynamics, or policy impacts (Anthis et al., 2025). Current approaches face a fundamental tension: prompt-driven agents with explicit persona descriptions produce stereotyped populations (as the paper demonstrates), while agents without persona conditioning produce overly random behavior. CVA offers a middle path: the population-level value distribution is calibrated to match real human diversity (Var% of +1.06%), while individual agents still exhibit coherent, value-consistent behavior. For a researcher modeling how a public health message might spread through a community, CVA agents would preserve the natural variation in how different value profiles respond to the same message—some people (high Security) might respond to risk-based framing, others (high Self-Direction) to autonomy-based framing—without collapsing into a few rigid archetypes. The practical benefit is simulations that capture distributional effects (not just average effects) with fidelity that has been empirically validated against real human population data.

**Game NPCs and interactive characters with authentic personality variation.** In game development, NPCs are typically hand-scripted with a small number of discrete personality types, producing repetitive interactions that break immersion. CVA provides a blueprint for generating NPC populations where each character exhibits a unique, value-consistent behavior pattern that varies appropriately with context—the exhausted NPC chooses rest, the same NPC well-rested chooses exploration—without requiring manual scripting of every personality-context combination. The paper's multi-domain validation (reviews, conversation, mobility) suggests the approach could apply to NPC dialogue, daily scheduling, and decision-making within a unified value framework. The key advantage over standard LLM role-play is the Var% alignment: a CVA-trained NPC population would have value diversity matching real human populations, avoiding both the "every NPC feels the same" problem (variance collapse) and the "NPCs act randomly" problem (excessive variance). For a studio building a large open-world game with hundreds of NPCs, the architecture offers a way to generate diverse, believable character behaviors without exponential manual content creation.

**User modeling for personalized systems with privacy-preserving value profiles.** The CVA architecture requires value profiles `V` as input, but once trained, these profiles serve as compact behavioral summaries—a 10-dimensional vector that captures broad behavioral tendencies without storing raw interaction data. This has implications for privacy-preserving personalization. A recommendation system or digital assistant could maintain a user's GPV-inferred value profile (derived once from historical data, then stored locally) rather than continuously logging detailed behavioral traces. The CVA generator could then produce personalized responses conditioned on this profile without accessing the raw history. The paper's demonstration that value-conditioned generation achieves competitive individual-level accuracy (Rating Accuracy 0.47, Sentiment Accuracy 0.36 in the media domain, Table 3) suggests that value profiles contain sufficient information to drive personalization. The privacy benefit is that the value profile is a lossy, interpretable compression of behavioral patterns—it captures psychological structure without preserving granular interaction details that could be exploited for re-identification. This application would require additional privacy analysis (how identifiable are individual users from their 10-dimensional value vectors?) that the paper does not provide, but the architectural separation between value profile and raw behavioral data is suggestive.

**Training data generation for value-aligned fine-tuning.** When using LLMs to generate training data for downstream fine-tuning (a common paradigm in instruction tuning and alignment), the diversity and representativeness of the generated data directly impact the fine-tuned model's behavior. A CVA-style generator, conditioned on a diverse set of value profiles, could produce training data that spans the full spectrum of human behavioral diversity rather than concentrating on modal or stereotyped behaviors. For example, a dataset of "how would someone respond to this situation?" examples generated by CVA would include responses reflecting high and low Self-Direction, high and low Conformity, and context-appropriate combinations thereof—not just the stereotypical "Self-Directed person always takes initiative" and "Conformist person always follows rules" patterns that unprompted LLMs produce. The paper's Var% result (+1.06%) provides evidence that CVA's output distribution matches real human diversity, which is exactly the property needed for representative training data. A follow-up experiment could fine-tune a model on CVA-generated behavioral data vs. standard LLM-generated behavioral data and measure whether the downstream model exhibits reduced stereotyping on behavioral simulation tasks—closing the loop from simulation fidelity to training data quality.

### When to Prefer This Method

The CVA architecture requires several prerequisites that not all behavioral simulation or value-alignment projects will satisfy. The paper does not provide an explicit decision framework (unlike the reference paper's "prefer test-time compute when R << 1, prefer pretraining when R >> 1"), but the architectural assumptions imply clear boundary conditions:

- **Prefer CVA when ground-truth behavioral data with observable actions is available for training.** The Value Verifier depends on pairwise preference pairs constructed from similarity to real human actions (Equation 13, Appendix F.2). Without clean behavioral ground truth—as in open-ended creative tasks, long-horizon planning, or subjective quality judgments without clear "correct" answers—the verifier training pipeline cannot be directly applied. Researchers would need to develop alternative preference signals (human ratings, learned reward models) before adopting the architecture.

- **Prefer CVA when value profiles can be reliably measured for the target population.** The generator is explicitly value-conditioned, and both training and inference require GPV-inferred (or equivalent) value vectors. For applications targeting specific known users with sufficient behavioral history, this is feasible. For applications targeting anonymous or first-time users without behavioral history, value profiles would need to be estimated from limited data, inferred from demographics, or elicited interactively—none of which the paper evaluates. The robustness to value measurement noise is unknown.

- **Prefer CVA when population-level distributional alignment matters alongside individual accuracy.** The paper's strongest result is the Var% of +1.06%—near-perfect recovery of population value diversity. If the application values individual prediction accuracy above all else, the SFT+DPO baseline without verifier (Table 3) achieves competitive point-estimate accuracy (Rating 0.43 vs. CVA's 0.47) with a simpler architecture, at the cost of severely distorted population distributions (Var% +27.98%). If the application is a single-user assistant rather than a population simulation, the distributional advantage of CVA may be irrelevant, and the simpler baseline may suffice.

- **Prefer prompt-driven methods when rapid prototyping without training infrastructure is the priority.** The CVA architecture requires SFT, DPO, and verifier training—each involving data preparation, hyperparameter tuning, and GPU compute. For exploratory research or applications where training data is unavailable, the prompt-driven reasoning baselines (despite their polarization problems) can be deployed immediately with any instruction-following LLM. The paper's evidence that these baselines produce caricatured populations (Var% −35% to −41% at higher reasoning strengths, Table 5) serves as a warning about their limitations, but they remain the only viable option when training is infeasible.

- **Prefer CVA when interpretability of value-driven decisions is required.** The paper demonstrates that the Value Verifier's cross-attention weights can be projected to vocabulary space (Figures 6, 10, Appendix E), revealing which specific words triggered which value activations. For applications where stakeholders need to understand *why* an agent made a particular choice—AI-assisted mental health support, educational agents that explain their reasoning, bias auditing of automated decisions—this transparency is a significant advantage over black-box prompt-driven agents where the "reasoning" is generated post-hoc and may not reflect actual decision drivers. No other method in the paper's comparison offers comparable interpretability.
