Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (a Staff Engineer sharing the story behind an infrastructure-leadership opportunity that helped earn him a promotion):

**Taking on the “boring” but impactful work**

In his first year at Instagram, Ryan volunteered to help with his team’s holiday preparations process—the unglamorous, high-stakes work of ensuring no reliability issues during peak holiday usage. “As you can imagine, no one was eager to take holiday oncall shifts so it was easy for me to get involved.” The following year, confident he could drive the entire process for his team, he volunteered again and took full ownership. Because the work was unattractive to most, it was effectively uncontested: “this work was unattractive but impactful, it was mine for the taking.”

Under his leadership, the team had its quietest holiday season yet in terms of SEVs. Ryan is careful to credit his teammates—many contributed to the effort—but he drove and owned the overall preparations. Afterward, he made a point of sharing both the process and the strong results with his broader org. That deliberate communication of success would prove pivotal.

**How visible results create their own opportunities**

At the same time, other areas outside Ryan’s team—across an org of 70+ engineers—suffered poor reliability during the holidays. A year later, his director was looking for someone to prevent a repeat across the entire org. “Guess who he reached out to? Because I had solved a similar problem and had a good track record, I was an obvious choice to ask.” The opportunity arrived not because Ryan had lobbied for it, but because his director already knew about his results. This is his core principle here: **visible results build trust and credibility**. He frames it as increasing your luck—doing good work and making sure people know about it so opportunities start coming to you.

**Scaling through coordination, not domain expertise**

Ryan was handed Staff-level scope for an org-wide infrastructure preparation initiative—despite not being a deep expert in every team’s domain. “I wasn’t an expert in these new areas but I had been around long enough to know who was.” He created a proposal based on his own experience, then ran it by a few domain experts to stress-test it and ensure the plan was sound. Once there was alignment on the high-level direction, he drove a weekly meeting with the relevant people and made sure everything got executed.

The work paid off: no issues during the holiday season, this time for the entire org. The experience cemented a lesson he now champions: **you don’t need to know everything**. If you can build alignment and coordinate work, you can lean on others to fill the gaps—the core skills of a tech lead, which “scale well.” This infrastructure-leadership role was just one small part of his eventual Staff promotion, but it’s a clean demonstration of how unglamorous, high-impact work—paired with visible results and deliberate coordination—can earn trust and unlock scope before you technically “deserve” it on paper.
