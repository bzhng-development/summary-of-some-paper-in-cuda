# GaLore: Memory-Efficient LLM Training by Gradient Low-Rank Projection

**ArXiv:** [2403.03507](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03507)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Gradient Low-Rank Projection (GaLore)**, a memory-efficient training strategy that enables full-parameter learning by projecting weight gradients into a low-rank subspace during optimization — rather than constraining the weight matrices themselves to be low-rank as LoRA does.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Gradient Low-Rank Projection (GaLore)**, a memory-efficient training strategy that enables full-parameter learning by projecting weight gradients into a low-rank subspace during optimization — rather than constraining the weight matrices themselves to be low-rank as LoRA does. Evaluated on pre-training LLaMA 1B and 7B architectures on the C4 dataset and fine-tuning RoBERTa on GLUE tasks, GaLore reduces optimizer state memory by up to 65.5% (from 42GB to roughly 14GB for LLaMA 7B), achieves up to 82.5% reduction when combined with 8-bit optimizers, and matches full-rank training perplexity — for instance, 8-bit GaLore reaches 14.65 perplexity on LLaMA 7B after 19.7B tokens, compared to 14.61 for the 8-bit Adam baseline. The paper demonstrates, for the first time, the feasibility of pre-training a 7B model on a single consumer GPU with 24GB memory (e.g., NVIDIA RTX 4090) without model parallelism, checkpointing, or offloading strategies, establishing that gradient projection can substitute for weight-matrix low-rank approximation across both pre-training and fine-tuning regimes, without requiring the full-rank warmup that ReLoRA demands.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Training Large Models Requires More Memory Than Consumer Hardware Provides

The fundamental bottleneck this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **the memory required to train large language models far exceeds what individual GPUs can hold, and existing solutions either sacrifice model quality or demand expensive distributed infrastructure**. When training a model like LLaMA 7B, the raw weight parameters consume 14GB in BF16, but that is only the beginning. The Adam optimizer — the de facto standard for LLM training — must maintain two additional state tensors per trainable parameter: a first-order momentum estimate and a second-order variance estimate, each the same size as the weight matrix. Together with the gradient itself, this means **the optimizer states and gradients consume roughly 3× more memory than the model weights alone** — 42GB for a 7B model, pushing the total to 58GB (Section 1). This is well beyond the 24GB available on a consumer-grade RTX 4090, or even the 80GB on a high-end A100 when larger batch sizes and activations are accounted for.

This memory wall has two direct consequences that the paper seeks to address:

**First, it gates who can participate in LLM research and deployment.** Pre-training a 7B model at a reasonable scale requires multi-GPU setups with model parallelism or memory offloading — techniques that demand significant engineering expertise and hardware investment. The paper's explicit framing around "consumer GPUs with 24GB memory" (Abstract, Section 5.5) signals that democratization of LLM training is a core motivation. When the barrier to entry involves 8-node clusters with 64 A100 GPUs (Section 5.3), the field narrows to well-resourced industrial labs. Reducing memory consumption to fit within a single consumer GPU would fundamentally change who can build and experiment with large models.

**Second, it forces tradeoffs between memory efficiency and model quality that are poorly characterized.** The practical outcome of the memory wall is that practitioners must choose between approaches that reduce memory but change what the model learns (like LoRA), approaches that maintain full-parameter learning but add engineering complexity (like gradient checkpointing or ZeRO offloading), or simply limiting themselves to smaller models. None of these options is satisfying, and the paper identifies a specific gap: **there is no method that enables full-parameter learning — learning across all weight dimensions — while simultaneously reducing the memory cost of optimizer states to fit within consumer hardware constraints**.

### Why This Problem Is Important Beyond Hardware Constraints

The memory bottleneck is not merely an engineering inconvenience; it has structural consequences for how the field develops and who benefits from it.

**Training dynamics matter, and low-rank weight constraints alter them.** A growing body of evidence, which the paper cites directly (Section 1, Section 2), demonstrates that methods like LoRA — which freeze a pre-trained weight matrix $W_0$ and learn only a low-rank additive adaptor $BA$ — do not achieve the same performance as full-rank fine-tuning (Xia et al., 2024). For pre-training from scratch, the situation is worse: ReLoRA, which periodically merges the low-rank adaptor back into the full weight matrix, still requires a full-rank training warmup before switching to low-rank updates (Lialin et al., 2024). The paper hypothesizes two reasons for this gap: "(1) the optimal weight matrices may not be low-rank, and (2) the reparameterization changes the gradient training dynamics." In other words, **forcing the weight update to live in a low-rank subspace changes not just what the model can represent at convergence, but the path it takes during optimization** — potentially missing solutions or getting stuck in suboptimal regions.

**The distinction between weight low-rankness and gradient low-rankness is critical and underexploited.** This is the paper's central conceptual move. The authors observe that while weight matrices may need to be high-rank to represent complex functions, **gradient matrices can become low-rank during training** — a property that has been studied theoretically and empirically in prior work (Zhao et al., 2022; Cosson et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023) but not systematically exploited for memory reduction in LLM training. If this property holds, then one can compress the optimizer states (which track gradient statistics) without constraining the weight matrices themselves. The memory savings come from the optimizer side, not the parameter side, so the model retains full representational capacity. This is a conceptually elegant insight: **compress what is naturally compressible (the gradient) rather than artificially constraining what may need to be expressive (the weight)**.

**The environmental and resource implications are substantial.** The paper's Impact Statement frames this explicitly: "By enabling the training of larger models on hardware with lower memory, our approach helps to minimize energy consumption and carbon footprint associated with training LLMs." Lower memory requirements translate directly to lower energy consumption per training run, and the ability to train on consumer hardware means fewer dedicated data center resources. This is not merely a convenience argument — it is a sustainability argument that connects individual hardware accessibility to collective environmental impact.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper situates itself within a landscape of several families of memory-reduction techniques, identifying specific limitations in each.

#### Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (LoRA and Variants)

LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) is the dominant approach and the paper's primary point of comparison. Its core idea is elegant: instead of updating a full weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, freeze a pre-trained $W_0$ and learn only a low-rank decomposition $W = W_0 + BA$ where $B \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$, and $r \ll \min(m, n)$. Because only $A$ and $B$ are trainable, the optimizer only needs to store gradient statistics for these small matrices, dramatically reducing memory.

The limitations the paper identifies (Section 1, Section 3.1) are:

1. **Performance gap in fine-tuning.** LoRA is "not shown to reach a comparable performance as full-rank fine-tuning" (Xia et al., 2024). This matters because if LoRA always leaves performance on the table, practitioners must choose between memory savings and model quality.

2. **Inability to pre-train from scratch.** LoRA was designed for fine-tuning where $W_0$ already embodies useful knowledge. For pre-training, $W_0$ is a random initialization, and learning only a low-rank adaptor severely constrains what the model can learn.

3. **Altered training dynamics.** Because the update is reparameterized as $\Delta W = BA$, the gradient flow through this decomposition differs from the gradient flow through a full-rank weight. This is not just a representational constraint — it changes the optimization trajectory itself.

ReLoRA (Lialin et al., 2024) attempts to extend LoRA to pre-training by periodically merging the learned $BA$ back into $W$ and reinitializing the low-rank adaptors. But the paper notes that ReLoRA "requires a full-rank training warmup to achieve comparable performance as the standard baseline" (Section 2). The need for full-rank warmup undermines the memory-saving motivation — if you need full-rank training at the start, the peak memory requirement is not reduced, and the approach is really a low-rank continuation strategy rather than a fundamentally memory-efficient training method.

#### Low-Rank Weight Factorization

An alternative approach is to directly parameterize the weight matrices as low-rank from the beginning: $W = BA$ where $B$ and $A$ are the actual weight parameters (Kamalakara et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023a). This avoids the need to store a separate full-rank $W_0$, achieving genuine memory savings in the parameters themselves. However, the paper's own experiments (Table 2) show this "Low-Rank" approach catastrophically underperforms: a 350M model trained this way reaches perplexity 37.41 compared to 18.80 for full-rank and 18.95 for GaLore. **The weight matrix itself is genuinely not low-rank in a way that supports training from scratch** — a finding that reinforces the paper's central thesis that gradient low-rankness is the right property to exploit, not weight low-rankness.

#### System-Level Optimizations

The paper acknowledges engineering approaches like gradient checkpointing (Chen et al., 2016), which trades computation for memory by recomputing activations during backpropagation rather than storing them, and memory offloading (Rajbhandari et al., 2020), which moves optimizer states to CPU RAM and pages them to GPU as needed. These are complementary to algorithmic approaches like GaLore — the paper explicitly combines GaLore with per-layer weight updates (Section 4.3), an engineering technique — but they come with their own costs. Checkpointing increases computation by 20–30%. Offloading is bottlenecked by PCIe bandwidth and adds latency. Neither fundamentally changes the scaling relationship between model size and optimizer memory; they shift memory to different physical locations or recompute it, but the total working set is still large.

#### Quantized Optimizers

8-bit Adam (Dettmers et al., 2022) and similar approaches reduce memory by storing optimizer states in lower precision. This is effective — the paper uses 8-bit Adam as a baseline and combines it with GaLore — but quantization alone does not change the **dimensionality** of the optimizer states, only the bits per element. The paper's approach is orthogonal and additive: quantization reduces the precision of each stored value, while GaLore reduces the number of values that need to be stored at all by projecting into a lower-dimensional subspace.

#### Processed Gradient Descent and Subspace Learning

The paper also connects its work to a longer tradition of projected gradient descent (PGD) in optimization (Chen & Wainwright, 2015; Chen et al., 2019) and subspace learning, where evidence has accumulated that "learning primarily occurs within a significantly low-dimensional parameter subspace" (Gur-Ari et al., 2018; Larsen et al., 2022). However, these prior works either treated the optimization problem abstractly (PGD in vector spaces) or observed the phenomenon empirically without building a practical memory-reduction system around it for large-scale training. The paper's contribution is bridging this theoretical observation to a concrete, deployable training algorithm for LLMs.

### How This Paper Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

The paper's positioning strategy is to draw a sharp distinction between **what is being compressed** — the weight versus the gradient — and to argue that targeting the gradient is both theoretically justified and empirically superior.

**The key contrast with LoRA is conceptual, not just methodological.** As stated in Section 3.3: "While both GaLore and LoRA have 'low-rank' in their names, they follow very different training trajectories." The paper provides a limiting-case argument to make this concrete: "when $r = \min(m, n)$, GaLore with $\rho_t \equiv 1$ follows the exact training trajectory of the original model, as $\tilde{G}_t = P_t P_t^\top G_t Q_t Q_t^\top = G_t$." In contrast, "when $BA$ reaches full rank (i.e., $B \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$), optimizing $B$ and $A$ simultaneously follows a very different training trajectory compared to the original model." This means GaLore has a natural "full-rank recovery" property that LoRA lacks — as the rank $r$ approaches the original dimensions, GaLore smoothly interpolates to standard training, while LoRA remains a fundamentally different optimization problem.

**The paper introduces gradient low-rankness as a principled memory-reduction target.** Prior work had observed that gradients become low-rank during training (Zhao et al., 2022; Cosson et al., 2023) and exploited this for communication compression in distributed settings (Wang et al., 2018; Vogels et al., 2020). But using this property to compress optimizer states for memory reduction — and doing so at the scale of 7B parameter models — is novel. The paper provides both theoretical justification (Theorem 3.2, Lemma 3.3) and practical algorithms (Algorithm 2) for this insight.

**GaLore is positioned as orthogonal to and compatible with existing memory-saving techniques.** The paper is careful to present GaLore not as a replacement for quantization, checkpointing, or per-layer updates, but as a complementary axis of optimization. Section 4.3 demonstrates this explicitly by combining GaLore with 8-bit Adam and per-layer weight updates. This modularity is a practical strength: practitioners can stack GaLore on top of their existing memory optimization toolkit.

**The paper claims a novel "full-stack" capability across pre-training and fine-tuning.** Unlike LoRA (fine-tuning only) or ReLoRA (pre-training with warmup), GaLore is presented as a single method that works for both pre-training from scratch and fine-tuning pre-trained models, with consistent or better performance and lower memory in both regimes (Tables 2 and 4). This unification is valuable because it simplifies the practitioner's decision: one approach for all training scenarios rather than switching between LoRA for fine-tuning and something else for pre-training.

### The Gap: No Method for Full-Parameter Learning with Compressed Optimizer States

Synthesizing the above, the paper's identified gap is:

> **There exists no training algorithm that enables full-parameter learning — where all weight dimensions are updated and the training dynamics are preserved — while simultaneously reducing the memory cost of optimizer states to levels that fit within consumer GPU memory constraints.**

LoRA and its variants achieve memory reduction but constrain parameter search to a low-rank subspace and alter training dynamics. ReLoRA attempts pre-training but requires full-rank warmup, negating the peak memory benefit. System-level approaches shift memory around but don't reduce the fundamental scaling. Quantization reduces bits per element but not the number of elements. GaLore is proposed as the first method that exploits the natural low-rank structure of the gradient during training to compress optimizer states while leaving the weight matrices and training dynamics intact — achieving memory savings on the optimizer side without constraining the model's representational capacity on the parameter side. The paper's theoretical analysis (Section 3.2) provides justification for why gradients become low-rank in the specific architectures used for LLMs, and its empirical results (Section 5) demonstrate that this approach scales to 7B parameter models with training runs of up to 19.7B tokens.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper is primarily a **training algorithm design paper** whose core idea is that instead of constraining weight matrices to be low-rank (which limits model expressivity), one can project the naturally-occurring low-rank **gradient** into a compact subspace and run the optimizer in that smaller space — thereby achieving full-parameter learning with dramatically reduced optimizer state memory, without changing the training dynamics. The system being built is a **memory-efficient training framework** that can be inserted into any existing optimizer (AdamW, Adafactor, 8-bit Adam) with two lines of code, solving the problem of VRAM constraints making large-model training inaccessible on consumer hardware.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The GaLore system has five major components that interact during every training step:

1. **Base Model Weights** — stored in full precision (BF16), occupying their standard memory footprint. These weights are **not factorized or constrained**; they remain full-rank throughout training.

2. **Gradient Projector** — a mechanism that, at configurable intervals, computes the SVD of the current weight gradient $G_t$ to identify its dominant left-singular vectors, producing a projection matrix $P_t$ (or $Q_t$, depending on which dimension is smaller) that maps the gradient from the original $m \times n$ space into a compact $m \times r$ (or $r \times n$) space.

3. **Compact Optimizer States** — instead of storing momentum $M$ and variance $V$ as $m \times n$ tensors, stores them as smaller tensors (e.g., $n \times r$), tracking the optimizer statistics of the **projected** gradient $R_t = P_t^\top G_t$ rather than the full gradient $G_t$ itself.

4. **Update Projector** — the inverse operation: after the optimizer computes an update in the compact space, it projects back to the original space via multiplication with the stored projection matrix $P_t$ (or $Q_t$) to produce the weight update $\tilde{G}_t$ that is added to the full-rank weight matrix.

5. **Subspace Switcher** — a periodic re-computation of the projection matrices (every $T$ steps) that allows the training trajectory to traverse multiple low-rank subspaces over time, preventing the weights from being confined to a single low-rank subspace and enabling true full-parameter learning.

Information flows as follows: forward pass computes loss → backward pass produces gradient $G_t$ → every $T$ steps, SVD is applied to $G_t$ to compute new projection matrices $P_t$ → gradient is projected to compact form $R_t = P_t^\top G_t$ → existing optimizer (e.g., Adam) updates compact momentum and variance tensors $M_t, V_t$ → optimizer produces compact update $N_t = M_t / (\sqrt{V_t} + \epsilon)$ → update is projected back $\tilde{G}_t = \alpha \cdot P_t N_t$ → added to full-rank weights $W_t = W_{t-1} + \eta \cdot \tilde{G}_t$. Note that the full-rank gradient $G_t$ is never stored in optimizer state memory; only its compact projection $R_t$ persists.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal theoretical justification — Theorem 3.2 (gradient form for reversible networks), Lemma 3.3 and its corollaries (why this gradient form becomes low-rank during training), and Theorem 3.8 (convergence guarantees with fixed projections) — since these establish *why* gradient projection is a principled approach rather than a heuristic hack.

- **Second**, the core GaLore algorithm (Definition 3.6 and Algorithm 2) — the exact sequence of operations during a training step, including when SVD is performed, how projection matrices are reused between subspace updates, and how optimizer states are maintained in the compact space.

- **Third**, the memory accounting — a detailed walkthrough of where the memory savings come from, comparing GaLore's $(mn + mr + 2nr)$ memory to LoRA's $(mn + 3mr + 3nr)$, and explaining why GaLore doesn't need to store a separate low-rank factorization.

- **Fourth**, the composition of subspaces mechanism (Section 4.1) — why a single low-rank subspace is insufficient for entire training runs, how periodic subspace switching achieves full-parameter learning, and the hyperparameter $T$ that controls switching frequency.

- **Fifth**, the integration with complementary memory-saving techniques (Sections 4.2–4.3) — per-layer weight updates, 8-bit optimizer quantization, and the design choice to project only on the smaller dimension.

- **Sixth**, the hyperparameter discussion — the rank $r$, scale factor $\alpha$, and subspace change frequency $T$, including why $\alpha$ does not depend on $r$ (unlike LoRA's $\alpha/r$ scaling).

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **training algorithm design paper with a strong theoretical foundation** whose central insight is that the memory bottleneck in LLM training is dominated by optimizer states that track gradient statistics, and that these can be compressed if the gradient matrix itself is low-rank — a property the paper proves holds for the specific gradient forms arising in deep network training. Rather than constraining the model's representational capacity (as LoRA does by freezing $W_0$), GaLore constrains only the optimizer's working memory, leaving the weight update trajectory identical to full-rank training in the limit $r \to \min(m, n)$.

#### The Mathematical Foundation: When and Why Gradients Become Low-Rank

The paper does not simply assert that gradients are low-rank — it provides a theoretical framework identifying the **specific gradient forms** under which this property emerges, and then shows these forms arise in practice for the architectures used in LLM training.

##### Reversible Networks and the Gradient Form

The theory begins with the concept of a **reversible network** (Definition 3.1 from Tian et al., 2020). A network $N$ mapping input $x$ to output $y = N(x)$ is "reversible" if there exists an operator $L(x; W)$ — which depends on the input $x$ and the network's weights $W$ — such that the forward pass can be written as a linear transformation of the input: $y = L(x; W)x$. Moreover, the backpropagated gradient $g_x$ at the input must satisfy the transposed relationship: $g_x = L^\top(x; W)g_y$, where $g_y$ is the gradient arriving at the output.

This definition is not as restrictive as it sounds. The paper notes (Appendix B.1) that **biased linear layers, ReLU activations, leaky ReLU, and polynomial activations are all reversible**, and crucially, compositions and parallel combinations of reversible networks remain reversible. This means that a ResNet architecture $x + N(x)$, where $N$ contains bias-free linear layers and reversible activations, falls within this framework. For transformers, the paper uses a separate analysis (Appendix B.3 via the JoMA framework, Tian et al., 2024) for the feed-forward network weight, since self-attention is not directly covered by the reversible network formulation.

For a **chained reversible network** — where $N(x) = N_L(N_{L-1}(\ldots N_1(x)))$ — Theorem 3.2 derives the exact form of the gradient $G_l$ of the weight matrix $W_l$ at layer $l$, for batch size 1, under two common loss functions:

**Case (a): $\ell_2$ objective.** For squared-error loss $\phi = \frac{1}{2}\|y - f_L\|_2^2$ between the network output $f_L$ and a target $y$:

$$G_l = \left(J_l^\top y - J_l^\top J_l W_l f_{l-1}\right) f_{l-1}^\top$$

where $J_l$ is defined as the product of Jacobians from the output back to layer $l+1$: $J_l := \text{Jacobian}(N_L) \ldots \text{Jacobian}(N_{l+1})$, and $f_{l-1}$ is the activation output of the previous layer: $f_{l-1} := N_{l-1}(\ldots N_1(x))$.

**What this computes:** For each training example, the gradient $G_l$ is a matrix of shape $m \times n$ (where $m$ is the output dimension of layer $l$ and $n$ is the input dimension). The first term $J_l^\top y f_{l-1}^\top$ captures the "signal" — the outer product of the backpropagated target direction and the layer input. The second term $J_l^\top J_l W_l f_{l-1} f_{l-1}^\top$ captures the "current prediction error" — how the network's current output projected through the Jacobian chain interacts with the weight matrix and input. The key structural property is that the gradient is a **sum of outer products**, specifically of the form $A - B W_l C$ where $A$ and $C$ depend on the input $f_{l-1}$ and $B$ depends on the Jacobian product.

**Case (b): K-way logsoftmax loss.** For classification with small logits $\|P_1^\perp f_L\|_\infty \ll \sqrt{K}$, where $P_1^\perp = I - \frac{1}{K}11^\top$ is the zero-mean projection matrix:

$$G_l = \left(J_l P_1^\perp y - \gamma K^{-1} J_l^\top P_1^\perp J_l W_l f_{l-1}\right) f_{l-1}^\top$$

where $\gamma \approx 1$ is a scaling factor, and $y$ is a one-hot encoded label with $y^\top 1 = 1$.

**What this computes:** The same structural form $A - B W_l C$, but now the backpropagated signal passes through the zero-mean projection $P_1^\perp$, which centers the logits before computing the softmax gradient. The factor $\gamma K^{-1}$ appears from the Taylor expansion of the log-softmax denominator (derived in Lemma B.2).

**Why this form matters:** Both cases produce a gradient that can be written as:

$$G_t = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N (A_i - B_i W_t C_i)$$

for batch size $N$, where $A_i$ is an input-dependent matrix, and $B_i, C_i$ are **positive semi-definite (PSD) matrices**. The outer-product-with-PSD structure is the critical property that enables the low-rank analysis that follows. If the gradient were an arbitrary matrix, there would be no reason to expect low-rank structure. The specific form $B_i W_t C_i$ — with PSD $B_i$ and $C_i$ sandwiching the weight matrix — emerges naturally from the chain rule in reversible networks because each Jacobian product produces PSD-like terms when squared (e.g., $J_l^\top J_l$ is always PSD).

##### Why This Gradient Form Becomes Low-Rank During Training

Lemma 3.3 is the central theoretical result explaining **when and how fast** the gradient becomes low-rank. The setup considers vanilla SGD weight updates $W_t = W_{t-1} + \eta G_{t-1}$ and assumes that after some time $t \geq t_0$, the matrices $A_i$, $B_i$, and $C_i$ in the gradient form remain approximately constant.

Define the operator $S$ as:

$$S := \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N C_i \otimes B_i$$

where $\otimes$ is the Kronecker product, making $S$ an $mn \times mn$ PSD matrix.

Let $\lambda_1 < \lambda_2$ be the two smallest distinct eigenvalues of $S$, and let $V_1$ be the eigenspace corresponding to $\lambda_1$. The **stable rank** of a matrix $G$ is defined as:

$$\text{sr}(G) := \frac{\|G\|_F^2}{\|G\|_2^2}$$

where $\|\cdot\|_F$ is the Frobenius norm (root sum of squared singular values) and $\|\cdot\|_2$ is the spectral norm (largest singular value). The stable rank is a continuous relaxation of the discrete rank: a rank-1 matrix with one dominant singular value has stable rank $\approx 1$, while a matrix with many equal-magnitude singular values has stable rank equal to its actual rank.

Let $G_{t_0}^\parallel$ be the projection of the gradient at time $t_0$ onto the minimal eigenspace $V_1$ of $S$. Then Lemma 3.3 bounds the stable rank at time $t$:

$$\text{sr}(G_t) \leq \text{sr}(G_{t_0}^\parallel) + \left(\frac{1 - \eta\lambda_2}{1 - \eta\lambda_1}\right)^{2(t - t_0)} \frac{\|G_0 - G_{t_0}^\parallel\|_F^2}{\|G_{t_0}^\parallel\|_2^2}$$

**What this inequality means in operational terms:** The stable rank of the gradient at time $t$ is bounded by two terms. The first term $\text{sr}(G_{t_0}^\parallel)$ is the stable rank of the component of the gradient that lies in the eigenspace of the smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ — this sets the asymptotic stable rank. The second term decays exponentially to zero at a rate determined by the ratio $(1 - \eta\lambda_2)/(1 - \eta\lambda_1)$. Since $\lambda_1 < \lambda_2$ by assumption, this ratio is strictly less than 1, so the second term vanishes as $t$ grows.

**Why this guarantees low-rank gradients:** The dynamics of SGD on this specific gradient form cause the components of the gradient corresponding to **larger** eigenvalues of $S$ to decay faster than the components corresponding to the **smallest** eigenvalue $\lambda_1$. Intuitively, $S$ acts as a "blending operator" that mixes gradient directions, and directions with larger eigenvalues get neutralized more quickly by the weight updates. Over time, the gradient becomes concentrated in the low-dimensional eigenspace $V_1$, which is associated with the slowest-decaying eigenvalue $\lambda_1$. The stable rank converges to $\text{sr}(G_{t_0}^\parallel)$, which is exactly the dimension of the subspace in which the gradient lives at convergence.

##### How Small Is the Asymptotic Rank?

The paper provides two corollaries that estimate $\text{sr}(G_{t_0}^\parallel)$ — i.e., how low-rank the gradient ultimately becomes.

**Corollary 3.4 (Effect of limited feature diversity).** If the gradient takes the slightly more specific form $G_t = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N (a_i - B_i W_t f_i) f_i^\top$ (where $C_i = f_i f_i^\top$ is a rank-1 outer product of the previous layer's activation for example $i$), all $B_i$ are full-rank, and $N' := \text{rank}(\{f_i\}_{i=1}^N) < n$ (the set of activation vectors does not span the full input space), then $\text{sr}(G_{t_0}^\parallel) \leq n - N'$, and for large $t$, $\text{sr}(G_t) \leq n/2$.

**Operational meaning:** If the batch contains activations that only span an $N'$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (which is common when the batch size is smaller than the hidden dimension, or when activations are correlated), the gradient's asymptotic stable rank is bounded by the dimension of the **unspanned** subspace $n - N'$. This makes intuitive sense: gradient updates can only occur in directions that are "excited" by the data; directions orthogonal to all observed activations receive no gradient signal.

The result $\text{sr}(G_t) \leq n/2$ for large $t$ comes from the observation that the gradient can also be written as a sum of at most $N'$ rank-1 matrices (one per linearly independent activation direction), so its rank is simultaneously bounded by $N'$. Taking the minimum of $n - N'$ and $N'$, the worst case is when $N' = n/2$, giving the $n/2$ bound. In practice, if $N'$ is small (limited feature diversity), the gradient is very low-rank; if $N'$ is large, the null space $V_1$ is correspondingly smaller, and the gradient still becomes low-rank.

**Corollary 3.5 (Rank-1 gradient special case).** If the minimal eigenspace $V_1$ of $S$ is 1-dimensional and its eigenvector $v$ decomposes as a Kronecker product $v = y \otimes z$ (for some $y \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and $z \in \mathbb{R}^n$), then $\text{sr}(G_{t_0}^\parallel) = 1$ and the gradient becomes **rank-1** in the limit.

**Operational meaning:** Under favorable structural conditions — a non-degenerate smallest eigenvalue with a decomposable eigenvector — the entire gradient collapses to a single outer product direction. This is the strongest possible low-rank guarantee and suggests that, for certain network configurations, the effective dimension of the optimization problem is dramatically smaller than the parameter count.

##### Handling Transformers: Why the Feed-Forward Network Gradient Is Low-Rank

Since transformers (with self-attention) are not strictly reversible networks, the paper provides a separate analysis for the FFN weight in Appendix B.3 using the **JoMA framework** (Tian et al., 2024). The analysis focuses on the **project-up** weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ in the feed-forward network, where $m$ is the embedding dimension and $n$ is the hidden dimension of the FFN.

Under the assumptions of JoMA — fixed column-orthonormal embedding matrix $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times M}$ (where $M$ is the vocabulary size), linear activations, and stationary backpropagated gradients — the transformed weight $V := U^\top W \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times n}$ follows the dynamics:

$$\dot{V} = \frac{1}{A} \text{diag}\left(\exp\left(\frac{V \circ V}{2}\right) \mathbf{1}\right) \Delta$$

where $A$ is the softmax normalization factor, $\circ$ is the Hadamard (element-wise) product, $V \circ V$ is element-wise squaring, $\mathbf{1}$ is an all-ones vector, and $\Delta \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times n}$ is a matrix of stationary gradient signals (each row $\mu_l^\top$ is the expected backpropagated gradient for a specific vocabulary token $l$).

**What this computes:** Each row of the weight matrix $V$ (corresponding to one vocabulary token) evolves along a **fixed direction** $\mu_l$ that is determined by the stationary gradient signal for that token. The magnitude of each row grows according to the differential equation $\dot{\alpha}_l = \frac{1}{A} \sum_{j=1}^n e^{\alpha_l^2 \mu_{lj}^2}$, meaning rows with larger entries in $\mu_l$ grow exponentially faster than rows with smaller entries.

**Why this produces a low-rank gradient:** The critical consequence is that rows corresponding to tokens with large $\bar{\mu}_l$ (the maximum entry in row $l$) grow rapidly, while rows with small $\bar{\mu}_l$ remain small. As training progresses, the weight matrix $V$ (and hence $W$) becomes dominated by a small number of rows, making it low-rank. Moreover, the gradient $\dot{V}$ is **even more low-rank** than $V$ itself, because the growth rate $\dot{\alpha}_l$ depends on $\alpha_l$ in the exponent — the disparity between fast-growing and slow-growing rows is amplified in the gradient.

This analysis provides a separate, architecture-specific justification for why the FFN gradients in transformers — which constitute a large fraction of the total parameters — should become low-rank during training, complementing the reversible network theory.

##### Convergence: Why GaLore Works with Fixed Projections

Theorem 3.8 provides convergence guarantees for GaLore when the projection matrices $P$ and $Q$ are held constant (i.e., between subspace switches). The setup assumes the same gradient form $G_t = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}(A_i - B_i W_t C_i)$ and that $A_i, B_i, C_i$ have Lipschitz continuity with respect to $W$ with constants $L_A, L_B, L_C$ respectively, and that weights are bounded $\|W_t\| \leq D$.

Let $R_t := P^\top G_t Q$ be the projected gradient, and define the projected versions of $B_i$ and $C_i$: $\hat{B}_i := P^\top B_i(W_t) P$ and $\hat{C}_i := Q^\top C_i(W_t) Q$. Define:

$$\kappa_t := \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \lambda_{\min}(\hat{B}_i) \lambda_{\min}(\hat{C}_i)$$

which is the average product of the minimum eigenvalues of the projected PSD matrices — essentially measuring how much "signal" the projection preserves.

Then for GaLore with $\rho_t \equiv 1$ (no momentum, i.e., SGD-style updates):

$$\|R_t\|_F \leq \left[1 - \eta(\kappa_{t-1} - L_A - L_B L_C D^2)\right] \|R_{t-1}\|_F$$

**What this inequality means:** The norm of the projected gradient at step $t$ is bounded by the norm at step $t-1$ multiplied by a contraction factor. If $\kappa_t > L_A + L_B L_C D^2$ for all $t$, then the contraction factor is strictly less than 1, and $R_t \to 0$, meaning the optimization converges within the projected subspace.

**Why the projection matrices should target the largest eigen-subspaces:** The convergence rate depends on $\kappa_t$, which is the product of the minimum eigenvalues of $\hat{B}_i$ and $\hat{C}_i$. If $P$ and $Q$ project onto subspaces where $\hat{B}_i$ and $\hat{C}_i$ have **large** eigenvalues, then $\kappa_t$ is large, and convergence is fast. If they project onto subspaces where these matrices have near-zero eigenvalues, $\kappa_t$ is small, and convergence can stall. This motivates the choice of $P$ and $Q$ as the **top singular vectors of the gradient $G$ itself**: the dominant singular vectors of $G$ correspond to the directions in which the gradient has the most energy, which empirically correlates with the directions where the projected PSD matrices have large eigenvalues.

**Why this is a meaningful guarantee:** Standard convergence analysis for gradient descent relies on assumptions about the objective function (convexity, smoothness) that do not hold for deep network training. Theorem 3.8 instead exploits the **specific structure of the gradient** to provide a convergence guarantee that holds for this restricted but practically relevant class of problems. It says: if the gradient has the $A - BWC$ form, and you project it into a subspace that captures the dominant eigenmodes of $B$ and $C$, then the optimization will make progress and converge.

#### The Core Algorithm: Gradient Low-Rank Projection

##### Definition and Operation

Definition 3.6 formalizes GaLore as a specific gradient update rule:

$$W_T = W_0 + \eta \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \tilde{G}_t, \quad \tilde{G}_t = P_t \rho_t(P_t^\top G_t Q_t) Q_t^\top$$

where $W_0$ is the initial weight matrix, $\eta$ is the learning rate, $G_t = -\nabla_W \phi_t(W_t)$ is the (negative) gradient matrix at step $t$, $\rho_t$ is any entry-wise gradient regularizer (e.g., Adam, Adafactor), and $P_t \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$, $Q_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ are projection matrices with orthonormal columns ($P_t^\top P_t = I$, $Q_t^\top Q_t = I$).

**What this computes, step by step:**

1. **Project the gradient into the compact space:** $R_t = P_t^\top G_t Q_t$. The full $m \times n$ gradient $G_t$ is multiplied by $P_t^\top$ on the left and $Q_t$ on the right, producing an $r \times r$ matrix $R_t$. This is the "core" of the gradient — the component that lies in the subspace spanned by the columns of $P_t$ and the rows of $Q_t$.

2. **Apply the gradient regularizer in the compact space:** $\rho_t(R_t)$. The optimizer (e.g., Adam) maintains its momentum and variance states as $r \times r$ tensors instead of $m \times n$ tensors, computes the normalized gradient $N_t = M_t / (\sqrt{V_t} + \epsilon)$ entirely in the compact space.

3. **Project the update back to the original space:** $\tilde{G}_t = P_t N_t Q_t^\top$. The compact update $N_t$ is expanded back to an $m \times n$ matrix by multiplication with $P_t$ on the left and $Q_t^\top$ on the right.

4. **Update the full-rank weights:** $W_{t+1} = W_t + \eta \tilde{G}_t$. The weight update is applied to the original full-rank weight matrix.

**Critical property — full-rank recovery:** When $r = \min(m, n)$ and $P_t, Q_t$ are chosen as the full sets of left and right singular vectors of $G_t$, the projection is lossless: $P_t P_t^\top G_t Q_t Q_t^\top = G_t$. In this limit, GaLore with $\rho_t \equiv 1$ (no optimizer state) exactly reproduces standard SGD. This is fundamentally different from LoRA, where even with $r = \min(m, n)$, optimizing $B$ and $A$ simultaneously follows a different optimization trajectory from optimizing $W$ directly, because the gradient of $B$ depends on $A$ and vice versa.

##### Practical Implementation: Projecting on Only One Side

The general definition projects on both left and right, producing an $r \times r$ compact gradient. In practice (Algorithm 2), the paper makes a key simplification: **project on only one side**, specifically the side with the smaller dimension.

For a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with $m \leq n$:

$$R_t = P_t^\top G_t$$

where $P_t \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$ contains the top-$r$ left singular vectors of $G_t$, and $R_t \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ is the projected gradient. The optimizer states $M_t, V_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ track statistics of $R_t^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ (the paper transposes for storage efficiency — storing $n \times r$ instead of $r \times n$ when $n$ is the larger dimension).

The back-projection is:

$$\tilde{G}_t = \alpha \cdot P_t N_t$$

where $N_t \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ is the normalized compact gradient, $\alpha$ is the scale factor (hyperparameter), and $\tilde{G}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is the weight update.

**Why project on only one side?** There are two reasons. First, memory: storing two projection matrices $P$ and $Q$ would require $(m + n)r$ extra memory; storing only one (the side with smaller dimension $m \leq n$) requires $mr$ memory. Second, the "energy" of the gradient is concentrated in the subspace associated with the smaller dimension — compressing along that dimension captures the dominant structure while keeping the other dimension at full resolution.

##### SVD-Based Projection Matrix Construction

The projection matrices are constructed from the **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)** of the gradient:

$$G_t = U S V^\top \approx \sum_{i=1}^r s_i u_i v_i^\top$$

where $U = [u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_m]$, $V = [v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n]$ are orthonormal matrices, $S = \text{diag}(s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_{\min(m,n)})$ contains the singular values in descending order, and the approximation retains only the top-$r$ terms.

The projection matrices are then:

$$P_t = [u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_r] \quad \text{(left projection, used if $m \leq n$)}$$
$$Q_t = [v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_r] \quad \text{(right projection, used if $m > n$)}$$

**What this does:** The SVD identifies the directions in the input and output spaces along which the gradient has the most "energy" (largest singular values). Retaining only the top-$r$ singular vectors means discarding gradient components along directions with small singular values — these directions correspond to dimensions where the gradient is small and optimization would make little progress anyway.

**Why SVD rather than random projections?** Random projections (as in Hao et al., 2024) are computationally cheaper but do not adapt to the gradient's structure — they might project away important gradient directions and retain irrelevant ones. SVD, while more expensive, is optimal in the sense that the rank-$r$ truncated SVD is the best rank-$r$ approximation of $G_t$ in Frobenius norm. This means GaLore retains the directions where the gradient is largest, which Theorem 3.8 suggests leads to faster convergence.

##### Integration with Adam Optimizer

Algorithm 2 shows the complete Adam-with-GaLore procedure for a single weight matrix with $m \leq n$:

1. **Initialize:** First-order momentum $M_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r} \leftarrow 0$, second-order momentum $V_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r} \leftarrow 0$ (note the transposed shape).

2. **At each step $t$:** Compute gradient $G_t \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ from backpropagation.

3. **Periodic SVD:** If $t \bmod T = 0$, compute SVD of $G_t$, set $P_t \leftarrow U[:, :r]$ (first $r$ left singular vectors). Otherwise, $P_t \leftarrow P_{t-1}$ (reuse the projection from the previous subspace).

4. **Project:** $R_t \leftarrow P_t^\top G_t$, producing $R_t \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$.

5. **Adam update in compact space:**
   - $M_t \leftarrow \beta_1 M_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1) R_t^\top$ (track momentum of transposed projected gradient)
   - $V_t \leftarrow \beta_2 V_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2) (R_t^\top)^2$ (track element-wise squared gradient)
   - $\hat{M}_t \leftarrow M_t / (1 - \beta_1^t)$ (bias correction)
   - $\hat{V}_t \leftarrow V_t / (1 - \beta_2^t)$ (bias correction)
   - $N_t^\top \leftarrow \hat{M}_t / (\sqrt{\hat{V}_t} + \epsilon)$ (normalized compact update)
   - (Equivalently, $N_t = \rho_t(R_t)$ where $\rho_t$ is the Adam operation)

6. **Project back:** $\tilde{G}_t \leftarrow \alpha \cdot P_t N_t$, producing the full-size weight update.

7. **Weight update:** $W_t \leftarrow W_{t-1} + \eta \cdot \tilde{G}_t$.

**Key implementation detail — transposed storage:** The optimizer states $M_t$ and $V_t$ are stored as $n \times r$ matrices (not $r \times n$) because $n$ is typically the larger dimension. This means the projected gradient $R_t \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ is transposed to $R_t^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ before being processed by Adam. This choice is purely a memory optimization: the per-element operations in Adam (element-wise multiplication, division, square root) are invariant to transposition, so the only effect is on storage layout.

##### The Scale Factor $\alpha$

The paper introduces a scale factor $\alpha$ that multiplies the projected-update before adding it to the weights. This is analogous to LoRA's $\alpha/r$ scaling, but with a crucial difference: **GaLore's $\alpha$ does not depend on the rank $r$**.

**Why LoRA divides by $r$:** In LoRA, the weight update is $\Delta W = \frac{\alpha}{r} BA$, where $A$ and $B$ are initialized with small random values. As the rank $r$ increases, the number of trainable parameters in $BA$ increases linearly. Without the $1/r$ scaling, the variance of the update would grow with $r$, causing instability. The $1/r$ factor normalizes the update magnitude to be independent of rank.

**Why GaLore does not need this:** In GaLore, the projected gradient $R_t = P_t^\top G_t$ naturally has magnitude that depends on how much gradient energy is captured by the top-$r$ singular vectors. As $r$ increases, the extra singular values are smaller (since they are ordered descending), so the incremental energy added to $R_t$ diminishes. The magnitude of $R_t$ does not grow linearly with $r$; it saturates as $r \to \min(m, n)$. Therefore, $\alpha$ can be chosen independently of $r$.

In practice, the paper uses $\alpha = 0.25$ for all pre-training experiments (Table 2), which effectively means the GaLore update is scaled to 25% of what the Adam-normalized gradient would produce. For fine-tuning, $\alpha$ is tuned per task (e.g., $\alpha = 4$ for rank-4 on most GLUE tasks, Table 7).

#### Memory Accounting: Where the Savings Come From

##### Optimizer State Memory

The fundamental memory savings come from storing optimizer states in the **compact space** rather than the full parameter space. For a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ (with $m \leq n$) and a standard Adam optimizer:

**Full-rank Adam:** Stores $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, $M \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, $V \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, plus the gradient $G \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ during backpropagation. Total memory: $4mn$ elements.

**GaLore:** Stores $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ (full-rank), $M \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$, $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ (compact, transposed), plus the projection matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$. The full gradient $G \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is transient — it exists only during backpropagation and is immediately projected and discarded. Total persistent memory: $mn + 2nr + mr$ elements.

**Numerical example for a typical transformer FFN layer:** Suppose $m = 4096$ (embedding dimension), $n = 11008$ (FFN intermediate dimension for LLaMA 7B). Full-rank Adam stores approximately $4 \times 4096 \times 11008 \approx 180$ million elements per FFN layer. GaLore with $r = 1024$ stores $4096 \times 11008 \approx 45$M (weights) + $2 \times 11008 \times 1024 \approx 22.5$M (optimizer states) + $4096 \times 1024 \approx 4.2$M (projection) = ~71.7M elements — a **60% reduction**.

Across the entire LLaMA 7B model, this translates to reducing optimizer state memory from 42GB (BF16 Adam) to approximately 14GB (GaLore with $r=1024$) — the 65.5% reduction claimed in the abstract.

##### Comparison with LoRA (Table 1)

| | GaLore | LoRA |
|---|---|---|
| **Weights** | $mn$ | $mn + mr + nr$ |
| **Optimizer States** | $mr + 2nr$ | $2mr + 2nr$ |
| **Multi-Subspace** | ✓ | ✗ |
| **Pre-Training** | ✓ | ✗ |
| **Fine-Tuning** | ✓ | ✓ |

**Why GaLore uses less memory than LoRA for weights:** In LoRA, the full-rank weight $W_0$ is frozen but must still be stored ($mn$ memory). Additionally, the low-rank adaptor matrices $B \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ must be stored and trained, adding $mr + nr$ memory. GaLore only stores the full-rank weight $W$ ($mn$ memory) and the projection matrix $P$ ($mr$ memory) — no separate low-rank decomposition.

**Why GaLore uses less memory than LoRA for optimizer states:** LoRA must maintain Adam states for both $B$ (size $m \times r$) and $A$ (size $r \times n$), totaling $2mr + 2nr$ (momentum and variance for each). GaLore maintains Adam states only for the projected gradient $R_t$ (size $r \times n$), using transposed storage, totaling $2nr$. The additional $mr$ in GaLore's optimizer state column comes from the projection matrix — but this is a minor term.

**The practical difference:** For the LLaMA 1B comparison in Table 2, GaLore requires 4.38G total memory (weights + optimizer states) versus 6.17G for LoRA — roughly 30% less. This margin increases with model size because the $2mr$ LoRA optimizer state term scales linearly with both $m$ and $r$, while GaLore's $mr$ term only comes from the projection matrix (not optimizer states).

##### Per-Layer Weight Updates (Reducing Gradient Memory)

In standard training, all layer gradients are accumulated during backpropagation and stored until the optimizer step at the end. For a large model, storing all gradients simultaneously requires memory equal to the model size. The paper adopts **per-layer weight updates** (Section 4.3, citing Lv et al., 2023b): as soon as a layer's gradient is computed during backpropagation, GaLore immediately projects it, computes the optimizer update, applies it to the weights, and discards the full gradient. This means only one layer's gradient exists in memory at any time.

**Memory impact:** For LLaMA 7B, this eliminates the need to store ~14GB of gradient tensors simultaneously, reducing total memory from 58GB to approximately 44GB before applying GaLore's optimizer state compression. Combined with GaLore, total memory drops to ~22GB (as shown in Fig. 4), fitting within the 24GB of an RTX 4090.

**Interaction with GaLore's design:** Per-layer updates are compatible with GaLore because the projection matrix $P$ for each layer is independent — it depends only on that layer's current gradient. The subspace switching still occurs globally (the same $T$ applies to all layers), but the actual projection matrices differ per layer.

##### 8-Bit Optimizer Integration

The paper applies 8-bit quantization (Dettmers et al., 2022) **on top of** GaLore's subspace projection. The 8-bit Adam optimizer stores momentum and variance in 8-bit integer format rather than 32-bit float or 16-bit bfloat. When combined with GaLore, this means the already-compressed $n \times r$ optimizer state tensors are further compressed from 16 bits per element to 8 bits per element.

**Additive savings:** GaLore reduces the **number of elements** in the optimizer states (from $mn$ to $nr$). 8-bit quantization reduces the **bits per element** (from 16 to 8). The two mechanisms are orthogonal and multiplicative: total optimizer state memory = number_of_elements $\times$ bits_per_element.

**Result for LLaMA 7B:** 8-bit GaLore reduces total training memory to 18GB (Fig. 4 and Table 3), enabling pre-training on consumer GPUs.

#### Composition of Low-Rank Subspaces: The Multi-Subspace Mechanism

A single low-rank projection — using one fixed $P$ computed early in training — cannot support full-parameter learning because the weights would only grow in the subspace spanned by the columns of that initial $P$. The paper explicitly addresses this: "In fact, if we keep the same projection $P$ and $Q$, then the learned weights will only grow along these subspaces, which is no longer full-parameter training" (Section 4.1).

##### The Problem: Training Dynamics Drift

The gradient's dominant subspace changes over the course of training because the loss landscape changes as weights evolve. Early in training, the gradient may be dominated by coarse features (learning basic syntax, word frequencies); later, it may be dominated by fine-grained semantic distinctions. A projection matrix computed from the gradient at step 0 would capture only the early-training subspace and would miss directions that become important later.

This is closely related to the theoretical analysis: Theorem 3.8 assumes constant $P$ and $Q$ and proves convergence within that fixed subspace, but it does not guarantee that convergence within the subspace is **sufficient** — the subspace itself might not contain directions relevant to the global optimum. The theorem proves that $R_t \to 0$ (the projected gradient vanishes), but the residual component of the true gradient that is orthogonal to the projection — i.e., $(I - P P^\top) G_t$ — is not accounted for and could be large.

##### The Solution: Periodic Subspace Switching

GaLore periodically recomputes the projection matrices by performing SVD on the **current** gradient:

$$W_t = W_0 + \Delta W_{T_1} + \Delta W_{T_2} + \ldots + \Delta W_{T_n}$$

where each segment $\Delta W_{T_i} = \eta \sum_{t=T_{i-1}}^{T_{i-1}+T-1} \tilde{G}_t$ is the sum of $T$ updates within the $i$-th subspace (characterized by the projection matrix $P_{T_{i-1}}$ computed at the start of that segment).

**What this accomplishes:** Over the course of training, the weight matrix accumulates updates from **multiple low-rank subspaces**. The total update $\Delta W = \sum_i \Delta W_{T_i}$ can span a subspace of dimension up to $\sum_i r_i$, which can be much larger than any individual $r$. If the subspaces are sufficiently diverse (they span different directions), the effective rank of the total update can approach the full rank $\min(m, n)$, enabling true full-parameter learning.

**Figure 2 (described in paper):** The training trajectory is depicted as a path through weight space that traverses multiple low-rank subspaces. The first segment (from $W_0$ to $W_0 + \Delta W_{T_1}$) lies in subspace 1; the second segment (from $W_0 + \Delta W_{T_1}$ to $W_0 + \Delta W_{T_1} + \Delta W_{T_2}$) lies in subspace 2; and so on. The full trajectory is not confined to any single low-rank subspace.

##### The Hyperparameter $T$: Subspace Change Frequency

The frequency $T$ at which the subspace is recomputed is a critical hyperparameter with a trade-off:

**Too frequent ($T$ small):** Computing SVD every few steps adds computational overhead. Moreover, the optimizer states ($M_t, V_t$) accumulate statistics over time, and resetting the projection matrix changes the meaning of these states — the momentum accumulated in previous subspaces may not be aligned with the new subspace directions. Conceptually, very frequent switching violates the assumption of Theorem 3.8 that $P$ and $Q$ are constant, potentially breaking convergence guarantees.

**Too infrequent ($T$ large):** The training spends many steps optimizing in a subspace that may be outdated — the gradient's dominant directions have shifted, but the projection is still targeting the old directions. This wastes optimization steps and can slow convergence. In the extreme, $T = \text{total steps}$ reduces to single-subspace training and fails to achieve full-parameter learning.

**Experimental findings (Fig. 5, left, on 130M models):** The ablation shows a "sweet spot" exists. For a rank $r = 128$, subspace change frequencies of $T = 50$ to $T = 1000$ produce similar validation perplexity, with very frequent changes ($T = 10$) degrading performance and very infrequent changes ($T = 5000$) also degrading somewhat. For $r = 256$, the sensitivity is lower — the larger rank means the subspace already covers more directions, so the penalty for not switching is reduced.

**Default setting:** The paper uses $T = 200$ for all pre-training experiments (Table 2 and Appendix C.1). At this frequency, the amortized cost of SVD is reported as "negligible (< 10%)" compared to other memory-efficient training techniques.

##### What Happens at a Subspace Switch

At step $t$ where $t \bmod T = 0$:

1. **Compute SVD:** $U, S, V \leftarrow \text{SVD}(G_t)$, where $G_t$ is the current gradient.

2. **Update projection matrix:** $P_t \leftarrow U[:, :r]$ (the first $r$ left singular vectors). This replaces the old $P_{t-1}$.

3. **Reset or preserve optimizer states?** The paper does **not** reset the Adam momentum and variance tensors $M_t, V_t$ when switching subspaces. This is a deliberate choice: resetting would discard all accumulated gradient history, which would harm optimization. Instead, the existing $M_{t-1}$ and $V_{t-1}$ continue to be used. However, the next projected gradient $R_t = P_t^\top G_t$ will have a different coordinate basis than the previous $R_{t-1} = P_{t-1}^\top G_{t-1}$, so there is a temporary misalignment between the optimizer states and the new gradient representation. The paper relies on the exponential moving average ($\beta_1 = 0.9, \beta_2 = 0.999$) to gradually adapt the states to the new basis.

**Why not project the optimizer states?** One could imagine projecting $M_{t-1}$ into the new basis: $M_t^{\text{new}} = P_t^\top P_{t-1} M_{t-1}^{\text{old}}$. The paper does not do this, likely because (a) it adds computational overhead, and (b) the optimizer states in the old basis contain momentum along directions that may be orthogonal to the new subspace, and forcing them into the new basis via projection could introduce bias.

#### Design Choices: Why This Approach Over Alternatives?

##### Why Project the Gradient Rather Than the Weight?

This is the paper's central conceptual distinction from LoRA. The weight matrix $W$ may need to be high-rank to represent complex functions (e.g., linear regression with a high-rank true weight matrix). Imposing a low-rank constraint on $W$ (as in LoRA or low-rank factorization) creates a fundamental representational bottleneck — the model simply cannot express the optimal solution.

In contrast, the gradient $G$ becoming low-rank is an **emergent property of the training dynamics**, not an architectural constraint. The paper provides theoretical conditions under which this property holds (Section 3.2, Appendix B), and the optimization still operates on the full-rank weight matrix. If the gradient is genuinely low-rank, then projecting it loses no information (the discarded singular vectors have singular values of zero or near-zero). If the gradient is not perfectly low-rank, the truncation introduces approximation error, but the periodic subspace switching ensures that important directions missed in one subspace can be captured later.

##### Why SVD Rather Than Random Projection?

Random projections (multiplying the gradient by a random Gaussian matrix) are computationally cheaper than SVD and have been used for gradient compression in communication-constrained settings (Hao et al., 2024). However, random projections treat all directions equally, whereas SVD explicitly targets the directions with the largest gradient magnitude. Theorem 3.8 suggests that convergence speed depends on how well the projection captures the dominant eigenmodes of the PSD matrices $B_i$ and $C_i$ — which correlate with the dominant singular vectors of the gradient. SVD is optimal in this sense: the rank-$r$ truncated SVD minimizes the Frobenius norm error $\|G - P P^\top G\|_F$ for any rank-$r$ projection.

The paper acknowledges the SVD overhead and argues it is amortized by the infrequent recomputation ($T = 200$ steps between SVDs). For a $4096 \times 11008$ weight matrix (LLaMA 7B FFN), the SVD cost is roughly $O(mn \cdot r) \approx 4096 \times 11008 \times 1024 \approx 4.6 \times 10^{10}$ operations per SVD, compared to the forward-backward pass cost of $O(\text{batch size} \times \text{sequence length} \times (12n_{\text{layers}} \times d_{\text{model}}^2 + 2n_{\text{layers}} \times d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{FFN}}))$, which is orders of magnitude larger.

##### Why Only One Projection Matrix?

The general definition uses both $P$ (left projection) and $Q$ (right projection), compressing the gradient to an $r \times r$ matrix. The practical implementation projects only on the smaller dimension (using $P$ if $m \leq n$, or $Q$ if $m > n$), compressing to $r \times n$ or $m \times r$.

**Memory argument:** Two projection matrices would require storing both $P$ and $Q$, consuming $(m + n)r$ memory. For a typical LLM layer where $m$ and $n$ are of similar magnitude (e.g., attention layers with $m=n=4096$), this would double the projection matrix storage.

**Information argument:** If $m \leq n$, the gradient $G \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ has at most $m$ non-zero singular values. The right singular vectors beyond the first $m$ correspond to zero singular values. Truncating to rank $r < m$ discards $m - r$ directions. Compressing further on the right side would discard additional information even if those singular values are non-zero, which would be lossy. Compressing only on the smaller dimension captures all the available rank reduction without additional truncation.

##### Why Adam State Storage in Transposed Form?

Storing $M_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ rather than $\mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ is a pure memory optimization for the common case where $n$ (the larger dimension) corresponds to the "features" dimension. The element-wise operations in Adam ($\beta_1 M_{t-1} + (1-\beta_1) G_t$, $\sqrt{V_t}$, division) are pointwise and hence unaffected by transposition. The transposition only affects the layout in memory, which matters for cache efficiency but not for correctness.

**When $m > n$:** In this case, the projection is applied on the right ($R_t = G_t Q_t$, with $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$), producing $R_t \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$. The optimizer states are then stored as $\mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$ (no transposition needed, since $Q$ projects on the smaller dimension).

---

#### Hyperparameters and Their Sensitivities

GaLore introduces only three additional hyperparameters beyond the base optimizer's settings:

**Rank $r$:** The dimensionality of the projected subspace. This is the primary knob controlling the memory-computation trade-off. The paper uses $r$ values of 128, 256, 512, or 1024 depending on model size (Table 2): $r = 128$ for 60M, $r = 256$ for 130M and 350M, $r = 512$ for 1B, and $r = 1024$ for 7B. These are chosen such that $r$ is roughly half the model dimension $d_{\text{model}}$ (e.g., $d_{\text{model}} = 2048$ for 1B, $r = 512 = 2048/4$; $d_{\text{model}} = 4096$ for 7B, $r = 1024 = 4096/4$). The ablation (Fig. 5, right) shows that within a reasonable range, decreasing $r$ slows convergence but "training with a rank of 128 using 80K steps achieves a lower loss than training with a rank of 512 using 20K steps" — meaning smaller $r$ can be compensated by longer training.

**Scale factor $\alpha$:** Controls the magnitude of the projected update relative to what the optimizer would produce in the full space. Default is $\alpha = 0.25$ for pre-training (Section 5, Appendix C.1). The paper notes this can be interpreted as a fractional learning rate: with $\eta = 0.01$ and $\alpha = 0.25$, the effective learning rate for the compact-space update is $0.0025$, which is "still a relatively large stable learning rate compared to the full-rank baseline" (Appendix C.1). For fine-tuning, $\alpha$ is tuned per task and can be larger (e.g., $\alpha = 4$ for GLUE tasks with $r=4$, Table 7), suggesting the fine-tuning regime benefits from stronger low-rank updates relative to pre-training.

**Subspace change frequency $T$:** How often (in steps) the SVD is recomputed. Default is $T = 200$. The ablation (Fig. 5, left) shows robustness across $T \in [50, 1000]$ with slight degradation at extremes.

**Why GaLore is insensitive to these hyperparameters:** The paper suggests that since $\alpha$ is effectively absorbed into the learning rate, and $T$ has a broad acceptable range, the only hyperparameter that requires careful selection is $r$ — and $r$ is shared with LoRA, so practitioners already familiar with LoRA rank tuning can apply similar heuristics. The learning rate for GaLore is the same across model sizes ($\eta = 0.01$), which the paper presents as evidence of stability.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Gradient Low-Rankness as a Memory-Reduction Target, Not Weight Low-Rankness

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is **redirecting the low-rank assumption from where it has universally been applied — the weight matrices — to where it actually holds during training — the gradient matrices**. This is not an incremental refinement of LoRA; it is a category shift in what property we exploit for memory efficiency.

**What the field did before:** The dominant paradigm, instantiated by LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) and its variants, treats the weight update as the object to compress: freeze a pre-trained weight $W_0$ and learn only a low-rank additive correction $BA$. For pre-training, ReLoRA (Lialin et al., 2024) periodically merges $BA$ back into $W$, but still operates on the premise that the *representation itself* should be factorized. The consequence, as the paper documents, is an inherent representational bottleneck — "the optimal weight matrices may not be low-rank" (Section 1) — and altered training dynamics because optimizing through the $BA$ factorization changes the gradient flow compared to optimizing $W$ directly.

**What GaLore does differently:** Instead of constraining what the model can *represent* (weight space), it constrains what the optimizer needs to *remember* (gradient statistics space). The weight matrix $W$ remains full-rank and is updated without reparameterization. Only the gradient $G_t$, which the paper proves becomes low-rank during training (Lemma 3.3, Corollaries 3.4–3.5), is compressed via projection before entering the optimizer's stateful memory. This is the difference between saying "the solution lives in a low-dimensional subspace" versus "the search direction at each step lives in a low-dimensional subspace" — the latter is a much weaker and more plausible assumption, and critically, it does not constrain the final solution.

**Why this is a fundamental shift, not an incremental tweak:** The paper provides a limiting-case argument that crystallizes the distinction (Section 3.3). When $r = \min(m, n)$, GaLore with identity regularizer $\rho_t \equiv 1$ exactly reproduces standard SGD: $P_t P_t^\top G_t Q_t Q_t^\top = G_t$. In the same limit, LoRA with full-rank $B$ and $A$ still follows a fundamentally different optimization trajectory because the gradient with respect to $B$ depends on the current value of $A$ and vice versa — the dynamics are coupled in a way that standard weight-space SGD is not. GaLore has a natural "full-rank recovery" property; LoRA does not. This means GaLore is a **genuine approximation to full-parameter training** that degrades gracefully as $r$ decreases, rather than a fundamentally different optimization problem that happens to share the same loss function.

**Tie to evidence:** Table 2 shows this distinction empirically. The "Low-Rank" approach — factorizing $W = BA$ directly — collapses to perplexity 37.41 on a 350M model, compared to 18.80 for full-rank and 18.95 for GaLore. LoRA, which freezes $W_0$ and learns a low-rank adaptor, reaches 25.58 — better than direct factorization but still far from full-rank performance. GaLore alone reaches 18.95, nearly matching full-rank while using less memory (1.22G vs. 2.06G). The rank used is the same for all methods ($r = 256$ on 350M). The fact that three low-rank methods with identical rank produce radically different results (37.41, 25.58, 18.95) demonstrates that *where* you apply the low-rank constraint matters more than the rank itself.

---

### Innovation 2: Multi-Subspace Composition as a Mechanism for Full-Parameter Learning from Low-Rank Updates

The paper identifies and solves a problem that single-subspace gradient projection creates: if you project gradients into **one** low-rank subspace for the entire training run, the weights only grow in that subspace, and you have not achieved full-parameter training. The solution — periodic recomputation of the projection matrix via SVD on the current gradient — enables the weight trajectory to traverse **multiple distinct low-rank subspaces** over time, so that the accumulated update $\Delta W = \sum_i \Delta W_{T_i}$ can span a space of dimension up to $\sum_i r_i$, potentially approaching full rank.

**What the field did before:** Subspace learning methods (Gur-Ari et al., 2018; Larsen et al., 2022) demonstrated that training occurs in a low-dimensional subspace, but did not develop a training algorithm that exploits this dynamically. LoRA operates in a fixed low-rank subspace defined by the initialization of $A$ and $B$. ReLoRA periodically merges $BA$ into $W$ and reinitializes new adaptors, which is superficially similar to GaLore's subspace switching, but with a critical difference: ReLoRA resets optimizer states and learning rate schedules at each merge, essentially treating the training as a sequence of independent low-rank fine-tuning episodes. GaLore preserves optimizer states across subspace switches, allowing the momentum and variance estimates to carry information across subspace boundaries.

**Why this is conceptually novel:** The composition of subspaces reframes low-rank training from a **static constraint** ("the solution lives in this subspace") to a **dynamic path** ("the search traverses through these subspaces sequentially"). The paper proves (Theorem 3.8) that within any single subspace, GaLore converges — the projected gradient $R_t \to 0$. But it correctly identifies that convergence within a subspace does not imply convergence to a good overall solution, because the residual $(I - P_t P_t^\top) G_t$ is unmodeled. The subspace switching mechanism is what closes this gap: by periodically redirecting the projection to capture the current gradient's dominant directions, the algorithm chases the moving target of what subspace is relevant now.

**The optimizer-state preservation choice is subtle and important:** When GaLore switches subspaces, the Adam momentum $M_t$ and variance $V_t$ are **not reset** — they continue accumulating in the new coordinate basis defined by the new $P_t$. This means there is a brief misalignment (old statistics in new coordinates), but the exponential moving average ($\beta_1 = 0.9$) adapts quickly. Resetting would discard all accumulated gradient history at each switch, which would be closer to ReLoRA's episodic training. The paper's choice to preserve states across switches is what makes the method a single continuous training run rather than a sequence of restarts, and it is justified empirically by the robustness to $T$ across a wide range (50 to 1000 steps, Fig. 5 left).

**Tie to evidence:** The ablation on subspace change frequency $T$ (Fig. 5, left) is the critical evidence for this innovation. With $r=128$, performance is stable across $T \in [50, 1000]$ but degrades at extremes ($T=10$, too frequent, and $T=5000$, too infrequent). The degradation at $T=5000$ confirms the claim that a single subspace is insufficient — waiting too long to switch means optimizing in an outdated subspace. The fact that $T=200$ (the default) works across all model sizes from 60M to 7B (Table 2) shows this mechanism is robust and does not require per-model tuning.

---

### Innovation 3: Theoretical Justification for Gradient Low-Rankness in Specific Network Architectures

The paper does not merely observe that gradients are low-rank — it provides a **constructive theoretical framework** identifying the gradient forms that arise in networks with reversible structure, proving that these forms become low-rank under SGD dynamics, and connecting this to the specific architectures used in LLMs. This transforms gradient low-rankness from an empirical curiosity into a principled basis for algorithm design.

**What the field did before:** Prior work observed gradient low-rankness empirically (Gur-Ari et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2022; Cosson et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023) and exploited it heuristically for communication compression (Wang et al., 2018; Vogels et al., 2020). But the conditions under which this property holds, the rate at which it emerges, and its dependence on architecture were not characterized. Without such characterization, a method built on gradient low-rankness is gambling that the property holds on your specific model and task — it might work, but you don't know why, and you can't predict when it will fail.

**What the paper contributes theoretically:** The chain of results from Theorem 3.2 through Corollary 3.5 provides a specific answer to *why* gradients become low-rank. Theorem 3.2 derives the exact gradient form for reversible networks: $G_l = (J_l^\top y - J_l^\top J_l W_l f_{l-1}) f_{l-1}^\top$ for $\ell_2$ loss, a sum of outer products with PSD structure. Lemma 3.3 then proves that under SGD, this form converges to low stable rank at an exponential rate governed by the eigenvalue gap of the operator $S = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i C_i \otimes B_i$. Corollary 3.4 bounds the asymptotic stable rank by $\min(n - N', N')$ where $N'$ is the rank of the activation set — connecting gradient rank to data diversity. Corollary 3.5 identifies a special case where the gradient becomes rank-1. The separate JoMA-based analysis (Appendix B.3) extends the justification to transformer FFN layers, showing that the project-up weight gradient becomes "exponentially more low-rank" than the weight itself.

**The significance is in connecting architecture to optimization dynamics:** The theory identifies that the PSD sandwich structure $B_i W_t C_i$ in the gradient is what enables the low-rank property — not a general property of all gradients, but specific to networks where the chain rule produces this form. This means the method is not a universal black-box optimizer; it is justified for the specific architectures (reversible networks, transformers with the analyzed FFN structure) that dominate modern LLM design. This is a strength, not a weakness: it tells you when the method should work and suggests where it might fail (e.g., architectures without this gradient structure).

**Tie to evidence:** The convergence theorem (Theorem 3.8) provides the algorithmic justification, proving that with fixed projections, GaLore converges at a rate determined by $\kappa_t$, the product of minimum eigenvalues of the projected PSD matrices. This directly motivates the use of SVD for projection: SVD captures the directions with largest singular values, which correlate with the largest eigenvalues of $\hat{B}_i$ and $\hat{C}_i$, maximizing $\kappa_t$ and thus convergence speed. The fact that GaLore empirically matches full-rank training (Table 2, Table 3) while using SVD-based projections validates this theoretical motivation — random projections (Hao et al., 2024) are cheaper but would not have this alignment with the convergence theory.

---

### Innovation 4: Pre-Training a 7B Model on Consumer GPU Hardware as a Feasibility Demonstration

This innovation is less about a conceptual advance and more about what the paper **demonstrates is now possible** — establishing a new lower bound on the hardware required for meaningful LLM training. By combining gradient projection, 8-bit quantization, and per-layer weight updates, the paper reduces LLaMA 7B pre-training memory from 58GB to 22GB (Fig. 4), crossing the critical threshold of 24GB that defines consumer GPU capacity (NVIDIA RTX 4090).

**What the field assumed before:** The prevailing assumption, encoded in system designs like ZeRO (Rajbhandari et al., 2020) and the standard practice of training 7B+ models on multi-GPU clusters, was that pre-training large models requires either model parallelism (splitting layers across GPUs), memory offloading (moving tensors to CPU RAM), or both. The paper's own baseline confirms this: standard BF16 AdamW requires 58GB for LLaMA 7B with a token batch size of 256, far exceeding a single consumer GPU. The innovation is showing that **algorithmic memory reduction** — not just engineering workarounds — can eliminate this requirement.

**Why this is significant beyond the headline number:** The feasibility of single-GPU 7B pre-training has structural implications for the field. It lowers the barrier to entry for LLM research from "requires access to an 8×A100 cluster" to "requires a high-end gaming GPU." This changes who can participate in pre-training research, enables faster experimental iteration (no distributed systems debugging), and reduces energy consumption (a single consumer GPU draws ~300W versus ~2400W for eight A100s). The paper frames this explicitly in its Impact Statement: "By enabling the training of larger models on hardware with lower memory, our approach helps to minimize energy consumption and carbon footprint associated with training LLMs."

**The breakdown of memory savings is informative (Fig. 1):** 8-bit GaLore achieves 63.3% total memory reduction versus BF16 AdamW. Of the 37.92GB saved, 9.6GB comes from low-rank gradient projection (reducing optimizer state memory by 65.5%), and 13.5GB comes from per-layer weight updates (eliminating gradient storage). Quantization alone (8-bit Adam) saves 13.4GB but doesn't cross the 24GB threshold — it takes all three techniques combined. This decomposition shows that GaLore's projection is the largest single contributor among the **algorithmic** savings, and that stacking orthogonal techniques is what makes the consumer-GPU threshold achievable.

**Caveats the paper acknowledges:** The throughput with all techniques enabled is 1019.63 tokens/second for LLaMA 1B, roughly 15% slower than 8-bit Adam alone (Table 11). The 150K-step training run for LLaMA 7B used 64 A100 GPUs in parallel (Section 5.3), not a single consumer GPU — the single-GPU feasibility claim is based on memory measurements, not an actual end-to-end 7B pre-training run on an RTX 4090. The paper is transparent about this, presenting the single-GPU claim as a memory feasibility demonstration rather than a completed training run.

**Tie to evidence:** Fig. 4 plots memory usage across model sizes for different methods, showing 8-bit GaLore consistently below the 24GB line for models up to 7B, while all other methods exceed it for 3B+ models. Table 3 shows that 8-bit GaLore achieves perplexity 14.65 on LLaMA 7B after 19.7B tokens, comparable to 8-bit Adam at 14.61 — confirming that the memory savings do not come at the cost of model quality.

---

### Innovation 5: Unifying Pre-Training and Fine-Tuning Under a Single Memory-Efficient Strategy

GaLore is presented not as a pre-training method or a fine-tuning method, but as a **unified training strategy** that works across both regimes with consistent hyperparameters and memory characteristics. This contrasts with the dominant approach, where LoRA handles fine-tuning and separate systems (full training, ReLoRA, or distributed setups) handle pre-training — forcing practitioners to switch tools and tune different hyperparameters for different phases of the model lifecycle.

**What the field did before:** LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) was designed and is predominantly used for fine-tuning. ReLoRA (Lialin et al., 2024) extends the low-rank adaptor idea to pre-training but introduces new complexity: full-rank warmup, periodic merging with optimizer and learning rate resets, and careful tuning of merge frequency. A practitioner moving from fine-tuning to pre-training must switch from LoRA to ReLoRA (or full training), learn a new set of hyperparameters, and accept that warmup requires full-rank memory anyway. GaLore eliminates this fragmentation: the same algorithm, with the same core hyperparameters ($r$, $\alpha$, $T$), works for both.

**Why unification matters practically:** Real-world LLM workflows often involve multiple training phases — pre-training from scratch, continued pre-training on domain data, and fine-tuning on specific tasks. Using different memory-reduction strategies for each phase means different memory profiles, different tuning procedures, and potential incompatibilities (e.g., LoRA fine-tuning changes the model format, making it harder to resume pre-training). A unified strategy means one set of hyperparameters to tune, one memory budget to plan for, and a seamless transition between training phases.

**The fine-tuning results are not just "also works" — they match or exceed LoRA:** On GLUE with RoBERTa-Base and rank $r=4$, GaLore achieves 85.89 average score versus 85.61 for LoRA, with slightly less memory (253M vs. 257M, Table 4). At $r=8$, GaLore achieves 85.94 versus LoRA's 85.93. On SQuAD (Appendix D.2), GaLore achieves 80.52 Exact Match / 88.29 F1 versus LoRA's 77.99 / 86.11 — a more substantial gap. These results challenge the assumption that low-rank weight adaptation is the optimal approach even for fine-tuning; projecting gradients can be equally or more effective.

**The pre-training results are where the unification is most striking:** Table 2 shows GaLore matching full-rank training across model sizes from 60M to 1B, while LoRA (19.21 perplexity on 1B) and ReLoRA (18.33) lag significantly behind full-rank (15.56) and GaLore (15.64). GaLore achieves this without LoRA's frozen $W_0$, without ReLoRA's full-rank warmup and periodic resets, and with lower memory than both. The method does not compromise between pre-training capability and fine-tuning memory efficiency — it provides both simultaneously.

**What this says about the nature of the problem:** The fact that gradient projection works for both pre-training and fine-tuning, while weight-matrix low-rank methods work well only for fine-tuning, suggests that gradient low-rankness is a more **fundamental property** of neural network training than weight low-rankness. It emerges in both regimes because it is driven by the structure of backpropagation (the $A - BWC$ form), not by the proximity to a pre-trained solution. This is a diagnostic insight: if a method exploits gradient structure rather than weight structure, it should generalize across training phases, and GaLore's empirical success in both settings validates the theory's architectural grounding.

**Tie to evidence:** Table 2 (pre-training across 60M–1B), Table 3 (7B pre-training), Table 4 (GLUE fine-tuning), Table 8 (SQuAD fine-tuning), Table 9 (OpenAssistant fine-tuning), and Table 10 (Belle-1M fine-tuning) collectively demonstrate the unified applicability claim. The consistent hyperparameter settings — $T=200$, $\alpha=0.25$ for pre-training, with only $\alpha$ tuned for fine-tuning — support the claim that the method transfers across regimes with minimal adaptation.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** Pre-training experiments use the C4 dataset (Raffel et al., 2020), a colossal, cleaned version of Common Crawl's web crawl corpus. Models are trained without data repetition, with token counts scaled from 1.3B tokens (for the 60M-parameter model) up to 19.7B tokens (for the 7B model). Fine-tuning experiments use the GLUE benchmark (Wang et al., 2019), a suite of 8 NLP tasks including sentiment analysis, question answering, and textual entailment, plus the SQuAD reading comprehension dataset (Rajpurkar et al., 2016) and two conversational datasets (OpenAssistant Conversations and Belle-1M).

- **Base model(s).** All pre-training experiments use LLaMA-based architectures (Touvron et al., 2023) with RMSNorm and SwiGLU activations, ranging from 60M to 7B parameters. The architecture follows the setup from Lialin et al. (2024). Fine-tuning experiments use pre-trained RoBERTa-Base (on GLUE and SQuAD) and also evaluate on Gemma-2b, Phi-2, and LLaMA-7B for conversational fine-tuning. The LLaMA family is chosen as representative of modern LLM design, while RoBERTa enables direct comparison with established LoRA fine-tuning results.

- **Metrics.** Pre-training performance is measured by **validation perplexity** on held-out C4 data — lower is better. Fine-tuning on GLUE uses task-specific metrics (Matthew's correlation for CoLA, Pearson correlation for STS-B, F1/accuracy for MRPC/QQP, and accuracy for RTE, SST-2, MNLI, QNLI), with an **average score across all 8 tasks** reported as the primary aggregate. SQuAD uses Exact Match and F1 scores. Memory measurements are actual GPU memory consumption (not theoretical estimates) measured in GB, with a token batch size of 256 on a single device without activation checkpointing, memory offloading, or optimizer state partitioning.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five distinct approaches:
  - **Full-Rank:** Standard Adam optimizer with full-rank weights and optimizer states — the upper bound for model quality.
  - **Low-Rank:** Direct factorization of weights as $W = BA$ with learnable $B$ and $A$ (Kamalakara et al., 2022).
  - **LoRA** (Hu et al., 2022): $W = W_0 + BA$ with frozen $W_0$ and low-rank adaptors, with default settings (LoRA alpha = 32, LoRA dropout = 0.05).
  - **ReLoRA** (Lialin et al., 2024): Periodic merging of $BA$ into $W$ with optimizer state and learning rate resets, evaluated **without** full-rank training warmup for fair comparison.
  - **8-bit Adam** (Dettmers et al., 2022): Full-rank training with quantized optimizer states, used as the primary baseline for the 7B experiments.
  - Additional optimizer baselines in Fig. 3: **Adafactor** (Shazeer & Stern, 2018) and **AdamW** (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Pre-training comparisons are matched on **total training tokens** (ranging from 1.3B to 19.7B depending on model size) and **number of training steps** (10K for 60M, 20K for 130M, 60K for 350M, 100K for 1B, 150K for 7B). Memory comparisons use a fixed token batch size of 256 across all methods and model sizes. Throughput is measured in tokens/second on identical hardware (A100 GPUs). The computational cost of GaLore's periodic SVD is amortized over the subspace change frequency $T = 200$ and is reported as "negligible (< 10%)" compared to other memory-efficient training techniques.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper tunes learning rate from $\{0.01, 0.005, 0.001, 0.0005, 0.0001\}$ for each method and model size, selecting the best based on validation perplexity. For the 7B model, due to computational constraints, only a single trial is run without hyperparameter tuning ("we compare 8-bit GaLore (r=1024) with 8-bit Adam with a single trial without tuning the hyperparameters," Section 5.3). All experiments use BF16 numerical format. Fine-tuning results report single-run performance without confidence intervals or statistical significance testing.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Pre-Training LLaMA Models on C4 (60M to 1B scales)

**Headline result (Table 2): GaLore matches full-rank training across all model sizes while reducing total memory (parameters + optimizer states) by 33–44%.** On the 1B model, GaLore achieves perplexity 15.64 versus 15.56 for full-rank training, while using 4.38G memory versus 7.80G — a 44% reduction. On the 350M model, GaLore reaches 18.95 versus 18.80 for full-rank, with 1.22G versus 2.06G memory. On the 130M model: 25.36 (GaLore) versus 25.08 (full-rank). On the 60M model: 34.88 versus 34.06.

**Comparison with low-rank weight methods at identical rank:** All low-rank methods use the same rank $r$ per model size ($r = 128$ for 60M, $r = 256$ for 130M and 350M, $r = 512$ for 1B). The performance ordering is consistent and dramatic:

| Model | Full-Rank | GaLore | LoRA | ReLoRA | Low-Rank |
|-------|-----------|--------|------|--------|----------|
| 60M | 34.06 | 34.88 | 34.99 | 37.04 | 78.18 |
| 130M | 25.08 | 25.36 | 33.92 | 29.37 | 45.51 |
| 350M | 18.80 | 18.95 | 25.58 | 29.08 | 37.41 |
| 1B | 15.56 | 15.64 | 19.21 | 18.33 | 142.53 |

The "Low-Rank" approach (direct factorization $W = BA$) catastrophically fails: 142.53 perplexity on 1B versus 15.56 for full-rank — a more than 9× degradation. LoRA and ReLoRA perform substantially better than direct factorization but still significantly worse than GaLore and full-rank training, with gaps of 3–7 perplexity points on 350M–1B models. ReLoRA without warmup actually underperforms LoRA on 60M and 130M, and only slightly edges ahead on 1B (18.33 vs. 19.21).

**Memory comparison (Table 2 and Table 6):** GaLore achieves the lowest memory of any method that produces competitive perplexity. For the 1B model:
- Full-Rank: 7.80G (2.60G weights + 5.20G optimizer states)
- GaLore: 4.38G (2.60G weights + 1.78G optimizer states)
- LoRA: 6.17G (3.79G weights + 2.38G optimizer states)
- ReLoRA: 6.17G (identical to LoRA in memory breakdown)

The memory gap between GaLore and LoRA/ReLoRA widens at larger scales because GaLore does not need to store a separate full-rank $W_0$ plus low-rank adaptors $B$ and $A$.

**Training progression (Appendix C.3, Figure 6):** The training curves show GaLore closely tracking the full-rank baseline throughout training, while LoRA consistently underperforms. Notably, GaLore "even converges slightly faster at the beginning of the training" — the perplexity curve for GaLore sits below full-rank for the first ~20% of steps on 130M and 350M models, before the two curves converge.

#### GaLore with Different Base Optimizers

**Headline result (Figure 3): GaLore is optimizer-agnostic — it works with AdamW, 8-bit Adam, and Adafactor without significantly affecting convergence.** Evaluated on LLaMA 1B for 10K training steps:

- **8-bit Adam + GaLore ($r=512$):** Reaches perplexity comparable to 8-bit Adam alone, while reducing memory by up to 62.5% (on top of the quantization savings). The validation perplexity curves for 8-bit Adam and 8-bit Adam + GaLore (both $r=512$ and $r=1024$) are nearly overlapping throughout training.
- **AdamW + GaLore ($r=512$):** Tracks AdamW baseline closely, with $r=1024$ slightly outperforming $r=512$.
- **Adafactor + GaLore ($r=512$):** Works effectively despite Adafactor already using factored second-moment estimates. The $r=1024$ variant shows mild improvement over $r=512$ later in training.

**Key insight:** The fact that GaLore works with Adafactor — which already compresses second-order statistics via row-column outer products — demonstrates that gradient projection targets a different and complementary source of redundancy than existing optimizer-level compression. Adafactor compresses the *representation* of optimizer states; GaLore compresses the *input* to those states.

#### Scaling to LLaMA 7B

**Headline result (Table 3): GaLore scales to 7B parameters and 19.7B training tokens with performance matching 8-bit Adam, while using 18GB memory versus 26GB for 8-bit Adam.** After 150K training steps:

| Method | Memory | Perplexity at 40K | 80K | 120K | 150K |
|--------|--------|-------------------|-----|------|------|
| 8-bit GaLore ($r=1024$) | 18G | 17.94 | 15.39 | 14.95 | 14.65 |
| 8-bit Adam | 26G | 18.09 | 15.47 | 14.83 | 14.61 |

The perplexity gap is 0.04 at the final checkpoint (14.65 vs. 14.61), which is essentially negligible given the single-trial setting and lack of hyperparameter tuning for the 7B runs. The memory reduction is 8GB (30.8%), consistent with the savings observed at smaller scales. This experiment used 8-node training with 64 A100 GPUs in parallel (Section 5.3), so it does not demonstrate single-GPU training at this scale — the single-GPU feasibility is established separately via memory measurements (Section 5.5).

**Training progression for 7B (Figure 6):** The training curve shows 8-bit GaLore tracking 8-bit Adam closely throughout, with a slight advantage for 8-bit Adam at the very end. Unlike smaller models where GaLore initially converges faster, the 7B curves are nearly parallel from the start, suggesting the benefit of early low-rank projection diminishes at scale.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Subspace change frequency $T$ (Figure 5, left):** On 130M models with $r=128$, validation perplexity is robust across $T \in [50, 200, 500, 1000]$ with noticeable degradation only at extremes — $T=10$ (too frequent) underperforms, and $T=5000$ (too infrequent) slightly degrades late in training. For $r=256$, the sensitivity to $T$ is lower across the entire range, since the larger rank provides more "cushion" — the subspace already covers more directions, so switching is less critical. The paper notes that "optimal $T$ depends on the total training iterations and task complexity, [but] we find that a value between $T = 50$ to $T = 1000$ makes no much difference."

**Subspace rank $r$ vs. training duration (Figure 5, right):** On 130M models, comparing $r=128$ with $r=512$ shows an expected trade-off: larger rank converges faster (lower perplexity at any given step count) but uses more memory. The critical finding is that **smaller $r$ can be compensated by longer training** — "training with a rank of 128 using 80K steps achieves a lower loss than training with a rank of 512 using 20K steps." This establishes that rank is not a hard constraint on achievable performance but a lever in the memory-compute trade-off space: in memory-constrained scenarios, one can reduce $r$ and train longer to preserve final model quality.

**Combining GaLore with multiple optimizer types (Figure 3):** The ablation shows GaLore is not coupled to any specific optimizer. The projected gradient $R_t = P_t^\top G_t$ is a drop-in replacement for the full gradient $G_t$, and standard optimizer update rules apply in the compact space without modification. The convergence curves for AdamW+GaLore, 8-bit Adam+GaLore, and Adafactor+GaLore all track their full-gradient counterparts closely, with rank $r=1024$ slightly outperforming $r=512$ across optimizers (consistent with more gradient information being preserved).

**Effect of per-layer weight updates on throughput and memory (Table 11):** Disabling per-layer weight updates increases GaLore memory from 5.63G to 7.95G (for LLaMA 1B) — the extra 2.32G is the gradient storage. Throughput increases from 1019.63 to 1109.38 tokens/second (8.8% improvement), since the fused backward-update operation is removed and the optimizer step is batched across all layers. This quantifies the memory-throughput trade-off explicitly: practitioners can choose between lower memory (per-layer updates enabled) or higher throughput (disabled) depending on their hardware constraints.

**Scaling of memory savings with model size (Figure 4):** The memory curves show that GaLore's advantage over 8-bit Adam **increases** with model size — the gap is roughly 2G at 350M, 5G at 1B, and 8G at 7B. This is because the optimizer state compression ratio is roughly constant (factor of ~3×), but the absolute memory saved scales with the model size. 8-bit GaLore without per-layer weight updates ("retaining grad" curve in Figure 4) would require approximately 26.5G for 7B — still exceeding the 24GB consumer threshold, demonstrating that both techniques (projection + per-layer updates) are **jointly necessary** for consumer-GPU feasibility.

**Fine-tuning rank sensitivity:** Comparing GaLore $r=4$ vs. $r=8$ on GLUE (Table 4), the average score improves from 85.89 to 85.94 — a very modest gain (0.05 points) for doubling the rank. This suggests that fine-tuning benefits saturate at very low ranks, consistent with the established finding that fine-tuning requires updating only a small number of effective parameters. For LoRA, the same rank doubling improves from 85.61 to 85.93 (0.32 points) — a larger gain, suggesting LoRA benefits more from increased capacity than GaLore in the fine-tuning regime.

**No full-rank warmup requirement (implicit ablation):** The pre-training experiments in Table 2 run GaLore from random initialization without any full-rank warmup period, unlike ReLoRA which "requires a full-rank training warmup to achieve comparable performance" (Section 2). This is a de facto ablation: the entire training is conducted with gradient projection active, and the results show no degradation versus full-rank training. This is not controlled as a separate experiment (there is no "GaLore with warmup" vs. "GaLore without warmup" comparison), but the comparison with ReLoRA — which was evaluated "without a full-rank training warmup for a fair comparison" and achieved 18.33 vs. GaLore's 15.64 on 1B — demonstrates that GaLore does not depend on warmup the way ReLoRA does.

**Negative result: ReLoRA without warmup underperforms GaLore substantially.** On the 1B model, ReLoRA without warmup achieves 18.33 perplexity, which is better than LoRA (19.21) but significantly worse than GaLore (15.64) and full-rank (15.56). This confirms the paper's claim that ReLoRA's dependence on full-rank warmup is a genuine limitation, and that GaLore's ability to train from scratch without warmup is a meaningful advantage. The experiment also implicitly ablates the importance of **preserving optimizer states across subspace switches**: ReLoRA resets optimizer states at each merge, while GaLore preserves them. The performance gap (18.33 vs. 15.64) is consistent with the hypothesis that state preservation matters, though the two methods differ in other ways (ReLoRA merges adaptors into weights rather than projecting gradients), so this is not a clean ablation.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim: GaLore matches full-rank training performance while reducing memory by up to 65.5% in optimizer states

**What is tested:** Pre-training on C4 for LLaMA architectures at 60M, 130M, 350M, 1B, and 7B scales, with validation perplexity as the metric (Tables 2 and 3). The 65.5% optimizer state reduction is directly measured on LLaMA 7B (Figure 1).

**What is and is not demonstrated:** The perplexity results are convincing at all tested scales — GaLore performs within 0.3 perplexity points of full-rank training for 60M–1B models, and within 0.04 points at 7B (though the 7B comparison is a single trial without hyperparameter tuning). However, the experiments use a single dataset (C4) and a single model architecture family (LLaMA-based), both chosen to be representative but not exhaustive. The perplexity metric is a standard pre-training proxy, but it does not directly measure downstream task performance — a model with slightly higher perplexity might still perform competitively on benchmarks, or might not. The paper does not evaluate the pre-trained models on any downstream tasks (e.g., few-shot evaluation, fine-tuning transfer), which would provide a more complete picture of whether the tiny perplexity gap translates to practical capability differences.

**Genuine weakness — single trial for 7B experiments:** The paper states explicitly (Section 5.3) that the 7B comparison uses "a single trial without tuning the hyperparameters" due to computational constraints. This means we cannot assess the variance of the result or whether hyperparameter tuning would change the gap between 8-bit GaLore and 8-bit Adam. The 0.04 perplexity difference (14.65 vs. 14.61) is small enough that trial-to-trial variance could plausibly reverse the ordering. For the strongest claim in the paper — that GaLore scales to practical LLM pre-training at 7B parameters — the evidence is suggestive but underpowered relative to the claim's importance.

**Missing experiment — end-to-end 7B pre-training on a single consumer GPU:** The paper demonstrates memory feasibility (22GB fits within 24GB), but does not actually run a complete 7B pre-training experiment on an RTX 4090. The 7B results in Table 3 were obtained on 64 A100 GPUs. The throughput numbers (Table 11) are for 1B models, not 7B. An end-to-end 7B consumer-GPU pre-training run would be extremely slow (potentially months), so the omission is understandable, but the claim of "feasibility of pre-training a 7B model on consumer GPUs" should be understood as a memory measurement claim, not a demonstrated training run — the paper is transparent about this, but the abstract's phrasing may lead readers to assume otherwise.

#### Claim: GaLore outperforms existing low-rank methods (LoRA, ReLoRA, Low-Rank factorization) in both pre-training and fine-tuning

**What is tested:** Pre-training comparisons in Table 2 across four model sizes; fine-tuning comparisons in Table 4, Table 8, and Appendix Tables 9–10.

**What is and is not demonstrated:** The pre-training superiority is unambiguous — GaLore dramatically outperforms all low-rank weight methods at every scale. The gap versus LoRA is 3.57 perplexity points on 1B (15.64 vs. 19.21), which is enormous. For fine-tuning, the picture is more nuanced. GaLore slightly edges out LoRA on GLUE (85.89 vs. 85.61 at $r=4$; 85.94 vs. 85.93 at $r=8$), and more clearly on SQuAD (80.52 vs. 77.99 EM). These differences are modest and come without confidence intervals — it is not clear whether the 0.28-point GLUE advantage at $r=4$ is statistically significant. On conversational fine-tuning (Tables 9–10), GaLore matches or slightly exceeds full fine-tuning in most cases, while LoRA shows a substantial degradation on Phi-2 (4.24 vs. 3.81 on OpenAssistant).

**Genuine weakness — hyperparameter tuning asymmetry:** The paper tunes LoRA hyperparameters using the default settings from Hu et al. (2022) (LoRA alpha = 32, LoRA dropout = 0.05), with learning rate tuned from the same grid as GaLore. But LoRA introduces additional hyperparameters (alpha scaling, dropout, which layers to apply LoRA to) that the paper does not explore. It is possible that LoRA with optimized settings would close the gap on some tasks. Similarly, ReLoRA requires tuning of merge frequency, learning rate reset schedule, and optimizer state reset — the paper does not describe how ReLoRA's hyperparameters were tuned, only that it was evaluated "without a full-rank training warmup for a fair comparison." If ReLoRA's hyperparameters are suboptimal relative to GaLore's, the comparison is not truly fair.

**Missing baseline — LoRA with higher rank:** The paper fixes a single rank for all low-rank methods at each model size. But LoRA stores its adaptors separately from the frozen weights, while GaLore stores a projection matrix. The memory comparison in Table 1 is favorable to GaLore, but a fairer comparison might ask: at a given memory budget, which method performs better? If LoRA used a higher rank that matched GaLore's total memory, would it close the performance gap? The paper does not run this experiment — it compares methods at identical ranks regardless of memory implications.

#### Claim: GaLore enables full-parameter learning without constraining the weight matrices to be low-rank

**What is tested:** The theoretical framework (Section 3.2) and the empirical comparison with "Low-Rank" factorization (Table 2) provide evidence. The limiting-case argument (GaLore with $r = \min(m, n)$ recovers exact full-rank SGD, while LoRA at full rank does not) is a mathematical argument, not an empirical one.

**What is and is not demonstrated:** The claim that GaLore "allows full-parameter learning" is partially supported by the multi-subspace mechanism: the accumulated weight update $\Delta W = \sum_i \Delta W_{T_i}$ can in principle span the full parameter space. However, the paper does not directly verify that the effective rank of the accumulated weight update actually approaches full rank in practice. One could measure the rank or stable rank of $\Delta W$ at the end of training and check whether it exceeds the per-subspace rank $r$ — this would provide direct evidence of the multi-subspace mechanism working as claimed. Absent this measurement, the claim rests on the indirect evidence that GaLore matches full-rank performance while using a per-subspace rank much smaller than the full dimension (e.g., $r=512$ for a 1B model with $d_{\text{model}}=2048$, meaning each subspace covers at most 25% of the column space).

**Genuine weakness — no measurement of effective rank of total weight update:** The paper proves that within a single subspace, GaLore converges (Theorem 3.8), and argues that periodic switching prevents stagnation. But it does not quantify how much the subspaces actually differ across switches, or whether the accumulated update spans a significantly higher-dimensional space than a single subspace would. A simple measurement — computing the singular value spectrum of $W_T - W_0$ and comparing the effective rank to $r$ — would substantiate the multi-subspace claim. Without this, the mechanism is plausible but unverified.

#### Claim: The gradient becomes low-rank during training, justifying the projection approach

**What is tested:** The theoretical analysis (Theorem 3.2, Lemma 3.3, Corollaries 3.4–3.5, and the JoMA analysis in Appendix B.3) provides a framework for when and why this occurs. The empirical evidence is indirect: if gradients were not low-rank, GaLore (which discards singular vectors beyond rank $r$) would lose important gradient information and underperform. The fact that GaLore matches full-rank performance with $r$ much smaller than the full dimension (e.g., $r=512$ vs. $d_{\text{model}}=2048$ for 1B) is empirical evidence consistent with gradient low-rankness.

**What is and is not demonstrated:** The paper does not directly measure gradient low-rankness during the actual training runs. It does not report the singular value spectrum of $G_t$ at different points during pre-training, nor compute the stable rank $\text{sr}(G_t)$ to verify Lemma 3.3's predictions. The theoretical analysis assumes specific conditions (reversible networks, constant $A_i, B_i, C_i$ after $t \geq t_0$, specific loss functions) that may not hold exactly in transformer pre-training with Adam and evolving data batches. The JoMA analysis for FFN layers provides a separate justification, but it applies only to the project-up weight under restrictive assumptions (fixed embedding, linear activations, stationary gradients).

**Missing evidence — singular value spectrum measurements:** A direct empirical confirmation would be to plot the singular value spectrum of $G_t$ for a few representative layers at different training stages, showing that the top-$r$ singular values capture the vast majority of the gradient energy. This is standard practice in low-rank approximation work and its absence is notable. The paper's empirical success provides strong circumstantial evidence, but the theoretical claims about gradient dynamics remain unverified by direct measurement.

#### Claim: GaLore is a general method that works across optimizers and training regimes

**What is tested:** Figure 3 demonstrates compatibility with AdamW, 8-bit Adam, and Adafactor. Tables 2–3 and Appendix D show pre-training; Tables 4, 8–10 show fine-tuning across GLUE, SQuAD, OpenAssistant, and Belle-1M datasets. Multiple model architectures are tested: LLaMA (pre-training), RoBERTa (GLUE, SQuAD), Gemma-2b, Phi-2, and LLaMA-7B (conversational fine-tuning).

**What is and is not demonstrated:** The range of tested scenarios is genuinely broad — across model scales (60M to 7B), training regimes (pre-training from scratch, fine-tuning pre-trained models), optimizers (three types), and datasets (five distinct benchmarks). This is a strength of the paper. However, all experiments use transformer-based language models. The paper does not test GaLore on vision transformers (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021), diffusion models (Ho et al., 2020), or other architectures, though these are mentioned as future work in Section 7.

**Genuine weakness — no test on architectures without the theoretical gradient form:** The theory justifies GaLore for reversible networks and transformer FFN layers. It does not justify GaLore for architectures like standard RNNs, U-Nets, or graph neural networks, where the gradient may not follow the $A - BWC$ form. The paper's title and abstract present GaLore as a general method for "LLM training," but the theoretical grounding is architecture-specific. Testing on a broader range of architectures would clarify whether GaLore's effectiveness is tied to the specific gradient structure analyzed, or whether gradient low-rankness is a more universal phenomenon that the theory only partially captures.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Have Strengthened the Paper

1. **Downstream evaluation of pre-trained models:** Perplexity is a proxy; few-shot performance on benchmarks like HellaSwag, PIQA, or MMLU would assess whether the 0.04–0.3 perplexity gap between GaLore and full-rank training translates to practical capability differences.

2. **Direct measurement of gradient singular value spectra:** Plotting the proportion of gradient energy captured by top-$r$ singular vectors over training would provide direct empirical support for the central theoretical claim and help guide rank selection.

3. **Memory-matched comparison with LoRA:** At a fixed memory budget, what rank can LoRA use versus GaLore, and which achieves better performance? This would address whether GaLore's advantage is due to the gradient-projection mechanism or simply using a more efficient memory layout.

4. **End-to-end 7B training on a consumer GPU:** Even a short run (e.g., 1000 steps) on an RTX 4090 would validate the single-GPU feasibility claim more directly than extrapolating from memory measurements.

5. **Ablation on projection matrix construction method:** Comparing SVD-based projection to random projection (as in Hao et al., 2024) or to a fixed projection from initialization would isolate the contribution of adaptive, SVD-based subspace selection.

6. **Ablation on optimizer state preservation across subspace switches:** Comparing GaLore's approach (preserving states) to resetting states at each switch (closer to ReLoRA's behavior) would quantify the importance of this design choice independently from the projection mechanism.

7. **Measurement of effective rank of accumulated weight update:** Computing $\text{sr}(W_T - W_0)$ at the end of training would verify whether the multi-subspace mechanism actually achieves higher effective rank than $r$.

8. **Statistical significance for fine-tuning results:** The GLUE differences between GaLore and LoRA are small (0.01–0.3 points); multiple seeds or confidence intervals would indicate whether these differences are reliable.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Linear Scaling of Memory Savings with Effective Rank Is Both the Core Strength and the Hard Constraint

**The assumption or constraint.** GaLore’s memory savings come entirely from reducing the dimensionality of optimizer states from the full parameter shape $m \times n$ to a compact $n \times r$ (or $m \times r$, whichever dimension is smaller). The memory saved is proportional to the compression ratio $r / \min(m, n)$. This means that as the rank $r$ approaches $\min(m, n)$, the memory savings vanish — and at $r = \min(m, n)$, GaLore uses *more* memory than full-rank training because it must additionally store the projection matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$. The paper is explicit about the memory model in Table 1: GaLore requires $mn + mr + 2nr$ elements versus $4mn$ for full-rank Adam, so the break-even condition is $mr + 2nr < 3mn$, which fails when $r$ is too close to $\min(m,n)$. The paper’s pre-training experiments use $r$ values that are 25% of the model dimension (e.g., $r = 512$ for $d_{\text{model}} = 2048$ at the 1B scale, and $r = 1024$ for $d_{\text{model}} = 4096$ at the 7B scale), but this ratio is an empirical choice, not a derived optimum. The theoretical framework (Lemma 3.3) provides a bound on the asymptotic stable rank of the gradient, but does not predict what $r$ is necessary for good performance on a specific model and task — it tells you the gradient *becomes* low-rank, but not *how* low-rank or whether the rate of rank reduction matches the rate at which you can afford to compress.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot freely choose $r$ to fit an arbitrary memory budget — there is a lower bound on $r$ below which performance degrades, and that bound is task- and model-dependent. The ablation in Figure 5 (right) shows that on a 130M model, $r = 128$ with 80K training steps achieves lower loss than $r = 512$ with 20K steps, which the paper frames positively: you can trade rank for training duration. But this tradeoff only works if you have the computational budget to train longer — it does not help if you are latency-constrained or have a fixed total FLOPs budget where longer training eats into the savings from reduced per-step memory. Furthermore, the required rank appears to grow with model size: the paper uses $r/d_{\text{model}}$ ratios of 128/512 = 0.25 for 60M, 256/768 ≈ 0.33 for 130M, 256/1024 = 0.25 for 350M, 512/2048 = 0.25 for 1B, and 1024/4096 = 0.25 for 7B. If this ratio must remain constant, the optimizer state memory scales as $n \cdot r \propto n \cdot d_{\text{model}} \propto d_{\text{model}}^2$, which is the same asymptotic scaling as full-rank training — only the constant factor improves. GaLore changes the slope of the memory-vs-model-size curve in Figure 4, but does not change its quadratic growth in $d_{\text{model}}$.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The memory measurements in Figure 4 show 8-bit GaLore’s memory curve growing with model size, staying below the 24GB threshold at 7B but continuing to rise. The paper does not project memory requirements beyond 7B — a 13B or 30B model would almost certainly exceed 24GB even with 8-bit GaLore, meaning the consumer-GPU feasibility claim is a point result at 7B, not a scaling guarantee. Table 6 provides the full memory breakdown across model sizes, confirming that the optimizer state component grows with model size even under GaLore. The ablation on rank (Figure 5, right) quantifies the convergence slowdown from reducing $r$: at equal step count, $r=128$ underperforms $r=512$, and only catches up with 4× the training steps — an implicit measurement of the minimum viable rank.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges none of this as a limitation. It presents the rank $r$ as a hyperparameter to be tuned, and the fact that smaller $r$ can be compensated by longer training as a feature, not a constraint. There is no analysis of how the minimum viable $r$ scales with model size, no projection of memory requirements for models beyond 7B, and no discussion of what happens when the required $r$ for acceptable performance exceeds what the memory budget allows. The statement in Section 7 about future work on “further enhancing memory efficiency by employing low-memory projection matrices” gestures at reducing the projection matrix storage cost but does not address the fundamental scaling of the compact optimizer states themselves.

### The SVD Overhead Is Amortized, Not Eliminated, and Its Scaling with Model Size Is Unexplored

**The assumption or constraint.** GaLore computes a full SVD of the gradient matrix $G_t \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ every $T$ training steps to construct the projection matrix $P_t$. The paper reports that with $T = 200$, “the total computational overhead induced by SVD is negligible (< 10%) compared to other memory-efficient training techniques such as memory offloading” (Section 4.1), and the throughput measurements in Table 11 show a 15–18% reduction compared to 8-bit Adam without GaLore. However, these measurements are on a 1B model (where $m \times n \approx 2048 \times 5461$ for FFN layers) and a batch size of 256 tokens. The cost of SVD on an $m \times n$ matrix scales as $O(mn \cdot \min(m, n))$ for the full decomposition — or roughly $O(mn \cdot r)$ if only the top-$r$ singular vectors are needed via randomized SVD algorithms. The paper does not specify which SVD algorithm is used, whether it exploits the fact that only $r \ll \min(m,n)$ singular vectors are needed, or how the SVD cost scales to larger models.

**The consequence.** For larger models, the relative cost of SVD may grow substantially because the gradient matrix dimensions increase while the forward-backward pass cost per step increases at a different rate. At the 7B scale ($d_{\text{model}} = 4096$, FFN intermediate dimension = 11008), the SVD of the FFN gradient (shape $4096 \times 11008$) costs roughly 5× more operations than at the 1B scale ($2048 \times 5461$), while the forward-backward pass cost grows roughly quadratically in $d_{\text{model}}$ as well — but the constants and parallelization characteristics differ. On a single consumer GPU (the target hardware for the paper’s feasibility claim), SVD computation may become a significant fraction of step time because SVD is harder to parallelize across GPU cores than matrix multiplications. Moreover, the paper’s throughput measurements (Table 11) are on A100 GPUs, not consumer GPUs. An RTX 4090 has approximately half the memory bandwidth and fewer tensor cores, so the relative overhead of SVD (which is memory-bandwidth-bound for the initial read of $G_t$) may be higher in the consumer-GPU setting that the paper targets.

A subtler consequence involves the interaction between SVD frequency $T$ and the theoretical convergence guarantees. Theorem 3.8 assumes constant $P$ and $Q$ — an assumption that holds only between subspace switches. If $T$ is made very large to amortize SVD cost, the training spends many steps optimizing in a potentially outdated subspace (as the gradient’s dominant directions shift). If $T$ is made small to track the gradient subspace more closely, the SVD overhead grows and the assumption of Theorem 3.8 (constant projections) is violated more frequently. The paper’s ablation (Figure 5, left) shows robustness across $T \in [50, 1000]$ for a 130M model, but this range is validated only on a small model trained for 20K steps — on a 7B model trained for 150K steps, the cumulative effect of suboptimal $T$ could be larger.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 11 provides throughput numbers for the 1B model only: 8-bit GaLore achieves 1019.63 tokens/second with per-layer updates versus 1205.31 for 8-bit Adam — a 15.4% reduction. Without per-layer updates, 8-bit GaLore achieves 1109.38 versus 1569.89 for 8-bit Adam — a 29.3% reduction. The paper attributes the overhead to GaLore generally, without breaking out the SVD-specific component. There is no throughput measurement at the 7B scale, no profiling of where time is spent in GaLore’s training loop, and no comparison of SVD algorithms (e.g., full SVD vs. randomized top-$r$ SVD). The claim that overhead is “< 10% compared to memory offloading” is an odd comparison — memory offloading is a different technique with different overhead characteristics — and does not quantify absolute overhead relative to a non-offloaded baseline.

**Mitigation status.** The paper mentions the SVD overhead in Section 4.1 and argues it is negligible, but does not provide the measurements that would let a practitioner assess this for their own model scale. It does not discuss SVD algorithm choices, the possibility of using more frequent but approximate subspace updates (e.g., power iteration), or the trade-off between SVD frequency and optimizer state staleness. Section 7 lists “employing low-memory projection matrices” as future work, which could reduce the *memory* cost of storing $P$ but does not address the *computational* cost of computing it.

### The Theoretical Justification Depends on Assumptions That May Not Hold in the Actual Training Setting

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper’s central theoretical result — Lemma 3.3 and its corollaries — proves that the gradient becomes low-rank under a specific set of conditions: (1) the gradient takes the parametric form $G_t = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N (A_i - B_i W_t C_i)$ with PSD matrices $B_i$ and $C_i$, (2) the matrices $A_i$, $B_i$, and $C_i$ are constant after some time $t \geq t_0$, (3) the optimizer is vanilla SGD (no momentum, no Adam preconditioning), and (4) the network is reversible (Definition 3.1) or, for transformer FFN layers, satisfies the JoMA assumptions (fixed embedding, linear activations, stationary backpropagated gradients — Appendix B.3). The paper uses this theory to motivate the SVD-based projection: “one way is to instead use the spectrum of $G_t$ via Singular Value Decomposition” (Section 3.3), since directly computing the eigenstructure of the theoretical $B_i$ and $C_i$ matrices is impractical.

**The consequence.** There is a chain of gaps between the theoretical setting where gradient low-rankness is *proven* and the empirical setting where GaLore is *deployed*. First, the actual optimizer is Adam, not SGD — the momentum and adaptive learning rate change the weight update dynamics, and it is not obvious that Lemma 3.3’s contraction argument extends to this case (the paper does not prove an Adam analog). Second, the “constant $A_i, B_i, C_i$” assumption is an approximation that may hold locally (over short time windows) but certainly fails globally as weights change — the paper acknowledges this implicitly by introducing subspace switching, but does not quantify how quickly the optimal projection subspace drifts. Third, the transformer architecture includes self-attention layers, which are not covered by the reversible network analysis, and the JoMA analysis for FFN layers assumes linear activations and stationary gradients — neither of which holds in practice (SwiGLU activations are nonlinear; gradient statistics evolve during training). Fourth, the theory is derived for batch size 1 (Theorem 3.2) and then extended via averaging to batch size $N$ (Equation 8), but the extension implicitly assumes that each example’s $A_i, B_i, C_i$ can be meaningfully averaged, which may not hold when different examples produce gradients with different dominant subspaces.

The practical consequence of these gaps is that the theory does not provide actionable guidance on the key hyperparameters. What rank $r$ is needed? The theory bounds the asymptotic stable rank by $\min(n - N', N')$ (Corollary 3.4), but $N'$ — the rank of the activation set — is not measurable in practice and depends on batch composition. How often should the subspace switch? The theory assumes constant $P$ and $Q$ (Theorem 3.8), so it provides no guidance on $T$. The paper falls back on empirical tuning for both $r$ and $T$, meaning the theory serves as a *qualitative justification* for why the method might work, but not a *predictive tool* for how to configure it.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The gap between theory and practice is visible in several places. The paper proves convergence for GaLore with $\rho_t \equiv 1$ (SGD, Theorem 3.8) but deploys GaLore with Adam ($\rho_t = \text{Adam}$) in all experiments. The JoMA analysis for transformer FFN layers (Appendix B.3) is elegant but applies only to the project-up weight, assumes a fixed embedding matrix $U$, linear activations, and stationary gradients — none of which match the LLaMA pre-training setup (learned embeddings, SwiGLU activations, evolving gradient statistics). The paper does not attempt to verify the theoretical predictions empirically — it does not measure whether the gradient singular value spectrum decays at the rate predicted by Lemma 3.3, whether the stable rank converges to the bound in Corollary 3.4, or whether the eigenvalue gap of $S$ governs convergence speed as Theorem 3.8 suggests. The theory and the experiments exist in separate sections of the paper without cross-validation of the theory’s predictions.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about the scope of the theory — it states the assumptions explicitly in each theorem, and the JoMA analysis is clearly labeled as applying to FFN layers only. However, it does not discuss the gap between the theoretical setting (SGD, reversible networks, constant $A_i, B_i, C_i$) and the experimental setting (Adam, transformers with attention, evolving data). The claim that GaLore is justified by the theory appears in the abstract and introduction (“We first show theoretically that the gradient matrix $G$ becomes low-rank during training”), but the limitations of that theoretical justification are not revisited in the discussion. A practitioner reading the theory might assume the method has stronger guarantees than it actually does.

### Single-Trial 7B Results and Absence of Statistical Rigor Leave the Scaling Claim Undersupported

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper’s most practically impactful claim — that GaLore scales to 7B parameter pre-training with performance matching full-rank training — rests on a single experimental trial. Section 5.3 states: “Due to computational constraints, we compare 8-bit GaLore ($r = 1024$) with 8-bit Adam with a single trial without tuning the hyperparameters.” The 7B model is trained for 150K steps on 19.7B tokens using 64 A100 GPUs — a substantial computational investment — but with only one run per method. The final perplexity difference is 0.04 (14.65 for GaLore vs. 14.61 for 8-bit Adam). No confidence intervals, standard deviations, or multiple seeds are reported for any experiment in the paper. The smaller-scale experiments (60M–1B) are also reported as point estimates without variance information, though the paper does tune learning rates and reports the best result.

**The consequence.** Without variance information, we cannot assess whether the 0.04 perplexity difference at 7B is statistically meaningful or within the noise of training stochasticity. Large-scale LLM pre-training is known to exhibit non-trivial run-to-run variance due to random initialization, data ordering, and hardware-level nondeterminism. It is entirely plausible that re-running both methods with different random seeds would reverse the ordering (GaLore slightly better than 8-bit Adam) or produce a larger gap (GaLore noticeably worse). The paper’s core claim about 7B pre-training — that GaLore provides “comparable performance to its full-rank counterpart” (Abstract) — is thus supported by a single data point at the most important scale. For a method that the paper explicitly targets at practitioners who want to train production-scale models (“we believe that GaLore will be a valuable tool for the community, enabling the training of large-scale models on consumer-grade hardware,” Section 7), this is a significant evidential gap.

The issue extends beyond the 7B result. The pre-training comparisons in Table 2 (60M–1B) use learning rate tuning and report the best result per method, but do not control for the number of tuning trials or report variance at the selected learning rate. The fine-tuning results in Table 4 show GaLore and LoRA separated by 0.01–0.28 points on GLUE tasks — differences small enough that they could easily be noise without statistical confirmation. The paper’s consistent use of point estimates without error bars or significance tests makes it difficult to distinguish genuine method effects from sampling noise across the entire empirical evaluation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 5.3 explicitly acknowledges the single-trial limitation for the 7B experiments. The paper does not report variance for any other experiment. The training curves in Figure 6 show a single trace per method with no shading or error bands. The GLUE results in Table 4 are single numbers per task. The throughput measurements in Table 11 are point estimates without variance.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the 7B limitation transparently (“Due to computational constraints, we compare ... with a single trial without tuning the hyperparameters”) but does not discuss its implications for the strength of the scaling claim. It does not suggest that future work should replicate the 7B results with multiple seeds, nor does it temper the abstract’s strong claim that GaLore “maintain[s] both efficiency and performance for pre-training on LLaMA 1B and 7B architectures” in light of this limitation. The computational constraints are understandable — 7B pre-training runs are expensive — but the paper’s conclusions do not reflect the appropriate uncertainty.

### The Consumer GPU Pre-Training Feasibility Claim Is a Memory Measurement, Not a Demonstrated Training Run

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper’s headline result — “we demonstrate, for the first time, the feasibility of pre-training a 7B model on consumer GPUs with 24GB memory (e.g., NVIDIA RTX 4090) without model parallel, checkpointing, or offloading strategies” (Abstract) — is based on memory consumption measurements, not an actual end-to-end pre-training run on consumer hardware. Figure 1 and Figure 4 show that 8-bit GaLore requires approximately 22GB to train LLaMA 7B with a token batch size of 256, which fits within 24GB. However, the paper does not report having actually executed a complete 7B pre-training run on an RTX 4090. The 7B training results in Table 3 were obtained on 64 A100 GPUs using distributed data parallelism (Section 5.3: “We train the model for 150K steps with 19.7B tokens, using 8-node training in parallel with a total of 64 A100 GPUs”). The throughput measurements (Table 11) are for the 1B model on a single device, not for the 7B model. The paper does not estimate how long a full 7B pre-training run would take on a single RTX 4090.

**The consequence.** “Feasibility” in the abstract conflates two distinct claims: (1) the model fits in memory during training (a memory measurement), and (2) the model can be trained to completion in a practical amount of time (a throughput measurement). The first claim is supported by Figure 4; the second is not addressed. A back-of-the-envelope estimate using the 1B throughput numbers: at 1019.63 tokens/second on an A100, training a 7B model on 19.7B tokens would take approximately $19.7 \times 10^9 / 1019.63 / 3600 \approx 5365$ GPU-hours. On an RTX 4090, which has roughly 60–70% of the A100’s effective throughput for this workload (accounting for lower memory bandwidth and fewer tensor cores), this could be 8000+ GPU-hours — nearly a year of continuous training. An actual consumer-GPU pre-training run at 7B scale may be “feasible” in the sense of being technically possible, but not in the sense of being practically useful for most users. The paper’s Impact Statement frames GaLore as enabling training “on hardware with lower memory” to “minimize energy consumption and carbon footprint,” but if the trade-off is dramatically longer wall-clock time, the energy savings from using a lower-power GPU may be offset by the longer total runtime.

Furthermore, a single-consumer-GPU training run would require different engineering than the distributed A100 setup used for the paper’s actual 7B experiments. Gradient accumulation to achieve effective batch sizes comparable to multi-GPU training, handling of batch normalization statistics across micro-batches, and potential numerical stability issues from very long training runs are practical concerns that the paper does not address because they were not encountered in the distributed setting.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 4 provides the memory measurement. Section 5.5 states that “8-bit GaLore requires significantly less memory than BF16 baseline and 8-bit Adam, and only requires 22.0G memory to pre-train LLaMA 7B with a small per-GPU token batch size (up to 500 tokens). This memory footprint is within 24GB VRAM capacity of a single GPU such as NVIDIA RTX 4090.” The paper does not report an end-to-end 7B training run on consumer hardware. The throughput numbers in Table 11 are for a 1B model only. Section 5.5 notes that “when activation checkpointing is enabled, per-GPU token batch size can be increased up to 4096” and that “it is possible that GaLore can be used for elastic training ... 7B models on consumer GPUs such as RTX 4090s” — “possible” being the operative word.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is generally careful to describe the consumer-GPU claim in terms of memory measurements rather than completed training runs, but the abstract’s phrasing (“we demonstrate ... the feasibility of pre-training a 7B model on consumer GPUs”) is stronger than the evidence warrants. The throughput measurements are limited to 1B models, and the paper does not project training time for 7B on consumer hardware. Section 7 lists “exploring the feasibility of elastic data distributed training on low-bandwidth consumer-grade hardware” as future work, which implicitly acknowledges that the consumer-GPU story is incomplete, but the abstract and Figure 1 present a more confident picture.

### The Method Is Evaluated Only on Autoregressive Language Models with a Specific Architecture Family, With No Evidence of Cross-Domain Transfer

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses transformer-based language models: LLaMA-based architectures for pre-training (60M–7B parameters), RoBERTa-Base for GLUE and SQuAD fine-tuning, and Gemma-2b, Phi-2, and LLaMA-7B for conversational fine-tuning. The theoretical framework justifying gradient low-rankness is developed for reversible networks (covering linear layers, ReLU, and ResNet-style architectures) and transformer FFN layers (via the JoMA analysis in Appendix B.3). The self-attention mechanism — which constitutes a significant fraction of parameters in the tested models — is not covered by either theoretical analysis. The paper acknowledges this explicitly in Appendix B.1: “For architectures like self-attention, one possibility is to leverage JoMA to analyze, and we leave for future work.” GaLore is applied to all layers (multi-head attention and feed-forward) in the experiments, but the theory only covers the feed-forward portion.

**The consequence.** A practitioner considering GaLore for a different architecture — vision transformers, encoder-decoder models, diffusion models, state-space models, or any architecture without the specific gradient structure analyzed — has no theoretical guarantee and no empirical evidence that the method will work. The gradient low-rankness property might hold more broadly (the paper cites prior work showing gradient low-rankness in various settings: Cosson et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023), but GaLore’s specific mechanism — SVD-based projection of the gradient before it enters the optimizer, with periodic subspace switching — is validated only on the specific architectures and tasks tested. The paper’s framing as a general method for “Memory-Efficient LLM Training” implicitly claims broader applicability than the evidence supports. Even within language modeling, the evaluation uses only the C4 dataset for pre-training, which may have specific statistical properties (web crawl text with a certain distribution of sequence lengths, token frequencies, and linguistic structures) that affect gradient dynamics. It is unknown whether GaLore would perform similarly on code, mathematical text, multilingual corpora, or other pre-training distributions.

A related concern is that GaLore’s performance on attention layers — which the theory does not cover — is bundled together with its performance on FFN layers in all experiments. If GaLore works well on FFN layers (theoretically justified) but poorly on attention layers (theoretically unexamined), the aggregate results could mask a significant weakness in half the model’s parameters. The paper does not report per-layer-type ablation (e.g., applying GaLore to FFN only vs. attention only) to determine where the benefits and costs are concentrated.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All experiments use transformer-based language models, as detailed in Section 5 and Appendix C. The theoretical gap for self-attention is acknowledged in Appendix B.1: “For architectures like self-attention, one possibility is to leverage JoMA (Tian et al., 2024) to analyze, and we leave for future work.” The paper does not ablate GaLore’s application by layer type — it is applied uniformly to “all multi-head attention layers and feed-forward layers” (Section 5.1). The fine-tuning experiments extend to additional model families (RoBERTa, Gemma-2b, Phi-2) but these are all transformer-based language models, so the architecture family remains constant. The future work section (Section 7) mentions “applying GaLore on training of various models such as vision transformers and diffusion models” as an open problem, explicitly acknowledging the current scope limitation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent in the future work section about the need to test GaLore on other architectures. However, the main text and abstract present the method without qualification as applying to “LLM training,” and the theoretical framework’s gaps (no attention-layer analysis) are buried in the appendix. A practitioner reading only the main paper would not be aware that the theory does not cover attention — a significant omission given that attention is the defining architectural component of the models being trained. The paper does not discuss what properties of attention-layer gradients might differ from FFN-layer gradients or whether the gradient form in Theorem 3.2 has an analog for attention.
