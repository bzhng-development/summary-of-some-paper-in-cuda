# How Do Large Language Models Acquire Factual Knowledge During Pretraining?

**ArXiv:** [2406.11813](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11813)

## 🎯 Pitch

Scaling pretraining data surprisingly fails to improve a model's immediate ability to absorb new facts—only larger model size does. Worse, repeated exposure to the same fact accelerates forgetting, though larger batch sizes help knowledge stick.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This work studies the fine-grained dynamics of how large language models acquire factual knowledge during pretraining, using the OLMo model family with a constructed FICTIONAL KNOWLEDGE dataset of novel entity descriptions. The paper reveals that factual knowledge acquisition in LLM pretraining occurs by **accumulating micro-acquisitions** (small log-probability increases each time the model encounters a fact) that are subsequently diluted by forgetting, establishing a power-law relationship between training steps and the loss of both memorization and generalization. Scaling model size improves the immediate gain per encounter (**effectivity**)—with the 7B model demonstrating consistently higher effectivity than the 1B model—but training with more pretraining tokens at later stages shows no significant improvement in this acquisition ability, suggesting that the benefits of data scaling and model scaling are qualitatively different. Training with duplicated data accelerates forgetting of generalized knowledge compared to paraphrased presentations, while larger batch sizes enhance robustness against forgetting, demonstrating that deduplication and batch-size tuning expand the set of learnable knowledge by lengthening the interval over which facts remain accessible.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Gap: We Know LLMs Store Facts, But Not How They Get There

The fundamental question this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **how do large language models actually acquire factual knowledge during pretraining?** This matters because, despite the widespread observation that LLMs can store and retrieve substantial factual knowledge from their training data — answering trivia questions, recalling historical events, and producing encyclopedia-like descriptions of entities — the *mechanism* by which this knowledge gets encoded into the model's parameters during the pretraining process remains poorly understood.

This gap is significant for several reasons that the paper establishes (Section 1):

- **Scientific understanding**: Without understanding the dynamics of knowledge acquisition, the field lacks a mechanistic explanation for *when* during pretraining a model learns a particular fact, *how many exposures* are needed for reliable learning, and *why* certain types of knowledge (e.g., long-tail facts) are systematically poorly learned. This is not merely an academic curiosity — it directly constrains our ability to design better pretraining recipes.

- **Practical deployment**: LLMs exhibit well-documented failures in acquiring unpopular or infrequent knowledge (Kandpal et al., 2022; Mallen et al., 2022). Understanding *why* this happens — whether it's a failure to acquire the knowledge in the first place or a failure to retain it against subsequent forgetting — has direct implications for data curation strategies (e.g., how often should key facts be repeated? does paraphrasing help?).

- **Data engineering decisions**: Practitioners must make concrete choices about pretraining data: how aggressively to deduplicate, what batch size to use, whether to invest in diverse paraphrases of key knowledge. These decisions are currently guided by downstream benchmark performance rather than a principled understanding of how they affect the knowledge acquisition process itself. As the paper notes (Section 4.4), widely observed practices like deduplication (Lee et al., 2021; Xue et al., 2023) and large-batch training (Chowdhery et al., 2022; Touvron et al., 2023) are empirically validated but mechanistically unexplained.

- **Training dynamics**: The broader community studying neural network training dynamics has investigated memorization and forgetting patterns (Tirumala et al., 2022; Ramasesh et al., 2021), but this work has largely focused on *sequence-level memorization* (can you extract a training example verbatim?) rather than *factual knowledge acquisition* (does the model learn the underlying fact and can it generalize it to new contexts?). These are distinct phenomena with potentially different dynamics.

### The Knowns and Unknowns in LLM Knowledge Encoding

Prior to this work, the literature on LLM factual knowledge was extensive but had a particular shape that left the acquisition dynamics unexplored. The paper carefully maps this landscape (Section 2) to situate its contribution.

**What was well-established:**
- LLMs encode substantial factual knowledge that can be queried post-training (Petroni et al., 2019; Roberts et al., 2020; Da et al., 2021), functioning as approximate "knowledge bases" in parameter form.
- Specific architectural components — particularly feed-forward layers — act as key-value memory stores for factual associations (Geva et al., 2021, 2023; Dai et al., 2022).
- The *format* in which knowledge is presented during training matters: Allen-Zhu and Li (2023) demonstrated that knowledge must appear in diverse formats to be reliably extracted later, suggesting that the generalization of factual knowledge depends on training data diversity, not just raw frequency.
- Scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) establish that model performance on knowledge-intensive tasks improves with both model size and pretraining data volume, but these are aggregate correlations that don't reveal per-fact dynamics.

**Where prior work fell short:**

The paper identifies three specific limitations in prior approaches that it aims to address:

**1. Post-hoc analysis rather than process-level investigation.** The vast majority of work on LLM factual knowledge (the probing literature, knowledge neuron identification, extraction studies) examines models *after* pretraining is complete. These studies can tell us what is stored and where, but not *how it got there* — the temporal dynamics of acquisition, the role of repeated exposures, or the competition between acquisition and forgetting. As the paper states (Section 2):

> "These works have mainly focused on investigating the factual knowledge encoded in LLMs after pretraining is complete."

This is analogous to studying memory in humans by examining what they know at age 30 without tracking their learning trajectory. You can infer what was learned but not the learning process itself.

**2. Training dynamics studied at wrong granularity.** Some prior work has examined LLM training dynamics (Hao et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024), but typically at the level of aggregate metrics (downstream task performance, perplexity on evaluation sets) or emergent phenomena (phase transitions, sudden capability jumps). These studies capture macro-level patterns but miss the fine-grained, per-fact acquisition process. Tirumala et al. (2022) conducted extensive analysis of memorization dynamics in LLM pretraining, but focused on sequence-level memorization (exact reproduction of training strings) rather than factual generalization. Similarly, Tänzer et al. (2021) studied memorization versus generalization in pretrained models but did not track per-fact acquisition trajectories.

**3. The long-tail knowledge problem is empirically observed but mechanistically unexplained.** Kandpal et al. (2022) and Mallen et al. (2022) demonstrated that LLMs struggle to learn facts that appear infrequently in the training data — the "long-tail knowledge" problem. This is observed as a correlation between training data frequency and downstream question-answering accuracy. However, the *mechanism* underlying this failure was unknown: is it that rare facts are never acquired in the first place (failure of initial encoding), or that they are acquired but subsequently forgotten (failure of retention)? The difference matters enormously for intervention design — the first suggests increasing exposure frequency, while the second suggests modifying training dynamics (e.g., batch size, learning rate scheduling) to slow forgetting.

**4. Conflicting signals about data repetition.** Practitioners face a tension: repeating information helps learning (more exposures → stronger encoding), but data deduplication is a widely validated best practice for improving model quality (Lee et al., 2021; Abbas et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2023). The paper notes this apparent contradiction and positions its analysis of duplication versus paraphrasing as a resolution: repeated *identical* sequences may strengthen memorization at the cost of generalization, while diverse rephrasings may support generalization without the memorization tax. Prior work had observed these effects in aggregate but had not traced them to the per-fact dynamics of acquisition and forgetting.

### The Methodological Challenge: Why This Problem Is Hard to Study

Understanding factual knowledge acquisition at fine granularity during pretraining is technically challenging for reasons the paper addresses implicitly through its experimental design:

- **Knowledge novelty control**: To study acquisition dynamics, you need to know exactly when the model first encounters a fact. With real pretraining data (terabytes of web text), you cannot know whether a model has already seen a fact in some earlier batch. The paper solves this by constructing entirely novel, fictional knowledge — entities and facts that literally did not exist before the experiment — ensuring a clean "time zero" for acquisition tracking.

- **Fine-grained measurement**: Aggregate metrics (accuracy, loss) are too coarse to reveal per-step dynamics. The paper uses log-probability of target spans at every training step as a continuous, high-resolution signal of knowledge encoding strength (Section 3), following Schaeffer et al. (2023)'s argument that log-probability provides more granular information about model knowledge than discrete metrics.

- **Isolating the effect of individual encounters**: In real pretraining, a fact appears at unpredictable intervals interleaved with billions of other tokens. The paper's controlled injection paradigm (injecting specific knowledge every 100 steps with known content and timing) allows clean measurement of the causal effect of each encounter on the model's subsequent knowledge state.

- **Separating memorization from generalization**: A model might be able to reproduce a fact verbatim (memorization) without being able to use it flexibly in new contexts (generalization). The paper's probe design — with memorization probes (exact sentences), semantic generalization probes (paraphrased sentences with same target), and compositional generalization probes (requiring integration of multiple facts) — provides a graded measurement of knowledge depth, not just presence.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames itself as filling a specific gap: the *fine-grained dynamics* of factual knowledge acquisition during pretraining. It positions itself not as proposing a new training method or architectural modification, but as providing the mechanistic understanding that the field currently lacks. This is explicitly stated as an "initial attempt" (Section 1):

> "In this work, we make an initial attempt to understand the dynamics of factual knowledge acquisition in LLM pretraining."

The three research questions the paper poses (Section 1) articulate this agenda precisely: (1) **How** is factual knowledge acquired step-by-step? (2) **How do training conditions** (pretraining stage, model size, batch size) affect acquisition effectiveness? (3) **How is acquired knowledge forgotten**, and what affects the forgetting rate?

These questions are chosen strategically. RQ1 addresses the *mechanism* (the fundamental gap), RQ2 addresses *scalability* (connecting to the scaling laws literature), and RQ3 addresses *retention* (connecting to the long-tail knowledge and deduplication literature). Together, they aim to build a complete picture of the knowledge acquisition lifecycle: initial encoding → reinforcement through repeated exposure → competition with forgetting → eventual stable knowledge state.

The paper also positions its contributions as explanatory rather than merely descriptive. Section 4.4 explicitly demonstrates that the observed dynamics can *explain* three previously-puzzling phenomena: (1) why more training data improves performance (not through an "emergent" improved learning ability, but through more facts crossing the learnability threshold), (2) why long-tail knowledge is poorly acquired (facts with encounter intervals longer than the learnability threshold are effectively unlearnable), and (3) why deduplication helps (paraphrased repetition slows forgetting compared to verbatim duplication). This explanatory ambition distinguishes the work from purely empirical scaling studies.

### Broader Significance

The paper's motivation extends beyond academic curiosity about training dynamics. If the mechanisms of factual knowledge acquisition during pretraining can be understood, the implications are substantial:

- **Data engineering can become principled rather than empirical**: Instead of running expensive ablation studies to determine optimal deduplication strategies or repetition ratios, practitioners could design training data informed by the known dynamics of acquisition and forgetting. For example, if the decay constant of retainability (a) can be estimated for a given model and batch size, one could compute the maximum allowable interval between fact exposures for that fact to remain learnable.

- **Pretraining efficiency could improve**: The finding that later pretraining stages show no improved ability to acquire facts (equal effectivity, Section 4.2) suggests that the enormous cost of training on trillions of tokens provides value primarily through *exposure breadth* (more facts encountered) rather than improved *learning capability* per exposure. This has implications for how pretraining compute should be allocated between model scaling and data scaling.

- **The memorization-generalization tradeoff can be managed**: Understanding that duplicated training data increases memorization effectivity but accelerates forgetting of generalized knowledge (Section 4.3) provides a concrete, mechanistic rationale for deduplication that goes beyond the empirical observation that "it works." It suggests that deduplication is not just about reducing verbatim regurgitation risk — it actively improves the retention of generalized factual knowledge.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This is an **empirical analysis paper** that builds a controlled experimental pipeline to observe and measure what happens inside a large language model at every single training step as it encounters, acquires, and then potentially forgets specific pieces of factual knowledge. The core idea is to treat factual knowledge acquisition as a traceable, step-by-step process—resuming pretraining from intermediate checkpoints, injecting carefully constructed novel facts at known intervals, and monitoring the model's log-probability on probe questions at every training step to establish the dynamics of acquisition, generalization, and forgetting as functions of training conditions (model size, pretraining stage, batch size, and knowledge presentation format).

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The experimental system has five major components connected in a pipeline:

1. **FICTIONAL KNOWLEDGE Dataset (Knowledge Source)** — A set of 120 GPT-4-generated passages describing entirely fictional entities and events, ensuring the model has never encountered these facts before. Each passage is paired with 15 evaluation probes (5 memorization, 5 semantic generalization, 5 compositional generalization) forming cloze tasks with target spans.

2. **OLMo Intermediate Checkpoints (The Subject Models)** — Pretrained OLMo-1B and OLMo-7B models at three stages (early: ~170B tokens, mid: ~500B tokens, late: ~1.5T tokens), loaded with their original optimizer and scheduler states so that continued training is as close as possible to uninterrupted pretraining.

3. **Controlled Knowledge Injection (Training Intervention)** — A procedure that continues pretraining each checkpoint on the original Dolma corpus but periodically replaces part of a batch with injected knowledge from the FICTIONAL KNOWLEDGE dataset, following one of three injection scenarios: duplication (identical knowledge repeated 10 times every 100 steps), paraphrase (paraphrased variants shown each of 10 encounters every 100 steps), or once (single exposure at the start).

4. **Step-Wise Log-Probability Monitoring (Measurement Apparatus)** — At every training step, the system evaluates the model's log-probability of the target span for each probe, producing a high-resolution temporal trace of how the model's encoding of each fact evolves.

5. **Metric Extraction Pipeline (Analysis Framework)** — From the per-step log-probability traces, the system computes three derived metrics: **local acquisition maxima** (the peak log-probability after an encounter, within a window), **effectivity** (the immediate gain in log-probability from an encounter), and **retainability** (the fraction of gained log-probability retained after `$t$` subsequent steps).

Information flows as follows: FICTIONAL KNOWLEDGE passages are generated and validated → probes are constructed for each passage → an OLMo checkpoint is selected and resumed with its original optimizer state → at specified step intervals, the injected knowledge replaces part of the normal pretraining batch → the model updates its parameters on this modified batch → immediately and for all subsequent steps, log-probabilities on all probes are recorded → after the full 2500-step run, the metrics (local acquisition maxima, effectivity, retainability) are extracted from the temporal traces → aggregated statistics across probes and injection events reveal the dynamics.

#### FICTIONAL KNOWLEDGE Dataset: Construction, Probes, and Validation

The paper constructs a dataset of 120 passages, each describing a fictional yet realistic entity. The purpose is to create factual knowledge that is guaranteed to be novel—the model cannot have encountered it during prior pretraining because these entities and events do not exist in the real world. This establishes a clean causal baseline: any subsequent improvement in the model's ability to predict target spans associated with this knowledge can be attributed to the controlled injection, not to prior exposure.

**Passage generation.** Each passage is generated by prompting GPT-4 with a template derived from the ECBD (Entity Cloze By Date) dataset (Onoe et al., 2022). The prompt (Appendix C.1) provides GPT-4 with an example ECBD passage—a Wikipedia-style description of a real entity—and instructs it to produce a new passage by replacing all named entities (people, countries, acts, organizations, etc.) with novel, fictional ones. The generated passage must be "more than 20 sentences" long, must describe events that "have not actually happened in real-world," must be "plausible enough to make someone believe that it is describing a true knowledge," and must "start and finish every sentence with a named entity" to facilitate probe construction. The prompt also forbids generating harmful, prejudiced, or discriminatory content.

A key design constraint emerges from the requirement that each sentence must end with a named entity: this ensures that the passage contains many sentences suitable for cloze-task construction, where the final entity of a sentence can be masked as the target span. The authors repeat the generation process until 120 valid passages are obtained, filtering out any that cannot produce at least five sentences amenable to cloze formatting. This yields the "injected knowledge" set.

**Paraphrase generation for the paraphrase injection scenario.** For each of the first 40 passages (used in the paraphrase injection condition), the authors prompt GPT-4 (Appendix C.2) to produce 9 paraphrased versions. The prompt simply asks GPT-4 to "paraphrase the above text clearly and concisely," yielding 9 alternative phrasings that convey the same factual content. These paraphrased versions are used in the paraphrase injection scenario: at each of 10 encounters (every 100 steps), a different paraphrase is shown rather than repeating the identical text. This design isolates the effect of surface-form variation from the effect of semantic repetition.

An example of an original passage and its paraphrase appears in Appendix Table 3. The original passage is approximately 300 words describing the "fortieth government of Mars" and its leaders Zorgon and Calidus; the paraphrase is roughly the same length but rephrases nearly every sentence while preserving the entity names and factual relationships.

**Probe design: three acquisition depths.** For each of the 120 passages, the authors construct 15 evaluation probes (5 memorization, 5 semantic generalization, 5 compositional generalization), yielding 1,800 probes total. Each probe is a "cloze task"—an incomplete sentence ending at a target span (a short named entity phrase) that the model must predict. The three probe types operationalize different depths of factual knowledge acquisition:

- **Memorization probes**: Exact sentences extracted verbatim from the injected knowledge passage, with the sentence-final named entity designated as the target span. Predicting this target requires the model to reproduce the exact training sequence. Example from Table 4: "Mars, historically known for its centralised sub-planet distribution, underwent significant political reform under **Zorgon's leadership**."

- **Semantic generalization probes**: Paraphrased versions of the memorization probes, produced by prompting GPT-4 (Appendix C.3) with the memorization probe and its target span. The prompt requires that the paraphrased sentence "should be semantically equivalent to the original sentence," "should not contain any additional factual knowledge," and must end with the identical target span. This evaluates whether the model has learned the underlying fact (that Zorgon led political reform) rather than merely the surface string. Example: "Mars, previously recognized for its focused distribution of sub-planets, experienced substantial political transformation during **Zorgon's leadership**."

- **Compositional generalization probes**: Novel sentences that require integrating information from multiple sentences in the injected knowledge passage. These are generated by prompting GPT-4 (Appendix C.4) with the full passage and instructing it to create cloze tasks where "the target can only be accurately answered if the participant can perform complex generalization and integration based on the provided knowledge." The prompt further requires avoiding lexical overlap with the passage, not asking for directly stated facts, and ensuring the probe is comprehensible without additional context. Example from Table 4: "The Zorgon-Calidus government rapidly expedited the transitory phase of the **Martian democratic system**." This fact is not stated verbatim anywhere in the passage—it requires combining the information that the Zorgon-Calidus government was the 40th government of Mars and that Mars's democratic system was entering a transitory phase under this government.

Each passage's 5 compositional generalization probes undergo a validation step (Appendix C.5): GPT-4 is asked to judge whether each probe is "properly designed" with no ambiguity and no need for additional context. Only probes receiving a "yes" judgment are retained. The validation prompt provides examples of ambiguous probes (rated "no") and clear probes (rated "yes") to calibrate GPT-4's judgment.

**Dataset split across injection scenarios.** The 120 passages are partitioned into three groups of 40 for the three injection scenarios: passages 1–40 used for paraphrase injection, 41–80 for duplication injection, and 81–120 for once injection. This ensures that each injection scenario is evaluated on a distinct set of factual knowledge, preventing cross-contamination.

**Why fictional knowledge is essential.** Using real-world facts would make it impossible to establish when the model first encountered the knowledge, since any pretrained checkpoint might have already seen it in prior training data. The fictional knowledge design guarantees that any improvement in log-probability after injection is causally attributable to the injection itself. Moreover, because the entities are entirely novel, there is no possibility of the model leveraging prior parametric knowledge to "guess" the target span—any correct prediction must come from the injected information.

#### Controlled Knowledge Injection: Batch Modification and Injection Scenarios

The core experimental paradigm is **continued pretraining with controlled knowledge injection**: the authors take an intermediate OLMo checkpoint (with its optimizer and scheduler states loaded), resume training on the original Dolma pretraining corpus in the original order, but at specific step intervals replace part of a training batch with injected knowledge from the FICTIONAL KNOWLEDGE dataset.

**Why OLMo.** The choice of OLMo (Groeneveld et al., 2024) is deliberate and practically motivated: OLMo is one of the few LLM projects that publicly releases not only model weights at intermediate checkpoints, but also (1) the exact sequence of training data instances used at each step, (2) the optimizer state (momentum vectors, etc.), and (3) the learning rate scheduler state. This makes it possible to resume pretraining in a way that is almost identical to the original training run—the model sees the same data in the same order, with the same optimizer momentum, and the same learning rate schedule—except for the injected knowledge at controlled intervals. Without these artifacts, continued pretraining would involve a distribution shift (different data order) and an optimizer cold-start that would confound the measurements.

**Batch modification procedure.** The authors load the training batch that OLMo would have seen at a specific pretraining step, then modify it as follows: for each row in the batch (each batch has 2048 rows of sequence length 2048, totaling approximately 4 million tokens), the injected knowledge passage is prepended to the beginning of the row, and the original sequence is truncated from the end by the token length of the injected knowledge. This preserves the original batch dimensions (2048 × 2048) and ensures that the total number of tokens per batch remains constant. The rest of the original Dolma sequence fills the remaining positions in each row. The authors state they "adopt this method to deviate as little as possible from the original pretraining data distribution."

Each injected knowledge passage is short enough to fit into one pretraining sequence in the batch, so it replaces only the prefix of one row, not the entire row.

**Injection timing and repetition.** For the duplication and paraphrase injection scenarios, the modified batch is presented to the model 10 times, with an interval of 100 training steps between each presentation. This means: at step 0, the batch containing injected knowledge is used; at steps 1–99, normal Dolma batches are used; at step 100, the same (duplication) or a paraphrased (paraphrase) injected knowledge batch is used again; and so on, for 10 total injections spanning 1000 steps. After the 10th injection (step 900 for duplication and paraphrase), the model continues training on normal Dolma batches for an additional 1500 steps (steps 1000–2500), enabling measurement of forgetting dynamics.

For the once injection scenario, the modified batch is presented only once at step 0, and the model continues on normal Dolma batches for the remaining 2500 steps.

The interval of 100 steps and 10 repetitions are chosen to provide enough signal for measuring both the immediate acquisition (effectivity) and the longer-term retention (retainability), while the total 2500-step run (approximately 10 billion tokens at batch size 2048) provides sufficient forgetting data to estimate power-law decay parameters.

**The Dolma corpus for continued pretraining.** The Dolma corpus (Soldaini et al., 2024) is the same corpus used for OLMo's original pretraining. To ensure that the model never sees the same data twice during the continued pretraining experiment, the authors use Dolma data starting from the 360,000th step of OLMo's original pretraining run. This guarantees that the data used in the continued pretraining experiment is novel (never seen by the model before in its original pretraining) while maintaining the same data distribution.

**Why this injection paradigm answers the research questions.** The controlled injection design allows the authors to:

- **Measure the causal effect of a single exposure** by comparing log-probability immediately before and after an injection step.
- **Measure the cumulative effect of repeated exposures** by tracking log-probability across the 10 injection events.
- **Quantify forgetting** by observing the log-probability decay in the 1500 steps after the final injection, when the model sees only unrelated Dolma data.
- **Compare duplicate vs. paraphrase presentation** by holding the semantic content constant while varying surface form.
- **Compare different pretraining stages and model sizes** by running the identical injection protocol on different checkpoints, using the same injected knowledge and the same Dolma continuation data, isolating the effect of the checkpoint's state (its optimization trajectory, learning rate, and prior knowledge) on acquisition dynamics.

#### The Mathematical Framework: Local Acquisition Maxima, Effectivity, and Retainability

The paper defines three core metrics that convert raw per-step log-probability traces into interpretable quantities characterizing the knowledge acquisition process. These definitions are formalized in Section 3 and operationalized through Equations 1–3.

**Local Acquisition Maxima (Definition 1).** The fundamental measurement challenge is that the effect of updating the model with a minibatch containing factual knowledge is not instantaneous at the single step: because LLM optimizers use momentum (AdamW with `$\beta_1 = 0.9$` in OLMo), the gradient from a given sequence contributes to parameter updates over multiple subsequent steps, with its influence decaying approximately as `$0.9^t$`. The model's log-probability of the target span therefore rises over several steps after an injection before falling again (as other training batches dilute the effect). The notion of a "local acquisition maxima" captures the peak of this transient improvement.

Formally:

$$t_{\text{LAM}}(q, i) = \arg\max_{t_i < t \leq t_i + t_w} \ell(q; \theta_t)$$

where `$q$` is a probe (an evaluation instance), `$t_i \in T_k$` is a training step where the model was updated with a minibatch containing the injected knowledge `$k$` (for the `$i$`-th time, where `$T_k$` is the set of all such steps), `$\theta_t$` is the model's parameters after the `$t$`-th update, `$\ell(q; \theta_t)$` is the log-probability of the target span of probe `$q$` under the model at step `$t$`, and `$t_w = 50$` is a fixed window size.

**What it computes:** For a given probe and a given injection event (the `$i$`-th time the model encounters the knowledge), the local acquisition maxima is the training step within a 50-step window after `$t_i$` where the model achieves its highest log-probability on the target span of that probe. It represents the peak of the transient improvement induced by that specific exposure.

**Why `$t_w = 50$`:** The authors justify this choice in Footnote 2: "The `$\beta_1$` of AdamW optimizer is configured to 0.9 in our experiments, implying that the contribution of the gradient of a given sequence to the momentum will be reduced to approximately `$0.9^{50} \approx 0.0052$` after 50 steps." In other words, after 50 steps, the momentum contribution from the injection batch has decayed to roughly 0.5% of its original value, meaning its influence on parameter updates is effectively exhausted. The window `$t_w = 50$` therefore captures the full temporal extent of the local effect. If an optimizer without momentum were used (e.g., RMSProp), the authors note in Footnote 3 that "the local effect of training the model at timestep `$t$` will be fully reflected immediately after that step" and `$t_w$` should be 1, reducing `$t_{\text{LAM}}$` to `$t + 1$`.

**Why this definition matters:** Without identifying a consistent reference point for "post-acquisition" state, comparisons across different probes, models, and injection events would be confounded by the shape of the transient response. The local acquisition maxima provides a standardized point for measuring the peak acquisition effect, analogous to measuring the maximum concentration of a drug in blood plasma after administration rather than measuring at an arbitrary post-dose time.

**Effectivity (Definition 2).** This metric quantifies the immediate improvement in the model's log-probability of a fact attributable to a single exposure.

$$E(q, i) = \ell(q; \theta_{t_{\text{LAM}}(q,i)}) - \ell(q; \theta_{t_i})$$

where `$\ell(q; \theta_{t_i})$` is the log-probability immediately *before* the `$i$`-th injection (specifically, at the step right before the batch containing the knowledge is processed), and `$\ell(q; \theta_{t_{\text{LAM}}(q,i)})$` is the log-probability at the local acquisition maxima after that injection.

**What it computes:** The absolute increase in log-probability from just before the knowledge encounter to the peak of the post-encounter transient. This represents the *marginal contribution* of that specific exposure to the model's encoding of the fact, net of any pre-existing knowledge from prior exposures.

**Why this form:** By measuring the pre-injection baseline at `$t_i$` (rather than some earlier step), effectivity isolates the contribution of the current exposure from the cumulative effect of all previous exposures. This is crucial because in the duplication and paraphrase scenarios, the model encounters the same knowledge multiple times; effectivity measures what the `$i$`-th exposure *adds* beyond what the first `$i-1$` exposures already contributed. The choice of using `$t_{\text{LAM}}$` rather than `$t_i + 1$` accounts for the momentum-induced delay, ensuring that the measurement captures the full benefit of the exposure rather than underestimating it.

**Why log-probability rather than accuracy:** The authors use log-probability because it provides a continuous, fine-grained signal of how strongly the model associates the target span with the probe context. Accuracy (correct vs. incorrect token prediction) would be a coarse binary signal that loses information about partial acquisition—a fact might be "known" in the sense that the model assigns it higher probability than alternatives, even if it's not yet the top-1 prediction. Log-probability reveals the *strength* of encoding, not just its presence or absence, following Schaeffer et al. (2023)'s argument that emergent abilities in LLMs are better captured by continuous metrics than discrete ones.

**Retainability (Definition 3).** This metric quantifies what fraction of the total acquisition (accumulated across all injection events) is retained after `$t$` steps of training on unrelated data.

$$R(q, t) = \frac{\ell(q; \theta_{t_{\text{LAM}}(q,N) + t}) - \ell(q; \theta_{t_{\text{pre}}})}{\ell(q; \theta_{t_{\text{LAM}}(q,N)}) - \ell(q; \theta_{t_{\text{pre}}})}$$

where `$N = |T_k|$` is the total number of times the knowledge was injected (10 for duplication and paraphrase, 1 for once), `$t_{\text{pre}} = \min(T_k)$` is the step just before the *first* injection (i.e., the baseline before the model has ever seen the knowledge), `$t_{\text{LAM}}(q, N)$` is the local acquisition maxima after the *last* (the `$N$`-th) injection, and `$t \geq 0$` is the number of steps after `$t_{\text{LAM}}(q, N)$`.

**What it computes:** The fraction of the total log-probability gain (from baseline to peak after last exposure) that remains at `$t$` steps after the last peak. At `$t = 0$`, `$R(q, 0) = 1$` (100% retained at the peak). As `$t$` increases and the model forgets, `$R(q, t)$` decreases toward 0, where 0 means the log-probability has returned to the pre-injection baseline (complete forgetting). Negative values would indicate the model has *lower* log-probability for the fact than before it was ever exposed—effectively "unlearning" it relative to its initial state.

**Why this normalization:** The denominator `$\ell(q; \theta_{t_{\text{LAM}}(q,N)}) - \ell(q; \theta_{t_{\text{pre}}})$` represents the total acquisition achieved across all `$N$` exposures. Normalizing by this quantity makes retainability comparable across probes with different total acquisition magnitudes—a fact that was strongly acquired and then partially forgotten can be compared to a fact that was weakly acquired and almost entirely forgotten, because both are expressed as fractions of their own peak improvement.

**The critical x-intercept.** A key quantity derived from the retainability curve is its x-intercept: the value of `$t$` (or equivalently, the number of training tokens) at which `$R(q, t) = 0$`. This represents the number of subsequent steps after which the model would completely lose the acquired knowledge if it were never encountered again. The authors use this as a proxy for the "learnability threshold"—the maximum interval between encounters of a fact such that the fact remains learnable. If a fact appears in the pretraining data with intervals longer than this threshold, each exposure's gain is fully erased before the next exposure occurs, and the fact never accumulates to a decodable probability.

**Outlier detection.** The authors apply outlier detection using the Interquartile Range (IQR) method with a factor of 1.5 to both effectivity and retainability measurements. Specifically, for each metric, data points falling outside `$[Q1 - 1.5 \times \text{IQR}, Q3 + 1.5 \times \text{IQR}]$` are excluded. The authors note this is "particularly important for the measurement of retainability, as the small number of cases which showed no acquisition through training can give a very large value due to the very small denominator in Eq. 3." In other words, if a probe's total acquisition (the denominator) is near zero, small fluctuations in the numerator produce enormous, unstable retainability values that distort averages. The IQR filter removes these pathological cases.

**The power-law fit to retainability.** The authors observe empirically (Section 4.3) that `$R(q, t)$` plotted against `$\log(t)$` fits a linear function very well, with `$R^2 > 0.80$` for memorization and semantic generalization and `$R^2 > 0.65$` for compositional generalization. This implies a power-law relationship:

$$\Delta R(p, t) \approx -a \cdot \log\left(\frac{t_2}{t_1}\right)$$

where `$a > 0$` is the decay constant (the absolute slope of the log-linear fit). The decay constant `$a$` quantifies the *rate* of forgetting: larger `$a$` means faster fractional loss of acquired log-probability per order of magnitude of training steps. The decay constant is the primary summary statistic used to compare forgetting dynamics across conditions (Table 2, Table 7, etc.).

#### Model Checkpoint Selection and Training Configuration

**Why three pretraining stages.** The authors select three OLMo intermediate checkpoints representing early, middle, and late pretraining. For OLMo-7B, these correspond to checkpoints trained on approximately 177B tokens (early), 500B tokens (mid), and 1.5T tokens (late). For OLMo-1B, the corresponding stages are 168B (early), 494B (mid), and 1.5T (late) tokens. The slight mismatch at the early stage (177B vs. 168B) is because "the initial checkpoints of OLMo-1B are stored in units of 10000," so the closest available checkpoint to 177B is selected.

These three stages span a 9× range in training data volume, from a model that has seen substantial but far-from-converged data (170B tokens) to one that is near the end of pretraining (1.5T tokens). Comparing acquisition dynamics across these stages tests whether the model's ability to acquire new facts *per exposure* improves, degrades, or remains constant throughout pretraining.

**Learning rate values at each stage.** Because OLMo uses cosine learning rate decay, the learning rate at the start of continued pretraining differs substantially across stages. Appendix Table 5 reports the initial learning rates:

| Model Size | Early | Mid   | Late    |
|------------|-------|-------|---------|
| OLMo-1B    | 0.000398 | 0.000379 | 0.000230 |
| OLMo-7B    | 0.000280 | 0.000237 | 0.000101 |

The late checkpoints have substantially lower learning rates (roughly 2.5–4× smaller than early checkpoints), which could confound comparisons of effectivity—a later checkpoint might show lower effectivity simply because a smaller learning rate produces smaller parameter updates per exposure, not because its inherent learning capability has changed.

**Constant learning rate control experiment (Appendix F).** To disentangle the effect of learning rate from the effect of pretraining stage, the authors conduct an additional experiment: they train all three OLMo-7B checkpoints (early, mid, late) with the same constant learning rate, obtained by "averaging the starting learning rates of three checkpoints." This control experiment uses a reduced batch size of 128 (due to computational constraints) and removes learning rate decay entirely. The optimizer states are loaded to provide a warm start, and all other conditions are identical to the main experiment. The results (Appendix Figure 17 and Table 9) demonstrate that effectivity still does not significantly improve at later stages even with equal learning rates, confirming that the non-increasing effectivity is a genuine property of the pretraining progression, not an artifact of learning rate decay.

**Batch size comparison experiment (Appendix G).** To study the effect of batch size on forgetting, the authors run an experiment with the OLMo-7B mid checkpoint using a batch size reduced by a factor of 16—from 2048 (the original pretraining batch size) to 128. The experiment uses the initial learning rate for that checkpoint (0.000237, from Appendix Table 5) and re-initializes the optimizer state (rather than loading the warm-start state). The authors acknowledge this causes "unstable dynamics in early steps, but the dynamics are stabilized soon." All other experimental parameters (injection intervals, injection scenarios, probe evaluation, continuation data) remain identical.

**Hardware and runtime.** Training each 2500-step run takes "approximately 3 days using 8 80GB A100 GPUs" (Appendix D). With 7 model configurations (3 stages × 2 model sizes, minus the 1B early checkpoint's unstable dynamics, plus the batch-size experiment), this represents approximately 20+ GPU-weeks of computation.

#### The FICTIONAL KNOWLEDGE Dataset as a Measurement Framework

The probes serve a dual role: they are both the *evaluation instruments* for measuring knowledge acquisition (providing the `$\ell(q; \theta_t)$` signal at each step) and the *operational definitions* of what it means to have acquired the knowledge at different depths.

**Operationalizing memorization.** A memorization probe is an exact sentence from the injected passage. If the model assigns high log-probability to the target span in this exact context, it has memorized that specific sequence. This is the shallowest level of acquisition—it does not require understanding the fact, only reproducing the training string.

**Operationalizing semantic generalization.** A semantic probe is a paraphrase of a memorization probe, preserving the target span but varying the surrounding words. If the model assigns high log-probability to the target span in this *new* context, it has learned the underlying fact (the association between the entity and its property) rather than merely the surface string. This is a deeper level of acquisition because it requires the model to have abstracted the fact from its training presentation format.

**Operationalizing compositional generalization.** A compositional probe requires integrating information from multiple sentences in the passage that are not contiguous and may not even be adjacent. The target span is typically a composite concept (e.g., "Martian democratic system") that is never stated as such in the passage but can be inferred by combining: (1) the Zorgon-Calidus government governed Mars, (2) Mars's democratic system was entering a transitory phase. This is the deepest level of acquisition because it requires the model to have formed a coherent internal representation of the entity and its properties, enabling novel inferences beyond what was explicitly stated.

**Why five probes per type per passage.** Using five probes per type per passage provides several benefits: it measures multiple facets of the same knowledge (the passage describes many facts), it averages out noise from individual probe difficulty, and it enables outlier detection at the probe level. With 40 passages per injection scenario and 5 probes per type, each injection scenario yields 200 probes per acquisition depth, providing sufficient statistical power for the aggregated analyses in Section 4.

**Why the target span is always a short named entity phrase.** The target span design (short, domain-specific entity phrases) is chosen because it makes the log-probability measurement unambiguous: the model either assigns high probability to the correct entity or it does not, and the measurement is not confounded by multiple acceptable completions. If the target were a common word or phrase, the model might assign high probability for reasons unrelated to the injected knowledge (e.g., because it fits the linguistic context generically). Named entities are highly specific and their association with the probe context is the direct product of factual knowledge.

#### How Design Choices Support the Research Goals

Each component of the experimental design serves a specific analytical purpose:

- **Fictional knowledge** → Clean causal identification: any log-probability change after injection is attributable to the injection.
- **Three probe depths** → Distinguishes rote memorization from flexible factual learning.
- **Three injection scenarios** → Tests whether surface-form variation (paraphrase vs. duplication) and total exposure count (once vs. 10×) affect acquisition and forgetting differently.
- **Three pretraining stages** → Tests whether the model's ability to acquire facts per exposure changes over the course of pretraining.
- **Two model sizes** → Tests whether larger models acquire facts more effectively per exposure, or merely benefit from greater capacity to store more facts.
- **Two batch sizes** → Tests whether the optimization noise level (smaller batches → noisier gradients) affects forgetting rates.
- **Step-wise log-probability monitoring** → Provides high-resolution temporal dynamics rather than endpoint-only comparisons.
- **The window-based local acquisition maxima** → Accounts for momentum-induced delayed effects, ensuring fair comparison across conditions.
- **The retainability normalization** → Makes forgetting comparable across facts with different acquisition strengths.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Factual Knowledge Acquisition as Stepwise Micro-Accumulation, Not Emergent Capability Gain

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is reframing factual knowledge acquisition in LLM pretraining not as an *emergent capability* that improves with scale, but as a **cumulative counting process**: each time the model sees a fact in a training batch, it receives a small but immediate bump in log-probability for that fact, which then partially decays before the next encounter. The implication — counterintuitive given the scaling laws literature — is that pretraining on more tokens does **not** make the model better at learning from each individual exposure.

This stands in direct tension with the dominant narrative around emergent abilities (Wei et al., 2022) and scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022), which frame larger models trained on more data as acquiring qualitatively improved capabilities — sudden jumps in task performance that "emerge" at certain scale thresholds. The micro-accumulation model offers a radically simpler explanation: later checkpoints perform better on knowledge-intensive tasks not because they have developed a superior *ability to learn*, but because they have simply accumulated more facts through more exposures over more training steps. The per-exposure gain — what the paper calls **effectivity** — is essentially constant across pretraining stages (Figure 3, left panel).

This reframing has deep implications. If factual knowledge acquisition is primarily **exposure-limited** rather than **capability-limited** during most of pretraining, then investments in longer training runs derive their value from *breadth of coverage* (more distinct facts encountered at learnable intervals) rather than from some qualitative maturation of the learning mechanism. The paper's constant-learning-rate control experiment (Appendix F, Figure 17) is crucial here: it shows that even when learning rate is held equal across stages, later checkpoints still show no improved effectivity. The late checkpoint's advantage is purely in *what it has already stored*, not in *how effectively it can store new information*.

This is a fundamental rather than incremental insight because it challenges the teleological assumption that later training stages are "smarter." The paper's evidence suggests they are instead merely *more experienced* — they have encountered more facts more times. The distinction matters enormously for resource allocation: if per-exposure acquisition efficiency doesn't improve, pretraining compute should be allocated to maximize the number of facts that cross the learnability threshold (sufficiently frequent encounters), not to achieve some qualitative capability milestone.

### Innovation 2: The Learnability Threshold — A Mechanistic Explanation for the Long-Tail Knowledge Problem

Prior work documented that LLMs perform poorly on rare factual knowledge (Kandpal et al., 2022; Mallen et al., 2022), but the *mechanism* of this failure was a black box. The paper provides the first mechanistic account: each fact has a **learnability threshold** — the maximum interval between exposures beyond which the knowledge gained from one encounter is completely erased by forgetting before the next encounter arrives. Facts in the long tail of the pretraining distribution have encounter intervals exceeding this threshold and are therefore structurally unlearnable regardless of total training duration.

What makes this distinctive is that it converts a purely statistical observation (frequency-performance correlation) into a dynamical constraint. The paper extracts the learnability threshold from the x-intercept of the retainability curve (Figure 5; Appendix Table 6), which represents the number of training tokens after which `$R(q, t) = 0$` — complete loss of the acquired log-probability gain. By fitting power-law decay models to the retainability data (Figure 4), the paper can estimate this threshold for different conditions. For the OLMo-7B mid checkpoint under duplication injection, the anticipated x-intercept for memorization is approximately `$10^{11}$` tokens — meaning if the same fact doesn't appear again within roughly 100 billion tokens, it's effectively erased.

This threshold is not a fixed property of the model; the paper shows it is substantially affected by training conditions. Reducing batch size from 2048 to 128 shortens the learnability threshold by a factor of dozens (compare Appendix Tables 6 and 11), dramatically shrinking the set of learnable knowledge. Paraphrasing versus duplication also shifts the threshold (paraphrase scenarios generally show smaller decay constants in Table 2, implying longer retention). This means the set of facts an LLM can ultimately learn is not just a function of what's in the training data — it's a function of the *interaction* between data frequency distribution and the model's forgetting dynamics, which are themselves shaped by training hyperparameters.

The significance goes beyond explaining long-tail failure. It provides a **design target** for data engineering: if you need a model to know a specific set of facts, you must ensure each fact's encounter interval in the training data is shorter than the learnability threshold for your chosen batch size and deduplication strategy. This makes data curation a quantitatively constrained optimization problem rather than an empirical guessing game.

### Innovation 3: Deduplication Helps by Slowing Generalization Forgetting, Not Just by Preventing Verbatim Memorization

Data deduplication is a widely validated best practice (Lee et al., 2021; Abbas et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2023), but the community's understanding of *why* it works has been largely intuitive: duplicated data causes models to memorize and regurgitate training strings, which is both a privacy risk and a quality degradation. The paper's finding that duplication accelerates forgetting of **generalized** factual knowledge compared to paraphrased presentation reveals a more subtle mechanism that has nothing to do with verbatim regurgitation.

The evidence is in the decay constants (Table 2): for OLMo-7B mid checkpoint, memorization under duplication forgets with `$a = 0.25$` versus `$a = 0.21$` under paraphrase; for semantic generalization, `$a = 0.25$` versus `$a = 0.23$`; for compositional generalization, `$a = 0.20$` versus `$a = 0.15$`. The duplication condition consistently produces higher decay constants — meaning the model loses its grasp on the generalized fact faster when it was trained on repeated identical sequences rather than diverse paraphrases. The paper's interpretation (Section 4.4) is that duplicated presentations cause the model to overweight the surface form, creating a representation that is more brittle and less transferable, which in turn is more vulnerable to being overwritten by subsequent unrelated training batches.

This is a significant reframing because it shifts the rationale for deduplication from *output quality* (reducing plagiarism) to *learning dynamics* (improving knowledge retention). It also resolves the apparent contradiction between "repetition helps learning" and "deduplication helps performance": repetition *does* increase the immediate gain per exposure (Figure 3 shows higher effectivity for duplication than paraphrase), but it also increases the forgetting rate, and the net effect over time favors paraphrased presentation for sustained generalization. The dynamic is visible in Figure 2: duplication produces a larger spike at each injection but faster subsequent decay, so the two conditions converge to similar terminal log-probability levels by `$t = 2000$`.

This insight has direct practical implications: when repeating important facts in training data (e.g., for domain-adapted models), practitioners should prefer diverse paraphrases over exact duplication — not primarily to avoid verbatim memorization, but to build more robust, forget-resistant knowledge representations.

### Innovation 4: Larger Batch Sizes as a Robustness Mechanism Against Forgetting

The finding that batch size dramatically affects forgetting rates is both novel and practically consequential. The paper shows that reducing batch size from 2048 to 128 (a 16× reduction) substantially increases decay constants (Table 10 vs. Table 2) — meaning smaller batches cause faster forgetting — and, critically, reduces the anticipated x-intercept (learnability threshold) by "dozens of times" (Section 4.4, comparing Appendix Tables 6 and 11). The effect is particularly pronounced for compositional generalization, where the decay constant rises more sharply under reduced batch size than for memorization or semantic generalization.

This is distinctive because batch size in LLM pretraining has primarily been studied through the lens of optimization: larger batches enable more parallel computation and reduce gradient noise, but their effect on *knowledge retention dynamics* has been unexplored. The dominant narrative has been that batch size is a throughput knob — you make it as large as hardware allows. The paper's finding adds a qualitatively different dimension: larger batch size is a **forgetting suppression mechanism** that extends the learnability threshold, thereby expanding the set of facts the model can ultimately acquire.

Why might this happen mechanistically? The paper doesn't fully answer this, but the likely mechanism relates to gradient noise: smaller batches produce noisier gradient estimates, which means each update pushes parameters in directions less aligned with the true data distribution gradient. Unrelated facts — facts whose gradients are stochastic approximations of the population loss rather than precisely aligned with it — may be more easily "overwritten" by this noise. Larger batches produce cleaner gradient signals that more precisely encode the training data distribution, potentially creating parameter updates that are less destructive to previously acquired knowledge. This is speculative but consistent with the observation that the forgetting acceleration under small batch size is worse for compositional generalization (which requires integrating multiple facts) than for simple memorization — more complex, distributed representations may be more vulnerable to noisy gradient interference.

The practical implication is significant: the choice of batch size in pretraining is not just a hardware efficiency decision — it directly shapes what knowledge the model can learn by modulating the forgetting rate. A batch size that is "large enough" for training throughput may still be suboptimal for knowledge retention, suggesting that batch size should be optimized as a knowledge-acquisition hyperparameter, not just a compute-utilization one.

### Innovation 5: Disentangling Model Scale Benefits from Pretraining Data Scale Benefits

The paper provides a clear empirical dissociation between the benefits of scaling model size and scaling pretraining data for factual knowledge acquisition. Larger models (7B vs. 1B) show consistently higher effectivity across all acquisition depths and injection scenarios (Figure 3, right panel) — they learn more from each exposure. But models trained on more tokens (late vs. early pretraining stage) show no improvement in effectivity (Figure 3, left panel). The benefits are qualitatively different: model scale improves *per-exposure learning efficiency*, while data scale provides *more exposures to more facts*.

This matters because the scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) has treated model parameters and training tokens as jointly optimizing factors under a total compute budget, but has not distinguished the *mechanisms* by which each factor contributes to performance. This paper's finding suggests that the mechanisms are distinct: more parameters → each encounter with a fact produces a stronger update (higher effectivity); more tokens → more facts are encountered with sufficiently short intervals to cross the learnability threshold and accumulate to decodable probability. This is not merely a nuance — it has implications for how pretraining compute should be allocated. If your goal is to maximize the breadth of factual knowledge, data scaling (more tokens → more facts crossing the threshold) may dominate model scaling. If your goal is depth of understanding for a fixed set of facts, model scaling (higher per-exposure efficiency) may be more important.

The finding also contextualizes why the late checkpoint's lower learning rate does not explain the flat effectivity across stages: the constant-learning-rate control (Appendix F) shows that even when the late checkpoint is trained with the same learning rate as the early checkpoint, its effectivity doesn't improve. This means the late checkpoint hasn't developed a superior learning mechanism — it has simply accumulated more facts through more exposures at a per-exposure rate that has been essentially constant throughout pretraining. This is consistent with recent work suggesting that the "emergent" appearance of capabilities at scale may be an artifact of nonlinear evaluation metrics (Schaeffer et al., 2023) — the underlying log-probability improvements are continuous and cumulative, not discontinuous jumps.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The FICTIONAL KNOWLEDGE dataset, constructed by the authors, contains 120 GPT-4-generated passages describing fictional entities with realistic properties. Each passage is paired with 15 evaluation probes: 5 memorization probes (exact sentences from the passage), 5 semantic generalization probes (paraphrased sentences preserving the target entity span), and 5 compositional generalization probes (requiring integration of information from multiple sentences in the passage), for a total of 1,800 probes. The 120 passages are partitioned into three groups of 40 for the three injection scenarios: passages 1–40 for paraphrase injection, 41–80 for duplication injection, and 81–120 for once injection.

- **Base model(s).** The experiments use OLMo (Groeneveld et al., 2024) at two scales: OLMo-1B and OLMo-7B. The choice of OLMo is driven by the public availability of intermediate checkpoints, optimizer states, learning rate scheduler states, and the exact sequence of pretraining data batches — enabling continued pretraining that closely replicates the original training run. Three intermediate checkpoints are used at each model scale, representing early, middle, and late pretraining stages: for OLMo-7B, approximately 177B tokens (early), 500B tokens (mid), and 1.5T tokens (late); for OLMo-1B, approximately 168B tokens (early), 494B tokens (mid), and 1.5T tokens (late).

- **Metrics.** The primary metrics are three quantities derived from per-step log-probability traces of target spans on evaluation probes. **Effectivity** — the absolute increase in log-probability from immediately before a knowledge injection to the local acquisition maxima (peak within a 50-step window after injection), measuring the immediate gain per exposure. **Retainability** — the fraction of total acquired log-probability (from pre-injection baseline to peak after the final injection) that remains after t subsequent steps, normalized so that R(q,0) = 1 (100% retained at the peak) and R(q,t) = 0 when log-probability returns to the pre-injection baseline. **Decay constant (a)** — the absolute slope of the linear fit of retainability against log(t), quantifying the rate of forgetting. Outlier detection using the IQR method with factor 1.5 is applied to effectivity and retainability measurements, particularly important for retainability where cases with near-zero total acquisition (denominator) produce unstable ratios.

- **Baselines.** This paper is primarily descriptive and analytical rather than comparative; it does not propose a new method competing against prior approaches. The internal baselines are the three injection scenarios themselves (duplication, paraphrase, once), the three pretraining stages (early, mid, late), the two model sizes (1B, 7B), and the two batch sizes (2048, 128). Comparisons are made across these conditions, not against external baselines.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The unit of training progress is the **training step**, where each step processes one batch of 2048 sequences × 2048 tokens = approximately 4 million tokens (at the standard batch size of 2048). For the reduced batch size experiment (batch size 128), each step processes 128 × 2048 = approximately 256K tokens. The total experimental run is 2,500 steps: 1,000 steps during the injection phase (10 injections at 100-step intervals, with the once scenario having only 1 injection at step 0) followed by 1,500 additional steps of normal Dolma pretraining to measure forgetting. At batch size 2048, this corresponds to approximately 10 billion tokens total. At batch size 128, it corresponds to approximately 640 million tokens. The x-axes in retainability plots are presented in both training steps and training tokens (Figure 5 shows the conversion). Each 2,500-step run takes approximately 3 days on 8 × 80GB A100 GPUs.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation in the traditional train/validation/test sense, since it is not training a model to select among competing methods. The outlier detection using IQR with factor 1.5 serves as the primary statistical filtering mechanism. For the power-law fits to retainability curves, the authors report R² values to assess fit quality: R² > 0.80 for memorization and semantic generalization, and R² > 0.65 for compositional generalization across most conditions. The decay constants in Table 2 are reported with standard errors (e.g., 0.25 ± 0.0019 for memorization under duplication at the mid checkpoint). For the learned constant learning rate control experiment (Appendix F), optimizer states are loaded (warm start) but the learning rate is fixed across stages to isolate the effect of pretraining stage from learning rate differences. For the batch size comparison experiment (Appendix G), optimizer states are re-initialized (cold start) since the goal is to compare batch size effects independently of the warm-start state.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Factual Knowledge Acquisition as Micro-Accumulation with Forgetting

The central empirical finding is illustrated in Figure 2, which plots the average log-probability of target spans across all probes against training steps for OLMo-7B mid checkpoint under three injection scenarios. The pattern is visually unambiguous: after each injection (marked by dotted vertical lines), there is an "immediate and distinctive increase" in log-probability across all three acquisition depths (memorization, semantic generalization, compositional generalization). However, between injections, the log-probability decays back downward as the model processes unrelated Dolma batches. The overall trajectory is a sawtooth pattern — upward jumps at injection steps, downward drift between them — that gradually accumulates to a higher baseline by the end of the injection phase.

The magnitude of the per-injection jump follows a consistent hierarchy: memorization shows the largest gain, followed by semantic generalization, with compositional generalization showing the smallest gain. This is visible in all three panels of Figure 2 (the purple memorization line has the highest peaks, the green compositional line the lowest). However, in the paraphrase injection scenario (Figure 2, center), the gap between memorization and semantic generalization "almost disappears" — the two lines track each other closely, suggesting that when knowledge is presented in varied surface forms, the model encodes the underlying fact approximately as strongly as it encodes the surface string.

The forgetting dynamics also differ by scenario. Under duplication injection (Figure 2, top), the post-injection decay is visibly steeper than under paraphrase injection — the peaks erode faster. By t = 2000 (the end of the measurement period), the terminal log-probability levels are similar across the duplication and paraphrase scenarios, because the larger per-injection gains under duplication are offset by faster decay. The once injection scenario (Figure 2, bottom) serves as a control: a single injection produces a single spike followed by monotonic decay, demonstrating that without repeated exposure, even an initially strong acquisition is largely erased within 2,000 steps.

These dynamics are consistent across all OLMo-7B pretraining stages (Appendix Figures 6 and 7 for early and late checkpoints), suggesting this is a general property of the pretraining process, not specific to a particular training phase. However, the OLMo-1B early checkpoint (Appendix Figure 8) shows "much more unstable dynamics than those of later checkpoints," with curves exhibiting "much more drastic fluctuations." The OLMo-1B mid and late checkpoints (Appendix Figures 9 and 10) stabilize to dynamics qualitatively similar to the OLMo-7B checkpoints. The paper interprets this as suggesting that "pretraining on a certain number of tokens may be required for the model to acquire factual knowledge stably and that such a threshold may be higher for smaller models" (Section 4.1).

#### Effectivity: How Much Is Learned Per Exposure

Figure 3 presents the average effectivity — the log-probability gain per injection, averaged across all probes and all injection events — broken down by pretraining stage (left panel) and model size (right panel), for each of the three acquisition depths and three injection scenarios.

**The null effect of pretraining stage on effectivity (Figure 3, left panel).** For OLMo-7B, comparing the early (170B), mid (500B), and late (1.5T) checkpoints across all three injection scenarios (duplication, paraphrase, once), there is no clear trend of increasing effectivity with more pretraining tokens. In the duplication scenario, average effectivity for memorization is approximately 0.45–0.55 for all three stages (the bars are of similar height). For semantic generalization, effectivity hovers around 0.2–0.3 across stages. For compositional generalization, it remains at 0.1–0.2. The paraphrase and once scenarios show the same flat pattern: no systematic improvement at later pretraining stages. The once scenario produces the highest absolute effectivity values (visible by the tallest bars in the rightmost panel), which the paper attributes to the fact that "the effectivity is higher when the model encounters the injected knowledge for the first time" (Section 4.2; see also Appendix H).

This finding is one of the paper's most surprising results: models trained on 1.5T tokens do not learn more efficiently *per exposure* than models trained on only 170B tokens. The late checkpoint has seen approximately 9× more pretraining data, yet its ability to encode a new fact from a single batch is essentially unchanged.

**The positive effect of model scale on effectivity (Figure 3, right panel).** Comparing OLMo-1B mid checkpoint against OLMo-7B mid checkpoint, the 7B model consistently shows higher effectivity across all acquisition depths and injection scenarios. In the duplication scenario, 7B effectivity for memorization is approximately 0.55 versus roughly 0.35 for 1B — a roughly 57% improvement. For semantic generalization, 7B shows approximately 0.28 versus roughly 0.15 for 1B. For compositional generalization, 7B shows approximately 0.15 versus roughly 0.08 for 1B. The pattern holds across the paraphrase and once scenarios, with the relative advantage of 7B over 1B being most pronounced in the once scenario. The paper notes (Section 4.2) that for "a fair comparison of the effectivity of the 1B and 7B models, the OLMo-1B Mid checkpoint is trained using the same initial learning rate as the OLMo-7B Mid checkpoint" (see Appendix Table 5 for specific values).

The contrast between the two panels of Figure 3 is the key empirical dissociation: scaling model size improves per-exposure learning efficiency, while scaling pretraining data volume does not. This is not an interaction effect that becomes apparent only at certain budgets — it is a qualitative difference in what each scaling axis contributes to factual knowledge acquisition.

**Effectivity across injection counts (Appendix H).** Appendix Figures 27, 28, and 29 show average effectivity as a function of injection count (i = 1 through 10) for OLMo-7B early, mid, and late checkpoints. The first injection (i = 1) consistently produces the highest effectivity across all conditions. Subsequent injections (i = 2 through 10) show "relatively constant" effectivity — the model continues to benefit from each additional exposure, but at a lower per-exposure rate than the initial encounter. This diminishing-returns pattern is visible in both duplication and paraphrase scenarios, though the absolute levels differ (duplication produces higher effectivity per injection than paraphrase at each i).

**Effectivity for OLMo-1B checkpoints (Appendix Figure 11).** The OLMo-1B checkpoints show the same qualitative pattern as OLMo-7B: no systematic improvement in effectivity from early to mid to late pretraining stages. The absolute effectivity values are lower than for 7B, consistent with the model-size comparison in Figure 3 (right panel). The paper explicitly notes this consistency: "This tendency is consistent across all model scales and injection scenarios" (Section 4.2).

#### Retainability: Power-Law Forgetting Dynamics

Figure 4 presents the core forgetting results: average retainability R(q,t) plotted against training steps past the local acquisition maxima of the final injection, on log-scale x-axes, for OLMo-7B mid checkpoint under duplication (left) and paraphrase (right) injection scenarios. The key empirical finding is that the relationship between R(q,t) and log(t) is approximately linear, indicating a power-law decay.

**Power-law fit quality.** For the duplication scenario (Figure 4, left), the linear fit achieves R² > 0.80 for memorization and semantic generalization, and R² > 0.65 for compositional generalization. The decay constants (absolute slopes) are a = 0.25 for memorization, a = 0.25 for semantic generalization, and a = 0.20 for compositional generalization. For the paraphrase scenario (Figure 4, right), the decay constants are a = 0.21 (memorization), a = 0.23 (semantic), and a = 0.15 (compositional). The compositional generalization curve consistently shows the smallest decay constant — meaning it forgets the slowest, even though it acquires the least per exposure (as shown by the lower effectivity in Figure 3). The paper characterizes this as: "the acquisition of compositional generalization accumulates most slowly but is more robust to forgetting" (Section 4.3).

**Decay constants across pretraining stages (Table 2).** Table 2 reports decay constants for all three OLMo-7B pretraining stages (early: 170B, mid: 500B, late: 1.5T). For the duplication scenario, the decay constants for memorization are 0.26 ± 0.0020 (early), 0.25 ± 0.0019 (mid), and 0.20 ± 0.0019 (late). The late checkpoint shows a notably smaller decay constant — roughly 20–25% lower than the early and mid checkpoints — suggesting slower forgetting. However, the paper attributes this to the reduced learning rate at the late stage (0.000101 vs. 0.000280 at the early stage; Appendix Table 5). In the constant-learning-rate control experiment (Appendix Table 9), the late checkpoint's decay constant does not decrease — in fact, it increases slightly (e.g., 0.35 ± 0.0025 for memorization under duplication at the late stage vs. 0.29 ± 0.0017 at early). This confirms that the apparent slower forgetting at the late stage in Table 2 is a learning rate artifact, not a genuine improvement in retention.

For the paraphrase scenario in Table 2, the decay constants are generally smaller than for duplication — consistent with the visual trend in Figure 2 where paraphrase curves show slower post-injection decay. For memorization at the mid checkpoint: a = 0.21 ± 0.0023 (paraphrase) vs. a = 0.25 ± 0.0019 (duplication). For semantic generalization: a = 0.23 ± 0.0024 (paraphrase) vs. a = 0.25 ± 0.0022 (duplication). For compositional generalization: a = 0.15 ± 0.0022 (paraphrase) vs. a = 0.20 ± 0.0032 (duplication). The gap between duplication and paraphrase decay constants is most pronounced for compositional generalization (0.15 vs. 0.20, a 25% relative reduction), consistent with the paper's argument that duplicated presentations particularly impair the retention of deeper, more integrated knowledge.

**Decay constants for OLMo-1B (Appendix Table 7).** For OLMo-1B, the decay constants are presented for the mid (494B) and late (1.5T) checkpoints; the early (168B) checkpoint values are omitted "due to the poor linear fitting (R² < 0.4), which is attributed to the highly unstable dynamics as shown in Appendix Figure 8 and 14" (Appendix Table 7 note). For the mid checkpoint under duplication: a = 0.24 ± 0.0021 (memorization), a = 0.22 ± 0.0020 (semantic), a = 0.19 ± 0.0021 (compositional). These values are similar to the OLMo-7B mid checkpoint decay constants, suggesting that model size does not strongly affect the forgetting rate, even though it strongly affects the acquisition rate (effectivity).

**Anticipated x-intercepts (learnability thresholds).** Appendix Table 6 reports the anticipated x-intercepts of the retainability curves — the number of training tokens at which R(q,t) is projected to reach 0 — for OLMo-7B at three pretraining stages. These values represent the maximum interval between fact encounters such that the fact remains learnable. For the mid checkpoint under duplication: the x-intercept for memorization is approximately 10^11.02 tokens, for semantic generalization 10^10.98, and for compositional generalization 10^11.32. Under paraphrase: approximately 10^11.37 (memorization), 10^10.94 (semantic), and 10^11.88 (compositional). These are enormous numbers — 10^11 tokens is roughly 100 billion tokens, or about 1/15th of the total OLMo-7B pretraining budget (1.5T tokens). The practical meaning is that if a fact appears only once every 100 billion tokens in the pretraining data, the model will essentially forget it completely between encounters.

The x-intercept values for the late checkpoint (under duplication) are generally larger than for early and mid, which the paper attributes to the reduced learning rate as discussed above. The values span a range from 10^10.86 (semantic, early, duplication) to 10^12.06 (memorization, early, paraphrase), a 15× range in token count — indicating that the learnability threshold is not a single fixed number but varies substantially with injection format, acquisition depth, and pretraining stage.

**Anticipated x-intercepts for OLMo-1B (Appendix Table 8).** For the OLMo-1B mid checkpoint, the x-intercepts are generally smaller than for OLMo-7B (e.g., 10^10.60 for memorization under duplication vs. 10^11.02 for 7B), suggesting that smaller models have shorter learnability thresholds and therefore can acquire a narrower set of facts from the same training data distribution.

#### The Effect of Batch Size on Forgetting Dynamics

Figure 5 compares forgetting dynamics between the standard batch size of 2048 (left panels, the same data as Figure 4 but with the x-axis converted to tokens rather than steps) and a reduced batch size of 128 (right panels). The token-based x-axis allows direct comparison because it accounts for the fact that the reduced batch size processes fewer tokens per step. The central finding is that training with a reduced batch size substantially accelerates forgetting.

**Decay constant comparison (Table 2 vs. Appendix Table 10).** For OLMo-7B mid checkpoint under duplication: with batch size 2048, a = 0.25 ± 0.0019 (memorization); with batch size 128, a = 0.31 ± 0.0021 (memorization) — a 24% increase in the forgetting rate. For semantic generalization: a = 0.25 ± 0.0022 (batch 2048) vs. a = 0.29 ± 0.0019 (batch 128). For compositional generalization: a = 0.20 ± 0.0032 (batch 2048) vs. a = 0.26 ± 0.0018 (batch 128) — a 30% increase. The pattern holds across all pretraining stages and both injection scenarios (Appendix Table 10 vs. Table 2).

**Learnability threshold comparison (Appendix Table 6 vs. Appendix Table 11).** The most dramatic effect of batch size reduction is on the anticipated x-intercepts. For OLMo-7B mid checkpoint under duplication: with batch size 2048, the memorization x-intercept is 10^11.02 tokens; with batch size 128, it drops to 10^9.45 tokens. This is a reduction by a factor of approximately 10^1.57 ≈ 37× in token count. Across all conditions, the x-intercepts under batch size 128 are roughly 1–2 orders of magnitude smaller than under batch size 2048. The paper states that "the anticipated x-intercept is significantly decreased by dozens of times" (Section 4.3) and that "the models trained with smaller batch sizes have shorter learnability threshold" (Section 4.3).

**Differential impact on acquisition depths.** The accelerated forgetting under small batch size is most pronounced for compositional generalization. Under duplication, the decay constant for compositional generalization increases from 0.20 (batch 2048) to 0.26 (batch 128) — a 30% relative increase — compared to 24% for memorization and 16% for semantic generalization. The paper notes that "accelerated forgetting with a smaller batch size is more pronounced for compositional generalization compared to memorization and semantic generalization" (Section 4.3).

**Effectivity under reduced batch size (Appendix Figure 21).** The OLMo-7B models trained with batch size 128 show higher effectivity values compared to the batch size 2048 experiments (Figure 3). For the mid checkpoint under duplication, effectivity for memorization is approximately 1.5–2.0 (Appendix Figure 21) compared to roughly 0.5 (Figure 3). The paper notes this but emphasizes that "LLMs trained with the smaller batch size show higher effectivity" but "the decay constant tends to be higher" — the net effect is worse retention despite stronger initial encoding (Section 4.3). The once injection scenario under batch size 128 shows very low effectivity, which the paper attributes to "the unstable dynamics after the re-initialization of the optimizer states" (Appendix Figure 21 caption).

**Retainability curve for early and late checkpoints under reduced batch size (Appendix Figures 22 and 23).** The power-law relationship between R(q,t) and log(tokens) remains approximately linear under batch size 128 for both the early and late OLMo-7B checkpoints, with R² values comparable to the batch size 2048 experiments. The slopes (decay constants) are consistently steeper than their batch size 2048 counterparts, replicating the pattern observed for the mid checkpoint.

#### Constant Learning Rate Control Experiment

Appendix Figure 17 presents average effectivity for OLMo-7B early, mid, and late checkpoints trained with a fixed constant learning rate (the average of the three checkpoints' initial learning rates). The key result is that effectivity still does not improve at later pretraining stages — the early checkpoint bars are similar in height to the late checkpoint bars across all acquisition depths and injection scenarios. This confirms that the flat effectivity trend in Figure 3 (left panel) is not merely an artifact of learning rate decay: even when all checkpoints are given equal learning rates, the later checkpoint does not learn more efficiently per exposure. Appendix Table 9 reports decay constants for this experiment: for duplication at the mid checkpoint, a = 0.30 ± 0.0025 (memorization), a = 0.28 ± 0.0023 (semantic), a = 0.28 ± 0.0031 (compositional). These values are higher than the corresponding values in Table 2 (0.25, 0.25, 0.20), which the paper does not directly explain but may reflect the combined effect of batch size reduction (the constant-learning-rate experiment also uses batch size 128, per Appendix F) and the learning rate averaging.

#### Summary of Key Quantitative Patterns

The experimental results establish a coherent set of relationships:

1. **Per-exposure acquisition (effectivity)** is improved by larger model size (7B > 1B) and by encountering the fact for the first time (i = 1 > subsequent injections), but is not improved by more pretraining tokens or by duplicated vs. paraphrased presentation (Figures 2, 3; Appendix Figures 11, 27–29).

2. **Forgetting rate (decay constant)** follows a power law in training tokens, is slower for compositional generalization than for memorization (a = 0.20 vs. a = 0.25 at mid checkpoint, duplication), is slower for paraphrased than duplicated presentation (a = 0.15 vs. a = 0.20 for compositional generalization), and is substantially accelerated by smaller batch sizes (a = 0.26 vs. a = 0.20 for compositional generalization at batch 128 vs. 2048) (Figures 4, 5; Tables 2, 10; Appendix Table 7).

3. **Learnability threshold (x-intercept)** is on the order of 10^10–10^12 tokens at batch size 2048 and shrinks by 1–2 orders of magnitude at batch size 128, is generally shorter for smaller models (1B < 7B), and varies with injection format and acquisition depth (Appendix Tables 6, 8, 11).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Injection scenario comparison (duplication vs. paraphrase vs. once):** The three injection scenarios serve both as experimental conditions and as mutual ablations. The once scenario establishes the single-exposure baseline: without repetition, knowledge is almost entirely forgotten within 2,000 steps (Figure 2, bottom). The paraphrase scenario isolates the effect of semantic repetition from surface-form repetition: the fact is encountered the same number of times (10) as in the duplication scenario, but in varied phrasings, testing whether the benefit of repetition comes from identical-string rehearsal or from varied re-encoding. Results show that paraphrase yields slower forgetting (smaller decay constants in Table 2) and narrower memorization-semantic generalization gaps (Figure 2, center vs. top), but similar terminal log-probability levels — the initial advantage of duplication's larger per-exposure gains is offset by faster decay.

**Pretraining stage (early vs. mid vs. late):** The comparison across three stages spanning 170B to 1.5T tokens serves as an ablation of the effect of cumulative pretraining experience on acquisition dynamics. The key negative result is that effectivity does not improve across stages (Figure 3, left; Appendix Figure 11), despite the late checkpoint having seen ~9× more pretraining data. This null result is robust to learning rate equalization (Appendix F, Figure 17). The one exception is the OLMo-1B early checkpoint, which shows unstable dynamics (Appendix Figure 8) with poor power-law fits (R² < 0.4 for retainability), suggesting that very early pretraining stages may involve qualitatively different dynamics that stabilize after sufficient training.

**Model scale (1B vs. 7B):** Comparing OLMo-1B mid against OLMo-7B mid isolates the effect of parameter count on acquisition dynamics. The finding that 7B shows consistently higher effectivity (Figure 3, right) but similar decay constants (Tables 2 vs. Appendix Table 7, both mid checkpoints show a ≈ 0.24–0.25 for memorization under duplication) suggests that model scale improves encoding efficiency without substantially affecting retention rates.

**Constant learning rate (Appendix F):** Training all three OLMo-7B checkpoints with the identical fixed learning rate isolates the effect of pretraining stage from the effect of learning rate differences. The result that effectivity still does not improve at later stages (Appendix Figure 17) and decay constants do not decrease (Appendix Table 9) confirms that the flat effectivity trend is not a learning-rate artifact. This is a critical robustness check because the late checkpoint's learning rate is approximately 2.8× smaller than the early checkpoint's (0.000101 vs. 0.000280), which could masquerade as reduced learning capability.

**Batch size (2048 vs. 128) (Appendix G):** Reducing batch size by 16× isolates the effect of gradient noise and update frequency on forgetting dynamics. The results are striking: effectivity increases (larger per-exposure gains), but decay constants increase substantially (Appendix Table 10 vs. Table 2), and the learnability threshold (x-intercept) shrinks by 1–2 orders of magnitude (Appendix Table 11 vs. Appendix Table 6). The paper interprets this as demonstrating that batch size is a first-order determinant of knowledge retention, not merely a throughput hyperparameter.

**Probe type (memorization vs. semantic vs. compositional):** The three probe types serve as an ablation of acquisition depth, testing whether facts encoded at different levels of abstraction exhibit different acquisition and forgetting dynamics. The results show a consistent trade-off: compositional generalization shows the lowest effectivity (smallest per-exposure gain) but the slowest forgetting (smallest decay constant), while memorization shows the opposite pattern (highest effectivity, fastest forgetting). This is visible across all conditions (Figures 2, 3, 4; Tables 2, 7).

**Outlier filtering (IQR method with factor 1.5):** The paper applies IQR-based outlier detection to effectivity and retainability measurements, removing data points outside [Q1 − 1.5 × IQR, Q3 + 1.5 × IQR]. This is particularly important for retainability, where probes with near-zero total acquisition produce unstable ratios (Equation 3 denominator → 0). The authors note this "is particularly important for the measurement of retainability, as the small number of cases which showed no acquisition through training can give a very large value due to the very small denominator in Eq. 3" (Section 3). The paper does not report what fraction of data points are filtered by this procedure.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: Factual knowledge acquisition occurs by accumulating micro-acquisitions with subsequent forgetting at each encounter.

This is the paper's central mechanistic claim and is directly supported by the step-wise log-probability traces in Figure 2 and Appendix Figures 6–10. The sawtooth pattern — upward jumps at injection steps, downward drift between them — is visually unambiguous and replicated across all model sizes and pretraining stages (except the unstable OLMo-1B early checkpoint). The claim is not merely that the log-probability goes up and down (which would be trivially true for any training process) but that this pattern continues across *multiple* exposures, with each exposure contributing a diminishing but positive increment and each inter-exposure interval causing partial erasure. The evidence for the "diminishing increment" part comes from Appendix H (Figures 27–29), which shows that effectivity is highest at i = 1 and relatively constant but lower at i = 2–10. The evidence for "partial erasure" comes from the retainability curves (Figure 4), which show that log-probability decays toward (but typically not all the way to) the pre-injection baseline between exposures.

**What the evidence shows vs. what it implies:** The direct evidence shows micro-accumulation *under the specific conditions of the experiment*: 100-step intervals between exposures, controlled injection of novel facts, continued training on Dolma data at step 360,000+. Whether the same dynamics hold at the much longer timescales of real pretraining (where facts may appear separated by millions of steps, not 100) is a necessary extrapolation. The power-law fit to retainability (Figure 4) provides a model for this extrapolation, and the paper uses it to estimate learnability thresholds on the order of 10^10–10^12 tokens. But this extrapolation assumes the power-law relationship holds over 6–8 orders of magnitude in t — from the measured range of ~10^0–10^5 steps to the extrapolated x-intercepts at ~10^6–10^8 steps. The paper does not test this assumption directly (which would require impossibly long training runs), but the quality of the power-law fit within the observed range (R² > 0.80 for memorization and semantic) provides reasonable support for extrapolation over the next 1–2 orders of magnitude. The extrapolation to 10^12 tokens (roughly two-thirds of OLMo-7B's total pretraining) is more speculative.

#### Claim 2: Effectivity does not improve with more pretraining data (pretraining stage), but does improve with larger model size.

The evidence for the null effect of pretraining stage is Figure 3 (left panel) and Appendix Figures 11 and 17. The bars for early, mid, and late checkpoints are visually similar in height, and the paper reports no significant increasing trend. However, the paper does not report formal statistical tests comparing the three stages (e.g., ANOVA or t-tests with multiple comparison correction). The evidence is therefore qualitative: visual inspection of bar charts. With 200 probes per acquisition depth per scenario (40 passages × 5 probes), the per-condition sample size is substantial, and the standard errors on the decay constants in Table 2 (±0.0019–0.0032) suggest the measurements are reasonably precise. But the absence of formal significance testing for the effectivity comparison is a genuine limitation — a reader cannot assess whether the observed flatness is a true null result or merely low statistical power masking a small positive trend.

The evidence for the model-size effect is Figure 3 (right panel), which shows consistently higher bars for 7B than 1B. The paper notes that for fair comparison, the 1B model is trained with the same initial learning rate as the 7B model (Section 4.2). However, this adjustment means the 1B model in this comparison is not using its original pretraining learning rate — it is using a learning rate optimized for a 7B model. Whether this affects the validity of the comparison is unclear. Additionally, the comparison is only between two model sizes (1B and 7B), a 7× scaling factor. Whether the effectivity advantage continues to scale with model size (e.g., to 70B, 175B, etc.) or saturates at some threshold is not tested. The claim that "the benefits from scaling model size and pretraining tokens are qualitatively different" (Section 1) is supported by the observed dissociation, but the evidence comes from two points on the model-size axis (1B, 7B) and three points on the data axis (170B, 500B, 1.5T). A continuous scaling relationship would require more data points on both axes.

**The constant-learning-rate control (Appendix F) is essential but imperfect.** It shows that effectivity does not improve at later stages even with equal learning rates, ruling out the most obvious confound. However, the control experiment uses a reduced batch size (128 vs. 2048) due to computational constraints, and the authors note they "reduce the batch size from 2048 to 128 for this experiment." Since the main finding is that batch size dramatically affects acquisition dynamics (Figure 5, Tables 2 vs. 10), running the control experiment at a different batch size than the main experiment is a concern. The paper implicitly assumes that the effect of pretraining stage on effectivity is independent of batch size, but this assumption is not tested. Ideally, the constant-learning-rate control would have been run at the original batch size of 2048.

#### Claim 3: There is a power-law relationship between training steps and forgetting of acquired factual knowledge.

This claim is supported by the linear fits in Figure 4 and Appendix Figures 12–16, 22–23. The R² values are reported as > 0.80 for memorization and semantic generalization and > 0.65 for compositional generalization. These fit qualities are decent but not exceptional. The R² > 0.65 for compositional generalization means that up to 35% of the variance in retainability is not explained by the power-law model — a substantial fraction. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by reporting the R² thresholds.

More fundamentally, the power-law fit is demonstrated over approximately 5 orders of magnitude in t (from ~10^0 to ~10^5 steps, corresponding to roughly 10^6 to 10^11 tokens at batch size 2048). The extrapolation to the x-intercepts (at ~10^11–10^12 tokens) extends ~1–2 orders of magnitude beyond the measured range. Power-law relationships are known to break down at extreme values in many physical and learning systems, and the paper provides no independent validation that the linear trend in log-log space continues to the x-intercept. This extrapolation is the foundation for the learnability threshold concept and the explanations in Section 4.4 — if the power-law relationship in fact curves (flattens) at very large t, the true learnability thresholds would be larger than estimated. If it steepens (perhaps due to catastrophic forgetting effects at very long intervals), the thresholds would be smaller.

**The OLMo-1B early checkpoint is a notable exception:** Appendix Table 7 reports that the decay constants for this checkpoint are "omitted due to the poor linear fitting (R² < 0.4), which is attributed to the highly unstable dynamics." This means that for the earliest pretraining stage of the smallest model, the power-law relationship does not hold, and the entire retainability framework (decay constant, x-intercept, learnability threshold) cannot be applied. This is not a minor edge case — it suggests that the power-law forgetting dynamic may only emerge after a certain pretraining "burn-in" period, and the paper does not characterize when or why this transition occurs.

#### Claim 4: Pretraining with duplicated data accelerates forgetting compared to paraphrased data.

The evidence is in the decay constant comparisons in Table 2 and Appendix Table 7. For OLMo-7B mid checkpoint, the duplication decay constants are consistently higher than the paraphrase decay constants: memorization a = 0.25 ± 0.0019 vs. 0.21 ± 0.0023; semantic a = 0.25 ± 0.0022 vs. 0.23 ± 0.0024; compositional a = 0.20 ± 0.0032 vs. 0.15 ± 0.0022. The differences are modest in absolute terms (0.02–0.05) but represent 10–25% relative increases in the forgetting rate. The standard errors on these estimates are small (±0.0019–0.0032), suggesting the differences are statistically significant, though formal tests are not reported.

However, the paraphrase injection scenario involves GPT-4-generated paraphrases, which introduces a confound: the paraphrases are not guaranteed to be semantically identical to the originals. Some paraphrases may omit details, introduce subtle semantic shifts, or vary in the prominence given to different facts. The probes are fixed (based on the original passage), so a probe testing a fact that is present in the original but partially obscured in a paraphrase may show lower effectivity and different forgetting dynamics — not because paraphrasing per se changes the forgetting rate, but because the model received a weaker or noisier signal for that specific fact. The paper does not evaluate the semantic fidelity of the GPT-4 paraphrases beyond stating that they were generated with a "clearly and concisely" prompt (Appendix C.2). This is a meaningful gap: if some paraphrases are lower-quality, the comparison is not purely "duplication vs. paraphrase" but "reliable fact presentation vs. sometimes-noisy fact presentation."

#### Claim 5: Training with larger batch sizes enhances robustness to forgetting.

This claim is supported by Figure 5 and Appendix Tables 10 and 11. The evidence is strong: the decay constants increase by 16–30% and the x-intercepts shrink by 1–2 orders of magnitude when batch size is reduced from 2048 to 128. However, there are important caveats:

**The batch size comparison confounds batch size with optimizer state.** The batch size 2048 experiments use warm-start optimizer states (loaded from OLMo pretraining), while the batch size 128 experiments re-initialize optimizer states (Appendix G). The authors acknowledge this causes "unstable dynamics in early steps." The re-initialization means the momentum and adaptive learning rate statistics are reset, which could affect the early-phase acquisition and forgetting dynamics independently of batch size. The paper argues that "the dynamics are stabilized soon," but does not quantify when stabilization occurs or whether the forgetting measurements (taken after 1,000+ steps) are affected by residual cold-start effects.

**Only two batch sizes are tested (2048 and 128, a 16× difference).** This is a single comparison, not a scaling relationship. Whether the benefit of larger batch sizes continues monotonically, saturates, or even reverses at extreme batch sizes (e.g., 4096, 8192) is not tested. The paper's framing — "pretraining LLMs with deduplicated data and larger batch sizes enhances the acquisition of factual knowledge" (Section 5) — implies a general monotonic relationship that the evidence does not fully support.

**The batch size experiment uses a single model checkpoint (OLMo-7B mid).** Whether the batch size effect interacts with pretraining stage or model size is not tested. It is possible that early-stage models (which already show unstable dynamics, as with OLMo-1B early) respond differently to batch size changes than mid-stage models.

#### What is missing from the experimental analysis

**No evaluation on generation output.** The entire analysis is based on log-probability of target spans — a continuous metric of encoding strength. The paper explicitly acknowledges this as a limitation (Appendix A): "we do not perform evaluations based on the generation output of the model, and we do not investigate the exact relationship between the model's accumulation of probability of factual knowledge and the model's generation output." This is a significant gap because the practical concern with LLM factual knowledge is not whether the model *assigns higher probability to the correct fact*, but whether it *produces the correct fact as output* in response to queries. Log-probability and generation accuracy may have a nonlinear relationship — a fact might need to exceed some probability threshold before it becomes the top-1 prediction, and below that threshold, improvements in log-probability produce no change in downstream accuracy. Without generation-based evaluation, the paper cannot claim that the observed log-probability dynamics translate to practical knowledge acquisition outcomes.

**No measurement of interference effects between facts.** The experiment injects 40 distinct facts per injection scenario, all at the same 100-step intervals, in the same batches. But in real pretraining, facts are not injected in clean temporal isolation — they are interleaved with thousands of other facts, and the acquisition of one fact may interfere with the retention of another (catastrophic interference). The paper's design intentionally avoids this complexity to isolate per-fact dynamics, but this means the results may overestimate acquisition and underestimate forgetting compared to realistic pretraining where many facts compete for encoding simultaneously.

**Single data distribution (Dolma), single model family (OLMo).** All experiments continue pretraining OLMo checkpoints on Dolma data. Whether the same power-law forgetting constants, effectivity levels, and batch-size effects hold for other model architectures (e.g., Llama, GPT-4), other pretraining data distributions, or other optimizer configurations is unknown. The paper's informal claim that OLMo is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4) is an assumption, not a demonstrated fact.

**The difficulty estimation cost is unaccounted for.** The paper's FICTIONAL KNOWLEDGE dataset generation requires GPT-4 prompting, manual filtering, and probe validation. While this is a one-time cost for the experiment, the methodology is not directly transferable to practitioners who want to measure acquisition dynamics for their own models on their own data. The paper does not discuss how the approach could be scaled or automated for practical use.

**No ablation on the injection interval (100 steps).** The 100-step interval between injections is a fixed parameter in all experiments. The paper's own framework predicts that the interval length is critical — if the interval exceeds the learnability threshold, cumulative acquisition should fail. But the paper never varies the interval to test this prediction. Running the same experiment with, say, 50, 200, 500, and 1000-step intervals would directly test whether the x-intercept extrapolation from the retainability curves accurately predicts when accumulation breaks down. This is perhaps the most important missing experiment, because it would validate (or invalidate) the central practical implication of the learnability threshold concept.

**No evaluation of knowledge acquired during actual pretraining.** The entire study examines injected fictional knowledge, not natural knowledge acquired during the original OLMo pretraining. The paper cannot claim that the same dynamics govern the acquisition of real-world facts during the original 1.5T-token training run. The micro-accumulation model predicts that the probability of real facts should increase steadily over pretraining as they are repeatedly encountered, but the paper never measures this for naturally occurring facts in the Dolma corpus to validate the framework against ground-truth pretraining dynamics.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Learnability Threshold Is Extrapolated From Controlled Conditions, Not Validated in Realistic Settings

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central practical concept — the **learnability threshold**, the maximum interval between fact encounters such that the fact remains learnable — is derived from power-law extrapolation of retainability curves measured over approximately 2,500 training steps (roughly 10 billion tokens at batch size 2048) to x-intercepts at 10^10–10^12 tokens (Appendix Table 6). This extrapolation assumes that the linear relationship between `$R(q, t)$` and `$\log(t)$` observed over roughly 5 orders of magnitude in `$t$` (from 10^0 to 10^5 steps) continues to hold over an additional 1–2 orders of magnitude to the x-intercept. The paper acknowledges this is a model-based extrapolation, not a direct measurement, but treats the resulting x-intercepts as quantitatively meaningful thresholds for real pretraining (Section 4.4: "if a given factual knowledge in the pretraining dataset is in the long-tail and the knowledge is presented to the model with an interval longer than a certain threshold, such knowledge will be impossible to be decoded as the top-k generation of the model").

**The consequence.** If the power-law relationship does not hold at very large `$t$`, the learnability thresholds are wrong — and the direction of error is unknown. If the forgetting curve flattens (forgetting slows at long intervals), the true threshold is larger than estimated; if it steepens (catastrophic forgetting accelerates at long intervals), the threshold is smaller. In either case, the paper's downstream explanations — why long-tail knowledge fails, why deduplication helps, how batch size affects learnability — become qualitatively suspect if their quantitative foundation is unreliable. Moreover, the paper never varies the injection interval to test whether the extrapolation accurately predicts when cumulative acquisition breaks down. The 100-step interval is fixed in all experiments. Running the same protocol with intervals of 50, 200, 500, or 1000 steps would directly test the prediction: if the x-intercept extrapolation is correct, intervals shorter than the threshold should permit accumulation and intervals longer than the threshold should not. This experiment is not performed, leaving the learnability threshold as an **untested theoretical construct** rather than a validated empirical quantity.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The power-law fits themselves have decent but not exceptional quality: `$R^2 > 0.80$` for memorization and semantic generalization, and `$R^2 > 0.65$` for compositional generalization (Section 4.3). The lower `$R^2$` for compositional generalization means up to 35% of variance is unexplained by the power-law model. Additionally, the OLMo-1B early checkpoint shows `$R^2 < 0.4$` for the power-law fit (Appendix Table 7), meaning the entire retainability framework fails for this configuration — the power-law relationship is not universal even within the paper's own experimental conditions. The anticipated x-intercepts span a 15× range (10^10.86 to 10^12.06 in Appendix Table 6) depending on injection format, acquisition depth, and pretraining stage, indicating substantial sensitivity to conditions. No experiment varies the injection interval.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this extrapolation uncertainty. Section 4.4 treats the x-intercepts as interpretable thresholds without qualification about the extrapolation range or assumptions. The authors do not suggest future work on interval-variation experiments or long-timescale validation, despite this being the clearest path to validating the learnability threshold concept.

### The Paper Never Evaluates Whether Log-Probability Improvements Translate to Generation Accuracy

**The assumption or constraint.** All measurements in the paper — effectivity, retainability, decay constants, learnability thresholds — are based on **log-probability of target spans** (Equation 1–3). The paper explicitly acknowledges this limitation in Appendix A: "we do not perform evaluations based on the generation output of the model, and we do not investigate the exact relationship between the model's accumulation of probability of factual knowledge and the model's generation output." The entire analysis therefore operates at the level of *encoding strength* — how much the model's internal probability distribution over tokens shifts toward the correct fact — rather than *behavioral manifestation* — whether the model actually produces the correct fact when queried.

**The consequence.** Log-probability and generation accuracy may have a nonlinear, thresholded relationship. A fact might require crossing some minimum probability threshold before it becomes the top-1 prediction under any decoding scheme. Below that threshold, improvements in log-probability — even large ones — produce no change in downstream accuracy. If this threshold is high relative to the baseline log-probabilities measured in the paper (which start near zero and increase to ~0.5–1.0 log-probability units for memorization in Figure 2), many of the "acquired" facts may remain below the generation threshold and thus practically useless. The paper's claims about "factual knowledge acquisition" and "learnability" are therefore claims about *encoding*, not about usable knowledge. A practitioner reading this paper to understand why their LLM fails to answer long-tail questions correctly cannot directly map the paper's findings to their problem, because the mapping from log-probability to generation accuracy is unmeasured. Section 4.4's explanations of model behaviors (long-tail failure, deduplication benefits) assume this mapping is monotonic and roughly linear, but the paper provides no evidence for this assumption.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no generation-based evaluation whatsoever. No probe is evaluated by having the model autoregressively complete the cloze and checking whether the generated token matches the target span. No analysis relates the measured log-probability values to any behavioral metric (accuracy, F1, exact match). The Appendix A acknowledgment is a single sentence with no quantification of the gap or discussion of its implications.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation. Appendix A lists it as a limitation and suggests that "future works exploring these would help us to further enhance our understanding of LLMs," but provides no concrete direction for how to bridge the log-probability-to-generation gap. This is a substantial omission because the gap is not merely a measurement detail — it potentially invalidates the practical interpretation of every quantitative result in the paper.

---

### The Learnability Threshold Concept Depends on a Clean Single-Fact Injection Paradigm That Ignores Interference Between Facts

**The assumption or constraint.** The experimental design injects 40 distinct fictional facts per injection scenario, all at the same 100-step intervals, in the same modified batches. The injected knowledge passages are constructed to be independent — they describe different fictional entities with no overlapping facts or shared properties. This design intentionally isolates per-fact acquisition dynamics by eliminating **factual interference**: the situation where learning fact A interferes with the retention of fact B because the facts share related concepts, entities, or linguistic patterns. In real pretraining, facts are not injected in clean temporal isolation — thousands of semantically related facts co-occur, compete for encoding in shared parameter space, and may mutually reinforce or interfere with each other's acquisition and retention.

**The consequence.** The measured effectivity, decay constants, and learnability thresholds may systematically **overestimate** acquisition and **underestimate** forgetting compared to realistic pretraining. If learning fact A partially overwrites the representation of fact B (catastrophic interference), the effective forgetting rate for fact B would be higher than the "passive forgetting" rate measured in this paper (where forgetting is driven only by unrelated Dolma batches, not by competing factual knowledge). Conversely, if semantically related facts mutually reinforce each other (shared representations), the effective forgetting rate might be lower. The paper cannot distinguish between these scenarios. The learnability threshold — the maximum interval between encounters of *the same fact* such that it remains learnable — may also depend on how many *other semantically similar facts* are being learned concurrently. A fact might be learnable at a given interval when it is the only fact being acquired, but not learnable at the same interval when the model is simultaneously learning 10,000 related facts. The paper's measurements provide an upper bound on retention (idealized, interference-free conditions) that may substantially overstate what is achievable in real pretraining.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no measurement of factual interference. All 40 facts per scenario are injected simultaneously in the same batches with no analysis of whether the acquisition of one fact correlates with (or anti-correlates with) the acquisition of others. The paper does not vary the number of facts injected concurrently to test whether acquisition dynamics scale with factual load. The paper does not analyze whether facts sharing entity types, linguistic patterns, or semantic domains exhibit different acquisition dynamics than isolated facts.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this limitation or discuss it as a direction for future work. The clean isolation design is treated as a methodological strength (enabling causal identification) rather than as a potential source of ecological invalidity. There is no suggestion that interference effects are worth studying or that the measured thresholds should be treated as idealized upper bounds.

---

### All Results Come From a Single Model Family (OLMo) on a Single Data Distribution (Dolma)

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses intermediate checkpoints of OLMo-1B and OLMo-7B (Groeneveld et al., 2024), resumed on the Dolma pretraining corpus (Soldaini et al., 2024) in the same data order used for original OLMo pretraining. The paper states that the OLMo models "are representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this is an assertion, not a demonstrated fact. OLMo uses a specific architecture (decoder-only transformer, SwiGLU activations, RoPE embeddings, specific initialization), a specific optimizer configuration (AdamW with `$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`, cosine learning rate decay), and a specific data mixture (Dolma, which is a curated web-text corpus with particular domain distributions). Whether the observed acquisition dynamics — particularly the power-law decay constants, the null effect of pretraining stage on effectivity, and the batch-size sensitivity of forgetting — generalize to other model families (Llama, GPT, Mistral), other data distributions (The Pile, RefinedWeb, domain-specific corpora), or other optimizer configurations (different `$\beta$` values, different schedulers) is entirely unknown.

**The consequence.** A practitioner using a different model family or data distribution cannot safely apply the paper's quantitative findings. The decay constants (e.g., `$a = 0.25$` for memorization under duplication at OLMo-7B mid) may be specific to OLMo's training dynamics. The learnability thresholds (10^10–10^12 tokens) may differ by orders of magnitude for other architectures. The paper's recommendations — use larger batch sizes, prefer paraphrased over duplicated presentations, expect diminishing returns from longer pretraining on per-exposure learning efficiency — may not transfer. This is a practical deployment concern: the paper frames its findings as explanations for general LLM behaviors (long-tail failure, deduplication benefits, data scaling), but those explanations are grounded in a single empirical instance. Without replication on at least one other model family, the generality of the mechanisms is assumed rather than established.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper uses exactly two model sizes (1B and 7B) from one family, on exactly one data distribution (Dolma), with one optimizer configuration (the original OLMo AdamW settings). Within this narrow scope, the findings are internally consistent — the qualitative patterns replicate across pretraining stages and model sizes. But there is no cross-family or cross-data validation. The paper does not compare OLMo's acquisition dynamics to, for example, a Llama-2 checkpoint trained on a different corpus, or to an OLMo variant trained on a different data mixture.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss this as a limitation. The claim of representativeness in Section 4 ("we believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs") is presented without supporting evidence or caveats. There is no suggestion that future work should replicate the experiments on other model families to establish generality.

### Difficulty Estimation and Controlled Injection Require Oracle Knowledge Unavailable in Real Deployment

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire experimental paradigm depends on **oracle knowledge** of (1) which facts the model has and has not encountered during pretraining (enabling the construction of truly novel fictional knowledge), (2) the exact text of the injected knowledge (enabling probe construction with guaranteed relevance), (3) the training step when each injection occurs (enabling temporal alignment of log-probability traces with exposure events), and (4) the correct target spans for all probes (enabling evaluation). In a real pretraining deployment, none of this information is available. A practitioner cannot identify whether a given fact in their pretraining data is novel to the model, cannot know exactly when the model first encounters it, cannot align probe evaluations with exposure events, and (for many real-world facts) may not even have a clean ground-truth target span to evaluate against.

**The consequence.** The paper's methodology is a **scientific instrument** for studying acquisition dynamics, not a **deployable monitoring tool**. The findings describe *what happens* under idealized observation conditions, but a practitioner running a real pretraining job cannot apply the same analysis to their own model to, for example, measure whether their learning rate schedule is causing accelerated forgetting of domain-specific facts, or whether their data deduplication strategy is succeeding in extending the learnability threshold. The paper provides general qualitative guidance (prefer larger batch sizes, prefer paraphrasing over duplication) but no practical procedure for measuring acquisition dynamics in a live pretraining run. A practitioner who wanted to tune their batch size to optimize the learnability threshold, or to verify that their data augmentation strategy actually slows forgetting, would need to replicate the entire fictional-knowledge injection paradigm — which requires access to optimizer states, data order, and the ability to run controlled continued-pretraining experiments, plus the computational cost of doing so (3 days per run on 8×A100 GPUs).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper makes no attempt to measure acquisition dynamics for naturally occurring facts in the Dolma corpus during the original OLMo pretraining. The entire analysis is confined to the controlled injection of fictional knowledge. The paper does not discuss whether the methodology could be adapted for retrospective analysis of real pretraining runs (e.g., by identifying facts that appear at known frequencies in the training data and tracking their log-probability trajectories without injection).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this as a practical limitation. The Acknowledgments note that the work is supported by an IITP grant, and the stated goal is "enhancing our understanding of the factual knowledge acquisition dynamics" (Section 1) — framing the work as scientific investigation rather than methodology development. The paper does not claim to provide a deployable monitoring tool, and the limitation is therefore more about *applicability scope* than about a broken promise. However, for a practitioner reading the paper to inform pretraining decisions, the gap between the idealized experimental paradigm and real deployment constraints is substantial and unacknowledged.

### The Batch-Size Comparison Confounds Batch Size with Optimizer State Initialization

**The assumption or constraint.** The batch-size comparison experiment (Section 4.3, Appendix G) compares OLMo-7B mid checkpoint trained with batch size 2048 (warm-start: original OLMo optimizer states loaded) against the same checkpoint trained with batch size 128 (cold-start: optimizer states re-initialized). The paper acknowledges this: "we re-initialize the optimizer state" for the batch size 128 experiment, while the batch size 2048 experiments use loaded optimizer states (Appendix D). The batch size 128 experiment further "results in unstable dynamics in early steps, but the dynamics are stabilized soon" (Appendix G).

**The consequence.** The observed differences in effectivity and forgetting between batch sizes are **confounded** with the effect of optimizer state initialization. The higher effectivity under batch size 128 (Appendix Figure 21 shows roughly 3–4× higher effectivity than Figure 3) could reflect the cold-start optimizer taking larger effective steps (no momentum smoothing, higher relative learning rate due to unscaled adaptive moments) rather than a genuine property of smaller batch sizes. Similarly, the accelerated forgetting and shortened learnability thresholds could reflect the model being in a less stable region of parameter space due to the cold start, rather than a batch-size-specific effect on retention dynamics. The paper's central claim about batch size — that "pretraining LLMs with a larger batch size can enhance the models' robustness to forgetting" — may be partially or entirely an artifact of the warm-start vs. cold-start confound.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no control experiment that isolates batch size from optimizer initialization: there is no condition with batch size 2048 and re-initialized optimizer (which would test whether warm-start vs. cold-start matters at fixed batch size), and no condition with batch size 128 and warm-start optimizer (which would test whether batch size matters with controlled initialization). The paper attributes the "unstable dynamics in early steps" to the re-initialization but claims they "are stabilized soon" without quantifying when stabilization occurs or providing evidence that the forgetting measurements (taken at t = 0 to t = 1500 after the 1000-step injection phase) are free of cold-start contamination. The once injection scenario under batch size 128 shows anomalously low effectivity (Appendix Figure 21), which the paper explicitly attributes to "the unstable dynamics after the re-initialization of the optimizer states" — acknowledging that the cold start affects at least some measurements, but not controlling for it.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the re-initialization but treats the resulting instability as a transient that does not affect the main results. No ablation on optimizer initialization is performed or suggested as future work. For a finding as practically consequential as the effect of batch size on learnability — which the paper uses to recommend larger batch sizes for improving knowledge acquisition (Section 4.4) — the confound is a significant validity concern that the paper does not adequately address.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper's primary contribution is not a new method or a new model, but a **conceptual reframing** of factual knowledge acquisition in LLM pretraining from an emergent, capability-level phenomenon to a **cumulative counting process** governed by exposure frequency, per-exposure encoding efficiency, and power-law forgetting. This reframing matters because it shifts the field's explanatory framework from *what* models know after pretraining to *how and when* they come to know it, providing mechanistic leverage over previously puzzling empirical observations.

The magnitude of this shift is best characterized as **a new diagnostic framework rather than a paradigm shift**. The paper does not overturn the scaling laws or the observation that larger models trained on more data perform better — it provides a *mechanism* for *why* more data helps (more facts cross the learnability threshold, not improved per-exposure learning ability) and *what* the limits are (facts whose encounter intervals exceed the threshold are structurally unlearnable). This is analogous to how the Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) did not overturn the Kaplanean observation that bigger models are better, but rather provided a more precise allocation function. Similarly, this paper provides a more precise allocation logic for pretraining data: it is not merely the *total volume* of data that determines factual knowledge coverage, but the *distribution of inter-encounter intervals* relative to the model's forgetting dynamics.

The work resolves an apparent contradiction in the literature that has practical consequences for data engineering. The long-tail knowledge problem — the observation by Kandpal et al. (2022) and Mallen et al. (2022) that LLMs perform poorly on rare facts — had been attributed vaguely to "insufficient exposure." This paper provides the mechanistic missing link: the **learnability threshold** (the x-intercept of the retainability curve, estimated at `10^10`–`10^12` tokens for OLMo-7B under standard batch sizes). A fact whose inter-encounter interval in the pretraining data exceeds this threshold will have each exposure's gain completely erased before the next exposure arrives, regardless of total training duration. This converts the long-tail problem from a statistical correlation (frequency → accuracy) into a dynamical constraint (interval → learnability) that can be estimated, predicted, and potentially engineered around.

The paper also reconciles the tension between "repetition helps learning" (intuitive) and "deduplication improves performance" (empirically validated by Lee et al., 2021; Abbas et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2023). The resolution — visible in the decay constant comparison in Table 2 and Figure 2 — is that duplicated presentations produce larger per-exposure gains (higher effectivity for duplication than paraphrase in Figure 3) but also faster subsequent forgetting (larger decay constants: `a = 0.25` for duplication vs. `a = 0.21` for paraphrase in memorization at the OLMo-7B mid checkpoint). The net terminal log-probability is similar, but the paraphrase condition produces more robust generalization (the memorization-semantic gap nearly disappears). This suggests that the community's move toward aggressive deduplication is well-founded, but for reasons that go beyond the standard narrative of reducing verbatim regurgitation — deduplication (or more precisely, diversity of surface forms) actively improves the retention of generalized factual knowledge by reducing the decay constant.

Several research directions become more attractive in light of this work. First, **pretraining data scheduling** — the idea that data should be presented to the model in an order and at intervals optimized for knowledge retention — becomes a quantitatively tractable problem rather than an empirical guessing game. If the decay constant `a` and the learnability threshold can be estimated for a given model and batch size, one could compute the maximum allowable interval between exposures of each fact and construct a curriculum that ensures all target facts cross the threshold. Second, **forgetting-aware optimizers** — optimizers that modulate update magnitudes based on the estimated "importance" of the gradient direction for previously acquired knowledge — become a concrete research target, since the paper shows that forgetting is the primary bottleneck, not initial encoding. Third, **verifier or critic models for knowledge retention** — separate models that track whether a fact is likely to have been forgotten and trigger re-exposure — become a plausible architectural addition to pretraining pipelines.

Conversely, some research directions become less attractive. The paper's finding that effectivity does not improve with pretraining stage (Figure 3, left panel; Appendix F confirming this is not a learning rate artifact) suggests that efforts to develop "better learning" through architectural innovations that improve per-exposure encoding efficiency may yield diminishing returns for factual knowledge — the bottleneck is retention, not encoding. Similarly, the finding that compositional generalization shows the slowest forgetting (smallest decay constant `a = 0.20` vs. `a = 0.25` for memorization at the mid checkpoint under duplication) suggests that deeper, more integrated knowledge representations are *already* more robust to forgetting than surface-level memorization. This implies that research efforts to improve generalization should focus on *increasing the effectivity* of compositional generalization (which is the lowest of the three depths in Figure 3), not on reducing forgetting (which is already the slowest).

Perhaps the most important landscape shift is methodological: the paper demonstrates that **controlled injection of fictional knowledge into continued pretraining** is a viable and informative experimental paradigm for studying acquisition dynamics at fine granularity. This paradigm — which requires access to intermediate checkpoints, optimizer states, and data order, all of which OLMo uniquely provides — opens up a new class of experiments that were previously impossible: measuring the causal effect of a single training batch on the encoding of a specific fact, tracking the temporal trajectory of that encoding at per-step resolution, and comparing acquisition dynamics across controlled conditions (model size, pretraining stage, batch size, presentation format). This is a substantial advance over post-hoc probing of fully trained models, which can reveal *what* is stored but not *how it got there*. The paradigm is not without limitations (it requires oracle knowledge of fact novelty, it assumes minimal interference between facts, it has not been validated on generation outputs rather than log-probabilities), but it provides a template that other model developers could adopt if they release the necessary artifacts (checkpoints, optimizer states, data order).

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Varying the injection interval to validate the learnability threshold extrapolation.** The paper's central practical construct — the learnability threshold — is derived from power-law extrapolation of retainability curves measured over ~2,500 steps to x-intercepts at `10^10`–`10^12` tokens. This extrapolation has never been tested. A direct validation experiment would replicate the paper's injection protocol (OLMo-7B mid checkpoint, duplication scenario, 10 total injections) but systematically vary the interval between injections: 50, 100 (the original), 200, 500, 1000, and 2000 steps. If the power-law extrapolation is correct, intervals shorter than the predicted x-intercept (converted from tokens to steps at the given batch size) should permit cumulative acquisition (terminal log-probability after 10 injections should increase with each injection), while intervals longer than the x-intercept should show no accumulation (each injection's gain is erased before the next, and terminal log-probability remains near the single-exposure level). This experiment would directly test whether the concept of a learnability threshold has predictive power — it is the single most important validation experiment the paper does not perform. A negative result (intervals shorter than the predicted threshold still fail to accumulate) would indicate that the power-law overestimates retention or that interference between facts (all 40 injected simultaneously) accelerates effective forgetting beyond the passive forgetting rate measured in the single-injection scenario.

**Generation-based evaluation to map log-probability to behavioral knowledge.** The paper's entire analysis operates at the level of log-probability of target spans. The relationship between log-probability and generation accuracy — whether the model actually produces the correct fact when prompted — is unmeasured. A natural extension would take the exact same experimental setup (fictional knowledge injection into OLMo checkpoints, step-wise monitoring) but add generation-based evaluation: at each measurement step, prompt the model autoregressively with each probe's context (the incomplete sentence) and check whether the generated token sequence matches the target span. This would produce a parallel set of trajectories — generation accuracy vs. training steps — that could be directly compared to the log-probability trajectories in Figure 2. The key question is whether there exists a log-probability threshold (a value of `ℓ(q; θ)` above which the target span becomes the top-1 prediction under greedy or temperature-sampled decoding) and, if so, where that threshold falls relative to the measured log-probability values. If the threshold is high (e.g., log-probability must exceed ~2.0 before the fact is reliably generated), many of the "acquired" facts in the paper may remain behaviorally inaccessible, which would substantially weaken the practical interpretation of the learnability threshold concept. If the threshold is low (e.g., ~0.5), the log-probability dynamics translate more directly to usable knowledge. This experiment would also reveal whether different acquisition depths (memorization, semantic generalization, compositional generalization) have different log-probability thresholds for generation — deeper generalization may require higher encoding strength before it manifests in outputs.

**Cross-model-family and cross-data-distribution replication.** The paper's findings are based entirely on OLMo-1B and OLMo-7B trained on Dolma. A strong replication study would apply the identical fictional knowledge injection paradigm to at least one other model family (e.g., a Llama-2 or Mistral intermediate checkpoint, if optimizer states and data order are available) and at least one other data distribution (e.g., continued pretraining on The Pile or RefinedWeb rather than Dolma). The key measurements to compare are: (1) the decay constant `a` for memorization and semantic generalization under duplication — does the power-law relationship hold with similar slopes across architectures, or is the forgetting rate architecture-specific? (2) the effectivity ratio between 1B and 7B models — does the model-size advantage in per-exposure encoding efficiency generalize, and does it continue to scale log-linearly or saturate? (3) the batch-size effect on the learnability threshold — does reducing batch size by 16× produce a similar 1–2 order of magnitude reduction in the x-intercept across architectures, or is this effect specific to OLMo's optimizer configuration (`β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95`, cosine decay)? A finding that the decay constants are architecture-specific would have immediate practical implications: different model families would have different learnability thresholds, and practitioners would need to tune data repetition strategies to their specific model, not just to their data distribution. A finding that the batch-size effect is universal would strengthen the paper's recommendation to use larger batch sizes for knowledge retention.

**Factual interference: measuring how concurrent fact learning affects acquisition dynamics.** The paper injects 40 independent fictional facts simultaneously, with no analysis of whether they interfere. A controlled interference experiment would replicate the injection protocol but systematically vary the *semantic overlap* between simultaneously injected facts. For example: inject 40 facts about fictional Martian politics (high semantic overlap), 40 facts about diverse fictional entities in different domains (low overlap, the original design), and 40 facts with varying degrees of shared entities or properties. The dependent variables would be: (1) per-fact effectivity — does effectivity decrease as the number of semantically related facts increases, suggesting competitive encoding? (2) decay constants — does the forgetting rate accelerate when many related facts compete for retention, suggesting catastrophic interference? (3) compositional generalization specifically — since compositional generalization requires integrating facts, does semantic overlap *help* (shared representations provide richer integration) or *hurt* (interference between overlapping fact sets)? The paper's current isolation design likely provides an upper bound on retention performance; measuring interference effects would provide a more realistic estimate and could reveal whether certain types of factual knowledge (e.g., domain-specific vs. general) are more vulnerable to interference than others.

**Optimizer state initialization ablation for the batch-size effect.** The paper's batch-size comparison confounds batch size (2048 vs. 128) with optimizer state initialization (warm-start from OLMo pretraining vs. cold-start re-initialization). A clean ablation would run four conditions in a 2×2 design: batch size {2048, 128} × optimizer initialization {warm-start from OLMo checkpoint, cold-start re-initialized}. The key comparison is batch size 2048 with cold-start vs. batch size 128 with cold-start — this isolates the pure effect of batch size on forgetting dynamics, controlling for initialization. If the batch-size effect persists (decay constants remain higher at batch size 128, learnability thresholds remain lower), the paper's claim is validated. If the effect disappears or substantially shrinks, then the observed batch-size differences are primarily an optimizer initialization artifact, and the paper's recommendations about batch size for knowledge retention would need to be substantially revised. This experiment is straightforward to run within the existing experimental framework (same OLMo checkpoints, same fictional knowledge, same injection protocol) and should be a high priority for anyone attempting to build on this work.

**Fine-tuning the revision model analogy: can knowledge be "re-acquired" after forgetting without full retraining?** The paper shows that acquired factual knowledge is forgotten with a power-law decay. An open question is whether knowledge that has been "forgotten" (log-probability returned to baseline) leaves any residual trace in the model's parameters that could be reactivated more efficiently than learning from scratch — analogous to the "savings" effect in human memory, where re-learning previously known information is faster than initial learning. A follow-up experiment would run the standard injection protocol (10 injections at 100-step intervals), then continue training until the retainability curve crosses zero (log-probability returns to pre-injection baseline), then re-inject the *same* knowledge for a second cycle of 10 injections. If the model shows higher effectivity during the second cycle (faster re-acquisition), this would indicate that forgetting is not complete erasure but rather a reduction in accessibility that leaves latent representations intact. This would have implications for continual learning, model updating, and the design of rehearsal strategies — if re-acquisition is substantially cheaper than initial acquisition, pretraining pipelines could include periodic "refresher" epochs on long-tail knowledge rather than relying on sustained high-frequency exposure throughout training.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Pretraining data scheduling for domain-specific models.** Organizations that pretrain or extensively fine-tune LLMs on domain-specific corpora (e.g., medical, legal, scientific) often have a fixed set of critical facts that the model must reliably acquire — drug interactions, case law precedents, physical constants. The paper's learnability threshold framework provides a concrete design principle: for each critical fact, estimate its encounter interval in the training data (from the data distribution), compare to the estimated learnability threshold for the target model size and batch size (from a calibration experiment using the fictional knowledge injection paradigm on a smaller proxy model), and if the interval exceeds the threshold, intervene by up-sampling or inserting additional diverse paraphrases of that fact at shorter intervals. The paper's numbers provide a starting point: for OLMo-7B at batch size 2048, the learnability threshold for memorization under paraphrased presentation is approximately `10^11.4` tokens (Appendix Table 6, early checkpoint, paraphrase, memorization). If a critical fact appears only once per 500 billion tokens in the domain corpus, it will not be learnable without intervention. The intervention cost can be estimated: to bring the interval below the threshold, the fact must appear at least `N = total_tokens / threshold` times, and each appearance costs one sequence in a batch. This converts data engineering from an empirical trial-and-error process to a budgeted optimization problem.

**Batch size selection for knowledge-intensive applications.** The paper's finding that reducing batch size from 2048 to 128 shrinks the learnability threshold by 1–2 orders of magnitude (comparing Appendix Tables 6 and 11) has direct implications for practitioners training models under hardware constraints that force smaller batch sizes (e.g., on-device fine-tuning, low-memory GPU setups). A team fine-tuning a 7B model on a single GPU with a batch size of 32 would face a learnability threshold potentially hundreds of times shorter than the same model trained at batch size 2048. This means that facts which would be learnable under the large-batch regime (with encounter intervals of ~100 billion tokens) would be unlearnable under the small-batch regime unless the interval between exposures is correspondingly shortened. The practical response is not necessarily "always use the largest possible batch size" (which may be infeasible) but rather "if you must use a small batch size, compensate by increasing the frequency of fact repetition in your training data." Gradient accumulation — simulating a larger batch size by accumulating gradients over multiple micro-batches before updating — is a standard technique that may recover some of the retention benefit, but the paper does not test this directly. A practitioner could run the fictional knowledge injection calibration at their target batch size to estimate their specific learnability threshold and adjust their data repetition strategy accordingly.

**Auditing and improving pretraining data deduplication pipelines.** The paper's finding that paraphrased presentation produces slower forgetting than duplicated presentation (decay constants `a = 0.21` vs. `0.25` for memorization at OLMo-7B mid, Table 2) provides a quantitative rationale for deduplication that goes beyond the standard motivation of reducing verbatim regurgitation. For organizations building or curating pretraining corpora, this suggests that deduplication should not be a binary decision (deduplicate or not) but a **spectrum**: near-exact duplicates should be removed (they accelerate forgetting), but diverse paraphrases of the same factual content should be *preserved or even amplified* (they slow forgetting and narrow the memorization-generalization gap). A concrete pipeline improvement: after running a standard deduplication step (e.g., MinHash or semantic deduplication as in Abbas et al., 2023), separately identify factually redundant passages across the deduplication threshold — documents that convey the same factual information in substantially different surface forms — and ensure they are not collapsed. The paper's experimental design (comparing verbatim duplication against GPT-4-generated paraphrases) demonstrates the principle; a practitioner would need to operationalize this with a factual-entailment detector or a paraphrase classifier tuned to their domain.

### When to Prefer This Method

This paper does not propose a method competing against named alternatives; it provides a diagnostic framework and a set of empirical findings about pretraining dynamics. There is no "compute-optimal factual knowledge acquisition" policy proposed that could be weighed against, for example, standard pretraining or data augmentation baselines. The paper's contributions are descriptive and explanatory, not prescriptive in a way that generates a clear decision rule against alternatives. A forced "prefer X when Y" matrix would invent tradeoffs not articulated in the paper.
