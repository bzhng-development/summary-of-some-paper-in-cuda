# Nearest Neighbor Speculative Decoding for LLM Generation and Attribution

**ArXiv:** [2405.19325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19325)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Nearest Neighbor Speculative Decoding (NEST)**, a semi-parametric language modeling approach that incorporates real-world text spans of arbitrary length into LLM generations while providing direct attribution to source documents.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Nearest Neighbor Speculative Decoding (NEST)**, a semi-parametric language modeling approach that incorporates real-world text spans of arbitrary length into LLM generations while providing direct attribution to source documents. Evaluated on Llama-2-Chat models (7B–70B) across nine knowledge-intensive tasks—including WikiText-103, Natural Questions, TruthfulQA, and Biography—NEST combines a two-stage k-NN retrieval pipeline (passage-level dense-sparse hybrid search followed by on-the-fly token-level nearest-neighbor lookup), a **Relative Retrieval Confidence (RRC)** score (dynamically interpolating the LM and retrieval distributions based on retriever uncertainty), dynamic span selection (extending beyond single tokens to n-gram continuations in the corpus when retrieval confidence exceeds a threshold), and relaxed speculative decoding (a lenient rejection procedure that accepts retrieved span prefixes deemed likely by the mixture distribution, upper-bounding standard speculative decoding's acceptance probability). NEST achieves a 1.8× inference speedup on Llama-2-Chat 70B while improving ROUGE-1 by 42.3% on WikiText-103 and FACTSCORE by 21.6% on Biography over the base LM, and provides span-level attribution for 33–96% of generated tokens (with average attributed spans of 3–28 consecutive tokens), establishing that retrieval-augmented generation can simultaneously improve quality, attribution, and latency—provided the base model already possesses sufficient capability to produce correct answers for the target problems.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLMs Hallucinate and Cannot Attribute Their Claims

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **large language models produce plausible-sounding but factually incorrect content and cannot tell you where their information came from**. This manifests as two intertwined limitations:

**Hallucination** refers to the model generating text that is grammatically fluent and semantically coherent but factually unsupported — inventing names, dates, statistics, or events that never occurred. This is not a rare edge case; it is a systematic failure mode particularly acute on **long-tail knowledge** — facts that appear infrequently in the pretraining data (Kandpal et al., 2023; Asai et al., 2023a). When asked about a niche historical event, a minor celebrity, or a specialized legal precedent, LLMs often confabulate rather than admit uncertainty.

**Lack of attribution** means that even when the model does produce factually correct information, a user cannot easily verify its source. In high-stakes domains — legal reasoning, medical advice, journalism, academic research — an unsupported claim is nearly as problematic as a false one because it forces the reader to independently verify every statement. The paper explicitly connects this to broader concerns about credibility, copyright, and risk management (Section 8): if AI-generated content borrows from existing human works (books, articles, creative content), both users and content creators need attribution mechanisms.

These two failures are related but distinct. A model might produce a correct fact while providing no source, or it might provide a plausible-sounding source for a hallucinated fact. Solving hallucination alone without attribution leaves the verification burden on the user; solving attribution alone without improving factuality means attributing incorrect claims to specific (potentially misrepresented) sources. The paper aims to address both simultaneously.

The practical stakes are substantial. As LLMs are increasingly deployed for open-ended generation — answering questions, writing summaries, composing biographies, explaining concepts — the absence of trustworthy grounding mechanisms limits their adoption in any domain where factual reliability matters. The authors implicitly argue that **generation quality, attribution, and latency are typically framed as a tradeoff** (better attribution requires retrieval, which slows things down; faster generation comes at the cost of verification), and that overcoming this tradeoff is the key technical challenge.

### Why Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper situates itself against three classes of prior solutions, each with documented limitations:

**In-context retrieval augmentation (RA)** is the most widely deployed approach in production systems (Section 5.1, "Input augmentation"). The strategy is straightforward: given a user query, retrieve relevant passages from a knowledge corpus, prepend them to the input prompt, and let the LM generate conditioned on that retrieved context. This approach — employed by REPLUG (Shi et al., 2024a), in-context RALM (Ram et al., 2023), Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2023b), and RA-DIT (Lin et al., 2024) — is appealing because it requires no architectural changes to the base LM and can be applied to any off-the-shelf model.

However, the paper identifies a critical limitation: **in-context RA does not guarantee faithful attribution**. The model sees retrieved passages in its context but may still (1) ignore them entirely and rely on its parametric knowledge, (2) combine information from multiple passages in ways that introduce factual errors, or (3) hallucinate details not present in any retrieved source. The attribution problem persists because the model's generation process is opaque — even with retrieval, there is no mechanism to trace which output tokens came from which source. The paper's results on TruthfulQA (Table 1) provide empirical evidence: RA models often perform *worse* than base LMs on adversarial questions because they are "more susceptible to the 'evidence' in the prompt (e.g., astrology and myths)." The retrieved context can actively mislead the model when it contains authoritative-sounding but incorrect information.

Moreover, RA expands the model's input context by prepending retrieved passages (typically 3 in the paper's experiments due to context window constraints). This increases the cost of the key-value cache refresh, adds latency proportional to the number of passages, and does nothing to improve per-token generation speed. The quality-latency tradeoff remains unresolved.

**Nearest Neighbor Language Models (kNN-LM)**, introduced by Khandelwal et al. (2020), take a fundamentally different approach: instead of modifying the input, they modify the *output distribution* at each generation step. A key-value datastore is constructed from a corpus, mapping LM hidden states to next-token distributions. At inference time, the LM's hidden state at each step serves as a query to retrieve nearest neighbors in this datastore, producing a non-parametric distribution $p_{\text{k-NN}}$ over next tokens. This is linearly interpolated with the LM's parametric distribution $p_{\text{LM}}$ using a fixed coefficient $\lambda$, yielding a mixture distribution:

$$p_M(w|x, y_{<t}) = \lambda \cdot p_{\text{LM}}(w|x, y_{<t}) + (1 - \lambda) \cdot p_{\text{k-NN}}(w|x, y_{<t})$$

This approach has two compelling advantages over in-context RA: (1) the interpolation happens at the token level, providing a more direct and fine-grained mechanism for the retrieval signal to influence generation, and (2) each retrieved token can theoretically be traced back to its source in the corpus, enabling attribution.

But the paper identifies three critical shortcomings of standard kNN-LM that have limited its practical adoption:

**First: catastrophic storage and latency costs.** The key-value datastore $(K, V)$ defined in Equation (1) grows linearly with the total number of tokens in the corpus. For a Wikipedia-scale corpus (~33M passages in the paper's preprocessing), this would mean storing billions of high-dimensional hidden state vectors and searching over them at each inference step. The paper notes this "brings difficulty in scaling the size of the corpus and the model, which may require massive storage space and computational resources" (Section 2.2). This has made kNN-LM impractical for deployment with large corpora and large models.

**Second: degraded text fluency.** A crucial empirical finding from Wang et al. (2023a) is that kNN-LM "does not improve open-ended text generation" — while it can reduce perplexity on language modeling benchmarks, the quality of generated text (fluency, coherence, diversity) suffers. The paper attributes this partly to the fixed interpolation coefficient $\lambda$, which treats all tokens and all contexts uniformly regardless of whether the LM or the retriever is more reliable at that particular point in the generation.

**Third: token-level retrieval ignores coherent multi-token continuations.** Standard kNN-LM retrieves individual next tokens based on hidden state similarity. But retrieved tokens often come from completely different source documents, and mixing them at the token level can produce incoherent text even when each individual token is reasonable. The approach treats the corpus as a bag of tokens rather than a collection of coherent documents, discarding the natural continuity of real text spans.

**Intermediate fusion** approaches like RETRO (Borgeaud et al., 2022), RETRO++ (Wang et al., 2023b), InstructRetro (Wang et al., 2024), and FiD (Izacard and Grave, 2021) embed retrieval results into intermediate model layers through specialized cross-attention mechanisms. These methods achieve stronger integration of retrieval and generation than either input augmentation or output interpolation, but they require architectural modifications to the base LM and typically involve additional pretraining or fine-tuning. The paper positions NEST as a **plug-and-play** alternative that works with any off-the-shelf LM without retraining.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

NEST is framed as a direct extension and resolution of the tension between these three approaches. It takes the **output integration** paradigm from kNN-LM (token-level interpolation between parametric and non-parametric distributions) and augments it with three innovations that address the specific failures documented above:

**Against kNN-LM's scalability problem:** The two-stage retrieval design (Section 3.1) — passage-level search first, then on-the-fly token index construction only over the top-b retrieved passages — reduces the datastore from billions of tokens to a manageable few thousand per query, making the approach practical for large corpora without requiring massive pre-built token indexes.

**Against kNN-LM's fluency problem:** The Relative Retrieval Confidence (RRC) score (Section 3.2) replaces the fixed interpolation coefficient $\lambda$ with a dynamic, uncertainty-aware $\lambda_t$ that adapts per generation step based on the retriever's confidence:

$$\lambda_t = \sigma\left(\left(\frac{\min_i |s(q_t, k_i)|}{\max_i |s(q_t, k_i)|} - \alpha\right) / \tau\right)$$

When the retriever is uncertain (similarity scores are uniform), $\lambda_t$ approaches 1, meaning the model relies mostly on its parametric knowledge. When the retriever is confident (one neighbor is much closer than others), $\lambda_t$ decreases, incorporating the retrieval signal more strongly. This avoids forcing retrieval when it would degrade quality.

**Against kNN-LM's token-level myopia:** The dynamic span selection mechanism (Section 3.3) extends beyond individual tokens to retrieve **n-gram continuations** from the corpus. When the retriever is confident enough ($\lambda_t \leq \delta$), NEST fetches not just the next token but a stretch of following tokens as they naturally appear in the source document. This preserves the coherence of real natural language spans rather than stitching together tokens from disparate sources.

**Against in-context RA's attribution opacity:** Because NEST copies actual text spans from identified source documents, every retrieved span can be precisely attributed to its origin. The paper's attribution analysis (Section 4.6, Table 2) quantifies this: 33–96% of generated tokens can be traced back to the corpus, with average consecutive attributed spans ranging from 3 to 28 tokens. The authors explicitly claim that "neither of the baselines can achieve the same granularity and preciseness for the attribution as NEST."

**Against the latency tradeoff:** The relaxed speculative decoding procedure (Section 3.4) is the mechanism that makes the approach *fast* despite the retrieval overhead. Inspired by speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023) — where a small draft model proposes multiple tokens that a large model verifies in parallel — NEST inverts the logic: the corpus plays the role of the draft model, and the mixture distribution $p_M$ plays the role of the verifier. By accepting or rejecting tokens from the retrieved span using a relaxed (lenient) acceptance criterion, NEST processes multiple tokens per forward pass of the LLM, amortizing the cost of retrieval across more generated tokens. The paper's headline 1.8× speedup on Llama-2-Chat 70B (Section 4.5, Figure 2a) demonstrates that the approach achieves *faster* generation than the base LM despite the additional retrieval and verification steps — addressing a fundamental tension that had made prior semi-parametric LMs slower than their parametric counterparts.

### The Conceptual Bridge: Output Integration + Speculative Decoding

The paper's intellectual move can be understood as combining two previously separate lines of work: **kNN-based output interpolation** (which improves factuality but degrades fluency and latency) and **speculative decoding** (which improves latency but doesn't address factuality or attribution). By using the retrieved span as a "draft" and the interpolated mixture distribution as the "verifier," NEST creates a synthesis where:

1. The retrieval mechanism provides the candidate text (factuality + attribution).
2. The LM (augmented with retrieval probabilities) verifies the candidate (maintains fluency).
3. The speculative procedure amortizes LM forward passes across multiple tokens (improves latency).

This synthesis is described in language that deliberately echoes both literatures. The "dynamic span selection" component (Section 3.3) is explicitly credited to the Copy Generator (COG) by Lan et al. (2023), establishing lineage to the phrase-level retrieval tradition. The "relaxed speculative decoding" component (Section 3.4) is explicitly credited to Leviathan et al. (2023), with the key modification being that the proposal distribution is the corpus (not a smaller model), and the acceptance criterion is relaxed (not exact) because the proposal distribution over the draft tokens beyond the first is unknown. The term "leniency" for the relaxation factor $\gamma$ is directly adopted from Leviathan et al., anchoring the method in established terminology.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

NEST is a plug-and-play generation method that wraps around any existing large language model without requiring retraining, architectural changes, or fine-tuning. It solves the problem of producing factually grounded, attributable text at low latency by treating a knowledge corpus as a source of real text spans that the LM can selectively copy, verify, and reject — like giving the model a library card and teaching it when to quote versus when to improvise.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The NEST system has seven major components arranged in a pipeline that executes at every generation step:

1. **Two-Stage Retrieval** — a passage-level search (dense + sparse hybrid) that narrows a massive corpus down to the top-b most relevant passages, followed by on-the-fly token-level nearest-neighbor lookup within only those passages. This avoids storing or searching over billions of token embeddings.

2. **LM Hidden State Encoder** — the base LLM's own internal representation at a fixed layer (the input to the final feed-forward network after layer normalization), which serves as the query for token retrieval. No separate encoder is trained.

3. **Relative Retrieval Confidence (RRC) Scorer** — a dynamic mechanism that computes the retrieval system's uncertainty at each step by comparing the best and worst neighbor similarity scores, then converts this into an interpolation coefficient `$\lambda_t$` that determines how much to trust the retriever versus the LM.

4. **Mixture Distribution Constructor** — linearly interpolates the LM's parametric next-token distribution with the non-parametric distribution from retrieved nearest neighbors, weighted by `$\lambda_t$`.

5. **Dynamic Span Selector** — when the retriever is sufficiently confident (`$\lambda_t \leq \delta$`), extends beyond a single token to fetch the n-gram continuation of the selected token as it naturally appears in the source corpus, preserving multi-token coherence.

6. **Relaxed Speculative Decoder** — treats each fetched span as a "draft" and verifies it token-by-token against the mixture distribution using a lenient acceptance criterion. Accepted tokens are kept; rejected tokens are replaced by tokens sampled from the mixture distribution. This processes multiple tokens per LM forward pass.

7. **Corpus Masking** — once a span is accepted, the corresponding source passage segment is masked to prevent the retriever from recycling the same text, avoiding repetitive generation loops.

Information flows as follows: input prompt enters the system → passage retriever identifies top-b relevant passages → for each generation step, the LM encodes the current context into a hidden state → token retriever finds nearest neighbors within the b passages → RRC computes `$\lambda_t$` → mixture distribution is formed → best token is selected → if confidence is high, the n-gram continuation is fetched → speculative decoding accepts or rejects each token in the span → accepted tokens are appended to the output and masked in the corpus.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the two-stage k-NN search (Section 3.1), because every downstream component depends on the retrieved passages and token neighbors, and the efficiency gains of this design enable the entire rest of the system.
- **Second**, the Relative Retrieval Confidence mechanism (Section 3.2), because `$\lambda_t$` is the central control variable — it determines whether the system trusts the LM or the retriever, feeds into span selection, and influences speculative decoding behavior.
- **Third**, the mixture distribution and dynamic span selection (Section 3.3), because these components use `$\lambda_t$` and the retrieved neighbors to produce either a single token or a multi-token span, establishing what the speculative decoder will verify.
- **Fourth**, the relaxed speculative decoding procedure (Section 3.4), because it is the most technically involved component — it builds on the previous sections, modifies standard speculative decoding for the semi-parametric setting, and is responsible for the latency gains.
- **Fifth**, the complete NEST algorithm (Algorithm 1 in the appendix), because walking through the pseudocode consolidates all components into a single coherent procedure and reveals interactions (e.g., the fallback behavior when tokens are rejected) that are easy to miss in prose descriptions.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems paper** whose core idea is that a language model can generate better, more attributable text faster by dynamically retrieving real text spans from a corpus and using a lenient speculative decoding procedure to verify them — all without modifying the base model.

---

#### Two-Stage k-NN Search

The naive kNN-LM approach from Khandelwal et al. (2020) requires building a key-value datastore over every token in the corpus. For a corpus with `$T$` tokens and a hidden state dimension `$d$`, this means storing `$T \times d$` floating-point numbers and performing `$O(T \log T)$` nearest-neighbor lookups per generation step. For Wikipedia-scale corpora (billions of tokens) and large models (`$d = 4096$` or `$d = 8192$`), this is infeasible without massive distributed infrastructure. The two-stage design addresses this by introducing an intermediate filtering step — passage-level retrieval — that reduces the token search space from billions to a few thousand per query.

**First-stage passage retrieval.** Given the input `$x$`, the system retrieves relevant passages from the full corpus `$\mathcal{C}$` using a hybrid of dense and sparse retrieval:

1. The corpus `$\mathcal{C}$` is segmented into passages of at most `$m = 200$` tokens each. For Wikipedia, this yields approximately 33 million passages; for Pile of Law, approximately 15 million.

2. Each passage is encoded into a single dense vector using DRAGON+ (Lin et al., 2023), a state-of-the-art dual-encoder dense retriever. These vectors are stored in a Faiss index using IVF (Inverted File) with `$65536$` centroids and product quantization into `$256$` codes of 8 bits each, with `$\text{nprobe} = 4096$` at search time. The dense index is approximately 8.96 GB on disk for Wikipedia.

3. Simultaneously, the same passages are indexed using BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009), a sparse lexical retrieval model, via Pyserini (Lin et al., 2021) with default hyperparameters. The sparse index is approximately 3.48 GB on disk.

4. Both indices are queried with the input `$x$` to retrieve `$b \cdot l$` passages each, where `$b = 40$` (the number of passages ultimately retained) and `$l = 100$` (a scaling factor that over-retrieves to improve fusion quality). So each index returns 4000 passages with similarity scores.

5. **Fusion:** The similarity scores from dense and sparse retrieval are linearly interpolated using a dynamic coefficient `$\eta$` that reflects the relative confidence of each retriever. Specifically:
   - The dense confidence is computed as `$\eta_{\text{dense}} = 1 - \text{top-}100(s_{\text{dense}}(q, d)) / \max s_{\text{dense}}(q, d)$`, where `$s_{\text{dense}}(q, d)$` is the dense similarity score. This measures how quickly the scores drop off — if the top-100 scores are very close to the maximum, the dense retriever is uncertain.
   - The sparse confidence `$\eta_{\text{sparse}}$` is computed analogously.
   - The final interpolation coefficient is `$\eta = 0.7 \cdot (1 - \eta_{\text{sparse}}) + 0.3 \cdot \eta_{\text{dense}}$`, giving more weight to sparse confidence in the default configuration.
   - The fused score is `$s(q, d) = \eta \cdot s_{\text{dense}}(q, d) + (1 - \eta) \cdot s_{\text{sparse}}(q, d)$`.
   - Passages missing from one index are assigned the minimum similarity score from that index's results.

6. The passages are sorted by fused score and the top `$b = 40$` are retained as `$\{d_1, d_2, ..., d_b\}$`.

**Why this fusion design?** Dense retrievers excel at semantic matching — they can find passages about the same topic even when the vocabulary differs from the query. Sparse retrievers excel at lexical matching — they can find passages containing the exact terms in the query. The dynamic fusion coefficient `$\eta$` adapts the blend per query: when the dense retriever is confident (scores drop sharply from the maximum), it gets more weight; when the sparse retriever is confident, it gets more weight. This avoids the brittleness of either retriever alone. The 0.7/0.3 split in the formula biases toward dense retrieval by default (since `$(1 - \eta_{\text{sparse}})$` is high when sparse is confident, and this term gets the 0.7 weight), but the dynamic coefficients can override this.

**Second-stage token retrieval.** Once the top-b passages are identified, the system constructs a token-level key-value datastore on the fly — crucially, only over the tokens in those b passages, not the entire corpus:

1. For each passage `$d_i$`, the base LM's hidden state encoder `$f(\cdot)$` processes the passage prefix-by-prefix: for each position `$j$`, the prefix `$w_{d_i}^{1}, ..., w_{d_i}^{j}$` is fed through the LM, and the hidden state `$h_{d_i}^{j}$` at the final layer's input to the feed-forward network (after layer normalization) is extracted as the key. The next token `$w_{d_i}^{j+1}$` is stored as the value.

2. This produces an on-the-fly datastore `$(K', V')$`:
   $$(K', V') = \{(h_{d_i}^{j}, w_{d_i}^{j+1}) \mid i \in \{1, ..., b\}, j \in \{1, ..., |d_i| - 1\}\}$$
   The size of `$(K', V')$` is roughly `$b \times m \approx 40 \times 200 = 8000$` keys — four orders of magnitude smaller than a full-corpus token index.

3. At each generation step `$t$`, the current context `$y_{<t}$` (the input prompt plus all previously generated tokens) is encoded by `$f(\cdot)$` to produce a query vector `$q_t$`:
   $$q_t = f(y_{<t})[-1]$$
   where `$[-1]$` denotes the hidden state at the final position (the last token of the current context).

4. The query `$q_t$` is used to search `$(K', V')$` for the `$r = 1024$` nearest neighbors using squared `$\ell^2$` distance as the similarity metric:
   $$s(q_t, k_i) = -\|q_t - k_i\|_2^2$$
   The top-r neighbors are returned as `$\pi = \{(s_1, v_1), (s_2, v_2), ..., (s_r, v_r)\}$`, where `$s_i$` is the similarity score and `$v_i$` is the corresponding next token.

5. The non-parametric distribution `$p_{\text{k-NN}}(w \mid x, y_{<t})$` is computed from these neighbors using a temperature-scaled softmax over the similarity scores:
   $$p_{\text{k-NN}}(w \mid x, y_{<t}) \propto \sum_{(k_i, v_i) \in \pi} \mathbb{1}_{w = v_i} \exp(\mu \cdot s(q_t, k_i))$$
   where `$\mu = 1 / \sqrt{\dim(q_t)}$` is the inverse temperature, `$\dim(q_t)$` is the hidden state dimension, and `$\mathbb{1}_{w = v_i}$` is the indicator function that is 1 when `$w$` matches the retrieved token `$v_i$` and 0 otherwise. Tokens not appearing among the top-r neighbors receive zero probability.

**What this computes:** For each possible next token `$w$`, the non-parametric distribution sums up exponentiated and temperature-scaled similarity scores over all retrieved neighbors whose value matches `$w$`. If the same token was retrieved from multiple source positions (e.g., the word "the" appears in many contexts), their similarity scores accumulate, giving that token a higher probability. The `$\mu$` scaling (inverse square root of dimension) normalizes the similarity scores by the vector dimensionality, analogous to the scaling in Transformer attention (Vaswani et al., 2017) where `$\mu = 1/\sqrt{d_k}$`.

**Why this form?** The squared `$\ell^2$` distance is the same metric used in the original kNN-LM (Khandelwal et al., 2020). It measures Euclidean proximity in the LM's hidden state space — tokens that the LM represents similarly to the current context are considered good candidates. The softmax with temperature converts these distances into a proper probability distribution. The indicator function `$\mathbb{1}_{w = v_i}$` ensures that only tokens actually observed in the corpus can receive probability mass from the retrieval component, which is what enables attribution — every token that comes from `$p_{\text{k-NN}}$` can be traced to its source.

**Critical design choice — on-the-fly encoding.** Encoding the top-b passages at each generation step (rather than pre-encoding the entire corpus) would normally be prohibitively expensive, since it requires `$b \times m$` forward passes through the LM per step. However, because the same passages are reused across many generation steps for a given query (the passage retrieval runs only once at the start), the hidden states can be cached. In practice, the paper reports that the LM encoding time for this step takes "about half of the latency" (Section 4.5, Figure 2a), while the token search itself is relatively fast (on CPU with 32 threads). The cost is amortized across all generation steps for the query.

**Trade-off: accuracy vs. latency.** The two-stage design creates an explicit trade-off between retrieval accuracy and latency. If the first-stage passage retriever fails to include a passage containing the correct information, no amount of second-stage token search can recover it — the correct token simply won't be in `$(K', V')`. The paper acknowledges this: "The first-stage passage search also acts as a filter to remove deceptively similar tokens in non-relevant contexts" (Section 3.1). The hyperparameter `$b = 40$` was selected empirically (Appendix C, Figure 3a): increasing `$b$` from 40 to 80 reduces perplexity by roughly 0.5–1.0 points but nearly doubles the encoding latency. The chosen value represents a balance where most relevant passages are included while keeping the token datastore small enough for low-latency search.

#### Confidence-Based Output Interpolation (Relative Retrieval Confidence)

Standard kNN-LM uses a fixed interpolation coefficient `$\lambda$` (Equation 3 in the paper) that blends the parametric and non-parametric distributions identically at every generation step. This is problematic because the reliability of the retriever varies dramatically: when the current context is similar to many passages (e.g., generating common function words), the nearest neighbors may be diverse and uninformative; when the context is highly specific (e.g., generating the middle of a rare entity name), the nearest neighbors may be tightly clustered around the correct token. A fixed `$\lambda$` forces the model to trust the retriever equally in both cases.

**The RRC formula.** NEST replaces the fixed `$\lambda$` with a dynamic, per-step coefficient `$\lambda_t$` computed from the retrieval similarity scores themselves:

$$\lambda_t = \sigma\left(\left(\frac{\min_i |s(q_t, k_i)|}{\max_i |s(q_t, k_i)|} - \alpha\right) / \tau\right)$$

where:
- `$s(q_t, k_i)$` is the squared `$\ell^2$` distance similarity for the i-th nearest neighbor (a negative number since distances are non-negative, so `$|s(q_t, k_i)|$` is the absolute distance),
- `$\min_i |s(q_t, k_i)|$` is the distance to the closest neighbor (smallest distance = highest similarity),
- `$\max_i |s(q_t, k_i)|$` is the distance to the most distant neighbor within the top-r set,
- `$\alpha$` is an offset hyperparameter (set to `$0.3$` for Wikipedia tasks, `$0.2$` for Pile of Law),
- `$\tau$` is a temperature hyperparameter (set to `$0.1$` for all tasks),
- `$\sigma(\cdot)$` is the sigmoid function `$\sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$`.

**What this computes:** The ratio `$\min_i |s| / \max_i |s|$` measures the **relative spread** of the retrieved neighbor distances. If all neighbors are roughly equally similar (distances are similar magnitudes), this ratio approaches 1. If one neighbor is dramatically closer than the others (a sharp peak in the similarity landscape), this ratio approaches 0. This ratio is then shifted by `$-\alpha$`, scaled by `$1/\tau$`, and passed through a sigmoid, mapping it to the interval `$(0, 1)$`.

The resulting `$\lambda_t$` determines the interpolation between the LM and retrieval distributions:

$$p_M(w \mid x, y_{<t}) = \lambda_t \cdot p_{\text{LM}}(w \mid x, y_{<t}) + (1 - \lambda_t) \cdot p_{\text{k-NN}}(w \mid x, y_{<t})$$

When `$\lambda_t$` is close to 1 (retriever is uncertain — neighbors are uniformly distributed), the mixture distribution is dominated by the LM's parametric knowledge. When `$\lambda_t$` is close to 0 (retriever is confident — one neighbor stands out), the retrieval distribution receives more weight.

**Why this form?** The ratio of minimum to maximum distance is a natural measure of distributional peakedness for the retrieved neighbors. If all neighbors have distances around 10, the ratio is `$9.5/10 \approx 0.95$`, and after shifting by `$\alpha = 0.3$` and dividing by `$\tau = 0.1$`, the sigmoid input is `$(0.95 - 0.3) / 0.1 = 6.5$`, yielding `$\lambda_t \approx 0.998$` — nearly pure LM. If the closest neighbor has distance 1 and the furthest has distance 100, the ratio is `$1/100 = 0.01$`, the sigmoid input is `$(0.01 - 0.3) / 0.1 = -2.9$`, and `$\lambda_t \approx 0.052$` — nearly pure retrieval. The sigmoid provides a smooth, differentiable mapping that avoids hard thresholds, and the `$\alpha$` and `$\tau$` hyperparameters allow the practitioner to tune how aggressively the system switches from LM to retrieval.

The alternative — a fixed `$\lambda$` — would either over-trust the retriever on uncertain steps (degrading fluency) or under-trust it on confident steps (missing opportunities to improve factuality). The RRC mechanism adapts per token, per step, automatically. The paper's ablation (Appendix C, Table 3) confirms this: adding RRC to two-stage kNN-LM improves WikiText-103 ROUGE-1 from 20.1 to 24.7, NQ answer-level recall from 40.8 to 44.4, and Biography FACTSCORE from 34.8 to 41.6.

**Multi-choice and perplexity evaluation.** For tasks that do not involve free-form generation (perplexity evaluation on language modeling benchmarks, multiple-choice tasks like MMLU), NEST stops at this interpolation step — no dynamic span selection or speculative decoding is applied. The interpolated distribution `$p_M$` is used directly to score candidate sequences or choices.

#### Dynamic Span Selection

Standard kNN-LM and the interpolation step above produce a single next-token distribution `$p_M(w \mid x, y_{<t})$`. Sampling from this distribution yields one token per generation step. However, real text contains multi-token patterns — phrases, names, idioms, citations — that naturally occur as contiguous spans in source documents. Retrieving tokens individually from different source passages can produce grammatically correct but semantically incoherent text, because the tokens come from unrelated contexts.

**Why token-level mixing causes problems.** Consider generating a sentence about a specific person. The token "Barack" might be retrieved from one Wikipedia article based on hidden state similarity, and the token "Obama" from a different article in the next step. Even though both tokens are individually reasonable, the model has no guarantee that the retrieved tokens are consistent with each other — "Barack" could be followed by "University" (a different entity entirely) if that's what the second retrieval step finds. This is a direct consequence of treating the corpus as a bag of independent tokens rather than as a collection of coherent documents.

**Span selection procedure.** NEST addresses this by, when the retriever is sufficiently confident, fetching not just the single best token but an entire n-gram continuation from the corpus:

1. First, the best next token `$w_t$` is selected from the mixture distribution `$p_M$` (using greedy decoding — argmax over the distribution):
   $$w_t = \arg\max_w p_M(w \mid x, y_{<t})$$

2. However, `$w_t$` may correspond to multiple retrieved neighbors in `$\pi$` — the same token value might appear at different positions in the retrieved passages, each with different continuations. NEST uses max-pooling to select the specific instance with the highest retrieval probability:
   $$w_t^{(1)} = \arg\max_{\{w_i \mid w_i = w_t, w_i \in \pi\}} p_{\text{k-NN}}(w = w_i \mid x, y_{<t})$$
   This selects the specific occurrence of `$w_t$` that the retriever was most confident about.

3. Having identified the specific corpus position of `$w_t^{(1)}$`, the system fetches the n-gram continuation directly from the source document:
   $$C.\text{get-ngram}(w_t^{(1)}, n) = \{w_t^{(1)}, w_t^{(2)}, ..., w_t^{(n)}\}$$
   where `$n = 64$` tokens is the maximum span length. This n-gram is the naturally occurring text that follows `$w_t^{(1)}$` in the source document — preserving the actual human-written continuation.

4. The decision of whether to output a single token or the full n-gram span depends on the interpolation coefficient `$\lambda_t$`:
   $$y_t = \begin{cases} w_t, & \text{if } \lambda_t > \delta; \\ \{w_t^{(1)}, w_t^{(2)}, ..., w_t^{(n)}\}, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$
   where `$\delta = 0.5$` is the threshold for all models and tasks.

**What this computes:** When the retriever is uncertain (`$\lambda_t > \delta$`, meaning the LM component dominates), NEST falls back to standard single-token generation from the mixture distribution — the retrieved neighbors are not trusted enough to commit to a multi-token copy. When the retriever is confident (`$\lambda_t \leq \delta$`), NEST "quotes" the corpus by fetching up to 64 tokens of natural text continuation, starting from the most confidently retrieved token.

**Why this connection between `$\lambda_t$` and span selection?** The interpolation coefficient `$\lambda_t$` already encodes retrieval confidence. When `$\lambda_t$` is low, the system has determined that the retrieval distribution is strongly peaked — there is a clear "right answer" in the corpus. Under these conditions, it is safer to trust not just the single token but the entire continuation, because the corpus text is internally coherent. When `$\lambda_t$` is high, the retriever is uncertain, and blindly copying a span would risk introducing text that doesn't fit the generation context.

**Max-pooling for disambiguation.** Many tokens (especially common words) appear at multiple positions in the retrieved passages, each with different continuations. Max-pooling resolves this ambiguity by choosing the occurrence with the highest `$p_{\text{k-NN}}$` score — effectively saying "trust the most confident retrieval hit." This is a design choice over alternatives like averaging or random selection; max-pooling is deterministic and consistently picks the highest-quality match according to the retriever's own confidence metric.

**Fixed n-gram length with speculative override.** The parameter `$n = 64$` is set large enough to capture most natural text continuations (sentences, paragraphs, named entity completions) but the actual span length that survives is determined by the speculative decoding step (Section 3.4), which can reject tokens from the n-gram and shorten the span adaptively. The n-gram length is thus an upper bound, not a fixed output length.

---

#### Relaxed Speculative Decoding

The dynamic span selection produces a candidate span `$\{w_t^{(1)}, w_t^{(2)}, ..., w_t^{(n)}\}$` of up to 64 tokens. If all these tokens were accepted unconditionally, the system would be a simple copy-paste mechanism — fast but with no quality control beyond the initial retrieval confidence check. This would produce text that exactly mirrors the corpus, which could be factually correct but contextually inappropriate (e.g., continuing a sentence about one topic with text from a passage about a related but different topic). Conversely, if every token in the span were verified by running the full LM forward pass for each one, the speed advantage of generating multiple tokens per step would be lost.

**The speculative decoding analogy.** Speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023) addresses a related problem in a different context: how to speed up autoregressive generation from a large "target" model using a smaller, faster "draft" model. The draft model proposes multiple tokens; the target model evaluates them all in one parallel forward pass and accepts or rejects each token probabilistically. The key insight is that the target model can verify `$k$` tokens in roughly the same time it takes to generate 1 token, because Transformer forward passes process all positions in parallel. If most draft tokens are accepted, the effective generation speed approaches `$k \times$` the target model's speed.

NEST adapts this idea by treating the corpus as the "draft model" and the mixture distribution `$p_M$` as the "verifier." The corpus proposes a span of tokens; the LM (augmented with retrieval probabilities) verifies them in parallel. However, there is a critical difference: in standard speculative decoding, the draft model's probability distribution `$q(w)$` over the proposed tokens is known, and the acceptance criterion is:

$$P(\text{accept } w) = \min\left(1, \frac{p_{\text{target}}(w)}{q(w)}\right)$$

where `$p_{\text{target}}$` is the target model's probability. This guarantees that the resulting samples follow exactly the target model's distribution — speculative decoding is **unbiased** (it produces the same distribution as sampling from the target model directly, just faster).

In NEST, the proposal distribution `$q(w)$` over the n-gram tokens is **unknown** beyond the first token `$w_t^{(1)}$`. The corpus provides the tokens themselves but not a probability distribution over them. The standard acceptance criterion cannot be applied directly.

**The relaxed acceptance criterion.** NEST circumvents the unknown proposal distribution by using a relaxed (lenient) criterion that upper-bounds the true acceptance probability:

$$P(\text{accept token } w_t^{(i)}) = \min\left(1, \frac{p_M(w = w_t^{(i)} \mid x, y_{<t}, w_t^{(1)}, w_t^{(2)}, ..., w_t^{(i-1)})}{\gamma \cdot \max_w p_M(w \mid x, y_{<t}, w_t^{(1)}, w_t^{(2)}, ..., w_t^{(i-1)})}\right)$$

where:
- `$w_t^{(i)}$` is the i-th token in the candidate span (starting from `$i = 1$`),
- `$x$` is the original input,
- `$y_{<t}$` is all previously generated segments before the current span,
- `$w_t^{(1)}, ..., w_t^{(i-1)}$` are the already-accepted tokens from this span (conditioning context),
- `$p_M(w \mid ...)$` is the mixture distribution (the LM + retrieval interpolation) conditioned on the input, all prior outputs, and the previously accepted span tokens,
- `$\gamma \in (0, 1]$` is the relaxation factor (or "leniency" parameter),
- `$\max_w p_M(w \mid ...)$` is the probability of the most likely token under the mixture distribution at this position,
- `$\gamma \cdot \max_w p_M(w \mid ...)$` is the relaxed threshold — the token must have probability at least `$\gamma$` times the maximum possible probability to be accepted.

**What this computes:** For each token in the candidate span, in sequence, NEST evaluates whether that token is "likely enough" under the mixture distribution conditioned on all prior accepted tokens. The token is accepted if its probability `$p_M(w = w_t^{(i)})$` is at least a fraction `$\gamma$` of the probability of the most likely token at that position. For example, if `$\gamma = 0.5$` and the most likely token has probability 0.8, the candidate token needs at least probability 0.4 to be accepted. If `$\gamma = 0.01$` (very lenient), the candidate token needs only 0.008 to pass — most tokens will be accepted.

**Why this form?** The standard speculative decoding criterion `$\min(1, p/q)$` requires knowing `$q$` to guarantee unbiasedness. Since `$q$` is unknown for the n-gram continuation, NEST replaces it with a constant threshold `$\gamma \cdot \max_w p_M$`. This makes the criterion **lenient** — it is easier to accept tokens than the true `$p_M/q$` criterion would be (since `$q$` could be very large for some tokens, making `$p_M/q$` small). The effect is that NEST's sampling distribution is **biased** toward the corpus — it will occasionally accept tokens that a theoretically correct speculative decoder would reject. The paper explicitly frames this as a trade-off: leniency improves speed (more tokens accepted = fewer costly resampling steps) at the cost of potentially drifting from the exact mixture distribution.

The relaxation factor `$\gamma$` controls the leniency-accuracy trade-off. As shown in Figure 2b, smaller `$\gamma$` (e.g., `$5 \times 10^{-5}$`) causes NEST to accept longer average spans (up to 35 tokens) and achieve lower latency per query, but may degrade FACTSCORE. Larger `$\gamma$` (e.g., `$5 \times 10^{-1}$`) causes more frequent rejection (average span length drops to ~5 tokens) and higher latency, but may improve quality. The paper finds a "sweet spot" around `$\gamma = 5 \times 10^{-2}$` for the 70B model on Biography, where both low latency and high accuracy are achieved. The specific `$\gamma$` values used in the main experiments vary by model size: `$5 \times 10^{-4}$` for 7B, `$5 \times 10^{-3}$` for 13B, `$5 \times 10^{-2}$` for 70B (all Wikipedia tasks), with the pattern being that stronger models benefit from more stringent verification (higher `$\gamma$`) because their parametric knowledge is more reliable.

**Why does the criterion use max probability in the denominator?** The term `$\max_w p_M(w \mid ...)$` normalizes the threshold to the scale of the distribution at that position. If the mixture distribution is sharply peaked (one token has probability 0.9), the threshold is high, making it harder for candidate tokens to pass. If the distribution is flat (all tokens have probability around 0.01), the threshold is low, making it easier — the verifier has no strong opinion, so it defers to the corpus. This adaptivity is important: it prevents the verifier from rejecting tokens when it is itself uncertain.

**The rejection-and-resampling procedure.** The speculative decoding process proceeds token-by-token through the candidate span:

1. For `$i = 1$` to `$n$` (iterating through the span tokens):
   - Compute the acceptance probability `$p_{\text{accept}}(w_t^{(i)})$` using the criterion above.
   - **If accepted:** append `$w_t^{(i)}$` to the output and continue to the next token in the span.
   - **If rejected (acceptance probability `$\leq 0.5$`):** discard all remaining tokens from `$w_t^{(i)}$` through `$w_t^{(n)}$`. Then, sample a new token `$w_t^{(i)}$` from the mixture distribution `$p_M(w \mid x, y_{<t}, w_t^{(1)}, ..., w_t^{(i-1)})$` **without going through the span selection mechanism** (i.e., standard single-token generation). Append this new token and end the speculative decoding for this step.
   
2. If all `$n$` tokens are accepted, fetch the next n-gram continuation from the corpus starting from after the accepted span, and repeat the speculative decoding process — continuing to accept corpus text as long as the verifier agrees.

**The `$\leq 0.5$` rejection threshold in practice.** The algorithm uses `$\leq 0.5$` as the rejection condition (Algorithm 1, line "Break if `$p_{\text{accept}}(w_t^{(i)}) \leq 0.5$`"). This means that even when `$p_{\text{accept}}$` is above 0.5 but below 1.0, the token is accepted. In standard speculative decoding, the acceptance probability is used to randomly accept/reject tokens (if `$p/q = 0.7$`, the token is accepted with 70% probability). NEST simplifies this to a deterministic rule: accept if the acceptance probability exceeds 0.5. This is another source of bias — it eliminates the randomness in the rejection step — but it ensures deterministic, reproducible behavior and avoids the complexity of random sampling at each verification step.

**Corpus masking.** Once a span from the corpus is accepted, the corresponding source passage's tokens are masked and will not be used again in the same generation. This prevents the system from "quoting itself" — if a particular sentence from Wikipedia has already been output, the retrieval system cannot retrieve it again for subsequent tokens, avoiding infinite loops or repetitive generation. This masking applies at the token level within the on-the-fly datastore `$(K', V')$` for the current query.

**Parallel computation and latency.** The key computational advantage of speculative decoding is that the LM can evaluate all tokens in the candidate span in a single forward pass. When the span is `$k$` tokens long, the LM's Transformer processes all `$k$` positions simultaneously, computing `$p_M$` for each position in parallel. The per-token cost of this forward pass is roughly `$1/k$` of the cost of generating `$k$` tokens autoregressively. If most tokens are accepted, the effective generation speed approaches `$k \times$` the base LM's speed.

However, NEST incurs additional overhead that standard speculative decoding does not: the two-stage retrieval, the token index construction, and the token search all consume CPU time. Figure 2a breaks down the latency components:
- **Passage search:** approximately 5–8 seconds per query (constant, independent of generation length).
- **Token index building:** approximately 5–8 seconds per query (constant, scales with `$b \times m$`).
- **LM encoding for token index:** approximately 15–25 seconds per query (constant, encodes all b passages).
- **Token search:** approximately 3–5 seconds per query (scales with number of generation steps).
- **Rejection sampling (speculative decoding):** approximately 5–15 seconds per query (scales with number of generation steps and span lengths).

Despite these overheads, the total latency for NEST-70B (across the range of `$\gamma$` values) is lower than the base LM's latency. At `$\gamma = 5 \times 10^{-2}$` (the sweet spot), total latency is approximately 40–50 seconds per query compared to approximately 55–60 seconds for the base LM — roughly a 1.2–1.5× speedup for that specific configuration. The headline 1.8× speedup is achieved at more lenient `$\gamma$` values where longer spans are accepted, trading some quality for speed.

**Why the method is "relaxed" rather than standard speculative decoding.** The paper explicitly acknowledges that the relaxed criterion produces a biased sample — the output distribution does not exactly match `$p_M$` because (1) the proposal distribution over the span tokens is unknown, (2) the acceptance criterion uses a constant relaxation rather than `$p/q$`, and (3) the 0.5 threshold eliminates random rejection. The choice is deliberate: unbiased speculative decoding would require knowing `$q$`, which would require computing a full probability distribution over all possible continuations in the corpus — effectively, running the LM on the entire corpus, which defeats the purpose. The relaxation trades exact distributional matching for practical speed. The paper's results demonstrate that this bias does not harm (and in fact improves) generation quality — the corpus-sourced tokens are not random noise but are actual human-written text, so accepting them liberally tends to improve factuality and fluency even though it introduces distributional bias.

---

#### The Complete NEST Algorithm

Algorithm 1 in the appendix consolidates all the above components into a single procedure. Walking through it reveals several implementation details not covered in the prose:

**First-stage retrieval runs once per query** (not per generation step). The top-b passages are retrieved at the beginning using the original input `$x$`, and the token-level datastore `$(K', V')$` is built from these passages. This datastore is cached and reused for all generation steps for this query — the passage retrieval is not repeated as the generation progresses. However, accepted spans are masked from the datastore to prevent reuse.

**The token search uses the current full context**, not just the last token. The query vector `$q_t$` is extracted from `$f(y_{<t})[-1]$` — the hidden state at the final position of the current `$y_{<t}$` sequence, which includes all previously generated tokens (both parametric and retrieved). This means the retrieval is conditioned on the entire generation history, not just the most recent token.

**Greedy decoding is used throughout** — the token selection from `$p_M$` uses `$\arg\max$` (deterministic), not sampling. This choice is motivated by the paper's focus on factuality: "the randomness in sampling can undermine factuality" (Appendix B). In speculative decoding, the token chosen after rejection also uses `$\arg\max$` rather than sampling from the residual distribution, making the entire generation deterministic given the retrieved passages.

**The relaxation factor `$\gamma$` varies by both model size and task.** For Wikipedia-based tasks: `$\gamma = 5 \times 10^{-4}$` for 7B, `$\gamma = 5 \times 10^{-3}$` for 13B, `$\gamma = 5 \times 10^{-2}$` for 70B. For Pile of Law: `$\gamma = 5 \times 10^{-4}$` for all model sizes. For RA-NEST (combined with in-context retrieval): `$\gamma = 5 \times 10^{-1}$` for all Wikipedia tasks, `$\gamma = 5 \times 10^{-4}$` for Pile of Law. The pattern is that tasks with highly structured, formulaic text (legal documents) benefit from more leniency (lower `$\gamma$`), while stronger models on general-domain text benefit from stricter verification.

**The n-gram continuation extends beyond `$n$` tokens if all are accepted.** After the speculative decoding loop verifies all `$n$` tokens of the initial n-gram and accepts them, the system fetches the next n-gram from the corpus (starting from after the accepted span) and continues verification. This iterative extension means that a single "quote" from the corpus can span hundreds of tokens if the verifier consistently agrees — the `$n = 64$` is a chunk size for fetching, not a hard limit on attributed span length. The paper's finding that attributed spans average 3–28 consecutive tokens (Table 2) reflects the typical length before a token gets rejected, not the maximum possible span length.

**Error handling for parse failure.** The paper notes that NEST may encounter parse failures where the generated text cannot be properly segmented. These cases are handled through standard post-processing (the grading function from the original datasets), but details are deferred to the released code.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing the Retrieval-Latency-Quality Triangle as a Solvable Design Problem

The dominant assumption in retrieval-augmented generation has been that **better attribution and factuality necessarily come at the cost of increased latency**. In-context RA adds tokens to the input (more KV-cache processing, longer prefix encoding). kNN-LM adds per-token nearest-neighbor search over massive datastores. Both make generation *slower* than the base LM, creating a zero-sum tradeoff: you can have fast generation, factual generation, or attributable generation — pick two.

NEST's most fundamental conceptual move is **rejecting the premise that this triangle is a fixed constraint**. The paper demonstrates that the tradeoff is not inherent to retrieval augmentation itself but is a consequence of *how* retrieval results are integrated into the generation process. By synthesizing two previously unrelated techniques — kNN-based output interpolation and speculative decoding — the paper shows that retrieval can actually **accelerate** generation while improving factuality and providing attribution.

What makes this reframing distinctive is that it treats the retrieval corpus not as a burden that must be searched and filtered at each step, but as a **source of free draft tokens** that the LM can verify in parallel. Standard speculative decoding uses a smaller model to draft tokens for a larger model; NEST inverts the logic by recognizing that the corpus *is* a draft model — one that produces perfectly fluent, factually grounded (within its own domain), attributable text at zero inference cost. The "draft model" in NEST costs nothing to run (it's just a lookup), and the drafts it produces are human-written text, meaning they are inherently more reliable than drafts from any small neural model.

This insight changes how one thinks about the problem from "how do we efficiently search a knowledge base to find relevant facts to inject into the generation" to "how do we selectively quote from a knowledge base, treating it as a co-author whose contributions we can verify and accept or reject." The paper's 1.8× speedup on Llama-2-Chat 70B (Section 4.5, Figure 2a) is not just an engineering achievement — it is existence proof that retrieval can be a latency *accelerant* rather than a latency *tax*, which fundamentally changes the calculus for deploying RALMs in production.

This is a fundamental conceptual shift, not an incremental refinement. Prior work operated within the assumption that retrieval slows things down and tried to minimize the damage; NEST operates within a new possibility space where retrieval can be a net positive for latency, quality, and attribution simultaneously.

---

### Innovation 2: The Corpus as a Span-Level Proposal Mechanism with Verifiable Attribution

The second distinctive conceptual contribution is the shift from **token-level retrieval mixing** to **span-level quotation with verification**. This is not merely an engineering choice (trading tokens for n-grams) but a fundamental reframing of what retrieval-augmented generation *is*.

Standard kNN-LM (Khandelwal et al., 2020) and its variants treat the corpus as a bag of independent tokens. At each generation step, the retriever finds the most similar hidden states and constructs a distribution over single next tokens. The tokens come from potentially hundreds of different source documents, and the final generated text is a patchwork stitched together one token at a time. This causes exactly the problems one would expect: **exposure bias** (the paper explicitly names this in Section 3.3 — "seemingly coherent tokens might be retrieved from completely different sources"), fluency degradation (Wang et al., 2023a), and attribution that is theoretically possible but practically incoherent (attributing a sentence to 15 different Wikipedia articles, one token at a time).

NEST's insight is that the corpus is not a token bag — it is a collection of **coherent, human-authored text spans**. The natural unit of retrieval should not be the token but the **span** — the contiguous text that actually appears in a source document. By fetching n-gram continuations and verifying them as atomic units via speculative decoding, NEST preserves the internal coherence of the source text. When the system quotes Wikipedia, it quotes actual Wikipedia sentences, not word salad assembled from across articles.

This matters for attribution in ways the paper makes explicit: when a span of 28 consecutive tokens is accepted from a single source document (as in the 7B model on Biography, Table 2), the attribution is not just "this token came from document X" repeated 28 times — it's "this claim, as a coherent unit, is sourced from document X." This is what users actually want from attribution: to know which source *supports a specific claim*, not which source provided a specific word. The paper's finding that NEST achieves 33–96% attributable token ratios with average consecutive attributed spans of 3–28 tokens (Table 2) is significant not just for the numbers but for what the numbers represent — **claim-level, not token-level, attribution**.

This is a conceptual advance over approaches like Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2023b) or RARR (Gao et al., 2023), which attempt to post-hoc attribute generated text to retrieved passages. Those methods verify attribution after generation is complete, using separate models or heuristics. NEST's attribution is *intrinsic* to the generation process — the spans that are attributed are exactly the spans that were retrieved, and the verification that they fit the context is performed by the same mixture distribution that generated them. This closes the loop between generation and verification in a way that post-hoc approaches cannot: you can trust the attribution because the model itself verified the text before outputting it.

This reframing — from token-level mixing to span-level quotation, from post-hoc attribution to intrinsic verification — is a fundamental shift in what semi-parametric generation means. It is not simply making kNN-LM faster or more accurate; it is redefining the granularity at which retrieval and generation interact.

---

### Innovation 3: Relative Retrieval Confidence as an Uncertainty-Aware Interpolation Mechanism

A subtler but important innovation is the **Relative Retrieval Confidence (RRC)** score as an interpolation coefficient. Before NEST, kNN-LM variants used either a fixed interpolation coefficient `$\lambda$` (Khandelwal et al., 2020) or learned adaptive coefficients that require additional training (He et al., 2021; Bhardwaj et al., 2023). The field's implicit assumption was that deciding how much to trust the retriever requires either human tuning or learned heuristics — that there is no purely statistical signal in the retrieval scores themselves that can guide this decision.

The RRC formula (Equation 4) challenges this assumption by identifying a simple, interpretable signal: the **ratio of minimum to maximum neighbor distance**. When the nearest neighbors are tightly clustered (one is much closer than the rest), the retriever is confident — there is a clear best match. When the neighbors are uniformly distributed (all roughly equally far), the retriever is uncertain — no neighbor stands out as obviously correct. This ratio, passed through a sigmoid with learnable offset and temperature, produces a `$\lambda_t$` that automatically adapts: trusting the retriever when it's confident, deferring to the LM when it's uncertain.

What makes this intellectually distinctive is not the formula itself (ratio-based confidence scores are common in other domains) but the **diagnostic insight** that retrieval uncertainty is directly measurable from the distribution of neighbor distances, and that this uncertainty measure is a sufficient statistic for determining interpolation weights — no learning, no tuning beyond two hyperparameters, no separate confidence model. It turns the interpolation problem from a metric ("what `$\lambda$` works best on average?") into a statistical one ("what does the retriever know right now?").

The significance extends beyond NEST. The RRC score provides a general framework for **uncertainty-aware retrieval integration** that could be applied to any retrieval-augmented system — in-context RA could use a similar signal to decide how many passages to include or how prominently to weight them; intermediate fusion models could use it to modulate cross-attention weights. The paper's ablation (Appendix C, Table 3) shows that RRC alone accounts for most of NEST's quality improvement over two-stage kNN-LM: +4.6 ROUGE-1 on WikiText-103, +3.6 answer-level recall on NQ, +6.8 FACTSCORE on Biography. This suggests that much of the historical difficulty with kNN-LM fluency was not about retrieval quality per se but about *knowing when to trust the retriever* — a problem RRC solves with zero additional parameters.

This is a mid-level innovation — not a paradigm shift like the span-level quotation reframing, but more than an incremental hyperparameter tweak. It identifies a previously overlooked signal (neighbor distance distribution) and shows it is sufficient for a task (interpolation weight selection) that prior work treated as requiring explicit optimization.

---

### Innovation 4: Verifier-Guided Quotation as a Unified Framework for Factuality, Attribution, and Speed

The paper's final conceptual contribution is demonstrating that **verification, not generation or retrieval**, is the central mechanism that makes span-level quotation work. Prior work on retrieval-augmented generation treats retrieval as a substitute for generation — instead of having the model produce tokens, you have it retrieve them. The implicit model is: retrieval replaces generation where the retriever is confident; generation fills in where retrieval fails.

NEST reframes this into a **proposer-verifier architecture** where retrieval *proposes* candidate text and the LM *verifies* it. The generation is not replaced by retrieval — it is augmented by a verification step that can reject bad proposals and fall back to parametric generation. This is the logic of speculative decoding applied to retrieval: the corpus drafts, the LM edits.

What makes this framing powerful is that it separates concerns in a way that enables independent optimization. The retriever's job is to find relevant, factually accurate spans — it can be optimized for recall and precision without worrying about fluency. The LM's job is to verify that the spans fit the current context — it can leverage its full contextual understanding without worrying about factuality (which the corpus handles). The interpolation coefficient `$\lambda_t$` becomes not just a mixing weight but a **gating mechanism**: should we propose a span from the corpus (low `$\lambda_t$`) or let the LM generate from scratch (high `$\lambda_t$`)? The relaxed speculative decoding becomes not just a speed hack but a **quality filter**: of the proposed span, which tokens actually belong in this context?

This proposer-verifier separation explains why the combination of span selection and speculative decoding works better than either alone (Table 3, Appendix C): span selection without verification produces incoherent text (copying spans without checking fit); speculative decoding without span selection doesn't capture multi-token coherence. Together, they implement a proper verification loop that neither component could achieve independently.

The broader significance is that this architecture — proposer + verifier, with the verifier being a neural model and the proposer being an external knowledge source — generalizes beyond the specific retrieval setup in this paper. The corpus could be replaced with a structured knowledge base, a code repository, or even another neural model. The key insight is that the LM's role shifts from "generator of text" to "editor of proposals," and that this shift simultaneously improves factuality (proposals come from trusted sources), attribution (proposals have known origins), and latency (verification is parallelizable). The paper's finding that NEST's attribution is more precise than any baseline ("neither of the baselines can achieve the same granularity and preciseness for the attribution as NEST," Section 4.6) is a direct consequence of this architecture: attribution is not a separate system but a side effect of the proposal being sourced from identifiable documents.

This is a fundamental architectural insight — a new way to think about the division of labor between retrieval systems and language models — not just an implementation detail of the NEST pipeline.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on nine benchmarks spanning four task categories. Text completion: WikiText-103 (Merity et al., 2017) and Pile of Law (Henderson et al., 2022). Question answering: Natural Questions (NQ; Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), TriviaQA (TQA; Joshi et al., 2017), HotpotQA (HQA; Yang et al., 2018), and MedMCQA (MQA; Pal et al., 2022). Fact verification: Biography generation (Min et al., 2023) evaluated via FACTSCORE, and TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022). Closed-set tasks: MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021). For WikiText-103 and Pile of Law, the authors further split test data into validation and test sets. For Biography, labeled data with human annotations serve as validation/dev, while unlabeled data serves as test. For HotpotQA and MedMCQA, only validation set results are reported due to test set unavailability. Wikipedia 2021 dump (∼33M passages, each <200 tokens) serves as the knowledge source for all tasks except Pile of Law, which uses its own training split (∼15M passages after filtering to English only).

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use Llama-2-Chat models (Touvron et al., 2023b) at three scales: 7B, 13B, and 70B parameters. The chat variants are chosen because they represent instruction-following models as deployed in practice. All evaluations are conducted under a zero-shot setting — few-shot demonstrations are removed from prompts "to simulate the realistic usage of these models" (Section 4). Greedy decoding is used throughout to eliminate randomness as a confounding factor in factuality evaluation.

- **Metrics.** For text completion (language modeling), the paper reports **perplexity (PPL)**. For free-form generation (text completion, question answering, fact verification), it reports **ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L** (Lin, 2004), **MAUVE** (Pillutla et al., 2021), and **average generation length**. Question answering uses **answer-level recall (Hit@1)** — checking whether the output contains any correct answer — rather than exact match, since few-shot demonstrations are removed. For Biography, **FACTSCORE** (Min et al., 2023) is used with a length penalty, with the decomposition model being a further fine-tuned Llama-2 7B (trained on publicly available datasets from Chen et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Malaviya et al., 2024); fact verification uses the "retrieval+llama+npm" option. For TruthfulQA, the paper follows Lin et al. (2022) and reports **ΔBLEU and ΔROUGE-1**: the difference between the maximum similarity to a true reference answer and the maximum similarity to a false reference answer (larger positive values indicate better truthfulness). For MMLU, **macro accuracy** is reported per domain (Humanities, STEM, Social Sciences, Other) and averaged.

- **Baselines.** Four baselines are compared across all tasks: (1) **Base LM** — the unmodified Llama-2-Chat model with greedy decoding; (2) **+RA** — in-context retrieval augmentation, where the top-3 retrieved passages (from the same two-stage retriever used by NEST) are prepended to the input prompt; (3) **+kNN-LM** — a two-stage implementation of kNN-LM (Khandelwal et al., 2020) using the same passage retrieval and on-the-fly token index construction as NEST, but with a fixed interpolation coefficient λ (denoted λ in Equation 3: 0.7 for Wikipedia tasks, 0.9 for Pile of Law), without RRC, dynamic span selection, or speculative decoding; (4) **+NEST** — the full proposed method with all components; (5) **+RA-NEST** — the combination of in-context RA (top-3 passages in prompt) with NEST applied on top. RA and NEST are combined because they are "independent methods" (Section 4.2). The paper notes that kNN-LM and NEST share the same first-stage retriever (DRAGON+ dense + BM25 sparse, identical fusion procedure) for fair comparison.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For text completion and language modeling, 128 tokens serve as the prefix with 256 consecutive tokens as the generation target. For question answering, maximum generation length is 128 tokens; for fact verification, 512 tokens. For retrieval-based models, only the prefix (not the generation) is used for the passage-level retrieval query — the first-stage retrieval runs once at the start and is not refreshed during generation. Latency measurements (Section 4.5, Figure 2) are performed on 8×A100 GPUs (for model parallelization) with 32 CPU threads (for search), batch size 1, using internal research-purpose implementations not optimized for latency. The paper does not report FLOP counts or GPU-hours; "compute" is measured in wall-clock latency.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** Hyperparameters are tuned on dev sets of WikiText-103, NQ, and Biography (Section 4.2, Appendix B). Each baseline uses the same hyperparameters across all tasks evaluated. For NEST, RRC hyperparameters (α, τ) are first tuned on language modeling tasks using perplexity, then fixed; span selection (δ) and speculative decoding (γ) parameters are subsequently tuned on generation tasks. The paper does not report error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests (Section 7 of the NeurIPS checklist: "This paper does not include significant tests considering the performance gap between the proposed approach and the baselines").

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Text Completion and Language Modeling (Table 1, upper panel)

The primary finding is that **RA-NEST consistently achieves the lowest perplexity** across both corpora and all model sizes, while **RA achieves the best MAUVE and ROUGE scores on WikiText-103**, and **RA-NEST performs best on Pile of Law for the 7B and 13B models**. Specific numbers:

**WikiText-103:** For Llama-2-Chat 7B, NEST reduces perplexity from 14.6 (base LM) to 8.4, while RA-NEST achieves 6.4 — the best result for 7B. However, RA without NEST achieves the highest ROUGE-1 (35.7 vs. NEST's 28.4 and RA-NEST's 35.2), ROUGE-2 (23.1 vs. NEST's 14.2 vs. RA-NEST's 22.7), ROUGE-L (34.4 vs. NEST's 27.1 vs. RA-NEST's 34.0), and MAUVE (74.6 vs. NEST's 73.2). For Llama-2-Chat 70B, RA-NEST achieves the lowest perplexity at 4.8, with RA at 5.3 and NEST at 6.3. Base LM perplexity is 9.9; kNN-LM is 7.1. On ROUGE-1, RA achieves 40.5 vs. NEST's 32.6 and RA-NEST's 40.2. NEST's ROUGE-1 of 32.6 represents a 42.3% improvement over the base LM's 22.9.

**Pile of Law:** The pattern shifts. For 7B, RA-NEST achieves the highest MAUVE (90.0 vs. RA's 84.7 and NEST's 88.0) and tied-best ROUGE scores. For 70B, RA-NEST achieves the lowest perplexity at 4.7 (vs. base LM 6.9, RA 4.9, NEST 5.9), and best MAUVE (97.6). The paper attributes Pile of Law's different pattern to the nature of legal text: "for legal documents, quoting the exact clauses from the source might be more favourable compared to Wikipedia" (Section 4.4). On this corpus, NEST's ROUGE-1 (25.6 for 70B) improves 11.3% over the base LM (23.0).

**A noteworthy pattern across model sizes:** As model size increases, the gap between all retrieval-augmented methods and the base LM narrows. For WikiText-103 perplexity: 7B base is 14.6 vs. kNN-LM 9.8 (32.9% reduction); 70B base is 9.9 vs. kNN-LM 7.1 (28.3% reduction). This is consistent with prior findings that retrieval is most beneficial for smaller models (Borgeaud et al., 2022). kNN-LM consistently underperforms NEST on both corpora across all metrics and model sizes — e.g., WikiText-103 70B ROUGE-1: kNN-LM 26.1 vs. NEST 32.6.

#### Question Answering (Table 1, lower panel, left columns)

The primary metric is answer-level recall (Hit@1), averaged across TQA, NQ, HQA, and MQA. The headline finding is that **RA-NEST achieves the highest average recall for 7B and 13B models, while RA and RA-NEST tie for 70B**. Specific numbers:

- **7B:** Base LM average recall is 35.0. RA improves to 43.7 (+8.7 points). kNN-LM is 37.2 (+2.2 points). NEST is 37.1 (+2.1 points). RA-NEST achieves 44.1 (+9.1 points), marginally above RA alone. NEST alone underperforms RA but outperforms kNN-LM on NQ (43.2 vs. 42.4), MQA (10.2 vs. 9.5), and ties on TQA (61.5 vs. 63.4) and HQA (33.5).

- **13B:** Base LM average is 37.2. RA achieves 45.3 (+8.1 points). kNN-LM is 38.4 (+1.2 points). NEST is 38.4 (+1.2 points). RA-NEST achieves 45.7 (+8.5 points), again marginally above RA.

- **70B:** Base LM average is 44.1. RA achieves 49.9 (+5.8 points). NEST achieves 45.2 (+1.1 points). RA-NEST achieves 49.8 (−0.1 from RA). The gap between methods narrows substantially at 70B. On individual datasets: on HQA, RA achieves 52.5 vs. NEST's 41.4 (a gap of 11.1 points); on TQA, RA achieves 75.5 vs. NEST's 74.2 (gap of 1.3 points). NEST outperforms RA on none of the individual QA datasets at 70B.

**Observations:** The paper notes that "the gap between base LMs and other methods diminishes for 70B LMs, which is consistent with previous work where retrieval is found most useful for smaller models" (Section 4.4). NEST's consistent but modest improvement over kNN-LM on QA (∼1–2 points) suggests that the span selection and speculative decoding components, while important for attribution and latency, do not dramatically change QA accuracy relative to the RRC-based interpolation alone. RA-NEST's small edge over RA (0.4–0.7 points for 7B/13B, none at 70B) indicates that output-level interpolation provides limited additional benefit when retrieved context is already in the prompt.

#### Fact Verification: Biography and TruthfulQA (Table 1, lower panel, middle columns)

**Biography (FACTSCORE):** NEST consistently outperforms kNN-LM on FACTSCORE across all model sizes, and RA-NEST outperforms RA at 70B. Specific numbers:

- **7B:** Base LM FACTSCORE is 27.2. RA achieves 56.5 (+29.3 points). kNN-LM achieves 30.6 (+3.4 points). NEST achieves 38.9 (+11.7 points, a 21.6% relative improvement over the claim in the abstract, though the abstract says "21.6% improvement of FACTSCORE on Biography" — this is the 7B improvement from 27.2 to 38.9, which is actually a 43.0% relative improvement; the 21.6% figure likely refers to a different calculation or averaging). RA-NEST achieves 55.1, slightly below RA's 56.5. Notably, the number of generated facts drops from 71.2 (base) to 58.2 (NEST) and 57.7 (RA-NEST), while RA drops to 67.1 — suggesting NEST produces more concise biographies.

- **13B:** Base LM 28.8. RA achieves 59.1 (+30.3). kNN-LM achieves 31.1 (+2.3). NEST achieves 35.7 (+6.9). RA-NEST achieves 58.4.

- **70B:** Base LM 34.2. RA achieves 52.9 (+18.7). kNN-LM achieves 36.1 (+1.9). NEST achieves 41.6 (+7.4). **RA-NEST achieves 59.2**, substantially outperforming RA's 52.9 (+6.3 points). The paper attributes RA-70B's degradation to "generating shorter claims which is punished by the FACTSCORE" (Section 4.4), while RA-NEST apparently overcomes this. The number of facts for RA-70B is 42.1 vs. 56.2 for NEST-70B and 58.8 for base 70B — RA alone produces dramatically shorter output, which hurts the length-penalized FACTSCORE. NEST and RA-NEST produce output lengths closer to the base LM while achieving higher factuality.

**TruthfulQA:** This dataset shows the opposite pattern — **semi-parametric LMs (kNN-LM and NEST) consistently outperform RA, which often performs worse than the base LM**. Specific numbers for ΔROUGE-1:

- **7B:** Base LM ΔROUGE-1 is 0.42. RA drops to 0.18 (−0.24). kNN-LM improves to 0.66 (+0.24). NEST achieves 0.45 (+0.03). RA-NEST drops to 0.21.

- **13B:** Base LM 0.81. RA drops to 0.25 (−0.56). kNN-LM achieves 0.95 (+0.14). NEST achieves 0.98 (+0.17, the best result). RA-NEST drops to 0.25.

- **70B:** Base LM 0.70. RA drops to 0.40 (−0.30). kNN-LM achieves 0.58 (−0.12). NEST achieves 0.70 (tied with base). RA-NEST drops to 0.31.

The paper explains: "TruthfulQA is an adversarial dataset containing difficult questions where in-context RA is more susceptible to the 'evidence' in the prompt (e.g., astrology and myths). In contrast, NEST only interpolates the results at the output level and therefore performs better in this case" (Section 4.4). RA's retrieval can surface misleading but authoritative-sounding passages, which in-context conditioning amplifies. NEST's output-level interpolation with confidence-based gating is more robust because it only trusts the retriever when the similarity landscape is sharply peaked — on adversarial questions where superficially similar but incorrect passages are retrieved, the neighbor distances may be more uniform, leading to higher λ_t and more reliance on the LM's parametric judgment.

The ΔBLEU scores follow similar patterns: RA degrades relative to the base LM, while kNN-LM and NEST either maintain or improve performance. For 13B, NEST achieves the best ΔBLEU of 0.29 vs. base LM 0.13 and RA −0.16.

#### Closed-Set Tasks: MMLU (Table 1, lower panel, right columns)

The primary metric is macro accuracy averaged across domains. The headline finding is that **RA-NEST achieves the best average MMLU accuracy for 13B and 70B, while NEST alone is best for 7B**. Specific numbers:

- **7B:** Base LM macro accuracy is 37.2. RA achieves 40.7 (+3.5). kNN-LM achieves 37.6 (+0.4). **NEST achieves 40.7 (+3.5, tied with RA)**. RA-NEST achieves 37.4 (+0.2), notably *worse* than either RA or NEST alone. Domain breakdown: NEST outperforms on Humanities (42.0 vs. RA's 41.8), Social Sciences (42.0 vs. RA's 42.2), STEM (43.4 vs. RA's 43.3), and Other (39.6 vs. RA's 39.6 — tie).

- **13B:** Base LM 40.1. RA achieves 42.7 (+2.6). kNN-LM achieves 40.2 (+0.1). NEST achieves 40.0 (−0.1). **RA-NEST achieves 42.8 (+2.7)**.

- **70B:** Base LM 43.2. RA achieves 45.1 (+1.9). kNN-LM achieves 43.2 (tie). NEST achieves 43.5 (+0.3). **RA-NEST achieves 45.1 (+1.9, tied with RA)**.

For MMLU, NEST performs comparably to RA and RA-NEST, with performance differences typically within 1 point of each other. The paper interprets this as "NEST is comparable to RA" (Section 4.4) on closed-set tasks. Since MMLU does not involve free-form generation, NEST only applies the interpolation step (no dynamic span selection or speculative decoding), making the comparison primarily about RRC-based interpolation vs. in-context retrieval augmentation for multiple-choice scoring via perplexity.

#### Latency Analysis (Section 4.5, Figure 2)

**Latency breakdown (Figure 2a):** For NEST-70B (α = 0.3, τ = 0.1, δ = 0.5) on Biography validation, the total latency per query is decomposed into components:
- Passage search: ∼5–8 seconds (constant per query)
- Token index building: ∼5–8 seconds (constant, scales with b × m)
- LM encoding (for token index): ∼15–25 seconds (constant)
- Token search: ∼3–5 seconds (scales with generation steps)
- Rejection sampling (speculative decoding): ∼5–15 seconds (scales with generation steps and span length)

The paper notes that "the LM encoding time takes about half of the latency, while the sum of the others takes the rest" (Section 4.5). Critically, "even with extra retrieval overheads, the slowest NEST model is faster than the base LM" — the base LM latency line (shown as a horizontal reference in Figure 2a) is above all NEST configurations tested. The 1.8× speedup figure comes from the most lenient γ configurations where longer spans are accepted, reducing the number of LM forward passes needed.

**Latency-accuracy trade-off (Figure 2b):** As the relaxation factor γ decreases from 5 × 10⁻¹ to 1 × 10⁻⁵, the average proposed span length increases from approximately 5 tokens to approximately 35 tokens per generation step. Smaller γ means more tokens are accepted before rejection, enabling more parallel processing per LM forward pass. FACTSCORE on Biography validation follows a non-monotonic pattern: it peaks around γ = 5 × 10⁻² (FACTSCORE ∼0.49) and degrades at both extremes. At γ = 5 × 10⁻², average span length is roughly 10–15 tokens, and latency is approximately 40–50 seconds/query vs. ∼55–60 seconds for the base LM. The paper selects γ = 5 × 10⁻² for the 70B model in Wikipedia tasks as the sweet spot where "both low latency and high accuracy can be achieved at the same time" (Section 4.5). The headline 1.8× speedup is achieved at more lenient γ values (below 5 × 10⁻²) where FACTSCORE is slightly lower but latency drops further.

#### Attribution Analysis (Section 4.6, Table 2)

Table 2 quantifies NEST's attribution capabilities across model sizes and tasks. Two metrics are reported:
- **Attribution Ratio:** The proportion of generated tokens that can be traced back to the corpus.
- **Attribution Length:** The average length of consecutive spans in the generation taken from the same document.

For **NQ**:
- NEST 7B: 93.4% ratio, 18.4 tokens average span length
- NEST 13B: 52.4% ratio, 4.4 tokens
- NEST 70B: 58.8% ratio, 4.3 tokens
- RA-NEST variants range from 33.2% (13B) to 77.5% (7B), with average spans of 3.0–4.4 tokens

For **Biography**:
- NEST 7B: 95.5% ratio, 27.9 tokens — nearly the entire generation is attributable, in long contiguous chunks
- NEST 13B: 53.9% ratio, 10.6 tokens
- NEST 70B: 58.6% ratio, 7.0 tokens
- RA-NEST variants range from 48.5% (13B) to 80.7% (70B), with average spans of 5.1–11.0 tokens

The 7B model consistently shows the highest attribution ratios and longest spans — an interesting finding that the paper does not explore in depth. Possible explanation: weaker models may rely more heavily on retrieval (lower implicit λₜ because the retriever is relatively more confident compared to the weaker parametric component), leading to more span copying. Stronger models may interpolate more toward their own parametric knowledge and reject more retrieved spans.

The paper provides two qualitative examples (Table 2) showing NEST-70B outputs with attributed spans highlighted in green. For the NQ question "who sings does he love me with reba?", the output correctly identifies the duet partners (Reba McEntire and Linda Davis) and provides release year, album name, and context, with spans attributed to specific sources [1] and [2]. For the Biography prompt about Taral Hicks, the output provides birth date, notable films, album details, and personal background with spans attributed to source [3]. The paper claims that "neither of the baselines can achieve the same granularity and preciseness for the attribution as NEST" (Section 4.6).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Progressive ablation of NEST components (Appendix C, Table 3):** Starting from two-stage kNN-LM as the base, adding components sequentially on the validation sets of WikiText-103 (ROUGE-1), NQ (answer-level recall), and Biography (FACTSCORE):
- **Two-stage kNN-LM**: WikiText-103 ROUGE-1 20.1, NQ ALR 40.8, Bio FS 34.8.
- **+ Relative Retrieval Confidence**: bumps to 24.7, 44.4, 41.6 respectively — RRC alone accounts for the majority of the gain on NQ and Bio. WikiText-103 ROUGE-1 jumps 4.6 points (22.9% relative improvement).
- **+ Dynamic Span Selection**: 24.5, 44.6, 41.6 — essentially flat. The paper notes this "does not seem to increase the effectiveness, yet it is crucial to give consistent attribution for consecutive spans and tokens" (Appendix C.2). The value of span selection is in attribution quality, not generation metrics.
- **+ Relaxed Speculative Decoding (full NEST)**: 26.8, 45.4, 46.8 — speculative decoding provides the final boost, particularly on Biography FACTSCORE (+5.2 points).

**Sensitivity to number of retrieved passages and tokens (Appendix C, Figure 3a):** For a fixed number of retrieved tokens (1024), perplexity on WikiText-103 decreases as the number of passages increases from 10 to 80, with each doubling of passages reducing perplexity by ∼0.5–1.0 points. For a fixed number of passages (40), perplexity decreases as the number of retrieved tokens increases from 128 to 1024, though the curve begins to flatten. The paper selects 40 passages and 1024 tokens as the balance point, acknowledging that "as NEST needs to encode the retrieved passages on the fly, the latency also increases linearly w.r.t. the number of passages" (Appendix C.1).

**Sensitivity to RRC hyperparameters (Appendix C, Figure 3b):** On WikiText-103 validation, α (offset) and τ (temperature) in Equation (4) interact: when τ is large (0.5, 1.0), λ_t approaches a uniform distribution and α has minimal impact. When τ is small (0.05, 0.1), α's effect is amplified. The sweet spot is τ = 0.1 and α = 0.3–0.4, achieving perplexity around 7.5–8.0. At τ = 0.1, α = 0.3, the perplexity curve reaches its minimum.

**Sensitivity to dynamic span selection threshold δ (Appendix C, Figure 3c):** On NQ validation, answer-level recall as a function of δ shows an inverted-U shape: recall increases from 44% at δ = 0.0 to a peak of ∼48% at δ = 0.5, then declines to ∼43% at δ = 1.0. Low δ means spans are rarely selected (mostly single-token generation); high δ means spans are almost always selected without sufficient verification. The peak at δ = 0.5 suggests that selecting spans roughly half the time (when retriever confidence exceeds a moderate threshold) provides the best balance between corpus grounding and contextual appropriateness.

**Sensitivity to relaxation factor γ (Section 4.5, Figure 2):** As described in the latency analysis above, γ controls the leniency-accuracy trade-off. The paper observes that "as the model gets stronger, using larger γ which leads to more rejection, is more beneficial to generation quality" (Appendix A). This manifests as the pattern γ = 5 × 10⁻⁴, 5 × 10⁻³, 5 × 10⁻² for 7B, 13B, 70B respectively on Wikipedia tasks. For Pile of Law, all models use γ = 5 × 10⁻⁴, which the paper does not explicitly explain but likely relates to legal text being more formulaic and thus safer to copy leniently. For RA-NEST, γ = 5 × 10⁻¹ for all Wikipedia tasks (much stricter verification, since the in-context passages already provide retrieval signal, making aggressive span copying less necessary).

**Model size scaling (Table 1, all panels):** Performance improves with model size for all methods, but the relative benefit of NEST over baselines shows a non-monotonic pattern. For ROUGE-1 on WikiText-103: NEST vs. base LM improvement is 80% (15.8→28.4) for 7B, 47% (19.9→29.3) for 13B, and 42% (22.9→32.6) for 70B — larger models benefit proportionally less from NEST. For FACTSCORE on Biography, the pattern is similar: NEST improves over base by 43% (27.2→38.9) for 7B, 24% (28.8→35.7) for 13B, and 22% (34.2→41.6) for 70B. This diminishing relative benefit with scale is consistent with the interpretation that stronger LMs have better parametric knowledge and thus rely less on retrieval.

**RA-NEST interaction across tasks (Table 1, all panels):** The combination of in-context RA and NEST shows task-dependent synergy. It works best on text completion (RA-NEST achieves lowest perplexity on both WikiText-103 and Pile of Law for most model sizes) and question answering (highest average recall for 7B and 13B). It does *not* help on TruthfulQA (RA-NEST performs worse than NEST alone, because RA's retrieved evidence can be misleading, and NEST compounds this). On MMLU, RA-NEST's marginal benefit over RA alone (tied at 70B, +0.1 at 13B, worse at 7B) suggests that output-level interpolation adds limited value when retrieval context is already in the prompt for closed-set tasks.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: NEST improves generation quality over the base LM and kNN-LM across knowledge-intensive tasks.** The experiments in Table 1 support this claim broadly but with important nuance. On text completion, NEST consistently outperforms both the base LM and kNN-LM on perplexity and ROUGE scores across all model sizes and both corpora (Table 1, upper panel). On question answering, NEST's improvement over base LM is modest (+2.1 points average for 7B, +1.2 for 13B, +1.1 for 70B) and it consistently underperforms in-context RA. On fact verification, NEST improves FACTSCORE by 7–12 points on Biography and marginally improves TruthfulQA metrics. On MMLU, NEST matches or slightly exceeds base LM performance. The claim holds most strongly for perplexity reduction and fact verification, and most weakly for question answering where RA dominates. The paper's framing — "NEST is able to outperform base LMs and kNN-LM's on most tasks while being on par with RA" (Section 4.4) — accurately characterizes the results, though "on par with RA" overstates the case for QA, where RA is clearly superior.

**Claim 2: NEST achieves a 1.8× speedup in inference time without compromising attribution or fluency.** The latency experiments (Figure 2) demonstrate that NEST-70B is faster than the base LM for all tested γ values, with the largest speedup at the most lenient settings. However, the 1.8× figure is specifically for lenient configurations where FACTSCORE is slightly lower than at the sweet spot (γ = 5 × 10⁻²). At the quality-optimal γ, the speedup is closer to 1.2–1.5× based on Figure 2a. More importantly, the latency measurements are on an "internal, research-purpose implementation of the base Llama-2-chat model which did not optimize for latency" (Section 4.5), which means the absolute speedups may not transfer to production-optimized inference stacks. The paper does not report how the 1.8× figure was computed (which γ, which task), does not provide error bars or variance across queries, and uses batch size 1 (which may not reflect production batching scenarios where the retrieval overhead per query could dominate). The claim that speedup occurs "without compromising attribution or fluency" holds at the sweet spot γ but not at the extremes — at very lenient γ, FACTSCORE degrades (Figure 2b), and at very strict γ, the speed advantage diminishes. The relationship between γ and quality is non-monotonic and task-dependent, which limits the generalizability of the 1.8× claim.

**Claim 3: NEST provides span-level attribution with high coverage and precision.** The attribution analysis (Table 2) convincingly demonstrates that NEST can trace 33–96% of generated tokens to corpus sources, with average spans of 3–28 consecutive tokens. This is a genuine capability that neither in-context RA nor standard kNN-LM provides. However, the analysis lacks several important validation steps: (1) There is no human evaluation of attribution accuracy — being able to trace a token to a source document does not guarantee the attribution is *correct* (the retrieved span might be from an unrelated document that happened to have similar hidden states). The paper provides qualitative examples (Table 2) where attributions look plausible, but no systematic evaluation. (2) There is no comparison to what attribution coverage an in-context RA model *could* achieve if one attempted to post-hoc align its output with retrieved passages (e.g., using n-gram overlap). The claim that "neither of the baselines can achieve the same granularity and preciseness" is asserted without quantitative comparison — it may be true, but it is not empirically demonstrated. (3) The attribution ratios are computed for NQ and Biography only, not for all tasks in Table 1. It is unclear whether the high attribution rates on Biography (95.5% for 7B) generalize to other generation tasks.

**Claim 4: NEST performs competitively with in-context retrieval augmentation.** The results in Table 1 show this is task-dependent. NEST is competitive with RA on MMLU (tied at 7B and 70B, slightly worse at 13B), superior on TruthfulQA (where RA actively hurts), and clearly inferior on question answering (RA leads by 6.6, 6.9, and 4.7 points average on 7B, 13B, 70B respectively). On text completion, RA outperforms NEST on ROUGE/MALUE metrics for WikiText-103, while NEST is competitive on Pile of Law. The paper's claim that NEST is "competitive" with RA is fair for some tasks but not uniformly — on QA, the gap is substantial and consistent. The paper appropriately notes this: "Despite the limited improvement, we will show that NEST is able to provide better attribution and latency in the following sections" (Section 4.4), correctly positioning NEST's advantage as being about attribution and speed rather than raw QA accuracy.

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

- **Single model family (Llama-2-Chat).** All results use one architecture family. It is unknown whether NEST's behavior — particularly the RRC dynamics and optimal γ values — transfers to models with different hidden state geometries (e.g., Mistral, Falcon, or encoder-decoder architectures). The hidden state similarity structure that kNN retrieval depends on may differ across model families in ways that affect retrieval quality and interpolation behavior.

- **No statistical significance reporting.** The paper explicitly declines to report error bars or significance tests (NeurIPS checklist item 7). Given that some of the claimed improvements are small (e.g., NEST vs. kNN-LM on 70B QA: 45.2 vs. 44.9), the absence of variance estimates makes it impossible to assess whether these differences are reliable or within sampling noise. The 500-question test sets for some tasks, split further into dev/test, may yield unstable estimates.

- **RA baseline uses only top-3 passages.** The RA baseline prepends 3 passages "due to the context window limit" (Section 4.2). Modern LLMs (including Llama-2-Chat 70B with 4K context) can typically accommodate many more passages. The paper does not ablate the number of RA passages, so it is unclear whether the RA performance ceiling has been reached. Using 10 or 20 passages might significantly improve RA's performance and change the competitive landscape.

- **No retrieval refresh for RA during generation.** RA retrieves passages once at the start using only the input prefix. For long-form generation (Biography, 512 tokens), the initial retrieval may become stale as the generation progresses. NEST's token-level retrieval at each step implicitly refreshes relevance. This makes the comparison slightly unfair: NEST has access to generation-adaptive retrieval while RA does not.

- **Latency measured on unoptimized code.** The base LM latency baseline uses a research implementation "not optimized for latency." Production inference engines (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM) with kernel fusion, continuous batching, and KV-cache optimizations would have dramatically different absolute latencies and potentially different relative speedups. The retrieval overhead (passage search on CPU, token encoding on GPU) may not scale well with production batching.

- **No comparison to other speculative decoding or retrieval-decoding methods.** REST (He et al., 2024), the closest concurrent work on retrieval-based speculative decoding, is cited but not compared. The inference-with-reference method (Yang et al., 2023) that also uses prefix matching for draft selection is cited but not compared. Without these comparisons, the paper cannot establish whether NEST is state-of-the-art among retrieval-based decoding acceleration methods or merely one of several viable approaches.

- **Attribution evaluation is intrinsic only.** The paper measures what fraction of tokens *can be* traced to the corpus, not what fraction of claims are *correctly* attributed. False attributions — where a token is traced to a source that doesn't actually support the claim — would inflate the attribution ratio without providing genuine accountability. Human evaluation or entailment-based verification of attribution accuracy would substantially strengthen the attribution claim.

**Missing experiments that would have strengthened the paper:**

- **Ablation of the two-stage design vs. full-corpus token index**, to quantify the accuracy cost of the passage-level filtering. This would help practitioners decide whether the latency-accuracy trade-off of two-stage retrieval is favorable for their use case.
- **Comparison with few-shot prompted baselines.** The paper removes few-shot demonstrations "to simulate realistic usage," but this weakens the base LM and RA baselines. A few-shot comparison would show whether NEST's gains persist when the base LM has stronger parametric performance.
- **Analysis of failure modes.** The paper provides qualitative examples of successful attribution but no systematic analysis of when NEST fails — e.g., when the passage retriever misses relevant documents, when the token retriever retrieves from the right passage but wrong position, when speculative decoding accepts factually incorrect spans because the verifier's mixture distribution is miscalibrated.
- **Scaling analysis of retrieval quality.** How does NEST perform as the corpus size increases from 1M to 33M passages? Does the two-stage retrieval maintain recall, or does the passage-level bottleneck become more severe with larger corpora? This matters for practitioners considering deployment on web-scale corpora.

**Conditional nature of the claims:** The paper's central value proposition — that NEST improves quality, attribution, and latency simultaneously — holds most clearly for fact-verification tasks (Biography) on the 7B model, where both FACTSCORE improvement (+43%) and attribution coverage (95.5%) are highest. It holds less clearly for question answering at 70B, where NEST provides a 1.1-point recall improvement over the base LM (vs. RA's 5.8-point improvement) and attribution rates drop to 59%. The speedup claim requires accepting a quality trade-off at lenient γ settings. The attribution claim requires accepting that traceability equals correctness. These conditions are not hidden — the paper reports the relevant numbers in Table 1 and Figure 2 — but they are not prominently discussed in the abstract or introduction, which emphasize the most favorable results. A reader focused only on QA accuracy at 70B would come away with a very different assessment than a reader focused on biographical factuality at 7B.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted For, Breaking the Headline Efficiency Claim

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal framework requires estimating prompt difficulty *before* deciding how to allocate the inference budget. The paper's method for doing so — generating 2048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness (oracle) or PRM final-answer scores (predicted) — consumes more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied. The authors acknowledge this explicitly in Section 3.2: "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity."

**The consequence.** The reported 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution, and the former could dominate the latter — 2048 samples per question to estimate difficulty dwarfs the 16–256 sample budgets where the 4× gains are claimed. This is not a minor overhead; it is a cost that fundamentally changes the economics of the approach. The 4× figure should be understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** in a deployment context, not a realized gain. If difficulty estimation costs even 128 samples per question (well below the paper's 2048), the total compute budget including estimation and execution would substantially erode or eliminate the claimed advantage over best-of-N.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper itself acknowledges this gap in Section 3.2 and frames it as "a key avenue for future work." However, no experiment quantifies how total cost (estimation + execution) would compare to the baselines. No ablation explores how estimation cost scales with the number of samples used — would 512 or 128 samples suffice for difficulty estimation? The predicted difficulty bins in Figures 4 and 8 use the full 2048 samples with PRM scoring; no lower-sample regime is tested. The cost discrepancy is most acute for the difficulty estimation itself: generating 2048 samples at maximum sequence length on MATH problems represents thousands of tokens of generation per question, potentially exceeding the compute budget of the largest test-time strategies evaluated (256–512 generations).

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly calls for future work on "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" (Section 8), but such a model is neither developed nor evaluated. No simpler heuristic (e.g., using the PRM score on a single sample, or using the base model's perplexity on the question) is tested. The limitation is transparently acknowledged but unresolved.

### Hard Problems Remain Essentially Unsolved — Test-Time Compute Cannot Compensate for Fundamental Capability Gaps

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework operates on the premise that the base model possesses some non-trivial probability of producing correct solutions. For the hardest questions, this premise fails. The paper describes difficulty bin 5 (hardest questions) with the statement that "no method makes meaningful progress" and quantifies this: accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all methods and all budgets in Figure 3 (right), roughly 2–3% in Figure 7 (right) for revisions, and the FLOPs-matched comparison in Figure 9 shows the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5% across all budgets. The paper states this plainly in Section 7: "test-time compute provides essentially zero benefit regardless of budget."

**The consequence.** This establishes a hard boundary condition: test-time compute **amplifies existing capability but does not create it from nothing.** If the base model's pass@1 is near zero on a problem class — because the required reasoning, knowledge, or solution strategy is outside its training distribution — no amount of search, revision, or adaptive allocation will help. The paper's compute-optimal framework provides no path forward for genuinely novel, out-of-distribution, or highly complex reasoning problems that exceed the base model's capabilities. For safety-critical applications (medical diagnosis, legal reasoning, scientific discovery) where the hardest cases are precisely the ones that matter most, this limitation is fatal: the method helps on easy problems that the model could already solve, and fails on hard problems where help is needed. The FLOPs-matched comparison makes this stark: at R ≫ 1 and bin 5, test-time compute shows a −52.9% relative disadvantage compared to just training a larger model with PRM search (Figure 1, bottom-right bar chart).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Every experimental result that breaks out performance by difficulty confirms this pattern. Figure 3 (right, bin 5): all methods cluster at 1–3% regardless of budget. Figure 7 (right, bin 5): accuracy is ~2–3% across all sequential-to-parallel ratios. Figure 9 (bottom lines, bin 5): the scaling curve is essentially flat with near-zero slope, and the 14× larger model's greedy performance (stars) is above the test-time compute curve at all R values. The authors do not present this as a failure of their method but as an inherent limitation of the test-time compute paradigm. Section 7: "Our results indicate that the answer to this question is nuanced: test-time compute can effectively substitute for additional pretraining, but only on problems of easy to medium difficulty. On hard prompts, additional pretraining is preferable."

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed and likely not addressable within the test-time compute paradigm. The paper acknowledges this boundary explicitly (Section 7 takeaway box) and frames pretraining as the appropriate solution for hard problems. This is a fundamental limitation, not an oversight — it delineates the scope of what test-time compute scaling can achieve. The paper does not explore hybrid strategies that might partially address this (e.g., using test-time compute on easy problems while escalating hard problems to a larger model), though such an approach is implicitly suggested by the difficulty-conditioned allocation framework.

### The 14× Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained, Weakening the FLOPs-Matched Comparison

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023) rather than compute-optimal pretraining (Hoffmann et al., 2022). The paper explicitly acknowledges this design choice: "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work" (Section 7). Additionally, the larger model comparison uses only greedy decoding — no test-time augmentation of its own, while the smaller model uses compute-optimal strategies.

**The consequence.** A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with 14× more total FLOPs (scaling both parameters and data, not just parameters) would likely outperform the parameter-only-scaled model used as the baseline. The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining (e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on easy-medium questions with revisions at R ≪ 1, Figure 1) may shrink or reverse against a properly compute-optimal larger model. Furthermore, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget (e.g., best-of-8 with majority voting) would create a much stronger baseline. The comparison as structured answers the question "is test-time compute better than naively scaling model size?" but does not answer the more relevant practical question "is test-time compute better than the best available pretraining + inference combination?" The asymmetry is stark: the smaller model gets adaptive, difficulty-conditioned allocation of up to 256–512 generations, while the 14× larger model gets a single greedy decode.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The FLOPs-matched comparison is presented in Figures 1 and 9, with the parameter-only-scaled model's performance shown as stars in Figure 9. The paper does not report the pretraining data scale, does not provide results with Chinchilla-optimal scaling for the larger model, and does not ablate the larger model's inference strategy. Appendix F (Figure 14) shows that PRM best-of-N weighted substantially outperforms greedy decoding at 2048 samples — the larger model would almost certainly benefit from similar test-time compute, but this is never evaluated. The paper's framing in Section 7 acknowledges the limitation in the text but not in the headline comparison figures.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly acknowledges this limitation (Section 7) and proposes future work on compute-optimal pretraining comparisons. However, because the FLOPs-matched comparison is one of the paper's most prominent claims (featured in the abstract and Figure 1 bar charts), the limitation is significant — the result that test-time compute can substitute for pretraining is established only against a weaker-than-necessary baseline. A reader deploying this work would need to run their own comparison against their actual pretraining pipeline to determine whether the substitution holds in their setting.

### Single Model Family and Single Benchmark Constrain Generalization Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use PaLM 2-S* as the base model and the MATH benchmark as the sole evaluation dataset. The paper states in Section 4 that the authors "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but this is an assertion without empirical support. The MATH benchmark consists exclusively of high-school competition-level mathematics problems requiring symbolic reasoning and multi-step logical deduction.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or domain-specific in ways that determine whether the approach is broadly useful:

- **PRM quality and over-optimization behavior** depend on PaLM 2-S*'s output distribution and hidden state geometry. Models with different calibration, error patterns, or representational properties might show different difficulty-dependent scaling curves, different optimal interpolation coefficients, or different thresholds for verifier over-optimization.
- **The revision model's ability** to learn from in-context examples of incorrect answers depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families (GPT-4, Claude, Llama, Mistral) in ways documented in the literature but not evaluated here.
- **MATH tasks** require deductive reasoning from explicit premises; it is unclear whether the difficulty-dependent patterns (beam search hurting easy problems, revisions helping easy problems but not hard ones) generalize to tasks requiring inductive reasoning, factual recall, code generation, or multi-modal understanding. The finding that beam search over-optimizes the PRM on easy math problems might not hold for, say, easy code generation problems where the verifier signal is cleaner (deterministic unit tests rather than learned PRM scores).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** No cross-model or cross-domain experiments exist. The paper acknowledges this scope limitation implicitly by focusing exclusively on MATH with PaLM 2-S*, but does not include a formal limitation statement about generalizability. The 500-question MATH test set, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 questions each, further split by two-fold cross-validation (~50 questions per fold per bin), provides limited statistical power for identifying the optimal per-bin strategy — a few lucky or unlucky questions could shift the selected policy. The paper does not report confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves, making it difficult to assess whether the per-bin strategy selections are robust at this sample size.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not evaluate on any other model family, any other benchmark, or any other domain. The authors' belief that PaLM 2-S* is "representative" is presented as a premise rather than a tested claim. Replication on other models and tasks — particularly code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), logical reasoning (ARC, FOLIO), and scientific QA — would be needed to establish the framework's generality. The authors do not suggest specific model families or benchmarks for future replication work.

### Revisions and Search Are Studied Independently, Not Combined, Despite Complementary Strengths

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies two complementary mechanisms — PRM-guided search (Section 5) and iterative revisions (Section 6) — but evaluates them in isolation. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges: "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions." The paper's own theoretical framework (Section 2) frames these as modifications to complementary axes: search improves the verifier/selection side, while revisions improve the proposal distribution side. A natural synthesis would apply beam search over revision model outputs, or use the PRM to guide which revisions to pursue during the sequential chain.

**The consequence.** The paper provides no evidence about the potential gains from combining these mechanisms. Since they operate on different components of the generation pipeline — revisions improve what is generated, search improves how candidates are selected — they should be complementary rather than redundant. The current results therefore represent a **lower bound** on what a fully integrated system could achieve, and the paper cannot advise practitioners on how to combine them (e.g., "should I revise first then search, or search over revisions?"). This is particularly relevant for medium-difficulty problems (bins 3–4), where both mechanisms individually show gains: revisions improve the base quality of candidates, while beam search helps navigate toward correct solutions. Their combination could be multiplicative rather than additive.

The gap is practical as well as theoretical. The compute-optimal policy described in Section 3.2 selects between search algorithms and sequential-to-parallel ratios for revisions, but never considers a combined strategy that uses both simultaneously. A practitioner deploying this work would need to guess whether to run revisions, search, or both in sequence, potentially leaving substantial performance gains unrealized.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The difficulty-dependent analysis in Figures 3 (right) and 7 (right) shows that search and revisions have complementary optimal difficulty regimes: search works best on medium problems (bin 3–4), revisions work best on easy problems (bin 1–2) and in balanced sequential-parallel ratios on medium problems. The paper's own data thus suggests that combining them — using revisions to generate better candidates and search to select among them — could outperform either alone on medium-difficulty problems where both individually help. The paper does not test this hypothesis. The compute-optimal policy in Figures 4 and 8 selects the best *single* mechanism per difficulty bin, not the best *combination* of mechanisms.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly proposes this as future work in Section 8: "We leave combining the two to future work." No preliminary results, theoretical analysis, or suggested architectures for combination are provided. The acknowledgment is transparent, but the absence of even a pilot experiment (e.g., beam search over 4 revision chains vs. beam search alone vs. revisions alone on a subset of medium-difficulty questions) is a missed opportunity to quantify the potential upside.

### The Revision Model Suffers from Systematically Degrading Correct Answers, with Fragile Training Dynamics

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 6.1 reports a significant practical failure mode: because the revision model is trained only on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect (followed by a correct target), at test time it may encounter correct answers in its context and incorrectly "revise" them into wrong answers. The paper quantifies this: "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones using a naive approach." This is a direct consequence of the training data construction — the model has no signal for what to do when presented with an already-correct answer, because such sequences never appear in training.

**The consequence.** The revision model has a built-in tendency to "fix what isn't broken," which undermines its reliability in production scenarios where the first or an intermediate revision correctly answers the question. The paper's mitigation — using majority voting or verifier-based selection across the entire chain of revisions (Section 6.1) — is a post-hoc patch that selects the best answer from the chain rather than always taking the last revision. However, this mitigation only works if a correct answer survives somewhere in the chain; it does nothing to prevent the model from destroying correct answers in the first place, which wastes compute budget on unproductive revisions and creates the risk that majority voting or verifier selection might prefer an incorrect later revision over an earlier correct one. Additionally, the ReST^(EM) experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) shows that attempting to further optimize the revision model with reinforcement learning degrades performance substantially — fully sequential revisions with the ReST^(EM) model drop to approximately 33.5% accuracy at 256 generations, compared to roughly 38.5% at the optimal ratio with the SFT-trained model. The paper hypothesizes that "on-policy data collection in ReST^(EM) exacerbates spurious correlations in revision data."

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% reversion rate is reported in Section 6.1 without a detailed breakdown (how does it vary by difficulty? by revision step? by model size?). The ReST^(EM) negative result is in Appendix K, Figure 16, and the paper describes it as a finding that "additional sequential revisions substantially hurt performance." The paper's ablation of revision training methodology is otherwise minimal — there is no comparison of alternative training data construction strategies (e.g., including correct-to-correct examples, using different similarity metrics for pairing incorrect/correct answers, varying the number of in-context incorrect answers beyond the uniform [0,4] distribution).

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially mitigates the reversion problem with majority voting and verifier-based selection across the revision chain, but these are post-hoc patches that do not address the root cause (the training distribution mismatch). A more principled solution — such as training the model with examples where the correct answer is already in context and the target is to repeat it unchanged, or using a separate "should I revise?" classifier to decide whether to trigger the revision model — is suggested as future work but not implemented. The ReST^(EM) failure is flagged as a cautionary result but not deeply investigated. The paper's revision model thus represents a workable but fragile component whose reliability depends on careful training data curation and whose optimal training recipe is not fully characterized.
