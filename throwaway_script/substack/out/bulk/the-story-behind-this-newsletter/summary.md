Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (the founder of The Peterman Post, on his own journey from a rough start as a new grad to Staff Engineer at Meta in 3 years, and the mentorship-driven vision behind this newsletter):

**The production-script nightmare**

Ryan’s first job out of school was an experience that left him feeling he “wasn’t growing as an engineer.” He was responsible for maintaining a 10,000-line database script that his manager had written alone, entirely outside of source control. The script was a web of interconnected, complex queries without the safety net of code review, so typos and avoidable bugs were endemic. The development practice was to edit the script directly in production, which caused frequent breakages. Each incident was prolonged and painful because, with no source control, there was no way to quickly revert. As a new grad with zero mentorship on that team, he had no internal model for what growth was supposed to look like or how to pursue it. After eight months, he accepted an offer to join Facebook.

**Three years to Staff (IC3 to IC6)**

His trajectory flipped the moment he landed on a team with what he calls “incredible mentors who taught me industry best practices.” The gap between his first environment and the new one was stark; he credits the guidance he received for compressing his path from new grad (IC3) to Staff Engineer (IC6) into three years—a velocity that surprised even him. The core insight that emerged from that experience is simple: **mentorship made a huge difference in my career trajectory.** He’s explicit that he doesn’t attribute the acceleration to raw talent, but to being shown the right things by people who already knew them.

**Paying it forward through a newsletter**

That gratitude for being pulled up animates the entire project. Ryan started The Peterman Post to “help close the gaps for engineers that don’t have dedicated mentorship at work.” He frames it as a form of scaled mentorship—distilling what he learned during his rapid growth so that engineers without the luck of a great manager or senior teammates can still access the playbook. His voice here is one of obligation rather than branding: someone handed him a ladder, and he sees a responsibility to describe the rungs.

**The format promise: no fluff, five minutes, Friday mornings**

He designed the newsletter with a clear usability thesis. Every post will be stripped of filler and readable in under five minutes—a deliberate constraint meant to honor the reader’s time and attention. He chose Friday mornings for distribution because companies tend to have fewer meetings that day, so engineers might actually have a window to read something career-relevant. The newsletter is free with no paywalls or upsells: “just focus on learning.” He invites direct feedback by replying to the emails, and commits to reading every response—a tight feedback loop that mirrors the code-review culture he learned to value at Meta, applied to writing. His top-line priority is straightforward: **make the content as good as it can be.**

---

Ryan’s own **mentorship made a huge difference** framing isn’t just his origin story—it’s the product thesis. The newsletter is built as a direct response to the mentorship vacuum he experienced in that first job, scaled through a format designed for maximum accessibility (free, short, Friday mornings). His commitment to a tight feedback loop (“I will read every response”) suggests he’s applying the same iterative, review-driven practices his Meta mentors taught him—treating the newsletter itself as a system that can be continuously improved, not a static broadcast.
