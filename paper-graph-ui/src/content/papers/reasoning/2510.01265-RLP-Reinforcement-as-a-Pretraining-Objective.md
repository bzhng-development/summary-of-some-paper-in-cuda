## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **RLP (Reinforcement Learning Pretraining)**, a verifier-free information-gain objective that brings reinforcement learning into the pretraining phase by treating chain-of-thought generation as an exploratory action — the model samples a reasoning trace before predicting each next token and receives reward proportional to how much that trace improves the token's log-likelihood over a no-think EMA baseline. Applied to QWEN3-1.7B-BASE, RLP lifts the overall average across an eight-benchmark math-and-science suite by 19% over the base model and by 17% over a compute-matched continuous pretraining baseline, with gains that survive and compound after identical SFT + RLVR post-training — yielding a final 7–8% relative margin over conventionally trained counterparts. The method scales effectively to a 12B hybrid Mamba-Transformer architecture (NEMOTRON-NANO-12B-V2), where it achieves a 43% relative improvement (42.81% → 61.32% overall average) using only 0.125% of the full pretraining data, establishing that reinforcement-driven reasoning can be instilled during pretraining and that the resulting capabilities transfer across architectures, datasets, and downstream alignment — with the strongest gains concentrated in scientific reasoning and the method remaining effective even when applied to general-purpose web corpora rather than curated reasoning datasets.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Standard Pretraining Doesn't Incentivize Thinking

The dominant paradigm for training large language models follows a well-established pipeline: pretrain with next-token prediction loss on vast amounts of data, then apply supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and finally use reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) as a post-training step to induce complex reasoning behaviors (Guo et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). This pipeline has produced impressive reasoning models, but it embeds a fundamental assumption that the paper challenges: **that reasoning should only be explicitly incentivized at the very end of training, after the model has already learned from trillions of tokens using a purely likelihood-based objective.**

The paper argues this assumption may be suboptimal for a simple reason. Next-token prediction trains a model to maximize `log p(xt | x<t)` — the probability of each token given its prefix. This objective is purely associative: it encourages the model to learn statistical patterns that predict what comes next, but it does not explicitly reward the model for engaging in the kind of multi-step, deliberative reasoning that precedes accurate prediction in complex domains. As the authors put it in Section 1:

> "human comprehension is not a linear token-by-token process, but rather a parallel integration of input with prior knowledge"

Current pretraining, by contrast, is precisely a linear token-by-token process — the model processes the prefix and predicts the next token without any mechanism for generating intermediate reasoning steps, evaluating their quality, or learning which kinds of thoughts improve prediction. The result is that reasoning capabilities must be retrofitted during post-training, often requiring expensive curated datasets with ground-truth solution traces (SFT) or task-specific verifiers that can check whether a final answer is correct (RLVR). Both approaches are bottlenecks: SFT requires high-quality reasoning demonstrations that are costly to produce at scale, and RLVR requires verifiable reward signals that only exist for problems with checkable answers (math, code), making them fundamentally inapplicable to the open-ended text that comprises the vast majority of pretraining data.

The paper's core premise, stated directly in the introduction, is that this pipeline gets the order wrong:

> "RLP reframes reinforcement learning for reasoning as a pretraining objective on ordinary text, bridging the gap between next-token prediction and the emergence of useful chain-of-thought reasoning."

In other words: **what if we taught the model to think during pretraining, on the same diverse text it's already learning from, rather than waiting until post-training to bolt reasoning on?**

### Why This Gap Matters: Practical and Theoretical Significance

This problem has both immediate practical implications and deeper theoretical significance for how we think about LLM training.

**Practical significance: the data and verifier bottlenecks.** Current state-of-the-art reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI o1) depend on post-training pipelines that are fundamentally constrained in what data they can use. RLVR requires a verifier — either a ground-truth answer checker (for math) or unit tests (for code) — which means the RL phase can only be applied to problems where correctness is binary and automatically checkable. This excludes the vast majority of natural language: explanations, summaries, arguments, creative writing, or any domain where "correctness" is nuanced or subjective. The result is that RLVR-trained models become specialists in math and code reasoning but may not develop the same depth of deliberative reasoning for general language understanding.

SFT similarly requires curated reasoning traces. Datasets like OpenThoughts (Guha et al., 2025) or Nemotron-Crossthink (Akter et al., 2025) contain high-quality chain-of-thought demonstrations, but producing them at web-scale is infeasible. You cannot write reasoning traces for every paragraph in Common Crawl. This means the reasoning skills learned during post-training are trained on a narrow distribution — predominantly structured math and science problems — and may not transfer robustly to the open-ended text that models encounter in deployment.

RLP addresses both bottlenecks simultaneously. By defining reward as **information gain for next-token prediction** — `log pθ(xt | x<t, ct) - log ¯pϕ(xt | x<t)` — the method produces a verifier-free signal that is computable for any text, in any domain, without requiring answer checkers or curated reasoning traces. This means the model can learn to reason from **the same diverse pretraining corpora** it already uses, potentially developing reasoning capabilities that are broader and more robust than those induced by narrow post-training datasets.

**Theoretical significance: rethinking the role of pretraining.** There is a deeper conceptual question at stake: what should pretraining actually optimize? The standard view is that pretraining builds general-purpose linguistic and world knowledge through maximum likelihood, while post-training handles alignment and specialized skills. But this division assumes that likelihood maximization is sufficient to extract all useful structure from text — that the optimal predictor of the next token doesn't need to think, it just needs to pattern-match.

The paper challenges this assumption, arguing that **next-token prediction alone may leave reasoning capabilities on the table** because it never rewards the model for the internal computational work that precedes accurate prediction in complex contexts. By augmenting the pretraining objective with a reward for information-gain through chain-of-thought, RLP essentially asks: can we make pretraining more efficient at extracting reasoning structure from text by explicitly incentivizing the model to generate and use intermediate thoughts?

This connects to a broader debate about whether reward-based pretraining is necessary for general intelligence (Han et al., 2025). If reasoning is fundamentally about reducing uncertainty through deliberation, then a likelihood-only objective — which never explicitly measures or rewards uncertainty reduction — may be fundamentally insufficient to induce robust reasoning behaviors, regardless of scale.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper positions itself against several lines of prior work, each of which has specific limitations that RLP is designed to address.

**Standard next-token prediction pretraining.** The default approach (Radford et al., 2018; Brown et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2025) optimizes only the likelihood of observed tokens. While this produces models with broad capabilities, the authors argue — and demonstrate empirically (Table 1) — that it does not explicitly incentivize the kind of multi-step reasoning that chain-of-thought requires. A model trained purely with NTP may have the latent capability to reason, but nothing in the objective pushes it to exercise that capability or learn which thoughts are useful. The consequence is that reasoning remains "implicit" and "weak" (Figure 1, top-right table) until post-training explicitly shapes it.

**Post-training RLVR (DeepSeek-R1, Guo et al., 2025).** This approach applies reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards after pretraining and SFT. The model generates chain-of-thought solutions to math or code problems, a verifier checks whether the final answer is correct, and the model is rewarded accordingly. This has produced dramatic improvements in reasoning benchmarks, but has three limitations the paper highlights:

1. **Domain restriction**: RLVR only works where verifiers exist — math, code, some science problems. It cannot be applied to general text.
2. **Late-stage intervention**: RLVR operates on an already-pretrained model. It can shape behavior but cannot fundamentally restructure the knowledge and representations learned during the ~99% of training compute spent on pretraining.
3. **Data inefficiency**: RLVR requires curated problem sets (MATH, GSM8K) which are tiny compared to pretraining corpora. The reasoning skills learned may not generalize beyond the narrow distribution of the training problems.

**Reinforcement Pretraining (RPT, Dong et al., 2025).** This is the most directly comparable prior work, and the paper positions RLP as addressing specific RPT shortcomings. RPT also applies RL during pretraining, but with a different reward structure:

- **Sparse, binary reward**: RPT rewards the model based on whether the predicted next token matches the ground-truth token — a binary correct/incorrect signal. RLP instead uses a continuous information-gain reward that measures *how much* the thought improved prediction, providing richer credit assignment.
- **Token preselection**: RPT uses a separate auxiliary model to identify "high-entropy" tokens where prediction is uncertain, and only applies RL to those positions. This means RPT does not train on full documents — it skips tokens deemed "easy" — and requires maintaining and running a separate filtering model. RLP applies rewards at every position (or, in practice, randomly sampled positions) without auxiliary filtering.
- **Dependence on strong initial checkpoints**: The authors note that "prior RPT demonstrations also depend on distilled checkpoints with strong prior reasoning ability, which clouds whether the method helps base models." RPT was evaluated on models that had already been trained with reasoning data, making it unclear whether the method induces reasoning from scratch or merely amplifies existing capability.

The empirical comparison (Table 3) confirms these distinctions matter: under matched conditions, RLP outperforms RPT by 4% relative in the token-matched setting and by 20% relative in the FLOP-matched setting.

**Self-rewarding and verifier-free post-training methods (Yuan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b; Zhao et al., 2025).** Several recent works have explored verifier-free rewards in post-training settings. For example, Yuan et al. (2024) uses iterative DPO where the model judges its own outputs to create preference pairs, and Zhao et al. (2025) proposes using the model's own confidence as a reward signal. These methods are conceptually related — they also avoid external verifiers — but they operate **during post-training** on already-pretrained models, often on curated datasets. RLP differs fundamentally in being a **pretraining objective** designed to work on arbitrary text streams, not a post-training alignment technique. The paper explicitly notes that "direct comparisons are not well-posed" because these methods target a different stage of the training pipeline.

**Continuous pretraining on reasoning data (Wang et al., 2025; Gandhi et al., 2025).** A simpler alternative to RLP would be to simply continue pretraining (CPT) on high-quality reasoning corpora without changing the objective. If the goal is to improve reasoning, why not just show the model more reasoning examples with standard next-token prediction loss? The paper addresses this through controlled experiments (Table 4) showing that RLP consistently outperforms CPT on the same data — and, critically, that RLP on 170M tokens outperforms CPT on 6B tokens (35× more data) when matched for total FLOPs. This demonstrates that the objective itself, not just the data, is responsible for the gains.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

RLP is presented not as a replacement for post-training, but as a reimagining of what pretraining can be. The paper's positioning can be understood along three axes:

**1. Pretraining as a reinforcement learning problem.** The paper reframes next-token prediction as a reinforcement learning task where the **action** is generating a chain-of-thought and the **reward** is the information that thought provides for predicting the next token. This is a conceptual shift: rather than treating reasoning as a specialized skill to be added after the fact, RLP treats it as an integral part of the prediction process itself — the model *thinks before it predicts*, and it's rewarded for thinking in proportion to how much the thinking helps.

**2. Verifier-free as a scalability enabler.** The signature feature of RLP is that it requires **no external verifier**. The reward `r(ct) = log pθ(xt | x<t, ct) - log ¯pϕ(xt | x<t)` is computed entirely from the model's own outputs (the reasoned prediction vs. the EMA baseline's no-think prediction). This makes RLP applicable to **any text, in any domain, at any scale** — unlike RLVR, which is gated by the availability of verifiable rewards. The paper emphasizes this repeatedly: RLP "allows uniform application to domain-agnostic web-scale text" (Section 1), and "unlike methods requiring narrow, curated datasets, RLP successfully extracts a powerful reasoning signal from diverse, general-purpose web corpora" (Section 1).

**3. Reasoning before prediction as a general principle.** The paper positions RLP as implementing a specific hypothesis about how language understanding should work: comprehension is not just pattern matching over prefixes, but involves active inference — generating and evaluating internal hypotheses (thoughts) that reduce uncertainty about what comes next. The information-gain reward operationalizes this hypothesis precisely: a thought is valuable exactly to the degree that it reduces predictive uncertainty, measured as the improvement in log-likelihood of the ground-truth next token. This connects the method to information-theoretic principles (the expected reward equals cross-entropy reduction, Proposition 1) and provides a principled foundation that distinguishes it from heuristic reward designs.

**4. Complementarity with post-training, not competition with it.** The paper is careful to position RLP as enhancing, not replacing, the standard post-training pipeline. The key experimental demonstration (Table 1, shaded vs. unshaded columns) is that RLP's gains **survive and compound** after identical SFT + RLVR post-training. An RLP-pretrained model that undergoes the same post-training as a conventionally pretrained model maintains a 7–8% relative advantage (Overall 42.51% vs. 39.90% for MCPT+Post). This suggests that RLP builds reasoning foundations during pretraining that post-training can then build upon, rather than being redundant with post-training or being "washed out" by downstream alignment.

The paper also explicitly contrasts RLP with a straw-man baseline — continuous pretraining on the same data with standard next-token prediction loss (MCPT) — to isolate the effect of the objective from the effect of the data. The consistent finding that `MRLP > MCPT` on the same tokens establishes that the reinforcement signal, not just exposure to reasoning text, is responsible for the improvements.

In summary, the paper addresses a gap in the current LLM training paradigm: pretraining optimizes only for likelihood, deferring reasoning incentives to post-training, which creates a structural inefficiency — the model spends the vast majority of its training compute without ever being rewarded for thinking. RLP proposes to fix this by making the pretraining objective itself reward information-gain through chain-of-thought, using a verifier-free signal that works on any text, thereby making reasoning a native capability learned during pretraining rather than a specialized skill bolted on at the end.

**Reasoned predictor `pθ(xt | x_<t, ct)`**: After sampling a thought `c_t`, the network conditions on the concatenated sequence `(x_<t, c_t)` — the original prefix followed by the generated thought — and produces a probability distribution over the next token `x_t`. This is a standard language modeling forward pass, no different from predicting `x_t` from any context. The difference is that the context now includes the model's own generated reasoning.

**No-think baseline `¯pφ(xt | x_<t)`**: This is a second set of parameters `φ` that also produces a probability distribution over the next token `x_t`, but conditioned only on the prefix `x_<t` — without access to any thought. Critically, `φ` is **not a separately trained model**. It is an exponential moving average (EMA) of the shared network's parameters `θ`, updated after every optimizer step as:

$$\phi \leftarrow \tau \phi + (1 - \tau) \theta$$

where `τ = 0.999` is the EMA decay rate, `φ` represents the EMA parameters, and `θ` represents the current model parameters.

**What this update computes:** At each step, the EMA parameters move 0.1% of the way toward the current model parameters (since `1 - τ = 0.001`). This means `φ` is a smoothed, lagged version of `θ` — it tracks the model's progress but changes much more slowly. If `θ` has a value of 1.0 at step `k` and the EMA has a value of 0.5 from previous updates, after the update the EMA becomes `0.999 × 0.5 + 0.001 × 1.0 = 0.5005`, barely moving.

**Why this form:** If the baseline were a frozen snapshot of the initial model, it would become increasingly stale as training progresses, making the comparison artificially easy (the current model would easily outperform a model from thousands of steps ago). If the baseline tracked the model without lag (`τ → 0`), the log-likelihood ratio would collapse toward zero because both models would be nearly identical, eliminating the reward signal. The slow EMA (`τ = 0.999`) strikes a balance: the baseline is current enough to provide a meaningful comparison (it represents what the model would predict if it hadn't thought) but lagged enough that improvements in the reasoned predictor register as positive reward. The paper explicitly states this rationale: "If the baseline were frozen, the counterfactual would drift too far from the evolving model; if it tracked the model without lag, the log-likelihood ratio would collapse toward zero and invite degenerate strategies."

The EMA is initialized from the current model parameters on the first training batch (`φ ← θ` when first used), ensuring the baseline starts as a meaningful "no-think" comparison rather than a random network.

**Why a single shared network:** This is a deliberate choice that distinguishes RLP from actor-critic architectures. The same parameters are responsible for both generating thoughts (the "actor") and predicting tokens from thoughts (the "critic" component). This means that improvements in prediction automatically improve the thought policy's reward (since the reasoned predictor gets better), and improvements in thought generation improve the prediction input. The two capabilities co-evolve. The EMA baseline is the only auxiliary component, and it requires no separate training — it is purely a moving average, not a learned function.

#### Classical Next-Token Objective (Context, Not Used)

The paper defines the standard next-token prediction loss purely for reference; it is **not part of the RLP training objective**. Given a text sequence `x = (x_0, ..., x_T)` and a position `t`, the standard objective for a predictor `q_η` is:

$$L_{\text{NTP}}(\eta) := \mathbb{E}_{(x_{<t}, x_t) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log q_\eta(x_t \mid x_{<t}) \right]$$

where `(x_<t, x_t)` is a prefix-next-token pair sampled from the data distribution `D`, and `q_η(xt | x_<t)` is the model's predicted probability of the observed token given the prefix.

**What it computes:** The expected log-probability the model assigns to the correct next token, averaged over positions and documents. Maximizing this is equivalent to minimizing the cross-entropy `CE(p*, q_η(· | x_<t))` between the true data distribution `p*(· | x_<t)` and the model's distribution. This is the standard pretraining objective used by essentially all current LLMs.

**Why it's mentioned but not used:** The paper includes this definition to establish the baseline it's improving upon. RLP does **not** include a standard NTP loss term in its training. Instead, as described in Section 2.4, "Our training does not include the standard next-token loss in equation 1. Instead, we optimize only the information-gain objective." This is an important design choice: RLP replaces, rather than augments, the standard likelihood objective with a reinforcement objective that still implicitly optimizes likelihood (as Proposition 1 shows) but does so by explicitly incentivizing useful thinking.

The cross-entropy notation is also needed for Proposition 1, which shows that the expected reward equals cross-entropy reduction. Defining it here avoids redundancy later.

#### Information-Gain Reward

This is the central mathematical contribution of the paper: a reward that measures how much a chain-of-thought improves next-token prediction, computed entirely from model outputs without any external verifier. The reward is computed under **teacher forcing** — meaning the ground-truth next token `x_t` is used (as is standard in language model training; the model never sees future tokens during autoregressive generation, but during training the correct next token is available for loss computation).

**Step 1: Reasoned log-evidence.** Given the prefix `x_<t`, a sampled thought `c_t`, and the ground-truth next token `x_t`, the reasoned scorer (the shared network conditioned on the prefix AND the thought) produces:

$$S_{\text{pred}}(c_t) := \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}, c_t)$$

where `S_pred(c_t)` is the log-probability the reasoned model assigns to the correct token, `p_θ` is the shared network's output distribution, `x_t` is the ground-truth next token from the corpus, and `(x_<t, c_t)` is the concatenated context.

**What it computes:** A single scalar representing how probable the model thinks the correct token is, given that it had the benefit of thinking first. This is exactly the log-likelihood that would be used in standard next-token training, but computed from the thought-augmented context. If the thought was helpful, this value should be high (close to 0, since log of a probability near 1 is near 0); if the thought was misleading or irrelevant, it will be low (very negative).

**Step 2: Baseline log-evidence.** The EMA baseline, given only the prefix (no thought), produces:

$$S_{\text{EMA}} := \log \bar{p}_\phi(x_t \mid x_{<t})$$

where `S_EMA` is the log-probability the EMA baseline assigns to the correct token without any thinking, `¯p_φ` is the EMA model's output distribution, and `x_<t` is the prefix alone.

**What it computes:** The counterfactual: how probable the model would have thought the correct token was if it had NOT engaged in chain-of-thought. This is the "no-think" baseline prediction quality.

**Step 3: Information-gain reward.** The reward is the difference:

$$r(c_t) := S_{\text{pred}}(c_t) - S_{\text{EMA}}$$

where `r(c_t)` is the reward for thought `c_t` at position `t`, `S_pred` is the reasoned log-evidence, and `S_EMA` is the baseline log-evidence.

**What it computes:** The **improvement in log-probability** achieved by thinking. If `r(c_t) > 0`, the thought increased the model's confidence in the correct token — it provided useful information. If `r(c_t) < 0`, the thought decreased confidence in the correct token — it was counterproductive. If `r(c_t) ≈ 0`, the thought was neutral. The magnitude of the reward measures the **information gain** in nats: `r(c_t) = 1.0` means the thought made the correct token `e ≈ 2.718` times more probable; `r(c_t) = -1.0` means it made the correct token `e` times less probable.

**Why this form — the log-likelihood ratio:** The authors choose this specific form because it has three critical properties. First, it is **verifier-free**: neither `S_pred` nor `S_EMA` requires any external knowledge, answer checking, or human labeling — both are computed from the model's own output probabilities on the ground-truth token. This means the reward is computable for any text in any domain. Second, it is **dense and continuous**: unlike binary rewards (correct/incorrect), the information-gain reward provides a graded signal — thoughts that help a lot get large positive rewards, thoughts that help a little get small positive rewards, thoughts that hurt a lot get large negative rewards. This richer credit assignment enables more efficient learning. Third, it has an information-theoretic interpretation (Proposition 1): in expectation, it equals the reduction in cross-entropy achieved by the thought, meaning maximizing reward is mathematically equivalent to improving prediction.

**Why use log-ratio rather than probability ratio:** The log transform serves two purposes. It makes the reward additive across independent contributions (log of product = sum of logs), which aligns with how log-probabilities are used throughout language modeling. It also compresses the dynamic range: probability ratios can vary by orders of magnitude (from near-0 to very large numbers), while log-ratios are more numerically stable and produce gradients that are better-behaved for optimization.

**Important detail on gradient flow:** The paper explicitly notes that when updating the policy, `r(c_t)` is "treated as a constant with respect to `θ` (no backpropagation through `p_θ` or `¯p_φ`)." This means the reward is computed using the current model, then detached from the computation graph before the policy update. The gradient only flows through the thought token log-probabilities (the `log πθ(ct | x_<t)` term), not through the reward computation itself. This is standard in policy gradient methods: the reward is the "signal" that scales the gradient, not something we differentiate through. If gradients were allowed to flow through `p_θ`, the model could improve reward by directly manipulating the scorer's output (making `S_pred` artificially high) rather than by generating better thoughts — this would be a form of reward hacking analogous to the verifier over-optimization documented in other papers.

**Teacher forcing detail:** The reward is computed for the ground-truth token `x_t` from the corpus, which is available during training (since training operates on existing text). This is a subtle but important point: RLP does NOT require the model to generate text autoregressively and then check if it was correct. It uses the same teacher-forcing setup as standard language model training — the prefix and next token are both drawn from the corpus. The only addition is that a thought is sampled before scoring the next token.

#### Expected Improvement Identity (Proposition 1)

The paper provides a formal result connecting the information-gain reward to cross-entropy reduction. This proposition serves as the theoretical justification for why maximizing reward is a sound objective: it proves that the reward measures exactly what we want it to measure.

**Statement:** For any fixed `(x_<t, c_t)`:

$$\mathbb{E}_{x_t \sim p^*}[r(c_t)] = \text{CE}\left(p^*, \bar{p}_\phi(\cdot \mid x_{<t})\right) - \text{CE}\left(p^*, p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{<t}, c_t)\right)$$

where the expectation on the left is over `x_t` drawn from the true data distribution `p*(· | x_<t)` (what token actually tends to follow this prefix in natural language), `CE(p*, q)` is the cross-entropy between the true distribution and a model distribution, `¯p_φ(· | x_<t)` is the EMA baseline's distribution over next tokens, and `p_θ(· | x_<t, c_t)` is the reasoned predictor's distribution.

**What it computes:** The expected reward of a thought equals how much that thought reduces the cross-entropy (improves the prediction quality) relative to not thinking. Cross-entropy measures the average number of nats needed to encode the true next token using the model's distribution — lower is better. If the thought reduces cross-entropy from 3.0 to 2.5 nats, the expected reward is `3.0 - 2.5 = 0.5` nats of information gain, meaning the thought makes the correct token `e^0.5 ≈ 1.65` times more probable on average.

**Why this form matters:** This identity converts an intuitive notion ("thoughts that help prediction are good") into a precise mathematical equivalence. Maximizing the expected reward is **exactly** equivalent to minimizing the cross-entropy of the reasoned predictor. This means RLP is not a heuristic — it is a principled objective that reduces to improving next-token prediction, just like standard NTP training. The difference is the path: standard NTP directly optimizes `p_θ(xt | x_<t)` by gradient descent on the log-likelihood; RLP optimizes `p_θ(xt | x_<t, c_t)` by reinforcing thoughts that make this quantity large. Both converge to the same goal (better prediction), but RLP additionally shapes the thought-generation policy as a side effect.

The proof (Appendix 8.2) is straightforward: expand the expected reward using the definition of cross-entropy (`CE(p, q) = E_{x~p}[-log q(x)]`), note that the expectation of the negative log-likelihood is cross-entropy, and rearrange. The key step is that `E[log p(x)] = -CE(p*, p)`. No approximations or assumptions beyond the definitions are required — this is an exact identity for any fixed thought and context.

#### Lower Bound via Marginalization over Thoughts (Proposition 2)

A natural question about RLP is: why not just marginalize over all possible thoughts when predicting, rather than conditioning on a single sampled thought? This would integrate over the model's uncertainty about what to think. The paper shows that the RLP objective (which conditions on a single sampled thought) is a **lower bound** on the improvement that marginalization would provide.

**Statement (part i, Jensen bound):** For any `(x_<t, x_t)`:

$$\mathbb{E}_{c_t \sim \pi_\theta}\left[S_{\text{pred}}(c_t)\right] \leq \log \tilde{p}_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})$$

where `˜p_θ(xt | x_<t) := E_{c_t ~ πθ}[p_θ(xt | x_<t, c_t)]` is the "collapsed predictor" — the expected probability of the next token when averaging over all possible thoughts, weighted by the thought policy.

**What it computes:** The left side is the average log-probability of the correct token when conditioning on a single sampled thought. The right side is the log of the average probability — i.e., the log-probability you would get if you could marginalize (integrate) over all thoughts before predicting. Jensen's inequality for the concave `log` function says the log of an average is always greater than or equal to the average of the logs. So conditioning on a single thought is worse (on average) than marginalizing over all thoughts — which makes intuitive sense: integrating over your uncertainty should give a better calibrated prediction than conditioning on one random sample from your uncertainty distribution.

**Statement (part ii, bound on the RLP objective):**

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[r(c_t)] \leq \mathbb{E}\left[\log \frac{\tilde{p}_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})}{\bar{p}_\phi(x_t \mid x_{<t})}\right]$$

where `J(θ)` is the expected information-gain reward being maximized, and the right side is the expected improvement from the marginalized (collapsed) predictor over the no-think baseline.

**What it computes:** The RLP objective `J(θ)` is a **computable lower bound** on the improvement you would get if you could optimally combine all possible thoughts. The right side represents the true benefit of thinking — how much better prediction becomes when you can consider all thoughts and weight them appropriately. The left side (RLP's actual objective) is what you can compute and optimize from a single sample.

**Why this form matters — the lower bound property:** This result provides a theoretical safety guarantee. By optimizing `J(θ)`, RLP is pushing up a lower bound on the true benefit of thinking. If `J(θ)` improves, the marginalized predictor's improvement must improve at least as much (or more). This means RLP is conservative in a principled way: it optimizes something that is guaranteed to be no better than the true benefit, so improvements in the objective translate to genuine improvements in thinking quality. The tightness condition is also informative: the bound is tight when `p_θ(xt | x_<t, c_t)` is constant across all thoughts `c_t ~ πθ` — i.e., when the thought either doesn't affect the prediction or when the policy is deterministic. In practice, the model learns to make its predictions somewhat invariant to the exact thought for easy tokens and highly thought-dependent for hard tokens, balancing between these regimes.

#### The RLP Objective and Optimization

This section describes how the theoretical reward is turned into a practical training algorithm. The paper presents this as Algorithm 1 in Section 2.4, which I will walk through in detail.

**The objective function.** RLP maximizes:

$$\max_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x_{<t} \sim \mathcal{D}} \mathbb{E}_{c_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{<t})} \left[ r(c_t) \right]$$

where the outer expectation is over prefix-next-token pairs sampled from the training corpus `D`, and the inner expectation is over thoughts sampled from the current policy. Equivalently, RLP minimizes the negative information-gain loss `L_IG(θ) = -J(θ)`.

**What it computes:** The expected reward across positions and thoughts. For each position in each document, sample a thought and compute how much it helps. Average this across the training distribution. This is a standard expected reward objective in reinforcement learning.

**Key detail — no standard NTP loss:** The paper states in Section 2.4: "Our training does not include the standard next-token loss in equation 1. Instead, we optimize only the information-gain objective." This means the model's parameters are updated **only** through the gradient of the thought token log-probabilities, scaled by the reward, without any direct likelihood gradient on the predicted tokens. This is a pure policy gradient approach — the model learns to think by being reinforced for thoughts that improve prediction, but the prediction quality itself improves indirectly as a consequence of better thoughts.

**Why no NTP loss:** Including a standard NTP loss term would create a mixed objective where the model is simultaneously trying to maximize the likelihood of tokens (which doesn't require thinking) and maximize the information-gain reward (which does). The authors choose to isolate the effect of the reinforcement signal by removing the direct likelihood gradient entirely. As Proposition 1 shows, maximizing the expected reward already minimizes the reasoned predictor's cross-entropy, so the NTP objective is in some sense redundant — but implementing it through RL rather than direct gradient descent changes the learning dynamics, potentially encouraging the model to invest more in thinking rather than pattern-matching.

**Gradient flow restriction:** The paper specifies that "gradients are applied only to the thought tokens; `r(c_t)` is treated as a constant (no backpropagation through `pθ` or `¯pφ`)." This means:

1. The reward is computed using the current model outputs, then detached from the computation graph.
2. The gradient update affects only the probabilities of the thought tokens themselves (the `log πθ(ct | x< t)` terms).
3. The reasoned predictor `pθ(xt | x_<t, c_t)` is not directly updated — it improves only because the shared parameters `θ` are updated through the thought token gradient, and better thoughts lead to better predictions.

This is a deliberate design choice that prevents the model from "cheating" by directly manipulating the predictor's confidence rather than improving thought quality — a form of reward hacking prevention built into the training algorithm.

#### Group-Relative Advantages

Computing the gradient of `J(θ)` with respect to `θ` involves estimating `E[r(c_t) ∇_θ log πθ(c_t)]` — the policy gradient. The naive approach (sample one thought, compute its reward, and scale the gradient by that reward) has high variance because individual rewards can vary widely. To reduce variance, RLP uses a **group-relative baseline** with a correction factor.

**Step 1: Sample a group of thoughts.** For each context `x_<t`, sample `G ≥ 2` thoughts `{c_t^(1), ..., c_t^(G)}` independently from the policy `πθ(· | x_<t)`. In practice, `G = 16` is used (determined by ablation in Figure 2a).

**Step 2: Compute inclusive mean.** Calculate the average reward across the group:

$$\bar{r} = \frac{1}{G} \sum_{j=1}^{G} r(c_t^{(j)})$$

where `¯r` is the mean reward for this group of thoughts at this position.

**What it computes:** The average performance of the `G` thoughts. If most thoughts are helpful, `¯r` will be positive; if most are unhelpful, it will be negative. This serves as a baseline for comparison — thoughts are evaluated relative to the average thought, not in absolute terms.

**Step 3: Compute corrected advantages.** For each thought `i` in the group:

$$A^{(i)} := \frac{G}{G - 1} \left( r(c_t^{(i)}) - \bar{r} \right)$$

where `A^(i)` is the advantage of thought `i`, `G/(G-1)` is a correction factor, and `r(c_t^(i)) - ¯r` is the deviation from the group mean.

**What it computes:** How much better (or worse) thought `i` is than the average thought in its group, multiplied by a correction factor. If `A^(i) > 0`, thought `i` was better than average and its probability should increase; if `A^(i) < 0`, it was worse than average and its probability should decrease. The correction factor `G/(G-1)` accounts for the fact that the inclusive mean `¯r` includes thought `i` itself, which introduces a bias of `(1 - 1/G)` (shrinkage toward the mean). Multiplying by `G/(G-1)` removes this bias, yielding an unbiased estimator of the advantage.

**Why this form — corrected inclusive mean:** An alternative would be to compute the mean excluding thought `i` (leave-one-out), which is unbiased but requires `G` separate mean computations. The inclusive mean with correction achieves the same unbiasedness with a single mean computation (`O(G)`) by applying a simple multiplicative factor. The paper states that this "yields an unbiased estimator with low variance." Compared to using a learned value function (critic), the group-relative baseline requires no additional parameters, no value function training, and no temporal credit assignment — the baseline is simply the average of peers sampled from the same policy at the same position. This simplicity is a key architectural choice that keeps RLP lightweight.

**Why group-relative rather than absolute rewards:** Using absolute rewards (without subtracting a baseline) would mean that even thoughts that are helpful but below average might get positive gradients (if the absolute reward is positive), and thoughts that are unhelpful but above average might get negative gradients (if the absolute reward is negative). The group-relative formulation ensures that probability always shifts from worse thoughts to better thoughts, regardless of the absolute reward level. If all thoughts are terrible (all `r(c_t^(i)) < 0`), the "least terrible" thought still gets a positive advantage and its probability increases — which is correct because shifting probability to the least-bad option improves expected reward. Appendix 9 provides a formal proof that this mechanism ensures monotonic improvement even when all rewards are negative.

#### Clipped Surrogate Objective (PPO-Style Update)

The policy is updated by maximizing a clipped surrogate objective on the thought tokens, following the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) pattern. For each thought token `ℓ_u^(i)` (the `u`-th token in thought `c_t^(i)`), with prefix `prefix_u^(i) = (x_<t, ℓ_1^(i), ..., ℓ_{u-1}^(i))`:

**Step 1: Per-token importance ratio.** Define:

$$\rho_u^{(i)} = \exp\left( \log \pi_\theta(\ell_u^{(i)} \mid \text{prefix}_u^{(i)}) - \log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\ell_u^{(i)} \mid \text{prefix}_u^{(i)}) \right)$$

where `πθ` is the current policy (after some updates), `π_{θ_old}` is the behavior policy that was used to sample the thoughts (frozen at the start of each sampling pass, as specified in Algorithm 1 line 5: "Set the behavior snapshot `θ_old ← θ`"), and `ℓ_u^(i)` is the specific token that was sampled.

**What it computes:** The ratio of probabilities — how much more (or less) likely the current policy is to generate this specific token at this position compared to when it was sampled. If the policy hasn't changed much, `ρ ≈ 1`. If the current policy thinks this token is much more likely than before, `ρ > 1`. If it thinks it's much less likely, `ρ < 1`.

**Why the exponential of log-difference:** This is equivalent to `π_θ / π_{θ_old}` but computed in log-space for numerical stability. The log-probabilities come directly from the model's output logits; exponentiating their difference recovers the probability ratio.

**Step 2: Clipped surrogate loss.** The loss for a single thought is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{clip}}^{(b,i)} = -\frac{1}{|c_t^{(i)}|} \sum_u \min\left( \rho_u^{(i)} \cdot \text{sg}(A^{(i)}), \text{clip}(\rho_u^{(i)}; 1 - \epsilon_\ell, 1 + \epsilon_h) \cdot \text{sg}(A^{(i)}) \right)$$

where `sg(A^(i))` means the advantage is treated as a constant (stop-gradient — no gradient flows through it), `clip(ρ; 1-ε_ℓ, 1+ε_h)` clips the importance ratio to the interval `[1-ε_ℓ, 1+ε_h]`, `|c_t^(i)|` is the number of tokens in the thought (for length normalization), and the sum is over all tokens in the thought.

**What it computes:** For each thought token, compute the importance ratio `ρ`. If `A^(i) > 0` (the thought was better than average), we want to increase the probability of these tokens — the unclipped objective would use `ρ × A^(i)`, which pushes `ρ` higher (increase probability). If `A^(i) < 0` (the thought was worse than average), we want to decrease probability — `ρ × A^(i)` would push `ρ` lower (decrease probability, since `A^(i)` is negative). The `min` with the clipped version ensures we don't update too aggressively: if `ρ` is already very high and the advantage is positive, the clipped version `1+ε_h × A^(i)` provides a ceiling; if `ρ` is very low and the advantage is negative, `(1-ε_ℓ) × A^(i)` provides a floor. The negative sign makes this a loss to minimize; the `1/|c_t^(i)|` normalizes by thought length so longer thoughts don't dominate the gradient.

**Why this form — PPO clipping:** Without clipping, a single large policy update could dramatically increase the probability of a thought that happened to get a high reward by chance, causing the policy to collapse to a deterministic mode and stop exploring. The clipping mechanism (standard in PPO) prevents the policy from changing too much in a single update, maintaining a trust region around the behavior policy. This is especially important early in training when reward estimates are noisy and the policy could easily overfit to spurious patterns.

**Clipping parameters:** The paper uses asymmetric clipping with `ε_ℓ` (lower clip) and `ε_h` (upper clip). The specific values are not stated in the main text, but the standard PPO values would be `ε_ℓ = ε_h = 0.2`. The asymmetry allows different update sizes for increasing vs. decreasing probability.

**Stop-gradient on advantage:** `sg(A^(i))` means the advantage is treated as a constant multiplier — no gradient flows back through the advantage to the parameters that computed it. This is essential because the advantage depends on the reward, which depends on the reasoned predictor's output, which depends on the model parameters. If gradient were allowed to flow through the advantage, the model could increase its objective by manipulating the reward computation (making `S_pred` arbitrarily large or `S_EMA` arbitrarily small) rather than by generating better thoughts. This is analogous to the standard practice in RL of treating the reward as external and not differentiating through it.

**Full batch loss:** The total loss aggregates over all `B` documents in the batch and all `G` thoughts per document:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{BG} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \mathcal{L}_{\text{clip}}^{(b,i)}$$

This is then minimized by gradient descent: `θ ← θ - η∇_θ L(θ)`.

---

#### EMA Baseline Update

After each optimizer step updates the shared parameters `θ`, the EMA baseline parameters `φ` are updated as:

$$\phi \leftarrow \tau \phi + (1 - \tau) \theta$$

with `τ = 0.999`.

**What it computes:** The EMA baseline takes a tiny step (0.1% of the distance) toward the current model parameters. Over many steps, `φ` tracks `θ` with a lag that depends on `τ`. With `τ = 0.999`, the effective window is roughly `1/(1-τ) = 1000` steps — the EMA reflects the average model state over the last ~1000 updates.

**Why after the optimizer step:** The EMA update happens after `θ` is updated, meaning `φ` at step `k+1` is a blend of the previous EMA and the newly updated model. This ensures that on the next batch, the baseline is slightly more current but still lagged relative to `θ`. The one-step lag is exactly what prevents the log-likelihood ratio from collapsing to zero: `θ` has just improved (hopefully), so `p_θ` should be slightly better than `¯p_φ`, producing a small positive reward that reinforces the improvements.

---

#### Putting It All Together — The Algorithm

Algorithm 1 in Section 2.4 composes these components. The training loop for each batch is:

1. **Snapshot the behavior policy:** `θ_old ← θ`. This freezes the parameters used for thought sampling, ensuring that all `G` thoughts for a batch are sampled from the same policy.
2. **Sample a minibatch:** `{ (x_<t^(b), x_t^(b)) }` for `b = 1, ..., B` documents. In practice, one random position `t` is selected per document rather than using every position.
3. **Sample G thoughts:** For each document, generate `G` thoughts from `π_{θ_old}(· | x_<t^(b))`, each with at least 1 token. These are generated autoregressively at temperature 0.7.
4. **Lazy-initialize EMA:** On the first batch only, set `φ ← θ` so the baseline starts as a no-think copy of the current model.
5. **Compute rewards:** For each thought, compute the reasoned log-evidence `S_pred` (using the current `θ`, not `θ_old`) and the baseline log-evidence `S_EMA` (using `φ`). Form the reward `r = S_pred - S_EMA`. Crucially, neither `p_θ` nor `¯p_φ` receives gradient through the reward.
6. **Compute advantages:** For each document, compute the group mean `¯r` and corrected advantages `A^(i)` as described above. Advantages are detached from the computation graph.
7. **Compute clipped surrogate loss:** For each thought, compute per-token importance ratios using the current policy `πθ` vs. the behavior policy `π_{θ_old}`, form the clipped loss with the advantage as a constant multiplier, and average over thought tokens.
8. **Update θ:** Take one gradient step on the aggregated loss over all thoughts in the batch. Only thought token log-probabilities receive gradient; the reward and advantage are treated as constants.
9. **Update EMA:** `φ ← 0.999φ + 0.001θ`.

**What is NOT updated:** The reasoned predictor `p_θ(xt | x_<t, c_t)` receives no direct gradient from the RLP loss. Its parameters `θ` are updated only indirectly through the gradient on the thought tokens. This means improvements to the predictor come from: (a) the shared parameters getting better at the language modeling task through the thought-generating pathway, and (b) the thoughts themselves becoming more informative, giving the predictor better conditioning context.

**Important practical detail — which tokens receive RLP:** The paper states that "theoretically RLP can be applied for every token in a document but in our experiments we randomly select one token per document. Hence, the number of tokens for which the reward is applied is far less than 1B." This is a crucial efficiency choice. If RLP were applied at every position, each document of length `L` would require `L × G` thought generations and `L × G` reward computations. By selecting one random position per document, the cost per document is `1 × G` generations, making the method practical for large-scale pretraining. The paper reports training on 1B input tokens for the main experiments, meaning roughly 1B documents (assuming ~1 selected token per document) with `G=16` thoughts each, for approximately 16B thought tokens generated during training.

#### Reward Properties and Guarantees

**Property 1: Positive reward means thinking helped.** The reward `r(c_t) = log pθ(xt | x_<t, c_t) - log ¯pφ(xt | x_<t)` is positive exactly when the thought-conditioned model assigns higher probability to the correct token than the no-think baseline. Since log is monotonic, `r(c_t) > 0` means `pθ(xt | x_<t, c_t) > ¯pφ(xt | x_<t)` — the thought increased confidence in the correct answer. The magnitude tells you by what factor: `r(c_t) = 2.3` means the correct token is 10 times more probable with thinking than without.

**Property 2: Positionwise credit assignment.** The reward is computed independently at each position `t`: `r(c_t) = log pθ(xt | x_<t, c_t) - log ¯pφ(xt | x_<t)`. There is no temporal credit assignment across positions — the thought at position `t` is rewarded only for helping predict token `x_t`, not for any future tokens. This is possible because teacher forcing provides the ground-truth next token immediately, unlike in sequential decision-making where rewards may be delayed. The paper provides Proposition 3 (Appendix 8.4) showing that summing positionwise rewards across a sequence recovers the per-token sequence-level cross-entropy improvement, confirming that positionwise credit assignment is consistent with sequence-level evaluation.

**Property 3: Monotonic improvement even when all thoughts are bad.** Appendix 9 addresses a potential concern: if all `G` thoughts have negative rewards (all thoughts make prediction worse than not thinking), won't the group-relative advantage still assign positive advantage to the "least bad" thought, reinforcing harmful behavior? The paper proves this is not a problem. The expected improvement rate under the replicator dynamic (the continuous-time limit of policy gradient) is:

$$\frac{d}{d\tau} J(\theta(\tau)) = \text{Var}_\pi[r(c)] \geq 0$$

where `Var_π[r(c)]` is the variance of the reward under the current policy. This is always non-negative, meaning expected reward never decreases in expectation — even when all rewards are negative, shifting probability from more-negative thoughts to less-negative thoughts increases `J(θ)`. The policy gradually improves until it finds thoughts that are genuinely helpful (positive reward). The EMA baseline ensures this improvement is genuine: because `φ` lags `θ`, sustained positive advantages require the reasoned predictor to genuinely outperform the baseline, not just temporarily inflate its own outputs.

**Property 4: Dense, verifier-free signal.** Unlike RLVR which provides a single reward at the end of a complete solution (sparse, often binary), RLP provides a continuous reward at every position where it's applied. This density means the model receives learning signal from every thought, not just from those that lead to a correct final answer. Even a thought that increases the probability of the correct token from `10^{-5}` to `10^{-4}` gets a positive reward (`log(10^{-4}) - log(10^{-5}) = log(10) ≈ 2.3`), even though the correct token is still unlikely — the thought provided information, and that information gain is rewarded.

**Why these properties matter together:** The combination of verifier-free rewards, positionwise credit, dense signal, and monotonic improvement guarantees makes RLP applicable to arbitrary text streams at scale. There is no need to filter for "high-entropy" tokens (unlike RPT), no need for verifiable answers (unlike RLVR), no need for curated reasoning traces (unlike SFT), and no risk of the model being rewarded for internally consistent but objectively wrong reasoning (since the reward is anchored to the ground-truth token from the corpus). The method should, in principle, work on any text where the task of predicting the next token benefits from intermediate reasoning — which is arguably most of natural language.

---

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Single shared network for policy and prediction** — avoids the complexity of separate actor-critic architectures; ensures that improvements in prediction and thought generation are tightly coupled; reduces memory and parameter count (the EMA baseline adds negligible overhead since it's just a moving average, not a separate trainable model).

**EMA baseline with τ = 0.999** — provides a slowly moving counterfactual that is current enough to be meaningful but lagged enough to prevent reward collapse; the specific value of 0.999 was chosen empirically (Appendix 12, Table S.6 shows τ = 0.999 as the peak of a bell-shaped performance curve across values 0.99, 0.995, 0.999, 0.9995); values that are too low make the comparison too easy, values that are too high make the comparison too hard.

**Log-likelihood ratio reward** — grounded in information theory (Proposition 1 shows it equals cross-entropy reduction); verifier-free (computed entirely from model outputs); continuous and dense rather than binary and sparse; prevents the model from being rewarded for internal consistency without objective improvement (since the reward is computed against the ground-truth token).

**Group-relative advantages with corrected inclusive mean** — reduces gradient variance without requiring a learned value function; unbiased estimator with a simple correction factor `G/(G-1)`; maintains monotonic improvement even when all thoughts are bad (Appendix 9).

**PPO-style clipped surrogate on thought tokens only** — prevents destructive policy updates from high-variance rewards; asymmetric clipping allows different update sizes for increasing vs. decreasing probability; updating only thought tokens means the model must learn to think better, not just predict better.

**No standard NTP loss in the objective** — isolates the effect of the reinforcement signal; ensures that all learning goes through the thought-generation pathway; Proposition 1 guarantees that maximizing reward still minimizes cross-entropy, just through a different gradient path.

**One random token per document** — makes the method computationally feasible at pretraining scale (RLP is ~2.25× slower per step than standard training, per Appendix 12 Table S.7, but drastically more data-efficient); the random selection ensures diverse coverage of document positions without the quadratic cost of processing every position.

**Temperature 0.7 for thought sampling** — provides sufficient stochasticity for exploration (generating diverse thoughts to learn which ones help) while maintaining coherence; the ablation in Figure 2a shows best performance at G=16 rollouts with this setting.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Next-Token Pretraining as a Reinforcement Learning Problem over Internal Thoughts

The defining conceptual move of this paper is not to add reasoning as a post-hoc skill, but to reframe the act of next-token prediction itself as a reinforcement learning problem where the **action** is generating an internal chain-of-thought before predicting the next token, and the **reward** is the information that thought provides for that prediction. This is a fundamental shift from the dominant paradigm, which treats reasoning as an extrinsic capability to be induced through supervised fine-tuning or verifier-based RL applied *after* pretraining is complete (Guo et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). In that paradigm, the pretraining objective — maximizing `log p(xt | x<t)` — is purely associative: the model learns statistical regularities that predict what comes next, but nothing in the loss function explicitly rewards the model for engaging in multi-step deliberation, for generating intermediate hypotheses, or for evaluating which kinds of thoughts improve prediction.

RLP changes the nature of what pretraining *is* by augmenting the prediction task with an explicit reasoning step. The model no longer predicts `xt` directly from `x<t`; it first samples a latent thought `ct ~ πθ(· | x<t)`, then predicts `xt` from the concatenation `(x<t, ct)`. The reward `r(ct) = log pθ(xt | x<t, ct) - log ¯pφ(xt | x<t)` measures whether that thought was useful — did it make the correct token more probable? This turns next-token prediction into a decision problem: the model must learn which thoughts to generate, and it is reinforced for thoughts that reduce predictive uncertainty. The policy gradient flows only through the thought tokens, meaning the model learns to think better, not just to predict better — though better prediction is the mechanism by which thinking is rewarded.

This reframing matters because it addresses a structural inefficiency in the standard training pipeline. A model pretrained purely with next-token prediction loss may have the latent capability to reason — its parameters encode enough world knowledge to generate useful thoughts — but nothing in the objective pushes it to exercise that capability. The paper's empirical results (Table 1) demonstrate this concretely: the same base model (QWEN3-1.7B-BASE) achieves dramatically different reasoning performance depending on whether it was trained with RLP or continued pretraining on identical data with standard NTP loss (Overall: 36.03% vs. 30.85% for MCPT on 1B tokens). The capability was latent; RLP made it active.

The significance here is not just a new method, but a new framing of what pretraining can optimize. The dominant assumption has been that likelihood maximization is sufficient to extract all useful structure from text, and that reasoning must be added later through specialized objectives. RLP challenges this by showing that an information-gain reward — computed entirely from the model's own outputs, without any external verifier — can be integrated into the pretraining objective itself, teaching the model to think on the same diverse text it already trains on. This moves the field from "pretrain for knowledge, post-train for reasoning" toward "pretrain for reasoning as an integral part of knowledge acquisition." The connection to information theory (Proposition 1: expected reward equals cross-entropy reduction) grounds this reframing in a principled objective rather than a heuristic, strengthening its intellectual foundation.

### Innovation 2: Verifier-Free, Dense Reward as the Enabler of Domain-Agnostic Reasoning Pretraining

The second key innovation is the specific design of the reward signal: a verifier-free, dense, continuous measure of information gain that is computable for any text in any domain. This is not an incremental engineering convenience — it is a conceptual unlock that makes reasoning pretraining scalable beyond narrow, curated datasets. Prior work on inducing reasoning through reinforcement has been fundamentally limited by the availability of verifiable rewards. RLVR (DeepSeek-R1, Guo et al., 2025) requires ground-truth answer checkers or unit tests, which are only available for math, code, and certain structured science problems. This means RLVR-trained models develop reasoning skills from a narrow distribution of problems and may not transfer those skills to the open-ended text they encounter in deployment. SFT-based approaches require curated reasoning traces (OpenThoughts, Nemotron-Crossthink), which are expensive to produce and impossible to scale to web-sized corpora. The result is that state-of-the-art reasoning models become math-and-code specialists whose deliberative abilities may not extend to natural language understanding, explanation, or argument evaluation.

RLP's reward — the log-likelihood ratio of the correct token with and without a thought — requires no answer checker, no curated solution trace, and no human labeling. It is computed entirely from the model's own probability distributions on the ground-truth next token, which is available for any text in any corpus via teacher forcing. This means RLP can be applied to academic papers, web crawl data, textbooks, or any other pretraining corpus (Table 4 demonstrates consistent gains across all six tested corpora, from structured SFT datasets like OmniMath to open-ended web crawl). The paper shows that even on purely non-reasoning corpora like web-crawl, RLP extracts a reasoning signal that translates to improved performance on math and science benchmarks — a form of cross-domain transfer that would be impossible with RLVR.

The "dense" property is equally important. RLVR provides a single reward at the end of a complete solution — it cannot tell the model which intermediate steps were helpful or harmful, only whether the final answer was correct. RLP provides positionwise credit at every token: each thought gets its own scalar reward based on its contribution to predicting the immediately following token. This density means the model receives learning signal from every thought, not just those that happen to lead to a distant correct answer. A thought that increases the probability of the correct token from `10^{-5}` to `10^{-4}` is rewarded even though the token remains unlikely, because the thought genuinely provided information (the expected reduction in cross-entropy is real). This richer credit assignment enables more efficient learning — the paper shows that RLP on 170M tokens outperforms continuous pretraining on 6B tokens (35× more data) when matched for FLOPs (Table 4), demonstrating that the quality of the signal matters more than the quantity of data.

The comparison with RPT (Dong et al., 2025) sharpens the significance of this innovation. RPT also applies RL during pretraining, but uses a sparse, binary reward (correct/incorrect) applied only to tokens pre-selected by an auxiliary model. RLP outperforms RPT by 4% relative under token-matched conditions and by 20% relative under FLOP-matched conditions (Table 3). The methodological difference is the reward design: RPT's binary signal discards information about *how much* a thought helped, and its token preselection limits where learning can occur. RLP's continuous, verifier-free signal extracts more learning per computation by leveraging the full information available in the model's own probability estimates.

### Innovation 3: Demonstrating That Reasoning Foundations Established During Pretraining Survive and Compound After Post-Training

The third key contribution is an empirical finding with significant implications for training pipeline design: **reasoning capabilities instilled during pretraining are not redundant with or overwritten by downstream post-training — they persist and compound**. This finding counters a natural concern about any pretraining intervention: that SFT and RLVR post-training, which are specifically designed to induce reasoning, might either wash out pretraining gains (because the model adapts to the post-training distribution and "forgets" its pretraining skills) or render them redundant (because post-training teaches the same skills anyway, just later). The paper's experimental design directly addresses this by subjecting all models — base, CPT-matched, and RLP-trained — to an identical, strong post-training pipeline (SFT on OpenThoughts followed by RLVR on MATH) and comparing their final performance.

The results (Table 1, shaded vs. unshaded columns) are striking: MRLP+Post achieves an Overall average of 42.51%, compared to 39.90% for MCPT+Post and 39.34% for Mbase+Post — a 7–8% relative margin that mirrors or even widens the pre-post-training gap. On individual benchmarks, the pattern is consistent: AIME25 goes from 5.02% (pre) to 7.05% (post) for RLP vs. 3.96% → 5.89% for CPT; MMLU-Pro goes from 34.62% to 42.40% vs. 27.81% → 39.92%. Importantly, the absolute gain from post-training is roughly comparable across models (Mbase gains ~9 points Overall from post-training, MRLP gains ~6.5 points), meaning that post-training provides additive benefits rather than compensating for pretraining deficiencies. The RLP-pretrained model starts from a higher baseline and ends at a higher ceiling.

The scaling results on NEMOTRON-NANO-12B-V2 (Table 2) confirm this pattern at a larger scale: RLP-pretrained reaches 68.09% Overall after post-training vs. 65.31% for the baseline, with the largest gains in scientific reasoning (Science Avg 64.52% vs. 58.91%). This is using only 250M tokens of RLP training on a model that had already seen 19.8 trillion tokens of standard pretraining — a 0.125% data increment producing a 43% relative improvement at the base stage that narrows but persists as a clear margin after post-training.

This finding upends the implicit assumption in current training pipelines that pretraining is for knowledge and post-training is for skills. It suggests instead that **pretraining and post-training are complementary investments that compound**: building reasoning foundations during pretraining creates a model that is better positioned to absorb and build upon post-training signals, while a model that lacks those foundations benefits less from the same post-training because it must learn both the skill and the foundational representations simultaneously. The practical implication is that training compute allocated to reasoning during pretraining has a multiplicative, not merely additive, effect on final model quality.

### Innovation 4: Identifying the EMA Baseline as a Stable, Self-Regulating Counterfactual for Intrinsic Reward

While the information-gain reward is the conceptual contribution, its practical viability depends on a specific design choice that the paper identifies and validates as critical: the exponential moving average (EMA) baseline that serves as the "no-think" counterfactual. This is an architectural innovation that enables stable, self-regulating training without the complexity of a separate critic network or value function. The question it addresses is: against what should the model's thought-conditioned prediction be compared to determine whether the thought was useful?

The naive options fail for different reasons. A frozen baseline (snapshot the model before RLP training and never update) would become increasingly stale, making the comparison artificially easy as the current model improves — the reward would grow over time not because thoughts are getting more helpful, but because the baseline is a weaker predictor. A constantly updated baseline (no lag) would track the model too closely, collapsing the log-likelihood ratio toward zero and eliminating the reward signal — the model would be compared against a nearly identical predictor, and any improvement would be invisible. A separately trained critic network would add parameters, require its own training objective, and introduce the instability and hyperparameter sensitivity typical of actor-critic methods.

The EMA baseline with `τ = 0.999` elegantly resolves these tensions. By updating `φ ← 0.999φ + 0.001θ` after each optimizer step, the baseline becomes a **slowly moving counterfactual** that is current enough to provide a meaningful comparison (reflecting the model's recent prediction quality) but lagged enough that improvements in the current model register as positive reward. The EMA smooths over the approximately last `1/(1-τ) = 1000` updates, meaning the baseline represents the average model behavior over the recent past. This auto-calibrates the difficulty of the comparison: as the model improves, the baseline improves too, but with a lag that ensures there is always room for positive reward from genuine improvements. If the model's predictions plateau, the reward naturally shrinks as the baseline catches up — preventing the model from being rewarded for stale improvements.

The paper validates this choice empirically (Appendix 12, Table S.6): performance follows a bell-shaped curve across `τ ∈ {0.99, 0.995, 0.999, 0.9995}`, with a clear peak at `τ = 0.999`. Higher values (0.9995, slower updates) underperform, likely because the baseline becomes too stale and the comparison loses discriminative power — the model gets easy rewards from outrunning an outdated baseline. Lower values (0.99, faster updates) also underperform, likely because the reward signal collapses as the baseline tracks too closely. The 0.999 value hits the sweet spot: enough lag to sustain a meaningful reward signal, enough currency to ensure the comparison remains relevant.

The significance of this innovation extends beyond the specific value. It demonstrates a **self-regulating mechanism** that avoids reward hacking without requiring adversarial training, reward model ensembles, or KL penalties to a fixed reference policy (the paper's ablation in Figure 2c shows that adding a KL term to a fixed reference actually *reduces* performance). The baseline cannot be gamed by the model because it is defined solely as a lagged version of the model itself — for the baseline to degrade, the model must degrade first, which is immediately penalized through the primary reward term. This contrasts with RLVR, where a fixed verifier can be exploited by a model that learns to produce outputs that score highly but are incorrect (the verifier over-optimization problem documented extensively in other work). The EMA baseline is a lightweight, parameter-free, self-calibrating solution that makes intrinsic reward practical at scale.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary experiments are conducted on a diverse set of corpora rather than a single benchmark dataset for training. For evaluation, the paper uses an eight-benchmark math-and-science suite accessed through NeMo-Skills: math reasoning benchmarks include AIME25, MATH500, GSM8K, AMC23, and Minerva Math; science reasoning benchmarks include MMLU, MMLU-Pro, and GPQA-Diamond. The MATH dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021b) with 7,500 examples is used for the RLVR post-training stage.

- **Base model(s).** The primary experiments use QWEN3-1.7B-BASE (Yang et al., 2025), a 1.7B parameter pure Transformer model. Scaling experiments extend to NEMOTRON-NANO-12B-V2 (a 12B hybrid Mamba-Transformer architecture, trained to 19.8 trillion tokens before applying RLP) and QWEN3-14B-BASE (a 14B parameter model). The 1.7B model is chosen as the main testbed because it represents "the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" while being small enough for extensive experimentation, and because its non-trivial but far-from-saturated performance leaves room for RLP to demonstrate gains.

- **Metrics.** All results are reported as **accuracy (%)** — the fraction of questions for which the model's final answer matches the ground-truth answer. For math benchmarks (MATH500, GSM8K, AMC23, Minerva), the paper reports Pass@1 average of 8 runs. For science benchmarks (MMLU, MMLU-Pro, GPQA-Diamond), the paper reports both greedy decoding accuracy and Pass@1 average from 4 runs (denoted Science Avg@1[4]). The overall average aggregates across all math and science benchmarks. For MMLU, MMLU-Pro, and GPQA, two versions are reported: standard and @1[4] (Pass@1 from 4 samples), capturing both the model's direct output quality and its ability to produce correct answers under sampling.

- **Baselines.** The paper employs three primary baselines: (1) **Mbase** — the original pretrained model without any additional training, serving as the lower bound; (2) **MCPT (Continuous Pretraining)** — the base model further trained on the same tokens used for RLP but with standard next-token prediction loss, serving as a compute-matched comparison that isolates the effect of the objective from the effect of the data; (3) **MRPT (Reinforcement Pre-Training, Dong et al., 2025)** — trained with the RPT objective under token-matched and FLOP-matched conditions. Additionally, an **MCPT [6B]** variant (trained on 6B tokens, 35× more data than RLP's 170M) serves as a FLOP-equivalent baseline to verify that gains are not due to increased compute. All models undergo identical post-training (SFT + RLVR) where applicable, ensuring fair comparison of the pretraining conditions.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute is measured along two axes: (1) **input tokens seen** — the number of tokens from the training corpus processed by the model, the standard metric for pretraining; (2) **total FLOPs** — accounting for both the input tokens and the tokens generated during thought rollouts. The FLOP-equivalent budget is computed as `T_flop = (n × l_seq × bs × iters) + T_inp`, where `n` is the number of rollouts per instance, `l_seq` is sequence length, `bs` is batch size, and `iters` is the number of RLP steps (Appendix 12). For the main RLP runs, the budget is 1B input tokens (with one random position selected per document) and 170M input tokens for the corpus ablation experiments. MCPT baselines are trained on matched input tokens or matched FLOPs (up to 6B tokens for the FLOP-matched comparison). All post-training runs use identical compute, ensuring that differences in final performance trace back to the pretraining condition.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation in the traditional sense, as there is no hyperparameter selection on the evaluation benchmarks. Instead, the experimental protocol relies on fixed evaluation benchmarks applied to models after each training stage, with results reported as raw accuracy numbers. The consistency of the findings across model sizes (1.7B, 12B, 14B), architectures (pure Transformer, hybrid Mamba-Transformer), and datasets (six diverse corpora) serves as the primary robustness check. The paper reports Pass@1 averaged over multiple runs (8 for math, 4 for science) to reduce sampling variance, but does not report confidence intervals or standard errors. Statistical significance is not formally tested.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### RLP vs. Baselines on QWEN3-1.7B-BASE

**Headline: RLP substantially outperforms both the base model and continuous pretraining.** Table 1 reports the full benchmark suite for QWEN3-1.7B-BASE under four conditions: Mbase (original base model), MCPT (continuous pretraining on the same 1B tokens), MRLP (RLP on 1B tokens), and their post-trained variants. The Overall average for MRLP is 36.03%, compared to 30.32% for Mbase and 30.85% for MCPT — a relative improvement of 19% over Mbase and 17% over MCPT. The gains are broad rather than concentrated: MRLP improves on 9 out of 10 individual benchmark entries (the sole exception being MMLU for MCPT, where MCPT is 41.95% vs. MRLP at 56.14%, indicating RLP actually improves substantially here; the closest-to-flat comparison is GPQA@1[4] where MRLP at 27.02% is slightly below Mbase at 27.52%).

**Reasoning-heavy benchmarks benefit most.** AIME25 (a challenging competition math benchmark) sees MRLP at 5.02% vs. Mbase at 2.25% (2.2× improvement) and MCPT at 3.96%. MMLU-Pro improves from 28.17% (Mbase) and 27.81% (MCPT) to 34.62% (MRLP). GSM8K jumps from 54.16% (Mbase) to 74.48% (MRLP), a gain of over 20 percentage points. In contrast, GPQA shows more modest gains (25.25% → 28.28% for the standard version, and 27.52% → 27.02% for Pass@1). This pattern — large gains on structured math reasoning, moderate gains on graduate-level science — suggests RLP induces reasoning capabilities that transfer most effectively to domains where chain-of-thought directly reduces predictive uncertainty.

**Science reasoning sees the largest relative improvement.** The Science Avg for MRLP is 39.68% vs. 34.50% for Mbase (15% relative gain) and 32.01% for MCPT (24% relative gain, since MCPT actually degrades on science relative to the base model). The Science Avg@1[4] follows a similar pattern: 36.67% for MRLP vs. 32.11% for Mbase and 29.79% for MCPT. This is notable because it demonstrates that RLP's benefits are not limited to math — the information-gain objective transfers to scientific reasoning tasks that require integrating world knowledge with logical inference, exactly the kind of "parallel integration of input with prior knowledge" that the paper's introduction positions as missing from standard pretraining.

---

#### Post-Training Compounding Effects

**Headline: RLP's advantages survive and compound after strong post-training.** The shaded columns in Table 1 show that when all models undergo identical SFT (on OpenThoughts) + RLVR (on MATH) post-training, the ranking established during pretraining persists and the absolute gap widens in some cases. MRLP+Post achieves Overall 42.51% vs. 39.90% for MCPT+Post — a 6.5% relative margin. This is despite the fact that post-training adds roughly 9 points to Mbase (30.32% → 39.34%) and roughly 9 points to MCPT (30.85% → 39.90%), while adding roughly 6.5 points to MRLP (36.03% → 42.51%). The smaller absolute gain for RLP from post-training is not a sign of diminishing returns — rather, it reflects the higher starting point, with the final absolute performance remaining clearly superior.

**Individual benchmarks show compounding effects.** AIME25 post-training: MRLP+Post at 7.05% vs. MCPT+Post at 5.89% vs. Mbase+Post at 5.32%. The gap between RLP and CPT actually widens from 1.06 points pre-post-training to 1.16 points post-post-training. MMLU-Pro: MRLP+Post at 42.40% vs. MCPT+Post at 39.92% vs. Mbase+Post at 37.85%. The Science Avg@1[4] shows MRLP+Post at 45.76% vs. MCPT+Post at 42.34% — a 3.4 point gap. These results directly refute the concern that post-training would wash out pretraining gains by teaching the same reasoning skills through a different mechanism. Instead, RLP establishes foundations that post-training builds upon additively.

**The MCPT baseline remains weak on science even after post-training.** Notably, MCPT+Post's Science Avg is 42.73% compared to MRLP+Post's 45.74% — a 3-point gap. The base MCPT actually underperformed Mbase on science (32.01% vs. 34.50%), and while post-training largely closes the gap to Mbase+Post (42.73% vs. 42.38%), it does not catch up to RLP. This suggests that standard next-token pretraining on reasoning data may even slightly degrade scientific reasoning (perhaps due to distribution shift toward math-heavy reasoning patterns), while RLP's objective extracts transferable reasoning signal without this degradation.

---

#### Scaling to Larger Models and Architectures

**Headline: RLP scales effectively to a 12B hybrid architecture with only 0.125% additional data.** Table 2 reports results on NEMOTRON-NANO-12B-V2, a hybrid Mamba-Transformer model. The base model (Mbase) is an intermediate checkpoint at 19.8T tokens; RLP is applied for only 250M tokens (0.125% of the full pretraining budget); the baseline Mbase continues to 20T tokens. The Overall average jumps from 42.81% to 61.32% — a 43% relative improvement. This is remarkable both for the magnitude of the gain and the extreme data efficiency: 250M tokens represents less than 0.13% of the total training data, yet produces a transformation in reasoning capabilities.

**Science reasoning shows the most dramatic scaling.** The Science Avg for MRLP reaches 57.26% vs. 34.51% for Mbase — a 66% relative improvement. The Science Avg@1[4] goes from 32.54% to 61.37%, nearly doubling. Individual benchmarks: MMLU jumps from 54.12% to 78.76% (+24.6 points); MMLU-Pro from 24.16% to 53.13% (+29 points); GPQA from 25.25% to 39.90% (+14.7 points). The math benchmarks also improve substantially: GSM8K from 72.31% to 85.98%, Minerva from 22.61% to 39.48%. Interestingly, MATH500 shows a slight decrease (79.95% → 78.68%), which the paper does not comment on — this could be noise, or could reflect a real tradeoff where the base model was already strong on MATH500 and the RLP training shifted its distribution slightly.

**Post-training preserves the gap.** After identical SFT + RLVR, MRLP+Post achieves Overall 68.09% vs. Mbase+Post at 65.31% — a 4.3% relative margin that, while narrower than the pre-post-training gap, is still substantial in absolute terms (2.78 percentage points). The Science Avg post-training: 64.52% for RLP vs. 58.91% for the baseline. The pattern mirrors the QWEN3-1.7B-BASE results: RLP establishes a higher floor, and post-training lifts all models while preserving the RLP advantage.

**Scaling to 14B parameters confirms the trend.** Appendix 12, Table S.3 reports results on QWEN3-14B-BASE trained with RLP on the general pretraining corpus (DPT) for 1B tokens. The Overall average improves from 60.66% to 65.00%, with Science Avg improving from 60.07% to 64.76% and Science Avg@1[4] from 56.14% to 61.92%. The gains at 14B are proportionally smaller than at 1.7B (7% relative improvement vs. 19%), which is consistent with the expectation that larger base models already possess stronger latent reasoning capabilities — there is less room for RLP to unlock capabilities the model doesn't already have. Nevertheless, the consistent positive trend across 1.7B, 12B, and 14B demonstrates that the method scales.

---

#### Comparison with Reinforcement Pre-Training (RPT)

**Headline: RLP outperforms RPT under matched conditions, with the gap widening under FLOP-matched comparison.** Table 3 presents the head-to-head comparison between RLP and RPT (Dong et al., 2025) using QWEN3-1.7B-BASE, conducted under two settings:

**Token-matched setting:** Both models are trained on Omni-MATH for one epoch using the same number of input tokens. MRLP achieves Math Avg 49.62% vs. MRPT 47.50% (+4.5% relative), Science Avg 37.07% vs. 35.88% (+3.3% relative), and Overall Avg 43.35% vs. 41.69% (+4.0% relative). The improvement is consistent across domains.

**FLOP-matched setting:** Both models are trained on Nemotron-Crossthink for one epoch with the same number of target tokens for which reward is applied. Here, the gap widens considerably: MRLP achieves Overall Avg 42.86% vs. MRPT 35.68% — a 20.1% relative improvement. Math Avg: 45.95% vs. 36.66% (+25.3% relative). Science Avg: 38.76% vs. 34.38% (+12.7% relative). The larger gap under FLOP-matched conditions suggests that RLP's dense, continuous reward extracts more learning per unit of computation than RPT's sparse, binary signal, even when the number of reward applications is equalized.

**Important caveat on the comparison:** The paper notes that in the token-matched setting, "the number of target tokens for which reward is applied is substantially larger for RPT" because RPT applies reward to multiple pre-selected tokens per document while RLP applies reward to only one randomly selected token per document. This means the token-matched comparison is actually **favorable to RPT** in terms of the number of reward signals — yet RLP still outperforms it. The FLOP-matched comparison corrects for this by equalizing the number of reward applications, revealing RLP's stronger per-signal efficiency.

---

#### RLP Across Diverse Corpora

**Headline: RLP produces consistent gains across both curated reasoning datasets and general-purpose pretraining corpora.** Table 4 reports results from applying RLP to QWEN3-1.7B-BASE for 200 steps (170M input tokens) across six distinct datasets:

- **SFT-style reasoning corpora:** OmniMath, OpenThoughts, Nemotron-Crossthink
- **General-purpose corpora:** Academic papers (ACAD), Math textbooks (Math-Text), Web-Crawl data

The Overall average for Mbase is 34.19%. Across all six RLP-trained variants, the Overall average ranges from 41.43% (OmniMath) to 43.36% (Nemotron-Crossthink) — a consistent 21–27% relative improvement regardless of corpus type. Specifically: OmniMath 41.43%, OpenThoughts 40.66%, Nemotron-Crossthink 43.36%, ACAD 41.71%, Math-Text 41.62%, Web-Crawl 42.13%.

**General corpora are competitive with curated reasoning data.** The Web-Crawl corpus produces an Overall average of 42.13%, which is within 1.2 points of the best SFT-style corpus (Nemotron-Crossthink at 43.36%). On math specifically, Web-Crawl achieves Math Avg 48.87% vs. Nemotron-Crossthink 49.76% — less than one point difference. This is a significant finding because it demonstrates that RLP does not require carefully curated reasoning traces to induce reasoning capabilities. Even open-ended web text contains latent reasoning structure that the information-gain objective can exploit. The paper emphasizes: "Even on purely non-reasoning general corpora such as web-crawl, RLP extracts a reasoning signal that scales with data diversity."

**FLOP-matched comparison confirms gains are from objective, not compute.** Table 4 also shows the MCPT baselines: when trained on 170M tokens of Nemotron-Crossthink, MCPT achieves Overall 35.01% vs. MRLP's 43.36% (+8.4 points). More tellingly, when MCPT is given a FLOP-matched budget of 6B tokens (35× more data), it achieves Overall 38.04% — still 5.3 points below MRLP on 170M tokens. Similarly, MCPT on the general pretraining data mix (DPT) for 1B tokens reaches Overall 35.60%, while MRLP on the same data achieves 40.90%. These comparisons isolate the effect of the training objective: standard next-token prediction simply cannot extract the same reasoning improvements from the data that RLP's information-gain reward can, even when given an order of magnitude more data to compensate for the compute difference.

**Domain mixing helps rather than hurts.** Appendix 12, Table S.2 ablates the effect of training on math-only, science-only, or combined data from Nemotron-Crossthink. Training on math alone yields Math Avg 48.23% but Science Avg 41.64%; training on science alone yields Math Avg 49.17% but Science Avg 39.65%; combining both domains yields Math Avg 49.76% and Science Avg 42.54% — the best overall average of 43.36%. This demonstrates that RLP benefits from multi-domain data, with diverse reasoning styles reinforcing one another rather than diluting within-domain performance.

---

#### Very Early Application of RLP

**Headline: RLP is effective even when applied to models at only 20% of the standard pretraining budget.** Appendix 12, Table S.4 reports an experiment where RLP is applied to NEMOTRON-NANO-12B-V2 at only 4T tokens of pretraining (about 20% of the full 20T budget), using 1B tokens of RLP training on the DPT corpus. The Overall average more than doubles from 12.05% to 24.08%. Math Avg jumps from 21.93% to 50.14% — a 2.3× improvement. Individual benchmarks show dramatic gains: GSM8K from 29.56% to 81.42%, MATH500 from 30.15% to 62.38%. Science improvements are more modest: Science Avg from 8.54% to 10.14%, though Science Avg@1[4] improves from 5.69% to 11.96%. While the absolute numbers are lower than when RLP is applied later in training (as in Table 2), the relative gains are actually larger — suggesting that RLP can meaningfully shape reasoning capabilities even when the model's knowledge base is far from complete.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Number of rollouts (G):** More rollouts improve performance up to G=16, with diminishing returns beyond. Figure 2a and Table S.1 report: G=4 yields Overall 41.38%, G=8 yields 41.95%, G=16 yields 42.17% (the peak), and G=32 yields 41.75% (a slight decrease). The drop at G=32 is driven mainly by GPQA (-2.13 points), while other tasks remain roughly flat. This suggests that for most tasks, 16 rollouts provides sufficient variance reduction in the group-relative advantage estimator, and additional rollouts may introduce noise from the increased number of low-quality thoughts in the group. All subsequent experiments use G=16.

**Completion length (thought length):** This is the single most impactful hyperparameter, showing a regime change between 512 and 1024 tokens. Figure 2b and Table S.1 report: at length 64, Overall is only 11.50% with Math Avg at 1.12% — very short thoughts are essentially useless. At length 512, Overall reaches 24.65%. At length 1024, there is a dramatic jump to Overall 38.89% (Math Avg 45.65%). At length 2048 (the default), Overall reaches 42.17% (Math Avg 48.06%, Science Avg 36.29%). Extending to 4096 yields only marginal improvement (Overall 42.21%, +0.04 points) at roughly twice the thought generation cost. The paper defaults to 2048 as the cost-effectiveness sweet spot. The large jump between 512 and 1024 suggests that a minimum thought length is necessary for the model to engage in meaningful multi-step reasoning — below this threshold, thoughts are too truncated to provide useful information gain.

**KL coefficient (β):** Adding a token-level KL penalty toward a fixed reference policy does not help and slightly hurts performance. Figure 2c and Table S.1 report: β=0 (no KL) yields Overall 42.17%; β=10^{-4} yields 41.35% (-0.82); β=10^{-3} yields 41.44% (-0.73). There are isolated improvements (MMLU-Pro +1.43 at β=10^{-4}, AMC23 +1.88 at β=10^{-3}), but these are offset by broader declines (GSM8K -1.26 and -2.82). The KL term also increases memory usage and step time. This negative result is informative: it suggests that the EMA baseline already provides sufficient regularization against policy collapse, and adding an explicit KL penalty to a static reference interferes with the self-improving dynamics. All subsequent experiments use β=0.

**EMA decay rate (τ):** Performance follows a bell-shaped curve peaking at τ=0.999. Appendix 12, Table S.6 reports: τ=0.99 yields Overall 38.82%; τ=0.995 yields 39.21%; τ=0.999 yields 39.54% (the peak, tested at slightly different conditions than the main experiments, hence the lower absolute numbers); τ=0.9995 yields 39.20%. The paper does not report values outside this range. The results confirm the theoretical analysis: a baseline that updates too quickly (low τ) makes the comparison too easy and produces a weak reward signal; a baseline that updates too slowly (high τ) becomes stale and loses discriminative power. The 0.999 value balances currency and lag.

**Context length for CPT baselines:** Increasing the CPT context length from 8K to 32K does not improve and may slightly degrade performance on the DPT corpus. Appendix 12, Table S.5 shows MCPT(8K) at Overall 32.90% vs. MCPT(32K) at 30.83% — a 2-point drop. Individual benchmarks mostly decline (GSM8K drops from 72.85% to 60.44%, MATH500 from 57.52% to 51.80%), with a few exceptions (MMLU@1[4] slightly up from 40.00% to 40.55%). This validates the choice of 8K context for the main CPT baselines and suggests that the DPT corpus does not contain long-range dependencies that would benefit from extended context, while the longer context may interfere with optimization dynamics.

**Data domain mixing (math vs. science vs. combined):** Training RLP on combined math+science data produces the best overall results, with no degradation on individual domains relative to single-domain training. Appendix 12, Table S.2: math-only training yields Math Avg 48.23% and Science Avg 41.64% (Overall 42.21%); science-only training yields Math Avg 49.17% and Science Avg 39.65% (Overall 42.36%); combined training yields Math Avg 49.76% and Science Avg 42.54% (Overall 43.36%). The combined training actually improves math performance relative to math-only (+1.53 points) while substantially improving science relative to science-only (+2.89 points). This cross-domain transfer is a strong signal that RLP learns general reasoning patterns rather than domain-specific heuristics.

**Model scale:** RLP produces consistent gains across three model sizes, with proportionally larger gains on smaller models. QWEN3-1.7B-BASE: Overall from 34.19% to 43.36% on Nemotron-Crossthink (+27% relative). NEMOTRON-NANO-12B-V2: Overall from 42.81% to 61.32% (+43% relative). QWEN3-14B-BASE: Overall from 60.66% to 65.00% (+7% relative, Table S.3). The decreasing relative gain with scale is expected: larger models already have stronger baseline reasoning from standard pretraining, leaving less room for RLP to add value. The fact that even at 14B parameters RLP produces a non-trivial improvement (+4.34 points Overall) on top of an already strong baseline suggests the method continues to be beneficial at scale.

**Very early checkpoint (4T tokens for 12B model):** RLP is effective even at early pretraining stages. Table S.4: applied to NEMOTRON-NANO-12B-V2 at 4T tokens (20% of full budget), RLP improves Overall from 12.05% to 24.08% with only 1B tokens. The relative gain (2×) is larger than at later stages, but absolute performance remains far below the later-checkpoint results, confirming that RLP amplifies existing knowledge rather than creating it from nothing. This clarifies a boundary condition: RLP can accelerate reasoning development during pretraining, but cannot substitute for the knowledge acquisition that occurs during the bulk of standard pretraining.

**Wall-clock time.** Appendix 12, Table S.7 reports that RLP training is approximately 2.25× slower per step than SFT/CPT (12.47s vs. 5.54s per step on 32 H100 GPUs with batch size 512 and G=16). However, the paper argues this must be viewed in context: RLP achieves an Overall average of 43.36% on 170M tokens, while the FLOP-matched CPT baseline required 6B tokens (35× more) to reach only 38.04%. The per-step cost is higher, but the data efficiency gains more than compensate — the total compute to reach a given performance level is substantially lower with RLP.

**Perplexity after RLP training.** Appendix 12, Table S.8 confirms that RLP does not degrade standard language modeling quality; it improves it. On Nemotron-Crossthink, perplexity drops from 2.91 (Mbase) to 2.36 (MRLP). On Wikitext-103, perplexity drops from 5.83 to 4.48. This is theoretically expected (Proposition 1: maximizing information-gain reward minimizes cross-entropy), but important to verify empirically because it demonstrates that RLP does not trade off general language quality for reasoning ability — the model becomes better at next-token prediction across both reasoning-heavy and general-domain text.

### Critical Assessment

The experimental results broadly support the paper's central claims, but several important qualifications and gaps merit attention.

**Claim: RLP "lifts the overall average across an eight-benchmark math-and-science suite by 19%" over the base model and "by 17%" over a compute-matched CPT baseline.** This claim is well-supported by Table 1, with the specific numbers being 30.32% → 36.03% (19% relative) for Mbase and 30.85% → 36.03% (17% relative) for MCPT. However, the claim should be interpreted carefully: the 19% refers to relative improvement, not absolute percentage points — the absolute gain is 5.7 percentage points. The relative framing makes the improvement sound more dramatic than the absolute numbers suggest, and the practical significance of a 5.7-point Overall improvement depends on the application context. Additionally, the MCPT baseline at 30.85% is actually worse than Mbase at 30.32% on some metrics (Science Avg: 32.01% vs. 34.50%), which raises questions about whether the CPT comparison is truly "matched" in a meaningful sense — the baseline may be underperforming due to optimization issues rather than representing the best that standard pretraining can achieve with that data.

**Claim: "With identical post-training, the gains compound."** Table 1 supports that RLP maintains its advantage after post-training (Overall: 42.51% vs. 39.90% for MCPT+Post and 39.34% for Mbase+Post), which is a genuine and important finding. However, the word "compound" may overstate the case — the absolute gap narrows slightly in some cases. Pre-post-training, MRLP leads MCPT by 5.18 points (36.03% - 30.85%); post-post-training, the lead is 2.61 points (42.51% - 39.90%). This is still a clear margin but it is smaller, not larger, in absolute terms. The relative gap also narrows from 17% to 6.5%. The gains persist — they are not washed out — but the claim of "compounding" would require the gap to widen, which generally does not happen. The exception is AIME25, where the gap widens from 1.06 to 1.16 points.

**Claim: RLP scales to a 12B hybrid model where it "achieves a staggering 35% relative improvement."** Table 2 shows Overall 42.81% → 61.32% — a 43% relative improvement, actually higher than the 35% claimed in the introduction. This is genuinely impressive, but several contextual factors matter. First, the baseline Mbase was trained on 19.8T tokens while MRLP used 250M additional tokens — the comparison is not purely about the objective change, since the RLP model has seen more total tokens. The baseline Mbase continues to 20T tokens, so the token difference is small (200M tokens), but the compute difference is larger due to rollout costs. Second, the hybrid architecture (Mamba-Transformer) may benefit differently from RLP than pure Transformers — the paper does not ablate architecture to determine whether the gain magnitude is architecture-specific. Third, the extremely small data budget (0.125% of pretraining) producing such large gains raises the question of whether the 19.8T checkpoint was near saturation on the standard objective, making any intervention appear disproportionately effective — a model earlier in training might have shown smaller relative gains from RLP but larger gains from continued standard pretraining. The fact that Table S.4 shows even larger relative gains at the 4T checkpoint (100% improvement) argues against this interpretation, but the absolute numbers are so low (12.05% → 24.08%) that the practical significance is limited.

**Claim: RLP "successfully extracts a powerful reasoning signal from diverse, general-purpose web corpora" (Table 4).** The evidence for domain-agnostic transfer is strong: Web-Crawl achieves Overall 42.13% vs. Nemotron-Crossthink at 43.36%, a difference of only 1.2 points. However, the paper does not analyze *what* in the web-crawl data provides the reasoning signal. It is possible that web-crawl contains substantial math and science content (Wikipedia articles, educational pages, forum discussions) that essentially makes it a noisy version of the curated datasets. Without a content analysis of the specific documents where RLP reward was high vs. low, the claim of "domain-agnostic" reasoning extraction rests on the assumption that web-crawl is genuinely diverse — which it is, but the relevant reasoning signal may be concentrated in a subset of the data. The paper also does not compare RLP on web-crawl to CPT on web-crawl for this specific ablation (the CPT comparison in Table 4 uses Nemotron-Crossthink and DPT, not the individual general corpora), so we cannot confirm that the gains on web-crawl are specifically due to the objective rather than the data.

**The FLOP-matched comparison with CPT (170M vs. 6B tokens) is a strong demonstration, but has a subtle asymmetry.** RLP on 170M tokens includes both the input tokens and the generated thought tokens in its FLOP calculation. CPT on 6B tokens includes only input tokens (since CPT doesn't generate thoughts). The FLOP accounting (Appendix 12) equates one generated token to one trained token in terms of FLOPs, but the paper acknowledges this is conservative: "The FLOPs of a forward pass for generating one token is approximately 2N. The cost of training on one token (forward + backward) is approximately 6N. By equating one generated token to one trained token in our FLOP calculation, we are effectively penalizing RLP (counting generation as 3× more expensive than it theoretically is in terms of FLOPs)." This means the 35× data advantage for CPT might actually underrepresent the compute advantage — if generation is cheaper than training, the FLOP-matched comparison could be even more favorable to RLP. This does not weaken the paper's claim, but it means the exact efficiency ratio is uncertain.

**Missing baselines and experiments that would strengthen the paper:** (1) A comparison against simply training on the same data with standard NTP loss but using chain-of-thought annotations (i.e., SFT on reasoning traces during pretraining) — this would test whether the RLP objective is doing something beyond exposing the model to the text that chain-of-thought generates. (2) An experiment that gradually phases out RLP and transitions to standard NTP to see if the reasoning behavior persists without continued reinforcement — this would test whether RLP permanently changes the model's behavior or requires the objective to be maintained. (3) Ablation of the EMA baseline against alternative counterfactuals (e.g., a frozen snapshot, a separate small model, or majority voting across thoughts) to confirm that the EMA design is specifically responsible for training stability. (4) Evaluation on a broader set of tasks beyond math and science — the paper's title and framing suggest a general pretraining objective, but all evaluation benchmarks are math and science, leaving open the question of whether RLP improves reasoning in domains like code generation, logical reasoning, or natural language inference. (5) Direct measurement of whether the model actually uses chain-of-thought at inference time after post-training, or whether the RLP-induced capabilities manifest as improved zero-shot or few-shot performance without explicit chain-of-thought generation — the paper does not analyze inference-time behavior.

**Statistical reliability:** The test sets for individual benchmarks range from a few hundred to a few thousand questions. The paper reports Pass@1 averaged over multiple runs, which reduces sampling variance from stochastic decoding, but does not report confidence intervals or conduct significance tests. The AIME25 benchmark in Table 1 has only 2.25% to 7.05% accuracy across conditions, which on a small test set could mean differences of a handful of correct answers. The overall averages aggregate across benchmarks with different sizes and characteristics, making the aggregate sensitive to benchmark selection. The consistency of the pattern across benchmarks and model sizes provides informal robustness, but formal statistical claims are not supported.

**Contamination concerns:** The paper uses the MATH dataset for RLVR post-training, which overlaps with the MATH500 evaluation benchmark. This is standard practice (the split between training and test sets is maintained), but the paper does not discuss potential contamination effects or verify that gains on MATH500 are not partially attributable to memorization from the RLVR phase. The RLP training itself uses general corpora that may include evaluation benchmark content (web-crawl could contain MATH problems), and the paper does not perform decontamination analysis.

In summary, the experimental evidence strongly supports the core claim that RLP induces reasoning capabilities during pretraining that standard NTP does not, with the gains persisting after post-training and generalizing across diverse corpora. The scaling results are compelling and the ablation studies are thorough for the chosen hyperparameters. The primary limitations are the narrow evaluation domain (math and science only), the lack of formal statistical testing, and the absence of analysis of *how* RLP changes model behavior at inference time. The paper demonstrates that RLP works; it does not demonstrate the mechanisms by which it works or the boundaries of its applicability beyond math and science.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Evaluation Is Confined to Math and Science Benchmarks, Leaving the Claim of "General Reasoning" Unverified

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper frames RLP as a "domain-agnostic pretraining augmentation" and claims it "reframes reinforcement learning for reasoning as a pretraining objective on ordinary text, bridging the gap between next-token prediction and the emergence of useful chain-of-thought reasoning" (Section 1). Yet every evaluation benchmark — AIME25, MATH500, GSM8K, AMC23, Minerva, MMLU, MMLU-Pro, GPQA — falls within math and science. The paper provides no evidence that RLP improves reasoning in code generation, logical deduction, natural language inference, multi-hop question answering, summarization, or any domain outside STEM problem-solving. The word "reasoning" is used throughout to describe the paper's contribution, but the empirical support covers only a specific subset of reasoning tasks.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to apply RLP to a general-domain language model cannot infer from the current results whether the method improves performance on non-STEM tasks. It is possible — and consistent with the evidence — that RLP specifically amplifies mathematical and scientific reasoning (where chain-of-thought reliably reduces predictive uncertainty) but provides negligible benefit or even harm for tasks where the relationship between deliberation and next-token prediction is weaker (e.g., creative writing, dialogue, sentiment analysis). The finding that RLP works on web-crawl data (Table 4, Web-Crawl achieves Overall 42.13% vs. Nemotron-Crossthink 43.36%) is evaluated only on the same math-and-science benchmarks — we learn that web-crawl training transfers to math tests, not that RLP produces a generally more capable language model. The paper's title ("Reinforcement as a Pretraining Objective") and framing imply generality that the experiments do not establish.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Every table in the main results (Tables 1–4) and every ablation (Figure 2, Tables S.1–S.6) reports only math and science accuracy. The paper does not evaluate on a single non-STEM benchmark (e.g., HellaSwag, ARC, HumanEval, TruthfulQA, MMLU non-STEM subsets). The closest evidence for generalization is the positive transfer from science-only training to math performance (Appendix 12, Table S.2: science-only RLP training yields Math Avg 49.17%), and vice versa (math-only training yields Science Avg 41.64%), which demonstrates cross-domain transfer within STEM but not beyond it.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation and does not suggest evaluating on non-STEM benchmarks as future work. The omission is structural: the paper's evaluation framework (Section 3.1) defines only Math Avg and Science Avg aggregates, with no provision for other reasoning domains. A practitioner must assume — without evidence — that the benefits extend to their domain of interest.

### Difficulty Estimation Through 16 Rollouts Per Position Adds a ~2.25× Per-Step Overhead, and the Headline Efficiency Numbers Do Not Amortize This Cost Against the Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** RLP requires sampling G=16 thoughts of length 2048 tokens per training position. Appendix 12, Table S.7 reports that this makes RLP training approximately 2.25× slower per step than standard SFT/CPT (12.47s vs. 5.54s per step on 32 H100 GPUs). The paper's headline efficiency claim — that RLP on 170M tokens outperforms CPT on 6B tokens (a 35× data advantage) — compares the total FLOPs of the two approaches and finds RLP more compute-efficient. However, this comparison uses a specific FLOP accounting that the paper acknowledges is conservative: "By equating one generated token to one trained token in our FLOP calculation, we are effectively penalizing RLP (counting generation as 3× more expensive than it theoretically is in terms of FLOPs)" (Appendix 12). The actual wall-clock overhead of autoregressive thought generation may be larger than the FLOP ratio suggests because autoregressive generation is memory-bandwidth-bound in a way that parallel training is not.

**The consequence.** The 2.25× per-step slowdown means that, for a fixed wall-clock training budget, RLP processes fewer unique documents and fewer training positions than standard pretraining. The paper claims RLP extracts more learning per FLOP (Table 4: RLP on 170M tokens beats CPT on 6B tokens), but this claim depends on the FLOP-to-performance conversion ratio being stable across the two methods. If autoregressive generation has additional hardware inefficiencies (lower GPU utilization, pipeline bubbles, KV-cache management overhead), the wall-clock efficiency gap may be larger than the FLOP ratio implies. Furthermore, the paper does not report total wall-clock time for the main experiments, making it difficult for practitioners to estimate the real cost of applying RLP to their training pipelines. A team with a fixed GPU-hour budget needs to know: if I can either run RLP for X hours or standard pretraining for X hours on Y× more data, which produces a better model? The paper's FLOP-matched comparison addresses this in principle, but the 2.25× overhead number is reported only for a specific hardware configuration and may not generalize.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Appendix 12, Table S.7 provides the per-step timing comparison. The FLOP accounting methodology is described in Appendix 12: `T_flop = (n × l_seq × bs × iters) + T_inp`. The paper notes that "the purpose of the baseline is to compare compute efficiency" and that RLP's per-step cost is offset by drastically higher data efficiency, but stops short of a comprehensive cost-benefit analysis that includes wall-clock time, memory usage, and hardware utilization.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed. The paper provides the wall-clock numbers and acknowledges the overhead, but does not integrate this into the main narrative or adjust headline claims. The 4× or 35× data efficiency claims are all computed in terms of input tokens or FLOPs, not wall-clock time. A practitioner reading the abstract or introduction would not learn that RLP training is 2.25× slower per step until consulting the appendix. The paper does not suggest optimizations (e.g., reducing G for later training stages, using speculative decoding for thought generation, or applying RLP to fewer positions) that could reduce the overhead.

### The Method Is Only Applied to One Randomly Selected Token Per Document, Which Leaves the Vast Majority of Training Positions Without Explicit Reasoning Incentive

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper states in Section 3: "theoretically RLP can be applied for every token in a document but in our experiments we randomly select one token per document. Hence, the number of tokens for which the reward is applied is far less than 1B." This is a practical concession: applying RLP at every position would multiply the already-expensive thought generation cost by the average document length (hundreds or thousands of tokens), making training infeasible. However, this design choice means that for a typical document of length L, only 1/L of the model's training positions receive any explicit reasoning incentive — the other (L-1)/L positions are trained with whatever implicit benefit the shared parameters carry over from the RLP-updated thought policy.

**The consequence.** The model learns to think at only a tiny fraction of the positions it encounters during training. It is unclear whether the reasoning behavior generalizes to the un-rewarded positions — does the model learn to engage chain-of-thought before predicting any token, or only before predicting tokens at positions that happen to resemble the randomly selected ones? Since the selection is random, the model should, in expectation, learn to think at all position types equally. But the sparsity of the signal (one reward per document) means the policy gradient is extremely noisy at the per-position level, and the model may converge to a strategy where it generates thoughts that are generically useful (improving prediction on average across many position types) rather than thoughts that are precisely tailored to the specific prediction task at each position. The paper does not analyze whether thought quality varies by position within a document or whether some position types benefit more from thinking than others.

Furthermore, the paper reports in Appendix 12 that RLP models have lower perplexity on both reasoning and general-domain text (Table S.8: Nemotron-Crossthink PPL 2.91 → 2.36, Wikitext-103 PPL 5.83 → 4.48), which suggests the benefits do transfer to un-rewarded positions. But perplexity measures average next-token prediction quality, not the presence or quality of chain-of-thought reasoning — the model could achieve lower perplexity through better associative pattern matching without engaging in explicit deliberation at most positions. The paper provides no analysis of inference-time thought generation behavior to determine whether the model actually uses chain-of-thought at positions it was not explicitly trained to think at.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 3 notes the one-token-per-document design choice. Appendix 12, Table S.8 provides perplexity evidence of generalization. Beyond this, the paper does not ablate the sampling rate (e.g., 1 vs. 5 vs. all tokens per document) or analyze whether the fraction of positions receiving RLP affects downstream reasoning performance.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the one-token-per-document choice as a practical necessity rather than a limitation and does not discuss its implications for the generality of learned reasoning behavior. The paper does not measure whether reasoning is actually deployed at inference time, how often the model generates internal thoughts, or whether thought quality varies by position. These are significant open questions for anyone seeking to understand what RLP actually teaches the model to do.

### The FLOP-Matched CPT Baselines May Underrepresent the Potential of Standard Pretraining Because They Use Identical Hyperparameters and Suboptimal Context Lengths

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central empirical argument — that RLP's gains stem from the objective, not the data or compute — rests on comparisons against continuous pretraining baselines (MCPT). The paper states in Appendix 12 that "we continue pretraining on our pretraining mixture (DPT), which consists almost entirely of relatively short documents without long-range dependencies" using an 8K context length. When a 32K context length is tested, performance degrades (Table S.5: MCPT(32K) Overall 30.83% vs. MCPT(8K) 32.90%), which the paper attributes to the short-document nature of the corpus. However, the QWEN3 model used for these experiments was originally pretrained with a 32K context window (per the Qwen3 technical report cited in the paper), raising the question of whether the 8K context length used for CPT is well-matched to the model's pretrained position embeddings and attention patterns.

**The consequence.** If the CPT baselines are suboptimally configured — using a context length that differs from the model's pretraining regime, or hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, optimizer settings) that are not tuned for the specific dataset — then the gap between RLP and CPT may partially reflect baseline weakness rather than RLP strength. The paper's strongest efficiency claim — that RLP on 170M tokens outperforms CPT on 6B tokens (35× more data) — depends on the CPT baseline being a fair representation of what standard next-token prediction can achieve. If CPT with better-tuned hyperparameters or a context length that matches the model's pretraining could close some of this gap, the claimed efficiency advantage would be overstated.

A subtler concern: the paper compares RLP (which uses a specific prompt template to elicit chain-of-thought, specified in Appendix 10) against CPT (which uses no prompt). The prompt template itself — "You are a continuation-and-reasoning assistant..." — may induce behavior in the base model that, combined with standard NTP training, partially replicates RLP's benefits. The paper does not test CPT with the same prompt template to control for this effect.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The context length ablation (Table S.5) shows 8K outperforming 32K, which the paper interprets as validating the choice. However, this only shows that 32K is worse, not that 8K is optimal — values between 8K and 32K, or below 8K, were not tested. The CPT hyperparameters are described in Appendix 10 (learning rate 1e-6, batch size 6M tokens, context length 8192) but no hyperparameter sweep is reported for CPT, making it unclear whether these values are optimal for the CPT task or inherited from RLP's settings. The paper acknowledges the context length question (Appendix 12) but frames it as a defense of the 8K choice rather than a limitation.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed. The paper runs two CPT variants (170M tokens and 6B tokens) and a context-length comparison, which is more thorough than many comparable works. However, the lack of learning rate or batch size sweeps for CPT, and the absence of a CPT+prompt ablation, means the baseline strength is assumed rather than demonstrated. A practitioner comparing RLP against their own CPT pipeline would need to verify that their CPT configuration matches or exceeds the paper's before accepting the claimed efficiency ratios.

### The Hardest Reasoning Tasks Show Only Modest Gains or Remain Near-Floor, Suggesting RLP Amplifies Existing Capability Rather Than Creating It from Nothing

**The assumption or constraint.** RLP defines reward as information gain for next-token prediction: a thought is reinforced if it increases the probability of the correct token relative to not thinking. This means the reward signal is fundamentally bounded by the model's existing predictive capability: if the base model assigns near-zero probability to the correct token even after thinking (because it lacks the necessary knowledge or reasoning capacity), the reward will be negative regardless of thought quality, providing no positive reinforcement signal. The paper does not explicitly state this as a limitation, but it is a direct consequence of the reward definition.

**The consequence.** On the hardest benchmarks, RLP's improvements are limited. AIME25 (a challenging competition math benchmark) improves from 2.25% to 5.02% for QWEN3-1.7B-BASE (Table 1) — a doubling, but still near-floor absolute performance. On GPQA, a graduate-level science benchmark, MRLP achieves 28.28% vs. Mbase 25.25% (Table 1) — a 3-point gain. On the 12B model (Table 2), AMC23 actually *decreases* from 70.63% (Mbase) to 57.19% (MRLP), which the paper does not comment on. This pattern — large relative gains on easy-to-medium tasks, modest or negative gains on the hardest tasks — mirrors the difficulty-dependent behavior documented in prior test-time compute scaling work: reinforcement and search help most when the model already has non-trivial capability on the task, and provide diminishing or negative returns near the capability frontier.

For a practitioner, this means RLP should not be expected to enable a model to solve problems that are fundamentally beyond its knowledge or representational capacity. RLP can teach the model to use its existing knowledge more effectively through deliberation, but it cannot inject new knowledge or fundamentally restructure the model's representations to enable qualitatively different reasoning. The 12B model's AMC23 regression is particularly concerning — it suggests RLP can, in some cases, interfere with existing capabilities, possibly by redirecting model capacity toward the reasoning patterns that are rewarded (improving next-token prediction generally) at the expense of specialized problem-solving strategies.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 shows AIME25 and GPQA as the benchmarks with the smallest absolute gains. Table 2 shows AMC23 regression (70.63% → 57.19%). The paper does not analyze these cases or discuss difficulty-dependent efficacy. The ablation on completion length (Figure 2b, Table S.1) shows that very short thoughts (64–256 tokens) produce near-zero math performance (Math Avg 1.12–5.02%), confirming that the method only works when the model can generate sufficiently elaborate reasoning — but this is a different point about thought quality rather than task difficulty.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the difficulty-dependent nature of its gains, does not analyze failure cases on hard benchmarks, and does not characterize the conditions under which RLP helps vs. hurts. The AMC23 regression in Table 2 is not mentioned in the main text. For a method presented as a general pretraining augmentation, the lack of boundary-condition analysis is a significant gap — practitioners need to know when RLP is likely to help and when it might harm existing capabilities, but the paper provides no guidance on this question.
