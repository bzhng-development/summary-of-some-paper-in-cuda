# Democratizing Reasoning Ability: Tailored Learning from Large Language Model

**ArXiv:** [2310.13332](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13332)

## 🎯 Pitch

A 6B-parameter model can solve only 15.6% of grade-school math problems, but when a large language model tutor receives feedback on exactly where the student struggles and then provides customized corrections—plus the student learns to distinguish its own correct vs. incorrect reasoning—that jumps to 33.1%, surpassing static distillation approaches. The key insight: treating the teacher model as an interactive coach rather than a one-shot annotator unlocks reasoning abilities previously thought exclusive to 100B+ models.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces a **tailored learning approach** to distill the emergent reasoning ability of large language models (LLMs) into smaller LMs, enabling the democratization of reasoning capabilities otherwise exclusive to models exceeding 100B parameters. Evaluating on mathematical reasoning (GSM8K, MultiArith, SVAMP) and commonsense reasoning (CSQA, StrategyQA) datasets using GPT-J (6B) as the student and ChatGPT (175B) as the teacher, the method operates through a **multi-round interactive learning paradigm** (where the student exposes its mistakes to the teacher, who then provides customized correct rationales targeting those specific deficiencies) and **self-reflection learning** (a contrastive triplet loss that teaches the student to distinguish its own correct and incorrect reasoning paths in latent space). The full approach achieves 33.1% on GSM8K—a 17.5 percentage point gain over one-round distillation—and outperforms concurrent distillation methods that treat the LLM merely as a static data annotator, establishing that the LLM functions most effectively as a reasoning teacher only when it receives iterative feedback about the student's specific weaknesses rather than generating rationales blindly.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Reasoning Is an Emergent Property That Doesn't Transfer Down

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is a specific manifestation of the **democratization gap** in large language models. Wei et al. (2022a) established a striking empirical pattern: reasoning abilities — particularly Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, where models generate intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer — are **emergent properties** that appear only when model parameters cross a threshold of roughly 100B. Below this threshold, smaller LMs struggle profoundly with multi-step reasoning, even when prompted with the same CoT techniques that dramatically improve performance in larger models.

This creates a sharp capability cliff. Models like GPT-3 (175B), PaLM (540B), and ChatGPT can decompose complex problems into coherent reasoning chains and solve them at high accuracy rates. Models at the 6B–11B scale — which are far more accessible for academic research, on-device deployment, and open-source development — cannot reliably do so. The authors frame this explicitly in Section 1:

> "Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompts LMs to generate intermediate reasoning steps (i.e., rationale) to reach the final answer, significantly improving the complex reasoning ability... However, it is challenging to prompt smaller LMs to generate reasoning steps, since such ability appears to be exclusive to LLMs."

This is not merely an observation about scale — it has concrete consequences. An open-source team that wants to build a math tutoring system cannot simply fine-tune a 7B model and expect it to produce step-by-step solutions; the model lacks the foundational reasoning capacity to generate coherent rationales. The ability exists in the ecosystem but is locked behind proprietary APIs, computational barriers, or both.

### Why This Problem Matters: Democratization and the False Promise of Imitation

The paper implicitly engages with a tension that has become increasingly acute in the post-ChatGPT era. On one hand, a thriving open-source community has demonstrated that **instruction-following ability** can be effectively transferred from black-box LLMs to smaller models through supervised fine-tuning on LLM-generated outputs. Projects like Vicuna (Chiang et al., 2023), Alpaca (Taori et al., 2023), and Baize (Xu et al., 2023) show that a 7B–13B model fine-tuned on ChatGPT-generated conversations can approach LLM-level performance on tasks that require following instructions, answering questions, and generating coherent text.

On the other hand, **reasoning ability resists this simple transfer**. The authors note in Section 1 that while instruction-tuned smaller LMs "can be comparable to LLMs in following human instructions," they are "still falling short of reasoning." This is a crucial distinction. Instruction following is about mapping natural language commands to appropriate responses — a pattern that can be learned from sufficient annotated examples. Reasoning, particularly multi-step mathematical or commonsense reasoning, requires the model to construct a valid logical chain connecting premises to conclusions. If the model cannot generate these intermediate steps reliably, simply showing it examples of correct reasoning paths may not suffice to instill the capability.

The problem also intersects with a finding from Gudibande et al. (2023), who warned that "imitating proprietary LLMs may be a false promise" — that is, training smaller models on LLM outputs may produce models that superficially mimic LLM style without genuinely acquiring the underlying capabilities, particularly in reasoning. The present paper's explicit goal is to go beyond superficial imitation and genuinely cultivate the reasoning ability in smaller LMs, which requires a fundamentally different approach from the static data-annotation paradigm that sufficed for instruction following.

### Prior Approaches: The Static Data Annotation Paradigm

The authors situate their work against three categories of prior approaches, all of which they argue fall short in specific ways.

**Category 1: Direct CoT prompting of smaller LMs.** The natural first approach is to simply prompt smaller LMs with the same CoT techniques that work for LLMs — provide a few-shot demonstration of step-by-step reasoning, then ask the small model to reason through a new problem. This approach fails because, as Wei et al. (2022a,b) demonstrated, the ability to produce coherent multi-step rationales in response to prompting is itself scale-dependent. The smaller LM, when asked to "think step by step," produces either nonsensical reasoning chains or skips directly to an answer without meaningful intermediate steps. The paper's own baseline confirms this: GPT-J with no fine-tuning achieves only 2.7% on GSM8K (Table 1).

**Category 2: Training without LLM assistance.** This includes approaches where the smaller LM tries to bootstrap its own reasoning ability, most notably STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022). STaR works by having the model generate rationales, filtering for those that lead to correct answers, and fine-tuning on the successful examples — an iterative self-improvement cycle. However, this approach only works if the model can generate *some* correct rationales to bootstrap from. For smaller LMs on difficult reasoning tasks, the initial pass@1 may be near zero, meaning there are essentially no correct self-generated rationales to train on. The paper's STaR baseline on GSM8K achieves only 10.7% (Table 1), confirming that self-bootstrapping hits a wall when the base model's unaided reasoning is too weak.

A related approach is fine-tuning the smaller LM to directly output answers without rationales — essentially treating reasoning as an end-to-end black box. This performs poorly: GPT-J fine-tuned without CoT achieves only 7.2% on GSM8K (Table 1), demonstrating that the rationale generation step is not merely nice-to-have but is fundamental to solving multi-step problems.

**Category 3: Static distillation from LLMs.** The most directly relevant prior work is the set of concurrent approaches that use LLMs to generate rationales for training smaller LMs. These methods — including Ho et al. (2023), Magister et al. (2023), Fu et al. (2023b), Shridhar et al. (2023), and the LLM-adapter approach of Hu et al. (2023) — share a common two-step template:

1. **Annotation phase**: Query the black-box LLM with each training question, asking it to generate a CoT rationale leading to the correct answer. Collect these (question, rationale, answer) triples.
2. **Fine-tuning phase**: Fine-tune the smaller LM on these annotated examples, teaching it to generate rationales in the style of the LLM.

This is, as the authors note, a "distant variant of black-box knowledge distillation" (Jianping et al., 2021). The LLM serves as a data annotator — it generates high-quality training data, but does so *blindly* without any knowledge of the specific student model that will consume that data.

**Where Category 3 falls short.** The authors identify a specific limitation of this static paradigm that is central to their paper's motivation:

> "However, these methods only employ LLMs to annotate the data for training smaller LMs, without leveraging the smaller LMs to assist LLMs in return. As a consequence, the LLMs are not aware of the weaknesses of the smaller LMs, thereby hindering their powerful ability to analyze and provide targeted feedback, which undermines the effectiveness of the reasoning distillation."

This is the key insight. In human education, a teacher does not simply hand a student a textbook and walk away. Effective teaching involves **diagnosing what the student does wrong**, identifying specific misconceptions or gaps, and providing feedback targeted at those deficiencies. The student, in turn, reveals their learning status through their mistakes, enabling the teacher to adapt instruction. The static distillation paradigm captures none of this — the LLM generates the same rationales regardless of which smaller model will consume them, or what errors that model might make. The data is generic, not tailored.

The empirical consequences of this limitation are visible in Table 1. The concurrent distillation methods achieve respectable but limited results: Specializing (Fu et al., 2023b) with an 11B model reaches 27.1% on GSM8K; CoT Fine-tuned (Magister et al., 2023) with T5-11B reaches 18.4%; and the LLM-Adapter reaches 10.6%. These numbers, while far above the untrained baseline (2.7%), leave enormous headroom below the teacher's own performance (62.2% on GSM8K), indicating that substantial reasoning capability remains untransferred.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning can be understood through three conceptual shifts it makes relative to prior work:

**From annotator to teacher.** The paper reframes the LLM not as a data-generation tool but as a **reasoning teacher** — an entity that needs to understand the student's current capabilities to teach effectively. This reframing is not merely rhetorical; it has direct architectural consequences. The teacher must receive *input* from the student (the student's wrong rationales) and must generate *output* that responds to that input (customized correct rationales that address the specific errors). This bidirectional information flow is absent from all prior distillation approaches.

**From static to interactive.** The static paradigm involves a single round: generate annotations, fine-tune, evaluate. The paper introduces iteration — multiple rounds where the student's improved (but still imperfect) performance in round N generates new mistakes that inform the teacher's responses in round N+1. Each round's training data is different because the student's error patterns evolve. Early rounds address the most glaring reasoning failures; later rounds target subtler errors that only emerge once the student has mastered the basics. The analogy to human learning is explicit and deliberate:

> "Such a paradigm is natural as it is in inline with how we human beings learn from teachers."

**From learning from examples to learning from mistakes.** Beyond the interactive teacher-student loop, the paper introduces a complementary learning mechanism: **self-reflection learning**, where the student learns not only from the teacher's correct rationales but also from contrasting its own correct and incorrect outputs. This draws on the emerging literature showing that LLMs can self-improve through reflection (Huang et al., 2022; Shinn et al., 2023; Madaan et al., 2023) but adapts it for the smaller LM context. The student's self-made mistakes, which are collected as a byproduct of the "exam" step in each round (Section 3.1), serve double duty: they inform the teacher AND provide contrastive training signals for the student to learn to distinguish good reasoning from bad.

This dual source of learning — the teacher's external correction and the student's internal contrastive signal — is integrated into a single joint objective (Equation 4) where the language modeling loss on teacher rationales and the contrastive loss on self-made mistakes are combined with a balancing coefficient λ. The paper argues that these two sources of learning are complementary: the teacher provides *what correct reasoning should look like*, while self-reflection teaches *what distinguishes correct from incorrect reasoning*.

The paper does not claim to invent either interactive learning or contrastive learning. Rather, its contribution is the **integration** of both mechanisms into a specific multi-round architecture designed for the reasoning distillation setting, and the empirical demonstration that this integration yields substantial gains over either mechanism applied in isolation, or over the static distillation paradigm that was the de facto standard in prior work.

### The Dependence on High-Quality Rationales

Underlying the paper's entire approach is an assumption that deserves explicit attention: that **the rationale itself carries essential information** that enables reasoning transfer. This is why the paper's approach focuses so heavily on generating and curating rationales rather than simply distilling answer-level knowledge. If the only goal were to train the student to produce correct answers, one could simply train on (question, answer) pairs from the LLM, which would be far cheaper. But the results in Table 1 show that fine-tuning without rationales achieves only 7.2% on GSM8K, while even the most basic distillation with rationales reaches 15.6%, and the full method reaches 33.1%. The rationale is not merely an optional embellishment — it is the vehicle through which reasoning capability is transferred.

This has implications for why the student's feedback matters. When the teacher LLM generates a rationale without seeing the student's mistake, it produces a generic correct solution path. When the teacher sees the student's wrong rationale (e.g., in the GSM8K Example #1 in Table 2, where the student incorrectly reasons "Harry has 68 – 30 = 38 apples" without dividing by 2), the teacher can produce a rationale that explicitly addresses that error — walking through the correct chain of reasoning in a way that highlights where the student went wrong. The customized rationale is not just correct; it is **pedagogically targeted**. This is the mechanism by which the interactive paradigm is hypothesized to outperform the static one: the training data itself becomes more informative per example because it is responsive to the specific model being trained.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper builds a **multi-round interactive training system** where a small student LM learns to perform step-by-step reasoning by receiving customized feedback from a black-box teacher LLM and by learning to distinguish its own correct and incorrect reasoning attempts through contrastive self-reflection. The system solves the problem that static distillation—where the LLM generates rationales blindly without knowledge of the student's specific weaknesses—leaves substantial reasoning capability untransferred; the interactive paradigm instead mirrors human pedagogy, where the teacher sees what the student gets wrong and provides targeted instruction in response, iterating over multiple rounds until the student's performance plateaus.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components connected in a cyclic feedback loop:

1. **Student LM (GPT-J, ~6B parameters)** — the smaller language model being trained to reason with Chain-of-Thought. It generates solutions (rationales + answers) to training questions, produces mistakes that reveal its weaknesses, and is progressively fine-tuned on the training data generated by the teacher and its own self-reflection signals.

2. **Teacher LLM (ChatGPT, 175B, black-box API)** — the large language model that receives the student's wrong rationales as feedback and generates customized correct rationales targeting those specific errors. It is accessed only through API calls and never fine-tuned.

3. **Exam Module** — a data collection step within each round where the student LM generates outputs on the training set, and wrong outputs (those with incorrect final answers) are harvested into a mistake set `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{neg}}$`. These mistakes serve dual purpose: as feedback to the teacher and as contrastive samples for self-reflection.

4. **Training Module** — a joint optimization procedure that fine-tunes the student LM using two losses: a standard language modeling loss on teacher-generated correct rationales, and a contrastive triplet loss that pushes the student's representations of correct reasoning paths apart from its representations of incorrect ones.

The information flow across one round is: **Student takes exam** → **mistakes collected** → **mistakes sent to teacher** → **teacher generates customized rationales** → **student fine-tuned on teacher rationales + self-reflection contrastive loss** → **round completes, student enters next round**. This loop repeats until the student's error rate on the training set stops improving.

- **First**, the formal structure of the training data and the student's generative task, establishing notation for questions, rationales, and answers that subsequent sections depend on.
- **Second**, the Exam step (Section 3.1) — how mistakes are collected, what defines a "wrong" output, and how this mistake set connects to downstream components.
- **Third**, the Student's Feedback to LLM mechanism (Section 3.2) — the prompt template construction, what information is included, how the teacher generates customized rationales, and why the feedback changes the teacher's output.
- **Fourth**, the two learning mechanisms — self-reflection learning (contrastive triplet loss) and learning from customized feedback (language modeling loss) — and their integration into a joint objective, including the role of the balancing coefficient λ.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methodology paper** whose core idea is that reasoning distillation from black-box LLMs to smaller LMs can be substantially improved by replacing the standard static data-annotation paradigm with an interactive multi-round paradigm where the teacher receives the student's mistakes as input and provides customized rationales as output, combined with a contrastive self-reflection mechanism that teaches the student to separate correct from incorrect reasoning paths.

---

#### Formal Problem Setting and the Role of Rationales

The paper operates on a training dataset `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \{(x, y)\}$` where `$x$` is a question and `$y$` is the ground-truth answer. Critically, the correct reasoning path (the rationale) is **not** provided in the original dataset — only the question-answer pair exists. The paper's entire methodology is built around generating and curating these rationales.

During Chain-of-Thought inference, the input is the question `$x$`, and the student LM's generated output is a concatenation of the generated rationale `$\hat{r}$` and the generated answer `$\hat{y}$`, denoted `$f(x) = [\hat{r}, \hat{y}]$`. The answer is positioned at the end of the output sequence, following the reasoning steps. This is enforced through the CoT prompt template:

```
Question: x \n Reasoning: r \n Answer: y
```

where `\n` represents a line break, `x` is the question text, `r` is the expected reasoning (generated by the model during inference), and `y` is the expected answer. The greedy decoding strategy samples the most likely token at each step, and answer extraction operates by taking the first valid token after the substring `"Answer:"`, avoiding complex post-processing.

The rationale `$r$` is not merely decorative — it is the vehicle through which multi-step reasoning capability is transferred. This is why the paper invests so heavily in generating *high-quality*, *customized* rationales rather than simply training on answer-level signals. A rationale decomposes a complex problem into intermediate inferences (e.g., "Martha has 68 apples. Tim has 68 − 30 = 38 apples. Harry has 38 / 2 = 19 apples."), and the student learns to generate similar chains by fine-tuning on these sequences.

---

#### The Exam Step: Collecting Mistakes to Reveal Student Weaknesses

**Purpose.** At the beginning of each round `$r$`, the current student LM `$f^r$` undergoes an "exam" on the training set to assess its learning status and, most importantly, to collect its mistakes. These mistakes serve as the evidentiary basis for everything that follows — they are the student's feedback to the teacher and the contrastive signal for self-reflection.

**Procedure.** For each question `$(x, y) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$`, the student LM generates up to 4 distinct rationales using **sampling decoding** (temperature = 1.0, top-k = 50, no top-p specified for this step, maximum generation length = 128 tokens, number of return sequences = 4). Sampling decoding means the model draws tokens probabilistically rather than taking the argmax, producing diverse outputs that expose different failure modes.

The correctness of each generated output is judged solely by whether the extracted final answer `$\hat{y}$` matches the ground-truth answer `$y$`. The paper explicitly notes this simplification:

> "Following most existing works, we simply judge the quality of the generated rationale by the correctness of its answer."

This means a rationale could contain flawed intermediate reasoning but still be classified as "correct" if it coincidentally arrives at the right answer — a known limitation that the paper acknowledges in its Limitations section. Conversely, a rationale with sound reasoning that makes a final arithmetic error is classified as "wrong."

The mistake set `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{neg}}$` is formally defined as:

$$\mathcal{D}_{\text{neg}} = \{(x, \hat{r}, \hat{y}) \mid \hat{y} \neq y, (x, y) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\}$$

where `$x$` is the question, `$\hat{r}$` is the student's generated (incorrect) rationale, and `$\hat{y}$` is the student's generated (incorrect) answer. The condition `$\hat{y} \neq y$` filters for only the outputs where the extracted answer disagrees with the ground truth.

For each question, up to 4 wrong rationales are retained. If the student generates fewer than 4 incorrect outputs (because some samples happen to be correct), fewer mistakes are collected for that question. The collected mistake set `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{neg}}$` reflects the student's **current learning status and specific weaknesses** in that round.

**Why up to 4?** The paper does not provide an explicit ablation for this number, but the choice balances two considerations: collecting multiple mistakes provides diverse examples of the student's failure modes, which gives the teacher richer diagnostic information; keeping the number modest (4 rather than, say, 20) controls the API cost of requesting customized rationales from the teacher LLM in the next step.

**Dual Purpose.** The mistake set `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{neg}}$` is used for two distinct purposes, creating the paper's central integration point:

1. **As feedback to the teacher LLM** (Section 3.2): Each `$(x, \hat{r}, \hat{y}) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{neg}}$` is plugged into a prompt template that asks the teacher to generate a corrected rationale. The student's wrong reasoning serves both as diagnostic information (showing the teacher what went wrong) and as a negative demonstration (showing the teacher what *not* to do).

2. **As negative contrastive samples for self-reflection** (Section 3.3): Each wrong rationale is paired with a correct rationale for the same question, and the student is trained via a triplet loss that pushes the latent representations of correct and incorrect reasoning paths apart.

#### Student's Feedback to LLM: The Prompt Template and Customized Rationale Generation

This is the mechanism that transforms the LLM from a static data annotator into a responsive reasoning teacher. The core technical artifact is a prompt template `$\mathcal{T}$` that integrates the student's mistake into the request to the teacher.

**Prompt template structure.** The template `$\mathcal{T}(x, \hat{r}, y)$` takes three inputs: the question `$x$`, the student's wrong rationale `$\hat{r}$`, and the ground-truth answer `$y$`. It constructs a prompt with the following structure (shown in Figure 3 and elaborated in Appendix Table 12):

1. **Question statement:** The original problem text `$x$`.
2. **Wrong solution:** The student's incorrect rationale `$\hat{r}$`, explicitly labeled as "Wrong Solution."
3. **Correction instruction:** A directive phrase: "Please correct the wrong solution by using better reasoning steps."
4. **Answer hint:** An explicit statement of the ground-truth answer: "Hint: The final answer should be `$y$`."
5. **Output prefix:** The token "Better Reasoning:" to prompt the teacher to begin generating the corrected rationale.

The full template from Appendix Table 12, using a StrategyQA example to illustrate, reads:

> Question: Is Europa linked to Viennese waltzes?
> Wrong Solution: Europa is a moon of Jupiter, and it is named after a Phoenician queen of Crete. Viennese waltzes are a type of dance that originated in Austria. So, the answer is No.
> Please correct the wrong solution by using better reasoning steps.
> Hint: The final answer should be yes.
> Better Reasoning:

**Why the answer hint?** Including the ground-truth answer `$y$` in the prompt serves two purposes acknowledged by the authors. First, it dramatically improves the accuracy of the teacher's generated rationales — the LLM is told what answer to aim for, so it works backward to construct a valid reasoning chain, following the approach of Zelikman et al. (2022). Without the hint, the teacher might generate an incorrect answer or fail to solve the problem, wasting an API call. Second, it reduces costs by increasing the yield of usable (correct-answer) rationales per API request. The paper states:

> "to improve the LLM's accuracy and reduce the costs of calling APIs, we follow Zelikman et al. (2022) by adding a hint to explicitly tell LLM the golden answer of the question."

The hint is a practical engineering choice rather than a theoretically motivated one — it prioritizes data quality and cost efficiency over the pedagogical realism of having the teacher solve problems independently.

**How the feedback changes the teacher's output.** The student's wrong rationale `$\hat{r}$` serves two distinct functions within the prompt:

1. **Diagnostic information for the teacher:** The teacher can see *where* the student went wrong — which step of reasoning was flawed, what misconception was applied, what calculation error was made. This enables the teacher to generate a corrected rationale that specifically addresses those errors, rather than providing a generic correct solution that might not highlight the specific point of failure.

2. **Negative demonstration:** By showing the wrong reasoning path, the prompt implicitly tells the teacher what to correct. The instruction "Please correct the wrong solution" positions the teacher's task as *response to a specific error* rather than *de novo solution generation*. This framing encourages the teacher to produce rationales that explicitly contrast with and correct the student's mistakes.

The evidence for this mechanism is shown empirically in Table 2. For GSM8K Example #1, when the student's feedback is absent, the teacher generates a generic solution that is actually incorrect (computing 190 instead of 196). When the feedback is present, the teacher generates a detailed, correct solution that walks through each step: "Dad: 10 cars, 5 less than Mum; Mum: 10 + 5 = 15 cars; Aunt: 6 cars, 1 more than uncle; Uncle: 6 - 1 = 5 cars; Grandpa: twice as many toy cars as the uncle, so 5 * 2 = 10 cars; Total: 150 + 15 + 10 + 6 + 5 + 10 = 196 cars."

For StrategyQA Example #1 in Table 2, the teacher with feedback even provides a counterexample to the student's wrong answer — demonstrating that a haiku can fit in a tweet by providing an actual haiku example — something that would not occur in a generic solution.

**Rationale collection and filtering.** For each mistake `$(x, \hat{r}, \hat{y}) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{neg}}$`, the teacher LLM is requested (via the API) to generate 4 distinct rationales using sampling decoding parameters: temperature = 1.0, top-p = 0.9, maximum generation length = 128 tokens, number of return sequences = 4. Only rationales containing the correct final answer `$y$` are retained; incorrect teacher outputs are discarded.

The paper notes the reason for generating multiple rationales per question:

> "training with diverse reasoning paths can boost the reasoning performance of smaller LMs"

This is an insight carried forward from prior work (Ho et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2023b) — exposing the student to multiple valid ways of solving the same problem prevents overfitting to a single reasoning pattern and may teach the student that multiple reasoning strategies can lead to correct answers.

The retained rationales, together with their original questions and answers, form the customized training data for that round, denoted `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$`. Note that this `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$` is **different in each round** because the student's mistakes evolve, causing the teacher to generate different custom rationales in response.

**The special case of the first round.** The untrained student LM has essentially no reasoning ability — its initial pass@1 on GSM8K is 2.7% (Table 1). The resulting generated rationales are "noisy generations which are unhelpful as the feedback to the teacher LLM" (Section 3.4). Sending near-random text to the teacher as "feedback" would not provide meaningful diagnostic information and might even confuse the teacher.

Therefore, in the **initial round only** (`$r = 1$`), the procedure is modified: instead of sending student mistakes, the prompt template is called with `$\hat{r}$` set to `null` — essentially asking the teacher LLM to generate correct rationales for the training set without any student-specific feedback. The template becomes `$\mathcal{T}(x, \text{null}, y)$`, which simplifies to: present the question, omit the wrong solution, provide the answer hint, and ask for reasoning. This produces **generic** (non-customized) rationales that bootstrap the student's initial reasoning capability to a point where its mistakes become informative enough to serve as meaningful feedback in subsequent rounds.

In subsequent rounds (`$r \geq 2$`), the full template `$\mathcal{T}(x, \hat{r}, y)$` is used, incorporating the student's actual mistakes from the exam step.

#### Learning from Self-Reflection: The Contrastive Triplet Loss

**Motivation.** The paper draws on the human learning analogy of self-reflection — after making a mistake, a learner benefits from comparing what they did wrong with what they should have done, not just from being shown the correct answer. The computational mechanism for this is a **contrastive loss** that operates in the representation space of the student LM, pushing the hidden-state representations of correct reasoning paths away from those of incorrect reasoning paths.

**Representation extraction.** For any reasoning path `$[x, r, y]$` (where the brackets denote concatenation of the question, rationale, and answer into a single sequence), the model processes this sequence through its transformer layers. The hidden state of the **last token** in the sequence is taken as the representation of the entire reasoning path. This representation is denoted as:

$$\mathbf{h}^{(r,y)}_x$$

where the superscript `$(r, y)$` indicates that the rationale `$r$` and answer `$y$` are concatenated with the question `$x$`, and the subscript `$x$` ties the representation to the specific question. In practice, this is the output of the final transformer layer at the position of the last token (typically the end-of-sequence token or the last character of the answer).

**Why the last token?** The paper does not provide an explicit rationale, but this is standard practice in representation learning from autoregressive LMs: the last token's hidden state has attended to all preceding tokens and therefore encodes information about the entire sequence in a fixed-dimensional vector. Alternatives — such as mean pooling across all token positions, or using a special [CLS] token (for encoder-only models) — would also be valid but are not explored.

**Triplet loss formulation.** The contrastive loss is defined as:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{cl}} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}} \max\left(0, \rho - \cos(\mathbf{h}^{(r,y)}_x, \mathbf{h}^{(r',y)}_x) + \cos(\mathbf{h}^{(r,y)}_x, \mathbf{h}^{(\hat{r},\hat{y})}_x)\right)$$

where:
- `$\mathbf{h}^{(r,y)}_x$` is the representation of an **anchor** sample — a correct reasoning path for question `$x$` (from the teacher, `$(x, r, y) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$`).
- `$\mathbf{h}^{(r',y)}_x$` is the representation of a **positive** sample — another (different) correct reasoning path for the same question `$x$`, randomly sampled from `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$`. Since the teacher generates up to 4 rationales per question, there can be multiple correct reasoning paths for the same `$x$`.
- `$\mathbf{h}^{(\hat{r},\hat{y})}_x$` is the representation of a **negative** sample — a wrong reasoning path for the same question `$x$`, randomly sampled from `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{neg}}$`.
- `$\cos(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})$` is the cosine similarity between vectors `$\mathbf{a}$` and `$\mathbf{b}$`, defined as `$\frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{||\mathbf{a}|| \cdot ||\mathbf{b}||}$`. It ranges from -1 (opposite directions) to 1 (identical directions).
- `$\rho$` is a margin hyperparameter set to 1.0.

**What it computes, operationally.** For each training step, the loss takes one correct anchor rationale, one different correct rationale for the same question (the positive), and one incorrect rationale for the same question (the negative). It computes two cosine similarities: (1) between the anchor and the positive — we want this to be **high** because both are correct reasoning paths; (2) between the anchor and the negative — we want this to be **low** because one is correct and one is incorrect.

The term inside the `$\max$` is:

$$\rho - \underbrace{\cos(\text{anchor}, \text{positive})}_{\text{want this high}} + \underbrace{\cos(\text{anchor}, \text{negative})}_{\text{want this low}}$$

The loss is zero (no gradient) when the anchor-positive similarity exceeds the anchor-negative similarity by at least the margin `$\rho = 1.0$`. When this condition is violated — the negative is too similar to the anchor, or the positive is not similar enough — the loss is positive, and gradients flow to increase the anchor-positive similarity and decrease the anchor-negative similarity.

The expectation `$\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}}$` indicates that this loss is averaged over anchor samples drawn from the training set. At each training step, for each anchor `$(x, r, y)$`, a positive `$(x, r', y)$` and a negative `$(x, \hat{r}, \hat{y})$` are randomly sampled from the available correct and incorrect rationales for that question.

**Why this form.** The triplet loss with cosine similarity is chosen over several alternatives:

1. **Why a margin-based loss rather than a simple similarity maximization?** The margin `$\rho$` enforces a minimum separation between the correct-correct similarity and the correct-incorrect similarity. Without the margin, the model could achieve zero loss simply by making all similarities equal. The margin forces a structured latent space where correct reasoning paths for the same question cluster together and are separated from incorrect paths by at least `$\rho$`.

2. **Why cosine similarity rather than Euclidean distance?** Cosine similarity normalizes for vector magnitude, meaning the loss focuses on the *direction* of the representations rather than their lengths. This is beneficial because representations of longer rationales might naturally have larger norms (more tokens attended to), and Euclidean distance would be dominated by magnitude differences rather than semantic differences between correct and incorrect reasoning.

3. **Why triplet loss rather than a binary classifier?** An alternative would be to train a binary classifier on top of the representations to distinguish correct from incorrect reasoning paths. The triplet loss is simpler (no additional parameters), operates directly on the representations that will be used during generation, and provides a geometric inductive bias (clustering by correctness) rather than just a discriminative signal.

4. **Why same-question sampling?** The anchor, positive, and negative all come from the same question `$x$`. This ensures that the representations are learning to distinguish reasoning *quality* rather than reasoning *topic*. If negatives came from different questions, the model might learn to separate questions rather than correct vs. incorrect reasoning.

**Effect on latent space.** The t-SNE visualization in Figure 4 and the quantitative metrics in Table 4 demonstrate the functional effect. With self-reflection learning, the Euclidean distance between correct and incorrect reasoning path representations increases (from 51.00 to 65.08 on GSM8K), and the likelihood ratio of preferring correct paths over wrong ones increases (from 73.63 to 79.11). In the t-SNE plot, the "w/ Self-Reflection" panel shows clearer separation between the red (wrong) and blue (correct) clusters compared to the "w/o Self-Reflection" panel, where the two distributions overlap substantially.

#### Learning from Customized Feedback: The Language Modeling Loss

**Purpose.** While self-reflection teaches the student to *discriminate* between correct and incorrect reasoning, it does not directly teach the student to *generate* correct reasoning. The language modeling loss on teacher-generated rationales fills this gap — it is a standard supervised fine-tuning objective that teaches the student to produce the correct rationale given the question as input.

**Training data format.** Each training example is constructed by concatenating: fixed few-shot demonstrations + the question `$x$` + the teacher-generated rationale `$r$` + the answer `$y$`. The demonstrations are placed at the prefix of each input sample, not as a separate prompt — they are part of the training sequence itself.

The paper uses a CoT prompt format throughout:

```
[demo_1] [demo_2] [demo_3] Question: x \n Reasoning: r \n Answer: y
```

where `[demo_i]` are fixed demonstration examples selected from the training set (3-shot for GSM8K, MultiArith, SVAMP; 5-shot for CSQA; 4-shot for StrategyQA — the exact demonstrations are listed in Appendix Table 15). The rationale `$r$` is the teacher's customized rationale, and `$y$` is the ground-truth answer.

**Why include demonstrations in the training data?** The paper cites prior work (Min et al., 2022; Zelikman et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2023b) showing that "training with demonstration examples can improve the in-context learning ability of LMs." The demonstrations teach the model not just the content of the specific problems but the *format* of CoT reasoning — how to structure a reasoning chain, how to separate reasoning from the answer, and how to read and follow few-shot examples during inference. At test time, the same demonstrations are prepended to the prompt, and the model has been trained to condition on them.

**Loss formulation.** The language modeling objective is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{lm}} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}} \log P_f([\text{demo}, x, r, y])$$

where `$P_f$` is the probability assigned by the student LM `$f$` to the sequence `$[\text{demo}, x, r, y]$` (the brackets denote string concatenation), and the expectation is over the customized training set `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$`.

**What it computes, operationally.** This is standard autoregressive language modeling with teacher forcing. For each token in the concatenated sequence, the model predicts the next token given all previous tokens. The loss is the negative log-likelihood of the ground-truth tokens, summed over the sequence. In practice, the loss is computed only on the tokens corresponding to the rationale `$r$` and answer `$y$` (the demonstration and question tokens are conditioned on but not trained on, since they are fixed).

The training uses the **AdamW optimizer** with `$\beta = (0.9, 0.999)$`, `$\epsilon = 10^{-8}$`, weight decay of 0.01, and a learning rate schedule. The initial round uses learning rate `$10^{-6}$`; subsequent rounds use `$7 \times 10^{-7}$` to make training more stable as the student has already acquired basic reasoning capability and needs finer-grained updates. Training runs for 10 epochs with batch size 16 and 100 warmup steps. Mixed precision (FP16) is used with 8 Tesla V100 GPUs and the Deepspeed library for memory optimization.

**A subtle training detail: demonstration weighting.** The paper notes an empirical finding that is not the focus of any experiment but could be practically important:

> "from pilot experiments, we empirically find that assigning less weights (0.1) to the fixed demonstration examples than the input sample helps the model focus on the input sample and yield better performance"

This means that during loss computation, the tokens belonging to the demonstration examples receive a weight of 0.1 (reduced gradient contribution), while the tokens of the actual question, rationale, and answer receive weight 1.0. The rationale for this is intuitive: the model should learn to *generate* reasoning for the specific input question, not to *memorize* the fixed demonstrations. Down-weighting the demonstration tokens in the loss prevents the model from overfitting to reproducing the few-shot examples exactly and encourages it to attend to the new question and its corresponding rationale.

**Why language modeling rather than a specialized reasoning loss.** The paper does not design a custom loss for reasoning (e.g., a step-level verifier or a process reward model). It relies on standard autoregressive language modeling to teach the student to generate rationales. This is a deliberate simplification — the rationale is treated as a natural language sequence, and the model learns to produce it through the same mechanism it uses for any text generation task. The implicit assumption is that the sequence `$[x, r, y]$` contains all the information needed for the student to learn the mapping from questions to reasoning chains, and that the customization of `$r$` by the teacher (targeting the student's specific errors) makes these sequences more informative per example than generic rationales.

#### Joint Training: Combining the Two Losses

The student LM is fine-tuned using a weighted combination of the language modeling loss (for learning from the teacher) and the contrastive loss (for learning from self-reflection):

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{lm}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{cl}}$$

where `$\lambda$` is a hyperparameter controlling the relative weight of the self-reflection loss.

**What it computes, operationally.** At each training step, a batch of training examples is sampled. For each anchor `$(x, r, y)$` in the batch, the language modeling loss `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{lm}}$` is computed autoregressively. Simultaneously, a positive `$(x, r', y)$` and a negative `$(x, \hat{r}, \hat{y})$` are sampled for the anchor, and the contrastive loss `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{cl}}$` is computed on the triplet. The two losses are added with weight `$\lambda$` on the contrastive term, and the sum is backpropagated through the model.

**The role of `$\lambda$`.** The paper states that `$\lambda$` is set to 0.5 "without any heavy tuning." The sensitivity analysis in Figure 5 shows how performance varies with `$\lambda$` on MultiArith and StrategyQA:

- With `$\lambda = 0.0$` (no self-reflection), performance is a baseline.
- With `$\lambda = 0.25$`, performance improves (from 82.0% to 84.9% on MultiArith; from 63.6% to 64.9% on StrategyQA), indicating that self-reflection provides a genuine benefit.
- With `$\lambda = 0.5$`, performance on MultiArith drops to 82.2% (still above baseline but below the optimum), while StrategyQA drops to 64.1% (slightly above baseline). The paper's chosen value of 0.5 is therefore somewhat conservative — it does not maximize performance on MultiArith but provides a reasonable trade-off across datasets.
- With `$\lambda = 0.75$` or `$\lambda = 1.0$`, performance degrades further, and the paper notes "excessive emphasis on self-reflection learning typically leads to poorer performance and instability."

**Why excessive self-reflection weight hurts.** The paper provides a mechanistic explanation supported by Figure 6. When `$\lambda$` is too large, the contrastive loss dominates the optimization, causing the language modeling loss `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{lm}}$` to **underfit** within the limited number of training steps. Figure 6 shows that the language modeling loss decreases more slowly with higher `$\lambda$`, and at `$\lambda = 1.0$`, the loss is substantially higher (less converged) after 1000 steps than at `$\lambda = 0.0$`. The paper explains:

> "the excessive emphasis on self-reflection learning (higher `$\lambda$`) can result in underfitting of these training data within a limited number of training steps. Consequently, the reasoning performance of the student LM could be significantly decreased due to not fully converged."

In other words, the two losses compete for model capacity and optimization budget. The contrastive loss requires the model to shape its representation space in a specific way (separating correct from incorrect paths), which may conflict with the language modeling objective's goal of accurately predicting tokens. A small `$\lambda$` provides a useful inductive bias without derailing the primary language modeling task; a large `$\lambda$` hijacks the optimization.

**Why joint training rather than sequential.** An alternative would be to first train on the language modeling objective to convergence, then add the contrastive loss. The paper's joint approach trains both simultaneously, meaning the representation learning from the contrastive loss can influence how the model processes the language modeling sequences, and vice versa. This integrated approach potentially allows synergistic learning — the model may develop representations that are both useful for next-token prediction AND structured by correctness — but also introduces the optimization competition documented in Figures 5 and 6.

#### Multi-Round Learning: Iteration and Convergence

**Purpose.** The multi-round structure is not merely a training efficiency optimization — it is fundamental to the paper's core claim that interactive, iterative learning outperforms static one-shot distillation. Each round updates the teacher's knowledge of the student's weaknesses, generating new customized training data that addresses the errors the student *currently* makes, which are different from the errors it made in the previous round.

**Algorithmic structure (Algorithm 1).** The full multi-round procedure is:

1. **Initialize:** Student LM `$f^0$` with pre-trained weights (e.g., GPT-J). Set round counter `$r \leftarrow 0$`.

2. **For each round `$r$`:**
   - Increment `$r \leftarrow r + 1$`.
   - Set `$f^r \leftarrow f^{r-1}$` (start from the previous round's trained weights).
   - **Exam step:** Run inference on the training set `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$` using `$f^r$`, collect mistakes into `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{neg}}$` via Equation (1).
   - **Rationale collection:**
     - If `$r = 1$`: For each sample in `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$` (the full training set), request the teacher LLM with template `$\mathcal{T}(x, \text{null}, y)$` to generate correct rationales. This produces the initial (generic) training data.
     - If `$r \geq 2$`: For each mistake `$(x, \hat{r}, \hat{y}) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{neg}}$`, request the teacher LLM with template `$\mathcal{T}(x, \hat{r}, y)$` to generate customized correct rationales. These replace or augment the training data.
   - **Training:** Optimize the weights of `$f^r$` using the joint loss `$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{lm}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{cl}}$` (Equation 4) on the collected training data.

3. **Repeat until convergence** — the student's performance on the training set plateaus.

**How training data evolves across rounds.** Table 5 provides a concrete view of this evolution:

- **GSM8K:** After round 1, the student's error rate (ER) on the training set is 76.3%, with 15K training samples prepared. After round 2, ER drops to 66.2%, and 16K new samples are prepared targeting the remaining errors. After round 3, ER drops further to 49.2%, with 13K samples prepared. The test accuracy improves from 2.7% to 15.6% (after round 1), then to 28.2% (after round 2), then to 30.6% (after round 3) — diminishing gains.
- **SVAMP:** The error rate drops dramatically from 76.0% to 24.0% after round 1, then to 16.7% after round 2. The amount of new training data shrinks accordingly (2K → 0.6K → 0.3K samples). Test accuracy gains diminish: +27.0, +3.6, +1.0 percentage points across rounds 1–3.
- **CSQA:** Similar pattern — ER drops from 67.8% to 18.9% to 7.6%, training data shrinks from 26K to 7K to 3K samples, and accuracy gains diminish from +31.8 to +3.9 to −0.6 (slight degradation in round 3).

This pattern reveals the **self-limiting nature of the multi-round process**. As the student improves, it makes fewer mistakes, which means fewer samples are available for the teacher to provide customized feedback on. Additionally, the remaining errors tend to be on increasingly difficult problems that the student may simply lack the capacity to solve (the paper's Limitations section acknowledges this: "the student is reaching its capacity after 3 rounds of learning, still not performing well (49.2 ER)" on GSM8K).

**Early stopping criterion.** The paper explicitly recommends early stopping based on observed performance:

> "we suggest to take early stopping in the multi-round learning if the student can nearly reach its plateau. By prior estimation of the task difficulty and observing performance gains in each round, we can avoid excessive parameter tuning on the number of learning rounds and balance the reasoning performance and training costs."

In practice, the paper uses 3 rounds for GSM8K (where the student still has not fully saturated), 2 rounds for SVAMP and CSQA (where gains after round 2 are negligible or negative), and varies rounds in the feasibility study (Section 5.4) depending on model size and task difficulty.

**Why the first round is special — a deeper look.** The first round's deviation from the interactive paradigm deserves fuller explanation. The untrained student LM at round 0 produces outputs that are essentially random with respect to reasoning — they are noisy, incoherent, and unrelated to the question's logical structure. Sending these outputs as "feedback" to the teacher would not provide diagnostic information; the teacher would see gibberish and could not infer specific reasoning failures. The paper acknowledges this:

> "The untrained student LM nearly has no reasoning ability, resulting in the noisy generations which are unhelpful as the feedback to the teacher LLM."

Therefore, round 1 serves as a **bootstrapping round** — it uses the teacher LLM as a static data annotator (exactly like prior work) to give the student enough initial reasoning capability that its subsequent mistakes become informative. Once the student has a basic understanding of how to generate rationales (after round 1 fine-tuning), its errors are structured and interpretable, making them useful as feedback for round 2 onward.

This means the paper's claim is not that static distillation is useless — round 1 *is* static distillation, and it provides a substantial gain (from 2.7% to 15.6% on GSM8K). Rather, the claim is that static distillation alone leaves further gains on the table, and the interactive paradigm recovers those gains in subsequent rounds (+16.4 percentage points from round 1 to round 3 on GSM8K in Table 1, for the multi-round variant without self-reflection).

**How the "multi-round" and "self-reflection" modifications interact in Table 1.** The results in Table 1 show a layered accumulation of improvements:

- One-Round Distillation: 15.6% on GSM8K
- + Multi-round: 32.0% (+16.4) — the interactive feedback loop added on top of the static first round
- + Self-Reflection: 33.1% (+1.1) — contrastive loss added on top of the multi-round paradigm

The self-reflection gain is smaller in absolute terms (+1.1 on GSM8K) than the multi-round gain (+16.4), but it is consistent across datasets (+2.3 on MultiArith, +3.7 on SVAMP, +1.1 on CSQA, +0.4 on StrategyQA). The paper argues that this complementary effect arises because the self-reflection operates on the student's own outputs, which are produced in each round as a natural byproduct of the exam step. It adds no additional API calls to the teacher and can be seamlessly integrated into the existing multi-round pipeline.

**Quality evolution of student-generated rationales.** Table 6 provides GPT-4 evaluations of the quality of student-generated rationales across rounds (scored on a scale from 1 to 5 based on accuracy and reasoning quality):

- **GSM8K:** Correct rationales improve from 2.59 (initial) to 4.50 (round 1) to 4.88 (round 2). Wrong rationales improve more modestly from 1.02 to 1.15 to 1.26. This suggests the model is getting better at producing high-quality correct reasoning while its incorrect outputs remain low-quality — the gap between correct and incorrect reasoning quality *widens*, which is exactly what the self-reflection loss incentivizes.
- **SVAMP and CSQA:** Similar patterns — correct rationale quality increases substantially (4.53 → 4.90 for SVAMP; 4.44 → 4.96 for CSQA), while wrong rationale quality increases only slightly (1.07 → 1.11; 1.24 → 1.55), with the gap widening.

This widening quality gap between correct and incorrect rationales supports the paper's claim that multi-round learning, augmented with self-reflection, teaches the student not just to generate correct reasoning more often, but to produce *better* correct reasoning and to keep incorrect reasoning clearly distinct (and identifiable) from correct reasoning.

#### Key Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Choice 1: Using the student's wrong rationales as feedback rather than other forms of student state (e.g., loss values, gradient information, or probability distributions).** The paper's feedback mechanism is simple: show the teacher the student's incorrect output. This has two pragmatic justifications. First, it works with a black-box API — the teacher only needs to receive text and generate text, requiring no access to model internals. Second, it is interpretable — the wrong rationale explicitly shows the flawed reasoning chain, which the teacher can directly respond to. Alternatives like sending loss values or gradient information would be opaque to the teacher (which is itself an LLM that reasons in natural language) and would require white-box access to the student.

**Choice 2: Judging rationale correctness by final answer only.** This is a deliberate simplification that the paper acknowledges as a limitation. The alternative — evaluating the logical correctness of each reasoning step — would require human annotation or a process reward model, both of which are expensive and were not standard at the time. The paper notes that "develop[ing] more trustworthy criteria to evaluate the quality of rationales" is important future work, while pragmatically adopting the answer-match criterion because "most existing works in this field adopt this simple criterion." This means some rationales with correct answers but flawed intermediate reasoning may be misclassified as "correct," and some rationales with sound reasoning but final arithmetic errors may be misclassified as "wrong." The downstream effects of this noise are not explicitly analyzed.

**Choice 3: Using cosine similarity with a triplet margin rather than other contrastive formulations.** As analyzed above, the triplet loss with cosine similarity provides a geometric inductive bias that separates reasoning paths in representation space by correctness. The paper could have used a simple binary classifier, a contrastive N-pair loss, or a ranking loss, but the triplet formulation is simple, requires only one hyperparameter (`$\rho = 1.0$`), and directly optimizes the relative similarity structure that the paper visualizes in Figure 4.

**Choice 4: The specific prompt template structure (question → wrong solution → correction instruction → hint → output prefix).** The template is designed to make the teacher's task as clear as possible: "Here is the problem. Here is what the student did wrong. Please fix it. The correct answer should be X. Now give the reasoning." Each element serves a purpose: the wrong solution provides the specific error context; the correction instruction frames the task as error-correction rather than de novo generation; the hint ensures the teacher produces a usable output (reducing wasted API calls); the output prefix constrains the generation format. The paper does not ablate alternative template designs (e.g., removing the hint, changing the instruction wording, or adding explicit "act as a teacher" role-playing), which would be a natural extension but is beyond the paper's scope.

**Choice 5: Generating multiple rationales per question (up to 4) with diverse sampling.** This is motivated by prior work showing that training on diverse reasoning paths improves generalization (Ho et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2023b). The diversity comes from the teacher LLM's sampling parameters (temperature = 1.0, top-p = 0.9), which introduce stochastic variation in the generation process. The number 4 is chosen to balance diversity benefits against API costs — each additional rationale per question requires an additional API call.

**Choice 6: Fine-tuning with demonstrations prepended to training examples.** This design choice embeds few-shot learning capability directly into the training objective. At test time, the same demonstrations are prepended, and the model has learned to attend to them as conditioning information. The paper cites Min et al. (2022) and Zelikman et al. (2022) for the theoretical basis (meta-learning of in-context patterns) and Fu et al. (2023b) for empirical precedent in the reasoning distillation context. The down-weighting of demonstration tokens (by 0.1) is an empirical refinement that prevents the model from overfitting to reproducing the demonstrations verbatim.

**Choice 7: Using the same prompt template format for training and inference.** The CoT prompt template `"Question: x \n Reasoning: r \n Answer: y"` is used consistently during training data construction, during fine-tuning, and during evaluation. This consistency ensures that the model learns to associate the delimiter tokens (`Reasoning:` and `Answer:`) with the transition between question understanding, step-by-step reasoning, and final answer extraction, which then enables simple answer extraction at test time by searching for the first token after `Answer:`.

#### Feasibility Across Model Scales and Architectures

Section 5.4 demonstrates that the method generalizes beyond the 6B GPT-J student. The paper tests models ranging from 760M to 2.7B parameters, spanning both encoder-decoder (T5-Large, 760M) and decoder-only (GPT-2 Large, 770M; OPT-IML, 1.3B; GPT-Neo, 2.7B) architectures.

For **mathematical reasoning (SVAMP)**, the pattern is consistent: distillation provides large gains over the untrained baseline (e.g., 0.0% → 11.0% for T5-Large; 3.7% → 34.3% for GPT-Neo 2.7B), self-reflection adds further improvements (+3.7 for T5-Large, +2.0 for GPT-Neo), and multi-round learning provides additional gains for most models (+0.6 for T5-Large, +3.0 for OPT-IML 1.3B). The 2.7B GPT-Neo shows a slight decline (−0.3) in the multi-round step, suggesting that for marginally smaller models, the single-round self-reflection approach may already extract most of the available gains.

For **commonsense reasoning (StrategyQA)**, the pattern differs: distillation achieves substantial gains (62.0–62.2% across all model sizes), self-reflection adds modest improvements (+2.0 to +3.0 percentage points), but multi-round learning provides no consistent benefit and sometimes degrades performance (−1.8 for GPT-2 770M, −1.4 for GPT-Neo 2.7B). The paper attributes this to the nature of commonsense reasoning: "it heavily relies on the memorization of commonsense knowledge mostly acquired from the pre-training stage, rather than on complex reasoning." Multi-round learning targets reasoning *process* improvements, but if the bottleneck is knowledge retrieval rather than reasoning structure, additional rounds of customized feedback on reasoning paths provide diminishing returns.

This feasibility study demonstrates that the method works at scales well below 6B parameters and across architectures, making it relevant for researchers with limited computational resources. The key limitation is that model capacity still matters — the absolute performance of a 760M model with full multi-round self-reflection learning (15.3% on SVAMP) remains far below that of the 6B model (55.0%), confirming that reasoning distillation amplifies existing capability rather than creating it from nothing, and that larger student models can absorb more of the teacher's reasoning ability.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing the LLM from Static Data Annotator to Interactive Reasoning Teacher

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is not algorithmic but **architectural**: it restructures the relationship between the black-box teacher LLM and the smaller student LM from a one-way data pipeline into a bidirectional feedback loop. Prior to this work, the dominant paradigm for reasoning distillation—exemplified by Ho et al. (2023), Magister et al. (2023), Fu et al. (2023b), and Shridhar et al. (2023)—treated the LLM as a **data generation oracle**: feed it questions, collect its rationales, and fine-tune the student on the resulting static dataset. This paradigm implicitly assumes that the LLM's value lies in the *quality of its outputs*, and that those outputs are equally useful for any student regardless of that student's specific weaknesses. It is, effectively, a textbook model of teaching: the teacher produces generic correct answers and hopes the student absorbs them.

The paper challenges this assumption by arguing that the LLM's value as a reasoning teacher lies not merely in generating correct rationales but in generating **pedagogically targeted** rationales—responses that diagnose and address the student's specific errors. This reframing is evident in the paper's own language: "We expect the black-box LLM to be a reasoning teacher instead of a data annotator." The distinction is more than semantic. A data annotator needs only to produce correct outputs. A teacher needs to understand what the learner currently knows, what they get wrong, and why—information that can only come from the learner themselves.

The evidence that this reframing matters beyond rhetoric is visible in Table 2. When the teacher LLM generates a rationale without seeing the student's mistake, it produces a generic correct solution that may contain errors of its own (e.g., computing 190 instead of 196 in the GSM8K example). When the same teacher sees the student's wrong rationale—which explicitly reveals that the student failed to account for Mum's contribution and miscalculated the uncle's cars—the teacher produces a detailed, step-by-step correction that walks through exactly the relationships the student missed: "Dad: 10 cars, 5 less than Mum; Mum: 10 + 5 = 15 cars; Aunt: 6 cars, 1 more than uncle; Uncle: 6 - 1 = 5 cars; Grandpa: twice as many toy cars as the uncle, so 5 * 2 = 10 cars." The customized rationale is not merely correct—it is **structured as a response to the specific error**.

The interactive framing also explains why multi-round learning provides substantial cumulative gains (+16.4 percentage points on GSM8K beyond one-round distillation, from 15.6% to 32.0% in Table 1) while additional rounds of static distillation on the same generic data would not. The student's error patterns *change* across rounds: early errors involve basic arithmetic confusion, while later errors involve subtler multi-step reasoning failures. A teacher that receives updated feedback in each round adapts its instruction accordingly. A static annotator cannot.

This reframing is **fundamental rather than incremental** because it changes what information flows between the two models and, consequently, what role each model plays in the training process. It also connects reasoning distillation to a broader literature on interactive knowledge distillation (Fu et al., 2021; Pham et al., 2021; Ren et al., 2023) that had not previously been applied to the reasoning domain with black-box teachers.

### Innovation 2: Self-Reflection as a Complementary Learning Signal Distinct from Teacher Supervision

The paper introduces a **dual learning architecture** where the student trains on two qualitatively different signals: the external supervision of the teacher's correct rationales (language modeling loss) and the internal discriminative signal of comparing its own correct and incorrect outputs (contrastive triplet loss). Neither component is individually novel—language modeling fine-tuning on LLM outputs is the standard distillation recipe, and contrastive learning is well-established in representation learning. What is distinctive is the **integration** of these two signals into a single training loop within the reasoning distillation context, and the paper's argument that they serve **complementary rather than redundant functions**.

The language modeling objective teaches the student *what to generate*: given a question, produce a reasoning chain in the style of the teacher. But it provides only a weak signal about *what distinguishes good reasoning from bad*. A model trained purely on language modeling sees only positive examples (correct rationales); it never encounters negative examples and therefore never learns to identify the patterns that make reasoning incorrect. The contrastive triplet loss fills this gap by explicitly teaching the model to **separate correct and incorrect reasoning paths in representation space**. The anchor-positive pair (two different correct rationales for the same question) pulls correct reasoning paths together; the anchor-negative pair pushes incorrect paths away.

The evidence that these signals are genuinely complementary—rather than the contrastive loss being a minor regularizer—comes from three sources. First, the ablation in Table 1 shows that adding self-reflection to multi-round distillation yields consistent gains across all five datasets (+1.1 on GSM8K, +2.3 on MultiArith, +3.7 on SVAMP, +1.1 on CSQA, +0.4 on StrategyQA). These gains are smaller than the multi-round gain itself, but they are **orthogonal**: self-reflection adds value even when the teacher feedback is already customized. Second, Figure 4 provides qualitative evidence that the contrastive loss restructures the latent space in functionally meaningful ways—the t-SNE visualization shows substantially better separation between correct (blue) and incorrect (red) rationale clusters with self-reflection. Third, Table 4 quantifies this restructuring: the Euclidean distance between correct and incorrect representations increases from 51.00 to 65.08 on GSM8K, and the preference ratio (likelihood of correct over incorrect paths) increases from 73.63 to 79.11.

The innovation here is best understood as a **diagnostic concept** about what makes reasoning distillation effective. The paper does not claim that contrastive learning is new; it claims that the specific pairing of external supervision (teacher rationales) with internal discriminative learning (self-reflection on mistakes) fills a gap that neither mechanism addresses alone. The teacher tells the student *what correct reasoning looks like*; self-reflection teaches the student *why incorrect reasoning is incorrect*. In principle, a model trained only on positive examples could learn to generate correct reasoning by pattern matching without ever developing a representation that distinguishes good from bad reasoning paths. The contrastive loss ensures this distinction is explicitly encoded, which the paper argues improves both the quality of generated correct rationales (Table 6 shows GPT-4 quality scores increasing) and the model's ability to avoid repeating errors.

This contribution is **incremental in mechanism** (triplet losses are standard) but **conceptual in framing**—it identifies a specific limitation of pure language modeling for reasoning transfer and proposes a targeted remedy. The sensitivity analysis in Figure 5, showing that excessive contrastive weight degrades performance by causing language modeling underfitting (Figure 6), further confirms that the two signals must be balanced, not simply combined at any ratio.

### Innovation 3: The Exam-Step as a Unified Mechanism for Student Diagnosis and Training Data Curation

The paper introduces a specific **operational primitive**—the "exam step"—that serves as the linchpin connecting the interactive teacher-student loop and the self-reflection mechanism. In each round, the student LM generates outputs on the full training set, and incorrect outputs are harvested into a mistake set D_neg. This single data collection step serves three distinct functions simultaneously: (1) it provides diagnostic feedback to the teacher LLM about the student's current weaknesses, (2) it provides negative contrastive samples for self-reflection training, and (3) it acts as an implicit difficulty filter, identifying which training examples the student has not yet mastered and which it has.

The elegance of this design—which the paper does not explicitly call out but which is apparent from the architecture—is that it creates **aligned incentives** across the system's components. The teacher's customized rationales target exactly the examples where the student is struggling, which are also exactly the examples used as anchors in the contrastive loss. The same mistakes that inform the teacher also serve as the negative signal for representation learning. There is no separate data curation pipeline for each component; the exam step produces a single mistake set that feeds both downstream modules.

This unification has practical consequences visible in the data statistics of Table 5. As the student improves across rounds, the error rate on the training set drops (GSM8K: 98.3% → 76.3% → 66.2% → 49.2%), and the size of the generated training data shrinks accordingly (from ~15K to ~13K samples). This means the multi-round process is **self-regulating**: it automatically allocates more teacher attention to harder rounds and difficult problems, and less to rounds where the student has largely mastered the material. For SVAMP, the error rate drops to 24.0% after round 1 and 16.7% after round 2, causing the training data to shrink from 2K to 0.6K to 0.3K samples. The process naturally terminates when the student plateaus—not because of an arbitrary round limit, but because there are too few remaining mistakes to generate meaningful new training data.

The exam step can also be understood as a form of **online curriculum learning**. In standard curriculum learning, the training data ordering is pre-determined based on difficulty estimates. In this paper's approach, the curriculum emerges dynamically from the student's own error patterns: round 1 addresses the most common and basic errors (the low-hanging fruit), round 2 addresses errors that persist or emerge after the student has fixed the basic ones, and so on. The teacher's feedback in round 2 is qualitatively different from round 1 not because the problems are harder in an absolute sense, but because the student's specific failure modes have changed.

This innovation is **architectural rather than theoretical**—it is a design pattern that unifies multiple objectives within a single data collection primitive, reducing system complexity and ensuring consistency across components. Its significance lies in showing that the complex machinery of interactive distillation (teacher feedback loops, contrastive training, multi-round iteration) can be orchestrated through a single, simple operation: "run the current student on the training set and collect what it gets wrong."

### Innovation 4: Empirical Demonstration That Reasoning Distillation Effectiveness Depends on Task Type and Model Capacity

While the paper's primary contributions are methodological, its experimental analysis yields an **empirical finding** with implications that extend beyond the specific method: the effectiveness of multi-round interactive distillation and self-reflection is **not uniform across task types or model scales**, and the conditions under which each component helps (or hurts) provide diagnostic insight into the nature of reasoning transfer.

Specifically, the paper reveals a sharp distinction between mathematical reasoning and commonsense reasoning in their amenability to multi-round learning. On mathematical tasks (GSM8K, MultiArith, SVAMP), multi-round learning provides substantial cumulative gains above one-round distillation (+16.4, +1.6, and +3.6 percentage points respectively in Table 1). On commonsense tasks (CSQA, StrategyQA), the gains are more modest (+2.1 and +1.7). The feasibility study in Section 5.4 sharpens this distinction: for StrategyQA, multi-round learning provides **no consistent benefit** across model sizes and sometimes degrades performance (GPT-2 770M: −1.8; GPT-Neo 2.7B: −1.4 in Table 7). The paper attributes this to the nature of StrategyQA: it "heavily relies on the memorization of commonsense knowledge mostly acquired from the pre-training stage, rather than on complex reasoning."

This finding is significant because it **diagnoses a boundary condition** on the paper's own method. Multi-round interactive distillation improves reasoning *processes*—how the model chains together intermediate inferences, decomposes problems, and corrects flawed logical steps. If a task primarily tests knowledge retrieval rather than reasoning structure, additional rounds of customized feedback on reasoning paths provide little benefit because the bottleneck is not reasoning quality but knowledge coverage. The student already knows how to reason through StrategyQA-style problems after one round of distillation; what it lacks is the specific commonsense facts needed to answer correctly, and those are not teachable through rationale refinement.

The feasibility study also reveals that model capacity imposes a ceiling on what multi-round learning can achieve. The 760M T5-Large model reaches only 15.3% on SVAMP even after full multi-round self-reflection training, while the 6B GPT-J reaches 55.0%. The absolute gap between model sizes (39.7 percentage points) is far larger than the within-model gains from the method (~15 percentage points from baseline to full method for T5-Large). This empirically confirms the intuitive point that reasoning distillation amplifies existing capability but does not create it from nothing, and that the student's capacity fundamentally bounds how much of the teacher's reasoning ability can be transferred.

This innovation is an **empirical contribution with diagnostic value**. It does not propose a new technique but instead characterizes *when* the paper's technique works and *why* it sometimes fails—information that future practitioners need to decide whether to adopt the method for their specific task and model scale. The finding that commonsense reasoning and mathematical reasoning respond differently to iterative feedback loops suggests that "reasoning" is not a monolithic capability and that distillation strategies should be tailored to the specific type of reasoning being targeted—a nuance absent from prior distillation work, which treated all reasoning tasks as interchangeable.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on five reasoning benchmarks spanning two categories. Mathematical reasoning: GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021; 7,473 training / 1,319 test, original split), MultiArith (Roy and Roth, 2015; 420 training / 180 test, 70:30 split), SVAMP (Patel et al., 2021; 700 training / 300 test, 70:30 split). Commonsense reasoning: CSQA (Talmor et al., 2019; 9,741 training / 1,221 test, original split), StrategyQA (Geva et al., 2021; 1,603 training / 687 test, 70:30 split). The splits follow Ho et al. (2023) where not originally provided.

- **Base model(s).** The primary student LM is GPT-J (Wang and Komatsuzaki, 2021), a 6B-parameter publicly available decoder-only model. The teacher LLM is ChatGPT (175B, provided by OpenAI via API). For the feasibility study (Section 5.4), additional student models span 760M to 2.7B parameters: T5-Large (760M, encoder-decoder; Raffel et al., 2020), GPT-2 Large (770M, decoder-only; Radford et al., 2019), OPT-IML (1.3B, decoder-only; Iyer et al., 2023), and GPT-Neo (2.7B, decoder-only; Gao et al., 2020; Black et al., 2021). GPT-J is chosen as the main student to represent a model large enough to benefit from distillation but small enough to lack emergent reasoning (pass@1 of 2.7% on GSM8K without fine-tuning, Table 1).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **accuracy (%)** — the fraction of test questions for which the extracted final answer matches the ground truth. During inference, the model generates output in the format `"Reasoning: [rationale] \n Answer: [answer]"`, and answer extraction uses the first valid token after the `"Answer:"` delimiter (greedy decoding, no beam search). For the analysis in Section 5.2, additional metrics measure representation space structure: **Euclidean distance** between correct and incorrect reasoning path representations in latent space, and **preference ratio** (the likelihood ratio of correct reasoning paths to incorrect ones). Table 6 reports GPT-4 evaluation scores (1–5 scale) for rationale quality, used as a diagnostic metric for multi-round improvement.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five categories:
  1. **Teacher LLM (ChatGPT, 175B)**: the upper bound, evaluated with CoT prompting.
  2. **Student LM (GPT-J, 6B) without fine-tuning**: the lower bound, tested with CoT prompting (2.7% on GSM8K).
  3. **Student LM fine-tuned to directly output answers without rationales**: isolates the contribution of rationales to reasoning transfer (7.2% on GSM8K, Table 1).
  4. **STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022)**: self-iterative training where the model generates rationales, filters those with correct answers, and fine-tunes on them. Represents the best available approach without LLM assistance.
  5. **Concurrent distillation methods**: LLM-Adapter (Hu et al., 2023; GPT-J + LoRA fine-tuned on LLM CoT data), Specializing (Fu et al., 2023b; FlanT5-XXL 11B fine-tuned on 130K LLM rationales), CoT Fine-tuned (Magister et al., 2023; T5-11B fine-tuned on GPT-3 175B CoT data). These represent the static distillation paradigm.
  6. **One-Round Distillation (the paper's own first-round baseline)**: the student fine-tuned on generic teacher-generated rationales without student feedback or self-reflection. This is the direct ablation baseline for measuring gains from multi-round learning and self-reflection.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not measure compute in FLOPs or GPU-hours. Instead, the primary compute metric is the **number of API calls to the teacher LLM** for rationale generation, and the **number of training samples** generated per round (reported in Table 5 as "# Data"). API efficiency is measured by the **success rate** of teacher calls — the fraction of requested rationales that contain the correct answer (Table 3 reports "# Request" and "# Success"). Student training compute is held constant across methods (same number of epochs, same batch size). One generation from the student during the exam step costs 4 samples per question, and one request to the teacher generates up to 4 rationales, of which only correct-answer ones are retained.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation for the main results. Evaluation is on fixed test splits (the original or 70:30 splits listed in Table 8). The multi-round process uses early stopping based on training set error rate plateau — the paper "suggest[s] to take early stopping in the multi-round learning if the student can nearly reach its plateau" (Section 5.3) — but this is a manual heuristic rather than a formal statistical procedure. All experiments use a fixed random seed of 42 for reproducibility. No confidence intervals or statistical significance tests are reported for any result.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Effect of Distillation and the Importance of Rationales

The headline finding in Table 1 is that reasoning distillation works: the 6B GPT-J student achieves 15.6% on GSM8K after one-round distillation from ChatGPT, compared to 2.7% without fine-tuning and 7.2% with fine-tuning on answers alone (no rationales). The 8.4 percentage point gap between answer-only fine-tuning and rationale-based distillation establishes that rationales are the critical vehicle for reasoning transfer — merely learning to map questions to final answers is insufficient for multi-step reasoning.

On commonsense reasoning, the gap between student and teacher narrows substantially. The distilled student reaches 68.1% on CSQA (teacher: 76.0%) and 63.8% on StrategyQA (teacher: 68.6%), demonstrating that for tasks relying more on knowledge retrieval than multi-step reasoning, a 6B model can approach 175B-level performance after distillation. The 20× size reduction comes with only an 8-percentage-point drop on CSQA.

STaR, which relies on self-generated rationales without LLM assistance, achieves only 10.7% on GSM8K — better than the untrained baseline but well below any distillation method. The paper notes that STaR performs well on CSQA (72.5%, exceeding the one-round distillation baseline of 68.1%) because CSQA "often only involves single-step reasoning," making self-generated rationales more likely to be correct and useful for bootstrapping. On multi-step mathematical tasks, the bootstrap fails because the initial pass@1 is too low to generate sufficient correct training examples.

The concurrent static distillation methods — LLM-Adapter (10.6%), Specializing (27.1% with 11B parameters and 130K rationales), and CoT Fine-tuned (18.4% with 11B parameters) — all underperform the paper's full method (33.1%). The comparison with Specializing is particularly informative: the paper achieves better performance (33.1% vs. 27.1%) with a smaller model (6B vs. 11B) and fewer rationales (54K total over 3 rounds vs. 130K single-round), suggesting that rationale *quality* (customization) matters more than rationale *quantity*.

#### Gains from Multi-Round Learning

The multi-round paradigm provides the largest absolute improvements. On GSM8K, moving from one-round distillation (15.6%) to multi-round distillation without self-reflection (32.0%) yields a **+16.4 percentage point gain** (Table 1). This is the clearest evidence for the paper's central claim that interactive feedback outperforms static annotation. The gains on other datasets are more modest: +1.6 on MultiArith, +3.6 on SVAMP, +2.1 on CSQA, +1.7 on StrategyQA.

The diminishing gains pattern is visible in the round-by-round breakdown of Table 5. On GSM8K:
- Round 1: +12.9 percentage points (2.7% → 15.6%)
- Round 2: +12.6 (15.6% → 28.2%)
- Round 3: +2.4 (28.2% → 30.6%)
- Round 4 (conducted only for GSM8K to probe saturation): +1.4 (30.6% → 32.0%)

The large jump in round 2 demonstrates that the student's mistakes after round 1 are informative enough to enable the teacher to generate substantially more useful training data than the generic round-1 rationales. The drop-off in round 3 suggests the student is approaching its capacity ceiling — the error rate on the training set is still 49.2% (Table 5), but the remaining errors may be on problems too difficult for the 6B model to solve regardless of training data quality.

On SVAMP, the pattern is compressed: round 1 provides +27.0 points (20.7% → 47.7%), round 2 adds +3.6, and round 3 adds +1.0. The error rate drops to 16.7% after round 2, meaning only 0.6K new training samples are available for round 3 (vs. 2K for round 1), limiting the potential for further gains. On CSQA, round 3 actually shows a slight degradation (−0.6), confirming that the method should be early-stopped when performance plateaus.

Table 3 quantifies the **mechanism** behind multi-round gains by isolating the effect of student feedback in round 2. When student feedback is removed from the prompt template (the "w/o Feedback" ablation), the teacher's success rate drops — on GSM8K, 4,641 of 5,701 requests produce correct rationales with feedback vs. 4,641 without (the table shows identical "# Success" for the "w/o Feedback" row, suggesting an error in reporting, but the accuracy drops from 28.2% to 26.5%). On SVAMP, the impact is clearer: 166 successful responses with feedback vs. 140 without, and accuracy drops from 51.3% to 48.3%. On StrategyQA, the difference is dramatic: 317 successful responses with feedback vs. only 134 without, with accuracy dropping from 65.5% to 63.9%. The larger impact on StrategyQA (where the number of correct teacher responses more than doubles with feedback) suggests that for tasks requiring factual reasoning, the student's wrong rationale provides crucial context that helps the teacher generate factually correct solutions rather than plausible-sounding but incorrect ones.

#### Gains from Self-Reflection Learning

Adding self-reflection to the multi-round paradigm yields consistent but modest gains across all five datasets: +1.1 on GSM8K (32.0% → 33.1%), +2.3 on MultiArith (83.1% → 85.4%), +3.7 on SVAMP (51.3% → 55.0%), +1.1 on CSQA (70.2% → 71.3%), +0.4 on StrategyQA (65.5% → 65.9%) — all from Table 1.

The Mechanism evidence comes from Figure 4 and Table 4. The t-SNE visualization (Figure 4) shows that self-reflection produces clearer separation between correct (blue) and incorrect (red) rationale representations. Quantitatively (Table 4), the Euclidean distance between correct and incorrect reasoning paths increases from 51.00 to 65.08 (+27.6%) on GSM8K, and from 5.03 to 24.78 (+393%) on StrategyQA. The preference ratio — the likelihood of the model assigning higher probability to correct reasoning paths over incorrect ones — increases from 73.63 to 79.11 on GSM8K and from 96.54 to 98.91 on StrategyQA.

The sensitivity analysis in Figure 5 establishes the optimal λ range. On MultiArith, λ = 0.25 achieves the peak accuracy (84.9%), while λ = 0.5 (the paper's default) reaches 82.2%, and λ = 0.0 (no self-reflection) reaches 82.0%. On StrategyQA, λ = 0.25 also peaks (64.9%), with λ = 0.5 at 64.1% and λ = 0.0 at 63.6%. Values of λ ≥ 0.75 consistently degrade performance, with the paper attributing this to underfitting of the language modeling objective — Figure 6 shows that the language modeling loss remains higher (less converged) at step 1000 when λ is larger, indicating that the contrastive loss competes with rather than complements the primary training objective when over-weighted.

Table 6 provides additional indirect evidence: across rounds, the GPT-4 quality scores for correct rationales improve substantially (GSM8K: 2.59 → 4.50 → 4.88), while scores for incorrect rationales improve only modestly (1.02 → 1.15 → 1.26). This widening quality gap between correct and incorrect outputs is consistent with the contrastive loss's objective of separating the two classes in representation space, though the table does not isolate self-reflection's contribution specifically (the quality improvements could also arise from the multi-round teacher feedback alone).

#### Comparison with Concurrent Work and the Cost-Efficiency Argument

The paper claims that its method achieves better performance with lower data requirements than concurrent static distillation approaches. Specifically:

- **Specializing (Fu et al., 2023b)** trains an 11B FlanT5-XXL model on 130K GPT-3-generated rationales and achieves 27.1% on GSM8K. The paper's method trains a 6B GPT-J on approximately 54K total rationales (15K + 16K + 13K across 3 rounds, from Table 5) and achieves 33.1%.
- **CoT Fine-tuned (Magister et al., 2023)** trains an 11B T5 on GPT-3 rationales and reaches 18.4% on GSM8K.
- **LLM-Adapter (Hu et al., 2023)** fine-tunes GPT-J with LoRA adapters and achieves 10.6% on GSM8K.

The paper makes an implicit cost argument: its method is both more effective (higher accuracy) and more efficient (smaller model, fewer total rationales) than the best concurrent approach. However, this comparison conflates several variables — model architecture (GPT-J vs. FlanT5 vs. T5), model size (6B vs. 11B), and teacher LLM (ChatGPT vs. GPT-3) — making it difficult to attribute the performance gap solely to the interactive distillation method. A fairer comparison would control for model family and size, which the paper does not do.

#### Generalization Results

Table 11 reports cross-domain generalization: models trained on one dataset and evaluated on others. The key findings:

- **In-domain improvements are substantial and expected**: Training on GSM8K improves GSM8K accuracy from 2.7% to 32.0% (last round). Training on MultiArith improves MultiArith from 9.0% to 83.1%. Training on CSQA improves CSQA from 34.5% to 70.2%.
- **Out-of-domain (OOD) performance is mixed**: Training on GSM8K improves MultiArith from 9.0% to 80.3% (a large positive transfer) but degrades CSQA from 34.5% to 30.0% and StrategyQA from 47.2% to 38.3%. Training on StrategyQA improves GSM8K from 2.7% to 9.6% (modest positive transfer) and MultiArith from 9.0% to 9.6% (essentially flat). Training on CSQA degrades mathematical reasoning across the board (GSM8K drops from 2.7% to 2.3%, MultiArith drops from 9.0% to 5.1%).
- **Multi-round learning sometimes helps OOD and sometimes does not**: On GSM8K→MultiArith transfer, round 1 achieves 46.6% and the last round achieves 80.3% (substantial improvement with more rounds). On GSM8K→SVAMP transfer, round 1 achieves 25.3% and the last round achieves 42.3%. However, on MultiArith→GSM8K, round 1 achieves 4.7% and the last round achieves 5.0% (minimal gain).

The paper interprets the positive transfers cautiously, noting that "the customized training data of the following rounds may assists the model in generalizing its reasoning abilities to other domains." The negative transfers — particularly the degradation of mathematical reasoning after training on CSQA — suggest that the fine-tuning process can overwrite or interfere with capabilities, likely because CSQA's reasoning patterns (single-step, knowledge-retrieval-heavy) differ structurally from mathematical multi-step reasoning.

#### Feasibility Across Model Scales

Table 7 demonstrates that the method scales down to models as small as 760M parameters. On SVAMP:

- **T5-Large (760M)**: 0.0% → 11.0% (distillation) → 14.7% (+ self-reflection) → 15.3% (+ multi-round)
- **GPT-2 Large (770M)**: 2.7% → 13.3% → 15.3% → 17.0%
- **OPT-IML (1.3B)**: 5.3% → 31.7% → 32.0% → 35.0%
- **GPT-Neo (2.7B)**: 3.7% → 34.3% → 36.3% → 36.0%

The marginal gains from each component (distillation, self-reflection, multi-round) are consistent across model sizes, with distillation providing the largest jump and self-reflection typically adding +2–4 percentage points. Multi-round learning adds further gains for the three smaller models on SVAMP but shows a slight decline for the 2.7B model (−0.3), suggesting that for this model size on this task, the single-round approach already extracts most available gains.

On StrategyQA, the pattern differs: distillation achieves approximately 62% across all model sizes (62.0–62.2%), and subsequent components provide at most +3 percentage points. The paper attributes this to the nature of StrategyQA: since it "heavily relies on the memorization of commonsense knowledge mostly acquired from the pre-training stage," increasing model size from 760M to 2.7B does not improve performance, and additional rounds of training do not help — the bottleneck is knowledge coverage, not reasoning skill.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Student feedback in prompt template (Table 3):** Removing the student's wrong rationale from the teacher prompt template reduces the teacher's success rate at generating correct rationales. On StrategyQA, the success count drops from 317 to 134 (a 58% reduction), with accuracy falling from 65.5% to 63.9%. On GSM8K, the accuracy drops from 28.2% to 26.5% in round 2. The ablation validates that the customized data — not merely the fact of having LLM-generated rationales — drives the multi-round gains. Note: the GSM8K "# Success" numbers appear duplicated in Table 3 (5,250 for both conditions), which may indicate a typographical error in the paper; the accuracy difference (28.2% vs. 26.5%) is the more reliable signal.

**Self-reflection weight λ (Figure 5, Figure 6):** On MultiArith, λ = 0.25 achieves peak accuracy (84.9%), outperforming both λ = 0.0 (82.0%) and the paper's default λ = 0.5 (82.2%). On StrategyQA, λ = 0.25 also peaks (64.9%) vs. λ = 0.5 (64.1%) and λ = 0.0 (63.6%). The paper's pragmatic choice of λ = 0.5 ("without any heavy tuning," Section 4.3) is therefore suboptimal for at least one dataset. Figure 6 reveals the mechanism: higher λ values cause the language modeling loss ℒ_lm to converge more slowly, with λ = 1.0 showing substantially higher loss after 1000 steps than λ = 0.0. The contrastive loss ℒ_cl converges across all λ values tested. This suggests that the optimization conflict is asymmetric — the contrastive loss can be satisfied without interfering with its own convergence, but it slows down the language modeling convergence, creating a speed-accuracy tradeoff in the number of training steps needed.

**Multi-round convergence and early stopping (Table 5):** The round-by-round analysis shows that gains diminish and eventually plateau. GSM8K round 3 adds only +2.4 percentage points, round 4 adds +1.4. CSQA round 3 shows a −0.6 point degradation. SVAMP round 3 adds only +1.0. The student's training set error rate (ER) drops across rounds (GSM8K: 98.3 → 76.3 → 66.2 → 49.2%), and the size of generated training data shrinks accordingly, creating a natural termination condition: when too few mistakes remain to generate meaningful new customized data, additional rounds provide diminishing or negative returns.

**Demonstration weighting (Appendix A):** The paper empirically finds that assigning a weight of 0.1 to fixed demonstration tokens (vs. 1.0 to the actual question/rationale/answer tokens) during loss computation helps the model focus on the input rather than overfitting to the few-shot examples. This is reported as a pilot observation, not a full ablation, and the paper notes it "can be investigated in the future." No quantitative results are provided comparing different weighting schemes.

**Number of rounds for different tasks (Section 5.3, Table 5):** The paper implicitly ablates the number of rounds by running 4 rounds on GSM8K (where gains continue), 3 rounds on SVAMP and CSQA (where gains plateau or reverse), and 2 rounds in the feasibility study for StrategyQA (where additional rounds do not help). The recommendation to use early stopping based on training set error rate and performance gains is empirically grounded but not systematically optimized.

**Rationale diversity through sampling (Section 3.2):** The teacher generates 4 diverse rationales per question using sampling (temperature = 1.0, top-p = 0.9) rather than greedy decoding. The paper cites Ho et al. (2023) and Fu et al. (2023b) for the claim that "training with diverse reasoning paths can boost the reasoning performance." This is not ablated directly in the paper — there is no comparison between training on a single rationale per question vs. multiple diverse rationales.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "Multi-round interactive learning substantially outperforms static one-round distillation."

**Assessment:** This is the paper's central claim and the evidence is strong but not airtight. Table 1 shows +16.4 percentage points on GSM8K (15.6% → 32.0%) from adding multi-round to one-round distillation, and these gains are corroborated across four of five datasets (the exception being StrategyQA, where the gain is +1.7). Table 3 provides mechanistic evidence that student feedback improves teacher rationale quality and downstream accuracy. Table 5 shows the round-by-round progression and error rate reduction.

However, several qualifications are necessary:

1. **The comparison conflates more training data with better training data.** Multi-round learning generates additional training data in each round (15K → 16K → 13K for GSM8K in Table 5). The one-round baseline uses only the initial generic rationales. It is possible that simply generating multiple rounds of *generic* rationales (i.e., calling the teacher LLM more times without providing student feedback) would also improve performance, simply because the student sees more training examples. The "w/o Feedback" ablation in Table 3 partially addresses this by comparing round-2 performance with and without student feedback while holding the amount of data constant, but it does not compare against a "multi-round generic" baseline that would have the same total data volume as the interactive approach.

2. **The first round is not comparable to subsequent rounds in how data is collected.** Round 1 queries the teacher for *every* training example (the full D_train), while rounds 2+ query only for examples where the student made mistakes (D_neg). The data volume differs across rounds, and the difficulty distribution of questions generating teacher rationales shifts toward harder problems in later rounds. The multi-round gains might partly reflect the benefit of focusing training on harder (not-yet-mastered) examples rather than uniform coverage — an effect that could be achieved without the interactive paradigm if one had an alternative difficulty estimator.

3. **The teacher LLM and student LM are from different providers and architectures** (ChatGPT is a 175B instruction-tuned model; GPT-J is a 6B base model). The paper does not control for whether the gains are specific to this teacher-student pair or would generalize to other combinations (e.g., a LLaMA teacher with a LLaMA student, where architectural similarity might affect knowledge transfer).

#### Claim 2: "Self-reflection learning with contrastive triplet loss improves reasoning performance."

**Assessment:** Supported but the effect is small relative to the multi-round gain. Across five datasets, self-reflection adds +0.4 to +3.7 percentage points (Table 1), with an average gain of approximately +1.7. These gains are consistent in direction but modest in magnitude. The mechanistic evidence — t-SNE visualization (Figure 4), increased Euclidean distance (Table 4), and improved preference ratios — supports the claim that self-reflection restructures the representation space in intended ways. However, the paper does not demonstrate that this representation restructuring is *causally* responsible for the accuracy gains rather than being a correlated side effect. An experiment that would strengthen the claim: show that models with better separation in round N achieve higher accuracy gains in round N+1.

The sensitivity analysis (Figures 5, 6) also complicates the claim. On MultiArith, the optimal λ is 0.25 (84.9%), and the paper's chosen λ = 0.5 achieves lower performance (82.2%). This means the paper's reported results (using λ = 0.5) *understate* the potential benefit but also that the method is sensitive to hyperparameter choice — a practitioner adopting the method would need to tune λ per dataset, contrary to the paper's claim that λ = 0.5 works "without any heavy tuning." On StrategyQA, the optimal λ = 0.25 achieves 64.9% vs. 63.6% with no self-reflection — a gain of only 1.3 points, which may not justify the added complexity.

#### Claim 3: "The method outperforms concurrent distillation approaches while using fewer resources."

**Assessment:** Partially supported but the comparison is confounded. The paper achieves 33.1% on GSM8K with a 6B model and ~44K total teacher rationales (summing the "# Data" across rounds in Table 5) vs. Specializing's 27.1% with an 11B model and 130K rationales. This is directionally impressive. However:

1. **Model architectures differ.** GPT-J (the paper's student) vs. FlanT5-XXL (Specializing) vs. T5 (CoT Fine-tuned) are different model families with different pre-training data, tokenizers, and inductive biases. The 6B vs. 11B comparison is not a clean ablation of the distillation method — it compares both the method AND the base model.
2. **The teacher LLM differs.** The paper uses ChatGPT (GPT-3.5-Turbo); Specializing and CoT Fine-tuned use GPT-3 175B. ChatGPT is a more capable model (instruction-tuned, RLHF-trained), which may produce higher-quality rationales independent of the interactive paradigm.
3. **Total compute is not reported.** The paper's method requires multiple rounds of student inference (exam step), teacher API calls, and retraining. The total FLOPs or GPU-hours consumed across 3 rounds may exceed those of a single-round approach, even if the number of rationales is lower. The paper does not account for this.

#### Claim 4: "The method works for models as small as 760M parameters."

**Assessment:** Supported by Table 7, with the important caveat that absolute performance remains low for the smallest models (15.3% on SVAMP for 760M) and that gains from self-reflection and multi-round are modest. The 760M T5-Large gains 3.7 points from self-reflection and 0.6 from multi-round — the latter is within the range of run-to-run variance (not quantified in the paper). The claim that multi-round learning helps smaller models is shaky for StrategyQA, where it reduces performance for two of four models tested (−1.8 for 770M GPT-2, −1.4 for 2.7B GPT-Neo).

#### Missing Experiments That Would Strengthen the Paper

1. **Ablation of the teacher LLM:** The paper never replaces ChatGPT with a weaker teacher to test whether the interactive paradigm helps *because of* the teacher's capabilities or despite them. If a smaller open-source LLM (e.g., LLaMA 7B or 13B) could serve as the teacher, the method would be fully open-source and reproducible. The paper's reliance on a proprietary, closed-source, versioned API (ChatGPT between February–April 2023, per footnote 3) means results may not replicate if the underlying model changes.

2. **Ablation of the number of rationales per question:** The teacher generates up to 4 rationales per question. Would 1 rationale suffice (reducing API cost by 4×)? Would 8 rationales help further? The paper does not explore this tradeoff, which is practically important since API costs are the primary constraint on scaling the method.

3. **Ablation of the answer hint:** The teacher prompt includes the ground-truth answer ("Hint: The final answer should be [y]"). This makes the teacher's task substantially easier and may inflate the quality of generated rationales relative to what the teacher would produce unaided. Removing the hint would test whether the interactive paradigm works when the teacher must solve problems independently — a more realistic setting for tasks where ground-truth answers are not available for the training set.

4. **Experiments with open-source teacher LLMs:** The paper acknowledges in Limitations that "Open-source LLMs (e.g., LLaMA) have boomed in recent months" and that "validat[ing] our findings using more powerful LMs" is valuable. Running the full method with LLaMA 2 70B as the teacher would test generalizability beyond ChatGPT and provide a fully reproducible pipeline.

5. **Statistical significance:** With test sets as small as 180 questions (MultiArith) or 300 questions (SVAMP), a ±1–2 percentage point difference (the magnitude of self-reflection gains) may not be statistically significant. The paper reports no confidence intervals, standard deviations across runs, or significance tests.

6. **Negative results on the "wrong rationale" filtering criterion:** The paper judges rationale quality by final answer correctness. A rationale with correct reasoning but an arithmetic error in the final step is classified as "wrong"; a rationale with flawed reasoning that coincidentally reaches the right answer is "correct." The downstream impact of this label noise on the contrastive loss is not analyzed. An experiment comparing answer-based filtering vs. human-annotated rationale quality filtering would quantify this.

#### When the Claims Hold Conditionally

- **Task type matters**: Multi-round learning provides large gains for mathematical reasoning (GSM8K, SVAMP) but minimal gains for commonsense tasks (CSQA, StrategyQA). The method is most valuable when the target task requires multi-step inference that the student struggles with and can improve on through iterative feedback. For knowledge-retrieval-heavy tasks, the bottleneck is pre-training coverage, not reasoning process, and the interactive paradigm provides little benefit.
- **Model capacity matters**: The absolute gains from the full method are bounded by the student's capacity. A 760M model reaches 15.3% on SVAMP even with full multi-round self-reflection training (Table 7), while a 6B model reaches 55.0%. The method amplifies existing capability but cannot overcome fundamental capacity limitations — a finding consistent with the compute-optimal test-time scaling literature.
- **The method requires a capable teacher**: All experiments use ChatGPT (175B) as the teacher. The paper provides no evidence that the interactive paradigm works with weaker teachers. If the teacher cannot reliably solve the training problems (even with the answer hint), the quality of customized rationales degrades, and the feedback loop may amplify teacher errors rather than correct student errors.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 1. The Method Requires Ground-Truth Answers for Training Data Generation

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire training pipeline — both the teacher's rationale generation and the student's self-reflection learning — depends on having ground-truth answers `$y$` for every training question. The teacher's prompt template explicitly includes `"Hint: The final answer should be [y]"` (Figure 3, Section 3.2), which the paper justifies as improving accuracy and reducing API costs. The self-reflection loss requires classifying student outputs as correct or incorrect, which is done by comparing the extracted answer `$\hat{y}$` against the ground-truth `$y$` (Equation 1). Without ground-truth answers, neither the teacher's customized rationales nor the contrastive training signal can be generated.

**The consequence.** This restricts the method to tasks where the training set already contains verified answers — i.e., supervised datasets with clean answer labels. For open-ended generation, creative writing, summarization, dialogue, or any task where correctness is ambiguous or multi-dimensional, the entire pipeline breaks. The paper's approach cannot be applied to self-supervised or unsupervised settings where a model needs to improve its reasoning from unlabeled data alone. This also means that discoveries made during training (e.g., the teacher generating a valid reasoning path that leads to a different but correct answer) cannot be exploited — the system is locked to the provided ground truths, potentially discarding novel correct solutions that don't match the expected answer format.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper is transparent about using the answer hint but frames it as a practical optimization rather than a fundamental constraint: "to improve the LLM's accuracy and reduce the costs of calling APIs, we follow Zelikman et al. (2022) by adding a hint to explicitly tell LLM the golden answer of the question" (Section 3.2). The mistake collection criterion (Equation 1, Section 3.1) explicitly conditions on `$\hat{y} \neq y$`, requiring ground-truth `$y$`. No experiment removes the answer hint or tests performance on unlabeled data.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this. The Limitations section acknowledges the reliance on answer-based rationale evaluation ("evaluating the correctness of generated rationale is mainly based on the final answer") and calls for "more trustworthy criteria to evaluate the quality of rationales," suggesting potential methods like "GPT-4 or a process reward model for automatic evaluation." However, this only addresses the *evaluation* side (judging rationale quality without answer matching), not the *teacher guidance* side — removing the answer hint from the teacher prompt would fundamentally change the quality and accuracy of generated rationales, an effect the paper does not measure.

---

### 2. The Bottleneck of Closed-Source, API-Based Teachers: Reproducibility, Cost, and Terms of Use

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire method depends on a black-box, closed-source LLM (ChatGPT, accessed via OpenAI's API between February and April 2023) as the reasoning teacher. This is not merely an implementation detail — the teacher must be capable of (a) understanding the student's wrong rationale, (b) diagnosing the error, and (c) generating a corrected reasoning path. These capabilities are specific to instruction-tuned, large-scale models like ChatGPT and may not transfer to weaker or differently-trained teachers.

**The consequence.** Three distinct problems arise. First, **reproducibility**: ChatGPT is a versioned, proprietary model that can change behavior at any time. The specific model accessed between February–April 2023 may produce different outputs than a later version, making exact replication impossible without archiving the API version. Second, **cost and scalability**: each round requires thousands of API calls (Table 3 reports 5,701 requests for GSM8K round 2 alone). Across three rounds and five datasets, the total number of API calls is substantial, and the cost scales with training set size and number of rounds — the paper does not report total API expenditure. Third, **legal constraint**: the paper's Ethics Statement explicitly notes that OpenAI's terms of use contain "a prohibition against 'use output from the Services to develop models that compete with OpenAI,'" creating ambiguity about whether models trained via this method violate service terms. For researchers or organizations seeking to build reasoning-capable models, this legal gray zone is a practical barrier.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper acknowledges the closed-source dependency in the Ethics Statement: "the annotated rationales in this paper are collected from close-source ChatGPT provided by OpenAI." It also notes that "Open-source LLMs (e.g., LLaMA) have boomed in recent months" and suggests validation using such models as future work. The paper never ablates the teacher — all experiments use ChatGPT, providing no evidence that the method works with open-source alternatives or even other closed-source teachers (e.g., Claude, GPT-4). The feasibility study (Section 5.4) varies the *student* model but keeps the teacher fixed.

**Mitigation status.** The paper suggests future work to "validat[e] our findings using more powerful LMs (e.g., LLaMA)" in the Limitations section, but this suggestion is about validating the student side, not the teacher. No experiment replaces ChatGPT with an open-source teacher, nor does any ablation test whether teacher quality (rather than the interactive paradigm itself) drives the gains. The cost issue is not addressed quantitatively — no total API call count or dollar estimate is provided.

---

### 3. The Gap Between Customized Rationale Quality Improvement and Student Accuracy Gains Narrows Sharply After Round 2

**The assumption or constraint.** The method assumes that continuing the interactive feedback loop will yield sustained improvements, and the paper presents the multi-round paradigm as a general strategy for iteratively improving reasoning. However, the empirical evidence shows that gains diminish rapidly, with most of the improvement concentrated in rounds 1–2 and round 3+ providing marginal or even negative returns.

**The consequence.** A practitioner implementing this method faces a sharp cost-benefit cliff: rounds 1–2 produce large gains that likely justify the API cost and training time, but rounds 3+ consume comparable resources (API calls, GPU training time) while delivering single-digit accuracy improvements that may not be statistically distinguishable from noise. On CSQA, round 3 actually degrades performance (−0.6 percentage points, Table 5), meaning the practitioner must monitor validation performance to avoid actively hurting the model. The paper provides no principled stopping criterion beyond manual observation of plateau, leaving practitioners to guess when additional rounds have become wasteful.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 5 quantifies this directly. On GSM8K: round 1 adds +12.9 points, round 2 adds +12.6, round 3 adds +2.4, round 4 adds +1.4. The training set error rate drops from 76.3% (after round 1) to 66.2% (after round 2) to 49.2% (after round 3), but the generated training data shrinks from 15K to 16K to 13K samples. On SVAMP: +27.0, +3.6, +1.0. On CSQA: +31.8, +3.9, −0.6. The paper's own recommendation reflects this observed pattern: "we suggest to take early stopping in the multi-round learning if the student can nearly reach its plateau" (Section 5.3). But "nearly reach its plateau" is defined post-hoc based on knowing the test accuracy curve — a practitioner would need a validation set to estimate when this occurs.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses this by recommending early stopping based on training set error rate and per-round performance gains (Section 5.3). However, this recommendation is qualitative — no specific threshold is given (e.g., "stop when per-round gain drops below 2%"), no validation-set based stopping protocol is evaluated, and the analysis in Section 5.3 uses test-set accuracy (which a practitioner would not have access to during training) to characterize the plateau. The paper also notes that "model capacity" limits further gains (the student "is reaching its capacity after 3 rounds of learning, still not performing well (49.2 ER)" on GSM8K), acknowledging an inherent ceiling but not providing a method to predict where that ceiling lies before training begins.

---

### 4. Rationale Quality Is Judged Solely by Final Answer Correctness, Creating Label Noise

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper classifies every student-generated rationale as "correct" or "incorrect" based exclusively on whether the extracted final answer matches the ground truth (Section 3.1): `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{neg}} = \{(x, \hat{r}, \hat{y}) \mid \hat{y} \neq y\}$`. This binary criterion is used for three critical purposes: (1) filtering which student outputs count as "mistakes" to send to the teacher, (2) selecting which teacher rationales to retain (only those with correct answers), and (3) determining the positive/negative labels for the contrastive self-reflection loss.

**The consequence.** Two types of label noise systematically contaminate the training process. A student rationale that contains valid intermediate reasoning but makes an arithmetic error in the final calculation is classified as "wrong," providing a negative contrastive signal even though most of the reasoning is sound. Conversely, a rationale that contains flawed or nonsensical intermediate steps but coincidentally arrives at the correct final answer is classified as "correct" and used as a positive anchor in the contrastive loss, teaching the model that flawed reasoning patterns should be grouped with genuinely correct ones. The downstream effect is that the self-reflection loss receives noisy supervision — the representations being pushed apart or pulled together may not actually correspond to "good reasoning" vs. "bad reasoning" but rather to "coincidentally correct answer" vs. "arithmetically sloppy." This is particularly problematic on multiple-choice tasks (CSQA, StrategyQA), where random guessing yields correct answers at rates of 20% or 50% respectively, inflating the "correct" rationale set with reasoning that is no better than chance.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper explicitly acknowledges this limitation: "Evaluating the correctness of generated rationale is mainly based on the final answer. Though most existing works... adopt this simple criterion, we call attention to develop more trustworthy criteria to evaluate the quality of rationales" (Limitations). However, no experiment quantifies the extent of label noise or its impact. There is no comparison against a cleaner labeling scheme (e.g., human evaluation of a subset of rationales, or GPT-4-based step-level evaluation as the paper itself suggests). The paper does not measure the false-positive rate (wrong rationales classified as correct) or false-negative rate (correctly-reasoned rationales classified as wrong) on any dataset. The contrastive loss sensitivity analysis (Figure 5, Figure 6) shows that over-weighting the self-reflection signal degrades performance, which could be partly caused by noisy labels rather than the optimization conflict the paper attributes it to — this alternative explanation is not considered.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not mitigate this. It suggests future work on "develop[ing] more trustworthy criteria to evaluate the quality of rationales" and proposes "GPT-4 or a process reward model for automatic evaluation." The feasibility study (Section 5.4) for StrategyQA is potentially affected: the paper finds that multi-round learning provides no benefit on this task, which could be partly because the 50% random baseline for yes/no questions means a large fraction of student outputs classified as "correct" in the exam step are actually random guesses, providing poor diagnostic feedback to the teacher and noisy contrastive labels.

---

### 5. The Method Has Not Been Demonstrated to Generalize Across Task Families, Model Families, or Teacher-Student Pairs

**The assumption or constraint.** All main experiments use a single student architecture (GPT-J, 6B, decoder-only), a single teacher (ChatGPT, 175B, instruction-tuned), and evaluate on reasoning benchmarks from two families (mathematical: GSM8K, MultiArith, SVAMP; commonsense: CSQA, StrategyQA). The paper implicitly assumes that the findings — interactive feedback outperforms static distillation, self-reflection provides complementary gains — will hold across other models, tasks, and teacher-student pairings.

**The consequence.** A practitioner considering this method for a different setting has limited evidence to predict whether it will work. Key open questions include: Do the gains from interactive feedback depend on the architectural similarity between teacher and student (GPT-J is decoder-only; ChatGPT is decoder-only; would an encoder-decoder student like T5 benefit differently)? Does the method work with open-source teachers (LLaMA, Mistral) that may generate lower-quality rationales? Would the findings hold for code generation, scientific reasoning, or legal reasoning tasks? The feasibility study (Section 5.4) partially addresses student model variation (testing T5, GPT-2, OPT-IML, and GPT-Neo at 760M–2.7B scales), but the teachers remain ChatGPT (175B) and the tasks remain SVAMP and StrategyQA — only two of the five original benchmarks, and only one from each reasoning family.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The generalization experiments in Table 11 test cross-dataset transfer (e.g., training on GSM8K and evaluating on MultiArith) but not cross-model or cross-teacher transfer. The paper's concurrent work comparisons (Specializing, CoT Fine-tuned, LLM-Adapter in Table 1) use different student models (FlanT5-XXL, T5-11B, GPT-J with LoRA) and different teachers (GPT-3 175B), but these are independent methods with their own training pipelines, not controlled comparisons where only the teacher or student is varied. The feasibility study (Section 5.4, Table 7) shows that the method works for smaller student models but does not replace the teacher. The paper acknowledges in Limitations that "validat[ing] our findings using more powerful LMs (e.g., LLaMA)" is important future work, but this suggestion is directed at the student side.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's single teacher, single architecture, and two task families represent a narrow slice of the space of possible configurations. The positive results on CSQA (where STaR actually outperforms one-round distillation, 72.5% vs. 68.1%) and the failure of multi-round learning on StrategyQA (Table 7, where it degrades performance for two model sizes) already hint that findings are task-dependent, but the paper does not systematically characterize *which* task properties predict method effectiveness.

### 6. The Absence of Statistical Rigor Makes the Smaller Gains Difficult to Interpret

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper reports all results as point estimates (single accuracy percentages) without confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical significance tests. The fixed random seed of 42 (Appendix A) ensures reproducibility of the exact numbers but does not provide information about variance across training runs. Test sets range from 180 questions (MultiArith) to 1,319 questions (GSM8K), meaning that a ±1 percentage point change corresponds to ±2 questions on the smallest set — well within the range of sampling noise.

**The consequence.** Several of the paper's claimed contributions involve gains of 1–3 percentage points: self-reflection adds +1.1 on GSM8K, +2.3 on MultiArith, +3.7 on SVAMP, +1.1 on CSQA, and +0.4 on StrategyQA (Table 1). The round 3 gains are even smaller: +2.4 on GSM8K, +1.0 on SVAMP, +1.0 on MultiArith (implied), and −0.6 on CSQA (Table 5). On the smallest test set (MultiArith, 180 questions), a +2.3 point gain represents approximately 4 additional correct answers. Without variance estimates, a practitioner cannot determine whether these gains are reliably achievable or are within the noise floor of the fine-tuning process. This is particularly relevant for the self-reflection component: the paper recommends λ = 0.5 as a default value (Section 4.3), but Figure 5 shows that the optimal λ on MultiArith (0.25) achieves 84.9% vs. 82.2% at λ = 0.5 — a 2.7 point difference that may or may not be statistically significant at the 180-question test set size.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports no variance metrics anywhere. The main results table (Table 1) contains only point estimates. The round-by-round analysis (Table 5) reports accuracy changes (∆) without error bars. The multi-round learning curves on GSM8K are described by three data points (round 1, 2, 3 accuracy) with no indication of run-to-run variability. The paper's recommendation to use early stopping based on per-round performance gains (Section 5.3) is made without quantifying how reliably those gains can be estimated from the available data.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. This is a methodological weakness common in the reasoning distillation literature at the time of the paper's writing (none of the baselines in Table 1 — STaR, Specializing, CoT Fine-tuned, LLM-Adapter — report statistical significance either), but it is particularly consequential for a paper where multiple claimed contributions are small in absolute magnitude and where the practical recommendation (adopt multi-round + self-reflection) carries additional computational cost that must be justified against uncertain marginal gains.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **conceptual reframing** rather than a revolutionary algorithmic breakthrough, but the reframing has substantial practical consequences that change how the field should think about reasoning distillation. The shift is from viewing black-box LLMs as **static data generation oracles** to viewing them as **interactive reasoning teachers**—entities that need diagnostic information about the learner to teach effectively. This distinction matters because it changes the design of distillation pipelines at the architectural level: instead of a single annotation phase followed by fine-tuning, the system now requires a feedback channel through which the student's mistakes flow back to the teacher, and a mechanism (the exam step) for collecting those mistakes.

The paper resolves a latent tension in the reasoning distillation literature that had not been explicitly articulated. Prior work (Ho et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2023b; Magister et al., 2023) demonstrated that LLM-generated rationales could improve smaller LMs' reasoning, but the gains hit a ceiling well below teacher performance—the 11B Specializing model reached only 27.1% on GSM8K compared to the teacher's 62.2%. The standard interpretation was that model capacity limits transfer. This paper demonstrates an alternative explanation: **the ceiling was partly an artifact of the static distillation paradigm**, not purely a capacity constraint. By making the training data responsive to the student's specific errors, the paper extracts substantially more reasoning capability from the same 6B model (33.1% vs. the 15.6% static baseline), suggesting that the teacher's value was being underexploited because it was generating "generic correct answers" rather than "targeted instruction."

The paper also provides the first empirical evidence that **the LLM's in-context learning ability can be leveraged for pedagogical diagnosis**, not just for task solving. When the teacher LLM sees the student's wrong rationale in the prompt template (Figure 3), it produces rationales that are not merely correct but specifically structured to address the student's error—the GSM8K Example #1 in Table 2 shows the teacher decomposing the problem differently when it sees that the student skipped the computation of Mum's apples. This implies that the LLM's ability to "understand" error patterns and generate corrective responses is a distinct capability from its ability to solve problems directly, and that this capability is currently underutilized in distillation work.

A more subtle landscape shift concerns the **relationship between reasoning transfer and task type**. The paper's finding that multi-round learning provides large gains on mathematical reasoning (+16.4 on GSM8K) but minimal gains on commonsense tasks (+2.1 on CSQA in Table 1, and no consistent benefit for StrategyQA in the feasibility study, Table 7) suggests that "reasoning" is not a unitary capability that transfers uniformly. Mathematical reasoning benefits from iterative feedback because it involves multi-step inference chains where errors compound and where customized correction (showing exactly which step was wrong) is pedagogically valuable. Commonsense reasoning, particularly in tasks like StrategyQA that require retrieving and applying factual knowledge, benefits primarily from the first round of distillation (which provides knowledge coverage) and sees little additional benefit from iterative error correction—the bottleneck is not the reasoning *process* but the knowledge *base*, which iterative feedback on reasoning paths cannot expand. This finding should cause the field to be more precise about what kind of reasoning is being distilled and whether interactive paradigms are appropriate for that type.

The paper also redirects research attention toward a previously underappreciated aspect of distillation: **the quality and specificity of the teacher's rationales matters more than their quantity**. The paper achieves 33.1% on GSM8K with ~44K total rationales (Table 5), compared to Specializing's 27.1% with 130K rationales. If this finding generalizes, the community should invest less in scaling up annotation volume (calling the teacher more times) and more in making each annotation maximally informative (by incorporating student feedback). This shifts the bottleneck from "how many teacher calls can we afford" to "how can we make each teacher call count more," which has different implications for API cost optimization and data curation strategies.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Replacing the answer hint with verifier-guided rationale selection.** The paper's reliance on ground-truth answers in the teacher prompt template ("Hint: The final answer should be [y]") is a practical weakness that restricts the method to labeled training sets. A direct follow-up would replace the hint with a **process reward model (PRM)** or outcome reward model (ORM) that scores teacher-generated rationales for correctness without access to the ground truth. Specifically: for each training question, request the teacher LLM to generate N candidate rationales *without* the answer hint, score each using a verifier trained via the Monte Carlo rollout procedure from Lightman et al. (2023) or Wang et al. (2023), and retain only rationales above a confidence threshold. This would test whether the interactive paradigm works when the teacher must solve problems independently, making the method applicable to unlabeled datasets. The key measurement would be: does removing the hint reduce teacher rationale quality, and if so, can verifier-guided filtering recover the loss? A negative result—verifier-filtered rationales being substantially worse than hint-guided ones—would reveal that the teacher's pedagogical value depends on knowing the target answer, which would limit the method's applicability to supervised settings. A positive result would open up semi-supervised or self-supervised reasoning distillation.

**Ablating the teacher LLM: does interactive distillation work with open-source teachers?** The paper exclusively uses ChatGPT (175B) as the teacher, making the pipeline dependent on a closed-source, versioned, paid API. A critical stress-test would replace ChatGPT with open-source models of varying capability: LLaMA 2 7B, 13B, and 70B. This would answer three questions simultaneously. First, does the interactive paradigm require a teacher that is qualitatively stronger than the student (175B vs. 6B), or would a moderately stronger teacher (13B vs. 6B) suffice? Second, is the benefit of student feedback teacher-dependent—do weaker teachers produce better customized rationales when shown student mistakes, or do they simply reproduce the error without effective correction? Third, can the method be fully open-sourced? The experiment would train GPT-J (6B) using each teacher, with the full multi-round + self-reflection pipeline, measuring whether the relative gain from interactive vs. static distillation holds across teacher scales. A negative result—interactive distillation providing no benefit with weaker teachers—would reveal that the method exploits the specific diagnostic capabilities of large instruction-tuned models and does not generalize.

**Testing whether the contrastive self-reflection loss genuinely improves reasoning or merely reshapes representations.** The paper demonstrates that self-reflection increases Euclidean distance between correct and incorrect rationale representations (Table 4) and improves t-SNE cluster separation (Figure 4), and argues that this restructuring causes accuracy gains. But this is correlational, not causal. A follow-up experiment would isolate the effect: train two models with identical teacher rationales and language modeling objectives, one with the contrastive triplet loss and one with a control loss that reshapes representations in an unrelated way (e.g., a contrastive loss that groups rationales by length rather than correctness, or by which teacher generated them). If the control loss produces similar representation separation but no accuracy gain, then the paper's representational argument is weakened—the accuracy gains come from something else about the triplet formulation (perhaps the specific geometry it enforces), and the observed t-SNE separation is a side effect. If the control loss produces no accuracy gain and no separation, while the correctness-based loss produces both, the causal link is strengthened. This experiment would clarify whether the paper has discovered a general principle (contrastive losses on correctness improve reasoning) or a specific useful trick that happens to work for unclear reasons.

**Combining interactive distillation with iterative self-improvement (STaR + tailored learning).** The paper positions STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) as a baseline and shows that it underperforms LLM-guided distillation on multi-step reasoning (10.7% vs. 15.6% on GSM8K, Table 1). But these approaches are not mutually exclusive: STaR's self-generated rationales could be *sent to the teacher as additional feedback*, and the teacher's customized rationales could be used to *augment* STaR's self-training loop. A concrete implementation: after each round of STaR self-training, collect the student's mistakes, send them to the teacher for customized correction, and add the corrected rationales to the next round's training data alongside the student's own correct self-generated rationales. This hybrid would combine STaR's strength (learning from self-generated correct paths, which the paper shows works well for single-step tasks like CSQA, where STaR achieves 72.5% vs. distillation's 68.1%) with the paper's strength (teacher-customized correction for multi-step errors). The key measurement would be whether the hybrid outperforms either approach alone on both mathematical and commonsense tasks, and whether the teacher's corrections reduce the "false positive" problem in STaR where incorrectly reasoned but answer-correct outputs contaminate training.

**Characterizing the label noise problem from answer-based rationale filtering.** The paper acknowledges but does not quantify the noise introduced by judging rationale quality solely on final answer correctness (Section 3.1, Limitations). A diagnostic experiment would: (1) take a subset of student-generated rationales from the exam step (e.g., 500 from GSM8K), (2) obtain human annotations or GPT-4 evaluations of step-by-step reasoning quality independent of the final answer, (3) measure the false-positive rate (wrong reasoning classified as correct because the answer matches) and false-negative rate (correct reasoning classified as wrong because of an arithmetic slip), and (4) measure how the contrastive loss performance changes when trained with clean vs. noisy labels. This would quantify whether the self-reflection gain (+1.1 on GSM8K) is an underestimate (because noisy labels dilute the contrastive signal) or an overestimate (because the loss is fitting noise patterns). If noise is substantial (>20% misclassification), developing better rationale quality filters becomes a prerequisite for scaling self-reflection to harder tasks where the base model's pass@1 is even lower and the proportion of "accidentally correct" rationales is higher.

**Scaling the approach to code generation and multi-step planning tasks.** The paper evaluates exclusively on mathematical and commonsense reasoning. Code generation (HumanEval, MBPP) provides an ideal testbed for the interactive paradigm because: (a) correctness is verifiable automatically through unit test execution, eliminating the need for answer hints or manual labels; (b) errors are often structural (the model's code produces a wrong output for a specific input, or fails on edge cases), providing rich diagnostic information when the student's wrong code is shown to the teacher; (c) diverse correct solutions exist (multiple algorithms can solve the same problem), testing the claim that rationale diversity improves generalization. The experiment would replicate the full multi-round + self-reflection pipeline on code generation, using the student's failing unit test outputs (not the full wrong code, which might be too long) as feedback to the teacher. A positive result—gains comparable to or exceeding those on GSM8K—would demonstrate that the interactive paradigm generalizes beyond natural language reasoning to formal symbolic reasoning. A negative result—no gain beyond static distillation—would suggest that code errors require a different feedback format (e.g., the teacher needs to see the full trace, not just the final output) and that the paradigm is specific to natural language reasoning chains.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient training of open-source reasoning models on proprietary benchmarks.** An organization with access to a proprietary LLM API (ChatGPT, Claude, GPT-4) but wanting to deploy an open-source model for reasoning tasks can use this method to maximize reasoning transfer per API dollar. The cost calculus is: one-round static distillation on GSM8K achieves 15.6% accuracy; reaching 33.1% with the full method requires ~44K total API calls across 3 rounds (Table 5). If each API call costs ~$0.002 (approximate ChatGPT pricing at the time), the total teacher cost is ~$88 for a 6B model reaching 33.1% on GSM8K—a fraction of the cost of training a larger model from scratch. For a team building a math tutoring application, this means they can deploy a small, fast, self-hosted model that handles a third of GSM8K-level problems correctly, routing harder problems to a human tutor or a larger model. The key benefit is that the *per-example ROI of API calls is higher* in the interactive paradigm because the teacher's responses are targeted at the specific errors the deployed model actually makes.

**Bootstrapping reasoning in extremely small models for on-device deployment.** The feasibility study (Section 5.4, Table 7) shows that the method works at the 760M–1.3B parameter scale, with models like OPT-IML 1.3B reaching 35.0% on SVAMP after full multi-round + self-reflection training. For on-device scenarios—smartphone keyboards that suggest reasoning steps, offline educational apps that help students work through math problems, or embedded systems that need to perform logical inference without cloud connectivity—this is practically significant. A 1.3B model can run on a modern phone CPU with quantization; a 175B model cannot. The pipeline enables taking a small, fast model and injecting enough reasoning capability to handle a meaningful fraction of queries offline, with the interactive teacher providing training data customized to the distribution of errors that the small model will encounter in deployment. The marginal cost of the distillation (API calls to a teacher LLM) is a one-time training expense, not a per-query inference cost.

**Curriculum design for adaptive learning systems.** The paper's exam-step mechanism—running the student on the full training set and collecting only its mistakes for targeted instruction—is effectively an automated curriculum generator. Each round identifies exactly which problems the current student cannot solve, sends those to the teacher for customized instruction, and leaves already-mastered problems out of the next training round (Table 5 shows training data shrinking from 15K to 13K samples as the error rate drops). An educational technology company building an AI tutor could use this pipeline to generate personalized problem sets and explanations: a student (human or LM) attempts problems, wrong answers are sent to an LLM that generates step-by-step corrections targeted at the specific errors, and the student practices on similar-category problems in the next round. The paper's finding that this works for mathematical reasoning (where errors follow identifiable patterns like "skipped a step" or "confused subtraction with division") but not as well for commonsense tasks (where errors reflect knowledge gaps rather than process failures) provides a diagnostic for which subjects an adaptive tutoring system based on this approach would be effective for.

**Data augmentation for reasoning datasets with limited annotations.** Many reasoning datasets (especially in specialized domains like medical diagnosis, legal reasoning, or scientific inference) have question-answer pairs but lack rationale annotations. Hiring domain experts to write step-by-step reasoning chains is expensive. The paper's pipeline—requesting an LLM to generate rationales, filtering by answer correctness, and iteratively improving them based on a trained student's errors—provides a recipe for generating high-quality rationale annotations at scale. The key value-add over simply asking an LLM to annotate the dataset once is the iterative refinement: after training an initial model on the LLM's generic rationales, that model's mistakes reveal which rationales were insufficient or confusing, and the teacher can regenerate them with better explanations. The result is a rationale-annotated dataset that has been "field-tested" against an actual learner's errors, potentially yielding more pedagogically useful explanations than single-pass LLM annotations. The paper's Table 6 evidence—that rationale quality scores (rated by GPT-4) improve from 2.59 to 4.88 on GSM8K across rounds—directly supports the claim that iterative refinement produces better rationales, not just more of them.
