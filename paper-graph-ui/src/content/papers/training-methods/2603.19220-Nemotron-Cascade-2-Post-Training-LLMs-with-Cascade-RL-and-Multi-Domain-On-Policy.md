# Nemotron-Cascade 2: Post-Training LLMs with Cascade RL and Multi-Domain On-Policy Distillation

**ArXiv:** [2603.19220](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19220)

## 🎯 Pitch

A 30B MoE model with just 3B active parameters achieves Olympic-level reasoning—gold medals at IMO 2025, IOI 2025, and ICPC World Finals—matching 20× larger systems. The key is aligning multi-domain RL stages with on-policy distillation from specialized teachers, crushing the usual trade-off between reasoning prowess and general instruction-following.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Nemotron-Cascade 2**, an open 30B Mixture-of-Experts model with 3B activated parameters post-trained using an expanded **Cascade RL** framework—sequentially applying domain-specific reinforcement learning stages (instruction-following RL, multi-domain RL, RLHF, long-context RL, code RL, and SWE RL)—integrated with **multi-domain on-policy distillation (MOPD)** from intermediate teacher checkpoints to recover benchmark regressions and sustain performance gains. Evaluated across reasoning benchmarks, competitive programming contests, and agentic tasks, the model achieves gold-medal performance at IMO 2025, IOI 2025, and the ICPC World Finals 2025, becoming the second open-weight LLM after DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale-671B-A37B to do so with roughly 20× fewer parameters, while also reaching state-of-the-art instruction-following accuracy of 83.5% on ArenaHard v2 and 82.9% on IFBench. The MOPD stage demonstrates substantially faster convergence than comparable GRPO-based RLHF—reaching 85.5 on ArenaHard Hard Prompt in 52 steps versus 160 steps for RLHF—establishing that dense token-level distillation advantages from capability-diverse teacher checkpoints can stabilize multi-stage post-training pipelines.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Scaling Multi-Domain RL Without Catastrophic Forgetting

The fundamental challenge this paper tackles is one of *coordination* rather than invention. Reinforcement learning has become the dominant paradigm for post-training large language models, driving advances in reasoning capabilities (Guo et al., 2025), instruction following (Ouyang et al., 2022), and agentic behavior. However, as the ambitions of model developers grow—requiring a single model to excel simultaneously at mathematical proof construction, competitive programming, multi-turn tool use, long-context reasoning, and human preference alignment—a critical tension emerges: **how do you train a model on many diverse RL environments without one capability cannibalizing another?**

This tension is not merely an inconvenience. It represents a fundamental obstacle to building general-purpose reasoning models. A model that achieves breakthrough mathematical performance through reinforcement learning on verifiable math rewards may, in the process, lose its ability to follow nuanced instructions or produce natural-sounding dialogue. Conversely, RLHF optimization for human preferences can trade off against the structured, constraint-following behavior that makes a model reliable on objective benchmarks. The problem is one of **catastrophic forgetting across RL stages**, where gains in a newly trained domain come at the expense of capabilities acquired in earlier stages.

Prior to Nemotron-Cascade 1 (Wang et al., 2025), the dominant approach to multi-domain RL was a "kitchen sink" strategy: mix all RL training data together and train jointly. This approach is conceptually simple but suffers from several practical pathologies:

- **Inter-domain interference**: Different domains have conflicting reward signals. Instruction-following RL rewards strict constraint adherence (e.g., "respond in under 200 words"), while RLHF rewards natural, helpful responses that may naturally run longer. Training on both simultaneously creates a tug-of-war where the gradient signals partially cancel.

- **Response length heterogeneity**: Math RLVR problems generate long reasoning traces (potentially tens of thousands of tokens), while IF-RL problems often require concise outputs. In a joint training setup, the straggler problem emerges—compute resources sit idle waiting for the longest generations to complete, making training inefficient.

- **Verification cost heterogeneity**: Verifying a math solution requires running code or checking against a ground-truth answer (fast, parallelizable), while verifying a software engineering agent trajectory requires executing code in instrumented Docker environments (slow, stateful). Joint training forces all workers to wait for the slowest verifications.

- **Curriculum rigidity**: Joint training applies uniform hyperparameters across domains, preventing domain-specific optimization of learning rates, rollout sizes, or temperature schedules. What stabilizes code RL may destabilize RLHF, and vice versa.

### Cascade RL: A Partial Solution with a Remaining Gap

Nemotron-Cascade 1 (Wang et al., 2025) introduced **Cascade RL** as a response to these problems. The core insight was deceptively simple: instead of training on all domains at once, train on them **sequentially**, in a carefully designed order. This transforms the problem from one of gradient interference (hard to debug, unpredictable) to one of catastrophic forgetting (well-studied, with known mitigation strategies).

The authors of Cascade 1 demonstrated three key advantages of this sequential approach:

1. **Resistance to catastrophic forgetting**: Domain-specific RL stages, when well-ordered, rarely degraded performance on earlier domains and sometimes even improved it. This was an empirical finding rather than a theoretical guarantee, but it held across their training runs.

2. **Customized learning dynamics**: Each stage could use its own hyperparameters, verification infrastructure, and training curriculum tailored to the domain's specific characteristics. Math RL could use long generation windows and strict binary rewards; RLHF could use a generative reward model with length penalties.

3. **Compute efficiency through homogeneity**: Within a single RL stage, all prompts share similar response lengths and verification costs, eliminating the straggler problem and enabling efficient batching.

Cascade RL achieved state-of-the-art results across reasoning and alignment benchmarks, establishing the sequential domain-wise approach as a viable alternative to joint training.

However, the Cascade 1 paper left an important gap unfilled. While Cascade RL *substantially reduced* catastrophic forgetting compared to an arbitrary sequential ordering, it did not **fully eliminate** it. The authors of Cascade 2 observe (Section 4.4):

> "In practice, we observe noticeable fluctuations across different benchmark categories tracked throughout training, and the dominant trade-offs differ by stage."

Specifically, they note two patterns:

- **RLVR training can reduce model entropy and shorten reasoning traces**, which hurts mathematical reasoning performance (long reasoning traces are often necessary for complex proofs).

- **RLHF-oriented optimization can partially trade off against instruction-following behavior**, as the model learns to prioritize natural conversational flow over rigid constraint satisfaction.

These regressions are not catastrophic in the sense of wiping out capabilities entirely, but they are significant enough to matter. A model that gains 5 points on ArenaHard through RLHF but loses 3 points on IFBench during the same stage leaves performance on the table. As the number of RL stages grows and the diversity of environments expands, these accumulated regressions become a first-order bottleneck on final model quality.

### The Missing Mechanism: Capability Rebalancing During Training

The gap that Nemotron-Cascade 2 addresses is therefore: **how can we recover benchmark performance that regresses during earlier specialized RL stages, without sacrificing the gains those stages achieved?**

This is a harder problem than it sounds. Simple solutions fail for specific reasons:

- **Replaying data from earlier stages**: Fine-tuning on old SFT data after RL training would erase RL gains, as the SFT data comes from a different distribution than the post-RL model's outputs.

- **Joint training on all domains simultaneously**: This is precisely what Cascade RL was designed to avoid, as it reintroduces the interference and straggler problems.

- **Training with a weighted multi-objective reward**: Combining rewards from different domains with hand-tuned weights requires expensive hyperparameter sweeps and assumes the optimal weighting is static, when in fact the model's needs change throughout training.

- **Distillation from external teacher models**: While effective in principle, using teacher models from different model families introduces tokenizer and vocabulary mismatches, distribution shift, and potential alignment issues. It also requires access to and inference with potentially very large external models, adding computational cost and complexity.

### Why This Problem Matters Now: The Expanding RL Landscape

The timing of this problem is not coincidental. In early 2025, the RL post-training landscape underwent rapid expansion. Models like DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) demonstrated that pure RLVR could unlock sophisticated reasoning behaviors. Simultaneously, agentic RL environments—where models interact with terminals, write and execute code, and navigate multi-turn tool-use scenarios—grew in complexity and importance. The number of distinct RL domains that a single "general-purpose" model needed to master grew from perhaps 2-3 in early 2025 to 6+ by the time of Nemotron-Cascade 2's development.

This expansion created a practical engineering crisis: the combinatorial space of ordering 6+ RL stages, each with their own hyperparameters and verification infrastructure, was becoming unmanageable. Without a principled mechanism for capability rebalancing, model developers faced a grim optimization landscape where late-stage RL on critical domains (like competitive coding or software engineering) risked undoing the careful work of earlier stages on alignment and instruction following.

### How MOPD Fits Into the Cascade RL Philosophy

The solution this paper proposes—**Multi-domain On-Policy Distillation (MOPD)**—is elegant precisely because it fits within the Cascade RL philosophy rather than replacing it. Instead of fighting the sequential nature of Cascade RL, MOPD adds a **stabilization stage** that explicitly rebalances capabilities using teacher checkpoints selected from earlier points in the training pipeline.

The key insight is that the Cascade RL process itself naturally produces a diverse set of teacher models, each specialized in different capabilities:

- The initial SFT checkpoint excels at mathematical reasoning (because of the meticulously curated 2.6M competition math SFT samples).

- An intermediate checkpoint after RLHF training excels at human preference alignment (ArenaHard performance).

- Another intermediate checkpoint after IF-RL + Multi-domain RL excels at instruction following and tool calling.

These teacher checkpoints share the same tokenizer, vocabulary, and SFT initialization as the student model currently in training. This eliminates the distribution shift issues that plague external distillation. Furthermore, they represent "snapshots" of the model at its peak performance on specific capability axes—checkpoints that were selected because they achieved the highest validation scores on particular benchmark categories.

The MOPD objective is then: **distill the best of each capability from these specialized teacher snapshots into the current student model, using on-policy sampling from the student itself**. The on-policy aspect is critical: the student generates responses under its own current policy, and then the domain-specific teacher provides a dense, token-level advantage signal by comparing its log-probability assignment for each token against the student's own distribution. This ensures the distillation targets are realistic—the student is learning from what it could have generated differently, not from outputs it cannot produce.

### Positioning Relative to Prior Work

The paper positions itself at the intersection of several active research threads:

**Relative to Nemotron-Cascade 1 (Wang et al., 2025):** This is a direct successor. Cascade 2 significantly expands the Cascade RL pipeline (from the 3-4 stages of Cascade 1 to 6+ stages covering instruction following, multi-domain, MOPD, RLHF, long-context, code, and SWE RL). The critical novelty is the MOPD stage, which addresses the benchmark regression problem that Cascade 1 only partially mitigated.

**Relative to on-policy distillation literature:** On-policy distillation has been explored in prior work (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026) primarily as a technique for training smaller student models from larger teacher models. The MOPD approach in this paper differs in a crucial way: the teachers are **intermediate checkpoints from the same model family**, not larger external models. This converts on-policy distillation from a compression technique into a **capability integration** technique—a way to weave together strengths developed at different points in training.

**Relative to RLHF and alignment work:** The paper's RLHF stage follows the GenRM approach established in prior NVIDIA work (HelpSteer3, Wang et al., 2025), using a generative reward model based on Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507. The contributed novelty is not in the RLHF methodology itself but in how RLHF is **positioned within the Cascade RL ordering**: after IF-RL (to lock in instruction following), after multi-domain RL (to lock in tool calling and STEM reasoning), and after MOPD (to rebalance capabilities), but before domain-specific RL stages like code and SWE. This ordering reflects the paper's empirical finding that IF-RL can negatively impact alignment capabilities, so it is better to maximize instruction following first and use subsequent RLHF and MOPD stages to recover alignment.

**Relative to competitive coding and IMO post-training:** The paper's achievement of IMO 2025 gold at 30B-A3B is remarkable primarily because of the parameter efficiency. Frontier open-weight models achieving comparable IMO performance (DeepSeek-Math-V2 at 671B-A37B) use ~20× more parameters. This is not claimed to come from a novel inference-time scaling technique—the paper uses the same generate-verify-refine pipeline from DeepSeek-Math-V2—but rather from the effectiveness of the Cascade RL + MOPD post-training in producing a high-quality reasoning policy from a much smaller base model.

**Relative to agentic RL:** The SWE RL stage in Cascade 2 represents a notable expansion over Cascade 1, incorporating both agentless code repair RL and execution-based agentic RL within OpenHands scaffolds. This reflects the broader field's recognition that software engineering capabilities require not just code generation skills but the ability to coordinate tool use, interpret environment feedback, and debug iteratively—all of which are natural targets for RL training. The paper's contribution here is demonstrating that these agentic capabilities can be integrated into a broader Cascade RL pipeline without destabilizing performance on non-agentic benchmarks.

### Summary of the Gap

Before Nemotron-Cascade 2, the field had two competing approaches to multi-domain RL post-training: (1) joint training on all domains simultaneously (interference-prone, computationally inefficient), and (2) sequential Cascade RL (better domain-by-domain optimization, but vulnerable to accumulated capability regression as the number of stages grows). Neither approach provided a built-in mechanism for **recovering lost capabilities mid-pipeline** without sacrificing newly acquired ones.

The paper's MOPD stage is designed to fill exactly this gap: a training stage that explicitly rebalances capabilities by distilling from the best checkpoint for each capability category, using on-policy sampling to maintain training stability. This transforms Cascade RL from a pipeline that *tolerates* some forgetting into one that can actively *recover* from it, enabling the scaling to 6+ specialized RL stages that Cascade 2 demonstrates.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper builds a **multi-stage post-training pipeline** that takes a pretrained 30B Mixture-of-Experts base model and produces a single unified model that excels simultaneously at mathematical proof construction, competitive programming, instruction following, long-context reasoning, and agentic software engineering. The core problem it solves is **capability regression during sequential RL training** — the phenomenon where gains from a new RL stage come at the expense of capabilities acquired in earlier stages — by inserting a novel **Multi-domain On-Policy Distillation (MOPD)** stage that rebalances capabilities using domain-specialized intermediate teacher checkpoints selected from earlier points in the same training pipeline, all without introducing external model families or requiring the computationally expensive search over stage orderings that would otherwise be necessary.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has six major components arranged in a fixed sequential pipeline, with an additional inference-time scaling framework for competition evaluation:

1. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)** — trains the base model on a meticulously curated multi-domain dataset spanning math, code reasoning, science, long context, general chat, instruction following, safety, conversational agent, software engineering agent, and terminal agent tasks. This establishes foundational capabilities across all target domains before any RL training begins.

2. **Instruction-Following RL (IF-RL)** — the first RL stage, using GRPO with verifiable rewards on instruction-following data. This establishes strict instruction adherence as a foundational prior before other RL stages can potentially interfere with it. Trained exclusively in thinking mode with dynamic filtering to remove samples where all rollouts are either entirely correct or entirely incorrect.

3. **Multi-domain RL** — a joint RL stage covering multiple-choice STEM QA, agentic tool calling, and structured output for instruction following. These domains are grouped together because they exhibit minimal negative interference and have similar response lengths and verification times, enabling efficient co-training.

4. **Multi-domain On-Policy Distillation (MOPD)** — the key innovation. This stage distills capabilities from domain-specialized teacher checkpoints (the initial SFT checkpoint for math, an RLHF-optimized checkpoint for alignment, and the post-IF-RL+Multi-domain checkpoint for instruction following and tool calling) into the current student model using on-policy sampling and a dense token-level reverse-KL distillation advantage. This recovers benchmark regressions accumulated during earlier specialized stages and produces a more balanced policy.

5. **RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback)** — trains human preference alignment using a generative reward model (Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507) with pairwise comparisons, length-normalized reward adjustment, and quality-gated conciseness bonuses. Trained exclusively in thinking mode to avoid degrading instruction-following performance.

6. **Domain-Specific RL Stages** — in sequence: (a) Long-context RL using an LLM judge for QA tasks on long documents, (b) Code RL on filtered competitive programming prompts with strict binary rewards and asynchronous verification, (c) SWE RL combining agentless code repair RL with execution-based agentic RL within OpenHands scaffolds.

Information flows strictly forward: the output checkpoint of each stage becomes the input to the next. The MOPD stage is the only stage that explicitly reaches backward in the pipeline to access teacher checkpoints from earlier points. At inference time, for competition evaluation (IMO, IOI, ICPC), a test-time scaling framework wraps the final model with generate-verify-refine loops and submission history integration.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**: The SFT data curation and training setup (Section 3.2 of the paper), because the quality and composition of SFT data determines the baseline capabilities upon which all subsequent RL stages build, and because the initial SFT checkpoint serves as the math domain teacher for MOPD.

- **Second**: The Cascade RL training framework (Section 4.1), including the GRPO objective, on-policy training constraints, and the principles that determine the ordering of stages — since understanding *why* stages are ordered as they are is prerequisite to understanding *what* each stage does.

- **Third**: IF-RL and Multi-domain RL (Sections 4.2–4.3) as the first two RL stages, because they establish the foundational priors (instruction adherence, tool calling, STEM reasoning) that later stages must preserve.

- **Fourth**: The MOPD objective and training mechanics (Section 4.4) in full detail, since this is the central technical contribution. This includes the reverse-KL distillation advantage, truncated importance weighting for train-inference mismatch, teacher selection, and the training efficiency advantage over GRPO.

- **Fifth**: RLHF (Section 4.5) and Long-context RL (Section 4.6), which continue the Cascade RL sequence after MOPD rebalancing.

- **Sixth**: Code RL and SWE RL (Sections 4.7–4.8), the final domain-specific stages, including the agentless and execution-based agentic SWE RL setups and why agentless RL improves agentic scaffold performance.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems and training methodology paper** whose core idea is that a sequential domain-wise RL pipeline can be stabilized against capability regression by inserting an on-policy distillation stage that uses intermediate teacher checkpoints from within the same training run, and that this enables scaling to 6+ specialized RL domains while achieving frontier-level reasoning performance from a compact 30B MoE model.

#### Supervised Fine-Tuning Data Curation and Training

**Training configuration.** The SFT stage packs all samples into sequences of up to 256K tokens and trains the model in a single stage. The authors empirically find that optimal performance is reached after approximately 1.5 epochs, corresponding to 33,000 training steps out of a scheduled maximum of 40,000. Training uses a global batch size of 64, a cosine learning rate schedule with maximum learning rate `$5 \times 10^{-5}$` and minimum learning rate `$5 \times 10^{-6}$`, with 200 warmup steps. The optimizer is AdamW with `$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.98$`, and weight decay 0.1.

**Chat template design (Figure 1).** The chat template uses `<|im_start|>system`, `<|im_start|>user`, and `<|im_start|>assistant` tokens with `<|im_end|>` terminators. Two important design changes from Nemotron-Cascade 1 are introduced:

- **Removal of explicit thinking mode tags**: Cascade 1 used `/think` and `/no_think` tags to toggle reasoning mode. Cascade 2 removes these for simplicity and instead uses an adjacent token sequence to indicate mode: `  thinking response` tokens placed immediately adjacent to each other signal non-thinking mode, while a single `  thinking` token followed by a newline character signals thinking mode. This is a syntactic convention rather than a semantic tag.

- **Empty thinking response block for non-thinking activation**: A prepended empty `  thinking response` block activates non-thinking mode by default, eliminating the need for explicit mode-switching instructions.

For tool-calling tasks, all available tools are specified in the system prompt within `<tools>` and `</tools>` tags, with each function described by its name, description, and parameter schemas. The model is instructed to call tools within `<tool_call>` and `</tool_call>` tags using a structured format.

**Math data (Section 3.2.1).** Non-proof math prompts are sourced from Nemotron-Cascade and Nemotron-Math-v2, collecting 1.8M tool-calling (Python) samples and 1.9M non-tool samples. Tool-calling responses are generated by DeepSeek-V3.2, while non-tool responses are generated by DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale. An additional 676K generation-selection samples (without tool calling) from Nemotron-3-Nano are included, with responses generated by GPT-OSS-120B. The total competition math SFT comprises 1.8M tool-calling samples and 2.6M samples without tool use. For mathematical natural language proof, 98K proof problems are collected from the AOPS split of Nemotron-Math-Proofs-v1. Multiple samples per problem are generated to cover two capabilities: proof generation (410K samples) and proof verification (400K samples), both using DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale, resulting in 816K total proof samples.

**Code reasoning data (Section 3.2.2).** Approximately 165K unique coding prompts are curated from OpenCode-Stage2, OpenCodeReasoning, and HardTests, sourced from competitive programming platforms (Codeforces, AtCoder, AIZU, CodeChef). Strict deduplication using sample I/O fingerprinting and n-gram-based text analysis removes approximately 24.2% of self-duplicated prompts. GPT-OSS-120B is chosen as the SFT teacher due to its strong code reasoning capabilities. For prompts with verifiable test cases, correctness filtering retains only reasoning traces that generate correct code. For prompts without verifiable test cases, longer reasoning traces are preferred under the assumption they reflect more thorough problem analysis. The pipeline yields 1.9M Python reasoning traces, 1.0M C++14 reasoning traces, and 1.3M Python tool-calling reasoning traces for competitive coding. Scientific research coding prompts spanning biology, material science, physics, chemistry, and mathematics add 1.1M SFT samples generated by GPT-OSS-120B.

**Science data (Section 3.2.3).** Science prompts span physics, chemistry, and biology, using 1.4M samples from Nemotron-Cascade and 1.3M samples from Nemotron-3-Nano, with responses generated by GPT-OSS-120B.

**Long context data (Section 3.2.4).** 160K samples are adopted from Nemotron-3-Nano with an average sequence length of 128K tokens, plus 74K samples from ChatQA-2 with an average length of 29K tokens.

**General chat and instruction following data (Sections 3.2.5–3.2.6).** General chat comprises 4.9M reasoning-on and 372K reasoning-off samples. Reasoning-on responses are generated by GPT-OSS-120B. For reasoning-off, 300K responses come from high-quality annotated short answers in the dataset, and 330K from DeepSeek-V3-0324. Multi-turn dialogue capabilities are enhanced by synthesizing approximately 700K multi-turn conversation samples using two GPT-OSS-120B instances in a role-playing setup, where one plays the user and the other the assistant, with the user-side model permitted to terminate the conversation at any point to prevent repetitive exchanges. An additional 4.6M reasoning-on chat samples from Nemotron-3-Nano are incorporated, with prompts from LMSYS and WildChat and responses from GPT-OSS-120B, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507, and Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507. Instruction following adds approximately 230K reasoning-on responses from GPT-OSS-120B, 64K reasoning-off responses from DeepSeek-V3-0324, and 497K samples from Nemotron-3-Nano (457K reasoning-on, 40K reasoning-off) from the same set of teacher models.

**Safety data (Section 3.2.7).** 4K safety SFT samples from Nemotron-3-Nano enable appropriate refusal behavior on unsafe inputs. Prompts are sourced from Nemotron Content Safety v2, Gretel Safety Alignment v1, Harmful Tasks, and Red-Team-2K.

**Conversational agent data (Section 3.2.8).** 822K conversational tool-use samples from Nemotron-3-Nano provide multi-turn tool-calling experience where multiple tools are available and the assistant must determine which tools to invoke. Responses are generated by Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507, Qwen3-32B, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507, and GPT-OSS-120B.

**Software engineering agent data (Section 3.2.9).** SWE data is curated using multiple agentic scaffolds: OpenHands, SWE-Agent, Mini-SWE-Agent, and the agentless scaffold from Wei et al. (2025). The data combines agentic trajectories from Nemotron-3-Nano and Super (generated using Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B-Instruct on problem instances from SWE-Gym, SWE-rebench, and R2E-Subset) with agentless data from Nemotron-Cascade 1 covering three tasks: buggy code localization, code repair, and test case generation. Code repair data is reconstructed using DeepSeek-V3.2. A preliminary study shows that incorporating agentless data improves SWE agentic performance: fine-tuning solely on agentic data achieves Pass@1 of 48.9 and Pass@4 of 62.8 on SWE-bench Verified using OpenHands, while combining agentic and agentless data improves to Pass@1 of 49.9 and Pass@4 of 65.2. The final SWE SFT combines 125K agentic samples and 389K agentless samples, with models trained in non-thinking mode on agentic data and thinking mode on agentless data.

**Terminal agent data (Section 3.2.10).** The Terminal-Task-Gen methodology is used to curate 490K samples, consisting of dataset adapters that transform static data into interactive terminal formats and synthetic tasks generated from diverse seed prompts and a structured terminal skill taxonomy. Specifically, 162K math, 32K code, and 32K SWE-specific samples are adapted from existing sources, plus 120K seed-based and 140K skill-based synthetic tasks. DeepSeek-V3.2 generates step-by-step solution traces via an execution-feedback loop within isolated Docker environments, using the Terminus 2 agent framework as scaffolding.

#### Cascade RL Training Framework and Stage Ordering Principles

**The GRPO objective with on-policy constraint (Section 4.1.2).** Throughout the entire Cascade RL process, the paper uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with strict on-policy training. At each iteration, a group of `$G$` rollouts is generated from the current policy `$\pi_\theta$`, and a single gradient update is performed. This ensures the policy used for data collection always matches the one being updated, making the importance sampling ratio exactly 1.

The paper removes the KL divergence term entirely, simplifying the GRPO objective to the standard REINFORCE objective with group-normalized rewards and token-level loss:

$$\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(q,a) \sim \mathcal{D}, \{o_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|q)} \left[ \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^G |o_i|} \sum_{i=1}^G \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \hat{A}_{i,t} \right]$$

where `$q$` is a question drawn from dataset `$\mathcal{D}$`, `$a$` is the ground-truth answer, `$\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G$` are the `$G$` sampled responses from the current policy `$\pi_\theta$`, `$|o_i|$` is the number of tokens in response `$o_i$`, and `$\hat{A}_{i,t}$` is the group-normalized advantage at position `$t$` of response `$i$`.

The advantage is defined as:

$$\hat{A}_{i,t} = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\{r_i\}_{i=1}^G)}{\text{std}(\{r_i\}_{i=1}^G)}$$

where `$r_i$` is the reward assigned to response `$o_i$`, `$\text{mean}(\{r_i\}_{i=1}^G)$` is the arithmetic mean of all `$G$` rewards in the group, and `$\text{std}(\{r_i\}_{i=1}^G)$` is the standard deviation of those rewards. The advantage `$\hat{A}_{i,t}$` is shared across all tokens within the same response — every token in response `$o_i$` receives the same group-normalized advantage.

**What it computes, operationally:** For each question in a batch, the model generates `$G$` complete responses. Each response receives a scalar reward based on the domain-specific verifier (for RLVR) or the generative reward model (for RLHF). These `$G$` rewards are standardized to zero mean and unit variance within the group. Every token in a given response is then weighted by this single standardized reward value when computing the policy gradient update. Responses that are better than the group average receive positive weight; responses worse than average receive negative weight.

**Why this form:** The removal of the KL term is motivated by the strict on-policy constraint — since the data collection policy and the training policy are identical at each step, there is no off-policy correction needed and no benefit from KL regularization against a reference policy. The group normalization serves as an adaptive baseline: instead of learning a separate value function, it uses the empirical mean of the current batch to determine which responses are above or below average. This is computationally simpler than learned baselines and automatically adapts to the reward scale of each domain. The token-level loss (assigning the same advantage to all tokens in a response) follows the approach of DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), where the sequence-level outcome signal is propagated to every token, encouraging or discouraging all the reasoning steps that led to that outcome.

**Principles determining Cascade RL ordering (Section 4.1.1).** The optimal ordering of stages is described as "a dynamic function of the model's underlying behaviors and learning trajectories," not a universal constant. The specific ordering in Cascade 2 differs from Cascade 1 due to improvements in SFT data quality and substantially scaled RL environment complexity, which fundamentally altered the model's behavioral dynamics.

The paper articulates three guiding principles:

- **Mitigating inter-domain interference**: Identify an ordering that minimizes negative interference across domains. The core design principle is to find an ordering where tasks that serve as foundational priors precede tasks that act as specialized refinements. For example, IF-RL is placed first because it can negatively impact human alignment capabilities, while subsequent RLHF has negligible impact on instruction following scores. By maximizing instruction following first, later stages can focus on recovering and refining human preference alignment without destroying the instruction-following foundation.

- **Scaling via multi-domain integration**: When specific domains are found to be non-conflicting or mutually beneficial, they can be grouped into a single multi-domain RL stage. This integrated approach becomes increasingly important as RL environments grow in number and complexity, allowing more domains to be covered without exploding the number of sequential stages.

- **Stabilization through on-policy distillation**: MOPD serves as a critical stabilization point in the ordering. It recovers benchmark performance that may have regressed during earlier specialized stages, producing a more balanced policy that can better withstand the further specialization of later stages (Code RL, SWE RL). Placing MOPD before RLHF, long-context RL, code RL, and SWE RL means these later domain-specific stages start from a policy that has already been rebalanced across math, instruction following, tool calling, and human preference alignment.

**The specific Cascade 2 ordering with rationale** (Section 4.1, Figure 2):

1. **IF-RL first**: Positions instruction adherence as the foundational prior. IF-RL can hurt alignment, but subsequent RLHF can recover alignment without hurting instruction following. This is the reverse of Cascade 1's ordering, where RLHF preceded IF-RL, motivated by the empirical finding that "an early IF-RL stage produces a model with superior instruction-following capabilities, which serves as a strong teacher for subsequent multi-domain on-policy distillation."

2. **Multi-domain RL second**: Groups non-conflicting domains (MCQA in STEM, agentic tool calling, structured output) that share similar response lengths and verification times. Trained jointly because no performance degradation is observed across evaluation benchmarks when training on the blended domains.

3. **MOPD third**: Rebalances capabilities using teacher checkpoints from earlier pipeline points. The SFT checkpoint provides the math teacher (strong mathematical reasoning from the curated SFT data), an RLHF-optimized checkpoint provides the alignment teacher, and the post-IF-RL+Multi-domain checkpoint provides the instruction-following and tool-calling teacher.

4. **RLHF fourth**: Trains human preference alignment on top of the already-rebalanced policy, using a generative reward model with pairwise comparisons and length controls.

5. **Long-context RL fifth**: Specializes on long-document reasoning. Isolated to long-context datasets only because incorporating other domains during this stage negatively affects performance on unrelated benchmarks.

6. **Code RL sixth**: Specializes on competitive programming. Trained on a compact filtered set of high-difficulty prompts to avoid spending compute on problems the model already solves perfectly.

7. **SWE RL seventh (final)**: Combines agentless code repair RL and execution-based agentic RL within OpenHands scaffolds. Placed last because agentic software engineering requires the broadest integration of capabilities.

**On-policy training constraint and its implications (Section 4.1.2).** The strict on-policy setup — generating rollouts from `$\pi_\theta$` and immediately updating `$\pi_\theta$` with those rollouts — contributes to stable RL training and mitigates entropy collapse. Entropy collapse is the phenomenon where the policy becomes overconfident and stops exploring, leading to premature convergence to suboptimal behaviors. By always generating fresh rollouts from the current policy (rather than replaying from a buffer), the model continually explores its own current output distribution, preventing the distributional mismatch that can cause off-policy RL to diverge. The removal of the KL term is specifically justified by this on-policy property: in off-policy RL, KL regularization against a reference policy prevents the training policy from diverging too far from the data collection policy, but when the two policies are identical, this regularization is unnecessary.

#### Instruction-Following RL (IF-RL)

**Dataset and curation (Section 4.2.1).** IF-RL uses the same instruction-following training data as NVIDIA Nano-v3 post-training. The instructions are designed for objective verifiability — for instance, constraints like "respond in under 200 words" or "include exactly 3 bullet points" — making them well-suited for RLVR with rule-based reward functions. The curation process primarily resolves formatting inconsistencies within keyword arguments for certain instruction types.

**Dynamic filtering (Section 4.2.2).** A key training technique carried forward from Nemotron-Cascade 1: samples where all rollouts are either entirely correct or entirely incorrect are filtered out from the batch. The rationale is gradient efficiency: if all rollouts for a prompt are correct, the advantage is zero for all of them (since all rewards are identical), and the prompt provides no gradient signal. Similarly, if all rollouts are incorrect, the negative advantage is applied uniformly, but the model cannot distinguish which aspects of its generation to improve since all attempts failed equally. By keeping only prompts with mixed outcomes (some correct, some incorrect), every remaining prompt provides a gradient that pushes the policy toward the successful response patterns and away from the unsuccessful ones.

The paper reports that dynamic filtering "stabilizes IF-RL training and pushes the upper bound of model performance."

**Overlong penalty (Section 4.2.2).** Extended IF-RL training can lead to excessive token usage — the model learns to satisfy constraints by generating verbose reasoning that is unnecessary for simple constraint fulfillment. To mitigate this, the overlong penalty assigns zero reward to samples that fail to complete generation within the maximum sequence length, effectively treating incompleteness as failure and discouraging the policy from generating responses that hit the context limit.

**Thinking mode exclusivity (Section 4.2.2).** IF-RL is trained exclusively in thinking mode, without incorporating a reward model. The authors found that thinking mode yields higher accuracy on instruction-following benchmarks (e.g., IFBench). This differs from Nemotron-Cascade 1, which used both thinking and non-thinking modes. The decision to forgo an auxiliary reward model is justified by the Cascade RL ordering: because subsequent RLHF and MOPD stages recover any regressions in human preference alignment introduced during IF-RL, the team can focus entirely on maximizing instruction adherence without the computational overhead of an auxiliary reward model to maintain alignment.

**Hyperparameters (Section 4.2.2, Appendix B).** IF-RL uses a batch size of 128, sampling 16 responses per prompt (`$G = 16$`) at temperature 1.0 and top-p 1.0. The learning rate is `$3 \times 10^{-6}$` with AdamW (`$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`), and both the entropy loss coefficient and KL loss coefficient are set to 0. The maximum response length is 49K tokens. Training with dynamic filtering takes approximately 180 steps.

#### Multi-Domain RL

**Domain composition (Section 4.3).** Multi-domain RL covers three capability categories trained jointly: (1) multiple-choice question answering (MCQA) in the STEM domain, (2) agentic tool calling using the Workplace Assistant setup from NVIDIA Nano-v3, and (3) structured output for instruction following. The data mixture consists of approximately 55% MCQA, 30% agentic tool calling, and 15% structured output.

**Rationale for joint training (Section 4.3).** These domains are grouped specifically for two reasons:

- **No performance degradation when blended**: Unlike other domain combinations, training these three domains together produces consistent improvements on MMLU-Pro, `$\tau$`2-Bench, and IF-Bench without regressions.

- **Similar response lengths and verification times**: This homogeneity minimizes training inefficiencies from the straggler problem — no single domain dominates the generation or verification wall-clock time, keeping GPU utilization high.

**Hyperparameters (Appendix B).** Multi-domain RL uses a batch size of 128, 16 rollouts per prompt (`$G = 16$`), temperature 1.0, and top-p 1.0. The learning rate is `$3 \times 10^{-6}$` with Adam (`$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`), and both the entropy loss coefficient and KL loss coefficient are set to 0. Overlong filtering is enabled (unlike IF-RL), meaning samples that fail to complete within the maximum response length are filtered out. The maximum response length is 49K tokens. Training runs for approximately 70 steps.

#### Multi-Domain On-Policy Distillation (MOPD)

**Motivation: why MOPD is needed (Section 4.4).** The Cascade RL authors observe that despite well-designed sequential ordering substantially reducing catastrophic forgetting, it does not fully eliminate capability drift as the number of training stages increases. They note two specific patterns:

- **RLVR training reduces model entropy and shortens reasoning traces**, which negatively impacts mathematical reasoning performance — long, careful reasoning traces are often necessary for complex proofs, and the pressure to produce concise outputs from instruction-following or tool-calling RL can bleed over.

- **RLHF optimization partially trades off against instruction-following behavior** — the model learns to prioritize natural conversational flow over rigid constraint satisfaction, causing measurable regressions on benchmarks like IFBench.

These observations motivate an additional training stage explicitly designed for re-balancing capabilities: "an additional training stage for re-balancing capabilities within the Cascade RL process."

**Why MOPD specifically (Section 4.4).** MOPD is attractive in this setting for three reasons directly tied to the Cascade RL framework:

- **Internal teacher availability**: Teacher checkpoints can be selected directly from the Cascade RL pipeline by choosing the strongest validation checkpoint for each benchmark category. No external model families are needed, and no additional training runs are required — the checkpoints already exist as byproducts of the sequential training.

- **Shared tokenizer and vocabulary**: Because teachers are derived from the same SFT initialization as the student, they share the same tokenizer and vocabulary. This eliminates the distribution shift and alignment issues that arise when distilling from external models with different tokenization schemes.

- **Dense token-level training advantage**: MOPD provides per-token supervision signals, whereas GRPO provides only a sparse sequence-level outcome reward shared across all generated tokens. The paper demonstrates that this makes MOPD substantially more sample- and step-efficient than comparable GRPO-based training.

**Teacher pool composition (Section 4.4).** The main experiments use three domain teachers:

- **Math teacher**: The initial SFT checkpoint, which exhibits strong mathematical reasoning capabilities from the meticulously curated SFT dataset (2.6M competition math samples plus 816K proof samples).

- **RLHF teacher**: A checkpoint optimized through RLHF from the initial SFT checkpoint (trained separately to convergence on alignment data).

- **Multi-domain teacher**: Selected from checkpoints after the IF-RL + Multi-domain RL stages, capturing peak instruction-following, tool-calling, and STEM MCQ performance.

**The MOPD objective: token-level distillation advantage (Section 4.4).** Let `$\pi_{\text{inf}}$` denote the student policy used for response generation in the inference engine (which generates rollouts), and let `$\pi_{\text{train}}$` denote the student policy optimized by the training engine (which receives gradient updates). For each prompt `$x$`, a response `$y = (y_1, \ldots, y_T) \sim \pi_{\text{inf}}(\cdot \mid x)$` is sampled. A domain teacher `$\pi_{\text{domain}_i}$` is then selected for that training example, where `$\text{domain}_i$` indicates the capability domain associated with the chosen teacher.

The token-level distillation advantage at step `$t$`, with state `$s_t = (x, y_{<t})$`, uses reverse-KL:

$$a_t^{\text{MOPD}} = \log \pi_{\text{domain}_i}(y_t \mid s_t) - \log \pi_{\text{train}}(y_t \mid s_t)$$

where `$a_t^{\text{MOPD}}$` is the scalar advantage at token position `$t$`, `$\log \pi_{\text{domain}_i}(y_t \mid s_t)$` is the log-probability that the domain teacher assigns to the student-sampled token `$y_t$` given the context so far, and `$\log \pi_{\text{train}}(y_t \mid s_t)$` is the log-probability that the current training policy assigns to the same token in the same context.

**What it computes, operationally:** For each token in a student-generated sequence, compute the difference between how likely the domain teacher thinks that token is and how likely the current training policy thinks it is. If the teacher assigns higher probability (the teacher "prefers" this token more than the student currently does), the advantage is positive. If the teacher assigns lower probability (the student is overly confident in this token relative to the teacher), the advantage is negative.

**Why this form (reverse-KL):** The reverse-KL form `$\log \pi_{\text{teacher}} - \log \pi_{\text{student}}$` naturally converges toward zero during training — as the student policy matches the teacher's token-level distribution, the advantage shrinks. This is a stable optimization target because it does not require the student to perfectly replicate the teacher (which might be impossible if the teacher checkpoint is from a different point in training with different capabilities), but rather nudges the student's distribution toward the teacher's where they differ. An alternative forward-KL form (`$\log \pi_{\text{student}} - \log \pi_{\text{teacher}}$`) would penalize the student for having higher probability than the teacher, which is not the desired behavior — the student should be allowed to be more confident if that confidence is justified by other domains.

**Truncated importance weighting for train-inference mismatch (Section 4.4).** Because responses are sampled under `$\pi_{\text{inf}}$` but optimized under `$\pi_{\text{train}}$`, there can be a train-inference mismatch if the two policies differ. To correct for this, truncated importance weighting is applied:

$$r_t = \frac{\pi_{\text{train}}(y_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{inf}}(y_t \mid s_t)}$$

$$w_t = \text{sg}[r_t] \cdot \mathbb{1}[\epsilon_{\text{low}} \leq r_t \leq \epsilon_{\text{high}}]$$

where `$r_t$` is the importance sampling ratio at token `$t$`, `$\text{sg}[\cdot]$` denotes the stop-gradient operator (treating the ratio as a constant during backpropagation), and `$\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$` is the indicator function.

**What it computes, operationally:** For each token, compute the ratio of how likely the training policy thinks the token is versus how likely the inference policy thinks it is. If the ratio is close to 1 (the policies agree), the weight is near 1. If the ratio falls outside the interval `$[\epsilon_{\text{low}}, \epsilon_{\text{high}}] = [0.5, 2.0]$`, the token is excluded from the loss entirely (weight set to 0). This truncation acts as a variance reduction technique — tokens where the training and inference policies strongly disagree receive extreme importance weights that can destabilize training, so they are dropped.

**Why this form:** Importance weighting is the standard off-policy correction technique from the RL literature — it reweights samples from the behavior policy (inference engine) to match the target policy (training engine). The truncation is specifically motivated by stability: gradient norms are substantially larger at the beginning of training (as shown in Figure 3b) and decrease rapidly after the warm-up phase. Without truncation, the large initial mismatch between `$\pi_{\text{inf}}$` and `$\pi_{\text{train}}$` would produce extreme importance weights that cause gradient spikes and training instability.

**The surrogate MOPD loss (Section 4.4).** The full optimization objective combines the token-level distillation advantage with the truncated importance weights:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MOPD}} = -\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_{\text{inf}}(\cdot|x)} \left[ \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}(y)|} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{V}(y)} w_t \cdot \text{sg}[a_t^{\text{MOPD}}] \cdot \log \pi_{\text{train}}(y_t \mid s_t) \right]$$

where `$\mathcal{V}(y)$` is the set of valid response tokens retained by a token mask (which may exclude padding, special tokens, or prompt tokens), and `$\text{sg}[a_t^{\text{MOPD}}]$` uses stop-gradient on the advantage — the advantage is treated as a constant coefficient, not differentiated through.

**What it computes, operationally:** For each token in the valid portion of each generated response, multiply three quantities: the importance weight `$w_t$` (accounting for inference-training mismatch), the distillation advantage `$a_t^{\text{MOPD}}$` (how much the teacher prefers this token over the student), and the student's log-probability of the token `$\log \pi_{\text{train}}(y_t \mid s_t)$`. Sum over valid tokens, average, and negate to convert to a minimization objective. This is essentially a weighted maximum likelihood objective where the weight is the token-level advantage, and the importance weight corrects for sampling bias.

**Why this form:** This objective has the structure of a policy gradient with a dense token-level advantage, but without the REINFORCE variance. In standard REINFORCE, the advantage multiplies the log-probability, and the expectation is over the policy's own samples. Here, the advantage is provided by an external teacher (not estimated from returns), so it is dense and low-variance. The stop-gradient on the advantage prevents the student from learning to adversarially manipulate the advantage signal (e.g., by learning to generate tokens that produce extreme teacher probabilities). The token mask `$\mathcal{V}(y)$` ensures the loss is only applied to the model's generated content, not to prompt tokens or padding.

**Teacher mixture and prompt sampling (Section 4.4).** For each batch, prompts are sampled from the RL training data pools corresponding to the teacher domains: RLHF data for the RLHF teacher, IF-RL and Multi-domain data for the multi-domain teacher, and AceReason-Math for the math teacher. This ensures each teacher provides distillation advantages only on prompts from its domain of expertise — the math teacher does not try to guide the student on alignment prompts, and the RLHF teacher does not try to guide the student on math proofs.

**Hyperparameters and convergence behavior (Section 4.4).** MOPD uses a rollout size of 4 (`$G = 4$`) and 128 prompts per update, giving an effective batch size of 512 responses. In later experiments, the authors find that using 512 prompts with rollout size 1 yields slightly more stable optimization with similar final results. The learning rate is `$2 \times 10^{-6}$` with linear warm-up over the first 30 optimization steps, starting from `$2 \times 10^{-7}$`. Training typically converges within 40–50 optimization steps.

The warm-up stage is explicitly identified as important for stability: "gradient norms are substantially larger at the beginning of training and decrease rapidly after the warm-up phase" (Figure 3b). For truncated importance weighting, `$\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.5$` and `$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 2.0$`. The maximum response length during MOPD is 98K tokens.

**Training efficiency advantage over GRPO (Section 4.4, Figure 3, Table 3).** MOPD's dense token-level distillation advantage provides a training efficiency benefit compared to GRPO's sparse sequence-level outcome reward. Two comparisons demonstrate this:

- **On AIME25** (Figure 3c): Under math-only training, GRPO improves from 89.9 to 91.0 after 25 steps, while MOPD reaches 92.0 within 30 steps and recovers teacher-level performance. Despite taking slightly more steps, MOPD achieves a higher final accuracy.

- **On ArenaHard v2** (Table 3): Starting from the same initial checkpoint (Hard Prompt 71.5, Creative Writing 40.6), MOPD improves Hard Prompt to 85.5 and Creative Writing to 71.0 after 52 steps. In contrast, RLHF training requires 160 steps to reach 80.7 on Hard Prompt and 71.2 on Creative Writing. MOPD achieves substantially better or comparable performance in roughly one-third the number of optimization steps.

This efficiency is attributed to the information density of the training signal: GRPO provides a single scalar reward for an entire sequence (potentially thousands of tokens), meaning that all tokens receive the same positive or negative weight regardless of their individual contribution. MOPD provides a per-token signal that identifies exactly which tokens the teacher would have assigned higher or lower probability, enabling more targeted gradient updates.

#### Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

**Dataset (Section 4.5.1).** The RLHF dataset is adopted from NVIDIA Nano-v3, comprising HelpSteer3, a commercially-friendly subset of the arena-human-preference-140k dataset, and a synthetic safety blend.

**Generative reward model (GenRM) (Section 4.5.1).** The reward model used is Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507, trained via the HelpSteer3 framework. Its operation is: given a conversation history, a user request, and two candidate responses, the GenRM first reasons through the strengths and weaknesses of each response before producing individual helpfulness scores and a final comparative ranking. This two-stage process (reasoning, then scoring) is designed to produce more calibrated and explainable preference judgments than a direct scoring approach.

**Pairwise comparison and reward aggregation (Section 4.5.2).** Pairwise comparisons are used for all pairs of rollouts per prompt. For a prompt with `$G = 16$` rollouts, this means `$\binom{16}{2} = 120$` pairwise comparisons are performed, each providing a training signal. The reward scores are aggregated in the same way as NVIDIA Nano-v3 RLHF training.

**Length-normalized reward adjustment and quality-gated conciseness bonus (Section 4.5.2).** A known pathology of RLHF training is that models learn to produce increasingly longer responses because verbose outputs tend to score higher with reward models (they provide more opportunities to demonstrate helpfulness). To combat this, the paper applies two mechanisms from NVIDIA Nano-v3:

- **Length-normalized reward adjustment**: The raw reward is adjusted to penalize excessive length, encouraging responses that are helpful without being verbose.

- **Quality-gated conciseness bonus**: An additional bonus is applied for concise responses, but only when the response quality (as judged by the GenRM) is above a threshold. This prevents the model from learning to produce short but unhelpful responses — conciseness is rewarded only when it does not sacrifice quality.

These mechanisms "encourage shorter responses without sacrificing quality, effectively mitigating the rapid growth of inference token usage."

**Thinking mode exclusivity (Section 4.5.2).** RLHF is trained exclusively in thinking mode, differing from Nemotron-Cascade 1 which incorporated both thinking and non-thinking modes. The rationale for this change is an empirical finding: "While incorporating both thinking and non-thinking modes can improve training convergence and yield slight gains on evaluation benchmarks, we observe a significant degradation in instruction-following performance. The resulting drop is substantial enough that the gains obtained in the earlier RLVR stage cannot be fully recovered." This observation directly motivates the placement of RLHF after MOPD in the Cascade ordering — the model enters RLHF with strong instruction-following capabilities established by IF-RL and rebalanced by MOPD, and the exclusive thinking-mode RLHF is designed to avoid undermining those capabilities.

**Hyperparameters (Section 4.5.3, Appendix B).** RLHF uses a batch size of 128, 16 rollouts per prompt (`$G = 16$`), temperature 1.0, and top-p 1.0. The maximum response length is 16K tokens during RLHF (substantially shorter than other RL stages), and no overlong filtering is applied. The learning rate is `$3 \times 10^{-6}$` with AdamW (`$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`). The entropy loss coefficient is 0, but the KL loss coefficient is set to 0.03 — this is the only RL stage that uses a non-zero KL coefficient, intended to "keep the model capabilities on other domains." Training takes approximately 30 steps.

#### Long-Context RL

**Dataset and scope restriction (Section 4.6).** Long-context RL uses the NVIDIA Nano-v3 RL data blend but is restricted to long-context datasets only. The authors explicitly note that incorporating other domains during long-context RL negatively affects performance on unrelated benchmarks, motivating this domain-specific training setup. This provides further empirical evidence for the Cascade RL philosophy — certain domain combinations are genuinely incompatible in joint training.

**LLM judge for QA evaluation (Section 4.6).** The Nemo-Gym RL environment is used with Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 as an LLM judge to evaluate model rollouts for question answering tasks. This is a generative evaluation: the judge reads the model's response and the ground-truth answer (or reference documents), then produces a judgment of correctness along with a score. This differs from the verifiable reward approach used in Code RL and Math RL, where execution results or exact matches determine correctness. The LLM judge introduces some noise (it is not a perfect evaluator) but enables reward estimation for tasks where ground-truth execution is not available, such as summarization, multi-document reasoning, and dialogue understanding over long contexts.

**Sequence length constraints (Section 4.6).** During training, input sequences are limited to 32K tokens, and the maximum sequence length is set to 49K tokens. Overlength filtering is disabled for this stage (unlike Multi-domain RL where it was enabled). The decision to limit input sequences to 32K tokens during training (while evaluating on context lengths up to 1M tokens) is a practical compute trade-off — training with full-length 128K-token inputs would dramatically increase memory requirements and reduce batch sizes. The model must generalize from 32K-token training examples to 1M-token evaluation examples.

**Hyperparameters and early stopping (Section 4.6, Appendix B).** Long-context RL uses a batch size of 128, 16 rollouts per prompt, temperature 1.0, and top-p 1.0. The learning rate is `$3 \times 10^{-6}$` with Adam (`$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`), and both entropy and KL loss coefficients are 0. Training runs for approximately 30 steps, with the authors noting they observe "a rapid increase in generated tokens beyond that point" — a sign of reward hacking where the model learns to produce increasingly verbose outputs to maximize the LLM judge's score.

#### Code RL

**Data curation and aggressive filtering (Section 4.7.1).** The Code RL training set is constructed from the Nemotron-Cascade coding corpus, containing prompts from competitive programming platforms (AtCoder, Codeforces, AIZU) with robust test cases for reward verification. The key data curation choice is aggressive filtering: prompts that GPT-OSS-120B solves correctly in all 8 of 8 rollouts are removed. This filtering is applied because prompts that the teacher model solves perfectly provide no useful training signal — the model already succeeds on them in expectation, and training on them wastes compute on easy problems. The resulting compact dataset contains only 3.5K samples.

The authors note that "high-difficulty prompts paired with strong test cases are critical for further boosting model performance," suggesting that Code RL's effectiveness comes primarily from challenging problems where the model has room to improve, not from reinforcing already-solved patterns.

**Extended generation windows (Section 4.7.2).** Compared to Nemotron-Cascade 1, the maximum response length during Code RL is increased to 118K tokens (from a lower value in Cascade 1), and the number of rollouts per sample is increased to 16. The longer generation window "enables the policy to better capture sparse reward signals on extremely difficult problems that require long reasoning traces." This is particularly important for competitive programming problems where the solution may require hundreds of lines of code or extensive algorithmic reasoning before reaching a correct implementation.

**Strict binary reward and verification infrastructure (Section 4.7.2).** The reward function is strict binary: a response that passes all test cases receives reward 1; any failure (even a single failing test) receives reward 0. This is chosen to "avoid potential reward hacking" — partial credit reward functions can be exploited by the policy learning to produce solutions that maximize intermediate scores without actually solving the problem. The binary reward provides a clean signal: only fully correct solutions are reinforced.

The verification throughput required is substantial: `$128 \times 16 = 2,048$` code executions per RL step. To handle this, the authors deploy "an asynchronous reward verification server that completes each batch in 427.2 seconds across 384 CPU cores." This asynchronous infrastructure prevents the RL training loop from being bottlenecked by code execution — generations proceed on GPUs while separate CPU workers compile and execute code in sandboxed environments.

**Hyperparameters (Section 4.7.2, Appendix B).** Code RL uses a batch size of 128, 16 rollouts per prompt, temperature 1.0, and top-p 0.95 (the only RL stage to use top-p below 1.0). The learning rate is `$3 \times 10^{-6}$` with AdamW (`$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`). The maximum response length is 118K tokens. Overlong filtering is enabled. Training runs for approximately 22 steps.

#### Software Engineering Reinforcement Learning (SWE RL)

**Agentless RL: training setup and reward model (Section 4.8.1).** The agentless code repair RL stage uses the same data source as Nemotron-Cascade 1 but modifies the reward mechanism. Since most instances do not provide executable Docker environments (code repair problems often involve patching codebases where full environment setup is impractical), the paper employs GPT-OSS-120B as a reward model to evaluate the quality of code repairs. The reward model assesses whether the generated patch correctly addresses the bug, providing a proxy for execution-based verification.

For each instance, prompts are constructed using both the golden localization (the exact lines or files that need to be changed, providing an upper-bound signal) and the top-5 retrieved localizations (simulating what a retrieval system would provide in practice). Relatively easy samples are filtered out, following the same principle as Code RL: training on problems the model already solves wastes compute.

**Agentless RL: loss masking for difficult prompts (Section 4.8.1).** A notable training detail: "During training, we mask the loss for prompts for which none of the rollouts receives a reward greater than 0.5." These are prompts where even the best generated patch is judged as low-quality by the reward model. The authors observe that "these difficult prompts degrade the stability and effectiveness of agentless SWE RL training" — when no response in a batch is even moderately good, the GRPO advantage signal is essentially noise (all responses receive similarly low rewards, so the standardized advantage provides no meaningful differentiation).

**Why agentless RL helps agentic tasks (Section 4.8.1, Table 4).** The paper provides concrete evidence that agentless RL — training on code repair without the full agentic scaffolding — transfers to agentic SWE performance. Table 4 shows:

- Agentless Mini scaffold performance improves from 41.9% avg@4 to 44.3% after agentless RL, and from 55.2% pass@4 to 57.4%.

- OpenHands (agentic scaffold) performance also improves from 49.8% avg@4 to 50.8%, and from 64.2% pass@4 to 65.0%.

This suggests that code repair capability is a foundational skill that generalizes across scaffolds — improving the model's ability to identify and fix bugs transfers whether the model is operating in a simplified agentless environment or a full agentic framework with tool use, repository navigation, and multi-turn interaction.

**Agentless RL hyperparameters (Section 4.8.1).** Agentless SWE RL uses a batch size of `$128 \times 16 = 2,048$` (128 prompts with 16 rollouts per prompt), a maximum sequence length of 98,304 tokens, and a learning rate of `$3 \times 10^{-6}$` with AdamW. Responses are sampled with temperature 1.0 and top-p 1.0. Training typically converges within 40–50 steps.

**Execution-based agentic SWE RL: motivation (Section 4.8.2).** Modern software engineering agents rely on scaffolding frameworks that coordinate repository interaction, tool calling, code editing, and test execution. Training agents for these environments requires optimizing not just individual model outputs but the entire problem-solving trajectory — the sequence of tool calls, observations, and edits that leads to a successful patch. To address this, the paper applies RLVR directly within agentic SWE scaffolds, enabling end-to-end optimization of the full agent workflow.

**Environment setup (Section 4.8.2).** The training environments integrate OpenHands frameworks, which provide structured tool usage, repository interaction, and iterative patch generation. Each episode corresponds to resolving a software issue instance from benchmarks like SWE-bench. The agent operates inside an instrumented repository with tools for file inspection, search, code editing, and test execution. Environment feedback — including compilation errors, failing tests, or successful test passes — provides deterministic rewards that directly reflect functional correctness, without human annotation.

**Training configuration (Section 4.8.2, Table 10).** Execution-based agentic SWE RL uses a batch size of 1024, corresponding to 16 prompts with 64 rollouts per prompt. This large rollout count reflects the high variance of agentic trajectories — many rollouts may fail entirely, and the model needs sufficient successful trajectories to provide a positive learning signal. The maximum context length is 256K tokens (the longest of any RL stage), and the agent is allowed up to 200 interaction turns. Data is drawn from SWE-Gym and R2E-Subset. Temperature during training is 0.8 (lower than the 1.0 used in other stages), and the maximum sequence length is 256K tokens. The learning rate is `$3 \times 10^{-6}$` with a minimum of 0 and 10 warmup steps.

**Data filtering by difficulty (Section 4.8.2).** A nuanced filtering strategy is applied based on instance difficulty:

- Instances where all 16 rollouts pass verification (100% accuracy) are considered overly simple and are removed entirely — training on them would provide no gradient signal.

- Instances where none of the 16 rollouts pass verification (0% accuracy) are considered extremely difficult. 90% of such cases are randomly discarded to reduce their proportion in the training data. The remaining 10% are retained to maintain some exposure to very challenging instances, but the heavy subsampling prevents these "hopeless" instances from dominating the training signal with uniformly negative rewards that provide no differentiation between better and worse attempts.

This filtering reflects a careful calibration of training difficulty: the sweet spot is instances where some but not all rollouts succeed, providing a mix of positive and negative examples for the advantage computation to differentiate.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: On-Policy Distillation as a Capability Integration Mechanism, Not a Compression Tool

The most intellectually distinctive move in this paper is the repurposing of on-policy distillation from a model compression technique — distilling a large teacher into a small student — into a **capability integration mechanism** within a single training pipeline. This represents a fundamental conceptual shift in how distillation is understood and deployed.

**What the field did before.** On-policy distillation, as developed in prior work (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026), was conceived as a solution to the problem of deploying large models: train an expensive, high-capacity teacher, then distill its knowledge into a smaller, cheaper student. The teacher-student relationship was external — different model families, different training runs, different tokenizers. The distillation signal traveled in one direction: from a larger, more capable model to a smaller, less capable one. This framing treated distillation as a *compressive* operation — extracting the essential knowledge from a big model and packing it into a small one, with inevitable information loss.

**What this paper does differently.** The MOPD stage in Nemotron-Cascade 2 uses teacher checkpoints that are **intermediate snapshots from the same model's own training trajectory** — the SFT checkpoint for math, an RLHF-optimized checkpoint for alignment, the post-IF-RL+Multi-domain checkpoint for instruction following. These teachers are not larger, more capable models. They are *differently specialized* models — each represents the same base model distilled at a different point in the Cascade RL pipeline, having absorbed domain-specific expertise that subsequent stages may have partially overwritten.

This transforms distillation from a compression operation into an **integrative** one. The MOPD stage is not asking "what does a bigger model know that this one doesn't?" but rather "what did this model used to know — at different points in its own training history — that it has since partially forgotten?" The distillation signal travels through time within a single training run, not across model capacities.

**Why this is fundamental, not incremental.** This reframing solves a problem that was previously addressed through fragile mechanisms like careful stage ordering heuristics or hand-tuned multi-objective reward balancing. Those approaches treat capability regression as a *routing* problem — find the right path through the training stages to avoid interference. MOPD treats it as an *information recovery* problem — the lost capabilities are still present in the checkpoint archive; they just need to be re-integrated into the current policy.

The evidence for this claim comes from the training dynamics. Figure 3(c) shows MOPD on math-only training reaching 92.0 on AIME25 within 30 steps, recovering teacher-level performance that had been partially lost during earlier RL stages. Table 3 shows MOPD improving ArenaHard Hard Prompt from 71.5 to 85.5 in 52 steps — substantially outperforming RLHF's 160-step path to 80.7. These are not marginal gains; they represent recovery of capabilities that RL alone could not efficiently restore.

The shared tokenizer and vocabulary — possible only because teachers come from the same training run — eliminates the distribution shift that plagues cross-family distillation. This is not a minor implementation convenience; it is the enabling condition for treating distillation as within-pipeline integration rather than cross-model compression.

### Innovation 2: The Cascade RL Ordering as a Diagnostic Framework for Inter-Domain Interference

The paper's explicit articulation of *why* stages are ordered as they are — and the empirical finding that the optimal ordering is not static but depends on model behavior and SFT quality — constitutes a diagnostic contribution to the understanding of multi-domain RL dynamics, not just a recipe.

**What the field did before.** In joint multi-domain RL training, interference between domains is an opaque phenomenon. When performance on benchmark A drops while training on domain B, it is difficult to determine whether the cause is gradient interference, distribution shift in the training data, entropy collapse from one domain's reward structure, or something else entirely. The "kitchen sink" approach provides no mechanism for isolating these effects. Even Cascade RL 1, while implementing sequential training, did not fully articulate a systematic framework for ordering decisions — it provided an ordering that worked, but the principles were implicit.

**What this paper does differently.** The authors provide three explicit principles governing ordering — mitigating inter-domain interference, scaling via multi-domain integration when domains are non-conflicting, and stabilization through on-policy distillation — and then demonstrate that these principles lead to a *different* ordering than Cascade 1 because the SFT data quality and RL environment complexity changed the model's behavioral dynamics.

The key diagnostic move is the decision to place IF-RL first. The paper reports an empirical finding: "IF-RL can negatively impact human alignment capabilities (e.g., ArenaHard), while our subsequent generative-reward-model-based RLHF has a negligible impact on instruction following scores." This is a directional interference claim: IF-RL → RLHF causes degradation, but RLHF → IF-RL does not (or does so much less). This asymmetry determines the ordering: put the stage that is vulnerable to interference first, and use later stages to recover whatever was lost.

**Why this is a conceptual contribution, not just a recipe.** The ordering principles are falsifiable. A different base model, different SFT data, or different RL reward structures could change the interference patterns, which would change the optimal ordering. The paper's framework provides the vocabulary for reasoning about these tradeoffs: identify which domains exhibit asymmetric interference, place the vulnerable domains early, group non-conflicting domains for efficiency, and use MOPD as a rebalancing checkpoint before final specialization stages.

The specific ordering documented in Section 4.8.2 — IF-RL → Multi-domain → MOPD → RLHF → Long-context → Code → SWE — reflects these principles in action. IF-RL is vulnerable to later alignment optimization, so it goes first. Multi-domain groups non-conflicting tool-calling and STEM tasks. MOPD rebalances before the heavy specialization of Code and SWE RL. RLHF can recover alignment after IF-RL has maximized instruction following. Long-context RL is isolated because "incorporating other domains during long-context RL negatively affects performance on unrelated benchmarks" — another directional interference finding.

This framework makes the Cascade RL methodology *teachable* and *transferable* in a way that a single fixed ordering would not be. Future work building on Cascade RL can use these principles to diagnose their own model's interference patterns rather than blindly copying the Cascade 2 ordering.

### Innovation 3: The Demonstration of Frontier Reasoning at Dramatically Reduced Parameter Scale

While this paper is primarily methodological, the empirical result — gold medals at IMO 2025, IOI 2025, and ICPC World Finals 2025 from a 30B MoE model with 3B activated parameters — constitutes an existence proof that challenges prevailing assumptions about the relationship between model scale and reasoning capability.

**What the field assumed.** Prior to this work, achieving gold-medal performance at the IMO was associated with either frontier proprietary models (Gemini Deep Think, at undisclosed scale but presumably enormous) or the largest open-weight models (DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale at 671B-A37B, and DeepSeek-Math-V2 at 671B-A37B). The implicit assumption was that olympiad-level mathematical reasoning required either massive parameter counts or undisclosed proprietary training recipes, likely both. A 30B model competing at this level was not on the roadmap.

**What this paper demonstrates.** Nemotron-Cascade-2-30B-A3B achieves 35/42 on IMO 2025 (gold threshold is 29), solving 5 of 6 problems correctly with full 7/7 marks from a human expert. On IMO-ProofBench, it achieves 72.9 overall, within 8 points of DeepSeek-Math-V2-671B-A37B (80.2) while using approximately 10× fewer active parameters. On IOI 2025, it achieves 439.28/600 (gold medal). On ICPC World Finals 2025, it solves 10/12 problems.

These results are not produced by novel inference-time scaling — the paper explicitly states it uses the same generate-verify-refine pipeline from DeepSeek-Math-V2. The performance comes from the quality of the post-training pipeline: carefully curated SFT data (2.6M competition math + 816K proof samples, generated by DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale and GPT-OSS-120B), systematically ordered RL stages, and the MOPD mechanism that prevents capability regression.

**Why this is significant beyond the raw numbers.** This result provides evidence for a specific claim about where reasoning capability lives: not primarily in the pretrained parameter count, but in the post-training data quality and training methodology. The base model — Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B-Base — is publicly available. The SFT data and RL data are released. The training methodology is documented in detail. This means the result is, in principle, reproducible by the community — a sharp contrast with proprietary models whose training recipes and data are opaque.

The comparison with Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B (Table 1) — which uses the same base model but a different post-training pipeline — reinforces this claim. Cascade 2 outperforms the earlier Nano model on nearly every benchmark, sometimes dramatically (LiveCodeBench v6: 87.2 vs. 68.3; ArenaHard v2: 83.5 vs. 67.7; IFBench: 82.9 vs. 71.5). Since the pretrained model is identical, all of these gains are attributable to the post-training pipeline design.

The paper is appropriately careful not to overclaim here. It acknowledges that on knowledge-intensive and agentic benchmarks, Cascade 2 underperforms Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (MMLU-Pro: 79.8 vs. 85.3; `$\tau$`2-Bench: 58.9 vs. 81.2), explicitly noting that "stronger knowledge-intensive pretraining and agentic RL" remain important for future work. This acknowledges a boundary: post-training cannot fully compensate for pretraining scale on tasks requiring broad factual knowledge or complex multi-turn agentic behavior — at least not with current methods.

### Innovation 4: Verifier-Based RL Stage Design with Dynamic Difficulty Filtering and Asymmetric Verification Infrastructure

The paper's approach to RL stage design — particularly for Code RL and SWE RL — introduces a set of filtering and verification strategies that treat *training data difficulty* as a first-class optimization variable rather than accepting the dataset as given.

**What the field did before.** Standard RLVR practice is to train on all available prompts with verifiable rewards, using uniform sampling or simple shuffling. The implicit assumption is that more data is better, and the training process will naturally learn to allocate its capacity across easy and hard examples. The danger of reward hacking is typically addressed through KL regularization against a reference policy, not through data selection.

**What this paper does differently.** The paper applies aggressive, domain-specific data filtering at multiple stages:

- **Code RL (Section 4.7.1):** Prompts solved correctly by GPT-OSS-120B in all 8/8 rollouts are removed entirely. This eliminates ~70%+ of the original dataset, retaining only 3.5K high-difficulty samples. The rationale is sharp: training on problems the model already solves in expectation provides no useful gradient signal — the reward is always 1, the advantage is zero, and the compute is wasted.

- **Agentless SWE RL (Section 4.8.1):** Prompts where no rollout achieves a reward above 0.5 are masked from the loss entirely. These are problems where even the best response is poor quality, providing no positive signal to differentiate better from worse attempts.

- **Agentic SWE RL (Section 4.8.2):** Instances with 100% pass rate (all 16 rollouts succeed) are removed as too easy. Instances with 0% pass rate are randomly subsampled to 10% retention, preventing "hopeless" problems from dominating the training signal with uniformly negative rewards.

**Why this is a conceptual contribution.** This filtering strategy operationalizes a principle that is often stated but rarely implemented systematically: the optimal training distribution for RL is *not* the natural distribution of problems, but a difficulty-filtered distribution that maximizes the information content of each gradient step. Problems that are too easy provide zero information (all rollouts succeed, no gradient). Problems that are too hard provide near-zero information (all rollouts fail, the advantage signal is noise). The sweet spot — where some rollouts succeed and some fail — provides the richest gradient signal because the group normalization in GRPO can differentiate effective from ineffective strategies.

The paper does not theorize this principle abstractly, but it is embedded in the concrete design choices. The use of GPT-OSS-120B as a difficulty oracle for filtering is pragmatic — it requires a teacher model strong enough to reliably solve the filtered problems, which would not be available in all settings. But the principle is transferable: if you can estimate problem difficulty (through a strong teacher, through historical pass rates, or through a learned difficulty predictor), filter your RL training data to concentrate on the boundary of your model's current capability.

The asynchronous verification infrastructure for Code RL — 384 CPU cores handling 2,048 code executions per step — is not conceptually novel but reflects a practical insight: **verification cost heterogeneity** is as important as response length heterogeneity in determining training efficiency. Joint training across code, math, and preference domains would force all workers to wait for the slowest verification (agentic SWE episodes with Docker execution). Cascade RL isolates these costs, enabling domain-specific scaling of verification resources.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on a comprehensive suite spanning mathematical reasoning, code reasoning, knowledge and STEM, alignment and instruction following, long-context understanding, in-context learning, multilingual capabilities, and agentic tasks. Key benchmarks include: IMO 2025 (6 problems, evaluated by human expert), IMO-ProofBench (60 proof-based problems, evaluated with LLM ProofAutoGrader using DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale), AIME 2025/2026 (30 problems each), HMMT Feb 2025 (30 problems), IOI 2025 (official competition problems), ICPC World Finals 2025 (12 problems), LiveCodeBench v6 (454 problems from 2024/08–2025/05), LiveCodeBench Pro 25Q1/25Q2 (166/167 problems each), ArenaHard v2 (750 diverse user prompts), IFBench (294 prompts), SWE-bench Verified (500 samples), Terminal Bench 2.0 (89 tasks), and several others detailed in Appendix A. Mathematical reasoning benchmarks use avg@64 for AIME/HMMT and avg@16 for IMO-AnswerBench; code benchmarks use avg@8 for LiveCodeBench; alignment benchmarks use avg@8 for ArenaHard/IFBench; knowledge benchmarks use single-generation pass@1 for MMLU-Redux/MMLU-Pro, avg@8 for GPQA-Diamond, and single-generation for HLE.

- **Base model.** All experiments use Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B-Base as the pretrained model. This is a 30B Mixture-of-Experts model with 3B activated parameters, chosen because it represents a compact but capable base that leaves substantial room for post-training improvements without reaching performance saturation. For the specific comparisons in Table 1, the post-trained Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B (same base, different post-training pipeline), Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B, and Qwen3.5-35B-A3B serve as reference points to isolate the effect of the Cascade RL + MOPD training methodology from the base model architecture.

- **Metrics.** The primary metrics are task-specific accuracy measures: exact match accuracy for MMLU benchmarks; pass@1 averaged over multiple generations (avg@N) for reasoning benchmarks; ELO rating with estimated rank for Codeforces performance; pass rates for SWE-bench Verified (pass@k, where k = 1 or 4 with OpenHands scaffold); LLM-judge scores for ArenaHard v2 (using GPT-4.1 as judge, reported relative to a baseline without style control); and human expert scoring on a 0–7 scale for IMO 2025. For IMO-ProofBench, the paper uses a custom aggregation rule: if any of 64 grading attempts with the LLM ProofAutoGrader assigns a score of 0, the final score is set to 0; otherwise, return the mean score. This rule is chosen because naive mean scoring overestimates performance — DeepSeek-Math-V2 obtains 73.8 mean vs. 61.9 human rating on Advanced, while the aggregation rule yields 57.7, reducing the discrepancy from 11.9 to 4.2 points (Appendix A.1.2).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against multiple recent models evaluated under their recommended inference configurations: Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (Qwen Team, 2026), Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B and Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B (Blakeman et al., 2025), Kimi-K2.5-1T-Thinking, DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale and DeepSeek-Math-V2-671B-A37B (Liu et al., 2025), GPT-OSS-120B (Agarwal et al., 2025), Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507 (Yang et al., 2025), and several proprietary models (GPT-5.2, Gemini-3 Pro, GPT-o4-mini) for code reasoning comparison. See Tables 1, 5, and 6 for specific versions.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper standardizes evaluation by specifying per-benchmark inference configurations: a thinking budget (maximum response length) of 128K tokens for most reasoning benchmarks, extended to 256K for IMO-AnswerBench and IMO-ProofBench; sampling temperature of 1.0 and top-p of 0.95 (or 1.0 for some math benchmarks); and specific generation counts per benchmark (avg@64 for AIME/HMMT, avg@16 for IMO-AnswerBench, avg@8 for LiveCodeBench and GPQA-Diamond, avg@4 for SWE-bench Verified and LongBench v2, avg@1 for MMLU and NIAH). For IMO 2025 evaluation, a test-time scaling budget is specified: up to 8 rounds of generate-verify-refine with 128 proof generations, 64 verifications per proof, selection of top 32 proofs for refinement, and 8 verification analyses per proof. For IOI 2025, at most 50 rounds per subtask with 40 candidate solutions per round (at most 2000 model generations for the gold-medal score). For ICPC World Finals 2025, up to 1000 solutions per problem. For Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR) results, the model can call a stateful Python executor for up to 100 calls with a maximum response length of 131K tokens.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No formal cross-validation or statistical significance testing is reported. For IMO-ProofBench evaluation, 64 grading attempts are run with the LLM ProofAutoGrader to ensure reproducibility, and the non-standard aggregation rule is applied to more closely match human ratings. For Codeforces ELO estimation, 40 recent Div.1 and Div.2 contests (rounds 2501–2507) are used with up to 8 submissions per problem, estimating model performance and relative ranking to human contestants with expected penalty consideration (Appendix D). For HLE evaluation, a modified prompt appending "Please place your final answer inside \boxed{}" is used instead of the official HLE response format, which the paper reports improves accuracy by 6–7 points on the math subset.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Mathematical Reasoning: IMO and Competition Math

The headline result appears in Table 2: Nemotron-Cascade-2-30B-A3B achieves **35/42 on IMO 2025**, solving 5 of 6 problems with full 7/7 marks from a human expert (IMO 2015 Gold medalist). The model earns 7/7 on problems 1, 3, 4, and 5, 7/7 on problem 2 (evaluated via LLM grader with reference solution due to the model's extensive analytic geometry approach), and 0/7 on problem 6 (Table 2, Appendix E). This score exceeds the IMO gold medal threshold of 29 points, making it "the second open-weight LLM, after DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale-671B-A37B, to achieve gold-medal performance" at the IMO (Section 5.1).

On IMO-ProofBench (Table 5), Nemotron-Cascade-2-30B-A3B achieves **72.9 overall** (92.5 Basic, 53.4 Advanced), placing it within 8 points of DeepSeek-Math-V2-671B-A37B at 80.2 (99.0 Basic, 61.9 Advanced). Under the paper's reproduced evaluation protocol (using DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale as judge with the custom aggregation rule), DeepSeek-Math-V2 obtains 78.6, making the gap 5.7 points. Notably, Nemotron-Cascade-2 substantially outperforms GPT-5.2-Thinking (high) on Advanced (53.4 vs. 35.7), GPT-5 Pro (53.4 vs. 28.6), and Gemini 3 Pro (53.4 vs. 30.0), despite using far fewer parameters. The gap to DeepSeek-Math-V2 narrows with increased test-time compute: Figure 4 shows Advanced split performance rising from 40.7 at round 1 to 53.4 at round 5 of generate-verify-refine, while DeepSeek-Math-V2's score (under the same grader) remains at 57.7.

On non-proof competition math (Table 1), Nemotron-Cascade-2 achieves:
- AIME 2025: **92.4** (without tools) and **98.6** (with TIR), compared to Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B at 89.1, Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B at 90.2, and Qwen3.5-35B-A3B at 91.9
- AIME 2026: **90.9** (without tools) and **95.0** (with TIR), compared to 89.9 (Nano), 89.8 (Super), and 91.1 (Qwen3.5)
- HMMT Feb 2025: **94.6**, compared to 84.6 (Nano), 93.7 (Super), and 89.0 (Qwen3.5)
- IMO-AnswerBench: **79.3**, compared to 70.4 (Nano), 77.2 (Super), and 74.8 (Qwen3.5)

The TIR boost is substantial: +6.2 points on AIME 2025 and +4.1 on AIME 2026, indicating the model effectively leverages Python execution for verification and computation.

On the hardest reasoning benchmark, HLE (no tool) (Table 1), Nemotron-Cascade-2 achieves **17.7**, compared to 10.6 (Nano), 18.3 (Super), and 22.4 (Qwen3.5-35B-A3B). The model trails Qwen3.5 on this knowledge-intensive frontier benchmark, consistent with the paper's acknowledgment that "stronger knowledge-intensive pretraining" remains important (Section 2).

#### Code Reasoning: Competitive Programming and Olympiad Results

The competitive programming results are reported in Table 2, Table 6, and Appendix D. The headline achievement: gold medals at both IOI 2025 (439.28/600) and ICPC World Finals 2025 (10/12 problems solved, #4 Gold medal placement), with the IOI full score achievable at 507.66 within 5000 generations. On IOI 2025, the model achieved full score on problems 3 and 4, and on problem 2 (requiring heuristic algorithm design and optimization), it "reached over 86 points in just 5 rounds (at most 200 model generations), demonstrating the effectiveness of self-refinement and cross-subtask insights" (Section 6.1). On ICPC World Finals 2025, "8 problems (except Problems A and I) solved within only 100 submissions" (Section 6.1).

On standard competitive programming benchmarks (Table 6), with avg@8 and 128K-token thinking budget:
- LiveCodeBench v6: **87.2** (without TIR) and **88.4** (with TIR), compared to Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B at 68.3, Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B at 78.7, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B at 74.6, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B at 83.6, Kimi-K2.5-1T-Thinking at 85.0, DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale at 88.7, GPT-5.2 (high) at unreported for v6, and Gemini-3 Pro at 90.7
- LiveCodeBench Pro 25Q2 Easy: **87.0** (without TIR) and **89.3** (with TIR), compared to 54.5 (Nano), 81.7 (Super), 81.1 (Qwen3.5-35B)
- LiveCodeBench Pro 25Q2 Medium: **27.6** (without TIR) and **36.8** (with TIR), compared to 3.50 (Nano), 23.2 (Super), 17.8 (Qwen3.5-35B), 37.9 (Kimi-K2.5-1T), 43.1 (DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale)

On Codeforces ELO (Table 6, Appendix D), Nemotron-Cascade-2-30B-A3B achieves **2320** (without TIR) and **2345** (with TIR), with estimated percentiles of 99.6 and 99.7 respectively. This places it above GPT-OSS-120B (2320), Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (2181), and Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B (2212), while trailing GPT-5.2 (high) at 2590, Gemini-3 Pro at 2440, DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale at 2353, and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B at 2350.

The paper specifically highlights the model's TIR capability as a differentiator: "With Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR), our model's performance can be further boosted especially on hard problems, and match the strongest open-source models with more than 300B total parameters, such as Kimi-K2.5-1T-Thinking, Qwen-3.5-397B-A17B, and DeepSeek-v3.2-Speciale, which either lack TIR support for deep reasoning or perform poorly with Python TIR" (Section 6.2). Notably, the model "achieves above 0% on the LiveCodeBench Pro hard split within 8 attempts, demonstrating strong reasoning ability on problems that are extremely difficult even for humans" (Section 6.2).

Detailed per-contest Codeforces results are provided in Appendix D (Tables 11–12), showing the model's performance on individual rounds. Key observations: the model achieves rank 4/11206 (Teza Round 1, Div. 1 + Div. 2, with TIR), rank 1 on multiple Div. 2 rounds (e.g., Round 997, Round 1005, Round 1007, Round 1011, Round 1021), and shows strong performance on Div. 1 rounds (e.g., rank 21/651 on Round 1021 with TIR for ELO 3143). The authors note weaknesses on "problems that require constructive algorithms, interactive manner, and hypothesis-driven ideas" (Appendix D).

#### Alignment and Instruction Following

The alignment results (Table 1) show substantial improvements over the base post-trained model and strong positioning against Qwen3.5-35B-A3B:
- ArenaHard v2 (Average): **83.5**, compared to 67.7 (Nano) and 65.4 (Qwen3.5-35B)
- ArenaHard v2 (Hard Prompt): **88.2**, compared to 72.1 (Nano), 73.9 (Super), and 64.5 (Qwen3.5-35B)
- ArenaHard v2 (Creative Writing): **78.7**, compared to 63.2 (Nano) and 66.3 (Qwen3.5-35B)
- IFBench (prompt): **82.9**, compared to 71.5 (Nano), 72.6 (Super), and 70.2 (Qwen3.5-35B)

These represent large improvements over the baseline: +15.8 points on ArenaHard v2 average, +16.1 on Hard Prompt, +15.5 on Creative Writing, and +11.4 on IFBench compared to Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B (which shares the same base model). The gap to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B is particularly striking on ArenaHard Hard Prompt (88.2 vs. 64.5, +23.7 points), suggesting that the Cascade RL + MOPD pipeline is especially effective for alignment compared to Qwen's post-training approach at this scale.

The IFBench result of 82.9 (described as "state-of-the-art" in Section 4.2) reflects the success of the IF-RL stage placed first in the Cascade RL ordering, combined with the MOPD stage that recovers any instruction-following regressions from subsequent training.

However, on Scale AI Multi-Challenge (Table 1), Nemotron-Cascade-2 scores **45.3**, compared to 38.5 (Nano), 55.2 (Super), and 60.0 (Qwen3.5-35B). This benchmark evaluates multi-turn conversation with instruction retention, inference memory, and self-coherence — capabilities that may be more dependent on pretraining scale and agentic RL than on the specific ordering of Cascade RL stages, consistent with the paper's acknowledgment of weaknesses in agentic benchmarks.

#### Agentic and Software Engineering Tasks

The agentic results are mixed (Table 1), revealing both strengths and clear limitations:
- SWE-bench Verified (OpenHands): **50.2**, compared to 38.8 (Nano), 60.5 (Super), and 69.2 (Qwen3.5-35B)
- BFCL v4: **52.9**, compared to 53.8 (Nano) and 67.3 (Qwen3.5-35B)
- `$\tau$`2-Bench: **58.9**, compared to 49.0 (Nano), 61.2 (Super), and 81.2 (Qwen3.5-35B)
- Terminal Bench 2.0: **21.1**, compared to 8.5 (Nano), 31.0 (Super), and 40.5 (Qwen3.5-35B)

The SWE-bench Verified result (50.2) represents a notable improvement over the same base model's other post-training (Nano at 38.8, +11.4 points), but it substantially trails Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (69.2, -19.0 points). This gap is explicitly acknowledged by the authors: the model "underperforms Qwen3.5-35B-A3B on knowledge-intensive and agentic benchmarks, highlighting the importance of stronger knowledge-intensive pretraining and agentic RL in future work" (Section 2).

Table 4 provides a within-pipeline comparison showing the effectiveness of the Agentless RL stage: before Agentless RL, the model achieves 49.8% avg@4 and 64.2% pass@4 on SWE-bench Verified with OpenHands; after Agentless RL, these improve to 50.8% avg@4 and 65.0% pass@4. While the absolute gain is modest (+1.0% avg@4, +0.8% pass@4), the paper notes this improvement generalizes across scaffolds — the Agentless Mini scaffold improves from 41.9% avg@4 to 44.3% avg@4 — supporting the claim that "improving models' code repair capability alone can generalize across different scaffolds" (Section 4.8.1).

The `$\tau$`2-Bench result of 58.9 includes the authors' modification of the thought retention policy: they adopt "latest-turn thought retention" (keeping reasoning content only from after the most recent user turn) rather than the official "no thought carry-over" policy, which the paper reports "consistently reduces scores by 3–5 points." Additionally, for the telecom subset, the system prompt is modified to repeat the dual-control instruction three times. These modifications make direct comparison with other models' reported numbers less straightforward.

#### Knowledge and STEM

Knowledge-intensive benchmarks (Table 1) reveal the primary limitation of the compact model scale:
- MMLU-Redux: **86.3**, compared to 93.3 (Qwen3.5-35B-A3B) — a 7.0 point gap
- MMLU-Pro: **79.8**, compared to 78.3 (Nano), 83.7 (Super), and 85.3 (Qwen3.5-35B)
- GPQA-Diamond: **76.1**, compared to 73.0 (Nano), 79.2 (Super), and 84.2 (Qwen3.5-35B)

The model outperforms its base-post-trained sibling (Nano) on MMLU-Pro (+1.5) and GPQA-Diamond (+3.1), showing that the Cascade RL pipeline improves knowledge reasoning somewhat. But the gap to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B remains substantial, especially on MMLU-Redux (-7.0), confirming that post-training alone cannot fully compensate for differences in pretraining scale and knowledge coverage.

#### Long Context and Context Learning

Long-context capabilities (Table 1) show a mixed picture:
- NIAH@1M (RULER Subset): **99.0**, compared to 94.8 (Nano), 98.3 (Super), and 94.3 (Qwen3.5-35B) — best in class
- AA-LCR: **39.1**, compared to 35.9 (Nano), 58.3 (Super), and 58.5 (Qwen3.5-35B) — competitive with Nano but trailing larger models
- LongBench v2: **40.3**, compared to 39.6 (Nano) and 59.0 (Qwen3.5-35B) — similar to Nano, trailing significantly
- CL-Bench: **12.2**, compared to 12.0 (Nano) and 15.5 (Qwen3.5-35B)

The NIAH@1M score of 99.0 indicates near-perfect needle-in-a-haystack retrieval at 1M context length, validating the long-context RL stage's effectiveness for retrieval tasks. However, the more complex long-context reasoning benchmarks (AA-LCR, LongBench v2) show minimal improvement over Nano and substantial gaps to both Super (which has 4× more parameters) and Qwen3.5-35B, suggesting that long-context *reasoning* (as opposed to retrieval) benefits more from pretraining scale than from post-training RL.

#### Multilingual Capabilities

Multilingual results (Table 1) are modest:
- MMLU-ProX: **72.5**, compared to 59.5 (Nano), 79.4 (Super), and 81.0 (Qwen3.5-35B)
- WMT24++ (en → xx): **84.1**, compared to 86.2 (Nano), 86.7 (Super), and 87.6 (Qwen3.5-35B)

The model shows improvement over Nano on MMLU-ProX (+13.0) but actually regresses slightly on WMT24++ (-2.1), suggesting that the Cascade RL pipeline, which does not include dedicated multilingual RL stages, may partially trade off translation quality for gains in other domains.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**MOPD vs. GRPO training efficiency on AIME25 (Figure 3c):** Starting from the same initial checkpoint at 89.9, GRPO improves to 91.0 after 25 steps, while MOPD reaches 92.0 within 30 steps and recovers teacher-level performance. Despite MOPD requiring 5 more steps, it achieves 1.0 point higher final accuracy, demonstrating that the dense token-level advantage signal provides more effective gradient updates per step than the sparse sequence-level reward.

**MOPD vs. RLHF training efficiency on ArenaHard v2 (Table 3):** Starting from the same initial checkpoint (Hard Prompt 71.5, Creative Writing 40.6), MOPD improves Hard Prompt to 85.5 and Creative Writing to 71.0 after only 52 steps. In contrast, RLHF requires 100 steps to reach 81.7 Hard Prompt and 68.6 Creative Writing, and 160 steps to reach 80.7 Hard Prompt and 71.2 Creative Writing. MOPD achieves +3.8 Hard Prompt and +2.4 Creative Writing improvement over RLHF's best results, while using roughly one-third the number of optimization steps. Notably, RLHF performance slightly degrades from step 100 to step 160 on Hard Prompt (81.7 → 80.7), suggesting potential overfitting, while MOPD converges more stably.

**MOPD training dynamics (Figure 3a, 3b):** The reverse-KL distillation advantage converges toward zero during training (Figure 3a), indicating the student policy successfully matches the teacher distributions. Gradient norms are substantially larger at the beginning of training and decrease rapidly after the 30-step warm-up phase (Figure 3b), validating the importance of the linear warm-up schedule in preventing training instability from large initial gradient spikes.

**Agentless RL effectiveness on SWE-bench Verified (Table 4):** Agentless code repair RL improves both agentless scaffold performance (Agentless Mini: 41.9% → 44.3% avg@4, 55.2% → 57.4% pass@4) and agentic scaffold performance (OpenHands: 49.8% → 50.8% avg@4, 64.2% → 65.0% pass@4). The cross-scaffold transfer is the key finding — improving pure code repair capability generalizes to full agentic settings, supporting the agentless RL stage's placement in the Cascade RL pipeline.

**SWE SFT data composition ablation (Section 3.2.9):** A preliminary study showed that fine-tuning on agentic data alone achieves Pass@1 of 48.9 and Pass@4 of 62.8 on SWE-bench Verified with OpenHands, while adding agentless data (code localization, repair, test generation) improves to Pass@1 of 49.9 and Pass@4 of 65.2. This +1.0 Pass@1 and +2.4 Pass@4 gain from incorporating agentless SFT data motivated the combined 125K agentic + 389K agentless SFT data mixture.

**Thinking vs. non-thinking mode for RLHF (Section 4.5.2):** An ablation comparing exclusive thinking-mode RLHF to a mixed thinking/non-thinking approach: "While incorporating both thinking and non-thinking modes can improve training convergence and yield slight gains on evaluation benchmarks, we observe a significant degradation in instruction-following performance. The resulting drop is substantial enough that the gains obtained in the earlier RLVR stage cannot be fully recovered." This finding directly motivates both the exclusive thinking-mode RLHF design and the placement of RLHF after MOPD (which has already rebalanced instruction following).

**RLHF KL coefficient ablation (Section 4.5.3):** RLHF uses a KL loss coefficient of 0.03, unlike all other RL stages which use 0. This is described as intended "to keep the model capabilities on other domains," representing an implicit ablation: removing KL regularization during RLHF would likely improve alignment metrics further but at the cost of regressions in math, code, and other capabilities. The paper does not report quantitative results for this ablation, but the design choice itself encodes the tradeoff.

**Difficulty filtering ablations across RL stages:** Multiple stages employ domain-specific data filtering, representing implicit ablations of training on unfiltered data:

- **Code RL filtering (Section 4.7.1):** Removing prompts solved by GPT-OSS-120B in 8/8 rollouts reduces the dataset to 3.5K samples. The paper does not report results with unfiltered data, but the choice implies that training on easy problems wastes compute and may dilute the gradient signal from hard problems.

- **Agentless SWE RL loss masking (Section 4.8.1):** Masking prompts where no rollout receives a reward > 0.5 improves "stability and effectiveness." No quantitative comparison is provided.

- **Agentic SWE RL difficulty subsampling (Section 4.8.2):** Removing 100%-pass instances and randomly discarding 90% of 0%-pass instances. Again, no quantitative ablation is reported, but the detailed justification — "these difficult prompts degrade the stability and effectiveness" — suggests this filtering was necessary for training to converge at all.

**Overlong penalty in IF-RL (Section 4.2.2):** An implicit ablation: IF-RL without overlong penalty leads to "excessive token usage, which is often unnecessary for fulfilling specific constraints in general chat domains." The paper does not report quantitative results, but the penalty's inclusion reflects an observed pathology where the model learns to satisfy simple constraints through verbosity.

**Long-context RL domain isolation (Section 4.6):** An ablation of joint vs. isolated training: "incorporating other domains during long-context RL negatively affects performance on unrelated benchmarks, motivating this domain-specific training setup." This finding directly validates the Cascade RL philosophy — not all domain combinations are compatible in joint training — but no quantitative results are provided for the joint training comparison.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does the paper demonstrate that Cascade RL + MOPD enables scaling to 6+ RL domains while preventing capability regression?

The evidence for this is primarily in the aggregate benchmark results (Table 1) and the MOPD training dynamics (Figure 3, Table 3), but it is **circumstantial rather than direct**. The paper convincingly shows that the final model achieves strong performance across reasoning, alignment, instruction following, long-context, and agentic benchmarks. However, the critical causal claim — that MOPD specifically *prevented* regressions that would have occurred without it — is supported by only two pieces of direct evidence: (1) the comparison of MOPD vs. RLHF on ArenaHard (Table 3), which shows MOPD achieves better final performance, and (2) the statement that "we observe noticeable fluctuations across different benchmark categories tracked throughout training" (Section 4.4), which is qualitative.

**What is missing:** A direct ablation of the full Cascade RL pipeline with and without MOPD. Such a comparison would show, for each benchmark category, the performance trajectory across training stages in both conditions, quantifying exactly how much regression MOPD prevents. The paper's release of intermediate checkpoints makes this ablation possible for future work, but the current paper does not perform it. Relatedly, the paper does not report per-benchmark performance at *each stage* of the Cascade RL pipeline — we see only the final model's results and isolated comparisons at specific stages. A stage-by-stage progression table would make the regression-and-recovery dynamics directly visible.

Without this evidence, alternative explanations for the final model's performance remain plausible: perhaps the specific stage ordering alone (without MOPD) would have achieved similar results; perhaps the SFT data quality is the dominant factor; perhaps the large-scale teacher model responses used for SFT data generation (DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale, GPT-OSS-120B) contribute more to performance than the RL pipeline design. The paper claims MOPD is the key innovation, but the *experimental* evidence for its necessity over simpler approaches (e.g., replaying SFT data from the strongest domains, or using a static multi-objective reward) is not provided.

#### Does the paper demonstrate that MOPD's efficiency advantage is due to dense token-level signals rather than other factors?

The comparisons in Figure 3c (MOPD vs. GRPO on AIME25) and Table 3 (MOPD vs. RLHF on ArenaHard) show MOPD achieving better final performance in fewer steps. The paper attributes this to "dense token-level distillation advantage" vs. "sparse sequence-level outcome reward." This is plausible but not rigorously isolated.

**What is missing:** A control where GRPO or RLHF receives an equivalent amount of per-token supervision. For example, using the same teacher checkpoints to provide per-token rewards (rather than per-sequence rewards) in a GRPO framework would test whether the advantage comes from the distillation signal or from the token-level granularity. Alternatively, ablating the token-level aspect of MOPD by providing only a sequence-level distillation advantage (averaging `$a_t^{\text{MOPD}}$` across all tokens) would isolate the contribution of dense vs. sparse signals. The current experiments compare MOPD to methods that differ in both the *source* of the training signal (teacher distillation vs. outcome reward) and the *granularity* (token-level vs. sequence-level), making it impossible to attribute the efficiency gains to one factor over the other.

Additionally, the AIME25 comparison (Figure 3c) uses "math-only training" for both MOPD and GRPO, but the ArenaHard comparison (Table 3) compares MOPD (trained with multi-domain teacher mixture) against RLHF (trained only on human preference data). These are not directly comparable training regimes — MOPD benefits from the RLHF teacher's knowledge as part of its multi-teacher distillation, while RLHF must learn alignment from scratch via environment interaction. The efficiency gain may partly reflect MOPD having access to already-optimized teacher policies rather than needing to discover alignment through exploration.

#### Does the paper demonstrate that the IMO 2025 gold medal is attributable to the Cascade RL pipeline rather than to test-time scaling?

The paper explicitly states that IMO 2025 evaluation uses "a self-improving test-time scaling framework" with the same generate-verify-refine pipeline from DeepSeek-Math-V2 (Section 5.1). This means the reported IMO score reflects the combination of the post-trained model *plus* substantial test-time compute. The contribution of the model's base policy (which is what Cascade RL optimizes) vs. the test-time scaling framework (which amplifies any competent base policy) is not separated.

**What is missing:** An evaluation of the model's IMO performance without test-time scaling (e.g., pass@1 with a single generation). This would reveal how much of the gold-medal performance comes from the policy itself vs. the generate-verify-refine amplification. If the model's single-generation IMO performance is, say, bronze-level, then the claim that Cascade RL produces IMO-gold-level mathematical reasoning needs the qualification "when combined with substantial test-time compute." This does not diminish the achievement — test-time scaling is a legitimate capability — but it does affect how one interprets the post-training pipeline's contribution.

The paper provides some indirect evidence: the IMO-ProofBench results (Figure 4) show performance improving from 40.7 at round 1 to 53.4 at round 5, indicating that the base policy alone (round 1) is substantially weaker than the test-time-scaled final result. The gap between round 1 (40.7) and the final reported score (53.4 on Advanced, 72.9 overall) suggests test-time scaling provides roughly 12+ points of improvement on Advanced problems.

#### Does the paper demonstrate that the performance improvements come from Cascade RL + MOPD rather than from SFT data quality?

The SFT data described in Section 3.2 is extraordinarily extensive and high-quality: 2.6M competition math samples generated by DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale, 4.2M code reasoning traces generated by GPT-OSS-120B with correctness filtering, 816K proof samples, and similar large-scale data for every domain. The SFT teachers include some of the strongest available models (DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale, GPT-OSS-120B, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507).

**What is missing:** A comparison showing the SFT model's performance (before any RL) on key benchmarks. The paper reports that the SFT checkpoint serves as the math teacher for MOPD and has "strong mathematical reasoning capabilities," but does not provide a full evaluation of the SFT-only model. Without this baseline, it is unclear what fraction of the final model's reasoning capability was already present after SFT and what fraction was genuinely acquired through RL. The statement that the MOPD math teacher "already exhibits strong mathematical reasoning capabilities thanks to the meticulously curated SFT dataset" (Section 4.4) suggests the SFT model may already be quite strong — potentially accounting for a significant portion of the final model's math performance, with RL providing refinement rather than fundamentally new capabilities.

The comparison between Nemotron-Cascade-2 and Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B (Table 1) partially addresses this — both use the same base model, so the performance differences reflect differences in the post-training pipelines. Cascade 2 substantially outperforms Nano on reasoning benchmarks (LiveCodeBench v6: 87.2 vs. 68.3; AIME 2025: 92.4 vs. 89.1), but the Nano post-training pipeline may use different (potentially weaker) SFT data, making it an imperfect control for isolating the RL contribution.

#### Are there concerns about evaluation contamination or benchmark-specific optimization?

Several evaluation choices raise questions about whether the reported numbers reflect genuine capability or are partially artifacts of evaluation methodology alignment with training:

- **HLE evaluation modification (Appendix A.3):** The paper modifies the official HLE prompt to use `\boxed{}` answer formatting, which "improves the accuracy by 6–7 points, primarily on the math subset, by better aligning with the answer format used in our math SFT data." This is a legitimate evaluation choice (the model was trained to output boxed answers), but it means the 17.7 HLE score is not directly comparable to other models' HLE scores using the standard prompt. The paper is transparent about this, but readers comparing HLE scores across papers need to account for the difference.

- **`$\tau$`2-Bench evaluation modifications (Appendix A.6):** The paper adopts "latest-turn thought retention" instead of the official "no thought carry-over" policy, which "consistently reduces scores by 3–5 points," and modifies the telecom system prompt. These changes make the 58.9 score not directly comparable to other models evaluated with the official protocol.

- **IMO-ProofBench evaluation protocol (Appendix A.1.2):** The paper uses a custom aggregation rule (score = 0 if any of 64 judges assigns 0, else mean) that reduces scores relative to naive averaging. This is well-justified (it brings DeepSeek-Math-V2's score closer to human ratings), but it means the 72.9 score is produced by a non-standard protocol. The paper reproduces DeepSeek-Math-V2 under the same protocol (78.6) for fair comparison, which partially addresses this concern.

- **Data contamination from SFT teacher models:** The SFT data is generated by DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale, GPT-OSS-120B, and Qwen3 models. If these teacher models were themselves trained on similar benchmarks (e.g., if DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale's training data included AIME problems), the SFT data could inadvertently contain solutions to benchmark problems. The paper does not describe decontamination procedures for the SFT data, making it difficult to assess whether any benchmark performance gains reflect genuine reasoning capability vs. memorization of teacher-generated solutions. This is a significant omission given the scale of SFT data and the use of frontier teacher models whose training data is not fully documented.

#### What experiments would have strengthened the paper?

1. **A full pipeline ablation without MOPD**: Train the identical Cascade RL pipeline but replace the MOPD stage with either (a) nothing (skip directly to RLHF), (b) SFT data replay, or (c) multi-objective RL with hand-tuned weights. Compare final benchmark performance across all categories.

2. **Per-stage benchmark tracking**: Report key benchmark scores (AIME, LiveCodeBench, ArenaHard, IFBench, SWE-bench) after each stage of the Cascade RL pipeline. This would make the regression-and-recovery dynamics directly visible and would identify which stages cause regressions in which capabilities.

3. **SFT-only baseline evaluation**: Report full benchmark results for the SFT model before any RL training. This would quantify the RL contribution and help assess whether the expensive RL pipeline is justified relative to simply doing better SFT.

4. **Single-generation IMO/IOI evaluation**: Report pass@1 without test-time scaling to separate the policy's capability from the test-time scaling framework's amplification.

5. **Token-level vs. sequence-level ablation for MOPD**: Compare the full MOPD objective against a variant where the distillation advantage is averaged to a single scalar per sequence (preserving the teacher signal but removing the token-level granularity). This would isolate whether the dense signal or the teacher knowledge drives MOPD's efficiency.

6. **MOPD with external teachers**: Replace the internal Cascade RL checkpoint teachers with external teachers (e.g., use DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale directly as the math teacher instead of the SFT checkpoint). This would test whether the "same tokenizer, same SFT initialization" property is actually important for MOPD's effectiveness or just a convenience.

7. **Scale analysis**: Vary the number of Cascade RL stages (e.g., 3 stages vs. 6 stages vs. 9 stages) and measure whether MOPD's importance grows with the number of stages. This would directly test the paper's implicit claim that MOPD becomes more necessary as the pipeline scales.

#### Summary of experimental strengths and limitations

**Strengths:**

- **Exceptionally comprehensive evaluation**: The paper evaluates on 25+ benchmarks spanning reasoning, alignment, agentic tasks, long context, and multilingual capabilities, providing a thorough characterization of the model's strengths and weaknesses.
- **Transparent reporting of evaluation modifications**: Changes to standard evaluation protocols (HLE prompt, `$\tau$`2-Bench thought retention, IMO-ProofBench aggregation) are explicitly documented, enabling informed comparison.
- **Reproducible IMO-ProofBench evaluation**: The paper reproduces DeepSeek-Math-V2 under the same LLM-judge protocol to enable fair comparison, rather than relying on previously reported numbers from different evaluation setups.
- **Detailed per-contest Codeforces results**: Appendix D provides granular performance data across 40 individual contests, allowing analysis of specific strengths and weaknesses in competitive programming.
- **Release of training data and checkpoints**: The paper releases SFT data, RL data, and the full collection of model checkpoints, enabling the community to perform the ablations and stage-by-stage analyses that the paper itself does not provide.

**Weaknesses:**

- **No direct ablation of MOPD**: The central methodological claim — that MOPD is necessary for preventing capability regression in multi-stage Cascade RL — lacks a direct experimental test comparing the full pipeline with and without MOPD.
- **No per-stage benchmark progression**: The paper reports only final model performance, making it impossible to trace how each benchmark score evolves across the 7+ RL stages. This obscures which stages cause regressions and whether MOPD actually recovers them.
- **No SFT-only baseline**: Without knowing the SFT model's performance, the contribution of the expensive RL pipeline cannot be isolated from the contribution of the extensive, high-quality SFT data.
- **Confounded training efficiency comparison**: MOPD vs. RLHF on ArenaHard compares methods that differ in both training signal source (teacher distillation vs. environment interaction) and granularity (token-level vs. sequence-level), making causal attribution impossible.
- **Evaluation protocol variations complicate external comparison**: Modifications to HLE, `$\tau$`2-Bench, and IMO-ProofBench protocols mean some reported numbers are not directly comparable to other models evaluated under standard protocols.
- **No decontamination analysis**: Given that SFT data is generated by frontier teacher models whose training data may overlap with evaluation benchmarks, the absence of a decontamination study is a significant gap that makes it difficult to rule out memorization as a partial explanation for strong benchmark performance.
- **Test-time scaling confounds IMO/IOI policy evaluation**: The gold medal results reflect the combination of the trained policy and substantial test-time compute, with no single-generation baseline to separate their contributions.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The MOPD Necessity Claim Lacks a Direct Ablation

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central methodological claim is that MOPD is necessary for preventing capability regression in multi-stage Cascade RL. Section 4.4 states:

> "We find that Multi-domain On-policy Distillation (§4.4) serves as a critical stabilization point in this ordering. It is effective at recovering benchmark performance that may have regressed during earlier, more specialized stages of the cascade RL, leading to a more balanced and robust final policy model."

However, the paper never compares the full 7-stage Cascade RL pipeline with and without MOPD. The evidence for MOPD's effectiveness consists of (a) the comparison with RLHF on ArenaHard (Table 3), which compares MOPD against a different training method (RLHF) rather than against the absence of MOPD in an otherwise identical pipeline, and (b) the comparison with GRPO on AIME25 (Figure 3c), which is under "math-only training" rather than within the full multi-stage pipeline. Neither experiment tests the counterfactual: what would happen if you simply skipped the MOPD stage and proceeded directly from Multi-domain RL to RLHF?

**The consequence.** Without a direct ablation, the paper's primary contribution — that MOPD enables scaling to 6+ RL domains by recovering benchmark regressions — rests on circumstantial evidence. Several alternative explanations for the final model's performance remain viable: (a) the specific stage ordering alone, without MOPD, might have been sufficient; (b) the SFT data quality (2.6M competition math samples generated by DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale, 4.2M code traces from GPT-OSS-120B) might be the dominant factor, with RL providing only marginal refinement; (c) simply replaying SFT data from the strongest domains after Multi-domain RL might achieve similar rebalancing at lower computational cost.

For a practitioner deciding whether to adopt this methodology, the absence of this ablation makes it impossible to estimate MOPD's marginal contribution. The cost of MOPD is non-trivial: it requires maintaining and loading multiple teacher checkpoints, implementing the reverse-KL distillation advantage with truncated importance weighting, and running 40–50 additional optimization steps. If 80% of the benefit comes from careful stage ordering and the other 20% from MOPD, the complexity may not be justified in all settings.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The closest evidence is indirect: the comparison in Table 3 shows MOPD achieving 85.5 on ArenaHard Hard Prompt in 52 steps vs. RLHF reaching 80.7 in 160 steps. But this compares MOPD (with multi-domain teacher distillation) against a different training regime (RLHF with pairwise preference optimization from a generative reward model). The training objectives, teacher signals, and data distributions differ. The AIME25 comparison (Figure 3c) is under isolated math-only conditions, not the full pipeline. The paper does not report per-stage benchmark trajectories that would reveal whether regressions actually occurred and whether MOPD actually recovered them. The statement that "we observe noticeable fluctuations across different benchmark categories tracked throughout training" (Section 4.4) is qualitative and unquantified.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation. It does not report the ablation, does not discuss alternative rebalancing strategies, and does not acknowledge this gap as a limitation. The release of intermediate checkpoints and training data makes this ablation possible for future work, but the paper itself does not perform it. A practitioner must either accept MOPD's necessity on faith or run their own ablation, which requires replicating the entire 7-stage pipeline — a substantial computational undertaking.

---

### 6.2 The SFT-Only Baseline Is Never Evaluated, Making the RL Contribution Unquantifiable

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire post-training pipeline — IF-RL, Multi-domain RL, MOPD, RLHF, Long-context RL, Code RL, and SWE RL — sits on top of an extraordinarily extensive SFT stage. The SFT data (Sections 3.2.1–3.2.10) includes 2.6M competition math samples, 816K proof samples, 4.2M code reasoning traces (generated by GPT-OSS-120B with correctness filtering), 2.7M science samples, 4.9M general chat reasoning-on samples, 822K conversational tool-use samples, 514K SWE agentic and agentless samples, and 490K terminal agent samples. The SFT teachers include DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale, GPT-OSS-120B, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507, and DeepSeek-V3.2 — among the strongest models available at the time of data generation.

The paper never reports a full evaluation of the SFT-only model before any RL training. The closest reference is Section 4.4, which states that the SFT checkpoint serves as the math teacher for MOPD and "already exhibits strong mathematical reasoning capabilities thanks to the meticulously curated SFT dataset." But this is a qualitative assessment, not a benchmark evaluation.

**The consequence.** Without knowing the SFT model's performance, it is impossible to determine what fraction of the final model's capabilities were already present after SFT and what fraction was genuinely acquired through the expensive 7-stage RL pipeline. If the SFT model already achieves, say, 85 on AIME 2025 and 80 on LiveCodeBench v6 (plausible given the scale and quality of the SFT data), then the RL pipeline contributes only a few points. The paper's framing — that Cascade RL + MOPD enables frontier reasoning from a compact model — would need to be qualified as "Cascade RL + MOPD provides modest refinement on top of SFT data that already encodes frontier reasoning through distillation from much larger teacher models."

This matters enormously for resource allocation decisions. The RL pipeline is computationally expensive: 7 sequential stages, each requiring generation of thousands of rollouts, verification infrastructure (384 CPU cores for Code RL alone, Docker environments for SWE RL), and hyperparameter tuning. If SFT alone gets 90% of the way there, the computational return on investment for the RL pipeline may not justify the cost for many practitioners.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The comparison between Nemotron-Cascade-2 and Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B in Table 1 provides a partial signal. Both models share the same base model (Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B-Base), but they use different post-training pipelines — Cascade 2 with the 7-stage RL process, Nano with a different (undocumented here) pipeline. Cascade 2 substantially outperforms Nano on reasoning benchmarks: LiveCodeBench v6 (87.2 vs. 68.3, +18.9), AIME 2025 (92.4 vs. 89.1, +3.3), IFBench (82.9 vs. 71.5, +11.4). However, this comparison conflates differences in SFT data, RL methodology, and inference configurations. The Nano SFT data is described as using different teacher models and curation processes, so the performance gap cannot be attributed to the RL pipeline alone.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It does not report SFT-only benchmark scores, does not discuss the relative contribution of SFT data quality vs. RL training, and does not provide an ablation of the RL pipeline with a weaker SFT baseline to demonstrate that RL adds value beyond what SFT alone provides. For a practitioner, this means the most important baseline — "what if I just do really good SFT?" — is never evaluated, leaving the case for the expensive RL pipeline unsubstantiated.

---

### 6.3 The Difficulty Estimation Cost for Practical Deployment Is Unaddressed

**The assumption or constraint.** The Cascade RL pipeline requires selecting teacher checkpoints that represent peak performance on specific capability axes. Section 4.4 states:

> "Teacher checkpoints can be selected directly from the Cascade RL pipeline by choosing the strongest validation checkpoint for each benchmark category, which makes it easy to assemble a capability-diverse teacher pool without introducing external model families."

This assumes that (a) validation benchmarks exist for each capability domain, (b) the model's performance on these benchmarks reliably indicates its capability on the broader task distribution, and (c) the cost of evaluating all intermediate checkpoints across all benchmarks to identify the "strongest" one is negligible. None of these assumptions are substantiated.

More broadly, the entire Cascade RL design — the ordering of stages, the grouping of domains into multi-domain stages, the decision to isolate long-context RL — depends on observing which domain combinations cause interference and which do not. Section 4.3 states that Multi-domain RL groups together MCQA, tool calling, and structured output because "we do not observe performance degradation across evaluation benchmarks when training on the blended domains." Section 4.6 states that long-context RL is isolated because "incorporating other domains during long-context RL negatively affects performance on unrelated benchmarks." These are empirical findings that required running and evaluating multiple pipeline variants to discover.

**The consequence.** The paper presents the Cascade RL ordering as a principled framework (Section 4.1.1: "Mitigating Inter-Domain Interference," "Scaling via Multi-Domain Integration," "Stabilization through On-policy Distillation"), but the principles are descriptive rather than prescriptive — they explain why the discovered ordering works, but do not provide a method for finding the optimal ordering without running the experiments. A practitioner with a different base model, different SFT data, or different RL domains cannot simply apply the Cascade 2 ordering and expect it to work. They must rediscover the interference patterns for their own setting, which means running the pipeline multiple times with different orderings and domain groupings.

The cost of this discovery process is not accounted for anywhere in the paper. The headline results reflect the performance of one specific ordering discovered through an undisclosed number of preliminary experiments. The computational cost of those preliminary experiments — which may include multiple full or partial pipeline runs — is not included in any cost model. This makes the methodology substantially more expensive than it appears from the description of the final pipeline alone.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides the ordering itself and the rationale for each stage's placement, but does not report:

- How many alternative orderings were tested before settling on the final one.
- How many domain groupings were tried for the Multi-domain RL stage.
- The computational cost of testing these alternatives.
- Whether the optimal ordering depends on the specific base model or SFT data (the paper notes the ordering differs from Cascade 1 "due to improvements in SFT data quality and substantially scaled RL environment complexity," suggesting it does depend on these factors).

The specific finding that long-context RL causes interference with other domains (Section 4.6) and that thinking-mode RLHF degrades instruction following (Section 4.5.2) are crucial to the final ordering but required running experiments that produced negative results — experiments whose cost is invisible in the final pipeline description.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially mitigates this by releasing the training data and checkpoints, which enables the community to reproduce the pipeline without rediscovering the ordering. But for practitioners adapting Cascade RL to different base models, domains, or reward structures, the ordering discovery cost remains. The paper does not suggest a method for predicting interference without running experiments (e.g., through gradient similarity analysis or representation overlap metrics), nor does it estimate the cost of the discovery process. The principles in Section 4.1.1 provide conceptual guidance but not operational procedures.

---

### 6.4 The MOPD vs. GRPO Efficiency Comparison Confounds Training Signal Source and Granularity

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 4.4 claims that MOPD provides a training efficiency advantage because "MOPD provides a dense token-level distillation advantage, whereas GRPO relies on a sparse sequence-level outcome reward that is shared across all generated tokens." Two experiments support this claim: Figure 3c showing MOPD reaching 92.0 on AIME25 vs. GRPO reaching 91.0, and Table 3 showing MOPD reaching 85.5 on ArenaHard Hard Prompt in 52 steps vs. RLHF reaching 80.7 in 160 steps.

However, these comparisons confound two distinct factors: (1) the source of the training signal (teacher distillation vs. environment reward) and (2) the granularity of the signal (token-level vs. sequence-level). MOPD uses a token-level signal from a teacher model; GRPO/RLHF uses a sequence-level signal from an environment reward or generative reward model. The paper attributes the efficiency gain entirely to granularity ("dense token-level distillation advantage"), but the teacher signal itself — which encodes knowledge from a checkpoint that already achieved strong performance — may be the more important factor.

**The consequence.** Without isolating these factors, the paper's claim that dense token-level signals are more efficient than sparse sequence-level signals is unproven. An equally plausible explanation for the efficiency gain is that the teachers (the SFT checkpoint for math, the RLHF-optimized checkpoint for alignment) have already solved the exploration problem — they know what good responses look like — and MOPD simply transfers this knowledge more directly than RL, which must rediscover it through environment interaction. If the teacher signal is the dominant factor, then any method that incorporates teacher knowledge (e.g., sequence-level distillation, supervised fine-tuning on teacher outputs, or even replaying SFT data) might achieve similar efficiency, without the complexity of token-level importance weighting and reverse-KL advantages.

For a practitioner, this confound matters because the token-level MOPD objective is more complex to implement than sequence-level alternatives. It requires computing per-token log-probability differences, truncated importance weighting, and token masks. If a simpler sequence-level distillation method achieves comparable results, the additional complexity of MOPD may not be justified.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no experiment that varies granularity while holding the signal source constant. Specifically, it does not compare:

- Token-level MOPD vs. sequence-level MOPD (where the distillation advantage is averaged across all tokens).
- Sequence-level distillation (using the same teachers as MOPD but with a single scalar reward per sequence) vs. RLHF.
- Token-level MOPD vs. SFT on teacher-generated outputs (which is sequence-level but uses teacher knowledge).

The ArenaHard comparison (Table 3) is particularly confounded because MOPD uses a multi-domain teacher mixture (including the RLHF teacher itself), while the RLHF baseline must learn alignment purely from environment interaction. MOPD essentially gets to "cheat" by distilling from a checkpoint that already solved the alignment problem, so its efficiency advantage may reflect having access to solved examples rather than the token-level granularity of the training signal.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this confound or attempt to separate the factors. The description of MOPD's efficiency advantage (Section 4.4) attributes it entirely to the token-level signal without discussing the alternative explanation. A practitioner would need to run their own ablations to determine whether the token-level granularity or the teacher knowledge is the active ingredient in MOPD's efficiency.

### 6.5 Hard Problems Remain Unsolved — The Pipeline Amplifies Existing Capability but Does Not Create It

**The assumption or constraint.** The Cascade RL + MOPD pipeline is fundamentally a post-training methodology — it operates on a pretrained base model and can only optimize and rebalance capabilities that are already latent in that model. Section 2 of this analysis noted this boundary explicitly: "test-time compute amplifies existing capability but does not create it." The Cascade 2 paper demonstrates this boundary through several results:

- On IMO 2025, the model scores 0/7 on Problem 6 (Table 2, Appendix E). Despite 5 rounds of generate-verify-refine test-time scaling, the model cannot solve this problem.

- On LiveCodeBench Pro 25Q2 Hard (Table 6), the model achieves only 0.7 (without TIR) and 2.2 (with TIR) — effectively near-zero pass rates on the hardest competitive programming problems.

- On HLE (Table 1), the model scores 17.7, trailing both Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (22.4) and Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B (18.3).

- On `$\tau$`2-Bench (Table 1), the model scores 58.9, substantially trailing Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (81.2).

- On IMO-ProofBench Advanced (Table 5), the model scores 53.4 vs. DeepSeek-Math-V2-671B-A37B's 61.9 (human-rated), despite the test-time scaling framework.

**The consequence.** The pipeline's effectiveness is bounded by the base model's pretrained capabilities. For problems where the base model's pass@1 is near zero — the hardest IMO problems, the hardest competitive programming problems, frontier scientific reasoning — no amount of RL stage ordering, MOPD rebalancing, or test-time scaling can produce correct solutions. The pipeline can only optimize the selection and refinement of solutions that the base model can already generate with some non-zero probability.

This limitation is partially acknowledged: Section 2 notes that the model "underperforms Qwen3.5-35B-A3B on knowledge-intensive and agentic benchmarks, highlighting the importance of stronger knowledge-intensive pretraining and agentic RL in future work." But this framing — "agentic RL in future work" — implies the gap can be closed by better RL, which the paper's own results contradict. The `$\tau$`2-Bench, Terminal Bench, and SWE-bench Verified gaps are so large (18–20 points) that they likely reflect fundamental capability limitations of the 30B-A3B base model, not just suboptimal RL stage design.

For practitioners, this means Cascade RL + MOPD is most appropriate when the base model already possesses non-trivial capability in the target domain and the goal is to optimize, stabilize, and integrate that capability. It is not a substitute for pretraining scale on tasks requiring broad factual knowledge, complex multi-turn planning, or novel problem-solving strategies that the base model cannot even attempt.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence for this limitation is distributed across multiple results:

- IMO 2025 Problem 6: 0/7 (Appendix E), showing the model cannot solve at least one problem at the frontier of olympiad mathematics.

- LiveCodeBench Pro Hard: near-zero pass rates (Table 6), showing competitive programming capability saturates at the hardest difficulty tier.

- `$\tau$`2-Bench: -22.3 points vs. Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (Table 1), the largest gap on any benchmark, showing fundamental limitations in multi-turn agentic reasoning.

- Terminal Bench 2.0: 21.1 vs. 40.5 for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (Table 1), a -19.4 point gap, showing limitations in terminal-based task execution.

These results are reported honestly — the paper does not cherry-pick favorable benchmarks or hide the gaps — but they are presented as areas for future improvement rather than as evidence that post-training cannot compensate for pretraining scale in these domains.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation for knowledge-intensive and agentic benchmarks (Section 2), suggesting "stronger knowledge-intensive pretraining and agentic RL" for future work. But it does not:

- Quantify the relationship between base model scale and post-training effectiveness. Would a 120B-A12B base model with the same pipeline close the gap to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B? This question cannot be answered from the current results.

- Distinguish between limitations that can be addressed by better RL stage design (e.g., more SWE RL environments, better reward functions) vs. limitations that require fundamentally more capable pretrained models. The paper's framing implies both are possible, but the evidence suggests the latter dominates for the largest gaps.

- Acknowledge that test-time scaling — which the paper uses for IMO and IOI evaluation — is subject to the same boundary: it cannot create capability that is absent from the proposal distribution. The 0/7 on IMO Problem 6 after 5 rounds of generate-verify-refine suggests the base model simply cannot produce a correct solution to this problem, even with substantial compute.

For a practitioner, this means the decision to invest in Cascade RL + MOPD vs. scaling pretraining depends on the target problem distribution. If the target distribution includes genuinely hard problems outside the base model's capability range, pretraining scale is likely the better investment. The paper provides no guidance on how to make this determination without running the pipeline.

### 6.6 The SFT Data Is Generated by Frontier Models with Unknown Benchmark Contamination

**The assumption or constraint.** The SFT data described in Section 3.2 is generated almost entirely by external teacher models: DeepSeek-V3.2, DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale, GPT-OSS-120B, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507, and Qwen3-32B. These models are used to generate millions of training examples across math, code, science, chat, instruction following, tool use, SWE, and terminal agent domains.

The paper does not describe any decontamination procedure for this SFT data. It is unknown whether the teacher models were trained on data that overlaps with the evaluation benchmarks used in this paper (AIME 2025/2026, HMMT Feb 2025, IMO-AnswerBench, LiveCodeBench v6, LiveCodeBench Pro, SWE-bench Verified, etc.). If the teacher models memorized solutions to benchmark problems, those solutions could appear in the SFT data, and the student model's performance on those benchmarks would partially reflect memorization rather than reasoning capability.

**The consequence.** The paper's headline results — particularly the strong math and code reasoning performance — may overstate the model's genuine reasoning capability. If the SFT data contains benchmark solutions generated by DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale (which itself achieves strong performance on these benchmarks), the student model may be reproducing teacher-generated reasoning patterns for specific problems rather than developing general problem-solving skills. This is a well-known concern in the LLM post-training literature, and the scale of SFT data (tens of millions of samples from frontier teacher models) makes it particularly acute.

The paper's reported improvements over Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B (e.g., LiveCodeBench v6: 87.2 vs. 68.3) could partially reflect differences in SFT data contamination rather than differences in post-training methodology. If Nano's SFT data used different (potentially less contaminated or differently contaminated) teacher models, the performance gap cannot be cleanly attributed to the Cascade RL + MOPD pipeline.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no evidence addressing this concern. No decontamination analysis is reported. No n-gram overlap analysis, no embedding similarity analysis, no benchmark-specific data filtering is described. The deduplication described for Code Reasoning SFT (Section 3.2.2) removes "self-duplicated coding prompts" using "sample I/O fingerprinting and n-gram-based text analysis," but this is deduplication of the prompts (input side), not decontamination of solutions (output side) with respect to evaluation benchmarks.

The paper does note that code reasoning SFT uses "correctness filtering" — "retaining only those that generate correct code" for prompts with verifiable test cases (Section 3.2.2). This means the SFT data includes correct solutions to competitive programming problems, some of which may closely resemble problems in LiveCodeBench or Codeforces evaluation sets. The filtering ensures the solutions are correct, but does not ensure they are not duplicates or near-duplicates of benchmark problems.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this limitation or attempt to mitigate it. This is a significant omission given the paper's emphasis on open-sourcing training data and enabling reproducibility. Without decontamination analysis, a practitioner reproducing the pipeline with the released SFT data cannot know whether the reproduced model's benchmark performance reflects reasoning capability or memorization of teacher-generated solutions.

The mitigation would be straightforward but computationally expensive: run n-gram overlap or embedding similarity analysis between the SFT data and the evaluation benchmarks, report contamination rates, and either filter contaminated examples or report benchmark performance on decontaminated subsets. The release of SFT data makes this analysis possible for future work, but the paper itself does not perform it. This is the most significant omission in the paper's experimental methodology, as it undermines the interpretability of all benchmark results — particularly the strong math and code reasoning numbers that form the paper's primary contribution claims.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper fundamentally reframes on-policy distillation from a model compression technique into a **capability integration mechanism** within a single training pipeline. Prior to this work, distillation was understood through the lens of teacher-student compression: a large, expensive model teaches a smaller, cheaper one, with information flowing across model capacities. MOPD demonstrates that distillation can instead operate *temporally* — recovering and re-integrating capabilities that the same model possessed at earlier points in its own training trajectory. This is not an incremental improvement to distillation; it is a conceptual reorientation that opens an entirely new use case: distillation as a stabilizer for multi-stage RL pipelines.

The practical consequence is that the field's approach to multi-domain RL post-training shifts from *avoiding* interference through careful ordering to *actively recovering* from it through periodic rebalancing. Cascade RL 1 showed that sequential domain-wise training substantially reduces catastrophic forgetting compared to joint training, but it left capability regression as an acknowledged but unaddressed problem — something to minimize rather than solve. Cascade 2's MOPD stage closes this gap by providing a mechanism that explicitly recovers regressed capabilities, transforming Cascade RL from a pipeline that *tolerates* some forgetting into one that can *correct* for it. This enables the scaling to 6+ specialized RL stages that Cascade 2 demonstrates, and sets a conceptual template for even larger post-training pipelines.

The paper also provides a **diagnostic framework for inter-domain interference** that makes Cascade RL *teachable* rather than merely replicable. The three principles articulated in Section 4.1.1 — mitigating inter-domain interference, scaling via multi-domain integration when domains are non-conflicting, and stabilization through on-policy distillation — give practitioners a vocabulary for reasoning about their own pipelines rather than blindly copying the Cascade 2 ordering. The specific empirical findings — that IF-RL hurts alignment but RLHF does not hurt instruction following, that long-context RL interferes with unrelated benchmarks, that thinking-mode RLHF degrades IF performance — establish that interference patterns are directional and domain-specific. This falsifies the implicit assumption in joint training that all domain combinations are equally compatible, and provides a template for how to discover interference patterns in new settings: run cross-domain evaluations at each stage boundary and look for asymmetric degradation.

The IMO 2025 gold medal result at 30B-A3B — roughly 20× fewer parameters than DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale — challenges the prevailing assumption that frontier mathematical reasoning requires massive pretraining scale. While the result combines the post-trained policy with substantial test-time compute (the DeepSeek-Math-V2 generate-verify-refine pipeline), the fact that a 3B-active-parameter model can produce correct proofs for 5 of 6 IMO problems after systematic post-training suggests that **reasoning capability is more malleable through post-training than the field has assumed**. This does not mean pretraining scale is irrelevant — the paper's own results on knowledge-intensive benchmarks and the 0/7 on IMO Problem 6 confirm sharp boundaries — but it rebalances the portfolio of investments: for reasoning tasks within a base model's rough capability range, post-training methodology may yield higher returns per FLOP than additional pretraining parameters.

The paper also provides the first clear evidence that **MOPD is substantially more training-efficient than GRPO-based RLHF** for recovering specific capabilities — 52 steps to reach 85.5 on ArenaHard Hard Prompt vs. 160 steps for RLHF to reach 80.7 (Table 3). While the comparison confounds signal source and granularity (as discussed in Section 6.4), the raw efficiency numbers are striking enough to shift practitioner attention toward distillation-based rebalancing as a practical tool for multi-stage training, even if the precise mechanism of the efficiency gain requires further study.

The research directions that become more attractive after this work include: (a) methods for predicting inter-domain interference without running full pipeline variants, (b) continuous MOPD variants that interleave distillation throughout training rather than inserting it as a single stage, (c) scaling Cascade RL to 10+ domains with automated stage ordering discovery, and (d) extending the internal-teacher distillation framework to iterative self-improvement loops where earlier checkpoints teach later ones. The directions that become less attractive include: naive joint training on all RL domains (the interference problems Cascade RL was designed to avoid), pure sequential RL without any capability recovery mechanism (since the paper demonstrates MOPD's necessity at scale), and external-teacher distillation for within-pipeline rebalancing (since internal teachers eliminate tokenizer mismatch and distribution shift).

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Direct MOPD ablation within the full Cascade RL pipeline.** The paper's central methodological gap is the absence of a controlled comparison between the full 7-stage pipeline with and without the MOPD stage. The released checkpoints and training data make this experiment newly tractable: a replication study should train two identical pipelines (IF-RL → Multi-domain → [MOPD or skip] → RLHF → Long-context → Code → SWE), evaluating key benchmarks (AIME, LiveCodeBench, ArenaHard, IFBench, SWE-bench) after each stage. This would directly quantify how much regression occurs without MOPD, which capabilities MOPD recovers, and whether the recovery is sustained through the later specialized RL stages. The hypothesis to test is that the performance gap between MOPD and no-MOPD pipelines *grows* with the number of post-MOPD stages — MOPD's rebalancing becomes more valuable as subsequent stages (Code RL, SWE RL) apply further specialization pressure.

**Token-level vs. sequence-level distillation advantage ablation.** The paper attributes MOPD's efficiency to its dense token-level advantage signal, but this claim conflates signal source (teacher knowledge) with signal granularity (token-level vs. sequence-level). A clean ablation would hold the teacher signal constant while varying granularity: train three MOPD variants using the same teacher checkpoints, but with (a) per-token reverse-KL advantages (the full MOPD objective), (b) sequence-averaged advantages (collapse `$a_t^{\text{MOPD}}$` to a single scalar per response), and (c) teacher-generated outputs as SFT targets (sequence-level supervision without any advantage weighting). If variant (b) matches variant (a) in final performance but requires more steps, the efficiency gain is due to granularity; if variant (b) matches variant (c) and both trail variant (a), then both granularity and the distillation advantage are necessary. This experiment would resolve the confound identified in Section 6.4 and provide practitioners with a clear justification (or refutation) for implementing the more complex token-level objective.

**Interference prediction without pipeline re-execution.** The Cascade 2 ordering required running and evaluating multiple pipeline variants to discover that IF-RL hurts alignment, long-context RL interferes with other domains, and thinking-mode RLHF degrades instruction following. For a practitioner adapting Cascade RL to a new base model or domain set, this discovery cost is prohibitive. A follow-up study should investigate whether interference patterns can be predicted from *gradient similarity* or *representation overlap* between domains, computed from a single base model without running any RL. Specifically: sample prompts from each candidate domain, compute the model's gradient with respect to the SFT loss on those prompts, and measure cosine similarity between domain-specific gradients. If domain pairs with high gradient similarity tend to benefit from joint training (the multi-domain RL finding for MCQA + tool calling + structured output), and pairs with low or negative similarity tend to interfere (long-context RL + other domains), then gradient similarity could serve as a cheap proxy for interference prediction. A strong result would show that the Cascade 2 ordering can be *recovered* from gradient similarity measurements alone, without running any pipeline variants.

**Continuous MOPD (interleaved distillation throughout training).** The current MOPD design inserts a single distillation stage at a fixed point in the pipeline (after Multi-domain RL, before RLHF). But the paper's own observation that "RLVR training reduces model entropy and shortens reasoning traces" (Section 4.4) and that RLHF "partially trades off against instruction-following behavior" suggests that capability regression is a continuous process, not a discrete event. A natural extension is *continuous MOPD*: interleave small MOPD updates (1–2 optimization steps with the multi-teacher objective) between every N steps of each RL stage, rather than concentrating all distillation in a single stage. The hypothesis is that continuous MOPD would maintain balanced capabilities throughout training, reducing the magnitude of regressions before they accumulate and potentially enabling more aggressive specialization in the later stages (Code RL, SWE RL) without destabilizing earlier capabilities. The experiment would compare the standard MOPD stage against interleaved MOPD updates at frequencies of 1:10, 1:20, and 1:50 (distillation steps per RL steps), measuring final benchmark performance and training stability.

**Scaling Cascade RL to 10+ domains with automated stage ordering.** Cascade 2 covers 7 RL stages across roughly 8–10 capability domains. As the number of domains grows — for instance, adding multilingual RL, vision-language RL, tool-creation RL, and multi-agent RL — the combinatorial space of possible orderings becomes unmanageable for manual exploration. An automated approach would treat stage ordering as a *meta-optimization problem*: define a search space over permutations of domains (with constraints for known interference patterns), evaluate each candidate ordering by training a lightweight proxy (e.g., LoRA adapters or small-scale RL runs), and use Bayesian optimization or evolutionary search to find orderings that maximize a multi-objective benchmark score. The Cascade 2 framework provides the necessary infrastructure — released training data, standardized evaluation protocols, and documented interference patterns — to build such a search system. The key measurement would be whether automatically discovered orderings match or exceed the manually designed Cascade 2 ordering on the same benchmark suite, and whether the automated approach scales to domain counts where manual search is intractable.

**Decontamination analysis of the released SFT data against evaluation benchmarks.** The paper's most significant unreported methodological gap — the absence of benchmark decontamination for SFT data generated by frontier teacher models — is both important and newly tractable because the SFT data is released. A follow-up study should run n-gram overlap analysis (e.g., 13-gram Jaccard similarity) and embedding-based semantic similarity (e.g., using a sentence transformer to compare SFT samples with benchmark problems and solutions) across all evaluation benchmarks used in the paper (AIME 2025/2026, HMMT Feb 2025, IMO-AnswerBench, LiveCodeBench v6 and Pro, SWE-bench Verified, and others). For benchmarks where contamination is detected, the study should report both original benchmark scores and decontaminated scores (evaluating only on benchmark instances with no near-duplicates in the SFT data). This would bound the contribution of memorization to the paper's headline results and provide the community with a clean baseline for future work building on the released training data. A strong negative result — significant contamination on key benchmarks — would not invalidate the Cascade RL methodology but would substantially recalibrate how the reasoning performance numbers are interpreted.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient post-training of compact models for specialized deployment.** The Cascade RL + MOPD pipeline enables organizations to start from a publicly available compact base model (Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B-Base or similar) and produce a model competitive with much larger alternatives on reasoning tasks. The paper's specific numbers: Nemotron-Cascade-2-30B-A3B achieves 87.2 on LiveCodeBench v6 vs. Qwen3.5-397B-A17B at 83.6, 92.4 on AIME 2025 vs. 91.9 for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, and gold medals at IMO/IOI/ICPC while using ~20× fewer parameters than DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale. For organizations with constrained deployment budgets — edge devices, on-premise inference, or high-throughput serving — this means they can achieve frontier-competitive reasoning from a model that costs a fraction as much to serve, provided they invest in the post-training pipeline. The released SFT data (generated by stronger teacher models) provides the distillation signal that substitutes for pretraining scale, making this approach feasible even for teams without the resources to train their own large-scale SFT teachers.

**Stabilization of multi-stage RL training for general-purpose assistant models.** Any team training a general-purpose LLM that must excel at reasoning, instruction following, alignment, tool use, and agentic tasks faces the same interference problem that Cascade RL addresses. The MOPD technique — using intermediate checkpoint teachers to periodically rebalance capabilities — is directly transferable to other post-training pipelines, even those not structured as strict sequential stages. The key operational insight is that the teacher pool can be assembled from the training run itself: simply checkpoint the model whenever it achieves peak validation performance on any benchmark category, and use those snapshots as distillation targets. The paper provides the recipe: 40–50 optimization steps with learning rate `$2 \times 10^{-6}$`, rollout size 1–4, reverse-KL distillation advantage, truncated importance sampling with `$\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.5, \epsilon_{\text{high}} = 2.0$`, and warmup over the first 30 steps. This can be inserted as a single stabilization stage or interleaved at lower frequency, and it requires no external model families — only disk space to store intermediate checkpoints.

**Domain-specific RL for competitive programming and mathematics education.** The paper's Code RL (Section 4.7) and Math RL (via the SFT + MOPD math teacher) demonstrate that aggressive difficulty filtering — removing prompts the model already solves perfectly and downweighting prompts where all rollouts fail — enables efficient specialization on competition-level problems. The 3.5K-sample Code RL dataset, filtered from the full Nemotron-Cascade coding corpus, produced a model that achieves 439.28/600 at IOI 2025 and solves 10/12 ICPC World Finals problems. This data curation strategy — use a strong teacher (GPT-OSS-120B) to identify the difficulty boundary, train only near that boundary — is directly applicable to any domain with executable verification. Educational technology companies building automated tutoring or problem-generation systems could use this approach to produce models specialized for specific competition formats (AMC, USACO, LeetCode contests) starting from a general-purpose base model, with the RL training focused exclusively on problems at the student's current capability frontier.

**Test-time scaling for olympiad-level problem solving with compact models.** The paper demonstrates that the DeepSeek-Math-V2 generate-verify-refine pipeline, when paired with a well-post-trained 30B model, can achieve IMO gold at a parameter scale previously associated only with models 10–20× larger. The specific compute budget — up to 8 rounds, 128 proof generations per round, 64 verifications per proof, top 32 proofs refined — is documented in Appendix A.1.2. For organizations running competition preparation programs or automated theorem proving systems, this provides a concrete inference recipe: invest in post-training quality (Cascade RL + MOPD), then amplify with test-time compute that costs a fraction of what serving a 600B+ model would cost. The IOI 2025 result is particularly instructive: 439.28/600 (gold) achieved within 2000 model generations, with the full score of 507.66 reachable within 5000 generations, using a multi-round generate-select-submit pipeline that exploits the IOI's official rules for submission feedback. This demonstrates that test-time scaling strategies can be *competition-aware*, using the specific structure of the evaluation (subtask scoring, judge verdicts, cross-subtask insights) to allocate compute efficiently.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions Cascade RL + MOPD as a post-training methodology that enables scaling to many specialized RL domains while preventing capability regression. The decision to adopt this approach over alternatives depends on several factors that the paper's results help quantify:

- **Prefer Cascade RL + MOPD when the number of RL domains is large (4+) and interference patterns are uncertain.** The paper demonstrates that naive joint training causes interference (long-context RL with other domains, thinking-mode RLHF with instruction following), and that domain-specific stage isolation plus MOPD rebalancing resolves these issues. If your post-training pipeline covers 5+ distinct capability categories with different reward structures and verification costs, the sequential domain-wise approach with periodic rebalancing is lower-risk than attempting to tune a joint reward function.

- **Prefer MOPD over standard RLHF for recovering alignment after reasoning-focused RL stages.** Table 3 shows MOPD reaching 85.5 on ArenaHard Hard Prompt in 52 steps vs. RLHF reaching 80.7 in 160 steps. If your pipeline has already invested in reasoning and instruction-following RL before alignment training, MOPD provides faster convergence and better final performance by distilling from an already-aligned teacher checkpoint rather than rediscovering alignment through environment interaction.

- **Prefer external-teacher SFT distillation over scaling pretraining when the target domain has high-quality teacher models available.** The paper's SFT data uses DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale and GPT-OSS-120B to generate 2.6M math samples and 4.2M code samples. If your target domain has strong existing models that can serve as SFT teachers, this distillation-first approach can produce competitive reasoning performance from compact base models without the cost of scaling pretraining. The paper's IMO 2025 gold at 30B-A3B vs. DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale at 671B-A37B is the headline evidence here.

- **Do not prefer Cascade RL + MOPD when the base model lacks fundamental capability in the target domain.** The paper's own results demonstrate clear boundaries: 0/7 on IMO Problem 6, near-zero on LiveCodeBench Pro Hard, -22.3 points on `$\tau$`2-Bench vs. Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, and -19.4 points on Terminal Bench 2.0. These gaps persist despite the full 7-stage RL pipeline and test-time scaling, suggesting they reflect pretraining-scale limitations that post-training cannot overcome. If your target domain includes problems where the base model's pass@1 is near zero, invest in pretraining scale before investing in extended post-training.

- **Do not prefer Cascade RL + MOPD when the inference-to-training ratio `$R$` is very high and per-query cost dominates.** The RL pipeline requires generating thousands of rollouts per training step (16–64 rollouts per prompt, 128–1024 prompts per step), plus teacher inference for MOPD and verification infrastructure (384 CPU cores for Code RL alone). This training cost is amortized over the model's lifetime, making it economical when the model will serve many queries. But for low-volume, high-stakes applications where each query's cost matters more than training efficiency, serving a larger pretrained model with simpler post-training may be preferable to serving a compact model with expensive post-training. The paper provides no lifetime cost analysis, so practitioners must estimate this tradeoff based on their own serving volumes.
