Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman article (on the importance of visibility and marketing your work for career growth, drawn from a Staff Engineer panel):

**The universal Staff+ consensus on visibility**

Ryan was part of a panel with four other Staff+ Engineering Creators discussing growth to Staff Engineer (L6). Their paths to Staff were all different, producing a wide variety of answers — except on one question. Every single panelist agreed that relationships and visibility of their work were critical parts of their career growth. The unanimity is what makes it striking: "It's no coincidence that every person on the Staff Engineer Q&A panel emphasized the importance of being visible."

**The non-ideal reality of promotions**

Promotions are decided by people. To get promoted, your work needs to be both impactful and known by the right people. Ryan paints the counterfactual clearly: "Imagine how smooth a promotion would be if everyone on the committee already thought your work was at the next level." In a perfect world, impactful work alone would suffice. But in reality, work can't be recognized unless people know about it. Communicating results is how you get the recognition you deserve — without it, impact risks staying invisible to the decision-makers who matter.

**A lightweight write-up format for every launch**

When you have results to share, Ryan advocates creating a short write-up that explains the problem you solved and the impact it had. The goal is brevity with the main takeaways up front. His go-to format has five sections: a **Tl;dr** (a one-liner explaining the launch and calling out the most impactful result), **Context** (a few sentences on the problem and solution), **Results** (bullet points of detailed benefits — top metric movements, user benefits, cost savings, etc.), **Next Steps** (brief rollout timeline and follow-up work), and **Tags** — where he recommends mentioning all relevant managers and tech leads, with the principle that "it is better to overcommunicate."

**Where to share it, and the dual benefit**

Once the write-up exists, send it somewhere public — a public Slack channel, an email with all relevant folks, a public Workplace group — so it's easy for everyone to see and look back on. This doesn't just help you get recognition; it also helps your team establish a change log. Ryan also suggests saving the tl;dr, context, and results into a personal **brag journal**. Your manager will thank you for documenting your work when performance reviews come around, and you'll have a ready-made trail of evidence.

**Preempting the self-promotion discomfort**

Some people worry this kind of sharing might look like unnecessary self-promotion. Ryan's framing pushes back: "it's not about overselling — it's about showcasing your genuine achievements that also helps your team." The write-up serves the team (a public record, a change log) at the same time as it serves you. The final advice is simple and direct: "Next time you launch something, don't do it quietly; share it." Across a panel of five Staff+ engineers with completely different journeys, that step — taking a little extra time to communicate results — was the one thing they all agreed on. It doesn't take much time and can make a big difference in recognition.

---

The panel's perfect consensus on visibility gives Ryan's **brag journal** and lightweight write-up format their force: they are the concrete practice behind the principle. The format itself enacts the lesson — Tl;dr first, impact quantified, decision-makers tagged — by making your work legible to exactly the people who decide promotions. That the write-up doubles as a team change log and performance-review evidence turns the uncomfortable act of self-promotion into a quietly systematic habit of **overcommunication**, one that serves everyone at once.
