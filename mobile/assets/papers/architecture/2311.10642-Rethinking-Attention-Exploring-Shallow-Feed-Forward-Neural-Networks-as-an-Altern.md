# Rethinking Attention: Exploring Shallow Feed-Forward Neural Networks as an Alternative to Attention Layers in Transformers

**ArXiv:** [2311.10642](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10642)

## 🎯 Pitch

Shallow feed-forward networks, trained via distillation, can replace every self-attention layer in a Transformer encoder and decoder without hurting translation quality—but the moment you swap out cross-attention, performance collapses. This stark asymmetry reveals that self-attention is far more replaceable than commonly assumed, while cross-attention’s mixing of distinct sequences poses a fundamentally harder mimicry challenge.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This work analyzes whether standard shallow feed-forward networks can mimic the behavior of the attention mechanism in the original Transformer model, using knowledge distillation from a trained vanilla Transformer teacher on the IWSLT2017 machine translation dataset. The paper systematically substitutes key attention components with **Attention Layer Replacement (ALR)** — the most effective of four proposed substitution methods — along with three other replacement strategies of increasing abstraction (ALRR replacing the attention block plus residual connection, ASLR replacing each attention head with a separate feed-forward network, and ELR replacing the entire encoder layer), and evaluates these across five network sizes on four language pairs. The encoder self-attention replacements achieve BLEU scores competitive with the baseline Transformer, while the full "attentionless Transformer" — where ALR replaces encoder self-attention, decoder self-attention, and decoder cross-attention — reveals a sharp performance boundary: decoder cross-attention replacement causes a substantial BLEU collapse (e.g., dropping from 0.257 to 0.115 on English–German with the largest "L" replacement network), establishing that shallow feed-forward networks can effectively emulate self-attention mechanisms but struggle to capture the more complex cross-attention interactions between differing sequences.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Do Transformers Necessarily Need Attention?

The Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017) has become the dominant paradigm for sequence-to-sequence tasks, with its multi-head self-attention mechanism celebrated as the key innovation enabling the model to capture long-range dependencies without the sequential bottlenecks of recurrent architectures. But beneath this success lies an underexplored question: **is attention itself the indispensable ingredient, or could simpler, more generic neural network components achieve comparable results if trained appropriately?**

This paper tackles that question head-on. Rather than proposing a new attention variant or improving translation performance, the authors ask whether the attention mechanism can be replaced by something far more basic — standard shallow feed-forward networks with a single hidden layer — without catastrophic performance degradation. This is not an engineering question about making Transformers better; it is a *conceptual* question about whether attention is fundamentally necessary or merely a convenient architectural choice that happens to work well with current optimization methods.

The framing is deliberately provocative. If shallow feed-forward networks — which lack any built-in notion of pairwise token interaction, dynamic weighting, or sequence-length flexibility — can replicate what attention does, then what exactly is attention contributing beyond what a sufficiently trained generic function approximator can provide? And if optimization rather than architecture is the bottleneck, what does that imply about the design principles guiding modern sequence models?

### Why This Question Matters

The significance of this investigation operates on two levels: **theoretical understanding** and **practical consequence**.

**On the theoretical side**, there is a long-standing tension in deep learning between specialized architectures and generic function approximators. The universal approximation theorem guarantees that feed-forward networks can approximate any continuous function given enough capacity, but this theoretical guarantee says nothing about whether such approximations are learnable with gradient-based optimization, practically sized networks, or finite data. Demonstrating that shallow feed-forward networks *can* learn attention-like behavior through knowledge distillation would provide empirical evidence that attention is not architecturally privileged — it is one of many parameterizations capable of solving the same underlying mapping problem. This aligns with a broader research thread questioning architectural necessity: Ba and Caruana (2014) showed that shallow networks could mimic deep networks on classification tasks, and Urban et al. (2017) extended this insight to convolutional networks, asking "Do Deep Convolutional Nets Really Need to be Deep and Convolutional?" This paper extends that lineage to the Transformer's defining mechanism — attention itself.

**On the practical side**, the question touches on model deployment and computational efficiency. Attention mechanisms, particularly multi-head self-attention, impose specific computational patterns: quadratic memory complexity with respect to sequence length (in the original formulation), specialized hardware kernels for efficient matrix multiplications, and dynamic computation graphs that depend on input length. A feed-forward replacement with fixed input-output dimensions — while potentially larger in parameter count — offers a substantially simpler computational footprint: a series of matrix multiplications with fixed weight matrices, no dynamic attention score computation, and no pairwise token interactions. If such a replacement works, it opens the door to hardware-friendly implementations that sidestep the engineering complexity of optimizing attention.

Additionally, understanding which components of the Transformer are truly essential has direct implications for architecture design. If self-attention can be replaced by feed-forward networks but cross-attention cannot (which the paper finds), this provides a functional decomposition of what makes attention valuable: it is not pairwise interaction *per se* that matters, but something specific about how cross-attention integrates information across heterogeneous sequences.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper situates itself within two distinct research threads, both of which contain gaps that motivate this work.

**Thread 1: Knowledge distillation for model compression.** Hinton, Vinyals, and Dean (2015) established knowledge distillation as a technique where a smaller "student" model is trained to match the output distribution of a larger "teacher" model. The typical goal is compression: producing a smaller model that retains most of the teacher's performance. Ba and Caruana (2014) applied this idea to depth reduction, showing that shallow feed-forward networks could match the accuracy of deep convolutional networks on CIFAR-10 by training on the deep network's logits. Urban et al. (2017) extended this to ImageNet-scale classification, demonstrating that a single hidden-layer network could achieve competitive accuracy when distilled from a deep convolutional teacher.

These prior works focused on **output-level distillation** — the student learns to match the teacher's final predictions. The gap this paper identifies is twofold. First, these works operated on classification tasks with fixed-dimensional outputs, not sequence-to-sequence generation where the output space is combinatorial and autoregressive. Second — and more critically — they did not attempt to replace *specific architectural mechanisms* with generic alternatives. They replaced entire models with shallower ones, not individual components within a larger architecture. This paper asks a finer-grained question: can we surgically replace the attention module while keeping the rest of the Transformer (layer normalization, residual connections, feed-forward blocks, the encoder-decoder structure) intact?

**Thread 2: Understanding and simplifying attention.** Since the Transformer's introduction, substantial research has attempted to understand what attention contributes and whether simpler alternatives exist. Some work has proposed replacing the quadratic self-attention with linearized approximations (e.g., Katharopoulos et al., 2020), sparse attention patterns (e.g., Child et al., 2019), or Fourier-style mixing (e.g., Lee-Thorp et al., 2022). Other work has explored whether attention weights are interpretable or necessary (e.g., Jain and Wallace, 2019; Wiegreffe and Pinter, 2019).

However, these approaches operate within the attention *paradigm* — they modify the attention computation to be more efficient or interpretable, but they preserve the core mechanism of dynamically weighting token interactions based on content. None of these prior works ask whether the entire attention computation can be replaced by a fixed-weight mapping that has no explicit notion of pairwise token interaction at all. This is the gap the paper targets: **not simplifying attention, but eliminating it entirely in favor of a generic feed-forward mapping**.

The closest conceptual prior work is likely the MLP-Mixer architecture (Tolstikhin et al., 2021) in computer vision, which showed that pure multi-layer perceptrons — without convolution or self-attention — could achieve competitive ImageNet performance by alternating token-mixing and channel-mixing MLP blocks. However, MLP-Mixer is a *de novo* architecture trained from scratch, not an investigation of whether attention layers in an existing trained Transformer can be retroactively replaced.

**Thread 3: The gap in optimization understanding.** Implicit in this paper's framing is a deeper question about the relationship between architecture and optimization. The Transformer's attention mechanism may succeed not because it is the "right" inductive bias for sequence modeling, but because it provides a loss landscape that gradient-based optimization can navigate effectively. If this is the case, then feed-forward replacements trained from scratch would fail (as the paper's results confirm — the replacements only work when trained via distillation), but replacements trained to mimic an already-optimized attention mechanism could succeed because they inherit the teacher's functional behavior without needing to discover it independently. This connects to broader questions about whether specialized architectures are necessary because of representational capacity or because of optimization dynamics — a distinction the paper explicitly raises in its conclusion:

> "These conclusions also point out the deficiencies of the current optimization methods, which are not able to train these 'attentionless Transformers' from scratch but need more advanced techniques, such as knowledge distillation to converge into desired parameter configurations."

### The Unexplored Distinction: Self-Attention vs. Cross-Attention

The paper's experimental design reveals a distinction that prior work had not systematically explored: **self-attention and cross-attention may be qualitatively different in their replaceability**. In the Transformer decoder, self-attention operates on the target sequence alone (with causal masking), processing tokens that come from the same distribution and represent the same language. Cross-attention, by contrast, integrates information from two different sequences (source and target) that live in different representation spaces — one from the encoder's output representing the source language, the other from the decoder's self-attention representing the target language so far. The paper's motivation includes the hypothesis that this integration across heterogeneous representations may be fundamentally harder for a generic feed-forward network to capture than the homogeneous token mixing within a single sequence.

This hypothesis — which the results confirm — provides a more nuanced answer to "do Transformers need attention?" than a simple yes or no. The answer is: **self-attention is not architecturally necessary** (feed-forward networks can learn to replicate its behavior), but **cross-attention may be** (shallow feed-forward networks fail to capture its functionality at current scales). This decomposition represents a genuine conceptual advance over prior work that treated attention as a monolithic mechanism.

### How the Paper Positions Itself

The paper is careful not to overclaim. It explicitly states that it "does not introduce a competitive advantage over established methods" and frames the contribution as "a conceptual analysis of existing techniques and potential alternatives." This humility is important: the goal is not to beat the Transformer on BLEU score or parameter efficiency, but to probe the *necessity* of attention through systematic replacement experiments.

The positioning relative to the knowledge distillation literature is as an extension from output-level distillation to **intermediate-activation-level distillation within a functioning architecture**. The positioning relative to the attention literature is orthogonal: rather than improving attention, the paper tests whether it can be removed entirely. And the positioning relative to the optimization literature is as empirical evidence that **architecture and optimization are coupled**: specialized architectures like attention may succeed partly because they provide favorable optimization landscapes, not because they are the only parameterizations capable of representing the desired function.

The choice of the IWSLT2017 dataset and the relatively small Transformer (embedding dimension reduced from 512 to 128, six encoder and six decoder layers) reflects a practical constraint — the method requires training separate replacement networks for each attention layer in each encoder/decoder block, which scales with model size. But this choice also serves a conceptual purpose: if feed-forward replacements can work on a representative machine translation task, the finding is more likely to generalize than if tested only on synthetic or toy problems.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper builds and evaluates a set of "attentionless Transformers" — modified Transformer architectures where specific attention mechanisms are surgically extracted and replaced with standard shallow feed-forward neural networks trained via knowledge distillation to mimic the original attention behavior. The system solves a diagnostic problem rather than an engineering one: **given a fully trained Transformer that performs well on machine translation, which of its attention components are genuinely necessary, and which can be replaced by a generic function approximator without catastrophic performance loss?** The solution takes the form of a systematic ablation study where the authors define four replacement strategies of increasing abstraction, train replacement networks of five different sizes on intermediate activations extracted from the teacher model, then plug these replacements back into the Transformer and measure the resulting BLEU scores across four language pairs.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major stages:

1. **Teacher Transformer Training**: A vanilla Transformer (embedding dimension 128, six encoder layers, six decoder layers) is trained from scratch on the IWSLT2017 machine translation dataset, establishing a baseline BLEU score for each of four language pairs.

2. **Intermediate Activation Extraction**: The trained teacher is run on the training data, and for each attention layer targeted for replacement, the input tensors to that attention block and the output tensors produced by that attention block are saved as supervised training pairs for the replacement network.

3. **Feed-Forward Network Training**: For each attention layer that is to be replaced, a shallow one-hidden-layer feed-forward network is trained independently to minimize the error between its output and the teacher attention layer's output, given the same input. This training uses the extracted input-output pairs as a supervised dataset.

4. **Evaluation via Surgical Transplantation**: Each trained feed-forward network is inserted into the Transformer in place of its corresponding attention layer. The modified Transformer is then run on the test set, and BLEU scores are computed. No further fine-tuning is performed on the modified Transformer — the replacement network is evaluated exactly as trained.

Information flows through these stages sequentially: teacher training produces a functional Transformer → the functional Transformer generates training data for replacement networks → replacement networks are trained independently → replacement networks are plugged in → the hybrid model is evaluated end-to-end on translation quality. There is no feedback loop or iterative refinement.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the teacher Transformer specification and the rationale for the reduced embedding dimension — because all replacement network dimensions depend on the teacher's architecture.
- **Second**, the four replacement strategies (ALR, ALRR, ASLR, ELR), what each one replaces, what it preserves, and the conceptual motivation for testing each level of abstraction — because these define the experimental design space.
- **Third**, the data extraction and preprocessing pipeline, including the critical fixed-length padding and masking scheme — because this is the interface between the teacher's variable-length attention mechanism and the replacement network's fixed-size input.
- **Fourth**, the replacement network architecture, its parameter scaling across five sizes, and the training procedure — because the size-performance tradeoff is central to the paper's claims about viability.
- **Fifth**, the decoder-specific adaptations for self-attention (causal masking) and cross-attention (concatenated encoder-decoder inputs) — because these are where the paper discovers the sharp performance boundary between replaceable self-attention and irreplaceable cross-attention.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **diagnostic ablation paper** whose core idea is that attention mechanisms in Transformers can be systematically replaced with shallow feed-forward networks trained via knowledge distillation from intermediate activations, and that the success or failure of such replacement reveals which attention components are architecturally necessary versus merely convenient.

---

#### The Teacher Transformer: Architecture and Training

The starting point for all experiments is a vanilla Transformer model following the architecture of Vaswani et al. (2017), consisting of six encoder layers and six decoder layers. Each encoder layer contains one multi-head self-attention block followed by a position-wise feed-forward network, with residual connections and layer normalization applied around each sub-layer. Each decoder layer contains a masked multi-head self-attention block, a multi-head cross-attention block that attends to the encoder output, and a position-wise feed-forward network, again with residual connections and layer normalization.

A critical design decision is the **reduction of the embedding dimension from the standard 512 to 128**. The authors state this explicitly:

> "To reduce training times and make testing faster, we reduced the embedding size from 512 to 128. These changes did not drop the overall score too much below the original BLEU score but resulted in significantly lower computational power demands."

This reduction has cascading consequences for the entire experimental pipeline. Since the replacement feed-forward networks take the concatenated word representations of an entire sentence as input, the input dimension to these networks is `embedding_dim × max_sequence_length`. With an embedding dimension of 128 and a maximum sequence length of 50, each replacement network receives an input vector of dimension `128 × 50 = 6400`. If the standard embedding dimension of 512 had been used, this input dimension would balloon to `512 × 50 = 25,600`, making the replacement networks substantially larger and more expensive to train. The reduced embedding dimension thus enables the replacement study to be feasible while still operating on a realistic translation task.

The teacher Transformer is trained on the IWSLT2017 dataset across four language pairs: English–German (E2G), German–English (G2E), English–French (E2F), and French–English (F2E). The baseline BLEU scores achieved by this teacher model on the test sets are:

| Language Pair | BLEU Score |
|---|---|
| English–German (E2G) | 0.257 |
| German–English (G2E) | 0.324 |
| English–French (E2F) | 0.276 |
| French–English (F2E) | 0.292 |

These scores serve as the reference point against which all replacement experiments are compared. When the paper reports "relative BLEU scores [%]" in Figures 2 and 3, these percentages are computed as the ratio of the replacement model's BLEU score to the teacher's BLEU score on the same language pair, expressed as a percentage, then averaged across the four datasets.

#### The Four Replacement Strategies: What Gets Replaced and Why

The paper defines four distinct replacement strategies, each operating at a different level of abstraction within the encoder self-attention block. These strategies are illustrated in Figure 1 of the paper and form the core experimental design space for the encoder ablation study. Each strategy replaces a different subset of the attention sub-layer's components, progressively removing more of the original Transformer's architectural scaffolding.

##### Attention Layer Replacement (ALR)

ALR replaces **only the multi-head attention block itself**, preserving the surrounding residual connection and layer normalization exactly as they exist in the original Transformer. The data flow through an ALR-modified encoder sub-layer is: the input to the sub-layer first passes through layer normalization, then through the feed-forward replacement network (which takes the place of multi-head attention), then the replacement network's output is added to the original sub-layer input via the preserved residual connection.

The motivation for ALR is to isolate the contribution of the attention computation itself. By keeping the residual connection and layer normalization intact, ALR tests whether attention's function — transforming a sequence of token representations into an updated sequence — can be performed by a generic feed-forward mapping, while still benefiting from the optimization-friendly properties of residual connections and normalization. Any performance difference between ALR and the teacher can be attributed specifically to the replacement of the attention computation, not to the removal of residual connectivity.

ALR was ultimately found to be the most effective replacement strategy overall, as it "was found to be the most effective approach in the case of encoder attention replacement, featuring both high performance and a small number of parameters." This motivated its later use for the full Transformer replacement including decoder self-attention and cross-attention.

##### Attention Layer with Residual Connection Replacement (ALRR)

ALRR replaces the **multi-head attention block and its residual connection together**, leaving only the layer normalization before the replacement network. The data flow is: the input passes through layer normalization, then through the feed-forward replacement network, and the output of the replacement network is used directly — there is no additive skip connection from the original input.

The motivation for ALRR is to decouple the effects of the attention mechanism from the effects of the residual connection. The residual connection is widely understood to be critical for training deep Transformers by providing gradient highways that mitigate vanishing gradients. By removing it alongside attention, ALRR tests whether the residual connection is necessary specifically because attention needs it, or whether it is a general-purpose architectural requirement. If ALRR performs substantially worse than ALR at the same replacement network size, this would indicate that the residual connection is important independently of the attention computation — perhaps because the feed-forward replacement, like the original attention, benefits from the identity-mapping shortcut that makes optimization easier.

The results confirm exactly this hypothesis: ALRR with small replacement networks (sizes XS and S) produces near-zero BLEU scores (e.g., 0.013 on E2G for XS, 0.018 for S), only recovering to competitive performance at the M and L sizes. This pattern suggests that larger feed-forward networks can partially compensate for the missing residual connection through increased capacity, but smaller networks cannot.

##### Attention Separate Heads Layer Replacement (ASLR)

ASLR takes the ALR idea and applies it at a finer granularity: instead of using **one feed-forward network to replace the entire multi-head attention block**, it uses **one separate feed-forward network per attention head**. If the original Transformer has $h$ attention heads, ASLR trains $h$ independent feed-forward networks for each attention layer, each receiving the same input but specializing to mimic the behavior of its corresponding head.

The motivation is to more closely mirror the original multi-head attention structure. In the original Transformer, multiple attention heads operate in parallel, each attending to different aspects of the input, and their outputs are concatenated and linearly projected. The hypothesis behind ASLR is that this per-head specialization captures something important about the attention function — perhaps that different heads learn different input-output mappings that are easier to represent with separate networks than with a single monolithic network. By training one network per head, ASLR gives the replacement mechanism the same inductive bias toward parallel, independent transformations that multi-head attention provides.

The parameter counts for ASLR are reported on a per-attention-head basis (Table 1): for example, the "S" size ASLR network has 290K parameters per head. If the teacher uses 8 attention heads, the total parameter count for an ASLR replacement of one attention layer would be `8 × 290K = 2.32M` for size S. ASLR achieves the most consistent performance across all sizes, with even the smallest XS variant reaching BLEU scores close to the baseline (e.g., 0.245 on E2G versus the baseline 0.257) — the only replacement method to do so at the smallest size.

##### Encoder Layer Replacement (ELR)

ELR is the most aggressive replacement strategy: it replaces the **entire encoder layer** — including the multi-head attention sub-layer with its residual connection and layer normalization, the feed-forward sub-layer with its residual connection and layer normalization, and all intermediate operations — with a single feed-forward network. The ELR replacement network receives the encoder layer's input and must directly produce the encoder layer's output, bypassing all interior Transformer structure.

The motivation is to test the highest possible level of abstraction: can a single shallow feed-forward network replace everything an encoder layer does, including both the attention-based token mixing and the position-wise feed-forward transformation, all learned holistically through distillation? If ELR succeeds, it would suggest that the entire encoder layer structure is an optimization convenience rather than a representational necessity.

ELR performs the worst of all four strategies, which the paper attributes to "the simplicity of the replacement model, which discards all of the encoder structures that aid training." Even at the largest size (L), ELR achieves BLEU scores noticeably below the baseline (e.g., 0.194 on E2G versus 0.257 baseline). This suggests that the structured decomposition of the encoder layer into attention and feed-forward sub-layers connected by residual pathways is genuinely important — not just for optimization during training, but for the representational capacity available to a shallow network even when trained via distillation.

#### Data Extraction and Preprocessing: The Interface Between Variable-Length Attention and Fixed-Size Feed-Forward Networks

The fundamental technical challenge in replacing attention with feed-forward networks is that attention operates on variable-length sequences with content-dependent computation graphs, while feed-forward networks require fixed-size input and output vectors. The paper's data pipeline bridges this gap through a specific extraction, padding, and masking protocol.

**Extraction of training data.** For each attention layer targeted for replacement, the trained teacher Transformer is run in inference mode on the training dataset. At each attention layer, the input tensor entering that attention block and the output tensor produced by that attention block are saved. The pair `(input_to_attention, output_from_attention)` becomes one supervised training example for the replacement network. This process is repeated for every sentence in the training set, producing a dataset of `(input, target)` pairs specific to each attention layer in each encoder/decoder block.

The paper describes this process explicitly:

> "As the initial step, intermediate activations (input-output pairs) are extracted from the trained Transformer and used as training data for the desired Feed-Forward replacement network."

This means that for a full encoder self-attention replacement across all six encoder layers, six independent datasets are created, and six independent feed-forward networks are trained — one per layer. The networks do not share weights or training data across layers, because each attention layer in a deep Transformer learns to perform different transformations at different levels of abstraction.

**The fixed-length representation.** Every attention layer transforms a sequence of word representations into an updated sequence of word representations. Concretely, if the input sequence has length $L$ and the embedding dimension is $d$, the attention layer receives a matrix of shape $(L, d)$ and produces a matrix of shape $(L, d)$. To feed this into a fixed-size feed-forward network, the paper flattens the entire sequence into a single vector: the input to the replacement network is the concatenation of all $L$ token representations, yielding a vector of dimension $L \times d$. The replacement network must then produce an output vector of the same dimension $L \times d$, which is reshaped back into a matrix of shape $(L, d)$ — the attention layer's expected output format.

The paper states this transformation clearly:

> "To mimic this behavior, the FF network takes in the concatenated word representations of a sentence as input and produces updated word representations as output in a single pass."

**Sequence length handling: padding and masking to a fixed maximum length of 50.** Because feed-forward networks require a fixed input dimension, all sequences must be standardized to a consistent length. The paper adopts a maximum sequence length of 50 tokens:

> "We also adopted a fixed upper bound to sentence length, which we set to 50. This limit consequently limited the size of our replacement FF networks. Since 96% of the samples in the datasets are of length 50 or less, the datasets were not significantly shrunk."

For each sentence, the word representations are padded to length 50 by appending zero vectors for positions beyond the actual sentence length. The zero values are explicitly chosen to prevent the padded positions from influencing the network's computation:

> "In order to handle the input sentences of varying lengths, we have decided to pad all sentences to a maximum fixed length and mask the padded values with zeros to prevent them from influencing the model's inference."

This padding and masking scheme has an important property: because the padded positions are all zeros, a matrix multiplication involving those positions produces zero contributions regardless of the weight values. This means the feed-forward network's computation for valid token positions is unaffected by the presence of padding, assuming no bias terms at the input layer (or that bias terms, if present, are small). The masking is **implicit** — achieved through zero-valued inputs — rather than explicit through a separate mask tensor.

With an embedding dimension of 128 and a maximum length of 50, the replacement network's input and output dimension is `128 × 50 = 6400`. Sentences shorter than 50 tokens are zero-padded to reach this dimension; sentences longer than 50 tokens are truncated (the paper notes this affects approximately 4% of samples). The output vector of length 6400 is reshaped to a `(50, 128)` matrix after the feed-forward network, and the positions corresponding to actual tokens (not padding) are extracted for downstream processing.

This fixed-length design has a significant practical consequence that the paper acknowledges: the replacement network is inherently tied to a specific maximum sequence length. The Transformer's original attention mechanism can handle arbitrary sequence lengths at inference time (up to memory constraints), scaling computation quadratically with length. The feed-forward replacement is **incapable of processing sequences longer than 50 tokens** because its input dimension is baked into its weight matrix at training time:

> "Another downside of our replacement of the attention with a fixed-size feed-forward network is the imminent lack of flexibility of the model in terms of the length of sequences the model can operate with."

#### The Replacement Network Architecture and Training

The replacement networks are deliberately simple: a standard shallow feed-forward network with a single hidden layer. The paper does not specify the activation function, but the standard choice for such distillation setups is ReLU or a variant. The network architecture is:

$$\text{FF}(x) = W_2 \cdot \sigma(W_1 \cdot x + b_1) + b_2$$

where $x \in \mathbb{R}^{6400}$ is the concatenated, padded word representations, $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times 6400}$ and $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{6400 \times h}$ are weight matrices with $h$ being the hidden dimension, $b_1$ and $b_2$ are bias vectors, and $\sigma$ is a nonlinear activation function.

**What it computes:** the input vector is multiplied by $W_1$, a bias is added, the result passes through a nonlinearity, the output is multiplied by $W_2$, and a final bias is added. The result is a vector of the same dimension as the input (6400), which is reshaped into a $50 \times 128$ matrix representing the updated token representations.

**Why this form:** a single-hidden-layer network is the minimal architecture that can represent nonlinear input-output mappings (two-layer networks are universal function approximators given sufficient hidden dimension). The choice is deliberately restrictive — the paper wants to know whether even this minimal architecture can learn attention-like behavior, not whether a more sophisticated architecture (deep MLP, convolutional, recurrent) could do better. The direct input-output mapping with no recurrence, no convolution, and no dynamic computation is the strongest possible test of "does attention add anything beyond a generic nonlinear transformation of the concatenated input tokens?"

**Network size scaling.** The paper defines five size variants — XS, S, M, L — with the parameter counts given in Table 1. The parameter counts vary depending on the replacement strategy because different strategies have different input-output dimensions:

| Size | ALR | ALRR / ELR | ASLR (per head) |
|---|---|---|---|
| XS | 320K | — | 290K |
| S | 640K | — | 1.5M |
| M | 10M | — | 11.5M |
| L | 41M | — | 46M |

The paper notes that these parameter counts mostly exceed the original attention layer's parameter count:

> "The original number of parameters for the attention layer (60,000 parameters in our case) is mostly exceeded by the replacement networks, mainly due to fixed-size inputs and outputs, and the processing format demanded by the FF network."

This is a crucial point: the feed-forward replacements are not parameter-efficient. The original attention layer achieves its behavior with approximately 60,000 parameters by using dynamic pairwise token comparisons — the weight matrices $(W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O)$ are applied to individual token representations, and the sequence-level interaction is handled by the dot-product attention scores, which are computation without parameters. The feed-forward replacement must encode all token-interaction patterns in its weight matrices directly, with the input including every token position explicitly. This requires far more parameters because the network must learn, for every possible position pair $(i, j)$, the equivalent of an attention weight — and it must do so in a single matrix multiplication rather than through the decomposed query-key-value computation.

**Training procedure.** Each replacement network is trained independently using the extracted input-output pairs as a supervised regression dataset. The training configuration is:

- **Optimizer:** Adam
- **Learning rate:** 0.001
- **Batch size:** 1400
- **Epochs:** 20
- **Training settings kept the same for all networks**

The loss function is not explicitly specified in the paper, but for a regression task where the goal is to match the teacher's output activations, the standard choice is mean squared error (MSE) between the replacement network's output and the teacher attention layer's output. The paper states simply that the networks are "trained for 20 epochs," implying a straightforward supervised learning loop with no early stopping, learning rate scheduling, or regularization mentioned.

The batch size of 1400 is notable — it is relatively large, suggesting that the training datasets (consisting of approximately 200,000 sentence pairs per language direction after length filtering) can be processed efficiently in large batches. The large batch size may also help stabilize training by providing more accurate gradient estimates.

**Why distillation instead of end-to-end training?** The paper explicitly addresses why the replacement networks are trained via distillation rather than training the entire "attentionless Transformer" end-to-end:

> "These conclusions also point out the deficiencies of the current optimization methods, which are not able to train these 'attentionless Transformers' from scratch but need more advanced techniques, such as knowledge distillation to converge into desired parameter configurations."

The replacement networks are trained on a local, supervised objective: match the teacher's activations given the teacher's inputs. The paper does not attempt to train the full Transformer with feed-forward replacements end-to-end on the translation objective. If it had, the optimization problem would be substantially harder: the feed-forward networks would need to discover from scratch — guided only by the BLEU score gradient — the complex token-interaction patterns that attention learns naturally through its built-in pairwise comparison mechanism. The distillation approach circumvents this by providing the replacement networks with a strong behavioral target: "produce exactly these output activations for these input activations," which is a well-conditioned regression problem.

#### Decoder-Specific Adaptations

The decoder presents two distinct challenges for replacement because it contains two different types of attention — masked self-attention and cross-attention — each requiring modifications to the basic ALR approach.

##### Decoder Self-Attention: Handling Causal Masking

Decoder self-attention differs from encoder self-attention in one critical aspect: it is **causally masked**, meaning that each token position can only attend to itself and previous positions, not to future positions. This masking is essential for autoregressive generation — during inference, the model generates tokens one at a time, and future tokens do not exist yet.

The paper's adaptation for decoder self-attention replacement handles this masking by processing tokens individually rather than in parallel:

> "The main distinction between the replacement method used for the self-attention in the encoder and the one used for the self-attention in the decoder is that the FF replacement networks in the decoder output the word embeddings one at a time, following the original masked self-attention. The network processes each word individually by feeding the entire sentence representation through the network and masking the representation of words that come after the word is processed."

In operational terms, for a sequence of length $L$, the replacement network is run $L$ times — once per token position. On the $i$-th run, the input is the full padded representation of the sentence (all $L$ token embeddings concatenated), but **token positions $j > i$ (future tokens) are masked to zero**. The network produces an output vector of dimension $L \times d$, and only the $i$-th position's output (the updated representation for token $i$) is retained. This procedure ensures that the representation of token $i$ is computed using only tokens $1$ through $i$, preserving the causal property of the original masked self-attention.

This sequential processing is expensive — it requires $L$ forward passes through the feed-forward network per decoder self-attention layer per decoding step — but it faithfully replicates the causal constraint. An alternative approach would be to design a network architecture that inherently respects causality (e.g., a triangular weight matrix that zeros out future-position interactions), but the paper opts for the simpler masking approach that directly mirrors the teacher's behavior.

##### Decoder Cross-Attention: Handling Heterogeneous Inputs

Decoder cross-attention is fundamentally different from self-attention because it involves **two distinct sequences**: the encoder's output representations (source language) and the decoder's self-attention output representations (target language generated so far). The original cross-attention mechanism uses the decoder's representations as queries and the encoder's representations as keys and values, allowing each target-language position to dynamically weight source-language information based on content similarity.

The paper's adaptation concatenates both sequences into a single input vector:

> "Cross-attention in the decoder accepts both word representations from the encoder and decoder layers, integrating them together. To replicate this process, word representations from both encoder and decoder were concatenated together and padded, having the input size doubled in comparison to the self-attention replacement networks."

If the self-attention replacement network takes an input of dimension `50 × 128 = 6400` (50 token positions, 128-dimensional embeddings), the cross-attention replacement network takes an input of dimension `50 × 128 × 2 = 12,800` — the concatenation of 50 encoder token representations and 50 decoder token representations. The output dimension remains `50 × 128 = 6400`, producing updated representations for the decoder positions only, matching the original cross-attention's behavior where the output is a sequence of target-language representations.

This doubling of the input dimension has significant consequences. The cross-attention replacement network must learn to integrate information from two distinct representation spaces — the encoder's source-language representations and the decoder's target-language representations — without any built-in mechanism for computing pairwise similarities between them. In the original cross-attention, the query-key dot product provides a natural similarity measure: decoder position $i$ attends to encoder position $j$ based on how similar their representations are. The feed-forward replacement has no such mechanism; it must encode all encoder-decoder interactions in its weight matrices, and these interactions must be learned purely from the statistical patterns in the teacher's output activations.

This increased complexity is the paper's explanation for why cross-attention replacement performs substantially worse than self-attention replacement:

> "The potential reason for this behaviour could be the lack of the expressiveness of the feed-forward network needed to describe the more complex mapping and interaction between sequences used in the cross-attention block."

The paper does not experiment with larger replacement networks for cross-attention beyond the "L" size (41M parameters), leaving open the question of whether even larger networks could eventually capture cross-attention behavior.

---

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Reduced embedding dimension (128 vs. 512):** Makes the replacement networks' input dimension tractable (6400 vs. 25,600) while maintaining "not too much below the original BLEU score." This is a practical tradeoff: the study prioritizes feasibility of the replacement experiments over maximizing baseline performance.

- **Single-hidden-layer architecture:** Provides the minimal test of whether a generic function approximator can learn attention-like behavior. A deeper network might perform better, but the paper's thesis is strongest if even the simplest possible architecture can succeed.

- **Knowledge distillation from intermediate activations rather than output logits:** The replacement networks learn to mimic the attention mechanism's specific transformation, not just to produce outputs that lead to the same final prediction. This is a stronger test: if the feed-forward networks can reproduce the attention layer's exact input-output mapping, they have truly learned the attention function, not just found an alternative path to the same translation.

- **Fixed maximum length of 50 with zero-padding:** Chosen because 96% of IWSLT2017 sentences fall within this length, minimizing data loss. Zero-padding provides implicit masking — zero inputs produce zero contributions to the output regardless of weight values.

- **Five size variants (XS through L):** Enables analysis of the performance-parameter tradeoff. The paper can assess whether feed-forward replacements need to be substantially larger than the original attention layer to achieve comparable performance, and whether there is a saturation point beyond which additional parameters provide no benefit.

- **ALR as the primary method for full Transformer replacement:** Chosen because it "was found to be the most effective approach in the case of encoder attention replacement, featuring both high performance and a small number of parameters." This selection is retrospective — ALR was evaluated against the other strategies on encoder replacement first, and the best-performing method was carried forward to the more ambitious full-Transformer experiments.

- **Independent training per layer:** Each attention layer has its own replacement network trained on its own teacher activations. This respects the fact that different Transformer layers learn different functions (lower layers tend to capture local syntax, higher layers capture more abstract semantics), and a single shared replacement network would need to learn a many-to-many mapping that is substantially harder.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Replaceability of Attention is Component-Dependent — Not a Binary Property

The dominant framing in architecture design research implicitly treats architectural mechanisms as binary choices: either a component is necessary or it isn't. Prior work questioning attention tended to ask "do Transformers need attention?" as a yes-or-no question — either proposing linearized or sparse approximations (Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Child et al., 2019) that preserve the attention paradigm, or replacing the entire architecture with alternatives like MLP-Mixer (Tolstikhin et al., 2021) that discard attention wholesale. What this paper reveals is that the question itself is poorly posed: the replaceability of attention is not uniform across attention *types*.

The paper decomposes attention into three functionally distinct mechanisms — encoder self-attention, decoder self-attention, and decoder cross-attention — and tests the replaceability of each independently. The results establish a sharp gradient: encoder self-attention is highly replaceable (the ALR-L variant achieves BLEU scores within 1–2% of the teacher across all four language pairs; Table 3), decoder self-attention is moderately replaceable (Dec SA-L reaches 0.253 on E2G versus the teacher's 0.257), and decoder cross-attention is substantially less replaceable (Dec CA-L reaches only 0.115 on E2G versus 0.257 — a collapse of more than 55% relative performance).

This is not an incremental finding about "which attention layer matters more." It is a **fundamental conceptual reframing**: attention is not one thing. The self-attention mechanisms that operate within a single sequence's representation space are behaviorally simpler than cross-attention, which must integrate information across two heterogeneous representation spaces. The implication is that what makes attention valuable is not pairwise token interaction per se — since self-attention's pairwise interactions can be learned by a feed-forward network — but rather the dynamic, content-based integration of information across distinct sequences, which appears to require either the attention mechanism's built-in query-key similarity computation or substantially more expressive feed-forward networks than those tested.

This decomposition also provides a principled explanation for why prior work produced conflicting signals about attention's necessity. Studies that focused on self-attention-only architectures (e.g., encoder-only BERT-style models or decoder-only GPT-style models) would find attention more replaceable; studies that examined encoder-decoder architectures with cross-attention would find it less so. The paper's granularity reveals that the binary question masks a more nuanced reality, and this more nuanced reality is the actual scientific contribution.

The evidence for this claim appears in multiple places: Figure 3 shows the performance gradient across replacement types at equivalent network sizes; Table 3 provides the raw BLEU scores where the cross-attention collapse is immediately visible; and the full-Transformer replacement (where all three attention types are substituted) performs poorly specifically because cross-attention drags down the aggregate score, not because all attention types fail equally.

---

### Innovation 2: The Architecture-Optimization Coupling Hypothesis — Demonstrated Through a Negative Result

A persistent question in deep learning is whether specialized architectures succeed because of superior representational capacity (they can express functions that generic architectures cannot) or because of superior optimization dynamics (they provide loss landscapes that gradient descent can navigate effectively). The paper provides a specific, empirically grounded answer — at least for the case of attention versus feed-forward networks — and the answer is **optimization dynamics**, not representational capacity.

The key evidence is a negative result: the replacement feed-forward networks **cannot be trained from scratch** to perform the attention function within the Transformer. The paper explicitly states:

> "These conclusions also point out the deficiencies of the current optimization methods, which are not able to train these 'attentionless Transformers' from scratch but need more advanced techniques, such as knowledge distillation to converge into desired parameter configurations."

This is a more significant statement than it first appears. The feed-forward replacements with sufficient capacity (M and L sizes) *can* represent the attention function — they achieve near-baseline BLEU scores when trained via distillation on the teacher's intermediate activations. The fact that they can represent the function but cannot discover it through end-to-end training on the translation objective implies that the Transformer's attention mechanism is not providing a *representational* advantage over feed-forward networks; it is providing an *optimization* advantage. The attention mechanism's structured computation — decomposing token interaction into query, key, and value projections followed by a normalized dot product — creates a loss landscape where gradient descent can efficiently find good solutions. The equivalent function parameterized as a generic fully-connected layer exists in the weight space of the larger feed-forward network, but gradient descent cannot find it from the translation loss alone.

This is a **fundamental shift** in how to think about architectural design. It suggests that the primary role of specialized architectures like attention is not to enable computations that generic networks cannot perform, but to provide a parameterization that makes the desired computation *discoverable* by current optimization algorithms. If optimization methods improve — through better initialization, more sophisticated optimizers, or training curricula — the architectural advantage of attention may diminish, and simpler architectures may become viable for tasks currently dominated by Transformers.

The intellectual lineage here connects to the lottery ticket hypothesis (Frankle and Carbin, 2019) and the broader literature on the relationship between architecture and optimization, but the paper's specific contribution is to demonstrate this coupling empirically for the Transformer's defining mechanism — attention — rather than for depth or width in convolutional networks. The demonstration is made possible by the experimental design: feed-forward networks of equivalent or greater capacity fail from scratch but succeed via distillation, isolating optimization as the bottleneck.

The evidence is indirect but consistent: the success of ALR at sizes M and L (Figure 2) shows representational capacity exists; the stated impossibility of end-to-end training (Discussion section) shows optimization is the barrier; and the gap between the two establishes that architecture matters for optimization, not for representation per se.

---

### Innovation 3: Component-Level Distillation as a Diagnostic Tool, Not Just a Compression Technique

Knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) has been overwhelmingly treated as a practical tool for model compression: train a smaller student to match a larger teacher's outputs, and deploy the student in resource-constrained environments. Prior work on distilling deep networks into shallow ones (Ba and Caruana, 2014; Urban et al., 2017) operated within this compression paradigm — the goal was to produce a smaller model that performs well, and the distillation setup (matching final logits) reflected this utilitarian framing.

This paper repurposes distillation as a **diagnostic instrument** rather than a compression technique. The goal is not to produce a deployable "attentionless Transformer" (the replacement networks are larger than the attention layers they replace, making them strictly worse for deployment). The goal is to answer a scientific question: *can* attention be replaced, and if so, under what conditions? Distillation from intermediate activations — not final logits — is the instrument that enables this diagnosis.

This is a **conceptual innovation** in methodology. By training replacement networks to match the teacher's internal representations (the specific input-output mapping of each attention layer), the paper can test whether a generic architecture can *represent* the attention function independently of whether it can *discover* it through end-to-end training. If the replacements were trained on the final translation loss only (as in standard distillation), a failure could mean either that the replacement cannot represent the function, or that the optimization cannot find it. By training on intermediate activations — a well-conditioned supervised regression problem — the paper eliminates the optimization uncertainty: if the replacement network can achieve low MSE on matching the teacher's attention outputs, then it can represent the function. Any residual gap in end-to-end BLEU score is then attributable to error propagation through the stacked replacement layers, not to representational insufficiency.

This diagnostic use of distillation is generalizable beyond this paper. Any architecture component can be subjected to the same test: extract its intermediate activations, train a generic replacement via distillation, and plug it back in. If performance is maintained, the component is not architecturally necessary. If performance degrades, either the replacement lacks capacity or the component provides something that even distilled generic networks cannot capture (as the paper finds with cross-attention). This methodology provides a principled way to distinguish between components that exist for optimization convenience and components that are genuinely essential.

The evidence that this is a diagnostic contribution, not a compression one, is explicit: the replacement networks are 5× to 680× larger than the original attention layer's 60,000 parameters (Table 1), and the paper acknowledges "this work does not introduce a competitive advantage over established methods." The value is in what the experiments reveal about Transformers, not in the replacement networks themselves.

---

### Innovation 4: The Residual Connection as an Independent Critical Component — Revealed Through Systematic Removal

The residual connection is a ubiquitous architectural element in deep Transformers, but its specific contribution is often conflated with the sub-layers it surrounds. Does the residual connection matter because attention needs it, or does it matter independently — providing a gradient highway that benefits any deep architecture regardless of the specific sub-layer operations? The paper's ALRR ablation provides a clean answer by separating these two hypotheses.

In the ALRR strategy, the multi-head attention block *and* its residual connection are replaced together by a single feed-forward network. This can be compared directly against ALR (which preserves the residual connection) at the same replacement network sizes. If the residual connection only mattered insofar as it helps attention, ALRR and ALR should perform similarly. If the residual connection matters independently — providing optimization benefits or representational shortcuts that help even when the sub-layer is a generic feed-forward network — then ALRR should perform worse, particularly at smaller network sizes where the replacement network has less capacity to compensate for the missing skip connection.

The results in Table 3 provide a striking pattern. At size XS, ALRR on encoder self-attention achieves a BLEU score of 0.013 on E2G (versus the teacher's 0.257), while ALR-XS achieves 0.180 — a factor of 13.8× difference. At size S, the gap is similar (ALRR-S: 0.018 vs. ALR-S: 0.196). Only at sizes M and L does ALRR recover to competitive performance (ALRR-M: 0.158, ALRR-L: 0.243). This size-dependent recovery reveals that **the residual connection's benefit can be partially substituted by increased feed-forward capacity**, but at a steep parameter cost. Small networks cannot compensate; large networks can.

This is a **fundamental finding** about Transformer architecture, not a marginal refinement. It demonstrates that the residual connection is not merely a training stabilizer for deep attention networks — it provides a functional benefit (identity-mapped information preservation) that generic feed-forward replacements must learn to replicate explicitly, and can only do so with substantial capacity. The fact that the residual connection matters *even when attention is replaced* means it is a first-class architectural component in its own right, not an accessory to the attention mechanism.

The finding also reframes how to think about the comparison between Transformer components and their replacements. ALR's success at mimicking attention is partly *enabled by* the preserved residual connection and layer normalization — the replacement network doesn't need to learn the identity mapping from scratch because the skip connection provides it for free. ALRR's failure at small sizes shows what happens when this scaffolding is removed: the replacement network must learn everything from scratch, and smaller networks cannot.

This insight connects to the broader literature on the importance of residual connections in deep networks (He et al., 2016) but provides specific evidence within the Transformer context: the residual connection matters independently of attention, and its removal cannot be trivially compensated for by increasing the capacity of the sub-layer it surrounds.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the IWSLT2017 machine translation dataset (Cettolo et al., 2017) across four language pairs: English–German (E2G), German–English (G2E), English–French (E2F), and French–English (F2E). After filtering to a maximum sentence length of 50 tokens, each subset consists of approximately 200,000 training sentences and 1,000 test sentences per language direction (Appendix C). The maximum-length filtering removes roughly 4% of samples, which the paper states "did not significantly shrink" the datasets.

- **Base model(s).** The teacher model is a vanilla Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) with the embedding dimension reduced from the standard 512 to 128 to reduce computational demands for the replacement network experiments. The architecture consists of six encoder layers and six decoder layers, with each encoder layer containing one multi-head self-attention block followed by a position-wise feed-forward network, and each decoder layer containing masked self-attention, cross-attention, and a feed-forward network, all with residual connections and layer normalization. The paper states this reduced model "did not drop the overall score too much below the original BLEU score" (Appendix B). The baseline BLEU scores achieved by this teacher are: 0.257 (E2G), 0.324 (G2E), 0.276 (E2F), and 0.292 (F2E) as recorded in Table 2.

- **Metrics.** The primary evaluation metric is the **BLEU score** (Papineni et al., 2002), the standard automated metric for machine translation quality that measures n-gram overlap between the model's output and reference translations, normalized to a 0–1 scale. The paper also reports **relative BLEU scores** — computed as the ratio of the replacement model's BLEU score to the teacher Transformer's BLEU score on the same language pair, expressed as a percentage, then averaged across the four language pairs (Figures 2 and 3). This relative metric abstracts away per-language-pair difficulty differences and provides a single number representing how close the replacement comes to matching the original.

- **Baselines.** The single baseline against which all replacement methods are compared is **the vanilla Transformer teacher model** with embedding dimension 128, trained from scratch on IWSLT2017. The paper does not compare against other "attentionless" architectures, linearized attention variants, or prior distillation methods because the goal is not to establish state-of-the-art performance but to measure how much of the original Transformer's performance can be recovered after surgical attention replacement. The teacher's BLEU scores (Table 2) are the reference values, and all relative percentages are computed against these per-language-pair scores.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use generation budgets or compute-matched comparisons in the conventional sense. Instead, the relevant resource accounting is **parameter count**: each replacement strategy and size variant has a specific number of parameters (Table 1), and the original attention layer contains approximately 60,000 parameters. The paper explicitly notes that the replacement networks "mostly exceed" this parameter count, with the smallest ALR-XS variant using 320K parameters (5.3× the original) and the largest ALR-L using 41M parameters (683× the original). There is no FLOPs accounting, latency measurement, or inference-time cost analysis. This is a significant omission: the reported competitive BLEU scores come at a parameter cost that the paper acknowledges as "one of the major bottlenecks for the deployment of these 'attentionless' Transformers in practice" (Appendix D).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper reports BLEU scores individually for each of the four language pairs (Table 3) but does not describe any cross-validation procedure, statistical significance testing, confidence intervals, or multiple-run averaging. The figures presenting "relative BLEU scores [%]" (Figures 2 and 3) average across the four language pairs, but no error bars, standard deviations, or variance estimates are provided. The replacement networks are trained for a fixed 20 epochs with a fixed learning rate and batch size, with no mention of early stopping, hyperparameter tuning, or repeated trials. This means the reported numbers should be interpreted as point estimates from single training runs, and the reliability of the relative comparisons — particularly between methods with small performance differences (e.g., ALR-L vs. ASLR-L in Table 3) — cannot be assessed statistically from the information provided.

---

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Encoder Self-Attention Replacement: All Strategies Achieve Competitive Performance at Sufficient Scale

The encoder self-attention replacement experiments test the four replacement strategies (ALR, ALRR, ASLR, ELR) across five network sizes (XS, S, M, L, and an implied XL that the paper states "brought no improvement in the BLEU score"; Table 1 note) on all four language pairs. The headline result, visible in Figure 2, is that **all four strategies can achieve relative BLEU scores within approximately 95–101% of the teacher** when using the largest (L) replacement networks, with the exception of ELR which peaks at roughly 75–85% relative BLEU.

**ALR (Attention Layer Replacement): The most effective overall strategy.** ALR preserves the residual connection and layer normalization, replacing only the multi-head attention block. The raw BLEU scores in Table 3 demonstrate near-complete recovery of teacher performance at sizes M and L across all language pairs:

| Language Pair | Teacher | ALR-XS | ALR-S | ALR-M | ALR-L |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E2G | 0.257 | 0.180 | 0.196 | 0.245 | 0.252 |
| G2E | 0.324 | 0.235 | 0.257 | 0.320 | 0.327 |
| E2F | 0.276 | 0.226 | 0.244 | 0.275 | 0.276 |
| F2E | 0.292 | 0.218 | 0.240 | 0.284 | 0.288 |

At size L, ALR matches or exceeds the teacher on three of four language pairs (G2E: 0.327 vs. 0.324; E2F: 0.276 vs. 0.276; F2E: 0.288 vs. 0.292 — a negligible 0.004 gap). On E2G, ALR-L reaches 0.252 versus the teacher's 0.257 — a difference of 0.005 BLEU, or approximately 98% relative performance. Even at size M, ALR achieves 0.245 on E2G (95.3% relative) and 0.320 on G2E (98.8% relative).

The scaling behavior from XS through L is notably smooth: each size increment brings a consistent improvement, with the largest jump occurring between S and M. This suggests that the ALR replacement networks have sufficient representational capacity at roughly 5–10M parameters (size M) to capture most of the attention mechanism's functionality, with the jump to 41M (size L) providing diminishing returns.

**ALRR (Attention Layer with Residual Connection Replacement): Catastrophic failure at small sizes, recovery at large sizes.** ALRR removes both the multi-head attention and its residual connection. The performance pattern is dramatically different from ALR: small replacement networks completely fail, while large ones eventually recover.

| Language Pair | Teacher | ALRR-XS | ALRR-S | ALRR-M | ALRR-L |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E2G | 0.257 | 0.013 | 0.018 | 0.158 | 0.243 |
| G2E | 0.324 | 0.010 | 0.013 | 0.181 | 0.315 |
| E2F | 0.276 | 0.003 | 0.008 | 0.153 | 0.263 |
| F2E | 0.292 | 0.001 | 0.012 | 0.150 | 0.276 |

At size XS, ALRR produces near-zero BLEU scores (0.001–0.013 across language pairs) — essentially random translation output. At size S, the scores remain in the 0.008–0.018 range. Recovery begins at size M, where scores jump to 0.150–0.181 (roughly 50–60% relative performance), and size L achieves 0.243–0.315 (approximately 95% relative on three language pairs, though E2F lags at 0.263 vs. teacher 0.276, or 95.3% relative).

This pattern directly demonstrates that the residual connection is independently critical — not merely an accessory to attention — and that its function can be partially substituted by increased feed-forward capacity, but only at a steep parameter cost. The factor-of-10 gap between ALRR-XS and ALR-XS (0.013 vs. 0.180 on E2G) cannot be explained by representational capacity alone (both networks are small), but by the structural difference: ALR receives the residual connection's identity-mapped information for free, while ALRR must learn everything from scratch.

**ASLR (Attention Separate Heads Layer Replacement): Most consistent performance across sizes.** ASLR uses one feed-forward network per attention head and achieves the most uniform scaling behavior. The raw scores in Table 3:

| Language Pair | Teacher | ASLR-XS | ASLR-S | ASLR-M | ASLR-L |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E2G | 0.257 | 0.245 | 0.250 | 0.251 | 0.252 |
| G2E | 0.324 | 0.319 | 0.323 | 0.326 | 0.326 |
| E2F | 0.276 | 0.257 | 0.260 | 0.269 | 0.271 |
| F2E | 0.292 | 0.272 | 0.285 | 0.289 | 0.290 |

ASLR-XS already achieves BLEU scores very close to the teacher (0.245 vs. 0.257 on E2G, 0.319 vs. 0.324 on G2E), and further scaling provides minimal gains — ASLR-L reaches 0.252 on E2G, only 0.007 higher than ASLR-XS. This is the only replacement strategy where the smallest network size achieves competitive performance, suggesting that the per-head decomposition provides a strong inductive bias that reduces the required capacity per network. Even though each ASLR-XS network has 290K parameters per head (Table 1), and multiple heads are used, the per-head specialization allows each small network to learn a narrower, more tractable function than the monolithic ALR or ALRR replacements.

However, the parameter efficiency is poor: if the teacher uses 8 attention heads, ASLR-XS requires `8 × 290K = 2.32M` parameters per attention layer for performance that ALR-M achieves with 10M parameters. The consistency is architecturally informative, but the total parameter cost is comparable to or larger than ALR at equivalent performance levels.

**ELR (Encoder Layer Replacement): The worst performer, confirming the value of encoder substructure.** ELR replaces the entire encoder layer with a single feed-forward network:

| Language Pair | Teacher | ELR-XS | ELR-S | ELR-M | ELR-L |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E2G | 0.257 | 0.012 | 0.016 | 0.116 | 0.194 |
| G2E | 0.324 | 0.010 | 0.015 | 0.120 | 0.248 |
| E2F | 0.276 | 0.011 | 0.132 | 0.124 | 0.225 |
| F2E | 0.292 | 0.011 | 0.012 | 0.110 | 0.219 |

ELR-XS and ELR-S produce effectively random output (BLEU scores 0.010–0.016, with the notable exception of ELR-S on E2F achieving 0.132 — an unexplained outlier). Size M improves to 0.110–0.124, and size L reaches 0.194–0.248, representing roughly 75–85% relative BLEU. Even at the largest tested size, ELR-L substantially underperforms the teacher (e.g., 0.194 vs. 0.257 on E2G, a 24.5% relative gap).

The paper attributes this failure to "the simplicity of the replacement model, which discards all of the encoder structures that aid training" (Discussion). This conclusion is well-supported: the structured decomposition of the encoder layer into attention and feed-forward sub-layers with residual pathways is not merely an optimization convenience — it provides a representational decomposition that even a 41M-parameter shallow network trained via distillation cannot fully replicate in a single monolithic mapping.

**Summary of encoder self-attention replacement (Figure 2).** Aggregating across the four language pairs, the relative BLEU scores (interpreted from Figure 2 and Table 3) show ALR, ALRR, and ASLR all converging to approximately 98–101% relative BLEU at size L, ELR stalling at roughly 80–85%, and ALRR-XS/ALRR-S/ELR-XS/ELR-S collapsing to near-zero. The paper's claim that the proposed approaches "are capable of achieving comparable performance to that of the original Transformer" (Conclusion) is supported for ALR, ALRR (at size L), and ASLR (at all sizes), but not for ELR, which consistently underperforms.

---

#### Full Transformer Attention Replacement: Self-Attention Is Replaceable, Cross-Attention Is the Bottleneck

The full Transformer replacement experiments use ALR (the most effective encoder replacement method) to replace attention in three configurations: decoder self-attention only (Dec SA), decoder cross-attention only (Dec CA), encoder and decoder self-attention together (E-D SA), and full replacement of all attention components (Full). The headline finding, visible in Figure 3 and Table 3, is that **decoder self-attention replacement achieves near-teacher performance, decoder cross-attention replacement causes a catastrophic collapse, and the full "attentionless Transformer" is bottlenecked entirely by cross-attention failure.**

**Decoder self-attention replacement (Dec SA): Nearly as replaceable as encoder self-attention.** The ALR method applied to the decoder's masked self-attention yields BLEU scores very close to the teacher at size L:

| Language Pair | Teacher | Dec SA-XS | Dec SA-S | Dec SA-M | Dec SA-L |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E2G | 0.257 | 0.227 | 0.240 | 0.252 | 0.253 |
| G2E | 0.324 | 0.305 | 0.313 | 0.322 | 0.323 |
| E2F | 0.276 | 0.251 | 0.255 | 0.267 | 0.273 |
| F2E | 0.292 | 0.273 | 0.281 | 0.290 | 0.291 |

At size L, Dec SA scores are within 0.001–0.004 BLEU of the teacher across all language pairs — essentially identical performance. Even at size M, the gap is minimal (0.252 vs. 0.257 on E2G, 0.322 vs. 0.324 on G2E). This confirms that decoder self-attention, despite the additional complexity of causal masking, is learnable by the same ALR approach that works for encoder self-attention. The sequential processing described in Appendix B (running the network once per token position with future positions masked) successfully replicates the causal constraint without degrading the replacement network's ability to learn the attention function.

**Decoder cross-attention replacement (Dec CA): The performance cliff.** Replacing only the decoder's cross-attention (while keeping all other attention components as the original teacher) produces dramatically worse results:

| Language Pair | Teacher | Dec CA-XS | Dec CA-S | Dec CA-M | Dec CA-L |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E2G | 0.257 | 0.035 | 0.054 | 0.104 | 0.115 |
| G2E | 0.324 | 0.036 | 0.058 | 0.125 | 0.130 |
| E2F | 0.276 | 0.035 | 0.042 | 0.089 | 0.109 |
| F2E | 0.292 | 0.039 | 0.053 | 0.108 | 0.115 |

At size XS, BLEU scores are uniformly in the 0.035–0.039 range — barely above random, and roughly 88–89% lower than the teacher. Size S is only marginally better (0.042–0.058). Size M reaches 0.089–0.125 (roughly 32–39% relative), and size L plateaus at 0.109–0.130 (roughly 40–45% relative). The scaling from M to L provides minimal improvement on three of four language pairs (e.g., E2G: 0.104 → 0.115, F2E: 0.108 → 0.115), suggesting that simply increasing capacity within the shallow feed-forward architecture is approaching diminishing returns at 41M parameters.

This is the paper's most striking result. Even with the rest of the Transformer completely intact — trained encoder, working decoder self-attention, fully functional feed-forward blocks and layer normalization — replacing just the cross-attention with the largest available feed-forward network (41M parameters) results in a BLEU collapse of more than 55% relative to the teacher. The cross-attention replacement alone causes the model to produce translations roughly half as good as the original, despite all other components functioning normally.

**Encoder + Decoder self-attention replacement (E-D SA): Verifying that self-attention is replaceable in both encoder and decoder simultaneously.** When ALR replaces self-attention in both the encoder and decoder (but keeps cross-attention as the original teacher), the results confirm that the self-attention replacements do not interact destructively:

| Language Pair | Teacher | E-D SA-XS | E-D SA-S | E-D SA-M | E-D SA-L |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E2G | 0.257 | 0.163 | 0.187 | 0.244 | 0.246 |
| G2E | 0.324 | 0.222 | 0.247 | 0.313 | 0.321 |
| E2F | 0.276 | 0.219 | 0.235 | 0.265 | 0.270 |
| F2E | 0.292 | 0.204 | 0.227 | 0.277 | 0.284 |

At size L, E-D SA scores are within 0.003–0.011 BLEU of the teacher across all language pairs (e.g., 0.246 vs. 0.257 on E2G, a gap of 0.011 or 95.7% relative). The small gap versus the teacher is consistent with the individual encoder and decoder ALR results — there is no apparent compounding of errors when both self-attention mechanisms are replaced simultaneously.

**Full replacement (Full): The attentionless Transformer is bottlenecked by cross-attention.** When ALR replaces all attention components — encoder self-attention, decoder self-attention, and decoder cross-attention — the results are dominated by the cross-attention failure:

| Language Pair | Teacher | Full-XS | Full-S | Full-M | Full-L |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E2G | 0.257 | 0.026 | 0.041 | 0.102 | 0.105 |
| G2E | 0.324 | 0.027 | 0.053 | 0.122 | 0.134 |
| E2F | 0.276 | 0.026 | 0.048 | 0.083 | 0.117 |
| F2E | 0.292 | 0.032 | 0.044 | 0.107 | 0.116 |

The Full replacement scores are essentially identical to the Dec CA scores at each size level (compare Full-XS: 0.026 vs. Dec CA-XS: 0.035 on E2G; Full-L: 0.105 vs. Dec CA-L: 0.115). This confirms that cross-attention replacement is the **sole bottleneck** — further improving the self-attention replacements would not help the full attentionless Transformer, because cross-attention failure alone determines the overall performance ceiling. The paper states this explicitly: "BLEU scores are significantly lower if the cross-attention mechanism is replaced by FF networks" and "in all cases where the decoder cross-attention was substituted for the FF networks, the final model performed significantly worse, regardless of whether other parts were substituted" (Appendix C).

**The performance gradient across replacement types (Figure 3).** Aggregating across the four language pairs, the relative BLEU scores at size L reveal the gradient: Encoder SA replacement (ALR-L): approximately 99–101% relative; Decoder SA replacement (Dec SA-L): approximately 98–100% relative; Encoder + Decoder SA replacement (E-D SA-L): approximately 96–98% relative; full replacement (Full-L): approximately 39–42% relative; cross-attention only replacement (Dec CA-L): approximately 40–45% relative. The self-attention replacements cluster near 100% relative BLEU; the cross-attention replacements cluster near 40% relative BLEU; and there is no intermediate level of performance. This binary outcome — self-attention: yes, cross-attention: no — is the paper's central empirical finding.

#### The Size-Performance Tradeoff: Replacement Networks Are Not Parameter-Efficient

While the BLEU scores demonstrate that feed-forward networks *can* replace self-attention, the parameter cost is substantial. The original multi-head attention layer contains approximately 60,000 parameters. Table 1 reports the replacement network parameter counts:

- ALR-XS: 320K (5.3× the original)
- ALR-S: 640K (10.7×)
- ALR-M: 10M (167×)
- ALR-L: 41M (683×)

ALR-L achieves near-identical BLEU to the teacher at 41M parameters per attention layer versus the original 60K — a parameter increase of roughly 680× per replaced attention layer. Across six encoder layers, replacing all six encoder self-attention blocks with ALR-L adds approximately `6 × 41M = 246M` parameters, compared to `6 × 60K = 360K` for the original attention — an increase of over 680× for the attention components alone. The paper does not report the total Transformer parameter count, but for context, a Transformer with embedding dimension 128 and six encoder/decoder layers is in the range of a few million to tens of millions of parameters total. Adding 246M parameters for encoder attention replacement alone would dominate the model's parameter budget.

The paper acknowledges this explicitly: "all of the replacement approaches come at a significant cost of having more parameters" (Discussion) and identifies "the size of the networks represents one of the major bottlenecks for the deployment of these 'attentionless' Transformers in practice" (Appendix D). The "XS" and "S" variants, while smaller, achieve substantially lower BLEU scores for ALR (XS: roughly 70–76% relative BLEU; S: roughly 76–83% relative), meaning the parameter-efficient variants do not maintain competitive performance.

ASLR has a different efficiency profile. At size XS, each per-head network has 290K parameters. If the teacher uses 8 heads, total ASLR-XS parameters per layer are `8 × 290K = 2.32M` — roughly 39× the original attention layer, but achieving BLEU scores within 95–98% relative (vs. ALR-XS at 70–76% with 320K). This suggests that the per-head decomposition improves parameter efficiency for a given performance level, though at the cost of architectural complexity (multiple networks per layer instead of one).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper's experimental design is itself structured as a series of ablations — varying the replacement strategy (ALR, ALRR, ASLR, ELR), the network size (XS through L), the attention type (encoder self-attention, decoder self-attention, decoder cross-attention), and the language pair (four pairs). Beyond this main experimental structure, the paper includes several additional analyses:

**Residual connection ablation (ALR vs. ALRR):** Comparing ALR (residual preserved) and ALRR (residual removed) at the same network sizes isolates the residual connection's contribution. The finding — that ALRR catastrophically fails at sizes XS and S while ALR maintains reasonable performance — is described in the main results above. This is the paper's clearest ablation: the only difference between ALR and ALRR is whether the residual connection is preserved or replaced along with attention, and the performance gap demonstrates the residual connection's independent criticality. The recovery of ALRR at sizes M and L shows that large feed-forward networks can partially compensate, but the compensation is parameter-expensive.

**Per-head decomposition ablation (ALR vs. ASLR):** ASLR's consistently strong performance at small sizes, compared to ALR's size-dependent scaling, reveals that the multi-head structure provides a useful inductive bias even when the attention mechanism itself is removed. ASLR-XS achieves 95–98% relative BLEU with 2.32M total parameters (assuming 8 heads), while ALR achieves comparable performance only at size M (10M parameters). This suggests that training separate small networks per attention head is more parameter-efficient than training one larger monolithic network, likely because each head in the teacher learns a narrower, more specialized function that is easier for a small network to replicate than the combined multi-head output.

**Encoder layer structure ablation (ELR vs. all other methods):** ELR's failure to reach competitive performance even at size L (75–85% relative BLEU) compared to ALR, ALRR, and ASLR all exceeding 95% relative BLEU at size L demonstrates that the structured decomposition of the encoder layer into sub-layers with residual connections is not merely an optimization convenience — it provides a representational decomposition that matters even when training via distillation. A single feed-forward network of 41M parameters (the same size as ALR-L) cannot learn to replicate the combined behavior of multi-head self-attention, residual connection, layer normalization, and position-wise feed-forward network as effectively as a structured arrangement where the attention replacement only needs to learn the attention component's specific function.

**Cross-attention failure across all sizes:** The Dec CA results in Table 3 constitute an implicit ablation showing that capacity alone (up to 41M parameters) is insufficient to close the cross-attention performance gap. The scores plateau between M and L sizes (e.g., E2G: 0.104 → 0.115, a marginal 0.011 improvement for a 4× parameter increase), suggesting that the shallow feed-forward architecture itself, not its size, is the limiting factor. The paper does not test deeper networks (two or more hidden layers), different activation functions, or alternative architectures for cross-attention — these are flagged as future work (Appendix D).

**Language pair robustness:** The four language pairs span two language families and two translation directions each. The relative BLEU patterns are consistent across all four pairs: ALR-L achieves 98–101% relative BLEU on all pairs, Dec CA-L achieves 40–45% relative BLEU on all pairs, and the rank ordering of replacement strategies is preserved. This consistency provides evidence that the findings are not specific to a particular language pair or translation direction. However, all four pairs involve relatively high-resource European languages with similar syntactic structures; the paper does not test on morphologically rich languages, low-resource pairs, or languages with substantially different word orders where attention might play a different functional role.

**Size scaling saturation:** The paper notes in Table 1 that "further scaling of these networks brought no improvement in the BLEU score," implying that an "XL" size was tested and found to saturate. This is an important negative result: it suggests that the remaining performance gap versus the teacher (e.g., ALR-L on E2G: 0.252 vs. 0.257) is not a capacity limitation but a fundamental expressivity limitation of the shallow feed-forward architecture, or an artifact of error propagation through independently trained replacement networks that cannot be corrected by more parameters.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim: Shallow Feed-Forward Networks Can Rival the Performance of Attention Layers

**What the experiments actually demonstrate:** ALR, ASLR, and ALRR (at size L) achieve BLEU scores within approximately 1–5% relative of the teacher Transformer on encoder self-attention replacement. ALR also achieves within 1–2% relative on decoder self-attention replacement. This is genuine evidence that self-attention mechanisms can be functionally replaced by feed-forward networks trained via distillation, with minimal performance degradation in the final translation quality.

**What is not demonstrated:** The replacement networks are 167–683× larger than the original attention layers at the sizes that achieve competitive performance. The claim of "rivaling" performance is true only if parameter count is ignored. A fairer characterization would be: feed-forward networks can match attention's functionality, but only with substantially more parameters, and the parameter inefficiency is severe enough that the replacement approach is not practically deployable. The paper acknowledges this (Discussion: "all of the replacement approaches come at a significant cost of having more parameters"), but the framing in the abstract and conclusion emphasizes performance parity while downplaying the parameter cost.

**What would have strengthened this claim:** A parameter-matched comparison — what BLEU score does a feed-forward network with exactly 60K parameters achieve? The smallest tested variant (ALR-XS at 320K) is already 5.3× larger than the original attention and achieves substantially lower BLEU (70–76% relative). A direct parameter-matched comparison would reveal whether any BLEU can be recovered at equal parameter count, or whether feed-forward replacements must necessarily be larger to approximate attention's function. Additionally, testing deeper feed-forward networks (2–4 hidden layers) at the same or lower parameter count as the single-hidden-layer variants would reveal whether depth can substitute for width.

#### Claim: The Full "Attentionless Transformer" Achieves Competitive Performance

**What the experiments actually demonstrate:** The full attentionless Transformer — where ALR replaces encoder self-attention, decoder self-attention, and decoder cross-attention — achieves BLEU scores of 0.105–0.134 at size L across the four language pairs. The teacher achieves 0.257–0.324. The relative BLEU is approximately 39–42%. This is not competitive performance; it is a catastrophic collapse.

**The claim requires qualification:** The paper's conclusion states that "the proposed approaches are capable of achieving comparable performance to that of the original Transformer, demonstrating that Transformers do not necessarily need to have attention." This claim is true only if "attention" is understood to mean "self-attention" and excludes cross-attention. The full attentionless Transformer — the literal interpretation of "Transformers do not necessarily need to have attention" — fails badly. A more precise statement would be: "Transformers do not necessarily need self-attention in the encoder and decoder, but cross-attention appears essential for encoder-decoder architectures on machine translation tasks."

The paper does not hide the cross-attention failure — it is reported prominently in Figure 3 and Table 3 — but the framing in the abstract emphasizes the positive results while mentioning cross-attention only as "comparatively worse." The discrepancy between "comparatively worse" (suggesting a modest gap) and the actual numbers (55–60% relative BLEU collapse) is a communication weakness in the paper's presentation.

**What would have strengthened this claim:** Testing cross-attention replacement with deeper or architecturally different feed-forward networks (e.g., two or three hidden layers, or networks that explicitly model pairwise encoder-decoder token interactions through a bilinear layer). The current negative result for cross-attention applies only to single-hidden-layer feed-forward networks; it does not establish that cross-attention is fundamentally irreplaceable, only that it is not replaceable by the specific architecture tested. A systematic exploration of alternative replacement architectures for cross-attention would sharpen the claim and potentially reveal what property of cross-attention makes it harder to replicate (the heterogeneous inputs, the need for content-based alignment, the higher dimensionality from concatenated encoder-decoder representations).

#### Claim: The Difficulty of Training "Attentionless Transformers" From Scratch Points to Optimization Deficiencies

**What the experiments actually demonstrate:** The paper states that feed-forward replacements cannot be trained from scratch and require knowledge distillation. However, this is stated as a conclusion without presenting any experimental evidence for it. The paper does not run an experiment comparing distillation-trained replacements to identically structured replacements trained end-to-end on the translation objective. The paper does not report results from attempting to train "attentionless Transformers" from scratch and failing. The claim about optimization deficiencies is an interpretation, not a demonstrated result.

**What would have strengthened this claim:** A direct experiment: initialize an "attentionless Transformer" (with feed-forward replacements in place of self-attention, using the same architectures as the ALR replacements) and train it from scratch on IWSLT2017, comparing the resulting BLEU score against the distillation-trained version at the same parameter count. If the end-to-end trained version performs substantially worse, the optimization deficiency hypothesis is supported. Without this experiment, the claim rests on reasoning rather than evidence.

#### Weaknesses in the Experimental Design

**Single teacher architecture and scale.** All experiments use a single Transformer architecture (embedding dimension 128, six encoder/decoder layers) at a single scale. The paper does not test whether the replaceability of attention depends on model scale — would a Transformer with embedding dimension 512 or 1024, or 12+ layers, show different replacement patterns? The reduced embedding dimension (128) was chosen for computational feasibility, but this choice may interact with the replacement results in unknown ways. A smaller model may learn attention functions that are easier for feed-forward networks to replicate (because they are lower-dimensional or less specialized), making the results more favorable to replacement than they would be at larger scales.

**No statistical reporting.** All BLEU scores in Table 3 are presented as point estimates from single training runs. No standard deviations, confidence intervals, or multiple-run averages are reported. For the smaller performance gaps — e.g., ALR-L E2G: 0.252 vs. teacher 0.257, a difference of 0.005 BLEU — it is impossible to determine whether this residual gap is statistically meaningful or within the run-to-run variance of either the teacher or the replacement network's training. BLEU score variance on IWSLT2017 test sets with ~1,000 sentences is non-negligible, and small differences should not be interpreted without variance estimates.

**No analysis of what the replacement networks actually learn.** The paper reports BLEU scores and parameter counts but does not analyze the internal representations of the replacement networks. Do they learn to approximate attention weights? Do they develop specialized patterns for different token positions? Do they exhibit anything analogous to the head specialization observed in attention mechanisms? Probing experiments — such as comparing the replacement network's output against the teacher's attention output on specific sentence pairs, or analyzing whether the replacement network's hidden representations encode positional relationships — would provide deeper insight into *how* the feed-forward networks approximate attention. Without such analysis, the paper demonstrates *that* replacement is possible but not *how* replacement works mechanistically.

**The "96% within length 50" heuristic may introduce subtle biases.** The maximum sequence length of 50 was chosen because 96% of samples fall within this length. However, the excluded 4% are systematically longer sentences — and longer sentences are typically harder to translate, require more complex attention patterns, and may benefit more from attention's ability to handle long-range dependencies. By truncating the test set to sentences of length ≤ 50, the evaluation may be biased toward shorter, easier sentences where the attention mechanism's advantages are less pronounced. The paper does not analyze whether replacement network performance degrades more on longer sentences within the 1–50 range, which would be the natural test of whether attention's value scales with sequence length.

**The teacher's attention layer has only ~60K parameters.** This is partially a consequence of the reduced embedding dimension (128 instead of 512), but it means the attention mechanism being replaced is relatively low-capacity. At larger embedding dimensions, the attention layer's parameter count scales as `4 × d_model^2` for the Q, K, V, and output projections. At d_model = 512, the attention parameters would be roughly `4 × 512^2 ≈ 1.05M` — substantially closer to the replacement network sizes and possibly reducing or eliminating the parameter efficiency gap. The paper's central finding about parameter inefficiency may not generalize to standard-scale Transformers where attention layers are already larger.

**No end-to-end fine-tuning after replacement.** The replacement networks are trained independently on intermediate activations and then inserted into the Transformer without any joint fine-tuning. The paper does not explore whether allowing the rest of the Transformer (or the replacement networks themselves) to adapt through additional end-to-end training on the translation objective could recover some of the residual performance gap, particularly for cross-attention replacement. Given that the replacement networks are initialized close to the teacher's behavior (via distillation), fine-tuning might allow error propagation issues to be corrected and could improve results.

#### Summary of Strengths

Despite these weaknesses, the experimental design has genuine strengths. The four-way comparison of replacement strategies (ALR, ALRR, ASLR, ELR) systematically varies the level of architectural abstraction being replaced, providing a clean ablation hierarchy. The testing across four language pairs demonstrates cross-linguistic consistency. The explicit negative results — ALRR failure at small sizes, ELR failure even at large sizes, cross-attention failure across all sizes — are reported transparently and are as informative as the positive results. The paper's refusal to overclaim (explicitly noting it "does not introduce a competitive advantage over established methods") is scientifically appropriate. The main contribution — a clear empirical demonstration of a sharp boundary between replaceable self-attention and irreplaceable cross-attention — is well-supported by the data.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Replacement Networks Are Massively Parameter-Inefficient — Up to 683× Larger Than the Attention Layers They Replace

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper demonstrates that shallow feed-forward networks can match the functional behavior of self-attention layers, but only at parameter counts that vastly exceed the original attention mechanism. The original multi-head self-attention layer in the teacher Transformer contains approximately 60,000 parameters. The replacement networks that achieve competitive BLEU scores — ALR-M (10M parameters, 167× the original) and ALR-L (41M parameters, 683× the original) — are larger by factors that make direct deployment impractical. The paper explicitly acknowledges this:

> "all of the replacement approaches come at a significant cost of having more parameters" (Discussion)

and identifies it as a practical bottleneck:

> "the size of the networks represents one of the major bottlenecks for the deployment of these 'attentionless' Transformers in practice" (Appendix D).

The smallest tested variant, ALR-XS at 320K parameters (5.3× the original), achieves only 70–76% relative BLEU across language pairs — a substantial performance degradation that rules it out as a viable drop-in replacement.

**The consequence:** The headline result — that feed-forward networks can "rival the performance of the original architecture" — is true only if parameter count is treated as an unconstrained resource. For any practical deployment scenario, the parameter inefficiency is disqualifying. A full encoder self-attention replacement with ALR-L adds approximately `6 × 41M = 246M` parameters to the model for the attention components alone, compared to `6 × 60K = 360K` for the original attention. This dominates the total parameter budget: the entire teacher Transformer likely contains only a few million to low tens of millions of parameters total (embedding dimension 128, six encoder/decoder layers), meaning the replacement networks alone would account for over 90% of the model's parameters while replacing only the attention sub-layers.

The parameter cost also scales poorly with model size. At standard Transformer scales (embedding dimension 512 or 1024), the replacement network's input dimension grows quadratically with the embedding dimension due to the concatenated sequence representation (`embedding_dim × max_sequence_length`). At d_model = 512 and length 50, the input dimension becomes 25,600, and the weight matrices `W_1` and `W_2` — which contain `hidden_dim × 25600` and `25600 × hidden_dim` parameters respectively — would balloon proportionally. The replacement network parameter counts reported in Table 1 are specific to the reduced embedding dimension of 128; scaling to standard Transformer sizes would make the parameter overhead even more extreme.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Table 1 provides the explicit parameter counts for each replacement strategy and size variant. Table 3 shows the BLEU scores at each size, with the steep performance drop from ALR-M (10M, ~95% relative BLEU) to ALR-S (640K, ~76–83% relative) and ALR-XS (320K, ~70–76% relative). The paper does not run a parameter-matched comparison — testing a feed-forward network with exactly 60K parameters to determine what BLEU score, if any, can be recovered at equal parameter count. This missing experiment makes it impossible to determine whether feed-forward replacements *must* be larger than attention to approximate its function, or whether the paper simply did not optimize for parameter efficiency.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not attempt to address parameter efficiency through architectural innovations (e.g., bottleneck layers, low-rank factorizations, weight sharing across layers, or pruning). Appendix D suggests that "further optimization of the FF networks' hyperparameters using advanced parameter search (e.g. using Bayesian optimization) could yield even better results in terms of translation quality and possibly even enable the usage of smaller FF networks for the replacement," but this refers to hyperparameter tuning rather than architectural changes that could fundamentally reduce parameter count. No experiments are reported that explore parameter-efficient replacement architectures.

---

### 6.2 The Replacement Networks Are Inherently Sequence-Length-Bound — They Cannot Process Variable-Length Inputs

**The assumption or constraint:** The feed-forward replacement networks operate on a fixed-size input vector formed by concatenating all token representations in a sentence padded to a maximum length of 50. This creates a hard constraint: the replacement network's weight matrices have a fixed input dimension of `128 × 50 = 6400`, and the network cannot process sequences longer than 50 tokens or shorter sequences without zero-padding them to exactly length 50. The paper acknowledges this explicitly:

> "Another downside of our replacement of the attention with a fixed-size feed-forward network is the imminent lack of flexibility of the model in terms of the length of sequences the model can operate with." (Discussion)

This stands in fundamental contrast to the original attention mechanism, which can handle arbitrary sequence lengths (up to memory constraints) with the same learned parameters, because attention's weight matrices are applied per-token and the sequence-length-dependent computation (dot products) is parameter-free.

**The consequence:** An attentionless Transformer built with these replacement networks is not just a different architecture — it is a fundamentally less flexible model that can only process inputs up to the pre-specified maximum length. For any deployment where input sequence lengths vary (which is essentially all real-world NLP), the model must either reject inputs longer than 50 tokens, truncate them (losing information), or process them through a sliding window with additional architectural machinery not described in the paper. The 50-token limit was chosen because 96% of IWSLT2017 sentences fall within this length, but this means 4% of the dataset was discarded during training and evaluation — and the discarded sentences are systematically longer, potentially more complex, and likely benefit more from attention's long-range dependency handling. The evaluation results therefore apply only to shorter sentences and may overstate the replacement networks' effectiveness on a full-length test set.

Additionally, the fixed-length representation creates a waste of computation for short sentences. A 5-token sentence must still be padded to 50 tokens, meaning the replacement network performs computations on 45 zero-valued token positions that contribute nothing to the output but consume FLOPs and memory identically to real positions. The original attention mechanism can process a 5-token sentence with a `5 × 5` attention matrix and only 5 token-wise transformations; the replacement network always processes the full 6400-dimensional vector regardless of actual sequence length. The ratio of wasted to useful computation grows as average sequence length decreases.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The maximum sequence length of 50 is established in Appendix B, along with the statement that 96% of samples are within this length. The paper does not analyze whether replacement network BLEU scores degrade on longer sentences within the 1–50 range (e.g., comparing performance on sentences of length 1–25 versus 26–50). No ablation tests different maximum lengths (e.g., 30 vs. 50 vs. 100) to characterize how the replacement network's required capacity grows with sequence length. Without this analysis, the practical scalability of the approach to longer sequences — which are common in document-level translation, summarization, and many other sequence-to-sequence tasks — is entirely unknown.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not propose any mechanism for handling variable-length sequences with the feed-forward replacement. No experiments test sliding window approaches, hierarchical processing, or position-agnostic architectures that could provide length flexibility. The limitation is stated but not addressed.

---

### 6.3 Cross-Attention Replacement Causes Catastrophic Performance Collapse — The "Attentionless Transformer" Fails as a Complete Model

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper's most ambitious claim — that "Transformers do not necessarily need to have attention" — is undermined by the fact that replacing all attention components, including cross-attention, produces a model that achieves only 39–42% of the teacher's BLEU score. The full attentionless Transformer (Full-L in Table 3) achieves BLEU scores of 0.105–0.134 across the four language pairs, compared to the teacher's 0.257–0.324 — a relative performance collapse of approximately 58–61%. The paper itself identifies cross-attention as the source of this failure:

> "BLEU scores are significantly lower if the cross-attention mechanism is replaced by FF networks... in all cases where the decoder cross-attention was substituted for the FF networks, the final model performed significantly worse, regardless of whether other parts were substituted." (Appendix C)

The comparison between Dec CA-L (replacing only cross-attention: 0.115 on E2G) and Full-L (replacing all attention: 0.105 on E2G) in Table 3 shows that cross-attention replacement alone accounts for nearly the entire performance gap — adding self-attention replacements on top of cross-attention replacement causes only a marginal further decline (0.010 BLEU on E2G).

**The consequence:** The paper's central conceptual finding — that self-attention is replaceable but cross-attention is not (at least with the tested architectures) — is scientifically interesting but practically limiting. Any real encoder-decoder Transformer for sequence-to-sequence tasks *requires* cross-attention to integrate encoder and decoder representations. A model with fully replaced self-attention but original cross-attention is not an "attentionless Transformer" — it is a hybrid model that still relies on attention for its most critical information-integration step. The claim that "Transformers do not necessarily need to have attention" must be qualified to: "Transformers do not necessarily need self-attention, but cross-attention appears essential for machine translation."

The paper hypothesizes that the failure stems from the increased complexity of cross-attention:

> "The potential reason for this behaviour could be the lack of the expressiveness of the feed-forward network needed to describe the more complex mapping and interaction between sequences used in the cross-attention block." (Discussion)

However, this explanation is purely speculative. The paper does not distinguish between two possible causes: (a) the single-hidden-layer architecture lacks *representational capacity* to model cross-attention (which could be addressed with deeper or wider networks), or (b) cross-attention requires *architectural mechanisms* that feed-forward networks fundamentally cannot provide (e.g., content-based pairwise similarity computation between heterogeneous sequence representations). If (a) is true, the limitation is fixable with better replacement architectures. If (b) is true, cross-attention is genuinely irreplaceable and attention is architecturally necessary for encoder-decoder models.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Table 3 provides the raw BLEU scores for Dec CA (cross-attention only), Full (all attention replaced), and E-D SA (encoder + decoder self-attention only, cross-attention preserved). The Dec CA scores at size L (0.109–0.130 across language pairs) are essentially identical to the Full-L scores (0.105–0.134), confirming cross-attention as the bottleneck. The scaling from Dec CA-M to Dec CA-L shows minimal improvement (e.g., E2G: 0.104 → 0.115, a gain of only 0.011 BLEU for a 4× parameter increase), suggesting that simply adding more parameters to the single-hidden-layer architecture is approaching diminishing returns. The paper does not test deeper networks, different activation functions, or architectures with explicit pairwise interaction modeling for cross-attention — experiments that could distinguish between capacity limitations and architectural necessity.

**Mitigation status:** Appendix D flags cross-attention modeling as a direction for future work: "another potential direction lies in the training of more complex FF networks for the purpose of modeling the cross-attention module of the decoder, as the current shallow network shows that, in contrast to self-attention which they can learn successfully, cross-attention proves to be more challenging due to its complexity." However, no experiments are conducted with multi-layer networks, networks with structured inductive biases for pairwise comparison (e.g., bilinear layers), or attention-like parameterizations embedded within the feed-forward architecture. The limitation is acknowledged but not explored, leaving the critical question — *why* cross-attention is harder to replace — unanswered.

---

### 6.4 The Paper Does Not Demonstrate That "Attentionless Transformers" Cannot Be Trained From Scratch — The Optimization Claim Rests on Assertion, Not Evidence

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper's conclusion makes a strong claim about the relationship between architecture and optimization:

> "These conclusions also point out the deficiencies of the current optimization methods, which are not able to train these 'attentionless Transformers' from scratch but need more advanced techniques, such as knowledge distillation to converge into desired parameter configurations." (Conclusion)

This claim — that feed-forward replacements can *represent* the attention function (as demonstrated by distillation success) but cannot *discover* it through end-to-end training (implying an optimization bottleneck) — is presented as a central insight. However, the paper presents **no experimental evidence** that end-to-end training of attentionless Transformers fails. No experiment compares distillation-trained replacements against identically structured replacements trained from scratch on the translation objective. The claim about "deficiencies of the current optimization methods" is an interpretation of the distillation results, not a demonstrated finding.

**The consequence:** Without this experiment, the paper's conceptual contribution — that architecture matters for optimization rather than representation — remains an untested hypothesis. Alternative explanations for why distillation is necessary are not ruled out:

- **Catastrophic forgetting in end-to-end training:** If the full attentionless Transformer were trained from scratch, the non-replaced components (feed-forward blocks, layer normalization, remaining attention layers) would co-adapt with the replacement networks. The distillation approach freezes the non-replaced components at their teacher-trained values, which may provide a more stable context for the replacements to learn within. End-to-end training might disrupt this stability, causing poor performance that is not an optimization failure per se but an interaction effect.
- **Insufficient training data or budget:** The IWSLT2017 training set (~200K sentence pairs per language direction) may be insufficient to train a 246M-parameter attentionless encoder from scratch, while distillation provides a well-conditioned supervised objective that requires less data. This would be a data efficiency problem, not an optimization deficiency.
- **The replacement architecture is genuinely less expressive:** If end-to-end training fails even with large data and extended training, the failure may indicate that the feed-forward replacement *cannot* represent the attention function, and distillation succeeds only because the teacher provides a shortcut that produces locally good but globally suboptimal behavior.

Any of these explanations would lead to different conclusions than the optimization deficiency hypothesis. Without the end-to-end training experiment, the paper cannot distinguish among them.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** None. The paper does not report any experiments with end-to-end training of attentionless Transformers. The claim appears in the Discussion and Conclusion sections as a reasoned interpretation without supporting data.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not acknowledge this as a gap. The claim is presented as a conclusion from the experimental results rather than as an interpretation requiring verification. No future work is suggested that would test the optimization deficiency hypothesis directly (e.g., end-to-end training experiments with improved optimizers, better initialization, or larger datasets).

### 6.5 Generalization Is Untested Beyond a Single Dataset, Single Model Scale, and Single Task Domain

**The assumption or constraint:** All experiments in the paper use a single base architecture (Transformer with reduced embedding dimension 128, six encoder and six decoder layers), a single dataset (IWSLT2017), a single task domain (machine translation between European languages), and a single model scale. The paper does not test whether the replaceability of attention generalizes across any of these dimensions. The reduction of the embedding dimension from 512 to 128 was motivated by practical constraints:

> "To reduce training times and make testing faster, we reduced the embedding size from 512 to 128." (Appendix B)

However, this reduction has unknown consequences for the generality of the findings. The paper does not argue that a model with embedding dimension 128 is representative of standard Transformer deployments; it was chosen for computational feasibility.

**The consequence:** Several aspects of the findings may be specific to the tested configuration and fail to generalize:

- **Model scale:** The teacher Transformer with embedding dimension 128 is at the small end of what is used in practice. At this scale, the attention layers contain only ~60K parameters and may learn relatively simple input-output mappings that are easier for feed-forward networks to approximate. At larger scales (embedding dimensions 512–2048, 12–96 layers), attention layers contain millions of parameters and may develop more specialized, multi-faceted behaviors that are harder to capture in a single feed-forward mapping. The paper provides no evidence that the replaceability of self-attention scales with model size.

- **Task domain:** Machine translation is a specific sequence-to-sequence task where the input and output share substantial structural similarities (both are natural language sentences with similar lengths, and word-level alignment is often monotonic or near-monotonic for related language pairs like English–French and English–German). Other sequence-to-sequence tasks with different structural properties — such as abstractive summarization (output much shorter than input), code generation (formal syntax, long-range structural dependencies), or speech recognition (continuous inputs, very long sequences) — may impose demands on attention that the current experiments do not probe.

- **Language typology:** All four language pairs involve European languages with relatively similar word orders and morphological complexity. The paper does not test on language pairs with fundamentally different syntactic structures (e.g., English–Japanese, where word order is substantially different and attention must learn long-distance reordering patterns), morphologically rich languages (e.g., Finnish, Turkish), or low-resource language pairs where the training data is sparser.

- **Dataset size:** IWSLT2017 contains approximately 200K training sentence pairs per language direction after length filtering. It is unknown whether the replacement networks require this amount of teacher activation data to converge, or whether they would work with smaller datasets (relevant for low-resource scenarios) or require more data at larger scales.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** None of these dimensions are varied experimentally. The paper uses four language pairs, which provides within-task-domain diversity, but all pairs are European languages from IWSLT2017, all involve the same model scale, and all use the same embedding dimension. The consistency of results across the four language pairs (Table 3) provides weak evidence of generalization within the tested domain, but no evidence beyond it.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not claim generalization beyond the tested configuration and does not discuss these limitations. The scope is implicitly defined by the experimental setup rather than explicitly bounded. The lack of generalization analysis is a standard limitation of papers reporting on a single dataset and model, but given the paper's conceptual ambitions — to draw conclusions about Transformer architectures in general, not just about this specific configuration — the scope limitation is particularly consequential.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper causes a **reframing rather than a paradigm shift**. It does not introduce a new architecture, a new training method, or a new state-of-the-art result. What it does — and does compellingly — is change how the field should think about the necessity of attention in Transformers. Before this work, the question "do Transformers need attention?" was treated as a binary yes-or-no proposition, and research proceeded accordingly: either preserve attention and make it more efficient (linearized, sparse, low-rank approximations) or discard the Transformer entirely in favor of alternative architectures (MLP-Mixer, state-space models, recurrent alternatives). This paper introduces a **third framing**: attention is not monolithic, and its necessity depends on which *type* of attention is being considered. The decomposition into encoder self-attention (replaceable), decoder self-attention (replaceable), and cross-attention (not replaceable with the tested architectures) converts a binary question into a structured one with a sharp empirical boundary.

The magnitude of this reframing is **diagnostic rather than prescriptive**. The paper does not tell practitioners to replace attention with feed-forward networks — the parameter cost makes that impractical. Instead, it provides a methodology for asking *why* attention works: by testing whether a generic function approximator can replicate its behavior when given a clear target through distillation, the paper separates the question of representational capacity from the question of optimization dynamics. This methodological contribution may outlast the specific findings: any architectural component can now be subjected to the same test — extract intermediate activations, train a generic replacement via distillation, plug it back in, and measure the performance gap. Components that survive this test are architecturally necessary; components that don't are optimization conveniences.

The paper also resolves a tension that was latent in the literature but not explicitly articulated. Prior work on attention simplification (linearized attention, sparse patterns, Fourier mixing) implicitly assumed that attention's value lies in its pairwise token interaction mechanism — the query-key-value decomposition and the softmax-normalized dot product. The success of self-attention replacement with feed-forward networks shows that this specific mechanism is *not* what makes self-attention valuable, at least for machine translation at this scale. A generic feed-forward mapping with no pairwise interaction structure, no dynamic weighting, and no content-based similarity computation can learn to produce the same output given the same input. This shifts the locus of attention's value from its computational mechanism to its **optimization properties** — the query-key-value structure creates a loss landscape where gradient descent efficiently discovers good token-interaction patterns, but those patterns themselves are not privileged representations.

The cross-attention failure sharpens this picture considerably. Cross-attention *does* appear to require something that the tested feed-forward architectures cannot provide, even with 683× more parameters and direct distillation supervision. The paper does not identify what this "something" is — it could be the content-based pairwise alignment computation, the ability to dynamically weight encoder tokens differently for each decoder position, or the inductive bias of attending across heterogeneous representation spaces — but the negative result establishes that cross-attention is functionally different from self-attention in a way that matters for replaceability. This boundary calls into question architectures that attempt to eliminate cross-attention entirely (e.g., decoder-only models that rely on long-context self-attention to absorb source information, or models that fuse encoder and decoder representations through fixed-weight mechanisms). If cross-attention provides a functional capability that generic architectures cannot easily replicate, then eliminating it may impose a real performance cost — not just a training difficulty, but a representational one.

For the research community, this work makes several directions more attractive:

- **Intermediate-activation distillation as a diagnostic tool** for probing architectural necessity across model components, tasks, and scales.
- **Characterizing what makes cross-attention special** — whether it's the heterogeneous input spaces, the need for dynamic per-position weighting, or the higher-dimensional concatenated input — to inform both architecture design and attention replacement strategies.
- **Investigating the architecture-optimization coupling** that this paper hypothesizes: if self-attention's primary value is optimization rather than representation, then improvements in optimization methods (better initialization, learned optimizers, training curricula) could progressively reduce the advantage of specialized architectures.

It makes some directions less urgent:

- **Further incremental improvements to attention mechanisms** for self-attention specifically, if the mechanism itself is replaceable with generic alternatives. Research focus should shift toward understanding *why* attention is learnable rather than fine-tuning its specific parameterization.
- **Pure parameter-count comparisons** between attention and alternatives without considering the optimization landscape. The paper's 683× parameter overhead for equivalent self-attention performance suggests that parameter count alone is a poor predictor of architectural value.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. End-to-end training of attentionless Transformers to test the optimization deficiency hypothesis.** The paper's most provocative claim — that feed-forward replacements cannot be trained from scratch and require distillation because of "deficiencies of the current optimization methods" — is currently unsupported by experimental evidence. A direct follow-up would train an attentionless Transformer (with the same feed-forward replacement architectures as the proven ALR networks) end-to-end on IWSLT2017, varying optimizer choice (Adam vs. SGD with momentum vs. LAMB), learning rate schedules (cosine annealing, warmup, cyclic), initialization schemes (Xavier, Kaiming, orthogonal), and training duration. If end-to-end training matches or approaches distillation performance under any configuration, the optimization deficiency hypothesis is falsified, and the barrier is simply that distillation provides a better starting point. If end-to-end training consistently fails even with extensive tuning, the hypothesis is supported, and the result becomes a benchmark for testing improved optimizers designed to discover attention-like solutions in generic architectures. A strong experiment would also measure the loss landscape geometry (Hessian eigenvalue spectra, interpolation between initializations, mode connectivity) for both the end-to-end trained and distillation-trained networks to characterize *how* optimization fails.

**2. Multi-layer feed-forward networks for cross-attention replacement to determine whether the failure is architectural or capacity-limited.** The single-hidden-layer architecture's inability to model cross-attention — even at 41M parameters with diminishing returns from M to L — leaves open whether deeper architectures could succeed. A systematic follow-up would train 2-, 3-, and 4-hidden-layer feed-forward networks for cross-attention replacement, keeping total parameter count matched to the existing ALR-L variant (41M parameters) by reducing width as depth increases. If deeper networks close the cross-attention performance gap, the failure is architectural shallowness, not a fundamental limitation of feed-forward computation. If performance remains at ~40% relative BLEU regardless of depth, cross-attention genuinely requires mechanisms (like explicit pairwise similarity computation) that standard feed-forward networks cannot provide, even with depth. A more ambitious variant would test architectures with explicit pairwise interaction layers — a bilinear layer computing `encoder_reps × W × decoder_reps` for each position pair, followed by a softmax and weighted sum — that provide an attention-like inductive bias without being full multi-head attention. This would identify the minimal architectural mechanism needed to recover cross-attention functionality.

**3. Scaling the replacement study to standard-capacity Transformers to test whether the findings are an artifact of the reduced embedding dimension.** The teacher Transformer's embedding dimension of 128 (reduced from 512) means the attention layers contain only ~60K parameters and operate in a 128-dimensional space. At standard scales (d_model = 512, attention layers with ~1M+ parameters), the attention mechanism may learn more complex, multi-faceted behaviors that are harder for feed-forward networks to capture. A replication of the ALR encoder self-attention replacement at d_model = 512, 768, and 1024 — with the replacement network input dimension scaling accordingly (25,600 at d_model=512 and length 50) — would establish whether the replaceability of self-attention is scale-dependent. The key metric is relative BLEU at matched or controlled parameter ratios: does the feed-forward replacement require proportionally more parameters relative to the attention layer as the embedding dimension grows, or is the overhead roughly constant in relative terms? If the overhead grows superlinearly, this would severely limit the practical relevance of the approach at scale. If the overhead remains constant or shrinks (because the attention layer itself grows with d_model² while the replacement input dimension grows with d_model × L), the approach becomes more viable at larger scales.

**4. Attention-weight analysis of the replacement networks through probing and representational comparison.** The paper demonstrates *that* feed-forward networks can replicate self-attention behavior but provides no insight into *how* they do so. A follow-up would compare the internal representations of ALR replacement networks against the original attention mechanism they replace. Specific experiments: (a) For a given input sentence, extract the original multi-head attention's weight matrix (an L × L matrix of attention scores per head) and attempt to reconstruct it from the replacement network's hidden activations via linear probing. If the attention weights are linearly decodable from the replacement network's hidden layer, the network has learned to implicitly compute attention-like pairwise interactions. (b) Ablate individual neurons in the replacement network's hidden layer and measure the impact on specific token-position interactions — does the network develop specialized neurons for common attention patterns (adjacent tokens, sentence boundaries, syntactic dependencies)? (c) Compare the replacement network's output activations against the teacher attention's output on a per-token, per-position basis to identify systematic errors — are there specific token positions, sentence lengths, or linguistic phenomena where the replacement deviates most from the teacher, and what do these deviations reveal about the limits of the feed-forward approximation? This analysis would move the paper's contribution from "attention can be replaced" to "here is how a generic network approximates attention-like computation."

**5. Cross-linguistic stress testing on typologically diverse language pairs to probe the generalization of self-attention replaceability.** The four IWSLT2017 language pairs all involve European languages with relatively similar word orders (SVO for English and French, SOV/V2 for German) and limited morphological complexity. A follow-up would test ALR encoder self-attention replacement on language pairs that stress attention differently: English–Japanese (drastically different word order requiring long-distance reordering; attention must learn to move verbs from clause-final to clause-initial position), English–Finnish (rich morphological system where grammatical relations are encoded in suffixes rather than word order, potentially reducing the importance of positional attention patterns), and English–Chinese (no overt morphology, highly analytic, where word order and positional relationships are paramount). If self-attention remains replaceable across these typologically diverse pairs, the finding is genuinely robust and attention's value for self-attention is convincingly optimization-related rather than representation-related. If replaceability degrades on pairs requiring complex reordering patterns, it would indicate that the feed-forward network's fixed position-dependent weights struggle with the flexible token reordering that attention's dynamic computation handles naturally — establishing a boundary condition on replaceability.

**6. Joint fine-tuning after replacement to recover cross-attention performance and close the self-attention gap.** The current replacement pipeline trains networks independently on static teacher activations and inserts them without any adaptation to the surrounding layers. This introduces a distribution shift: the replacement network's outputs, while close to the teacher's attention outputs in MSE, contain small errors that propagate through subsequent layers, and the subsequent layers were trained on the teacher's exact outputs, not the replacement's approximate ones. A follow-up would insert the trained ALR replacements into the Transformer and then fine-tune the entire model end-to-end on the translation objective, allowing both the replacement networks and the surrounding components to co-adapt. For self-attention replacements, this might close the residual 1–5% BLEU gap between ALR-L and the teacher. For cross-attention replacements, this is the critical experiment: can end-to-end fine-tuning recover performance that static distillation alone cannot, perhaps by allowing the cross-attention replacement to organize its computation differently while the decoder learns to interpret its outputs? If fine-tuning recovers even 60–70% relative BLEU on cross-attention (compared to the current 40–45% with static replacement), it would fundamentally change the conclusion about cross-attention's replaceability. If fine-tuning provides no improvement, the static replacement result is confirmed as a representational ceiling.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**1. Diagnostic toolkit for architecture design decisions.** The paper's methodology — extract intermediate activations, train a generic replacement network via distillation, measure the performance gap — is immediately useful as a **component importance scoring tool** in architecture design workflows. When designing a new sequence model, practitioners can systematically test which proposed components are genuinely essential: replace each candidate component with a generic feed-forward network trained via activation distillation, and measure the impact on task performance. Components whose removal causes a large, irrecoverable performance gap (like cross-attention in this paper) are architecturally necessary; components whose removal causes a small or recoverable gap (like self-attention) are optimization conveniences that could potentially be simplified, replaced, or removed in future iterations. This changes architecture design from an intuition-driven or ablation-driven process to a principled diagnostic one, with the specific benefit of **distinguishing optimization-motivated design from representation-motivated design** — a distinction that is typically conflated in architecture papers.

**2. Prioritization of optimization research over architecture research for self-attention mechanisms.** The paper's finding that self-attention can be replaced by a 41M-parameter feed-forward network achieving within 1–2% relative BLEU of the original — combined with the stated (though unverified) claim that end-to-end training fails — provides a concrete signal to research labs allocating effort between architecture innovation and optimization innovation. If self-attention's value is primarily in making the desired computation *discoverable* by gradient descent rather than in representing it uniquely, then **improving optimization algorithms may yield larger gains than further architectural refinements of attention**. Specific benefit: a team deciding between a project on "improved multi-head attention variants" vs. "training curricula for generic architectures on sequence tasks" now has evidence suggesting the latter may have higher ceiling. The practical takeaway is not that attention should be replaced (the parameter cost makes that infeasible), but that investment in understanding *why* attention trains well could unlock strategies for training simpler architectures that currently fail.

**3. Lightweight attention-free encoders for resource-constrained sequence tasks with fixed maximum input length.** While the full attentionless Transformer fails due to cross-attention collapse, the encoder-only self-attention replacement (ALR) achieves competitive performance. For tasks that require only an encoder — sentence classification, semantic similarity, embedding extraction, or as the encoding half of a retrieval-augmented pipeline where cross-attention is handled by a separate mechanism — the ALR replacement demonstrates that a **feed-forward encoder can substitute for a self-attention encoder** with minimal BLEU loss at the cost of increased parameters. In deployment scenarios where maximum sequence length is known and bounded (e.g., processing fixed-length chunks of text, or applications where inputs are naturally short like search queries or social media posts), the fixed-size input limitation is acceptable, and the feed-forward encoder provides a simpler computational graph — no dynamic attention score computation, no pairwise operations, just a series of matrix multiplications with fixed dimensions — that can be optimized for specific hardware (embedded devices, FPGAs, inference accelerators). The specific benefit is a reduction in **architectural complexity and implementation effort** for the encoder component, at a known and bounded parameter cost. However, this application is speculative: the paper provides no latency, throughput, or hardware efficiency measurements to quantify the practical benefit.

**4. Targeted cross-attention improvement as the highest-leverage investment for encoder-decoder translation models.** The paper's sharp performance boundary — self-attention is replaceable, cross-attention is not — directly implies that **cross-attention is the most critical attention mechanism in encoder-decoder architectures** and should be the primary target for optimization, improvement, or careful preservation. For teams building encoder-decoder translation models, this finding justifies disproportionate investment in cross-attention quality: better cross-attention training (improved alignment supervision, larger batch sizes for more stable key-value statistics, specialized initialization), cross-attention-specific architecture improvements (deeper cross-attention relative to self-attention, dedicated cross-attention heads with higher capacity), and cross-attention preservation during model compression (pruning or quantizing self-attention more aggressively than cross-attention). The paper provides numbers to ground this prioritization: replacing all self-attention in both encoder and decoder (E-D SA-L) drops BLEU by only ~2–4% relative, while replacing only cross-attention (Dec CA-L) drops BLEU by ~55–60% relative. The cross-attention mechanism is responsible for nearly all the non-redundant value of attention in this architecture.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not propose a method that practitioners would choose over alternatives for deployment. The replacement networks are 167–683× larger than the attention layers they replace, cannot handle variable-length sequences, and fail catastrophically on cross-attention — making them strictly inferior to standard attention for any practical machine translation system. The paper explicitly states that it "does not introduce a competitive advantage over established methods" and frames the contribution as conceptual analysis. There is no realistic deployment scenario where the feed-forward replacements described in this paper should be preferred over original attention mechanisms for sequence-to-sequence tasks.

The decision framework the paper implicitly provides is not about choosing replacements over attention, but about **choosing where to invest research effort** based on what the replacement experiments reveal. The relevant tradeoff is: for encoder-decoder architectures, self-attention improvement research has lower ceiling than cross-attention improvement research, because self-attention's function can be approximated by generic architectures (suggesting it serves primarily an optimization role) while cross-attention's function resists such approximation (suggesting it serves a fundamental representational role). However, this is an inference from the paper's results rather than a tradeoff the paper itself articulates or validates, so a formal "prefer X when Y" matrix would exceed what the evidence supports.
