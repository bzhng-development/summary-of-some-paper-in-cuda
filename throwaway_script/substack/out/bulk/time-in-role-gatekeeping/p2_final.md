Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman article (answering a reader question on bypassing time-in-role promotion gatekeeping):

**Meta’s lack of time-in-role restrictions enabled fast growth**

The core premise Ryan establishes is that a company’s philosophy on time-in-role gatekeeping is one of the biggest factors in promotion velocity — and it’s something you can evaluate before joining. At Instagram (Meta), he emphasizes, “you can get promoted as long as you are performing consistently at the next level.” There were no hard restrictions blocking someone based on how long they’d held their current title. This policy was foundational to his own trajectory: going from Junior (IC3) to Staff (IC6) in three years, a pace he flatly states “would not have been possible at many other companies.”

**Extreme outliers reveal what’s structurally possible**

He points out that his case, while rare, isn’t the ceiling. He’s witnessed a few engineers go from Junior (IC3) to Principal (IC8) in five years at Meta — a trajectory he calls “insane given that scope at the next level is exponentially rarer at the highest levels.” The takeaway isn’t that this is a likely outcome for everyone, but that **“it is possible at the right companies.”** The existence of these outliers demonstrates that when structural gatekeeping is absent, individual performance and scope can compound unusually fast — faster even than his own three-year IC3-to-IC6 sprint.

**The contrast with other big tech companies**

Ryan contrasts Meta’s approach with anecdotes from peers at other major firms where time-in-role friction is real, even when performance isn’t the issue. At Google, he describes a “high-performing friend who got to Senior (L5) in 2 years” but has since been “blocked on Staff (L6) promotion for years even though his manager said he’s performing at the next level.” At Amazon, the Senior (SDE III) promotion is “notorious for being difficult to achieve.” These aren’t presented as exhaustive research but as concrete data points to triangulate which companies actually let performance dictate pace — and which ones impose invisible ceilings that can stall recognized high-performers.

**Choose your company wisely if velocity matters to you**

The actionable frame closes tight: **“If you want to get promoted fast, choose your company wisely.”** He frames the alternative as a motivation tax — it’s “demotivating if you can’t get promoted even if you’re already doing what is expected at the next level.” For engineers evaluating a potential employer, his practical advice is to check Blind or talk directly to current engineers to surface whether time-in-role restrictions exist before signing on. The underlying principle: promotion velocity is often gated less by your own capability and more by a structural variable you can screen for in advance.
