Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (went from L4 to Staff Engineer / L6 at Meta in three years):

**The two-part speed equation: volume and thinking ahead**

Ryan breaks his rapid Staff promotion into two components. The first is obvious: he averaged 50-60 hours per week, which let him run multiple workstreams simultaneously. He often had one or two extra projects in flight outside his main work, giving his manager more content for promotion packets than a typical tenure would produce. A concrete example: his Senior (L5) promotion got a boost from an extra workstream that turned into a cross-team initiative with massive impact, on top of his main project work that was already at L5 expectations. His Staff (L6) promotion was driven by a main workstream he found by working extra hours on top of his normal commitments. "Working harder can help you build a track record faster, but that's obvious compared to the next bit."

**Thinking two levels ahead**

The less obvious piece is a model he calls **thinking two levels ahead**. Every promotion has two phases—a growth phase where you grow into the next level's behaviors, and a sustain phase where you keep up those behaviors to build the necessary track record. The standard path alternates them sequentially across promotions, but that's slower than it needs to be. Ryan's faster way: skip the sustain phase. While waiting for your N+1 promotion to land, start growing toward N+2 behaviors. This builds a track record for the level-after-next faster.

He executed this concretely at Meta. Starting from L4: in his first half he did L4 work and learned L5 behaviors; in his second half he did L5 work and, once his manager confirmed those behaviors looked good, started thinking about L6 expectations (his L5 promotion was blocked that half since Meta paused promotions during COVID); in his third half at L4 he was already doing L6 work when the L5 promotion landed; his fourth and fifth halves at L5 were pure L6 work, and the L6 promotion came through. This gave him a **two-half head start** on the Staff packet.

A byproduct he values: he never stressed about immediate promotions. "I was already aiming at N+2, so any N+1 delays like the COVID-induced one, didn't phase me. This longer-term thinking kept me focused on growing my skills and behaviors." The cautionary note is that you need to be solid on the N+1 behaviors first—a Junior (L3) "is going to struggle with team-level influence if their engineering fundamentals aren't strong."

Ryan also credits Meta's policy of rewarding high performers without strict time-based blocking, noting that other big tech companies like Google block promotions based on time even when expectations are met—which is why people often suggest job hopping as a faster career growth path.

---

Ryan's **thinking two levels ahead** model is, at bottom, a strategy for compressing the career timeline by refusing to coast. Where the standard path treats each level's sustain phase as a necessary waiting room, he treats it as dead time to be eliminated—redirecting that energy into N+2 growth. His high-volume work habits (the "obvious" half) supply the raw material, but the framework is what turns extra hours into a coherent acceleration rather than burnout. It's a playbook that assumes a permissive promotion culture (no time gates), yet the core insight—that you can parallelize the *behavioral growth* across levels instead of processing them serially—travels beyond any single company's ladder.
