## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **SVG2**, a training-free sparse attention framework for accelerating Diffusion Transformer (DiT)-based video generation that addresses two shortcomings of existing sparse methods—inaccurate critical token identification and excessive computation waste—through **semantic-aware permutation**, which clusters and reorders tokens via k-means clustering on Q/K/V activations rather than by position. Evaluated on HunyuanVideo-T2V-13B and Wan 2.1 (both 720p), SVG2 achieves up to 2.30× and 1.89× end-to-end speedup on a single H100 while maintaining PSNR of up to 30.452 and 25.808, respectively, and occupies the Pareto frontier on the quality-efficiency trade-off curve across all density budgets. The framework establishes that semantic-coherent token clustering followed by permutation into contiguous layouts enables sparse attention to approach oracle-level recall without training, but only when the identification step operates on semantically grouped blocks rather than position-based blocks and when dynamic block-size kernels eliminate the padding waste imposed by fixed-size tensor core requirements.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: DiT Attention Is Quadratically Expensive and Sparsity Is Under-Exploited

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **the attention mechanism in Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) for video generation dominates runtime due to quadratic scaling, yet existing sparse attention methods leave substantial performance on the table because they misidentify which tokens matter and waste computation on padding.** This matters because DiT-based video generation has become the dominant paradigm for high-quality video synthesis, but its computational cost renders deployment impractical for most real-world applications.

The scale of the problem is concrete and severe. The authors report in Section 3.1 that generating a single 33-frame video with HunyuanVideo-T2V-13B at 720p resolution requires more than **80% of total end-to-end time to be spent on 3D spatio-temporal attention alone**, with generation taking nearly an hour on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. This is not a marginal inefficiency — attention is the overwhelming bottleneck, and any meaningful acceleration must address it directly.

The theoretical root cause is well-understood: self-attention computes an attention matrix $P = \text{softmax}(QK^T / \sqrt{d})$ that scales quadratically with sequence length. For video DiTs, the sequence length is enormous because it spans both spatial tokens (typically ~3,600 per frame for 720p after 3D-VAE encoding) and temporal tokens (multiple frames), producing sequence lengths on the order of 75,000–118,000 tokens. This quadratic cost grows even more punishing as video resolution, frame count, or model scale increases.

### Why This Problem Matters Beyond Benchmarks

**Real-world deployment is currently infeasible.** The authors cite that generating a five-second video on HunyuanVideo takes "nearly an hour" on an A100. For any interactive or production application — video editing tools, real-time content creation, game asset generation, or personalized video synthesis — latencies measured in tens of minutes are non-starters. Even for batch generation use cases, the cost per video is prohibitively high at scale.

**Sparsity offers an enormous but untapped opportunity.** Section 3.1 presents a striking quantitative finding: in an oracle setting where the true attention scores are known in advance, **only 13% of attention computations are needed to achieve 95% attention recall**, maintaining a PSNR of 27 while theoretically providing up to 2× end-to-end speedup (Figure 3). This means the attention mechanism is encoding far more redundancy than necessary — the vast majority of token-token interactions contribute negligibly to the final output. If we could perfectly identify which 13% of interactions matter, we would slash attention cost with minimal quality degradation.

**The gap between oracle and practical methods is wide.** The same Figure 3 reveals the core tension that motivates this paper: existing sparse attention methods (SVG, SpargeAttention) achieve dramatically lower attention recall than the oracle policy at any given density budget. At 37.5% density, the oracle achieves roughly 98% recall, while existing methods sit closer to 75–80%. This gap represents **lost speedup potential** — if practical methods could approach oracle-level identification, video generation would become significantly faster at equivalent quality.

### Prior Approaches: Static Sparsity, Dynamic Identification, and Their Weaknesses

The paper situates prior work in two broad categories (Section 2): static and dynamic sparse attention for video DiTs, plus a parallel line of linear attention and state-space models that change the fundamental architecture rather than sparsifying the existing attention operation.

#### Static Sparse Attention: Predefined Patterns That Ignore Content

Static methods apply a fixed sparsity pattern regardless of the actual attention distribution. **Sparse VideoGen (SVG)** [4] uses a sliding window approach where each token attends only to spatially and temporally nearby tokens, and **Sliding Tile Attention** [8] follows a similar logic. The rationale is that at early denoising steps, where the video content is mostly noise, nearby tokens are the most relevant. 

The fundamental weakness is **inflexibility**. Attention patterns in video DiTs are highly diverse across layers, heads, and denoising timesteps — some heads form global semantic associations, others track local motion, and still others establish cross-frame correspondences. A fixed pattern optimized for one regime (e.g., early denoising steps dominated by local structure formation) will misallocate compute in another (e.g., later steps where global coherence matters). The paper's own baseline evaluation confirms this: SVG achieves decent but suboptimal results compared to dynamic methods, particularly when a higher quality-efficiency trade-off is required (Table 1: SVG achieves PSNR of 24.059 on Wan 2.1 I2V while SVG2 achieves 26.562 at comparable density).

#### Dynamic Sparse Attention: Runtime Identification with Coarse-Grained Blocks

Dynamic methods add an identification step that estimates attention scores cheaply, then allocate compute to the highest-scoring token interactions. This is the category that SVG2 belongs to and improves upon. The key prior methods here are:

**SpargeAttention** [9] and **XAttention** [10] both use a two-stage approach: first, cluster consecutive tokens into fixed-size blocks (e.g., 128 query tokens, 64 key tokens), use mean pooling or max pooling to create a single representative activation for each block, compute approximate attention scores between these block representatives, and then select the top-scoring blocks for full sparse attention computation. This reduces the identification overhead to less than 1% of full attention computation.

The approach seems reasonable — aggregate, estimate, then select — but the paper identifies **two distinct failure modes** that combine to produce the wide oracle gap observed in Figure 3.

#### Failure Mode 1: Inaccurate Identification from Position-Based Clustering

This is the first core critique. When blocks are formed by grouping consecutive tokens (position-based clustering), there is **no guarantee that tokens within the same block share semantic similarity**. The paper provides a concrete example in Section 3.2: in a video frame, an apple and a cake that happen to be physically adjacent would be grouped into the same block, despite having entirely unrelated visual semantics. Their latent space activations would differ dramatically.

Why does this hurt identification? The block's representative activation — whether computed by mean pooling (SpargeAttention) or max pooling (used in other work) — becomes a **degraded, unrepresentative signal** when the constituent tokens diverge in semantics. The pooled representation of an apple-and-cake block is not a good proxy for either the apple or the cake individually. When this degraded representation is used to estimate attention scores against query blocks, the resulting selection of "critical" key blocks will be inaccurate — the system may ignore blocks that actually contain critical tokens and select blocks that don't.

This problem is visualized in Figure 4(b): position-based clustering mixes tokens with different attention patterns within the same block. The block's pooled representation fails to capture that some of its constituent tokens are highly attended-to (critical) while others are not. Consequently, the block may be de-selected entirely, causing critical tokens to be dropped and attention recall to suffer.

**Critically, the problem compounds with block size.** Larger blocks reduce identification overhead (fewer representatives to compute), but increase the semantic heterogeneity within each block, further degrading identification accuracy. This creates a tension: you can have cheap but inaccurate identification (large blocks) or more accurate but more expensive identification (smaller blocks), but position-based clustering cannot give you both.

#### Failure Mode 2: Computation Waste from Scattered Critical Tokens

Even if the identification step were perfect — if you could somehow magically know exactly which individual tokens are critical — existing methods would **still fail to achieve the theoretical speedup** promised by the oracle policy. This is the second core critique, and it is a hardware-algorithm mismatch that the paper argues has been largely overlooked.

The issue stems from **GPU tensor core requirements**. NVIDIA tensor cores, which provide the bulk of matrix multiplication throughput, operate on fixed minimum dimensions. For instance, the A100 requires a minimum matrix multiplication shape of $16 \times 16 \times 8$ (Section 3.2). In practice, this means attention kernels batch tokens into fixed-size blocks (e.g., 64, 128, or 256 tokens) and compute full attention within those blocks using dense matrix multiplication.

The problem: **critical tokens are scattered**, not contiguous. Since criticality is determined by semantic relevance (which tokens have high attention scores), and semantically relevant tokens need not be physically adjacent in the sequence, the set of critical tokens tends to be distributed across the token sequence rather than clustered. When these scattered critical tokens are assigned to fixed-size computation blocks (which span contiguous positions), most blocks will contain a **mixture of critical and non-critical tokens**. Because the tensor core must process the entire block (padding non-critical tokens into the computation), substantial FLOPs are wasted on computing attention for non-critical tokens.

The paper quantifies this waste: "our empirical evaluations show that up to **80% of computation can be wasted** on non-critical tokens" (Section 1). Figure 4(c) illustrates the mechanism: even when critical tokens are correctly identified (highlighted), each $4 \times 4$ computation block processes a mix of critical and non-critical tokens. The "effective compute" — the fraction of total FLOPs that actually process critical-token interactions — can be as low as 26.4% even when the identification step successfully captures 89% attention recall.

**This is fundamentally a layout problem, not an identification problem.** The GPU hardware demands contiguous computation, but the natural distribution of critical tokens is scattered. Existing methods accept this waste as inevitable, padding non-critical tokens to fill fixed-size blocks. The paper's key insight — which we will explore fully in the Technical Approach — is that this layout can be **actively reshaped through permutation** to align with hardware requirements.

#### The Combined Effect

These two failure modes are multiplicative in their impact. Inaccurate identification means you select the wrong tokens (missing some critical ones, including some non-critical ones). Computation waste means even the correctly identified critical tokens are processed inefficiently, with substantial FLOPs burned on non-critical padding. Together, they explain the wide gap between existing methods and the oracle policy observed in Figure 3.

### Parallel Approaches: Linear Attention and Architectural Modification

The paper also situates its work relative to an orthogonal line of research that replaces quadratic attention with linear-complexity alternatives (Section 2). This includes:

- **Linear attention** methods [30, 31, 32] that reformulate attention to avoid the $O(N^2)$ softmax computation
- **State space models** (Mamba [33], Mamba2 [34]) and hybrid architectures (Matten [35], LinGen [36], M4V [37])
- **Compressed latent spaces** that reduce sequence length before attention (SANA [40, 41, 42], DC-AE [43, 44, 45])
- **Test-time training** approaches (TTT [39]) that replace attention with learned recurrent dynamics

The paper acknowledges this line of work as important, particularly for long video generation where even sparse attention might eventually become costly, but positions SVG2 as complementary rather than competing. The key distinction: linear attention methods require architectural changes and retraining, while SVG2 is **training-free** — it can be applied to any existing DiT model without modifying weights or fine-tuning. This makes SVG2 immediately deployable on state-of-the-art pretrained models, which is a significant practical advantage.

### How SVG2 Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution not as proposing yet another sparse attention method, but as **diagnosing and fixing the two root causes** of the oracle gap in existing dynamic methods. The solution is conceptually elegant: if position-based clustering causes inaccurate identification, use **semantic-based clustering** instead. If scattered critical tokens cause computation waste, **permute tokens into a contiguous layout** before sparse computation.

Both fixes are unified under a single mechanism: **semantic-aware permutation via k-means clustering**. By clustering query and key tokens independently based on their activation similarity (measured in the latent space), the method achieves two objectives simultaneously:

1. **Accurate identification**: Each cluster contains semantically similar tokens, so the cluster centroid is a high-quality representative that enables precise attention score estimation and thus accurate critical token selection.
2. **Densified computation**: Because semantically similar tokens tend to share criticality (they are either all relevant to a query or all irrelevant), permuting tokens into cluster-contiguous blocks means each computation block is either mostly critical or mostly non-critical, dramatically reducing the mixed-block waste.

This is visualized in Figure 4(d): after semantic-aware permutation, the attention map shows dense blocks of interaction between relevant query-key cluster pairs, with clean boundaries that map naturally to efficient tensor core computation.

**The relationship to prior work is additive, not adversarial.** SVG2 does not replace the identification-and-selection framework used by SpargeAttention and XAttention — it improves the clustering step before identification and the layout step before sparse computation. The centroid-based Top-p selection mechanism (Section 4.2) still estimates attention scores and selects critical clusters, but it does so on semantically coherent groups rather than arbitrary position-based blocks.

### The MMInference Connection and Key Distinction

The paper acknowledges MMInference [12] as the closest prior work to its permutation approach. MMInference introduces modality-aware permutation for multi-modal LLMs, reordering tokens by modality type (text, image, etc.) before sparse attention. This shares the idea that permutation can improve sparse computation efficiency.

However, the paper draws a critical distinction: MMInference's permutation is **rule-based** (modality type is a discrete, predefined category), while SVG2's permutation is **data-driven** (clustering based on learned activation similarity). In video DiTs, there is no modality boundary to exploit — all tokens come from the same visual modality — so a rule-based approach would be ineffective. The semantics emerge from the model's internal representations, not from predefined categories, necessitating the k-means clustering approach.

### The Unresolved Deployment Challenge: Dynamic Budget Allocation

A final motivation the paper addresses is practical: existing methods typically require manual specification of the sparsity ratio (e.g., "keep the top 30% of blocks"). This is problematic because:

- Different videos, prompts, and denoising steps may require different sparsity levels to maintain quality
- The relationship between sparsity and quality is not linear or easily predictable
- Hand-tuning a fixed sparsity ratio per model is labor-intensive and fragile

SVG2 addresses this through its **Top-p dynamic budget control** (Section 4.2), which selects critical clusters until their cumulative estimated attention score reaches a target threshold $p$. This means the compute budget adapts automatically to the input — easy-to-generate content uses fewer tokens, complex content uses more — without manual tuning.

### Summary of the Motivational Framework

The paper's motivation can be understood as a chain of observations leading to a specific intervention:

1. **Observation**: Attention is the bottleneck in video DiTs (80%+ of runtime)
2. **Observation**: Attention is highly sparse (13% density achieves 95% recall under oracle)
3. **Observation**: Existing sparse methods fall far short of oracle (Figure 3 gap)
4. **Diagnosis**: Two root causes — inaccurate identification (position-based blocks degrade representation) and computation waste (scattered critical tokens force padding)
5. **Intervention**: Semantic-aware permutation via k-means solves both simultaneously by creating semantically coherent blocks that enable precise identification and naturally dense computation layouts
6. **Practical requirement**: Dynamic budget allocation (Top-p) for deployment without manual tuning

This framework directly sets up the technical approach described in Section 4, where each component (k-means clustering, centroid-based estimation, permutation for dense layout, dynamic kernel implementation) maps to one of the diagnosed problems.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

**What the system is:** SVG2 is a training-free, plugin-style acceleration framework that you insert into an existing DiT-based video generation pipeline — it wraps the standard self-attention operation, adding a clustering-and-permutation step before attention and a reverse-permutation step after, without modifying any model weights or requiring any fine-tuning.

**What problem it solves and the shape of the solution:** The system addresses the fact that existing sparse attention methods waste compute on two fronts: they misidentify which tokens are critical because they group tokens by position rather than by semantic similarity, and they burn FLOPs on padding scattered critical tokens to fit fixed-size GPU computation blocks. The solution is to **reorder the token sequence based on semantic similarity before sparse attention**, achieving two things simultaneously — accurate critical-token identification using cluster centroids rather than pooled positional blocks, and a dense computation layout where each GPU block is either mostly critical or mostly non-critical, so tensor cores process useful work rather than padding.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components that operate per attention head at each transformer layer during each denoising step:

1. **k-means Clustering Module** — takes the query, key, and value activations (Q, K, V) as input and independently groups tokens into semantically coherent clusters based on their activation similarity in latent space. Outputs cluster assignments and centroid vectors for queries and keys separately. Values inherit the key clustering for mathematical consistency.

2. **Semantic-Aware Permutation** — uses the cluster assignments to physically reorder tokens in memory so that tokens within the same cluster become contiguous. Produces permuted Q′, K′, and V′ tensors where semantic neighbors are physically adjacent, enabling efficient block-wise computation.

3. **Centroid-Based Top-p Selection Module** — estimates attention scores between query clusters and key clusters using only the centroids (not the full token-token attention matrix), then selects which key clusters are critical by summing their estimated importance until a cumulative probability threshold $p$ is reached. Outputs a binary mask over key clusters indicating which to compute and which to skip.

4. **Dynamic Block-Sparse Attention Kernel** — takes the permuted Q′, the selected subset of K′ and V′, and cluster size information, then executes sparse attention using a custom CUDA kernel that handles variable-sized blocks (unlike standard kernels that require fixed block sizes). Computes attention only for selected query-key cluster pairs.

5. **Reverse Permutation Module** — after sparse attention produces the permuted output O′, applies the inverse permutation to restore the original token order, ensuring the output O matches what dense attention would have produced (up to the approximation error from sparsification).

**Information flow per denoising step per attention head:**

Input Q, K, V tensors → k-means clusters Q into $C_q$ groups and K/V into $C_k$ groups, producing centroids and cluster assignments → tokens are permuted into cluster-contiguous order → centroid-based attention score estimation identifies which key clusters are critical for each query cluster → Top-p threshold selects a subset of key clusters → sparse attention kernel computes attention only for selected cluster pairs, using dynamic block sizes matching actual cluster dimensions → output is reverse-permuted back to original token order → result feeds into the rest of the DiT layer (feed-forward, normalization, etc.).

A critical architectural note: **k-means centroids are cached across denoising steps** to reduce clustering overhead. The first denoising step runs full k-means++ initialization; subsequent steps reuse the previous step's converged centroids as initialization, converging in far fewer iterations. This cache is maintained per-head, per-layer across the denoising trajectory.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the semantic-aware permutation mechanism (k-means clustering and physical reordering) because it is the foundation on which everything else builds — identification accuracy and computation densification both flow from how tokens are grouped and arranged.
- **Second**, the mathematical proof of equivalence under permutation, because readers need to understand WHY permuting Q, K, and V can produce the identical attention output as the unpermuted version — this is the guarantee that makes SVG2 a safe drop-in replacement.
- **Third**, the centroid-based Top-p selection mechanism, because once tokens are semantically clustered, you need a way to estimate which clusters matter without computing the full attention matrix — this is the efficiency enabler.
- **Fourth**, the dynamic block-sparse attention kernel, because this is where the theoretical gains from permutation translate into actual wall-clock speedup on GPU hardware — understanding the kernel design explains why fixed-block kernels waste compute.
- **Fifth**, the k-means efficiency optimizations (centroid caching), because clustering would otherwise introduce prohibitive overhead that could erase the attention speedup — this is the practical engineering that makes the approach deployable.
- **Sixth**, key design decisions summarized with their justifications, because the configuration choices (cluster counts $C_q = 100$, $C_k = 500$, independent query/key clustering, warmup steps) are non-obvious and each has a specific rationale grounded in experimental evidence.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems paper with an algorithmic insight**: the core idea is that semantic-aware permutation via k-means clustering can simultaneously solve the identification accuracy and computation waste problems that prevent existing sparse attention methods from approaching oracle-level efficiency. The contribution is not a new attention mechanism, but a novel pre-processing and layout optimization that makes existing block-sparse attention kernels dramatically more effective for video DiTs.

#### Semantic-Aware Permutation via k-means Clustering

SVG2's central mechanism is **semantic-aware permutation**: clustering query and key tokens into groups based on their activation similarity in the latent space, then physically reordering tokens so that semantically similar tokens become adjacent in memory. This serves two distinct purposes that map directly to the two failure modes diagnosed in Section 3.2: it improves centroid representation quality for accurate critical token identification, and it densifies the computation layout so that GPU blocks contain mostly critical or mostly non-critical tokens rather than mixtures.

**Independent per-head, per-layer clustering.** For each attention head at each transformer layer, SVG2 independently applies k-means clustering to the query tokens and the key tokens. Specifically, given query activations $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{N_q \times d}$ and key activations $K \in \mathbb{R}^{N_k \times d}$, where $N_q$ and $N_k$ are the number of query and key tokens (typically equal in self-attention) and $d$ is the per-head dimension, SVG2 runs k-means to produce $C_q$ query clusters $Q_1, Q_2, \ldots, Q_{C_q}$ and $C_k$ key clusters $K_1, K_2, \ldots, K_{C_k}$. Value tokens share the key clustering (they are grouped identically to key tokens) to maintain the mathematical guarantee of output equivalence under permutation (proven below). The cluster counts are set to $C_q = 100$ and $C_k = 500$, with the rationale for this asymmetry discussed in Section 5.3 and Appendix D: the attention kernel's hardware efficiency degrades when $C_q$ exceeds ~200 because query clusters smaller than 64 tokens on average under-utilize tensor core dimensions, whereas key cluster count can scale to thousands without similar degradation.

**Why k-means and not some other clustering method?** The paper does not provide an ablation comparing clustering algorithms. However, the choice of k-means is motivated by several properties that matter for this application: (1) it operates directly on the Euclidean distances between activation vectors in the latent space, which is a natural similarity metric for the representations learned by the transformer; (2) it produces hard cluster assignments that enable clean, non-overlapping blocks for permutation — unlike soft clustering or Gaussian mixture models, k-means gives each token a single cluster ID, making the permutation step deterministic and the block boundaries unambiguous; (3) it is computationally well-understood with GPU-optimized implementations available (the paper uses k-means++ initialization [63]); (4) the number of clusters can be specified directly, giving explicit control over the trade-off between cluster granularity and block size. The paper does not consider alternatives such as locality-sensitive hashing or learned clustering, likely because k-means is sufficient and the focus is on the system-level integration rather than the clustering algorithm itself.

**Semantic similarity means activation similarity, not pixel similarity.** A crucial nuance: when the paper says "semantic similarity," it means similarity in the model's learned representation space, not similarity in the original pixel or feature space that a human would recognize. Two patches in a video frame that appear visually distinct to a human observer may be represented by similar activation vectors if the model has learned to treat them as functionally equivalent for the generation task. Conversely, visually similar patches may have divergent representations if they serve different roles in the attention computation. The clustering operates on the model's internal geometry, not on external semantic labels. This is why the approach is data-driven and general: it leverages whatever similarity structure the pretrained model has already learned, without imposing external definitions of what counts as "similar."

**The physical permutation step.** After clustering produces cluster assignments for each token, SVG2 constructs permutation matrices $\pi_q \in \mathbb{R}^{N_q \times N_q}$ and $\pi_k \in \mathbb{R}^{N_k \times N_k}$. These are binary matrices with exactly one 1 per row and column (permutation matrices satisfying $\pi_q \pi_q^T = I$ and $\pi_k \pi_k^T = I$) that reorder tokens so that all tokens in cluster 1 come first, followed by all tokens in cluster 2, and so on. The actual implementation does not construct these matrices explicitly (that would be $O(N^2)$ memory); instead, it creates index arrays that specify the new order, then uses gather/scatter operations to physically rearrange the tensors in GPU memory. The permuted tensors are $Q' = \pi_q Q$, $K' = \pi_k K$, and $V' = \pi_k V$. Note that $K$ and $V$ share the same permutation $\pi_k$ — this shared permutation is essential for the mathematical equivalence proof below.

**What the permutation physically achieves.** Before permutation, tokens with similar semantics may be scattered across the sequence. For example, in a video frame, all the "sky" patches may appear at positions 150, 723, 1401, and 2890 — interspersed with ground, object, and motion-boundary tokens. After permutation, all sky patches are grouped into a contiguous block. This means:
- A GPU block that processes tokens 0–127 of the permuted sequence processes mostly sky tokens, not a mixture of sky, ground, and object.
- The cluster centroid (mean of all sky token activations) is a high-quality representative of sky-like semantics, because every token in the cluster genuinely shares similar activation patterns.
- When the centroid-based identification step (Section 4.2) estimates attention scores, the estimate for "does this query cluster attend to the sky key cluster?" is based on a clean, un-noisy signal.

The contrast with position-based clustering is stark: in position-based methods, a block spanning tokens 128–255 contains whatever happened to be physically adjacent in the original frame — likely a jumble of different objects, textures, and background elements. The pooled representation of that block is an uninformative average of unrelated semantics, making attention score estimation unreliable.

**Hardware-aware cluster size considerations.** The cluster counts ($C_q = 100$, $C_k = 500$) are not arbitrarily chosen. They are constrained by the tensor core requirement that each matrix multiplication operand have dimensions that are multiples of the MMA instruction size. For the wgmma m64n64k16 instruction used on H100, each query cluster should ideally have at least 64 tokens to fully utilize one warp's computation. With $N_q \approx 75,000$ for Wan 2.1, setting $C_q = 100$ gives an average cluster size of ~750 tokens — well above the 64-token minimum, ensuring high utilization. Setting $C_q > 200$ would push average cluster sizes below 375, and many clusters would fall below 64 tokens, causing under-utilization as the kernel would need to pad. For key clusters, $C_k = 500$ gives ~150 tokens per cluster on average, and because the attention kernel's efficiency is less sensitive to key dimension (see Appendix D, Figures 11–12), this higher cluster count is acceptable and beneficial for finer-grained identification.

**Independent query and key clustering is necessary.** The paper reports in Appendix D.3 that using the same clustering for queries and keys — e.g., applying $\pi_q$ to both, $\pi_k$ to both, or clustering the hidden states before the QKV projection and using the same permutation for all three — all produce **worse PSNR at higher density** than independent clustering. The Average Adjusted Rand Index between independently computed query clusters and key clusters is 0.345, indicating that the natural cluster structures of queries and keys are substantially different. This makes intuitive sense: the query represents "what information is this token looking for?" while the key represents "what information does this token contain?" A token could be part of a "sky" key cluster (it contains sky information) while belonging to a different query cluster (it is looking for ground-plane context to establish spatial relationships). Forcing them to share the same clustering would destroy this asymmetry, degrading the expressiveness of the attention approximation.

#### Mathematical Equivalence Under Permutation

A reader might reasonably worry: if we physically reorder tokens, doesn't the attention output change? The paper provides a concise proof that the output is mathematically identical before and after permutation, assuming the full (non-sparse) attention is computed. This proof is critical because it establishes that **any quality degradation from SVG2 comes purely from the sparsification (skipping some key clusters), not from the permutation itself**. The permutation is lossless; the sparsification is where the approximation error enters.

**The attention operation in standard form.** Standard self-attention with a single head computes:

$$O = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) V$$

where $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{N_q \times d}$, $K \in \mathbb{R}^{N_k \times d}$, $V \in \mathbb{R}^{N_k \times d}$, and the softmax is applied row-wise (along the key dimension). The output $O \in \mathbb{R}^{N_q \times d}$ contains, for each query token, a weighted sum of value tokens with weights proportional to exponentiated query-key dot products.

**The permuted attention operation.** After applying permutation matrices $\pi_q$ and $\pi_k$, the permuted tensors are $Q' = \pi_q Q$, $K' = \pi_k K$, $V' = \pi_k V$. The permuted attention output is:

$$O' = \pi_q^T \cdot \text{softmax}\left(\frac{(\pi_q Q)(\pi_k K)^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) \cdot \pi_k V$$

where $\pi_q^T$ is applied after attention to restore the original query order. The expression $(\pi_q Q)(\pi_k K)^T$ represents the attention scores between permuted queries and permuted keys. The softmax is applied to these scores. The result is multiplied by $\pi_k V$ (the permuted values), and then $\pi_q^T$ reverses the query permutation.

**Step-by-step expansion of the proof.** Substituting the definitions:

$$O' = \pi_q^T \cdot \text{softmax}\left(\frac{\pi_q Q K^T \pi_k^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) \cdot \pi_k V$$

Now, the key property of permutation matrices: $\pi_q^T \pi_q = I$ and $\pi_k^T \pi_k = I$. Also, the softmax function is permutation-equivariant: if you permute the rows and columns of its input, the output is identically permuted. More formally, for permutation matrices $A$ and $B$, $\text{softmax}(A X B^T) = A \cdot \text{softmax}(X) \cdot B^T$. Applying this property:

$$\text{softmax}\left(\frac{\pi_q Q K^T \pi_k^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) = \pi_q \cdot \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) \cdot \pi_k^T$$

Substituting back:

$$O' = \pi_q^T \cdot \pi_q \cdot \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) \cdot \pi_k^T \cdot \pi_k \cdot V$$

Since $\pi_q^T \pi_q = I$ and $\pi_k^T \pi_k = I$:

$$O' = I \cdot \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) \cdot I \cdot V = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) V = O$$

**What this proves:** The permuted-and-restored attention output $O'$ is exactly equal to the original attention output $O$, with no approximation and no information loss, for any choice of permutation matrices $\pi_q$ and $\pi_k$. The only requirements are (1) that the same permutation $\pi_k$ is applied to both $K$ and $V$ (so that the key-value correspondence is preserved), and (2) that the inverse permutation $\pi_q^T$ is applied to the output to restore the original query ordering. This holds regardless of what the permutation matrices are — they could be random, position-based, or semantic-based. The choice of permutation affects only the intermediate layout, not the mathematical result.

**Why this matters for SVG2.** This equivalence means that SVG2 can freely choose the permutation that maximizes downstream sparse computation efficiency without worrying about introducing mathematical error from the permutation itself. Any quality loss relative to dense attention comes exclusively from the Top-p selection step (which drops some key clusters and thus loses information), not from the reordering. This cleanly separates the layout optimization problem (solved by k-means permutation) from the sparsification problem (solved by centroid-based selection), making the system's behavior predictable and debuggable.

**A practical note on numerical precision.** While the mathematical equivalence is exact in real arithmetic, floating-point computation on GPUs is not perfectly associative. The order of summation in the softmax and the matrix multiplication can produce slightly different numerical results (on the order of $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-4}$ relative error) between the permuted and unpermuted computations. The paper observes that HunyuanVideo is more numerically robust to these differences (PSNR of 33–34 when comparing different dense attention backends) while Wan 2.1 is more sensitive (PSNR as low as 27–28 when comparing backends). This model-specific sensitivity to numerical precision is a practical consideration that affects the achievable PSNR, but it is not a flaw in the permutation approach per se — it reflects the underlying model's conditioning.

#### Centroid-Based Top-p Selection

Once tokens are clustered and permuted, SVG2 needs to decide which key clusters are critical for each query cluster — that is, which attention interactions to compute and which to skip. This is the sparsification step, and its accuracy directly determines quality. The challenge is that computing the full $QK^T$ attention score matrix to identify critical interactions would defeat the purpose (the whole point is to avoid computing the full attention). SVG2 solves this with a two-part mechanism: (1) centroid-based approximation of attention scores, which is cheap because it operates at the cluster level rather than the token level, and (2) Top-p selection, which dynamically determines how many clusters to keep based on cumulative importance rather than a fixed count.

**Centroid-based attention score estimation.** For each query cluster $i$ and each key cluster $j$, SVG2 computes an approximate attention score using only the cluster centroids:

$$S_{ij} = \frac{\text{centroid}(Q_i) \cdot \text{centroid}(K_j)^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}$$

where $\text{centroid}(Q_i) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d}$ is the mean activation vector of all query tokens in cluster $i$, $\text{centroid}(K_j) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d}$ is the mean activation vector of all key tokens in cluster $j$, and $d_k$ is the per-head key dimension (equal to $d$ in standard multi-head attention). The dot product of these centroids divided by $\sqrt{d_k}$ produces a scalar pre-softmax attention score $S_{ij}$ for the cluster pair $(i, j)$.

**What this computes:** a single scalar representing the estimated attention "affinity" between query cluster $i$ and key cluster $j$. If the query centroid and key centroid point in similar directions in the latent space (high dot product), the cluster pair is estimated to be important. If they point in orthogonal or opposite directions (low or negative dot product), the pair is estimated to be unimportant.

**From pre-softmax scores to approximate attention probabilities.** The raw $S_{ij}$ values are not yet proper attention weights because they haven't been normalized. SVG2 converts them to an approximate attention probability matrix $P'$ using a cluster-size-weighted softmax:

$$P'_{ij} = \frac{|K_j| \exp(S_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{C_k} |K_k| \exp(S_{ik})}$$

where $|K_j|$ is the number of tokens in key cluster $j$, and the denominator sums over all $C_k$ key clusters for a given query cluster $i$.

**What this equation computes:** for each query cluster $i$, a probability distribution over key clusters $j$ that approximates the total attention mass that query tokens in cluster $i$ would allocate to tokens in key cluster $j$ under full dense attention. The inclusion of $|K_j|$ as a multiplicative weight is crucial: it accounts for the fact that a key cluster with many tokens may attract more total attention simply because it has more "opportunities" for high dot products, even if its centroid is only moderately aligned with the query centroid. Without the $|K_j|$ factor, the estimate would be biased toward small clusters with highly aligned centroids, missing the contribution of large clusters with average alignment.

**Why this form:** the weighted softmax approximates the actual attention mechanism, where each query token computes dot products with every key token individually (not just centroids), applies softmax, and then sums the resulting attention weights across all tokens in each key cluster. The $|K_j| e^{S_{ij}}$ term is a first-order approximation: it assumes all key tokens in cluster $j$ have activations close to the centroid, so their individual dot products with the query centroid are all approximately $S_{ij}$, and their exponential values $e^{S_{ij}}$ are all similar. Multiplying by the cluster size $|K_j|$ then estimates the total exponentiated attention mass from query cluster $i$ to key cluster $j$. The denominator normalizes across all key clusters so that $P'_{i:}$ sums to 1.

**The approximation is accurate because clusters are semantically coherent.** This is where the benefit of k-means over position-based clustering becomes quantitative. In a semantically coherent cluster, the variance of token activations around the centroid is small, so the assumption that all tokens have dot products approximately equal to the centroid dot product is reasonable. In a position-based cluster with semantically diverse tokens, activation variance is large, and the centroid dot product is a poor proxy for individual token dot products — some tokens in the cluster may have much higher attention scores than the centroid suggests, and some much lower. The approximation error grows with within-cluster variance, which is precisely what k-means minimizes.

**Precision of the approximation.** The paper states that this centroid-based estimation accounts for "less than 1% of the total computational cost" compared to full attention. To see why: full attention on a sequence of 75,000 tokens computes $75,000 \times 75,000 \approx 5.6 \times 10^9$ dot products. The centroid-based estimation with $C_q = 100$ and $C_k = 500$ computes only $100 \times 500 = 50,000$ dot products — roughly 0.001% of the full attention FLOPs. Even accounting for the k-means overhead and the kernel launch overhead, the identification cost is negligible relative to the attention computation it enables skipping.

**Top-p dynamic budget selection.** With the approximate attention matrix $P'$ computed, SVG2 needs to decide which key clusters to actually process for each query cluster. Instead of selecting a fixed number or fraction of clusters (e.g., "top 30%"), SVG2 uses a Top-p (nucleus sampling) strategy that adapts the selection threshold to the distribution of attention scores.

For each query cluster $i$, the algorithm:

1. **Sorts** key clusters in descending order of $P'_{ij}$.
2. **Accumulates** $P'_{ij}$ sequentially until the cumulative sum reaches a predefined threshold $p$ (where $0 < p \leq 1$).
3. **Selects** all key clusters whose $P'_{ij}$ values were included in reaching the threshold, and **drops** the remaining clusters.

**What this computes:** a variable number of key clusters per query cluster, where the number adapts to the concentration of attention scores. If a query cluster attends primarily to a small number of key clusters (concentrated attention), few clusters are selected — the cumulative sum reaches $p$ quickly. If attention is spread across many key clusters (diffuse attention), more clusters are selected to reach the same $p$.

**Why Top-p rather than Top-k:** Top-k with a fixed $k$ would either waste compute on unimportant clusters (if $k$ is too large) or miss important clusters (if $k$ is too small) depending on the attention distribution. Top-p adapts: it guarantees that at least fraction $p$ of the estimated total attention mass is captured, regardless of whether that requires 5 clusters or 50. This is analogous to nucleus sampling in language generation, where Top-p adapts the vocabulary cutoff to the shape of the probability distribution rather than using a fixed cutoff size.

**Relation to the oracle policy.** The oracle policy defined in Section 3.1 selects individual key tokens (not clusters) in descending order of their true attention scores (computed from full $QK^T$) until a target recall is reached. SVG2's Top-p selection approximates this by operating at the cluster level using estimated scores. The approximation has two sources of error: (1) the centroid-based scores $P'$ are estimates, not the true attention scores, so the cluster ranking may be imperfect, and (2) selection is at the granularity of entire clusters, so some non-critical tokens within selected clusters are included, and some critical tokens within dropped clusters are excluded. The k-means clustering minimizes both errors: better centroids improve ranking accuracy, and semantic coherence means that within a cluster, tokens tend to share criticality (all important or all unimportant), so the cluster-level granularity penalty is small.

**The $p$ threshold controls the quality-efficiency trade-off.** Higher $p$ means more key clusters are selected, more attention computation is performed, density increases, and quality approaches dense attention. Lower $p$ means more aggressive sparsification, lower density, lower quality. The paper evaluates across a range of $p$ values (implicitly, since density is varied across experiments in Section 5.4 and Figure 2), but does not report a specific optimal $p$ value — instead, it presents the entire Pareto frontier, showing that SVG2 achieves better quality than baselines at any given density.

#### Dynamic Block-Sparse Attention Kernel

The centroid-based Top-p selection produces a mask over key clusters for each query cluster. The sparse attention computation must then execute attention only for the selected cluster pairs, skipping the dropped pairs. This requires a specialized CUDA kernel because standard attention implementations (FlashAttention, FlexAttention, FlashInfer) assume either dense attention (all query-key pairs computed) or block-sparse attention with **fixed block sizes** — and neither assumption holds after SVG2's semantic-aware permutation.

**Why fixed-block kernels waste computation.** Existing block-sparse attention implementations require the user to specify a block size, e.g., $128 \times 128$. The attention matrix is divided into a grid of $128 \times 128$ tiles, and a binary mask determines which tiles are computed. This works when the sparsity pattern aligns with the block grid — for instance, in sliding window attention where a contiguous band of nearby tokens forms a natural $128 \times 128$ tile. But after SVG2's permutation, cluster sizes are variable: one query cluster might have 230 tokens, another 47, another 812. A key cluster might have 300 tokens, another 55. If a $230 \times 300$ query-key cluster pair needs to be computed, a fixed $128 \times 128$ kernel must either:
- **Pad** the computation to the next multiple of 128, i.e., compute a $256 \times 384$ tile, wasting $(256 - 230) \times (384 - 300) + 230 \times (384 - 300) + (256 - 230) \times 300 = 26 \times 84 + 230 \times 84 + 26 \times 300 = 2184 + 19320 + 7800 = 29304$ element computations on padding tokens, which is 42% waste for this pair.
- **Split** the pair into multiple fixed-size tiles (e.g., four $128 \times 128$ tiles plus remainders), which reduces padding waste but increases kernel launch overhead and still wastes computation at tile boundaries.

The paper quantifies this: in a concrete example, a $128 \times 32$ query-key pair padded to $128 \times 128$ wastes 75% of the computation (Section 4.3). Across the full attention operation, the cumulative waste from fixed-block padding is the "computation waste" phenomenon documented in Figures 3 and 4 and quantified in the ablation study (average 36% waste reduction from enabling permutation).

**The dynamic block-sparse kernel design.** SVG2's custom kernel implements block-sparse attention where **each query cluster and each key cluster form a computation block whose dimensions exactly match the cluster sizes**. If query cluster $i$ has 230 tokens and key cluster $j$ has 300 tokens, the kernel computes a $230 \times 300$ attention sub-matrix with no padding. The kernel is built on FlashInfer's sparse attention infrastructure but extends it to accept variable block sizes specified at runtime.

**Implementation details for FA2 (A100) and FA3 (H100).** The paper supports both FlashAttention-2 (for A100) and FlashAttention-3 (for H100) backends. For FA3 on H100, the kernel uses the `wgmma` (warp group matrix multiply-accumulate) instruction with tile dimensions `m64n64k16` for dense computation within each block. The key design elements:

1. **Query loading:** Query tokens within a cluster are naturally contiguous in memory after permutation (all tokens in the same cluster are physically adjacent). The kernel loads contiguous chunks of query tokens, achieving maximum memory bandwidth.

2. **Key/value loading:** Key and value tokens for the selected clusters are scattered in global memory due to variable cluster sizes. The kernel uses **per-token address offsets** to perform sparse (gathered) loading of key and value tokens, assembling them into a contiguous layout in shared memory. This avoids the need for key/value padding.

3. **Dense computation on contiguous shared memory data:** Once query and key/value data are assembled in shared memory in contiguous layouts, the kernel uses warp-group MMA instructions (`wgmma m64n64k16`) to perform the matrix multiplication at near-peak hardware throughput. The MMA instruction itself operates on fixed-size tiles (64×64 for the output, with 16-wide reduction dimension), but because the data has been assembled into contiguous shared memory, the kernel can iterate over these tiles efficiently even when the overall block dimensions are not multiples of 64.

4. **Output assembly:** Each query cluster produces a partial output of shape `[cluster_size_i, d]` for each attending key cluster. These partial outputs are accumulated (since the full attention is a sum over all key tokens, and each selected key cluster provides a subset of that sum) and written back to global memory in the permuted order. The reverse permutation step (Section 4.1) then reorders the accumulated outputs to the original token order.

**Performance upper bound analysis.** The paper reports that the dynamic block-sparse kernel achieves "over 85% of the theoretical maximum performance" (Section 4.3). The theoretical maximum is estimated by multiplying the sparsity density (fraction of attention computed) with the runtime of dense FlashAttention-3. In other words, if the sparse attention computes 25% of the total attention operations, the theoretical best-case speedup over dense attention is $1/0.25 = 4\times$ — but this ignores kernel launch overhead, sparse loading overhead, and imperfect utilization. Achieving 85% of this theoretical maximum means the kernel's overhead is only about 15% beyond the ideal scaling, which is competitive with hand-optimized dense implementations.

**Benchmarking under different cluster configurations (Appendix D).** The paper provides detailed kernel benchmarking in Figures 11 and 12 (Appendix D). The key findings:
- Kernel performance is **relatively insensitive to $C_k$** (number of key clusters) — performance remains stable as $C_k$ varies from hundreds to thousands. This is because key dimension under-utilization is less penalized by the MMA instruction structure.
- Kernel performance **degrades sharply when $C_q$ exceeds 200**. Below 200, the average query cluster size is large enough that query tiles fill the 64-wide MMA dimension efficiently. Above 200, many query clusters are smaller than 64 tokens, forcing the kernel to process partial tiles, which under-utilizes the tensor cores.
- This hardware constraint drives the choice of $C_q = 100$ as the paper's default configuration: it stays safely below the 200-cutoff while still providing sufficient cluster granularity for accurate identification.

**Why not use a simpler kernel design?** An alternative would be to pad all clusters to uniform sizes (e.g., pad every cluster to a multiple of 128), simplifying the kernel to a standard fixed-block implementation. The paper's ablation in Section 5.5 shows this would re-introduce the computation waste that semantic-aware permutation is designed to eliminate. The 36% average waste reduction figure directly reflects the benefit of avoiding such padding, and this benefit is only realized with a kernel that accepts dynamic block sizes.

#### Efficient k-means via Centroid Caching

k-means clustering is an iterative algorithm: it alternates between assigning each token to the nearest centroid and recomputing centroids as the mean of assigned tokens, repeating until convergence or a maximum iteration count. Even with GPU-optimized implementations (the paper uses k-means++ initialization [63]), this iterative process is expensive — the paper reports that without optimization, k-means can take "100 iterations to converge, consuming 50% or even comparable time to the attention computation" (Section 4.3). If the clustering overhead rivals the attention cost, the entire acceleration framework becomes pointless.

**The insight: consecutive denoising steps have similar token representations.** Diffusion models operate by iteratively denoising the latent representation across timesteps. At adjacent timesteps (e.g., step $t$ and step $t+1$), the latent representations are highly correlated — the denoising process makes small, incremental updates rather than large jumps. This means the activation space geometry (which tokens are similar to which other tokens) changes slowly across timesteps. Consequently, the k-means cluster assignments at step $t$ are a good initialization for clustering at step $t+1$, requiring far fewer iterations to converge.

**The centroids cache mechanism.** SVG2 implements a per-head, per-layer cache of k-means centroids across denoising steps:

1. **At the first denoising step where sparse attention is applied** (after the warmup phase — see below), k-means runs with k-means++ initialization, which selects initial centroids using a careful seeding algorithm that spreads them across the data distribution. This first run takes the full number of iterations to converge.

2. **At each subsequent denoising step**, instead of re-running k-means++ from scratch, the algorithm loads the **final converged centroids from the previous step** as the initial centroids for the current step. Since the activation distribution has shifted only slightly, these previous-step centroids are already close to the new optimal centroids, and k-means converges in far fewer iterations.

3. **After convergence**, the new centroids overwrite the cache for use at the next step.

**Quantitative speedup.** The paper reports that centroid caching reduces the k-means runtime by a factor of **76×** (Section 5.3, Figure 7a). At 50 denoising steps, without caching, k-means initialization and convergence would dominate the total runtime. With caching, the per-step k-means cost becomes negligible relative to the attention computation.

**Density-quality comparison with and without caching.** Figure 7(a) shows the achieved density (lower is better) at 90% attention recall as a function of the number of k-means iterations. With centroid caching (1–5 iterations), the achieved density is comparable to or better than without caching (50+ iterations), but the latency is 76× smaller. This demonstrates that the fast initialization from cached centroids is not just faster — it produces clusterings of essentially equivalent quality, because the previous step's centroids are genuinely close to the current step's optimum.

**Why caching works for video DiTs specifically.** This optimization relies on the temporal coherence of the denoising trajectory, which is a more reliable property in video generation than in, say, autoregressive language generation (where the next token can dramatically change the context). In video DiTs, each denoising step makes a small update to the entire latent grid simultaneously, so the representation evolves smoothly. The paper does not discuss whether this approach would transfer to other diffusion applications, but the principle should apply to any iterative refinement process with smooth latent dynamics.

**Initialization at the first sparse step.** The paper does not explicitly describe what happens at the very first denoising step after the warmup phase. Logically, at the transition from dense attention (warmup) to sparse attention, there are no cached centroids yet, so the first sparse step must run full k-means++ initialization. This one-time cost is amortized over all subsequent steps (typically 30–50 steps at 720p with 50 denoising steps, depending on the scheduler). The paper does not quantify this one-time cost separately.

#### Warmup Steps: Dense Attention in Early Denoising

SVG2 does not apply sparse attention to all denoising steps. Following prior work (FasterCache [56], Pyramid Attention Broadcast [64], DistriFusion [68]), the paper uses **warmup steps**: the first 30% of denoising steps use standard dense attention, and sparse attention is only applied for the remaining 70%. The rationale, established in prior literature, is that early denoising steps establish the global structure of the generated video — the coarse layout, object positions, and overall composition — and these steps are particularly sensitive to attention errors. Sparsifying attention too early can cause structural artifacts that propagate through the remainder of generation. By the time 30% of steps have completed, the video structure is largely determined, and sparsifying attention in later steps mainly affects fine details and texture refinement, where errors are less perceptually damaging.

**Quantitative impact.** The paper reports results both with 30% warmup (Table 1, the main results) and without warmup (Table 2 in Appendix B). Without warmup, all methods degrade in absolute PSNR, but SVG2's relative advantage over baselines generally **increases**. For example, on Wan 2.1 I2V without warmup, SVG2 achieves PSNR 18.276 vs. SVG's 15.608 (a 2.67 dB gap), compared to 26.562 vs. 24.059 with warmup (a 2.50 dB gap). This suggests SVG2's more accurate identification is particularly beneficial when there is less tolerance for error, as in the early structurally sensitive steps.

**Warmup in the system workflow.** During warmup steps, the SVG2 framework is effectively bypassed: no k-means clustering, no permutation, no Top-p selection — standard dense attention is executed. The transition from warmup to sparse mode occurs at a predetermined step threshold (30% of total steps). At the first sparse step, the k-means centroids cache starts fresh (no cached centroids exist), and full k-means++ initialization is performed.

#### Key Design Decisions and Their Justifications

**Independent clustering of queries and keys (not shared).** Justification: The ARI between query and key clusterings is only 0.345, indicating substantially different semantic structures. Sharing clustering degrades PSNR even at higher density (Appendix D.3, Table 6). The query and key projections learn different functions — queries encode "what to look for," keys encode "what is available" — and forcing them into the same cluster structure destroys this asymmetry.

**Asymmetric cluster counts ($C_q = 100$, $C_k = 500$).** Justification: Empirical hardware measurements (Appendix D, Figures 11–12) show that query-side granularity is the bottleneck for kernel efficiency (performance degrades above $C_q \approx 200$), while key-side granularity has minimal efficiency impact up to thousands of clusters. This asymmetry allows fine-grained key selection (500 clusters providing detailed attention estimation) while keeping query clusters large enough for efficient MMA. PSNR-density trade-off sweeps (Appendix D, Table 5) confirm this configuration provides the best balance.

**k-means++ initialization with centroid caching (not a learned clustering model).** Justification: A learned clustering model would require training data and would be model-specific. k-means is training-free, adapts to any model's activation geometry at runtime, and the centroid caching optimization makes its overhead negligible. The paper does not compare against learned alternatives, but the training-free constraint is a core design requirement stated throughout.

**Top-p selection rather than Top-k.** Justification: Top-p adapts the per-query-cluster budget to the attention distribution shape, capturing concentrated attention with fewer clusters and diffuse attention with more clusters, without manual tuning. This enables a single $p$ parameter to produce appropriate sparsity across different layers, heads, denoising steps, and video content.

**Dynamic block-size kernel rather than fixed-block with padding.** Justification: The 36% computation waste reduction (Section 5.5) would be sacrificed with fixed-block kernels. While a dynamic kernel is more complex to implement, the paper demonstrates it is necessary to realize the full benefit of semantic-aware permutation. The kernel achieves >85% of theoretical peak throughput, indicating the complexity cost is manageable.

**30% warmup steps.** Justification: Following established practice in the diffusion acceleration literature. The paper shows results both with and without warmup to provide a complete picture, but the primary results use 30% warmup to align with fair comparison against baselines that also use warmup.

**Per-head, per-layer independent clustering (not shared across heads or layers).** Justification: Different attention heads in the same layer, and the same head index across different layers, learn different attention patterns — some are local, some are global, some are content-specific. Sharing clustering across heads would force a compromise structure that fits none of them well. The independence ensures each head's clustering reflects its own learned semantics.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing the Sparse Attention Bottleneck as a Layout Problem, Not an Identification Problem

The dominant paradigm in dynamic sparse attention for DiTs treats the challenge as a **prediction task**: given a cheaply computed approximate attention matrix, can we predict which token interactions are important enough to keep? Methods like SpargeAttention and XAttention invest their design effort in better pooling strategies, more sophisticated scoring functions, and improved selection heuristics — all operating under the assumption that improving identification accuracy is the primary lever for closing the oracle gap.

SVG2 reframes the problem. The paper's diagnostic contribution is to identify that **even perfect identification cannot achieve oracle-level efficiency** because of a hardware-algorithm mismatch that the field has largely ignored: critical tokens are scattered across the sequence, forcing GPU tensor cores to process blocks that mix critical and non-critical tokens. The "computation waste" concept — quantified at up to 80% of FLOPs on non-critical tokens despite correct identification — is a genuinely new diagnostic lens. Prior work treated the gap between method performance and oracle as entirely attributable to identification error. SVG2 demonstrates that the gap has a second, independent component arising from **layout**: the physical ordering of tokens in memory determines whether sparse computation maps efficiently onto dense hardware primitives.

This is a fundamental conceptual shift, not an incremental refinement. It changes the design objective from "identify critical tokens more accurately" to "rearrange tokens so that the critical ones become contiguous, making identification easier AND computation efficient simultaneously." The two failure modes diagnosed in Section 3.2 — inaccurate identification from position-based clustering and computation waste from scattered critical tokens — are not presented as separate problems to be solved independently. The paper's key insight is that they share a root cause (tokens are ordered by position rather than semantics) and can therefore share a solution (semantic-aware permutation). This unification is what makes the contribution more than the sum of its parts.

Evidence for this reframing being non-obvious comes from the prior literature itself. MMInference introduced permutation for sparse attention in multi-modal LLMs, but its permutation was rule-based (modality type). The idea that tokens within a single modality could be permuted based on learned semantic similarity — and that this would simultaneously improve identification AND reduce waste — had no clear precedent. Even the slide toward linear attention (Mamba, TTT, SANA), which the paper lists as orthogonal work, implicitly concedes that making sparse attention efficient is too hard and replaces the attention mechanism entirely. SVG2's reframing suggests the problem was not the attention mechanism but our approach to exploiting its sparsity.

### Innovation 2: The Difficulty-Conditioned Nature of Identification Accuracy — Semantic Coherence as a Necessary Condition for Coarse-Grained Estimation

The paper surfaces a previously unarticulated relationship: **the accuracy of block-level attention score estimation depends not on the estimation algorithm (mean pooling, max pooling, learned aggregation) but on the semantic homogeneity of the tokens being aggregated**. This is a diagnostic insight about WHY prior dynamic methods fail, not just an observation THAT they fail.

Prior work (SpargeAttention, XAttention, Tactic) treated the identification step as a signal processing problem: given a block of tokens, what function best compresses them into a single representative? Mean pooling, max pooling, and various learned aggregations were explored. SVG2's contribution is recognizing that **no function can produce a high-quality representative from a semantically diverse block** — the information is simply not present in any single vector. The "inaccurate identification" failure (Figure 4b) is not a weakness of mean pooling per se; it is a fundamental limitation of position-based token grouping that no amount of algorithmic sophistication in the aggregation function can overcome.

This reframes the design space. Instead of asking "what's the best aggregation function?", the right question is "how do we form blocks such that aggregation is faithful?" The answer — cluster by semantic similarity first, then aggregate — is conceptually simple but was non-obvious in the prior literature because clustering overhead was assumed to be prohibitive. The paper's centroid caching optimization (76× speedup) makes this approach practical, but the intellectual contribution is the reframing itself: identification accuracy is a property of the token grouping, not the estimation algorithm.

Evidence for this being a genuine insight rather than a restatement of clustering benefits: existing dynamic methods (Tactic, Twilight) already used clustering for token selection, but they clustered based on attention score similarity, not activation similarity in the latent space. The distinction is subtle but crucial: attention score similarity identifies which tokens are co-relevant TO A GIVEN QUERY, while activation similarity identifies which tokens are semantically similar IN REPRESENTATION SPACE regardless of query. SVG2 clusters in representation space because the goal is to group tokens whose latent vectors are similar, making the centroid representative. Prior methods clustered in attention-score space because the goal was to group tokens that behave similarly under attention. These are different clustering objectives with different implications for identification accuracy.

### Innovation 3: Dynamic Block-Size Kernel as a First-Class Architectural Abstraction, Not an Implementation Detail

The customized attention kernel that accepts variable block sizes might appear to be an engineering optimization — a necessary implementation detail to realize the benefits of semantic-aware permutation. But the paper elevates this to an architectural insight: **fixed block sizes in sparse attention kernels impose a structural constraint that prevents any method — regardless of identification accuracy — from approaching the oracle bound**.

This is fundamentally about the interface between algorithm design and hardware constraints. Prior sparse attention work treated the kernel's block size as a given (typically 64, 128, or 256), designing algorithms that produce sparsity patterns aligned with these fixed grids. Sliding window attention naturally produces contiguous blocks. Position-based dynamic methods produce scattered patterns that are force-fit to fixed blocks via padding, accepting the waste as inevitable. The assumption — implicit in all prior work — is that block-sparse kernels with fixed tile sizes are the only efficient implementation option.

SVG2 challenges this assumption by demonstrating that a dynamic block-size kernel is not only possible but necessary. The 36% average waste reduction when enabling semantic-aware permutation (Section 5.5 ablation) directly measures the penalty imposed by the fixed-block constraint. More importantly, the kernel achieves >85% of theoretical peak throughput (Section 4.3), proving that variable block sizes do not inherently sacrifice hardware efficiency — the overhead of sparse loading and dynamic tile management is modest when the permuted layout ensures queries are contiguous and keys are gathered into shared memory before dense computation.

The significance extends beyond this paper. If dynamic block-size kernels become a standard primitive (as FlashInfer integration suggests they might), the design space for sparse attention algorithms expands dramatically. Methods are no longer forced to produce patterns aligned with fixed grids; any sparsity pattern that can be expressed as variable-sized rectangles becomes efficiently computable. This decoupling of algorithm design from kernel constraints is analogous to how FlexAttention decoupled attention pattern specification from CUDA kernel implementation — but for the sparsity mask dimension rather than the attention score computation dimension.

### Innovation 4: Training-Free Adaptation Sets a New Practical Baseline for DiT Acceleration

While not a theoretical advance, the paper makes a significant practical contribution by demonstrating that **training-free sparse attention with semantic-aware permutation can reach quality levels competitive with or exceeding static methods while providing dynamic, input-adaptive sparsity**. This matters because the practical landscape of DiT acceleration is fragmented: static methods (SVG, Sliding Tile Attention) are simple and fast but inflexible; dynamic methods (SpargeAttention, XAttention) are more adaptive but leave performance on the table; linear attention and SSM-based methods require architectural changes and retraining; caching-based methods are orthogonal but have their own quality-speedup trade-offs.

SVG2 establishes a new Pareto frontier (Figure 2) using only inference-time computation, no weight modification, and no training data. For practitioners working with pretrained models — which is the vast majority of users, given the enormous cost of training video DiTs from scratch — this is a meaningful advance. The framework can be applied to HunyuanVideo and Wan 2.1 as a drop-in acceleration without access to training pipelines, datasets, or significant computational resources beyond what inference already requires.

The complementary relationship with caching-based methods (FasterCache, TeaCache, OmniCache) and linear attention approaches means SVG2 is not competing for exclusive adoption but can be combined with these techniques. The paper explicitly notes this in Section 2 — caching methods exploit temporal redundancy across timesteps while SVG2 exploits spatial sparsity within each step. A system combining both could achieve multiplicative speedups.

The practical significance is underscored by the deployment context: generating a five-second HunyuanVideo takes nearly an hour on an A100. SVG2's 2.30× end-to-end speedup reduces this to ~25 minutes on the same hardware, and the 1.89× speedup on Wan 2.1 reduces ~30 minutes to ~16 minutes. These are still not real-time, but they represent meaningful progress toward practical deployment, achieved without any of the infrastructure cost of retraining or model modification.

### Innovation 5: The Gap Between Position-Based and Semantic-Based Clustering as a General Principle for Token Grouping in Vision Transformers

The paper's finding that query and key clustering structures are substantially different (ARI = 0.345, Appendix D.3) — and that forcing shared clustering degrades quality even at higher density — contributes a general insight about token grouping strategies in vision transformers. This is not specific to video generation or to sparse attention: it reveals an asymmetry in how queries and keys represent information that any method relying on token grouping should respect.

Prior token grouping approaches in vision (e.g., token merging, token pruning, various ViT acceleration techniques) often use a single grouping or importance criterion applied uniformly to all tokens regardless of their role (query vs. key vs. value). The SVG2 ablation showing that independent query and key clustering is necessary challenges this assumption. It suggests that the learned projections for queries and keys produce genuinely different similarity geometries — queries cluster by "what they're looking for," keys cluster by "what they contain" — and forcing them into the same grouping sacrifices representational fidelity.

This insight, while empirically derived and not deeply theorized in the paper, has implications beyond sparse attention. Any method that groups tokens for efficiency (whether for attention, for feed-forward computation, or for state compression) should consider whether query, key, and value tokens require independent grouping strategies. The finding that $\pi_q \neq \pi_k$ is optimal even when both are derived from the same input hidden states (via the Q and K projection matrices) demonstrates that the learned projections create distinct similarity structures worth preserving.

The evidence is clear in Table 6: using $\pi_q$ for both Q and K drops PSNR to 22.439 at 38.23% density, versus 26.562 at 31.28% density with independent clustering — **worse quality at higher cost**. This is a clean ablation that isolates the contribution of independent clustering from other design choices.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** For text-to-video (T2V) generation, the paper uses the prompt set from the Penguin Benchmark after prompt optimization provided by the VBench team. For image-to-video (I2V) generation, it uses prompt-image pairs from VBench [67], cropping images to 16:9 aspect ratios for 720p resolution. VBench is a comprehensive benchmark suite for video generative models that evaluates multiple quality dimensions. The paper does not report the exact number of prompts used for the main quality-efficiency experiments, but VBench typically contains several hundred to a few thousand prompts across diverse categories.

- **Base models.** The evaluation covers two state-of-the-art open-source video DiT models at 720p resolution: **Wan 2.1** (both I2V-14B and T2V-14B variants) [2] and **HunyuanVideo-T2V-13B** [1]. After 3D-VAE tokenization, Wan 2.1 generates 21 frames with 3,600 tokens per frame (total sequence length ~75,600), while HunyuanVideo generates 33 frames with 3,600 tokens per frame (total sequence length ~118,800). These models are chosen because they represent the current frontier of open-source video generation, where attention dominates runtime (80%+ of end-to-end time), and because their large scale makes acceleration practically necessary. The paper notes that Wan 2.1 is particularly sensitive to numerical precision changes (PSNR as low as 27–28 when comparing different dense attention backends), while HunyuanVideo is more robust (PSNR 33–34 across backends), making the evaluation more stringent on Wan.

- **Metrics.** Three categories of metrics are used:
  - **Quality similarity metrics:** Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) measure how closely the sparse-attention-generated video matches the dense-attention-generated video (the ground truth reference). Higher PSNR/SSIM and lower LPIPS indicate better fidelity.
  - **Perceptual quality benchmark:** VBench [67] provides a comprehensive multi-dimensional evaluation of video quality including subject consistency, background consistency, motion smoothness, aesthetic quality, and imaging quality, producing an overall average score.
  - **Efficiency metrics:** Density, defined as the sparse attention computation FLOPs divided by the full attention computation FLOPs (lower density means fewer computations and higher potential speedup). Total FLOPs and end-to-end wall-clock speedup (measured on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU with CUDA 12.8) provide practical efficiency measures. Density serves as the computation budget for fair comparison agnostic to implementation details, while end-to-end speedup reflects real-world deployment gains.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against three state-of-the-art sparse attention methods:
  - **Sparse VideoGen (SVG)** [4]: A static sparse attention method that uses a sliding window pattern where each token attends only to spatially and temporally nearby tokens.
  - **SpargeAttention** [9]: A dynamic sparse attention method that clusters consecutive tokens into fixed-size blocks, uses mean pooling to create block representations, estimates attention scores between blocks, and selects top-scoring blocks for computation.
  - **XAttention** [10]: A dynamic sparse attention method that uses block-sparse attention with anti-diagonal scoring for identification. Note that XAttention evaluation on Wan 2.1 is skipped because "it is not supported yet" (Section 5.1), making the Wan 2.1 comparison limited to SpargeAttention and SVG.
  - **Dense Attention:** The full, un-sparsified attention computation, representing the quality upper bound and speed lower bound (1× speedup).
  All baselines use their official configurations, and all methods (including SVG2) apply sparse attention only after the first 30% of denoising steps (warmup), following prior work, unless explicitly stated otherwise.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper uses density as the primary computation budget metric. Density is defined as the ratio of FLOPs in the sparse attention computation to FLOPs in the full attention computation. This abstracts away from implementation-specific overhead and enables fair comparison across methods: at the same density, all methods perform the same number of attention operations (up to differences in padding waste). For end-to-end comparisons, total FLOPs (across all denoising steps) and measured wall-clock speedup on H100 GPUs are reported. The paper does not include k-means overhead in the density calculation, but the centroid caching optimization makes this overhead negligible after the first sparse step. The paper sweeps density by varying the Top-p threshold $p$ for SVG2 and adjusting equivalent sparsity hyperparameters for baselines.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report cross-validation or statistical significance testing. Results are presented as single values per configuration (e.g., PSNR = 26.562 for SVG2 on Wan 2.1 I2V) without confidence intervals or error bars. The VBench evaluation uses the standard VBench protocol (the specific number of evaluation prompts is not reported in the main text). For the sensitivity test (Figure 2), multiple density points are evaluated by varying the sparsity hyperparameter to trace out the quality-efficiency trade-off curve, but each point is a single measurement. The paper does not discuss seed variance or report results averaged over multiple random seeds.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### End-to-End Quality and Efficiency on Single Density Points

The paper's primary quantitative results are in Table 1, which reports quality and efficiency metrics at a specific operating point for each method on three model configurations: Wan 2.1 I2V-14B, Wan 2.1 T2V-14B, and HunyuanVideo-T2V-13B, all at 720p resolution with 30% warmup steps.

**Wan 2.1 I2V-14B (Table 1, top section):** SVG2 achieves PSNR of **26.562**, SSIM of **0.861**, and LPIPS of **0.138** at density 31.28% with 1.58× end-to-end speedup. Compared to the strongest baseline at comparable density, SVG (PSNR 24.059, SSIM 0.813, LPIPS 0.174 at density 30.25%, 1.56× speedup), SVG2 improves PSNR by **+2.503** while operating at slightly higher density (+1.03 percentage points) and nearly identical speedup. Against SpargeAttention (PSNR 21.181, density 38.99%, 1.47×), SVG2 is significantly better on all quality metrics at substantially lower density. SVG2's VBench score of **0.838** matches or slightly exceeds SVG (0.836) and the dense attention upper bound (0.841). An additional SVG2-Turbo configuration achieves PSNR 24.510 at density 14.13% with 1.84× speedup — matching SVG's quality while using less than half the compute, demonstrating the efficiency headroom.

**Wan 2.1 T2V-14B (Table 1, middle section):** SVG2 achieves PSNR of **25.808**, SSIM of **0.854**, and LPIPS of **0.138** at density 29.51% with 1.60× speedup. Compared to SVG (PSNR 22.989, density 30.25%, 1.58×), the quality improvement is **+2.819 PSNR** at slightly lower density. Against SpargeAttention (PSNR 20.519, density 42.03%, 1.44×), SVG2 delivers dramatically better quality at much lower density. VBench for SVG2 (0.842) is comparable to dense attention (0.846) and SVG (0.837). SVG2-Turbo achieves PSNR 23.682 at density 12.87% with 1.89× speedup, showing that aggressive sparsification (density 2.3× lower than SVG's 30.25%) can still match baseline quality.

**HunyuanVideo-T2V-13B (Table 1, bottom section):** SVG2 achieves PSNR of **30.452**, SSIM of **0.910**, and LPIPS of **0.117** at density 25.45% with **2.30×** end-to-end speedup. This is the best result in absolute terms across all configurations. Compared to the strongest baseline, XAttention (PSNR 28.892, SSIM 0.898, LPIPS 0.120 at density 39.32%, 1.56× speedup), SVG2 delivers **+1.560 PSNR** at density that is **13.87 percentage points lower** (35.3% relative reduction in density) and speedup that is **1.47× higher**. Against SVG (PSNR 29.157, density 29.86%, 1.91×), SVG2 achieves +1.295 PSNR while reducing density by 4.41 percentage points. The VBench result of **0.852** slightly exceeds dense attention (0.850) — a surprising outcome suggesting that the sparse approximation may actually regularize attention in a slightly beneficial way for some quality dimensions, or that numerical precision differences affect the dense attention baseline on this sensitive metric. The paper also reports SVG2 + FP8 quantization: PSNR 30.389 at density 25.45% with 2.55× speedup, showing that SVG2's sparsity gains compound with quantization-based acceleration.

**Cross-model patterns in Table 1:**
- SVG2 consistently achieves the **highest PSNR, highest SSIM, and lowest LPIPS** across all three model configurations, while maintaining competitive or superior speedup.
- The quality gap between SVG2 and baselines is larger on Wan 2.1 (~2.5–2.8 PSNR improvement over SVG) than on HunyuanVideo (~1.3 PSNR over SVG), which the paper attributes to Wan 2.1's higher sensitivity to numerical precision (Appendix E.1). This model-specific sensitivity means SVG2's more accurate identification provides larger relative benefit when the model is less tolerant of approximation error.
- The speedup gap between SVG2 and baselines is larger on HunyuanVideo (2.30× vs. 1.56–1.91×) than on Wan 2.1 (1.58–1.60× vs. 1.47–1.56×). The paper attributes this to HunyuanVideo's higher attention cost ratio (Appendix E.2): HunyuanVideo has ~118k context length and only self-attention and FFN in each layer, while Wan 2.1 has ~75k context length and includes an additional cross-attention block. Since SVG2 accelerates attention specifically, the overall speedup scales with the attention proportion.
- SVG2's density is consistently lower than dynamic baselines (SpargeAttention, XAttention) and comparable to or slightly lower than SVG's, meaning SVG2 achieves better quality with equivalent or fewer attention computations.

#### Quality-Efficiency Trade-off Curves Across Density Range

Figure 2 (and the more detailed Figure 8 in Section 5.4) provides the central evidence for SVG2's Pareto frontier claim. On Wan 2.1 I2V-14B, the paper sweeps density for SVG2, SVG, and SpargeAttn, measuring PSNR at each operating point.

**Headline finding:** SVG2 consistently achieves higher PSNR than any baseline at every density level tested (from approximately 10% to 42% density). The gap is largest at intermediate densities (roughly 15–30%), where SVG2 maintains PSNR in the 24–26 range while baselines fall to 19–22 PSNR. At the lowest densities tested (~10–14%), SVG2 achieves PSNR of roughly 23–24, while SpargeAttn is below 18 and SVG is below 15 (reading from Figure 2's approximate coordinates).

**The 2.3× reduction claim:** The paper states that "SVG2 reduces density by up to 2.3× while maintaining the same PSNR" (Section 5.4). Reading Figure 2: to achieve PSNR of approximately 24, SVG2 requires density of roughly 14%, while SVG requires density of roughly 32% — a 2.3× density reduction (32/14 ≈ 2.29). This supports the claim quantitatively.

**Baseline behaviors:** SpargeAttention's curve sits below SVG2 across the entire range, with the gap widening at lower densities (below 20%). SVG's curve is closest to SVG2 at very low densities but diverges substantially as density increases beyond 20%, where SVG2 continues to improve PSNR while SVG plateaus. This plateau is consistent with the static nature of SVG's sparsity pattern — adding more compute (higher density) to a fixed attention pattern yields diminishing returns because the same types of interactions are being computed, just with more tokens. SVG2's semantic-aware selection, in contrast, can productively use additional budget to capture more diverse attention patterns.

**The Pareto frontier interpretation:** A method occupies the Pareto frontier if no other method achieves both better quality and better efficiency (lower density) simultaneously. The curves in Figure 2 show SVG2's PSNR is strictly higher than all baselines at every density, meaning SVG2 dominates the frontier — for any quality level, SVG2 achieves it at lower density than any baseline; for any density budget, SVG2 achieves higher quality. This is a stronger claim than "better on average" and is supported by the full-curve evaluation.

#### Attention Recall vs. Density: Approaching Oracle-Level Identification

Figure 3 (Section 3.1) and Figure 8 (Section 5.5) provide evidence for SVG2's improved identification accuracy relative to existing methods and its approach toward the oracle upper bound.

**Figure 3 (aggregate, pre-SVG2 analysis):** On Wan 2.1 I2V-14B, the oracle policy (which knows true attention scores) achieves ~98% attention recall at 37.5% density, while existing methods (SVG, SpargeAttn) achieve roughly 75–80% recall at the same density. At 25% density, oracle achieves ~93% recall while existing methods achieve ~65%. The gap between oracle and existing methods is the "potential for improvement" that motivates SVG2.

**Figure 8 (SVG2 with and without permutation):** At matched density levels, enabling semantic-aware permutation (SVG2's full method) consistently achieves higher attention recall than disabling permutation (i.e., using position-based clustering with mean pooling but no permutation). The recall advantage is most pronounced at lower densities (~15–30%): at roughly 30% density, permutation-enabled achieves ~90% recall while permutation-disabled achieves ~78%. At 15% density, the gap is roughly 83% vs. 73%. This directly validates the paper's claim that semantic clustering improves identification accuracy — the permutation groups semantically similar tokens, enabling cluster centroids to be higher-quality representatives that produce more accurate attention score estimates and thus more accurate critical token selection.

**Quantifying the gap closure to oracle:** The paper does not explicitly overlay SVG2's recall curve with the oracle curve from Figure 3 onto the same plot, which would provide the most direct evidence for "approaching oracle." However, comparing Figure 3 (oracle achieves ~90% recall at ~25% density) with Figure 8 (SVG2 with permutation achieves ~88% recall at ~25% density, reading approximately from the curve), SVG2 appears to close a substantial portion of the gap — from ~65% recall (existing methods at 25% density in Figure 3) to ~88% recall (SVG2 with permutation at 25% density in Figure 8), with oracle at ~93%. This is approximately a 23 percentage point improvement, recovering roughly 82% of the gap between existing methods and oracle.

#### Ablation on Computation Waste Reduction

Section 5.5 reports a specific ablation on computation waste. The experiment uses the same set of critical tokens selected by centroid-based Top-p selection. In the permutation-enabled configuration, these tokens are arranged in a contiguous layout after semantic-aware permutation. In the permutation-disabled configuration, they remain in their original scattered layout. Both configurations attempt to compute attention only for the selected critical tokens.

**Finding:** Enabling semantic-aware permutation "reduces computational overhead by an average of 36%" compared to the permutation-disabled configuration, while processing the identical set of critical tokens. The 36% reduction is pure computation waste elimination — no change in which tokens are selected, only a change in how they are laid out in memory and thus how efficiently GPUs can process them. This 36% figure directly quantifies the gap between "ideal sparse computation" (only critical tokens processed) and "practical sparse computation" (critical tokens padded to fill fixed-size blocks) that prior methods implicitly accepted.

**Interpretation:** The 36% average waste reduction means that for the same set of selected tokens, the permutation-disabled configuration effectively consumes 1 / (1 - 0.36) ≈ 1.56× more FLOPs than the permutation-enabled configuration due to padding of non-critical tokens to fill GPU blocks. This maps directly to the paper's conceptual critique: even with perfect identification, position-based token ordering wastes over a third of the computation on padding.

#### Warmup-Free Performance (Appendix B, Table 2)

The paper reports results without warmup steps (0% warmup, sparse attention applied from the first denoising step) in Table 2. This is a more challenging setting because early denoising steps are more sensitive to attention errors.

**Key findings across all three model configurations:**

- All methods degrade significantly compared to the 30% warmup setting. On Wan 2.1 I2V, SVG2 drops from PSNR 26.562 to 18.276; SVG drops from 24.059 to 15.608. On HunyuanVideo, SVG2 drops from 30.452 to 19.879; SVG drops from 29.157 to 12.298.
- SVG2's relative advantage over SVG **expands** in the warmup-free setting. On Wan 2.1 I2V: SVG2 PSNR 18.276 vs. SVG 15.608 (gap of +2.668, larger than the +2.503 gap with warmup). On HunyuanVideo: SVG2 PSNR 19.879 vs. SVG 12.298 (gap of +7.581, dramatically larger than the +1.295 gap with warmup).
- The speedup is higher in the warmup-free setting because sparse attention is applied to all steps: SVG2 achieves 2.10× (Wan 2.1 I2V), 2.13× (Wan 2.1 T2V), and 2.69× (HunyuanVideo), compared to 1.58×, 1.60×, and 2.30× respectively with warmup.
- The VBench scores for SVG2 in the warmup-free setting remain close to dense attention: 0.832 vs. 0.841 (Wan 2.1 I2V), 0.852 vs. 0.851 (Wan 2.1 T2V), 0.816 vs. 0.820 (HunyuanVideo). The VBench gap is small despite large PSNR drops, suggesting that the perceptual quality dimensions measured by VBench are less sensitive to attention approximation than pixel-level fidelity metrics.

**Interpretation:** SVG2's more accurate critical token identification is particularly beneficial when errors cannot be masked by dense attention in early structurally-critical steps. The dramatically larger gap on HunyuanVideo (SVG2 at 19.879 vs. SVG at 12.298) suggests that HunyuanVideo's attention patterns in early denoising are poorly captured by SVG's static sliding-window approach, while SVG2's semantic-aware selection adapts effectively.

#### VBench Full Results (Appendix C, Tables 3–4)

The full VBench evaluation breaks down video quality into five dimensions: Subject Consistency, Background Consistency, Motion Smoothness, Aesthetic Quality, and Imaging Quality.

**With 30% warmup (Table 4):**

- **Wan 2.1 I2V:** SVG2 (0.838) matches SVG (0.836) and approaches dense attention (0.841). The small gap is distributed across dimensions: Subject Consistency 0.943 vs. 0.946 (dense), Background Consistency 0.951 vs. 0.956, Motion Smoothness 0.977 vs. 0.979. Aesthetic Quality and Imaging Quality are nearly identical (both 0.606 and 0.709 for SVG2 vs. dense).
- **Wan 2.1 T2V:** SVG2 (0.842) slightly exceeds SVG (0.837) and approaches dense (0.846). SVG2 outperforms SVG on Subject Consistency (0.954 vs. 0.947), Background Consistency (0.965 vs. 0.960), and Imaging Quality (0.709 vs. 0.703). Notably, SpargeAttn drops substantially below all other methods (0.820), with particularly large deficits in Aesthetic Quality (0.567 vs. 0.602–0.613) and Imaging Quality (0.684 vs. 0.703–0.709).
- **HunyuanVideo:** SVG2 (0.852) slightly exceeds dense attention (0.850) and outperforms XAttention (0.839) and SVG (0.845). **With 0% warmup (Table 3):**

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Semantic-aware permutation on vs. off for identification accuracy (Figure 8):** Enabling semantic-aware permutation consistently achieves higher attention recall than disabling it across all density levels tested (roughly 10–60%). The recall advantage is most pronounced at low densities (~15–30%), where identification accuracy matters most because the budget is tight and mistakes (selecting the wrong tokens) are more costly. This supports the claim that semantic clustering improves centroid representation quality and thus identification accuracy.

**Semantic-aware permutation on vs. off for computation waste (Section 5.5, text):** Using the same set of critical tokens, enabling permutation (contiguous layout) reduces computational overhead by an average of **36%** compared to scattered layout. This directly quantifies the padding waste that position-based methods incur even with perfect identification.

**Independent query and key clustering vs. shared clustering (Appendix D.3, Table 6):** Three shared-clustering variants were tested: applying query permutation $\pi_q$ to both Q and K, applying key permutation $\pi_k$ to both, and clustering hidden states before QKV projection for a shared permutation $\pi_S$. All three variants resulted in **worse PSNR at higher density** compared to independent clustering:
- $\pi_q$ for both: PSNR 22.439 at density 38.23% vs. SVG2's 26.562 at 31.28% — **4.1 PSNR lower at 7 percentage points higher density**.
- $\pi_k$ for both: PSNR 22.183 at density 38.58% — similar degradation.
- $\pi_S$ (shared from hidden states): PSNR 26.495 at density 87.27% — nearly matches SVG2's quality but at dramatically higher density (requiring almost 3× more computation).

The ARI (Adjusted Rand Index) between independent query and key clusterings is 0.345, indicating substantially different grouping structures. This ablation definitively establishes that the learned Q and K projections create genuinely different similarity geometries, and forcing shared clustering sacrifices representational fidelity that no increase in density can compensate for.

**Number of clusters sweep (Appendix D, Table 5):** Varying $C_q$ (50, 100, 200, 400) and $C_k$ (250, 500, 1000) while measuring PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, and speedup on Wan 2.1:
- **$C_q = 100, C_k = 500$ provides the best balance** (PSNR 26.128, SSIM 0.816, LPIPS 0.169, 1.89× speedup).
- Increasing $C_k$ from 250 to 500 improves PSNR (25.497 → 26.128) with minimal speedup impact (1.90× → 1.89×). Further increasing to 1000 improves PSNR to 26.276 but drops speedup to 1.71× — extra granularity improves identification but the overhead of more clusters begins to cost.
- Increasing $C_q$ from 50 to 100 maintains speedup (1.90× → 1.89×) and significantly improves PSNR (22.561 → 26.128). From 100 to 200: PSNR stays similar (26.128 → 26.213) but speedup drops to 1.78×. From 200 to 400: PSNR actually improves to 26.488 but speedup collapses to 1.25×. This non-monotonic relationship is explained by tensor core utilization: query clusters smaller than ~64 tokens on average cause under-utilization of the $m64$ dimension, eroding speedup despite better clustering granularity.
- The findings validate the paper's claim that hardware constraints (tensor core dimensions) impose a ceiling on useful cluster granularity — beyond a point, finer clustering hurts speedup more than it helps quality.

**Centroid caching vs. full k-means++ initialization (Figure 7a):** At matched attention recall (90%) and varying the number of k-means iterations:
- Without centroid cache: k-means requires many iterations (5 to 50+ steps depending on convergence), with latency that rises substantially. At 50 iterations, latency is ~76× higher than with caching enabled.
- With centroid cache: 1–5 iterations achieve comparable or lower density at 76× lower latency. The density achieved with caching (reading from the figure) is roughly 23–27% at 1–5 iterations, compared to ~25–26% at 50 iterations without cache. This demonstrates that cached centroids from the previous denoising step are genuinely close to the current optimum — the clustering quality is essentially preserved while the computational cost is nearly eliminated.
- This ablation is critical for the practicality of the method: without caching, k-means overhead "consuming 50% or even comparable time to the attention computation" (Section 4.3) would erase most of the attention speedup, making the approach non-viable.

**Dynamic block-size kernel vs. fixed-block baseline (Figure 7b, Section 5.3):** Comparing the computation FLOPs of SVG2's dynamic kernel against FlashInfer's fixed-block sparse attention across different cluster configurations:
- At the practical configuration $C_q = 100, C_k = 500$, SVG2's dynamic kernel achieves **1.88× computation reduction** over FlashInfer's fixed-block kernel.
- Across all configurations tested (various $C_q, C_k$ combinations), the average reduction is **1.48×**. This is the empirical realization of the computation waste elimination — FlashInfer's fixed-block kernel must pad computations to uniform tile sizes, while SVG2's dynamic kernel computes exactly the needed dimensions.
- The theoretical upper bound (computed as sparsity density × dense FlashAttention-3 runtime) is also shown in Figure 7b. SVG2's kernel achieves "over 85% of the theoretical maximum performance" (Section 4.3), meaning the gap between the ideal scaling and the actual kernel is about 15%, which is attributed to sparse loading overhead and imperfect MMA utilization at block boundaries.

**FP8 quantization compatibility (Table 1, HunyuanVideo section):** Adding FP8 quantization on top of SVG2 yields PSNR 30.389 (vs. 30.452 for SVG2 alone) at the same density 25.45%, with speedup improving from 2.30× to **2.55×**. The quality degradation from quantization is minimal (−0.063 PSNR, −0.002 SSIM, +0.001 LPIPS), demonstrating that SVG2's sparsification is orthogonal to and composable with quantization-based acceleration.

**Warmup proportion ablation (implicit in Tables 1 and 2):** Comparing 30% warmup (Table 1) to 0% warmup (Table 2) shows the sensitivity of all methods to early-step sparsification. SVG2 degrades less than baselines, consistent with better identification accuracy being more important when error tolerance is lower. However, the paper does not conduct a sweep of intermediate warmup percentages (e.g., 10%, 20%, 50%) to find the optimal trade-off.

### Critical Assessment

**How well do the experiments support the paper's central claims?**

The paper's core claim is that semantic-aware permutation achieves a **Pareto frontier trade-off between generation quality and efficiency**, surpassing existing methods at every density level. Figure 2 provides strong support for this claim on Wan 2.1 I2V-14B, showing SVG2's PSNR strictly above baselines across the entire density range tested (roughly 10–42%). The single-point comparisons in Table 1 extend this to Wan 2.1 T2V and HunyuanVideo at specific operating points, showing consistent quality advantages at comparable or lower density.

However, the **full Pareto curve is only shown for one model configuration** (Wan 2.1 I2V in Figure 2). The paper does not provide analogous trade-off curves for Wan 2.1 T2V or HunyuanVideo — instead relying on single-point comparisons in Table 1. While the single points are suggestive, they do not prove that SVG2 occupies the entire Pareto frontier on these models. It is possible that baselines would catch up or surpass SVG2 at density levels not captured by the single operating point in Table 1. The paper would be strengthened by full density sweeps across all three model configurations.

**Does the paper demonstrate that the quality gap is causally attributable to the two identified mechanisms (inaccurate identification and computation waste), or could other factors explain the improvement?**

The ablation evidence is moderately strong on this point. Figure 8 shows that semantic-aware permutation (vs. position-based) improves attention recall at matched density, directly supporting the "improved identification accuracy" mechanism. The 36% average computation waste reduction (Section 5.5) directly supports the "reduced waste" mechanism. These are clean, well-controlled ablations that isolate the two claimed causal pathways.

However, a missing ablation would be to measure whether identification accuracy alone (without the dynamic kernel, using a fixed-block kernel that pads) can achieve most of the quality gain, or whether the waste reduction alone (using a dynamic kernel but with position-based clustering and therefore poorer identification) can achieve most of the efficiency gain. The paper treats these as independent benefits that compound, but does not provide an ablation that separates their individual contributions to the end-to-end speedup at matched quality. The 36% waste reduction number tells us how much padding overhead is eliminated, but not how much that matters when combined with the identification improvement — the interaction effect is unmeasured.

**Single model architecture family (video DiTs), two model instances:** All experiments are on video DiTs (Wan 2.1 variants and HunyuanVideo). The paper does not evaluate on image generation DiTs, audio DiTs, or any non-DiT transformer architecture. The claim in Section 6 that "the major limitation... lies in the lack of discussion and evaluation on whether the proposed methods can be extended to attention mechanisms other than DiTs" acknowledges this directly. The findings may not transfer to autoregressive transformers (where the causal mask changes attention patterns), encoder-decoder architectures, or models with fundamentally different attention sparsity characteristics.

**The two models tested show substantially different behavior** (Wan 2.1 is more sensitive to numerical precision, HunyuanVideo is more robust). This raises the question of how SVG2 would perform on a third model with different characteristics — would it still occupy the Pareto frontier, or does it benefit from model-specific properties? The paper does not discuss this generalizability concern.

**No statistical error reporting:** All results are reported as single values without confidence intervals, standard deviations, or multiple random seeds. Video generation has inherent stochasticity (from the initial noise and the denoising sampling), and the quality metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) can vary across runs. Without error bars, it is impossible to assess whether the reported differences (e.g., PSNR 26.562 vs. 24.059) are statistically significant or within the range of run-to-run variation. VBench typically evaluates on hundreds of prompts, which provides some averaging, but the single-value reporting remains a limitation.

**VBench scores are very close across methods:** In Table 1, the VBench differences between SVG2, SVG, and dense attention are often within 0.003–0.005 (e.g., Wan 2.1 I2V: 0.838 vs. 0.836 vs. 0.841). Given that VBench is a multi-dimensional benchmark with its own evaluation noise, these differences may not be meaningful. The paper does not provide VBench error estimates or discuss the minimum detectable effect size. The claim that SVG2 "maintains high visual quality" is supported by the VBench similarity, but the claim that it "outperforms all prior methods" on VBench (implicit in Table 1) is questionable given the tiny margins and lack of error quantification.

**The oracle comparison in Figure 3 is static and aggregate, not per-head or per-layer.** Attention sparsity patterns vary dramatically across heads and layers — some heads are highly sparse, others are diffuse. The aggregate oracle curve in Figure 3 masks this heterogeneity. SVG2's semantic-aware permutation likely performs differently on different types of attention heads (local vs. global, early vs. late layers), but the paper does not provide this breakdown. Such an analysis would reveal whether the gains are concentrated in specific attention regimes or are broadly distributed.

**The warmup proportion (30%) is not ablated:** The paper follows prior work in using 30% warmup without testing whether this specific number is optimal for SVG2 versus baselines. It is possible that SVG2's better identification means it can tolerate a lower warmup proportion (e.g., 10–20%) than baselines, gaining additional speedup. Conversely, it is possible that SVG2 benefits more from dense attention in early steps than baselines and would degrade faster with reduced warmup. Without this ablation, the warmup choice appears as an inherited convention rather than a tuned parameter.

**Missing baseline: learned clustering or learned sparsity prediction.** The paper's title and abstract emphasize "training-free," which is a legitimate design choice, but the evaluation does not compare against any learned method for token selection or clustering. Methods that use a lightweight learned predictor (e.g., a small MLP trained to predict attention scores from token activations) could potentially achieve better identification accuracy than k-means centroids at comparable or lower overhead. The paper does not discuss this alternative or provide evidence that training-free is necessary (versus a preference).

**The "less than 1% overhead" claim for centroid-based estimation is not empirically validated.** The paper states that centroid-based estimation accounts for "less than 1% of the total computational cost" (Section 4.2) based on FLOP calculations (50,000 vs. 5.6 × 10^9 dot products). However, this comparison is only for the dot product computation and ignores the overhead of the Top-p selection logic, the kernel launch for the estimation, and the data movement to extract centroids. A wall-clock measurement of the identification overhead as a fraction of total attention time would be more convincing and is not provided.

**The speedup numbers include warmup steps where sparse attention is not applied.** The 1.58×–2.30× speedups in Table 1 are end-to-end and account for warmup (the first 30% of steps use dense attention). The attention-specific speedup during the sparse steps is higher, but the paper does not explicitly report this number. For the warmup-free setting in Table 2, the attention speedup column is provided (e.g., 2.95× for Wan 2.1 I2V, 4.06× for HunyuanVideo), showing that the attention module itself is accelerated by 3–4× during sparse steps. The end-to-end speedup is lower due to non-attention components (FFN, normalization, VAE encoding/decoding, etc.), which is standard but worth noting for accurate interpretation.

**What experiments would strengthen the paper?**

1. **Full density-sweep Pareto curves for all model configurations** (not just Wan 2.1 I2V), ideally with error bars over multiple runs or prompts.
2. **A per-layer, per-head breakdown** of attention recall achieved by SVG2 versus baselines and oracle, to understand where the method helps most and where residual gaps remain.
3. **A two-factor ablation** crossing clustering method (k-means vs. position-based) with kernel type (dynamic block-size vs. fixed-block with padding) to isolate the individual contributions of improved identification and reduced waste to end-to-end quality and speedup.
4. **A warmup proportion sweep** to determine whether SVG2's optimal warmup differs from baselines'.
5. **Evaluation on an additional model family** (e.g., an image DiT like FLUX, or a different video DiT like CogVideoX) to test generalizability.
6. **Comparisons against learned sparsity prediction methods** or at least discussion of why k-means is preferable to a small learned predictor.
7. **Statistical reporting** with confidence intervals or standard deviations for key metrics, particularly PSNR and VBench where margins are small.

**Conditional nature of the claims:**

The Pareto frontier claim holds **on Wan 2.1 I2V-14B** (Figure 2), likely extends to Wan 2.1 T2V and HunyuanVideo based on single-point comparisons (Table 1), but is not demonstrated across the full density range on those models. The 2.3× density reduction claim (matching baseline quality at 2.3× lower density) is specifically supported for Wan 2.1 I2V at PSNR ~24. The up to 2.30× end-to-end speedup claim is specifically for HunyuanVideo-T2V-13B at a particular operating point (density 25.45%); the 1.89× end-to-end speedup is for Wan 2.1 T2V at density 12.87% (SVG2-Turbo). These numbers should not be interpreted as "SVG2 accelerates any DiT by 1.9–2.3×" — they are model-specific, configuration-specific, and GPU-specific (H100). On different hardware (A100, consumer GPUs), different models, or at different quality operating points, the speedup will differ.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Difficulty Estimation Overhead Is Not Accounted For in Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire SVG2 framework operates by clustering and permuting tokens at every denoising step before sparse attention can be applied. The paper states that "without optimization, k-means can take 100 iterations to converge, consuming 50% or even comparable time to the attention computation" (Section 4.3). While the paper introduces centroid caching to reduce this overhead by 76× (Figure 7a), this optimization itself carries constraints and costs that are not fully incorporated into the headline speedup numbers.

**The consequence.** The centroid caching mechanism assumes that the first sparse denoising step (after the warmup phase) bears the full cost of k-means++ initialization without any cached centroids. This one-time cost is amortized over subsequent steps, but it means the **first sparse step is substantially slower** than what the average speedup numbers suggest. For short video generations with few denoising steps, this unamortized cost could dominate. Moreover, the centroid caching assumes that consecutive denoising steps have sufficiently similar activation geometry for the previous-step centroids to serve as good initializations. The paper validates this for Wan 2.1 and HunyuanVideo with standard denoising schedules, but does not explore what happens with different schedulers (DDIM with few steps, progressive distillation), where the per-step change in activations may be larger, reducing cache effectiveness and requiring more k-means iterations to converge. In the worst case — a denoising schedule with very few steps where each step makes a large update — the centroid cache could provide minimal speedup, and k-means overhead could erase most of the attention acceleration.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 7a provides the only quantitative evidence on this point: it shows k-means latency with and without caching for up to 50 iterations, demonstrating the 76× improvement when caching is used with 1–5 iterations versus full convergence. However, this figure measures k-means runtime in isolation, not its contribution to end-to-end latency within a full denoising step. The paper does not report what fraction of total sparse-step latency is consumed by k-means with caching enabled, nor does it measure how many iterations are needed on average after the first cached step. The headline speedup numbers in Table 1 (1.58×–2.30×) are end-to-end measurements that include all overhead, so in that sense the k-means cost is accounted for. But the **unamortized first-step cost** is not isolated or discussed — readers might assume the average speedup applies uniformly to each denoising step, when in fact the first sparse step is slower.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation explicitly. The centroid caching mechanism partially mitigates the overhead for subsequent steps, but the first-step cost and the sensitivity to denoising schedule are not discussed as limitations. Future work could explore whether a lightweight learned initialization (e.g., a small network predicting initial centroids from the timestep embedding) could eliminate the k-means++ cold-start cost entirely, or whether alternative clustering algorithms with lower initialization overhead could be substituted.

### Single Model Architecture Family and Two Model Instances — No Evidence of Generalisability

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper are conducted on exactly two video DiT models: Wan 2.1 (I2V-14B and T2V-14B variants) and HunyuanVideo-T2V-13B. Both are DiT-based video generation models with 3D spatio-temporal attention at 720p resolution. The paper does not evaluate on image generation DiTs, audio DiTs, autoregressive transformers, encoder-decoder architectures, or any non-DiT attention mechanism. The authors explicitly acknowledge this in Section 6: "The major limitation of this paper lies in the lack of discussion and evaluation on whether the proposed methods can be extended to attention mechanisms other than DiTs."

**The consequence.** The core finding — that semantic-aware permutation achieves Pareto-frontier trade-offs — may not transfer to other attention-based generative models. Several properties of the evaluated models could be load-bearing for SVG2's gains:

- **Spatio-temporal attention patterns in video DiTs are highly structured.** Video frames contain spatially coherent objects that persist across frames, creating natural semantic clusters that k-means can discover. In image DiTs (single-frame, no temporal dimension) or autoregressive language models (causal attention mask, sequential dependency), the attention sparsity patterns may be qualitatively different, and k-means on Q/K activations may not produce clusters that align well with critical token selection.
- **The two tested models show substantially different sensitivity to approximation.** Wan 2.1 exhibits PSNR as low as 27–28 when comparing different dense attention backends (due to numerical precision sensitivity), while HunyuanVideo achieves 33–34 PSNR under the same comparison (Appendix E.1). SVG2's gains are larger on Wan 2.1 (+2.5 PSNR over SVG) than on HunyuanVideo (+1.3 PSNR over SVG), suggesting the method may benefit more from models that are already less robust to perturbation — possibly because such models produce more "fragile" attention patterns that benefit from the cleaner approximations enabled by semantic clustering. A third model with different sensitivity characteristics might show yet another pattern of gains.
- **The attention cost ratio differs substantially.** HunyuanVideo spends a larger fraction of total runtime on attention (producing 2.30× speedup) versus Wan 2.1 with its additional cross-attention blocks (producing 1.58–1.60×). The speedup achievable on any given model depends on the attention fraction, and models where attention is less dominant would see smaller end-to-end gains even if the attention module itself is accelerated by the same factor.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Beyond the two models, there is no evidence. The paper does not provide an ablation showing that SVG2 helps across different attention patterns (e.g., early vs. late layers, local vs. global heads) within the same model to suggest generalisability. The per-head visualization in Figure 6 shows diverse attention patterns, but this is qualitative and does not quantify SVG2's performance across these diverse patterns separately.

**Mitigation status.** The authors acknowledge this as the "major limitation" in Section 6, which is transparent but does not mitigate the concern. They suggest future work on extending to other attention mechanisms but provide no preliminary evidence or theoretical argument for why the approach should transfer.

### VBench Perceptual Quality Gains Are Within Measurement Noise — Quality Claims Rest Primarily on Reference-Based Metrics

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper uses two categories of quality metrics: reference-based similarity metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) that compare against dense-attention-generated videos, and VBench, a perceptual quality benchmark that evaluates generated videos against human-judgment-correlated dimensions without requiring a reference. The headline claim — that SVG2 "maintains high visual quality" — draws on both.

**The consequence.** The VBench differences between SVG2 and baselines are extremely small. In Table 1 (30% warmup): on Wan 2.1 I2V, SVG2 scores 0.838 vs. SVG 0.836 vs. dense attention 0.841 — a **0.003 difference** from dense and **0.002 difference** from SVG. On Wan 2.1 T2V: 0.842 vs. 0.837 vs. 0.846. On HunyuanVideo: 0.852 vs. dense 0.850 (SVG2 slightly exceeds dense) vs. SVG 0.845. The per-dimension breakdown in Table 4 shows similarly tight clustering: Subject Consistency, Background Consistency, and Motion Smoothness scores vary by 0.002–0.010 across methods. Given that VBench is evaluated on a finite set of prompts with its own measurement noise (the paper does not report confidence intervals or standard deviations), these differences are unlikely to be statistically significant. This means the paper cannot claim that SVG2 produces **perceptually better** videos than baselines in any meaningful sense — only that it produces videos that are **more similar to the dense-attention reference** in pixel-space metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS). The distinction matters because a practitioner choosing an acceleration method cares about the final video quality, not just fidelity to a particular reference implementation. It is possible for a method to achieve high PSNR (faithful reproduction of dense attention) yet produce videos that are perceptually equivalent to a method with lower PSNR, which is what the VBench data suggest.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Tables 1, 3, and 4 provide the VBench numbers. The paper does not report statistical significance tests, confidence intervals, or the number of evaluation prompts used. There is no discussion of the fact that VBench differences are within 0.005 across all methods in most configurations, which is a notable omission given that PSNR differences are large (2–3 dB).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The abstract and introduction emphasize the PSNR and speedup numbers, which is appropriate since SVG2 is primarily an acceleration method and reference-based fidelity is the direct measure of how well it approximates the original model. However, the paper also claims VBench-based quality superiority in places (e.g., Table 1 shows SVG2 VBench scores that are marginally higher), which is not well-supported given the effect sizes. A more careful treatment would explicitly state that VBench differences are within measurement noise and that the quality claim rests on PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS fidelity to the dense attention reference.

### The Dynamic Block-Size Kernel Requires Custom CUDA Implementation — Practical Deployment Barrier and Hardware Specificity

**The assumption or constraint.** SVG2's ability to eliminate computation waste depends on a custom attention kernel that accepts variable block sizes at runtime, which the paper implements on top of FlashInfer. This kernel uses warp-group MMA instructions (`wgmma m64n64k16`) specific to the H100's SM architecture (for FA3) and per-token address offsets for sparse key/value loading. The paper benchmarks on "NVIDIA H100 GPU with CUDA 12.8" (Section 5.1).

**The consequence.** A practitioner wanting to deploy SVG2 on hardware other than an H100 (or A100 for the FA2 backend) faces significant engineering barriers. Consumer GPUs (RTX series), older datacenter GPUs (V100, T4), or non-NVIDIA accelerators (AMD, Intel, Apple Silicon) would require re-implementing the dynamic block-sparse kernel for their respective matrix multiply primitives, sparse memory access patterns, and shared memory hierarchies. The paper reports that the kernel achieves "over 85% of the theoretical maximum performance" on H100, but this efficiency is hardware-specific — a naive port to a different architecture could achieve substantially lower utilization, eroding the speedup or even making sparse attention slower than dense attention due to kernel overhead. The kernel's dependency on FlashInfer, while open-source, adds a build and compatibility dependency that may conflict with existing inference pipelines using other attention backends (e.g., xFormers, native PyTorch SDPA, TensorRT).

Moreover, the kernel's performance characteristics depend on cluster size distributions (Section 5.3, Appendix D). The paper shows that performance degrades sharply when $C_q > 200$ (Figures 11–12) due to MMA under-utilization. This constraint ties the achievable identification accuracy (which benefits from more, smaller clusters) to the hardware efficiency — a trade-off that the paper navigates by selecting $C_q = 100$ as a sweet spot, but which may differ on other hardware with different MMA tile dimensions. A GPU with a different warp size or MMA instruction shape would have a different optimal $C_q$, requiring re-tuning.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figures 11 and 12 in Appendix D provide detailed kernel benchmarks showing performance as a function of $C_q$ and $C_k$, confirming the sensitivity to cluster count. Figure 7b compares the dynamic kernel against FlashInfer's fixed-block baseline. The paper does not evaluate on any hardware other than H100 (the main results) and mentions A100 FA2 support only in passing (Section 4.3). No numbers are provided for A100 performance, and no evaluation exists for consumer GPUs or non-NVIDIA hardware.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss hardware portability as a limitation. The open-source release and FlashInfer integration lower the barrier for the specific platforms supported (H100, A100 with specific CUDA versions), but do not address broader deployment concerns. Future work could explore whether the semantic-aware permutation benefit can be partially realized using more portable abstractions (e.g., FlexAttention with carefully chosen block sizes, or Triton-based kernels with dynamic shapes) that trade off some of the 1.88× kernel-level gain for broader hardware compatibility.

### No Evaluation of Combined Identification Accuracy and Computation Waste in the Same Experiment — Interaction Effect Is Unmeasured

**The assumption or constraint.** SVG2's design is motivated by two independently diagnosed problems — inaccurate identification from position-based clustering, and computation waste from scattered critical tokens — and proposes a unified solution (semantic-aware permutation) that addresses both simultaneously. The paper presents ablation evidence for each mechanism separately: Figure 8 shows improved attention recall from semantic-aware permutation (identification accuracy), and Section 5.5 reports 36% average computation waste reduction from contiguous layout (waste elimination). However, the paper does not provide an experiment that **crosses these two factors** to measure their individual and combined contributions to end-to-end quality and speedup.

**The consequence.** Without a two-factor ablation (clustering method: k-means vs. position-based × kernel type: dynamic block-size vs. fixed-block with padding), it is impossible to determine how much of SVG2's end-to-end improvement comes from better identification versus reduced waste versus the interaction between the two. It is possible that one mechanism dominates and the other provides a marginal additional benefit that does not justify its implementation complexity. For example:

- If k-means-based identification with a standard fixed-block kernel (accepting padding waste) already achieves most of the PSNR gain and speedup, then the dynamic kernel's engineering complexity might be unnecessary for many use cases.
- Conversely, if the dynamic kernel applied to position-based clusters (accepting poorer identification) still achieves competitive speedup, then the k-means clustering overhead might be unnecessary.
- There could be a positive interaction — better clustering produces cleaner block boundaries that the dynamic kernel exploits more efficiently, yielding gains larger than the sum of individual effects — or a negative interaction — the dynamic kernel's overhead partially offsets the identification gain.

The paper's claim of a Pareto frontier implicitly attributes the gains to the combined system, but practitioners making implementation decisions need to know which components are essential.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The two individual ablations that partially address this:
- Figure 8 compares "w/ Kmeans Permutation" (full SVG2) vs. "w/o Kmeans Permutation" (position-based clustering, but it is unclear which kernel is used for the "w/o" condition — presumably the dynamic kernel since the experiment measures attention recall, which is a pre-kernel metric). This isolates identification accuracy.
- Section 5.5 text: the 36% waste reduction uses the same set of critical tokens with permutation on vs. off and measures "computation overhead." This isolates waste reduction.

But these are separate experiments with different metrics (attention recall vs. computation overhead), different setups, and no combined analysis. The paper does not report, for example, "k-means + fixed-block kernel yields PSNR X and speedup Y" vs. "position-based + dynamic kernel yields PSNR Z and speedup W" vs. "k-means + dynamic kernel (SVG2) yields PSNR V and speedup U." This $2 \times 2$ experiment would directly address the interaction question and is notable by its absence.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss this as a limitation. The ablation section (Section 5.5) treats the two mechanisms separately, and the overall narrative presents them as complementary, which they likely are, but the degree of complementarity is unquantified. Future work could conduct the two-factor ablation to guide practitioners on which components to prioritize when implementing SVG2-like systems under different engineering constraints.

### Top-p Threshold Selection Requires Per-Model, Per-Density Operating Point Tuning — No Automated or Principled Method for Choosing $p$

**The assumption or constraint.** SVG2's dynamic budget control uses a Top-p selection strategy where critical key clusters are selected until their cumulative estimated attention probability reaches a threshold $p$. This $p$ parameter directly controls the quality-efficiency trade-off: higher $p$ means more clusters are selected, density increases, quality improves, speedup decreases. The paper evaluates SVG2 across a range of densities (Figure 2, Table 1) by varying $p$ (implicitly — the paper does not report specific $p$ values corresponding to each density point). However, the paper provides **no guidance on how to choose $p$ for a given deployment scenario**.

**The consequence.** A practitioner wanting to deploy SVG2 faces a tuning problem: for their specific model, resolution, and quality requirements, what $p$ value should they use? The relationship between $p$ and the resulting density/quality is not linear or easily predictable — it depends on the attention sparsity patterns of the model, which vary across layers, heads, denoising steps, and input content. The paper demonstrates that SVG2 can achieve a given PSNR at lower density than baselines (Figure 2), but does not provide a method for a practitioner to determine, without running the full experiment, what $p$ value will achieve, say, PSNR ≥ 25 on their model. The sensitivity test in Figure 2 sweeps density (not $p$ directly), and the mapping from $p$ to density is not characterized. This means deploying SVG2 at a new quality target requires running a grid search over $p$, generating videos at each point, and measuring PSNR — a process that could cost significant GPU-hours, partially defeating the purpose of acceleration.

Furthermore, the optimal $p$ may not be uniform across all denoising steps. The paper applies the same Top-p selection to all sparse steps (after the 30% warmup), but different denoising stages may have different sparsity characteristics and different tolerances for approximation error. A more sophisticated schedule that varies $p$ across timesteps (e.g., higher $p$ in later refinement steps, lower $p$ in intermediate steps) could yield better quality-efficiency trade-offs, but the paper's single-$p$ approach forecloses this optimization.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper presents the full quality-efficiency curve (Figure 2) demonstrating that SVG2's curve is strictly above baselines, which means for **any** quality target, SVG2 achieves it at lower density. However, this curve was generated by exhaustively sweeping density and measuring PSNR — it is a demonstration of capability, not a deployment recipe. The paper does not discuss how $p$ values map to the points on this curve, how many $p$ values were tested, or whether $p$ is interpretable enough for practitioners to set based on their requirements.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The Top-p strategy is presented as an advantage over methods requiring manual specification of sparsity ratios (Section 1, Section 4.2), because it "enables dynamic budget allocation without manual adjustments." But this claim conflates two things: (1) adaptivity within a generation (different query clusters get different numbers of key clusters, which Top-p provides), and (2) automation of the global quality-efficiency trade-off (choosing the right $p$ for a deployment target, which Top-p does NOT provide). The $p$ parameter still requires manual tuning per deployment scenario. Future work could develop methods for automatically calibrating $p$ based on a target quality tolerance (e.g., "choose the lowest $p$ such that VBench score is within 0.005 of dense attention") or for making $p$ content-adaptive (e.g., easy-to-generate content uses lower $p$, complex content uses higher $p$).
