# Quantile Advantage Estimation: Stabilizing RLVR for LLM Reasoning

**ArXiv:** [2509.22611](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22611)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper analyzes the training instability of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for LLM reasoning, tracing both entropy collapse and entropy explosion in value-free methods like GRPO and DAPO to a defective mean-baseline that mishandles negative-advantage samples under reward outliers.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper analyzes the training instability of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for LLM reasoning, tracing both entropy collapse and entropy explosion in value-free methods like GRPO and DAPO to a defective mean-baseline that mishandles negative-advantage samples under reward outliers. The authors propose **Quantile Advantage Estimation (QAE)**, a drop-in replacement that substitutes the mean with a group-wise K-quantile baseline, implementing a response-level two-regime gate — on hard queries (p ≤ 1 − K) it amplifies rare successes, while on easy queries (p > 1 − K) it targets remaining failures — and prove that this yields two-sided entropy safety under first-order softmax updates. Empirically, QAE sparsifies credit assignment so that roughly 80% of responses receive zero advantage and delivers sustained pass@1 gains across AIME'24, AIME'25, and AMC'23 on Qwen3-8B/14B/30B-Base models when layered onto DAPO, Clip-Cov, KL-Cov, and GSPO, establishing that baseline design — rather than token-level heuristics — is the primary mechanism for scaling RLVR only when the baseline correctly separates the low-success and high-success regimes.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RLVR Training Is Unstable, and Token-Level Fixes Aren't Enough

The fundamental question this paper tackles is: **why does RLVR training for LLM reasoning oscillate between two failure modes, and how can we stabilize it at the source?** RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) is the dominant paradigm for teaching LLMs to reason — models like DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI's o1 rely on it — but the training process is notoriously fragile. The policy (the LLM's output distribution) tends to either collapse into a deterministic, narrow set of reasoning patterns too early (entropy collapse), or spiral into unproductive randomness that dilutes learning signals (entropy explosion). Both failure modes prevent the model from extracting maximal reasoning capability from the reward signal.

This gap is significant for several practical reasons the authors establish through their empirical analysis:

- **Performance plateaus remain unsolved despite existing fixes.** The paper shows (Figures 1 and 2) that even with state-of-the-art token-level controls like DAPO's Clip-Higher, pass@1 performance on AIME'24 plateaus around step 100–150 while policy entropy surges. The model improves rapidly in early training and then stalls — a ceiling that token-level adjustments cannot break through.

- **Existing methods address only one side of the entropy dilemma.** Clip-Higher (Yu et al., 2025) prevents entropy collapse but inadvertently triggers entropy explosion (Figure 1, left). The entropy spike at steps 10–80 doesn't immediately hurt performance, but it creates long-term instability: after step 100, entropy remains high and volatile while accuracy plateaus. This reveals that *avoiding collapse is insufficient* — the real requirement is keeping entropy within a productive range.

- **The problem is structural, not parametric.** The authors demonstrate that simply tuning the token-level clip threshold `ε_high` from 0.20 to 0.28 produces only marginal effects (Table 1): performance peaks near `ε_high` = 0.26 (40.63% pass@1) but the late-stage plateau persists regardless. This indicates the issue isn't a hyperparameter tuning problem at the token level — it's a design flaw in the underlying advantage estimation mechanism.

- **Scaling RLVR to larger models and harder tasks** requires stable training dynamics. As the community pushes toward stronger reasoning models through increased RLVR training, the entropy dilemma becomes a bottleneck: you cannot simply scale up compute or model size if the optimization itself oscillates between collapse and explosion.

### Tracing the Root Cause: The Mean Baseline Mishandles Negative-Advantage Samples

The paper's central diagnostic insight is that the instability is mechanically rooted in the **advantage baseline**, not in token-level clipping or KL regularization. Value-free methods like GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) compute the advantage for each sampled response as:

$$\hat{A}_i = \frac{R_i - \text{mean}(\{R_k\}_{k=1}^G)}{\text{std}(\{R_k\}_{k=1}^G)}$$

where $R_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is a binary reward (correct or incorrect). The mean baseline is the empirical success rate $p(q)$ for that query. This seems innocuous but creates a systematic pathology under binary rewards:

- **Reward outliers inflate the baseline.** When a group contains a mix of correct and incorrect responses, the mean baseline is $p$. If the query is hard ($p$ is low), the baseline is close to 0, and correct responses get strong positive advantages while incorrect responses get near-zero advantage. But if the query is moderately hard ($p$ ≈ 0.3–0.5), the baseline is substantial, and *incorrect responses receive strongly negative advantages* even though they may contain useful partial reasoning. The baseline penalizes exploration on problems where diverse attempts are most needed.

- **Negative-advantage samples drive entropy explosion.** The authors decompose policy entropy by the sign of the advantage (Figure 4, left) and find that entropy growth is **disproportionately driven by negative-advantage samples**. These samples show both the steepest increase and the largest share of entropy early in training. This makes mechanistic sense: when the model penalizes a response for being incorrect (negative advantage), it pushes probability mass *away* from that response's tokens, but without specifying *where* that mass should go — the result is diffusion into arbitrary high-entropy regions of the token space, not focused improvement.

- **Token-level controls cannot fix a response-level problem.** The authors make a crucial distinction between *response-level* and *token-level* control mechanisms. Token-level clipping (like Clip-Higher's asymmetric clip range) rescales the magnitude of per-token updates but does not change *which* responses get updated or the sign of their advantage. The baseline — which is computed once per response group — determines the sign and structure of the advantage signal. Token-level interventions operate downstream of this decision and therefore cannot correct a fundamentally mis-specified advantage distribution. As the paper states:

> "Token-level mechanisms only rescale steps and do not change the response-level baseline, so they cannot realize these guarantees."

### Conflicting Prior Approaches and Their Shortcomings

Prior work on entropy regulation in RLVR falls into three strands, each addressing only part of the problem:

**Objective-level regulation (one-sided fixes).** Methods like Zhu et al. (2025) propose learning primarily from negative samples to preserve diversity, while entropy minimization approaches (Agarwal et al., 2025) push toward lower entropy. These are inherently one-sided — they push entropy in a single direction (up or down) without a mechanism to adapt based on the current state of training. They can prevent one failure mode while inadvertently triggering the other.

**Mechanistic analyses (diagnostic, not prescriptive).** Works like Cui et al. (2025) and Wang et al. (2025b) characterize *where* exploration concentrates — identifying high-entropy "forking tokens" or "thinking tokens" — but don't provide a mechanism for dynamically regulating that exploration. They help us understand the problem but don't solve it.

**Recipe-level heuristics (fragile, additive).** Techniques like advantage shaping (Cheng et al., 2025), Pass@k training (Chen et al., 2025), and modulated gradients (Wang et al., 2025a) inject exploration through auxiliary mechanisms. These are additive — they layer new components onto the training recipe — rather than addressing the root cause. They add complexity and hyperparameters without fixing the underlying baseline pathology.

The critical missing piece across all three strands is succinctly stated by the authors:

> "a baseline-level entropy control that is data-adaptive yet preserves standard policy-gradient updates remains missing."

### The Baseline Design Gap: Symmetric Weighting for an Asymmetric Problem

The GRPO/DAPO objective, when expressed in the discriminative form introduced by DisCO (Li et al., 2025), reveals the structural problem:

$$J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_q \left[ \underbrace{\sqrt{p(q)(1-p(q))}}_{\text{query weight}} \cdot \mathbb{E}_{o \sim \pi^+_{\text{old}}, o' \sim \pi^-_{\text{old}}} \left[ s^+_\theta(o, q) - s^-_\theta(o', q) \right] \right]$$

The query weight $\sqrt{p(q)(1-p(q))}$ is **symmetric and bell-shaped** — it peaks at $p = 0.5$ and decays symmetrically toward $p = 0$ and $p = 1$ (Figure 4, middle). This means the objective gives equal treatment to correct-answer signals and incorrect-answer signals regardless of query difficulty. On a problem with $p = 0.1$ (very hard), the model gets a small weight on the few successes *and* a small weight on the many failures — neither signal is strong enough to drive learning. On a problem with $p = 0.5$ (medium difficulty), both signals are strong, but the baseline $p = 0.5$ means half the samples get negative advantage, driving entropy upward.

The symmetric weighting conflates two fundamentally different situations that call for asymmetric treatment:
- **Hard queries ($p$ low):** The model needs to *amplify rare successes* (exploit what works) while *not penalizing common failures* (which represent the best the model can currently do). Penalizing failures on hard problems is counterproductive — it pushes probability mass away from the only regions the model has explored.
- **Easy queries ($p$ high):** The model needs to *eliminate residual failures* (exploit to fix remaining errors) while *not further reinforcing already-correct responses* (which would drive entropy toward zero). Reinforcing successes on already-solved problems accelerates collapse.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution not as another additive technique but as a **re-conceptualization of the advantage baseline as the primary knob for entropy control**. The key claim is: baseline design *is* entropy regulation. Specifically:

- **The baseline choice determines how many samples receive positive vs. negative advantages.** A higher baseline (closer to 1) makes more samples appear unsuccessful (negative advantage), pushing the model to diversify (increasing entropy). A lower baseline (closer to 0) marks fewer samples as negative, encouraging exploitation (decreasing entropy).

- **The mean baseline has no tunable parameter to control this balance.** It's fixed at $p(q)$. There's no way to say "on this type of problem, I want to be more conservative about what I penalize" — the mean mechanically produces whatever $p$ happens to be, and that determines the advantage distribution.

- **QAE replaces the mean with a K-quantile baseline**, introducing exactly one hyperparameter $K$ that simultaneously controls (a) the threshold at which the update regime switches, (b) the share of responses that receive non-zero advantage, and (c) the direction of entropy flow. This transforms baseline design from a fixed computation into a controllable lever.

The paper positions QAE as orthogonal to existing token-level and sequence-level controls: it works as a drop-in replacement for the baseline in DAPO, GRPO, GSPO, Clip-Cov, and KL-Cov without changing their hyperparameters (Table 2). This is important because it means QAE isn't competing with prior entropy-control methods — it operates at a different level of the optimization stack (response-level baseline vs. token-level rescaling) and composes cleanly with them.

Finally, the paper's theoretical contribution — the two-sided entropy safety proof (Proposition 4.2) — positions QAE as having guarantees that token-level methods cannot provide. Under first-order softmax updates, the K-quantile baseline achieves the **minimal** entropy increase in the low-success regime (explosion-proof) and the **maximal** entropy increase in the high-success regime (collapse-proof). No token-level rescaling can make this guarantee because it doesn't change the response-level baseline. This theoretical grounding elevates baseline design from a heuristic choice to a principled mechanism with provable stability properties.

## 3. Technical Approach

This is primarily an **empirical analysis and mechanism design paper** whose core idea is that the mean-based advantage baseline in value-free RL (GRPO, DAPO) is the root cause of both entropy collapse and entropy explosion, and that replacing it with a K-quantile baseline provides a single, tunable lever that stabilizes policy entropy with provable two-sided guarantees.

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper builds a **drop-in replacement for the advantage estimation step** in value-free RL algorithms for LLM reasoning. Instead of computing whether each sampled response is "better than average" (the mean baseline), QAE computes whether each response is "among the best K-fraction of the group" (the quantile baseline). This transforms the advantage signal from a continuous normalization to a discrete, two-regime gate that separates hard queries (where we want to amplify rare successes) from easy queries (where we want to eliminate residual failures). The solution's "shape" is: one hyperparameter `$K \in (0,1)$`, one line of code changed (replacing `mean` with `quantile_K`), and the rest of the RL pipeline stays identical.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The QAE system operates as a modification to the **advantage computation step** within a standard value-free RLVR pipeline. The full training loop has five sequential stages:

1. **Prompt Sampling:** A batch of queries (math problems) is drawn from the training dataset.
2. **Response Generation (Proposal):** For each query, the current policy `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$` generates `$G$` candidate responses (e.g., `$G = 32$`). Each response is independently sampled with temperature `$T = 0.7$`.
3. **Reward Verification:** Each response receives a binary reward `$R_i \in \{0, 1\}$` based on whether its extracted final answer matches the ground-truth answer (using exact equivalence checking).
4. **Advantage Estimation (QAE's domain):** For each query's group of `$G$` responses, QAE computes the K-quantile of the rewards (the `$\lceil K \cdot G \rceil$`-th order statistic), subtracts this baseline from each reward, and divides by the group standard deviation. This produces standardized advantages `$\hat{A}_i$` that are non-zero on only one outcome type (correct or incorrect) depending on whether the query's success rate exceeds `$1 - K$`.
5. **Policy Update (Consumer of advantages):** The standardized advantages are fed into the policy gradient objective (DAPO, GRPO, GSPO, etc.), which computes token-level probability ratios, clips them, and updates the model parameters `$\theta$` via gradient descent. This stage is **unchanged** from the baseline algorithm — QAE only modifies the input advantages.

Information flows linearly through stages 1–5 at each training step. The **only changed interface** is the scalar advantage value `$\hat{A}_i$` passed from stage 4 to stage 5. The generation, verification, and update machinery are identical to the baseline.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the **standardized advantage formula** (Equation 3) and how the quantile baseline reduces to a threshold on the group success rate `$p(q)$` (Equation 4). This is the mathematical core — understanding why the quantile creates two regimes (hard vs. easy) is essential before seeing what those regimes do.
- **Second**, the **discriminative decomposition** (Proposition 4.1). This rewrites the QAE objective in the style of DisCO (Li et al., 2025) to reveal the two structural changes QAE makes compared to GRPO: asymmetric masking of one outcome type, and asymmetric re-weighting via `$\sqrt{p/(1-p)}$` or `$\sqrt{(1-p)/p}$`. This connects the math to the intuition about amplifying rare successes vs. residual failures.
- **Third**, the **gradient analysis and entropy-covariance identity** (Proposition 4.2). This proves that under first-order softmax updates, the K-quantile baseline achieves extremal one-step entropy change — minimal in the low-success regime (explosion-proof) and maximal in the high-success regime (collapse-proof). This section explains *why* the baseline is the right knob and *why* token-level controls cannot replicate these guarantees.
- **Fourth**, the **response-level gate mechanism** in operational detail. This translates the equations into what physically happens: which responses get zero advantage, which get positive, which get negative, and how the `$K$` hyperparameter shifts the boundary. This is the practitioner's interface — how to choose `$K$` and what it controls.
- **Fifth**, the **masking ablation design** (Equations 7–8). This explains how the authors isolate the two sides of QAE (positive-masking on easy queries, negative-masking on hard queries) to empirically validate the two-regime theory and diagnose whether a given training run is suffering from collapse or explosion.
- **Sixth**, the **integration surface**: how QAE composes with existing token-level controls (Clip-Higher, Clip-Cov, KL-Cov) and sequence-level optimization (GSPO). This clarifies what QAE changes vs. what it leaves alone, and why the composition is additive rather than conflictual.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

#### The Quantile-Based Advantage Estimator: Core Formula

The central computation QAE modifies is the **standardized advantage** for the `$i$`-th response in a group of size `$G$`:

> $$\hat{A}_i = \frac{R_i - b_K(q)}{\text{std}(\{R_j\}_{j=1}^G) + \varepsilon}, \quad \varepsilon > 0$$
>
> where `$R_i \in \{0, 1\}$` is the binary reward (1 if the response is correct, 0 otherwise), `$b_K(q)$` is the K-quantile baseline for query `$q$`, `$\text{std}(\{R_j\}_{j=1}^G)$` is the sample standard deviation of the `$G$` rewards, and `$\varepsilon$` is a small constant (unspecified in the paper, but standard practice sets it to `$10^{-8}$` or similar) that prevents division by zero when all rewards are identical (`$p \in \{0, 1\}$`).

**What it computes:** For each response, this produces a scalar advantage that is positive if the response's reward exceeds the K-quantile baseline, negative if it falls below, and zero if it equals the baseline. The standardization by the group standard deviation ensures the advantages are on a comparable scale across groups with different variances. Because rewards are binary (0 or 1), the standard deviation is `$\sqrt{p(q)(1-p(q))}$`, which is exactly the denominator used in the GRPO/DAPO advantage formula — so the standardization step is identical to the baseline; only the numerator changes.

**Why this form:** The subtraction of a baseline is standard in policy gradient methods to reduce variance without biasing the gradient estimator — any baseline that is independent of the action (conditionally on the state) yields an unbiased estimate. The mean baseline is the minimizer of the variance of the advantage under squared loss, but it is not the only admissible baseline. The quantile baseline trades some variance optimality for **robustness to reward outliers** and **explicit controllability** via `$K$`. The key insight is that with binary rewards, any baseline `$b \in [0, 1]$` produces unbiased gradient estimates, and the choice of `$b$` determines *which* samples get non-zero updates — a degree of freedom that the mean discards.

#### The Two-Regime Reduction: Why Binary Rewards Make the Quantile a Threshold

When rewards are binary (`$R_i \in \{0, 1\}$`), the empirical reward distribution for a query has at most two distinct values. The K-quantile baseline therefore collapses to a simple threshold on the group's empirical success rate `$p(q)$`:

> $$b_K(q) = \begin{cases} 0, & p(q) \leq 1 - K \\ 1, & p(q) > 1 - K \end{cases}$$
>
> where `$p(q) = \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G R_i$` is the fraction of correct responses among the `$G$` samples for query `$q$`.

**What it computes:** The baseline is either 0 or 1 — it never takes intermediate values. The boundary is set by `$1 - K$`: if the success rate is at or below this threshold, the baseline is 0 (the query is "hard"); if above, the baseline is 1 (the query is "easy"). For the default `$K = 0.4$`, the threshold is `$1 - 0.4 = 0.6$`: queries where fewer than 60% of sampled responses are correct use baseline 0, and queries where 60% or more are correct use baseline 1.

**Why this form:** This is a direct consequence of the empirical CDF of binary data. For a group of `$G$` binary values, the `$K$`-quantile (right-continuous) is the smallest value `$x$` such that at least a fraction `$K$` of the values are `$\leq x$`. If `$p > 1 - K$`, then strictly more than a fraction `$1 - K$` of the values are 1, meaning fewer than a fraction `$K$` are 0 — so the `$K$`-quantile must be 1 (the baseline "jumps" to the larger value). Conversely, if `$p \leq 1 - K$`, at least a fraction `$K$` of the values are 0, so the `$K$`-quantile is 0. The right-continuity convention (using `$\leq$` rather than `$<$` in the first case) ensures the baseline is well-defined for all `$p$`. This binary reduction is what makes the quantile baseline practical: it is computed by simply comparing `$p(q)$` to `$1 - K$` — no sorting or interpolation needed.

#### The Two Training Regimes: Operational Behavior of the Gate

Substituting the threshold baseline into the advantage formula yields two distinct operational regimes:

**Regime 1 — Hard queries (`$p(q) \leq 1 - K$`, baseline = 0):**

- Correct responses (`$R = 1$`): `$\hat{A} = \frac{1 - 0}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}} > 0$`. These rare successes receive **positive advantage** — the policy is reinforced to produce more responses like these.
- Incorrect responses (`$R = 0$`): `$\hat{A} = \frac{0 - 0}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}} = 0$`. The common failures receive **zero advantage** — they are ignored by the update. The policy is not penalized for producing responses that are, by the current standard, the best it can do most of the time. This is the "exploitation-focused" behavior: amplify what works, don't punish what doesn't (yet).

**Regime 2 — Easy queries (`$p(q) > 1 - K$`, baseline = 1):**

- Correct responses (`$R = 1$`): `$\hat{A} = \frac{1 - 1}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}} = 0$`. The common successes receive **zero advantage** — they are ignored. The policy is not further reinforced to do what it already does well, which would accelerate entropy collapse.
- Incorrect responses (`$R = 0$`): `$\hat{A} = \frac{0 - 1}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}} < 0$`. The rare failures receive **negative advantage** — the policy is pushed away from these residual mistakes. This is the "exploration-focused" behavior: target the remaining failure modes while leaving successful behavior unchanged.

The critical design property: **in each regime, exactly one outcome type receives non-zero advantage.** On hard queries, only correct responses get updates; incorrect responses do not contribute to the gradient. On easy queries, only incorrect responses get updates; correct responses are silent. This is the sparsification mechanism that concentrates computational effort on the informative minority — empirically, roughly 80% of responses receive zero advantage with `$K = 0.4$` (Figure 5, right).

#### The Discriminative Decomposition (Proposition 4.1): What QAE Changes Compared to GRPO

To understand *why* this gating works, the paper rewrites the QAE objective in the discriminative form introduced by DisCO (Li et al., 2025). This decomposition separates the objective into a **query-level weight** that determines how much each query contributes to the gradient, and a **discriminative term** that contrasts correct and incorrect response distributions.

**Step 1: GRPO's discriminative form.** For a policy `$\pi_\theta$`, let `$\pi_{\text{old}}^+(\cdot \mid q)$` be the distribution of correct responses (conditioned on `$R = 1$`) and `$\pi_{\text{old}}^-(\cdot \mid q)$` be the distribution of incorrect responses (conditioned on `$R = 0$`). Let `$s_\theta^+(o, q)$` and `$s_\theta^-(o, q)$` be **score functions** for a response `$o$`: these are token-normalized policy ratios that aggregate, per response, how much the new policy `$\pi_\theta$` differs from the old policy `$\pi_{\text{old}}$` on that response's tokens. For positive-advantage updates, the score uses a clipped-upper-bound ratio; for negative-advantage updates, it uses a clipped-lower-bound ratio. (The exact forms are in Appendix A.2 and follow standard PPO-style clipping.)

The GRPO objective (with mean baseline) can then be expressed as:

> $$J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_q \left[ \underbrace{\sqrt{p(q)(1 - p(q))}}_{\text{query weight}} \cdot \mathbb{E}_{o \sim \pi_{\text{old}}^+, o' \sim \pi_{\text{old}}^-} \left[ s_\theta^+(o, q) - s_\theta^-(o', q) \right] \right]$$
>
> where `$p(q)$` is the empirical success rate, `$\sqrt{p(q)(1-p(q))}$` is the symmetric query weight, `$s_\theta^+(o, q)$` is the positive score for a correct response `$o$`, and `$s_\theta^-(o', q)$` is the negative score for an incorrect response `$o'$`. The expectation is over queries and over pairs of correct/incorrect responses.

**What it computes:** For each query, GRPO (1) computes a query-level scalar weight `$\sqrt{p(1-p)}$`, then (2) takes a correct response and an incorrect response, computes their respective score functions (which measure how much the new policy agrees with the old policy's assignment of probability to those responses), and (3) takes the difference `$s^+ - s^-$`. This difference is then scaled by the query weight and averaged. The score functions encode the per-token probability ratio clipping (the standard PPO `$\min(r_t \hat{A}, \text{clip}(r_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) \hat{A})$` mechanism), but aggregated to the response level.

**Why GRPO's form is symmetric:** The query weight `$\sqrt{p(1-p)}$` is the standard deviation of a Bernoulli(`$p$`) variable. It is symmetric around `$p = 0.5$`: a query with 10% success has the same weight as a query with 90% success (`$\sqrt{0.1 \cdot 0.9} \approx 0.3$`). Both types of queries contribute equally to the gradient, and within each query, both correct and incorrect responses contribute (via `$s^+$` and `$s^-$` respectively). There is no mechanism to say "on a very hard problem, penalizing failures is harmful" or "on a very easy problem, reinforcing successes is redundant."

**Step 2: QAE's discriminative form (Proposition 4.1).** Under the K-quantile baseline, the objective changes in two ways:

> $$J_{\text{Quantile}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_q \Bigg[ \mathbf{1}\{p(q) \leq 1-K\} \underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{p(q)}{1-p(q)}}}_{\text{hard-query weight}} \mathbb{E}_{o \sim \pi_{\text{old}}^+} s_\theta^+(o, q) - \mathbf{1}\{p(q) > 1-K\} \underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{1-p(q)}{p(q)}}}_{\text{easy-query weight}} \mathbb{E}_{o' \sim \pi_{\text{old}}^-} s_\theta^-(o', q) \Bigg]$$
>
> where `$\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}$` is the indicator function (1 if the condition holds, 0 otherwise), `$\sqrt{p/(1-p)}$` is the weight for hard queries, and `$\sqrt{(1-p)/p}$` is the weight for easy queries.

**What it computes:** The objective is now a **sum of two mutually exclusive terms**, not a single balanced term. For each query:
- If `$p \leq 1-K$` (hard): only the **positive term** is active. The query contributes `$\sqrt{p/(1-p)} \cdot \mathbb{E}[s_\theta^+(o, q)]$` — the expected positive score for correct responses, weighted by a factor that grows as `$p$` decreases. The negative term is zero (masked out by the indicator).
- If `$p > 1-K$` (easy): only the **negative term** is active. The query contributes `$-\sqrt{(1-p)/p} \cdot \mathbb{E}[s_\theta^-(o', q)]$` — the expected negative score for incorrect responses, weighted by a factor that grows as `$p$` increases. The positive term is zero.

**Why this form — two structural changes from GRPO:**

1. **Asymmetric masking:** GRPO includes both `$s^+$` and `$s^-$` for every query. QAE **selectively nullifies one of the discriminative terms** based on query difficulty. This implements the response-level gate: hard queries don't penalize failures; easy queries don't reward successes. This is the mechanism that produces the sparsity pattern (80% zero advantages).

2. **Asymmetric re-weighting:** GRPO's symmetric weight `$\sqrt{p(1-p)}$` is replaced by **monotonic, asymmetric factors**. On hard queries (`$p$` low), the weight is `$\sqrt{p/(1-p)} \approx \sqrt{p}$` for small `$p$` — it's proportional to `$\sqrt{p}$`, which means the rarest successes (smallest `$p$`) get the *largest relative boost* in their contribution (since the score itself is divided by `$\sqrt{p(1-p)}$` in the advantage; the net contribution of a correct response on a query with success rate `$p$` is roughly `$1/(1-p)$` in the unclipped case). On easy queries (`$p$` high), the weight is `$\sqrt{(1-p)/p} \approx 1/\sqrt{p}$` — the remaining failures on queries that are mostly solved get substantial weight. This is visible in Figure 4 (middle/right): GRPO's weight is bell-shaped, peaking at `$p=0.5$`; QAE's weight on positives is monotonically decreasing in `$p$`, and QAE's weight on negatives is monotonically increasing in `$p$`.

The proof of Proposition 4.1 (Appendix A.1) proceeds by: (1) applying the homogeneity property of the PPO-style clipped objective — `$f(x, c) = c \cdot f^+(x, 1)$` for `$c > 0$` and `$f(x, c) = |c| \cdot (-f^-(x, 1))$` for `$c < 0$` — which separates the sign-dependent scaling; (2) substituting the two-regime advantage values from Equation 13; and (3) collecting terms, noting that the inactive regime contributes zero. The constant factor from `$\varepsilon$` in the denominator is absorbed into the learning rate.

#### The Entropy Safety Proof (Proposition 4.2): Why the Quantile Baseline Is Provably Two-Sided

The theoretical core of the paper is a proof that the K-quantile baseline provides **two-sided entropy safety** — it simultaneously prevents entropy explosion on hard queries and entropy collapse on easy queries. This is established through an entropy-covariance identity adapted from Cui et al. (2025).

**Setup: Bandit reduction and first-order updates.** The analysis simplifies the full token-level policy gradient to a **response-level bandit**. Instead of modeling each token as a separate action, a "response" is treated as a single action. The policy `$\pi(\cdot \mid q)$` is a softmax distribution over responses. The analysis considers a **first-order logit update**: for a response `$y$` with reward `$r(y, q) \in \{0, 1\}$` and advantage `$\hat{A}(y, q) = r(y, q) - b(q)$`, the logit for `$y$` is incremented by `$\eta \cdot \hat{A}(y, q)$` where `$\eta > 0$` is the step size. (This is an approximation of the full gradient step, ignoring the token-level clipping and the denominator normalization, but it captures the directional effect of the baseline on entropy.)

**Entropy-covariance identity.** Let `$H(q) = -\sum_y \pi(y \mid q) \log \pi(y \mid q)$` be the policy entropy for query `$q$` (token-averaged, length-normalized). Under the first-order logit update, the change in entropy satisfies:

> $$\Delta H(q) \approx -\eta \cdot \text{Cov}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid q)}\left(\log \pi(y \mid q), \pi(y \mid q) \cdot \hat{A}(y, q)\right)$$
>
> where `$\text{Cov}(\cdot, \cdot)$` is the covariance over responses drawn from the current policy `$\pi$`, and `$\hat{A}(y, q) = r(y, q) - b(q)$` is the response-level advantage with baseline `$b(q)$`.

**What it computes:** This identity says that the direction and magnitude of the entropy change are determined by the covariance between two quantities per response: (1) `$\log \pi(y \mid q)$`, the log-probability (which is more negative for unlikely responses), and (2) `$\pi(y \mid q) \cdot \hat{A}(y, q)$`, the probability-weighted advantage. Since `$-\eta$` is negative, a *positive* covariance means entropy *decreases* (the update pushes probability mass toward lower-entropy regions), and a *negative* covariance means entropy *increases*.

**Why this identity matters:** It establishes that the baseline `$b(q)$` is a **linear knob** controlling entropy change. Because `$\hat{A} = r - b$` appears linearly in the second argument of the covariance, the entropy change `$\Delta H(q; b)$` is a decreasing function of `$b$` — increasing the baseline makes the advantage smaller (more negative), which makes the covariance more negative, which makes entropy increase more (or decrease less). Formally:

**Step 1: Baseline monotonicity.** For any query `$q$` and non-uniform policy `$\pi$`, the function `$F_q(b) := \text{Cov}_\pi(\log \pi, \pi \cdot (r - b))$` can be expanded by linearity of covariance:

> $$F_q(b) = \text{Cov}_\pi(\log \pi, \pi \cdot r) - b \cdot \text{Cov}_\pi(\log \pi, \pi)$$

The second covariance `$\text{Cov}_\pi(\log \pi, \pi)$` is **strictly positive** whenever the policy is non-uniform. (This is a known inequality: for a random variable `$U = \pi(Y \mid q)$` with `$Y \sim \pi$`, the log function and identity function are co-monotone — both strictly increasing — so their covariance is positive by Chebyshev's rearrangement inequality. Since the policy starts non-uniform and RLVR training generally keeps it non-uniform, this condition holds throughout.) Therefore, `$F_q(b)$` is **strictly decreasing** in `$b$`, and `$\Delta H(q; b) = -\eta F_q(b)$` is **strictly increasing** in `$b$`.

**Operational meaning:** A higher baseline `$\to$` smaller `$F_q(b)$` `$\to$` larger `$\Delta H$` (more entropy increase / less entropy decrease). A lower baseline `$\to$` larger `$F_q(b)$` `$\to$` smaller `$\Delta H$` (less entropy increase / more entropy decrease). This monotonicity is the lever that `$K$` controls.

**Step 2: Two-regime extremality.** For binary rewards with success rate `$p(q)$`, the K-quantile baseline is `$b_K = 0$` when `$p \leq 1-K$` (hard) and `$b_K = 1$` when `$p > 1-K$` (easy). Since any baseline `$b \in [0, 1]$` produces a valid (unbiased) gradient estimate, we can compare:

- **Low-success regime (`$p \leq 1-K$`):** `$b_K = 0$` is the **minimum possible baseline** in `$[0, 1]$`. Therefore, for any other baseline `$b \in [0, 1]$` (including the mean `$b = p$` or any token-level-adjusted baseline), `$\Delta H(q; b_K) \leq \Delta H(q; b)$`. The K-quantile baseline produces the *smallest* entropy increase — it is **explosion-proof**. It prevents negative-advantage samples from driving entropy upward because it makes `$b_K = 0$`, so incorrect responses have `$\hat{A} = 0$` and don't contribute to the covariance term.

- **High-success regime (`$p > 1-K$`):** `$b_K = 1$` is the **maximum possible baseline** in `$[0, 1]$`. Therefore, for any other baseline `$b \in [0, 1]$`, `$\Delta H(q; b_K) \geq \Delta H(q; b)$`. The K-quantile baseline produces the *largest* entropy increase — it is **collapse-proof**. It prevents the policy from converging too quickly by making `$b_K = 1$`, so correct responses have `$\hat{A} = 0$` and only residual failures (with `$\hat{A} < 0$`) push probability mass away, maintaining diversity.

**Why token-level controls cannot achieve this:** Token-level mechanisms like Clip-Higher, Clip-Cov, or KL-Cov operate on the *magnitude* of the update (by scaling the probability ratio `$r_t(\theta)$` or the clipping range) but do not change the *sign* of the advantage nor the *response-level baseline* `$b(q)$`. In the entropy-covariance identity, the baseline `$b$` appears in the advantage `$\hat{A} = r - b$`, which determines the sign of each response's contribution to the gradient. Token-level rescaling multiplies `$\hat{A}$` by a per-token factor but cannot flip its sign or zero it out. Therefore, token-level controls cannot reduce the entropy increase driven by negative-advantage samples on hard queries (since those samples still have `$\hat{A} < 0$`), nor can they increase the entropy decrease driven by positive-advantage samples on easy queries (since those samples still have `$\hat{A} > 0$`). The K-quantile baseline operates one level upstream: it determines which responses have non-zero advantage at all, before any token-level rescaling is applied.

The proof of Proposition 4.2 (Appendix A.2) proceeds by: (1) stating the entropy-covariance identity for softmax policies under first-order updates (adapted from Cui et al., 2025); (2) proving `$\text{Cov}(\log \pi, \pi) > 0$` for non-uniform `$\pi$` via rearrangement inequality; (3) establishing linearity and monotonicity of `$\Delta H(q; b)$` in `$b$`; and (4) noting the extremal property of `$b_K \in \{0, 1\}$`. Strict inequalities hold when the policy is non-uniform and the comparison baseline `$b$` differs from `$b_K$`.

#### The Response-Level Gate Mechanism: Operational Walkthrough

Here is what happens at a single training step for a single query, step by step:

**Step A — Generate:** The current policy generates `$G$` candidate responses with temperature `$T = 0.7$`. For the default DAPO configuration, `$G$` is the group size (typically 16–64 depending on the batch configuration; the paper's training uses a global batch size of 512 with 16 gradient accumulation steps and a mini-batch size of 32).

**Step B — Verify:** Each response is checked against the ground-truth answer. Binary rewards are assigned: 1 if the extracted final answer matches exactly, 0 otherwise. The empirical success rate `$p$` is computed as `$(\text{number of correct responses}) / G$`.

**Step C — Classify difficulty:** The system compares `$p$` to the threshold `$1 - K$`. With `$K = 0.4$`, the threshold is 0.6. The query is classified as:
- **Hard** if `$p \leq 0.6$`: fewer than 60% of the `$G$` responses were correct.
- **Easy** if `$p > 0.6$`: more than 60% were correct.

**Step D — Assign advantages:**

If **hard**: baseline = 0. The standard deviation is `$\sqrt{p(1-p)}$`.
- Each correct response: `$\hat{A} = 1 / \sqrt{p(1-p)} > 0$`. These responses get positive updates.
- Each incorrect response: `$\hat{A} = 0 / \sqrt{p(1-p)} = 0$`. These responses are ignored.

If **easy**: baseline = 1. The standard deviation is still `$\sqrt{p(1-p)}$`.
- Each correct response: `$\hat{A} = (1-1) / \sqrt{p(1-p)} = 0$`. These responses are ignored.
- Each incorrect response: `$\hat{A} = (0-1) / \sqrt{p(1-p)} = -1 / \sqrt{p(1-p)} < 0$`. These responses get negative updates.

**Step E — Update policy:** The advantages are passed to the policy gradient objective (DAPO, GRPO, or GSPO). The per-token probability ratios `$r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(o_t \mid q, o_{<t}) / \pi_{\text{old}}(o_t \mid q, o_{<t})$` are computed, clipped according to the algorithm's clip range (e.g., `$[1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}}, 1 + \epsilon_{\text{high}}]$` for DAPO), multiplied by the response's advantage, and aggregated into a loss. The key detail: for responses with `$\hat{A} = 0$`, the per-token gradient contribution is zero regardless of the probability ratio — these responses are **completely excluded** from the parameter update.

**The sparsity effect:** The fraction of responses with non-zero advantage depends on `$K$` and the distribution of `$p$`. At `$K = 0.4$` on the DAPO-Math-17K dataset, roughly 80% of responses across all training steps receive zero advantage (Figure 5, right). This means only about 20% of generated responses actually contribute gradient information at each step. The 80/20 split is an empirical regularity, not a guaranteed property — it emerges from the interaction between the `$K$` threshold and the distribution of query difficulties in the training data.

#### The K Hyperparameter: How to Choose It and What It Controls

The paper provides an **operational rule-of-thumb for selecting `$K$`** that is independent of evaluation metrics (Section 6):

> "We select K once per baseline by inspecting the entropy of the baseline policy, rather than the evaluation metric. When entropy is low (risk of mode collapse), choose K = 0.6 to inject diversity; when entropy is high (risk of unstable updates), choose K = 0.4 to temper exploration."

**Mechanism:** `$K$` controls the threshold `$1 - K$` at which the query difficulty classification switches. A larger `$K$` (e.g., 0.8) means `$1 - K = 0.2$` — only queries with `$p > 0.2$` are classified as "easy." Most queries are treated as "hard" (baseline = 0), so most responses that are incorrect get `$\hat{A} = 0$` (ignored) — but the *correct* responses on those hard queries get strong positive reinforcement. However, a larger `$K$` also means that queries with intermediate difficulty (e.g., `$p = 0.3$`) are classified as hard, and their *incorrect* responses are ignored even though penalizing them might be productive. The net effect: larger `$K$` increases entropy because it reduces the fraction of incorrect responses that get negative advantages (fewer queries cross the `$1-K$` threshold to become "easy" where failures are penalized).

**Empirical behavior (Figure 9, Appendix B.3):**
- `$K = 0.2$`: Low entropy, stable but exploration-poor. Most queries are "easy" (since `$1-K = 0.8$`), so most incorrect responses get negative advantages — the policy is aggressively pushed away from failure modes. This produces a low-entropy, over-regularized regime with limited accuracy gains.
- `$K = 0.4$`: The default used in all main experiments. Bounded entropy, stable response lengths, sustained accuracy improvements. This is the recommended setting when using Clip-Higher (`$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$`).
- `$K = 0.6$`: Higher entropy, suitable when the baseline policy has low entropy (risk of collapse). More queries are treated as "hard," so fewer incorrect responses are penalized, preserving diversity.
- `$K = 0.8$`: Very high entropy, volatile training with an early accuracy plateau. Most queries are "hard," so most incorrect responses are ignored — the policy wanders without sufficient negative signal to prune unproductive regions.

The paper emphasizes that `$K$` is tuned by **inspecting entropy, not pass@1**, to avoid evaluation overfitting. The default `$K = 0.4$` with Clip-Higher is presented as a robust choice that avoids the high-entropy instability of `$K = 0.8$` while maintaining sufficient stochasticity to prevent collapse. The sensitivity analysis in Figure 9 confirms that `$K = 0.4$` sits in a stable middle region where entropy is bounded, response length is controlled, and accuracy monotonically improves.

#### Masking Ablation: Isolating the Two Sides of QAE

To empirically validate that QAE's two-sided mechanism (positive-masking on easy queries, negative-masking on hard queries) corresponds to the two entropy failure modes (collapse and explosion), the paper constructs two **one-sided ablations**:

**POS-MASK (Equation 7):** This objective masks the positive term on easy queries but leaves the negative term unmasked on all queries:

> $$J_{\text{POS-MASK}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_q \left[ \mathbf{1}\{p(q) \leq 1-K\} \sqrt{\frac{p(q)}{1-p(q)}} \mathbb{E}_{o \sim \pi_{\text{old}}^+} s_\theta^+(o, q) - \sqrt{\frac{1-p(q)}{p(q)}} \mathbb{E}_{o' \sim \pi_{\text{old}}^-} s_\theta^-(o', q) \right]$$

The positive term is gated by `$\mathbf{1}\{p \leq 1-K\}$` (only active on hard queries), but the negative term is **not gated** — it is active on all queries, including hard ones. This ablation preserves the "amplify rare successes on hard queries" behavior but removes the "don't penalize failures on hard queries" protection. It is the half of QAE that prevents collapse (by not over-reinforcing successes on easy queries) at the cost of not preventing explosion.

**NEG-MASK (Equation 8):** This objective masks the negative term on hard queries but leaves the positive term unmasked on all queries:

> $$J_{\text{NEG-MASK}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_q \left[ \sqrt{\frac{p(q)}{1-p(q)}} \mathbb{E}_{o \sim \pi_{\text{old}}^+} s_\theta^+(o, q) - \mathbf{1}\{p(q) > 1-K\} \sqrt{\frac{1-p(q)}{p(q)}} \mathbb{E}_{o' \sim \pi_{\text{old}}^-} s_\theta^-(o', q) \right]$$

The negative term is gated by `$\mathbf{1}\{p > 1-K\}$` (only active on easy queries), but the positive term is **not gated** — it is active on all queries, including easy ones. This ablation preserves the "eliminate residual failures on easy queries" behavior but removes the "don't reinforce successes on easy queries" protection. It is the half of QAE that prevents explosion (by nullifying negative updates on hard queries) at the cost of not preventing collapse.

**What the ablation reveals (Figure 6, b–c):**

- **When `$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$` (weak clipping, Figure 6b):** The dominant failure mode is entropy explosion, because weak high-end clipping allows aggressive positive updates. NEG-MASK nearly matches full QAE and substantially outperforms POS-MASK. This confirms that the negative-masking (preventing negative updates on hard queries) is the critical mechanism when the baseline is explosion-prone.

- **When `$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.20$` (strong clipping, Figure 6c):** The dominant failure mode is entropy collapse, because strong clipping constrains positive updates. POS-MASK outperforms NEG-MASK and is competitive with QAE. This confirms that the positive-masking (preventing positive updates on easy queries) is the critical mechanism when the baseline is collapse-prone.

The key insight from this ablation is that **the full QAE provides both protections simultaneously**, so it works regardless of which failure mode dominates. A practitioner using QAE doesn't need to diagnose whether their training run is collapsing or exploding — the quantile baseline automatically provides the appropriate protection based on the per-query success rate. This is the "two-sided" property that distinguishes QAE from one-sided entropy-control methods.

#### Integration Surface: How QAE Composes with Existing Controls

QAE is designed to be **orthogonal** to token-level and sequence-level mechanisms. The composition model is:

1. **Response-level baseline (QAE's domain):** Computes `$\hat{A}_i$` using the K-quantile. This determines the **sign** of each response's gradient contribution and **which** responses contribute at all.

2. **Token-level clipping (Clip-Higher, Clip-Cov, KL-Cov):** Operates on the per-token probability ratio `$r_t(\theta)$` *after* the advantage is assigned. For a response with `$\hat{A} = 0$`, token-level clipping has no effect (the gradient is zero regardless of the ratio). For a response with `$\hat{A} \neq 0$`, token-level clipping rescales the magnitude of the per-token update but cannot change the sign.

3. **Sequence-level optimization (GSPO):** Applies group-level constraints on the sequence of responses. Since GSPO's optimization operates on the same advantage signals, QAE's modified advantages simply feed into GSPO's objective without conflict.

**Practical integration (Table 2):** The paper applies QAE as a **drop-in replacement for the mean baseline** in DAPO (with Clip-Higher), DAPO + Clip-Cov, DAPO + KL-Cov, and GSPO — all without changing any of the baseline algorithm's hyperparameters. The only change is replacing `mean({R})` with `quantile_K({R})` in the advantage computation. All other settings remain at their defaults:
- DAPO: `$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$`, `$\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$`, dynamic sampling on, overlong reward shaping on, max response length 20,480 with 4,096-token cache for reward shaping, global batch size 512, 16 gradient accumulation steps, mini-batch size 32, learning rate `$10^{-6}$` with no warmup or decay, no KL penalty, no entropy loss.
- GSPO: clipping ranges `$3 \times 10^{-4}$` (left) and `$4 \times 10^{-4}$` (right), four gradient accumulation steps per batch, initializing from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base with a cold start.
- QAE: `$K = 0.4$` uniformly across all recipes and model sizes, chosen once by inspecting baseline entropy as described above.

**Why this composition works:** The baseline and the token-level mechanisms operate on **different degrees of freedom**. The baseline determines *which responses are informative* (the selection problem). The clipping determines *how much to trust the policy ratio estimate* for those selected responses (the magnitude problem). QAE addresses selection; Clip-Higher/Clip-Cov/KL-Cov address magnitude. They are complementary, not competitive, which is why QAE provides additional gains when layered on top of these existing methods (Table 2 shows consistent improvements).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Entropy Regulation as a Baseline-Design Problem, Not a Token-Level Tuning Problem

The dominant prior assumption in RLVR for LLMs — visible across the three strands of prior work (mechanistic analyses, objective-level regulation, and recipe-level heuristics) — was that entropy instability must be addressed by controlling *how much* the policy updates per token. Clip-Higher (Yu et al., 2025) adjusts the asymmetric clipping range to allow larger updates for positive advantages; Clip-Cov and KL-Cov (Cui et al., 2025) penalize specific token-level behaviors; entropy-based exploration methods (Agarwal et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025) modify the optimization objective to push entropy up or down. All of these operate downstream of the advantage computation: they rescale or reshape the gradient *after* the advantage sign and magnitude are already determined by the baseline.

This paper's fundamental conceptual move is to identify the **advantage baseline itself** — specifically, the choice between a mean and a quantile — as the primary mechanism for entropy regulation. The baseline determines not *how much* to update, but **which samples to update at all**. This shift from *magnitude control* to *selection control* is not an incremental refinement — it operates at a different level of the optimization stack. The paper makes this explicit: "Token-level mechanisms only rescale steps and do not change the response-level baseline, so they cannot realize these guarantees."

The diagnostic evidence that supports this reframing is the decomposition of entropy dynamics by advantage sign (Figure 4, left). The fact that negative-advantage samples disproportionately drive the entropy surge — and that token-level clipping cannot remove those negative advantages, only reduce their impact — reveals that the root cause is upstream of any token-level intervention. This is a structural insight: if you get the baseline wrong, no amount of downstream rescaling can fix the resulting entropy imbalance, because you're rescaling signals with the wrong sign structure.

The practical implication is significant: rather than layering additional heuristics onto already-complex RLVR recipes, practitioners can address entropy stability with a single, principled change at the advantage estimation step. The paper's drop-in compatibility results (Table 2) — where QAE improves performance when composed with Clip-Cov, KL-Cov, and GSPO without altering their hyperparameters — empirically validate the orthogonality claim: baseline design and token-level control are independent axes, and the former had been neglected entirely.

---

### Innovation 2: The Two-Regime Gate as a Unifying Mechanism for Asymmetric Credit Assignment

Prior value-free RL methods (GRPO, DAPO) use a **symmetric** treatment of correct and incorrect responses. The mean baseline `$p(q)$` ensures that on every query, regardless of difficulty, both correct responses (with `$R = 1$`) and incorrect responses (with `$R = 0$`) contribute to the gradient, weighted symmetrically by the standard deviation `$\sqrt{p(1-p)}$`. The discriminative decomposition from DisCO (Li et al., 2025) makes this explicit: GRPO's objective contrasts `$s^+$` (correct) with `$s^-$` (incorrect) for every query, with a bell-shaped query weight that peaks at `$p = 0.5$` and decays symmetrically toward both extremes.

QAE's core conceptual innovation is the recognition that this symmetry is **misaligned with the asymmetric nature of learning from verifiable rewards**. On a hard problem (low `$p$`), penalizing incorrect responses is counterproductive — those incorrect responses represent the best the model can currently do, and pushing probability mass away from them diffuses the policy into arbitrary, unproductive regions of token space (the entropy explosion mechanism). On an easy problem (high `$p$`), reinforcing correct responses is redundant — the model already succeeds most of the time, and further reinforcing successes accelerates entropy collapse without improving capability on the remaining failures.

The two-regime gate (Equation 4) implements a principle that was missing from prior work: **credit assignment should be asymmetric and difficulty-dependent**. Hard queries need *exploitation-focused* updates that amplify rare successes without punishing common failures. Easy queries need *exploration-focused* updates that target residual failures without further reinforcing already-correct behavior. The quantile baseline achieves both simultaneously with a single threshold `$1 - K$`, producing a discrete switch between the two regimes based on the empirical success rate `$p(q)$`.

This is fundamentally different from prior asymmetric approaches. Zhu et al. (2025) proposed learning primarily from negative samples, but this is a *global* asymmetry (always favor negatives) rather than a *conditional* one (favor positives on hard queries, negatives on easy queries). Cheng et al. (2025) shape advantages to control exploration, but through additive modifications to the advantage function rather than through a structural gate that completely silences one outcome type. QAE's gating is binary and regime-conditional — it doesn't just reweight, it selectively nullifies — and this binary structure is what produces the striking 80/20 sparsity pattern (Figure 5, right) where the vast majority of generated responses contribute zero gradient.

The significance extends beyond the performance gains. QAE demonstrates that sparse, selective credit assignment — updating on only the most informative subset of samples — is not only stable but *more effective* than the dense updates of mean-baseline methods. This challenges the implicit assumption in prior work that more gradient signal is better, and suggests that RLVR scaling is bottlenecked by *selection quality*, not update magnitude.

### Innovation 3: Two-Sided Entropy Safety as a Provable Guarantee, Not an Empirical Heuristic

Prior work on entropy control in RLVR is entirely empirical and heuristic. Methods like Clip-Higher (Yu et al., 2025) are validated by showing that entropy doesn't collapse in training runs, but they provide no formal guarantees about entropy behavior under policy updates. The mechanistic analyses of Cui et al. (2025) and Wang et al. (2025b) characterize entropy dynamics post-hoc but don't provide mechanisms for controlling them. Even the more principled entropy-regularized MDP approaches (Geist et al., 2019; Ahmed et al., 2019) provide convergence guarantees for the regularized objective but not for the *direction* of entropy change under specific baseline choices.

The paper's theoretical contribution — Proposition 4.2 — provides a different kind of guarantee: **two-sided entropy safety** that is a direct consequence of the quantile baseline's extremal position in the space of possible baselines. Under first-order softmax updates, the K-quantile baseline achieves the *minimum* possible entropy increase in the low-success regime (where explosion is the risk) and the *maximum* possible entropy increase in the high-success regime (where collapse is the risk). This is not an empirical observation — it follows from the covariance structure of softmax policies and the fact that `$b_K \in \{0, 1\}$` are the endpoints of the admissible baseline interval `$[0, 1]$`.

The key insight is that the baseline acts as a **linear knob** on the entropy change. The entropy-covariance identity (adapted from Cui et al., 2025) establishes that `$\Delta H(q; b)$` is strictly increasing in `$b$`. This means:
- A lower baseline always reduces entropy increase (or increases entropy decrease). The lowest possible baseline (0) is therefore optimal for preventing explosion.
- A higher baseline always increases entropy increase (or reduces entropy decrease). The highest possible baseline (1) is therefore optimal for preventing collapse.

The mean baseline `$b = p$` sits somewhere in between and is optimal for neither regime — it compromises on both. Token-level controls cannot change this because they operate on the *gradient magnitude*, not the *baseline value* that determines the sign structure of the advantage. This is what makes the claim fundamental rather than incremental: QAE doesn't just empirically stabilize entropy better than prior methods; it achieves the **theoretical extremum** of the one-step entropy change in each regime, a property that no token-level mechanism can replicate because they leave `$b$` unchanged.

The two-sided nature of the guarantee is equally important. Prior methods are one-sided: Clip-Higher prevents collapse but induces explosion (Figure 1, left). Entropy maximization prevents collapse but risks unproductive divergence. QAE is the first method that provides simultaneous protection against *both* failure modes, because the quantile baseline automatically selects the appropriate extremum based on the per-query success rate. When `$p$` is low, it selects `$b = 0$` (explosion-proof). When `$p$` is high, it selects `$b = 1$` (collapse-proof). The threshold `$1 - K$` simply determines where the boundary between "low" and "high" is drawn.

This theoretical framing elevates QAE from an empirical trick to a principled mechanism. It explains *why* the quantile baseline works — not just that it does — and provides a foundation for understanding why token-level controls, despite their sophistication, cannot substitute for correct baseline design.

### Innovation 4: The 80/20 Sparsity Pattern as a Diagnostic Signal of Efficient Credit Assignment

A striking empirical finding that emerges from QAE — not by design but as a consequence of the two-regime gate — is that with an appropriately tuned `$K$`, roughly 80% of all sampled responses receive exactly zero advantage throughout training (Figure 5, right). This means that at each training step, the model generates `$G$` responses per query, verifies all of them for correctness, but updates its parameters using gradient information from only ~20% of those responses. The other 80% are discarded — not because they're uninformative in principle, but because under the quantile baseline's asymmetric gating, their contribution would be counterproductive (either penalizing exploration on hard queries or reinforcing collapse on easy queries).

This sparsity pattern is significant for several reasons beyond the immediate efficiency implication:

**First, it reveals a deep redundancy in mean-baseline methods.** In GRPO and DAPO, every response contributes to the gradient — correct responses get positive advantages, incorrect responses get negative advantages, and the magnitudes are determined by the group standard deviation. QAE shows that selectively *silencing* the majority of these contributions produces not just equivalent but *superior* learning dynamics. This suggests that much of the gradient signal in standard RLVR is noise — it pushes the policy in directions that are mathematically well-defined (gradient of the surrogate objective) but practically harmful (toward entropy explosion or collapse). The 80/20 pattern is an empirical manifestation of the principle that in RLVR, **selection quality dominates update magnitude**.

**Second, it provides a diagnostic tool for tuning `$K$`.** The paper shows that `$K$` directly controls the fraction of responses that receive non-zero advantage. A `$K$` that is too low (e.g., 0.2) makes most queries "easy," so most incorrect responses get negative advantages — the update fraction is high, entropy is low, and exploration is suppressed. A `$K$` that is too high (e.g., 0.8) makes most queries "hard," so most incorrect responses are ignored — the update fraction is low, entropy is high, and training becomes volatile. The ~80/20 split at `$K = 0.4$` emerges as a robust operating point that balances the two regimes. While the paper doesn't claim this ratio is universally optimal, it provides a concrete, observable metric — the fraction of responses with zero advantage — that practitioners can monitor to diagnose whether their `$K$` is appropriately tuned, independent of evaluation metrics.

**Third, it reframes the efficiency question in RLVR.** Prior work largely treats generation budget (number of sampled responses) as the primary compute cost, and aims to improve performance at a given budget. QAE suggests a different perspective: if 80% of responses don't contribute to the gradient, much of that generation budget is wasted on producing samples that either (a) are correct but don't need reinforcement, or (b) are incorrect on hard queries and shouldn't be penalized. This opens the door to *adaptive sampling* strategies that allocate generation budget where updates will actually occur — for instance, generating fewer responses on queries that are clearly easy or clearly hard, and focusing compute on the boundary region near the `$1 - K$` threshold where both regimes are in play. The paper doesn't explore this direction, but the sparsity pattern provides the empirical foundation for it.

This finding is observational rather than theoretical, but its implications are fundamental: it suggests that the standard practice of generating large numbers of responses per query and updating on all of them is not just computationally wasteful but *actively harmful* to learning dynamics, because it introduces gradient contributions with the wrong sign structure. QAE's gating mechanism corrects this by design, and the 80/20 sparsity is the visible signature of that correction.

### Innovation 5: The Masking Ablation as a Diagnostic Framework for the Collapse-Explosion Trade-off

The most methodologically innovative aspect of the paper's experimental design is the **masking ablation framework** (Equations 7–8, Figure 6b–c). Rather than simply comparing QAE against baselines and reporting accuracy gains, the authors construct two one-sided variants — POS-MASK (which only masks positive updates on easy queries) and NEG-MASK (which only masks negative updates on hard queries) — to **diagnose which entropy failure mode is dominant** in a given training configuration.

This framework transforms the entropy dilemma from an observed phenomenon into an experimentally testable hypothesis. The logic is:
- If NEG-MASK (preventing explosion) performs comparably to full QAE while POS-MASK (preventing collapse) underperforms, then the dominant failure mode is entropy explosion — negative-advantage samples are driving instability, and suppressing them is the primary mechanism of improvement.
- If POS-MASK outperforms NEG-MASK and nearly matches QAE, then the dominant failure mode is entropy collapse — over-reinforcement of successes is the primary bottleneck, and preventing that reinforcement is what matters.
- If both one-sided variants underperform full QAE, then both failure modes are active simultaneously, and the two-sided gating is necessary.

The paper validates this framework by varying `$\epsilon_{\text{high}}$` to shift the dominant failure mode. With weak clipping (`$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$`), the baseline DAPO exhibits entropy explosion (Figure 1, left), and NEG-MASK nearly matches QAE (Figure 6b). With strong clipping (`$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.20$`), the baseline is more collapse-prone, and POS-MASK outperforms NEG-MASK (Figure 6c). This confirms that the masking ablation correctly identifies the active failure mode, and that QAE's full two-sided mechanism provides protection regardless of which mode dominates.

The conceptual significance of this framework extends beyond QAE itself. It provides a **principled method for diagnosing RLVR training instability** that doesn't depend on post-hoc entropy analysis. A practitioner can run POS-MASK and NEG-MASK as diagnostic probes on a small scale, observe which one tracks full method performance, and thereby determine whether their training setup is collapse-limited or explosion-limited. This diagnosis can then guide hyperparameter choices — not just `$K$`, but also clipping ranges, learning rates, and sampling strategies — in a targeted rather than trial-and-error fashion.

Prior work lacked such a diagnostic framework. Researchers could observe that entropy was collapsing or exploding, but couldn't cleanly attribute the instability to *which specific gradient contributions* were responsible. The masking ablation makes this attribution explicit: collapse is driven by positive updates on easy queries, explosion is driven by negative updates on hard queries, and the relative severity of each can be measured by comparing POS-MASK and NEG-MASK against the full method. This is a conceptual contribution that enables more systematic empirical investigation of RLVR dynamics — it transforms entropy instability from a qualitative observation into a quantitatively diagnosable phenomenon with a clear experimental protocol.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use three standard math-reasoning benchmarks: AIME'24, AIME'25, and AMC'23. These are zero-shot evaluated. The training data is the DAPO-Math-17K dataset (Yu et al., 2025), consisting of 17,000 math problems. The paper does not report a separate validation set for hyperparameter selection — `K = 0.4` is chosen once by inspecting entropy of the baseline policy (not evaluation metrics), and used uniformly across all experiments.

- **Base model(s).** Three model scales from the Qwen3 family are used: Qwen3-8B-Base, Qwen3-14B-Base, and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base. The 8B model serves as the primary testbed for most experiments (DAPO + QAE, Clip-Cov, KL-Cov, ablations, and sensitivity analysis). The 14B model validates scaling behavior (Figure 6a). The 30B-A3B model tests compatibility with GSPO in a cold-start configuration. The authors do not provide a justification for why Qwen3 was chosen over other model families (e.g., Llama, DeepSeek).

- **Metrics.** Two metrics are reported: **pass@1** and **pass@16**. For each query, `k = 32` completions are sampled with temperature `T = 0.7`. Pass@1 is the accuracy when selecting a single completion (typically the one with the highest reward or the first generated). Pass@16 is the accuracy when selecting the best of 16 completions evaluated against the ground-truth answer — it serves as an upper bound on what the verifier could recover and is reported as a secondary metric. The number of average tokens per response is also tracked as a diagnostic. No confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical tests are reported for any metric — all numbers are point estimates from single training runs.

- **Baselines.** The paper uses four baseline algorithms, all drawn from prior work:
  - **DAPO (Yu et al., 2025):** The primary baseline. DAPO is a state-of-the-art value-free RLVR method that refines GRPO by removing the KL penalty, introducing asymmetric clipping (`ε_high` and `ε_low`), token-level loss normalization, and dynamic sampling constraints. The paper uses DAPO's recommended configuration: `ε_high = 0.28`, `ε_low = 0.2`, Clip-Higher enabled, maximum response length 20,480 with 4,096-token cache for overlong reward shaping, global batch size 512, 16 gradient accumulation steps, mini-batch size 32, learning rate `10^{-6}` with no warmup or decay, and no KL divergence or entropy loss. This is referred to as "Clip-Higher" in tables and figures.
  - **Clip-Cov (Cui et al., 2025):** A token-level entropy control method that penalizes tokens contributing to covariance-driven entropy changes. Used as a baseline to test whether QAE is orthogonal to token-level controls. Configuration details are not provided beyond "we keep all training and decoding hyper-parameters identical."
  - **KL-Cov (Cui et al., 2025):** Another token-level method from the same work, using KL-based covariance penalties. Tested for the same orthogonality purpose.
  - **GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025):** A sequence-level optimization method that applies group-level constraints. Tested on the 30B-A3B model with GSPO-specific clipping ranges (`3 × 10^{-4}` left, `4 × 10^{-4}` right) and four gradient accumulation steps per batch, following the official VERL implementation script. GSPO initializes from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base with a cold start (no SFT warmup).

  The paper does not include a simple GRPO baseline without DAPO's modifications — all comparisons are against DAPO or DAPO-augmented methods. There is also no comparison against offline methods (DPO, rejection sampling fine-tuning) or against PPO with a learned value network.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not report FLOPs or wall-clock time. The implicit unit of compute is the number of sampled responses per query per training step, determined by the group size `G` (embedded in the batch configuration: global batch size 512 with 16 accumulation steps and mini-batch size 32 implies 512 queries per step, with `G` responses generated per query). At evaluation time, 32 completions are sampled per query. The paper does not discuss whether QAE changes the effective computational cost per training step — since the advantage computation is a trivial quantile operation replacing the mean, the overhead is negligible. However, the difficulty estimation overhead that was a major limitation in the reference example (generating 2048 samples to estimate difficulty in the compute-optimal test-time scaling paper) is absent here — QAE's quantile is computed on the same `G` samples already generated for the training step, requiring no additional generation.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** None reported. There is no mention of multiple random seeds, train/validation/test splits (the MATH training data is the DAPO-Math-17K set; evaluation is on AIME'24, AIME'25, and AMC'23, which are held-out benchmarks), or statistical significance testing. The training dynamics curves (Figures 5, 10, 11) appear to be from single runs. The ablation masking experiments (Figure 6) appear to be single-run comparisons. This is a limitation — without error bars or multiple seeds, the reported pass@1 differences (e.g., 39.69 → 48.23 on AIME'24 with 8B Clip-Higher + QAE, an 8.54 percentage point gain) cannot be assessed for reliability against training noise.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Drop-in Performance Gains Across Methods and Model Sizes

**Headline result:** Replacing the mean baseline with the K-quantile baseline (QAE, `K = 0.4`) in DAPO yields consistent pass@1 improvements across all three benchmarks and all three model sizes, while pass@16 remains largely comparable (Table 2).

**Qwen3-8B-Base with Clip-Higher (DAPO):** QAE improves pass@1 on AIME'25 from 32.71 to 34.90 (+6.7% relative), on AIME'24 from 39.69 to 48.23 (+21.5% relative), and on AMC'23 from 92.11 to 92.97 (+0.9% relative). Pass@16 changes are smaller: AIME'25 56.66 → 57.92 (+2.2%), AIME'24 71.23 → 71.63 (+0.6%), AMC'23 unchanged at 97.50. The AMC'23 pass@1 gain is negligible because the baseline is already at 92.11 — a ceiling effect on an easier benchmark.

**Qwen3-8B-Base with Clip-Cov:** QAE improves pass@1 on AIME'25 from 33.02 to 37.40 (+13.3%), AIME'24 from 42.40 to 46.04 (+8.6%), and AMC'23 from 87.42 to 90.23 (+3.2%). Pass@16 gains are more substantial here: AIME'25 52.27 → 56.29 (+7.7%), AIME'24 68.58 → 73.16 (+6.7%), AMC'23 unchanged at 96.25. The fact that QAE provides gains on top of Clip-Cov — which already applies token-level entropy control — supports the orthogonality claim.

**Qwen3-8B-Base with KL-Cov:** Results are mixed. Pass@1 on AIME'25 improves marginally (33.33 → 33.44, +0.3%), AIME'24 is slightly worse (44.90 → 44.69, -0.5%), and AMC'23 improves from 86.02 to 87.97 (+2.3%). Pass@16 gains are more consistent: AIME'25 45.86 → 51.62 (+12.6%), AIME'24 73.00 → 77.08 (+5.6%), AMC'23 95.00 → 96.25 (+1.3%). The negligible or negative pass@1 changes on AIME'24/'25 suggest that KL-Cov's token-level regularization and QAE's response-level gating may partially overlap in their effect when the baseline already has strong KL-based entropy control. The paper does not discuss this interaction or provide a hypothesis for the mixed result.

**Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base with GSPO:** QAE improves pass@1 on AIME'25 from 31.15 to 32.50 (+4.3%), AIME'24 from 43.75 to 47.50 (+8.6%), but AMC'23 declines from 90.00 to 89.38 (-0.7%). Pass@16 on AMC'23 also declines from 99.39 to 97.21 (-2.2%), while AIME'24 pass@16 improves (67.91 → 71.72, +5.6%) and AIME'25 improves (46.59 → 48.01, +3.0%). This is the only configuration where QAE shows a pass@1 decline (AMC'23), though the paper provides no analysis of this failure case.

**Scaling to 14B (Figure 6a):** On the larger Qwen3-14B-Base model with DAPO + Clip-Higher, QAE improves pass@1 on AIME'25 from 45.21 to 46.88 and on AIME'24 from 56.56 to 58.96. These are reported as bar chart values without a table, so exact relative improvements must be read from the figure. The gains are smaller in absolute percentage points than on the 8B model (approximately +1.7 and +2.4 points vs. +2.2 and +8.5 on 8B), but still positive. Pass@16 is not reported for this comparison.

**Interpretation:** The consistent pass@1 improvements — across three benchmarks, three model sizes, and four base algorithms, with only the KL-Cov 8B and GSPO AMC'23 configurations showing flat or negative results — suggest that QAE's mechanism is genuinely beneficial in most scenarios. The pass@16 stability (or modest improvement) is noteworthy: QAE makes the model better at generating a correct answer on the first attempt (pass@1) without degrading its best-of-16 performance. This implies QAE tightens the distribution around correct answers rather than simply shifting probability mass around. The paper does not explicitly discuss why pass@1 improves while pass@16 stays similar, but the mechanism is consistent with QAE's gating: by preventing entropy explosion (which dilutes probability mass) and entropy collapse (which traps the model in narrow modes), QAE helps the model assign higher probability to the correct answer without losing the ability to occasionally sample it when it isn't the top prediction.

#### Training Dynamics: Entropy Stabilization and the 80/20 Sparsity Pattern

**Headline result:** QAE eliminates the entropy surge observed in DAPO, suppresses negative-advantage-driven entropy growth, and concentrates gradient updates on approximately 20% of generated responses (Figure 5).

**Pass@1 continues to improve while DAPO plateaus (Figure 5, left).** The training curve on AIME'24 (Qwen3-8B-Base) shows DAPO's pass@1 rising to approximately 40% by step 100 and then stalling, while QAE's pass@1 continues climbing past step 250 to approximately 48%. DAPO's pass@16 reaches roughly 70% and stays flat; QAE's pass@16 is comparable but reported as "similar" without quantitative detail in the plot. The figure shows training out to 600 steps, substantially longer than the 300-step window used in most other figures, but pass@1 curves after step 300 for DAPO are not shown — the text states DAPO "exhibits entropy surge and pass@1 stalls" from step ~100, while QAE "maintains stable training and continues to improve."

**Negative-advantage entropy is the primary instability driver (Figure 5, middle).** Decomposing policy entropy by advantage sign reveals that DAPO's entropy growth is dominated by negative-advantage samples — their entropy rises sharply in early training, while positive-advantage sample entropy remains relatively stable. Under QAE, both positive and negative entropy are suppressed, and the overall mean entropy remains bounded. This is the direct empirical validation of the paper's core diagnostic (Section 3.2, Observation 3): the mean baseline mishandles negative-advantage samples, and QAE fixes this by zeroing out those advantages on hard queries. The paper does not report the actual entropy values on the y-axis — the curve is shown in Figure 5 (middle) but must be read from the plot, and the axis labels indicate entropy ranges from approximately 0.2 to 1.6 for DAPO and a narrower, lower range for QAE.

**Response-level sparsity: ~80% of responses receive zero advantage (Figure 5, right).** Throughout training, the proportion of responses with exactly zero advantage hovers around 80% under QAE. The remaining ~20% is split among positive advantages (~15%, shown as `Advantage ≈ 1.0`), negative advantages (~3–5%, shown as `Advantage ≈ -1.0`), and a tiny fraction of "other" (non-zero but non-unit advantages, likely from the denominator normalization when `std` is not exactly `sqrt(p(1-p))` due to finite `ε`). The paper does not report the corresponding fractions for the DAPO baseline. This sparsity is the empirical signature of QAE's two-regime gate: on hard queries, only correct responses get non-zero advantage; on easy queries, only incorrect responses get non-zero advantage. The 80/20 split is presented as a robust empirical regularity at `K = 0.4`, not as a theoretical guarantee.

**Entropy stabilization on 8B and 14B models (Figures 10 and 11, Appendix B.4).** Training dynamics curves for both model sizes confirm that QAE consistently reduces and stabilizes policy entropy, keeps response length bounded, and produces smoother accuracy trajectories. On Qwen3-8B (Figure 10), the baseline exhibits a pronounced entropy spike around step 100 and a corresponding divergence in response length, while QAE's entropy stays low and flat and response length grows more gradually. On Qwen3-14B (Figure 11), the baseline entropy is lower and less spiky than on 8B, but QAE still reduces it further and produces a more monotonic accuracy curve. The response length under QAE stays bounded around 2,000–4,000 tokens on 8B and 4,000–6,000 on 14B, compared to the baseline which shows more volatile and upward-trending lengths on 8B.

**Anthropomorphic token co-growth (Figures 7 and 8, Appendix B.2).** The paper tracks "anthropomorphic tokens" — markers like "wait," "perhaps," "alternatively" that Yang et al. (2025b) identify as indicators of exploratory "aha-moment" reasoning — as a diagnostic of exploration quality. Under DAPO with Clip-Higher (Figure 2, right), anthropomorphic token counts spike in early training (steps 20–80) and then decouple from pass@1 — token counts decline while accuracy plateaus, indicating that early exploration was not converted into sustained reasoning improvement. Under QAE (Figure 7), anthropomorphic tokens and pass@1 rise together throughout training ("co-growth"), and even when token counts eventually stabilize, pass@1 continues improving, indicating a shift from "more randomness" to targeted refinement. Token-level snapshots at steps 20/80/200 (Figure 8) show that under QAE, anthropomorphic markers like "perhaps" and "wait" emerge in a controlled manner at step 80 and persist at step 200, rather than being overtaken by rigid templates like "so" and "let" as in the DAPO baseline (Figure 3).

**Interpretation:** These dynamics results are the paper's strongest empirical evidence. They directly validate the mechanism proposed in Sections 3 and 4: QAE prevents entropy explosion by zeroing out negative advantages on hard queries (Figure 5, middle), sparsifies updates to focus on informative samples (Figure 5, right), and thereby sustains productive exploration that converts into continued accuracy gains (Figures 7 and 8). The contrast with DAPO's trajectory — early anthropomorphic token spike followed by decoupling and plateau — provides a clean before-and-after demonstration that the quantile baseline changes the qualitative nature of the optimization dynamics, not just the final accuracy.

#### K Sensitivity: Entropy, Accuracy, and Response Length Trade-offs

**Headline result:** The quantile parameter `K` directly controls policy entropy, response length, and accuracy, with `K = 0.4` providing a robust balance for Clip-Higher configurations (Figure 9, Appendix B.3).

**K = 0.2 (low, most queries treated as "easy"):** Entropy is low and flat (~0.2–0.4), response length stays around 2,000–4,000 tokens, and accuracy (AIME'24 @ 32 samples) improves slowly to approximately 0.30–0.35. The low entropy indicates a collapse-prone regime where positive advantages on easy queries drive the policy toward deterministic behavior. Accuracy gains are limited because insufficient exploration prevents the model from discovering new reasoning patterns.

**K = 0.4 (default):** Entropy is moderate and relatively stable (~0.4–0.8), response length grows gradually to ~4,000–6,000 tokens, and accuracy climbs to approximately 0.40–0.45. This is the recommended setting — the paper states it "avoids the high-entropy instability seen at K = 0.8 while maintaining sufficient stochasticity to prevent collapse."

**K = 0.6 (high):** Entropy is higher (~0.6–1.2) and more volatile, response length grows more aggressively to ~6,000–10,000 tokens, and accuracy peaks around 0.35–0.40 before declining or plateauing. The higher entropy reflects more queries being classified as "hard" (since `1-K = 0.4`), so fewer incorrect responses receive negative advantages, and the policy explores more broadly but also less efficiently.

**K = 0.8 (very high):** Entropy is highest and most volatile (~1.0–3.5), response length spikes to ~10,000–14,000 tokens, and accuracy plateaus early around 0.25–0.35. This is the "entropy explosion" regime: most queries are "hard" (baseline = 0), so incorrect responses are largely ignored, and the policy wanders without sufficient negative signal to prune unproductive regions. Accuracy stalls because the model is exploring but not converting exploration into improved reasoning — consistent with the entropy explosion diagnosis in Section 3.

**Interpretation:** The K-sensitivity analysis empirically confirms the theoretical claim that `K` is a controllable knob for the exploration–exploitation trade-off. The progression from `K = 0.2` (collapse-prone, low entropy, limited accuracy) through `K = 0.4` (balanced) to `K = 0.8` (explosion-prone, high entropy, volatile) matches the monotonic relationship between baseline value and entropy change established in Proposition 4.2. Lower `K` → higher baseline on average → lower entropy → more exploitation. Higher `K` → lower baseline on average → higher entropy → more exploration. The optimal `K = 0.4` sits at the boundary between the two failure modes. The paper does not provide a formal procedure for selecting `K` beyond the heuristic of inspecting baseline entropy, nor does it test whether the optimal `K` varies by model size, dataset, or training stage.

#### Masking Ablation: Diagnosing Collapse vs. Explosion Regimes

**Headline result:** NEG-MASK (preventing explosion) is the critical mechanism when high-end clipping is weak (`ε_high = 0.28`), while POS-MASK (preventing collapse) dominates when clipping is strong (`ε_high = 0.20`), confirming that QAE's two-sided mechanism provides protection against both failure modes (Figure 6b–c).

**With weak clipping (`ε_high = 0.28`, Figure 6b):** On AIME'25, DAPO achieves approximately 31.5 pass@1 (read from bar chart). NEG-MASK improves to approximately 35.5, approaching QAE's approximately 37.0. POS-MASK reaches only approximately 33.0, substantially below both. On AIME'24, DAPO is at approximately 40.0, NEG-MASK at approximately 43.5, QAE at approximately 46.0, and POS-MASK at approximately 41.0. The ordering is clear: NEG-MASK ≫ POS-MASK, and NEG-MASK nearly recovers full QAE performance. This confirms that when `ε_high` is permissive (allowing large positive updates), the dominant failure mode is entropy explosion driven by negative-advantage samples, and suppressing those negative updates (NEG-MASK) is the primary mechanism of improvement.

**With strong clipping (`ε_high = 0.20`, Figure 6c):** The ordering flips. On AIME'25, DAPO achieves approximately 29.0, POS-MASK approximately 33.0, QAE approximately 34.5, and NEG-MASK approximately 30.5. On AIME'24, DAPO at approximately 37.5, POS-MASK at approximately 41.5, QAE at approximately 42.0, and NEG-MASK at approximately 38.5. Now POS-MASK ≫ NEG-MASK, and POS-MASK nearly matches QAE. With strong clipping constraining positive updates, entropy collapse becomes the dominant risk — over-reinforcement of correct responses on easy queries drives premature determinism. POS-MASK prevents this by silencing positive updates on easy queries, and this is the primary mechanism.

**Interpretation:** The masking ablation is the paper's most elegant experimental contribution. It provides direct causal evidence for the two-regime theory: the two sides of QAE's gate (positive-masking and negative-masking) correspond to the two entropy failure modes (collapse and explosion), and which side matters depends on the clipping configuration. It also explains why prior one-sided methods are fragile: Clip-Higher (which adjusts `ε_high`) shifts the dominant failure mode without solving it, so tuning the clip threshold trades collapse risk for explosion risk. QAE's two-sided gate sidesteps this trade-off entirely by providing both protections simultaneously. The paper does not report the combined POS-MASK + NEG-MASK (which should be equivalent to QAE, since both masks together implement the full quantile gating) — this would have been a useful sanity check that the masks are independent and additive, but the near-equivalence of NEG-MASK (or POS-MASK, depending on regime) to QAE already suggests the unmasked side contributes little in the dominant failure regime.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Varying `ε_high` in DAPO (Table 1):** The high-end clip threshold controls the maximum allowed probability ratio for positive-advantage updates. Sweeping `ε_high` from 0.20 to 0.28 on AIME'24 with Qwen3-8B-Base (QAE not applied) produces pass@1 values of 32.29 (0.20), 34.90 (0.22), 34.17 (0.24), 40.63 (0.26), and 39.69 (0.28). The peak at `ε_high = 0.26` (40.63%) is a modest improvement over the extremes, but the 0.20→0.28 range only spans ~8 percentage points overall. This supports the paper's claim that "simply adjusting token-level clipping cannot resolve the core exploration–exploitation tension" — even the optimal `ε_high` leaves substantial room for improvement that QAE later fills (48.23% with QAE at `ε_high = 0.28`).

**QAE with different base algorithms (Table 2):** The drop-in compatibility is tested with Clip-Cov, KL-Cov, and GSPO, all at `K = 0.4`. QAE provides gains on top of Clip-Cov (e.g., +13.3% relative on AIME'25 pass@1), mixed results on KL-Cov (+0.3% on AIME'25, -0.5% on AIME'24), and gains on GSPO except for AMC'23 where it slightly declines (-0.7% pass@1, -2.2% pass@16). The authors attribute this to orthogonality — baseline design and token-level/sequence-level controls operate on independent axes — but the KL-Cov and GSPO-AMC'23 results suggest that the composition is not uniformly additive. There may be cases where the gating introduced by QAE conflicts with the gating already implicitly present in the base method's regularization, but the paper does not investigate this.

**Anthropomorphic token diagnostics (Figures 2, 3, 7, 8):** These are monitoring tools, not controlled ablations, but they serve as robustness checks for the claim that QAE sustains productive exploration. Under DAPO (Figure 2), anthropomorphic tokens spike early and then decouple from accuracy. Under QAE (Figure 7), they grow in tandem with accuracy throughout training. Token-level snapshots (Figures 3 vs. 8) show that DAPO's token distribution homogenizes toward formulaic reasoning markers ("so," "let") while QAE maintains diversity of exploratory markers ("perhaps," "wait," "alternatively"). The paper does not provide a quantitative metric for this diversity — it relies on visual inspection of probability mass bar charts — but the pattern is consistent with the entropy stabilization narrative.

**Cold-start GSPO on 30B-A3B (Table 2):** This experiment validates that QAE works when the base model has not been SFT-warmstarted on reasoning data. The 30B-A3B-Base model is a mixture-of-experts architecture with 30B total parameters and 3B active parameters, initialized directly from pretraining. QAE improves AIME'24 pass@1 from 43.75 to 47.50 (+8.6%) and AIME'25 from 31.15 to 32.50 (+4.3%), with comparable or slightly worse AMC'23. This demonstrates that QAE's mechanism does not depend on a specific initialization scheme.

**Missing ablations:** Several experiments that would strengthen the paper are absent. (1) No comparison against a simple **fixed-baseline** (e.g., `b = 0.5` for all queries) — this would isolate whether the quantile's data-adaptive threshold matters, or whether any non-mean baseline provides similar benefits. (2) No test of **continuous quantile interpolation** (e.g., `b = λ·0 + (1-λ)·1` for `λ` depending on `p`) — this would test whether the hard binary gate is necessary or a softer transition works equally well. (3) No **K-sweep on non-Clip-Higher configurations** — the sensitivity analysis in Figure 9 is only shown for DAPO with Clip-Higher; the optimal `K` might differ for Clip-Cov, KL-Cov, or GSPO. (4) No test on **non-math reasoning benchmarks** (code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA) — all results are on AIME and AMC. (5) No **multiple random seeds** for any experiment — all reported numbers are single-run point estimates, and the training dynamics curves show only one trajectory per configuration, making it impossible to assess run-to-run variance. (6) No test of **QAE with a value-network baseline** (PPO) — Proposition 4.2's guarantee is for value-free baselines; whether the quantile idea extends to learned value functions is untested.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does QAE genuinely solve the entropy dilemma, or does it shift the trade-off to a new hyperparameter?

The paper's central claim is that QAE provides "two-sided entropy safety" — provably preventing both collapse and explosion. The empirical evidence for stabilization is strong: entropy curves are flatter and lower under QAE (Figures 5 middle, 10, 11), the entropy spike at step 100 is eliminated, and accuracy continues improving past DAPO's plateau (Figure 5 left). The K-sensitivity analysis (Figure 9) shows that `K = 0.4` sits in a stable regime while `K = 0.2` (collapse-prone) and `K = 0.8` (explosion-prone) verify the two endpoints.

However, the provable guarantee (Proposition 4.2) is for **one-step entropy change under first-order softmax updates** in a bandit simplification. This is a substantial abstraction from the actual training dynamics, which involve token-level policy gradients, clipping, normalization, and the full autoregressive sequence generation process. The proof shows that the quantile baseline achieves the extremal one-step entropy change *directionally* — it does not guarantee bounded entropy over the full training trajectory, nor that the optimal `K` is 0.4 specifically. The paper acknowledges this indirectly by noting that `K` must be tuned (by inspecting entropy, not evaluation metrics), but the gap between the theoretical guarantee (extremal one-step change) and the practical outcome (stable multi-step training) is not explicitly discussed.

Moreover, the claim that baseline design is "the primary mechanism for scaling RLVR" overstates what was demonstrated. The paper shows that QAE improves pass@1 on top of DAPO, Clip-Cov, KL-Cov, and GSPO, but all experiments use small-scale training (17K math problems, 300–600 steps). There is no evidence at the scale of DeepSeek-R1 or o1 training (millions of problems, thousands of steps). The paper's title claims "stabilizing RLVR for LLM reasoning" without qualification, but the experiments only cover Qwen3 models (8B/14B/30B) on math reasoning benchmarks using DAPO-family algorithms. Whether QAE stabilizes RLVR for other model families (Llama, DeepSeek), other reasoning domains (code, science), or at larger scales is unsubstantiated.

#### Are the pass@1 gains practically meaningful, or are they within training noise?

The pass@1 improvements in Table 2 are substantial in relative terms: +21.5% on AIME'24 (8B Clip-Higher), +13.3% on AIME'25 (8B Clip-Cov), +8.6% on AIME'24 (30B GSPO). In absolute percentage points, the gains range from negligible (AMC'23, where the baseline is already >90%) to large (AIME'24, +8.5 points on 8B Clip-Higher). The AIME benchmark is a challenging competition math dataset where even single-point gains are non-trivial.

However, the absence of error bars, confidence intervals, or multiple random seeds makes it impossible to assess the statistical reliability of these differences. The numbers in Table 2 are presented to two decimal places (e.g., 48.23) — implying precision that is likely spurious given the single-run evaluation protocol and the fact that AIME'24 has only 30 questions (so pass@1 changes in units of ~3.3 percentage points per question). The paper does not specify the exact number of questions in AIME'24, AIME'25, or AMC'23. If AIME'24 has 30 questions, then the 39.69 → 48.23 improvement corresponds to the model solving roughly 2–3 more questions correctly — a meaningful gain but one that could plausibly arise from run-to-run variance without the baseline change.

The pass@16 results provide a partial robustness check: QAE generally maintains or slightly improves pass@16, with a few declines (KL-Cov AIME'24 pass@1 -0.5%, GSPO AMC'23 pass@1 -0.7% and pass@16 -2.2%). The pass@16 stability suggests that QAE is not simply finding a different local optimum with better pass@1 at the expense of overall capability — it's genuinely improving the policy's top-1 accuracy without degrading its coverage. However, the negative results on KL-Cov and GSPO-AMC'23 are unexplained and could indicate that QAE's mechanism sometimes conflicts with the base algorithm's implicit gating or the specific benchmark's difficulty distribution.

#### Does the masking ablation genuinely validate the two-regime theory, or does it just show that removing some updates helps?

The masking ablation (Figure 6b–c) is cleverly designed and provides compelling evidence for the two-regime mechanism. The key finding — that NEG-MASK dominates under weak clipping (explosion regime) and POS-MASK dominates under strong clipping (collapse regime) — is a clean demonstration that the two sides of QAE address different failure modes, and that the dominant mode shifts with the clipping hyperparameter.

However, the ablation does not completely rule out an alternative interpretation: that *any* sparsification of updates (masking some fraction of responses) would produce similar benefits, regardless of whether the masking is regime-conditional. A **random masking baseline** — where a fixed fraction of responses (e.g., 80%) is randomly assigned zero advantage, regardless of correctness or query difficulty — would test whether the benefit comes from sparsity per se or from the specific difficulty-conditional gating. If random masking also improves performance, then QAE's mechanism is less about the two-regime theory and more about the general principle that dense gradient updates in RLVR are suboptimal. The paper does not include this ablation.

Relatedly, the paper does not test **POS-MASK + NEG-MASK** as a combined ablation. Since POS-MASK masks positive updates on easy queries (leaving negatives on easy, and both positives and negatives on hard queries), and NEG-MASK masks negative updates on hard queries (leaving positives on hard, and both positives and negatives on easy queries), their combination should be exactly QAE. Verifying that POS-MASK + NEG-MASK = QAE would confirm that the two masks are independent and additive, and that there is no interaction effect from applying both simultaneously. The near-recovery of QAE performance by the dominant mask in each regime suggests this is true, but direct verification would close the loop.

#### How dependent are the results on the specific configuration of DAPO and the `K = 0.4` default?

The paper's strong result is the drop-in compatibility: QAE with `K = 0.4` improves DAPO, Clip-Cov, KL-Cov, and GSPO without changing any of their hyperparameters. This is an appealing simplicity — one knob, one value, works everywhere.

The sensitivity analysis (Figure 9) shows that `K = 0.4` is indeed a robust choice for DAPO with Clip-Higher (`ε_high = 0.28`), but the paper does not show K-sensitivity for any other base configuration. Would `K = 0.4` still be optimal for Clip-Cov, which already applies stronger entropy control? Would a lower `K` work better for GSPO, which uses much tighter clipping ranges? The KL-Cov results (mixed pass@1, consistent pass@16 gains) suggest that `K = 0.4` may not be uniformly optimal, and the paper provides no guidance on how to adapt `K` to the base method beyond the entropy-inspection heuristic (which itself requires running the baseline first to measure its entropy, adding a step to the workflow).

Additionally, all experiments use the DAPO-Math-17K training dataset. The distribution of query difficulties in this dataset — what fraction are "hard" vs. "easy" for the base model, and how that distribution shifts during training — directly determines the effective update fraction under QAE. On a dataset with very different difficulty characteristics (e.g., mostly very hard or mostly very easy problems), the optimal `K` would likely differ. The paper does not analyze the difficulty distribution of the training data or how it interacts with the `K` threshold.

#### What is the practical significance of the 80/20 sparsity pattern?

The observation that ~80% of responses receive zero advantage under QAE (Figure 5, right) is striking and provides a concrete diagnostic. The paper frames this as evidence that "selection, not update magnitude, is the primary bottleneck" in RLVR, and suggests that prior methods waste compute on uninformative gradient contributions.

However, the 80/20 split is an empirical observation at `K = 0.4`, not a designed property or a theoretical prediction. The paper does not show that this specific sparsity ratio is optimal — it might be that 60/40 or 90/10 would work equally well or better. The K-sensitivity analysis (Figure 9) shows that performance degrades at both extremes (`K = 0.2` with presumably higher update fraction, `K = 0.8` with lower), but the middle region (`K = 0.4` and `K = 0.6`) is not finely resolved enough to claim that 80% is special.

More practically, the sparsity does not translate to computational savings in the current implementation. The model still generates `G` responses per query and verifies all of them — the 80% that receive zero advantage still consume generation and verification compute. The paper does not propose adaptive sampling strategies that would reduce generation budget on queries or responses that are likely to be masked. The sparsity is a signal that such strategies could be developed, but no efficiency gains are realized in the reported experiments.

#### Missing comparisons that would strengthen the paper

Several baselines and experiments are notably absent:

- **Value-network PPO:** The paper claims that baseline design is "the primary mechanism for scaling RLVR," but the comparison is entirely within value-free methods. A natural test would be to compare QAE against a PPO baseline with a learned value function — does the quantile baseline outperform a trained critic, or does it simply close the gap between value-free and value-based methods? The paper's title says "stabilizing RLVR" but only tests on one class of RLVR algorithms.

- **Offline methods (DPO, rejection sampling):** The paper focuses on online RL, but offline preference optimization and rejection sampling fine-tuning are strong baselines for math reasoning. If QAE's main benefit is preventing entropy explosion/collapse — problems specific to online policy optimization — then it's worth asking whether offline methods avoid these issues entirely.

- **GRPO without DAPO modifications:** DAPO adds several heuristics on top of GRPO (dynamic sampling, Clip-Higher, overlong reward shaping, token-level normalization). Testing QAE on a simpler GRPO baseline would isolate whether the quantile baseline's benefits are independent of these heuristics or interact with them.

- **Iterated/SFT baselines:** Several recent works show that simply fine-tuning on correct reasoning traces (STaR, ReST) can match or exceed RLVR performance. Comparing QAE against a strong SFT baseline would contextualize whether the gains from RLVR stabilization are meaningful relative to simpler approaches that avoid online optimization entirely.

- **Longer training and scaling curves:** All experiments run for 300–600 steps. It's unclear whether QAE's benefits persist, diminish, or amplify at larger scales (more steps, larger models, more data). The paper's title implies general applicability, but the evidence is at a single, modest scale.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Entropy Safety Proof Assumes a Simplified Setting That Abstracts Away the Full Training Dynamics

**The assumption or constraint.** Proposition 4.2 — the two-sided entropy safety guarantee that is the paper's primary theoretical contribution — is proven under a **response-level bandit reduction** with first-order softmax logit updates. In this simplified setting, generating a full multi-token reasoning trace is treated as a single action, and the policy update is a one-step logit increment proportional to the advantage. The paper states this explicitly in Section 4.3:

> "Adopt a bandit reduction in which producing a full response y to q is a single action... Under first-order logit updates of a softmax policy with step size η > 0, the entropy–covariance identity (adapted from Cui et al. (2025)) yields, ∆H(q) ≈ −η Cov..."

This abstracts away the actual training procedure, which involves: (a) token-level autoregressive generation with per-token probability ratios, (b) PPO-style clipping (`min(rt Â, clip(rt, 1−ε, 1+ε) Â)`), (c) token-level loss normalization by total sequence length, (d) the standard deviation denominator in the advantage (which changes the effective step size per query), and (e) multi-step optimization with momentum-based optimizers (AdamW).

**The consequence.** The guarantee is **directional but not quantitative**. It establishes that the K-quantile baseline minimizes entropy increase in the low-success regime and maximizes it in the high-success regime *relative to any other baseline in [0, 1]*, but it does not provide bounds on the absolute magnitude of the entropy change, nor does it guarantee that the entropy will remain within a specific productive range over the full course of training. The proof shows `∆H(b_K) ≤ ∆H(b)` for `p ≤ 1−K`, but `∆H(b_K)` could still be positive — meaning entropy could still grow on hard queries, just more slowly. Similarly, `∆H(b_K) ≥ ∆H(b)` for `p > 1−K` doesn't guarantee that entropy *increases* — it only guarantees it increases *more* than under the mean baseline, which might still mean entropy decreases if the mean baseline would have caused a larger decrease. The proof does not establish that QAE prevents either collapse or explosion in an absolute sense; it only establishes that QAE is the *best possible* baseline choice in [0, 1] for mitigating each failure mode one step at a time.

Additionally, the bandit reduction ignores the **credit assignment within a response**. In the bandit model, an incorrect response on a hard query gets exactly zero advantage — no update at all. In the actual token-level training, the advantage is broadcast to every token in the response. The bandit model cannot capture phenomena like partial correctness (a response that gets the wrong final answer but contains useful intermediate reasoning), which is precisely the kind of signal that value-free RL struggles with. The paper's analysis in Section 3.2 demonstrates that token-level controls produce homogenized exploration (Figure 3), but the theoretical framework doesn't provide tools to analyze or guarantee against this token-level homogenization — it only addresses the response-level entropy.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The empirical results (Figures 5, 10, 11) show that QAE *does* stabilize entropy and sustain accuracy gains in practice, which provides supporting evidence that the bandit-level insight transfers to the full setting. The K-sensitivity analysis (Figure 9) is consistent with the theoretical monotonicity: higher K → lower baseline → higher entropy, as predicted. However, the quantitative mapping between the proof's predictions and the observed entropy values is not established — the proof doesn't predict *how much* entropy should change, only the sign relative to other baselines. The observed stability at `K = 0.4` is an empirical finding, not a consequence of the proof.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the gap between theory and practice only indirectly, by noting in the Proposition 4.2 statement that it uses first-order updates and the bandit reduction (Section 4.3, Appendix A.2). There is no discussion of whether the token-level clipping, normalization, or multi-step dynamics could violate the entropy monotonicity property, nor any attempt to extend the proof to the full autoregressive setting. The paper presents the proof as a theoretical justification for the mechanism, but does not claim it provides quantitative guarantees for the full training procedure. The limitation is thus partially acknowledged by the proof's explicit assumptions, but the implications of those assumptions for the strength of the claim are not analyzed.

### 6.2 All Experiments Are on a Single Model Family, Single Task Domain, and Single Training Dataset

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses models from the **Qwen3 family** (8B, 14B, 30B-A3B) trained on the **DAPO-Math-17K dataset** and evaluated on **math competition benchmarks** (AIME'24, AIME'25, AMC'23). There are no experiments on other model families (e.g., Llama, DeepSeek, Gemma), other reasoning domains (code generation, logical reasoning, scientific question answering, multi-step planning), or other training datasets. The paper states in Section 5: "We apply this method to the Qwen3-32B and Qwen3-8B base models, training them on the DAPO-Math-17K dataset (Yu et al., 2025)."

**The consequence.** Three forms of uncertainty arise:

**Model-family dependence.** The Qwen3 models share architectural DNA (attention mechanism, tokenizer, pretraining data mixture, training recipe). It is unknown whether QAE's mechanism — and specifically the optimal `K = 0.4` — transfers to models with different inductive biases. For instance, models pretrained with different data mixtures might have different baseline entropy profiles, different distributions of `p(q)` across the training set, or different sensitivities to negative-advantage updates. The paper provides no evidence that QAE's benefits are architecture-independent. A practitioner using a Llama- or DeepSeek-based model cannot assume that `K = 0.4` is optimal, and the recommended entropy-inspection heuristic for choosing `K` (Section 6) requires running the baseline first — which adds friction and compute cost.

**Task-domain dependence.** All results are on math reasoning with verifiable ground-truth answers (exact string match grading). Math reasoning has specific properties that may interact with QAE's mechanism: (a) the reward signal is perfectly clean and binary (unlike, e.g., code generation where unit tests may pass for partially correct solutions), (b) the difficulty distribution is relatively well-behaved (most problems in the training set are neither trivially easy nor impossibly hard for the base model), and (c) exploration manifests through symbolic manipulation rather than semantic diversity. For code generation — where the reward might be continuous (fraction of test cases passed) or where exploration involves different algorithmic approaches — the binary-reward reduction (Equation 4) would need modification, and the two-regime gate might not cleanly separate "hard" from "easy" queries. For open-ended tasks without ground-truth verifiability (summarization, dialogue), the entire RLVR paradigm requires a learned reward model, and it's unclear whether QAE's quantile baseline would interact differently with learned (noisy, potentially miscalibrated) rewards than with oracle binary rewards.

**Dataset-dependence.** The DAPO-Math-17K dataset has a specific difficulty distribution that determines the fraction of queries falling into each regime for a given `K`. If the training data's difficulty distribution shifts — for example, a curriculum that starts with easier problems and progresses to harder ones — the effective behavior of a fixed `K` would change during training. At the start (mostly easy queries), `K = 0.4` would classify most queries as "easy" (baseline = 1), focusing updates on failures. Later (more hard queries), more queries would be "hard" (baseline = 0), focusing updates on successes. This implicit curriculum might be beneficial, but it's uncontrolled — the paper provides no analysis of how the distribution of `p(q)` evolves during training or how that interacts with the fixed `K = 0.4`. A practitioner training on a dataset with very different difficulty characteristics (e.g., mostly very hard problems where `p(q)` is near zero for most of training) would find that `K = 0.4` classifies almost everything as "hard," meaning incorrect responses are almost never penalized — which might lead to the `K = 0.8`-like high-entropy regime from Figure 9.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper demonstrates consistent gains across three model sizes within the Qwen3 family (8B, 14B, 30B-A3B) and across four base algorithms (DAPO, Clip-Cov, KL-Cov, GSPO), which provides some evidence of robustness within the tested regime. The three evaluation benchmarks (AIME'24, AIME'25, AMC'23) cover different years of the same competition, providing difficulty variation but no domain variation. The AMC'23 benchmark is substantially easier (baseline pass@1 > 90% in some configurations), and QAE's gains there are smaller or sometimes negative (GSPO AMC'23: -0.7% pass@1), which could indicate a ceiling effect or a limitation on problems that are already nearly solved. However, there is no evidence about behavior on code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA, or any non-math domain.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss domain or model generalization as a limitation. The title claims "stabilizing RLVR for LLM reasoning" without qualification, and the abstract states the method yields gains "on AIME'24/'25 and AMC'23," which is accurate but leaves the scope ambiguous. The conclusion (Section 8) proposes future work on "Dynamic K" and "PPO integration" but does not mention cross-domain or cross-model evaluation as a priority. A practitioner considering QAE for a non-math reasoning task or a non-Qwen3 model has no guidance from this paper on what to expect.

### 6.3 No Statistical Significance or Run-to-Run Variance Is Reported for Any Result

**The assumption or constraint.** All quantitative results in the paper — the pass@1 and pass@16 numbers in Table 2, the training dynamics curves in Figures 5, 10, and 11, the K-sensitivity analysis in Figure 9, and the masking ablation in Figure 6 — are presented as **single-run point estimates**. The paper reports pass@1 to two decimal places (e.g., 48.23 on AIME'24 for Qwen3-8B with QAE) without any indication of measurement error. There are no confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical significance tests. The number of evaluation questions per benchmark is not stated (AIME typically has 30 questions; AMC has 25), meaning pass@1 changes are quantized in increments of ~3.3–4.0 percentage points per question.

**The consequence.** The reliability of the reported gains is impossible to assess from the paper alone. A practitioner cannot determine whether the +8.54 percentage point pass@1 gain on AIME'24 (8B Clip-Higher, 39.69 → 48.23) is a robust improvement or could plausibly arise from different random seeds, different data ordering, or different sampling stochasticity. This uncertainty is compounded by the small evaluation set sizes: if AIME'24 has 30 questions, the 39.69% → 48.23% improvement corresponds to the model solving roughly 12 → 14–15 questions correctly — a change of 2–3 questions. A single additional correct answer (due to sampling luck or a slightly different training trajectory) would shift pass@1 by ~3.3 percentage points.

The training dynamics curves (Figures 5, 10, 11) show only one trajectory per configuration. Without multiple runs, it is impossible to distinguish a genuine effect (QAE stabilizes entropy and extends accuracy growth) from a single-run artifact (the baseline happened to have a worse random seed, or QAE happened to have a particularly favorable one). The fact that the pattern replicates across two model sizes (8B and 14B) in Appendix B.4 is some evidence against single-run artifacts, but two runs per configuration are still insufficient for statistical inference.

The K-sensitivity analysis (Figure 9) shows four curves (K = 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8), each presumably from a single run. The boundaries between these curves — particularly between K = 0.4 and K = 0.6 in terms of final accuracy — could shift substantially with different random seeds. The paper's recommendation of K = 0.4 as the default is based on this single-run comparison.

The masking ablation (Figure 6b–c) compares DAPO, POS-MASK, NEG-MASK, and QAE, each as a single bar per benchmark. The relative ordering (NEG-MASK outperforming POS-MASK under weak clipping, and vice versa under strong clipping) is the paper's key causal evidence for the two-regime theory. If these orderings are not statistically significant — say, if NEG-MASK and POS-MASK differ by less than the run-to-run standard deviation — the causal interpretation would be substantially weakened.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. There is no mention of multiple random seeds, standard deviations, or statistical tests anywhere in the paper. The only replication across runs is the implicit replication across model sizes (8B and 14B) and base algorithms (DAPO, Clip-Cov, KL-Cov, GSPO), which provides some correlational evidence that the gains are not purely a single-run fluke, but does not substitute for direct measurement of variance.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. This is a standard practice in many RLVR papers (including the DAPO and GRPO papers that serve as baselines), but it is nonetheless a limitation that affects the strength of the claims. The paper could report pass@1 with standard errors from multiple evaluation runs, or at minimum state the number of questions per benchmark so readers can compute the quantization granularity. The conclusion (Section 8) does not mention this as a limitation or as an area for more rigorous evaluation in future work.

### 6.4 The Choice of K = 0.4 as a Universal Default Is Insufficiently Justified and May Not Transfer

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper uses `K = 0.4` as the quantile parameter for **all experiments** — across three model sizes, four base algorithms, and three benchmarks. The justification for this choice is a single heuristic (Section 6):

> "Operational rule-of-thumb: we select K once per baseline by inspecting the entropy of the baseline policy, rather than the evaluation metric. When entropy is low (risk of mode collapse), choose K = 0.6 to inject diversity; when entropy is high (risk of unstable updates), choose K = 0.4 to temper exploration. Since all our recipes use Clip-Higher, we default to K = 0.4."

The K-sensitivity analysis (Figure 9, Appendix B.3) only tests K on **one configuration**: Qwen3-8B-Base with DAPO + Clip-Higher (`ε_high = 0.28`). There is no sensitivity analysis for K with Clip-Cov, KL-Cov, GSPO, the 14B model, or the 30B-A3B model.

**The consequence.** Several failure modes are possible:

**Suboptimal K for non-Clip-Higher configurations.** Clip-Cov and KL-Cov already apply token-level entropy control, which changes the baseline entropy profile of the training run. A practitioner using these methods might need a different K than the Clip-Higher default. The mixed QAE results on KL-Cov (Table 2: pass@1 -0.5% on AIME'24) could indicate that `K = 0.4` is suboptimal for that configuration — perhaps KL-Cov's regularization already provides strong collapse protection, making a lower K (more exploitation) more appropriate. But without a K-sweep, this remains speculation.

**Interaction between K and model scale.** The K-sensitivity in Figure 9 is on the 8B model. Larger models may have different baseline entropy profiles (the 14B model in Figure 11 shows lower and less spiky entropy than the 8B model in Figure 10 even under the DAPO baseline). If a larger model is less explosion-prone, the optimal K might be lower (to prevent under-exploration). Conversely, if a model starts with very low entropy, a higher K might be needed. The paper provides no guidance on how optimal K scales with model size.

**Interaction between K and training data difficulty distribution.** The K-sensitivity analysis is on the DAPO-Math-17K dataset. As discussed in Limitation 6.2, if a different dataset has a different difficulty distribution, the fraction of queries classified as "hard" vs. "easy" at a given K changes, which changes the effective update sparsity and entropy dynamics. The paper's K = 0.4 produces ~80% zero advantages on DAPO-Math-17K (Figure 5, right), but this 80/20 split is an emergent property of the interaction between K and the dataset's difficulty distribution, not a designed outcome. On a harder dataset, the same K would produce a higher fraction of zero advantages (since more queries are "hard" and have baseline = 0, zeroing out incorrect responses), potentially drifting toward the high-entropy K = 0.8 regime. On an easier dataset, the opposite would occur.

**The entropy-inspection heuristic requires running the baseline.** To apply the recommended rule-of-thumb ("inspect the entropy of the baseline policy"), a practitioner must first train the baseline method for some number of steps to observe its entropy profile, then select K, then restart training with QAE. This is computationally expensive — it essentially doubles the training cost for the first deployment. The paper does not discuss whether there are cheaper ways to estimate the appropriate K (e.g., a short probe run, or a heuristic based on model size and dataset statistics).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The K-sensitivity analysis (Figure 9) provides credible evidence that `K = 0.4` is a good default for the specific configuration tested (8B, Clip-Higher, DAPO-Math-17K). The consistent gains across configurations in Table 2 suggest that `K = 0.4` is not wildly inappropriate for the other tested methods, but they do not demonstrate that it is optimal. The KL-Cov negative pass@1 result and the GSPO AMC'23 negative result hint at possible suboptimality.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through the entropy-inspection heuristic, but the heuristic's practical cost is not discussed, and its validity is not empirically tested (the paper doesn't show that applying the heuristic to a new configuration would recover the optimal K). The conclusion proposes "Automatic K: Adapt K to model state" as future work, acknowledging that manual tuning is a limitation. However, the paper's strong claim that `K = 0.4` works as a universal drop-in is not fully supported by the evidence.

### 6.5 QAE Assumes Binary Rewards and Clean Verifiability, Limiting Applicability to Non-Math Domains

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire mechanism of QAE — the reduction of the K-quantile baseline to a threshold on `p(q)` (Equation 4), the two-regime gate, and the sparsification pattern — depends on the assumption that **rewards are binary** (`R_i ∈ {0, 1}`). The paper states this in the problem setup (Section 2): "Each response is assigned a binary reward Ri based on its correctness against a ground-truth answer a." The binary reward structure is what makes the quantile baseline collapse to either 0 or 1 (since the empirical CDF of binary data has at most two distinct values). It is also what makes the discriminative decomposition (Proposition 4.1) and the entropy safety proof (Proposition 4.2) tractable, as both rely on the reward being Bernoulli-distributed.

All experiments use math competition benchmarks (AIME, AMC) with exact-answer matching against a ground-truth key. The verifiable reward is perfectly clean: either the extracted answer matches exactly, or it doesn't.

**The consequence.** QAE in its current form cannot be directly applied to domains where rewards are **non-binary** or **noisy**. Several important scenarios fall into this category:

**Continuous or multi-level rewards.** In code generation, a solution that passes 8/10 unit tests receives a reward of 0.8 (or could be thresholded to binary, losing information). In mathematical proof generation, partial credit might be assigned for correct intermediate lemmas even if the final proof is incomplete. In multi-step reasoning tasks with rubric-based evaluation, rewards could be ordinal (0, 1, 2, 3) rather than binary. For continuous rewards, the K-quantile baseline would no longer reduce to a simple threshold on the success rate — it would be a more complex function of the empirical reward distribution, potentially taking values between 0 and 1. The two-regime gate would no longer be a clean switch between "hard" and "easy"; it would become a more graded transition, and the theoretical guarantees from Proposition 4.2 would not apply.

**Learned or noisy reward models.** For open-ended generation tasks (summarization, dialogue, creative writing) or for training on preference data where ground-truth correctness is unavailable, RLVR requires a learned reward model (RM). Learned RMs produce continuous scores with calibration errors, bias, and noise. Applying a binary threshold to RM scores (to recover binary rewards) discards information and may introduce systematic errors if the RM is miscalibrated (e.g., systematically overestimating the quality of certain response styles). Applying QAE directly to continuous RM scores would require generalizing the quantile baseline to handle real-valued rewards, and the entropy safety properties might not hold if the reward distribution is not well-behaved.

**Sparse or delayed rewards.** The MATH setup provides immediate, per-response binary rewards. In multi-turn agentic tasks or long-horizon planning, rewards may be sparse (only at the end of an episode) or delayed. QAE's mechanism depends on the per-query group of responses having a well-defined empirical success rate `p(q)`. With sparse rewards, most groups would have `p(q) = 0` (no responses succeed), making K = 0.4 classify almost all queries as "hard" and zeroing out all incorrect responses — the model would get almost no gradient signal except on the rare queries where at least one response succeeds. This is essentially the `K = 0.8` regime from Figure 9, which shows volatile training and early accuracy plateaus.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not test QAE on non-binary rewards, noisy rewards, learned reward models, or sparse-reward settings. There is no discussion of how QAE would need to be modified for these settings, nor any acknowledgment that the binary-reward assumption is a constraint on applicability. The paper's framing (title, abstract, introduction) positions RLVR for LLM reasoning without specifying this constraint, potentially leading practitioners to assume the method applies to any RLVR setting.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not mention the binary-reward assumption as a limitation. The conclusion (Section 8) does not propose extending QAE to continuous or learned rewards. A natural extension — using the K-quantile of continuous reward scores rather than of binary values — is not discussed. The paper also does not discuss whether the method could be applied to the common RLHF setting where a reward model provides continuous scores, which would be the most direct path to broader applicability. A practitioner working on code generation with test-case-based rewards, or on general instruction-following with a reward model, would need to determine independently whether and how to adapt QAE.

### 6.6 No Comparison Against Simple Fixed Baselines or Alternative Sparsification Mechanisms

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's core claim is that the **K-quantile baseline specifically** — with its data-adaptive, two-regime threshold — is the mechanism that resolves the entropy dilemma. The alternative hypothesis is that *any* sparsification of the gradient signal (reducing the fraction of responses that receive non-zero advantage) would provide similar benefits, or that a simpler fixed baseline would work comparably. The paper does not test these alternatives.

Specifically, the paper does not compare QAE against:

**A fixed baseline.** Setting `b = c` for some constant `c ∈ [0, 1]` independent of the query difficulty. For example, `b = 0.5` would give correct responses `Â = 0.5/std` and incorrect responses `Â = -0.5/std`, always treating both outcomes symmetrically but with a fixed baseline rather than the group mean. This would test whether the benefit comes from the baseline being **fixed and non-adaptive** (avoiding the mean's sensitivity to group composition) or from the baseline being **quantile-based and adaptive** (the two-regime gate).

**A random masking baseline.** Keeping the mean baseline but randomly masking (zeroing out) a fraction of responses' advantages — say, 80% to match QAE's sparsity pattern. This would test whether the benefit comes from **sparsity per se** (reducing the number of gradient-contributing samples, which might reduce noise or allow larger per-sample updates) or from **difficulty-conditional sparsity** (masking positives on easy queries and negatives on hard queries specifically).

**A continuous interpolation baseline.** Using `b = λ · 0 + (1 − λ) · 1` where `λ` varies continuously with `p(q)`, rather than the hard threshold at `p = 1−K`. For instance, `b = 1 − p(q)` would set the baseline to the failure rate instead of the success rate (a natural symmetric alternative). This would test whether the hard binary gate is necessary, or whether a softer, continuous transition between regimes works as well or better.

**The consequence.** Without these comparisons, the paper cannot rule out the possibility that QAE's benefits come from a simpler mechanism than the two-regime theory suggests. If a fixed `b = 0.5` or random 80% masking also improves performance over the mean baseline, then QAE's advantage is not specifically about the K-quantile's adaptive gating — it's about the general principle that the mean baseline produces harmful gradient contributions, and *any* method that reduces those contributions helps. The paper's theoretical contribution (Proposition 4.2) would still be valid — the K-quantile does have extremal entropy properties — but the *practical* takeaway might be simpler: sparsify your gradients, and the specific sparsification pattern matters less than the fact of sparsification.

This is particularly relevant because the 80/20 sparsity pattern (Figure 5, right) is presented as evidence of QAE's efficiency, but it could equally be interpreted as QAE simply throwing away 80% of the gradient signal — and the fact that this *helps* is a strong indictment of the mean baseline, but doesn't necessarily validate the specific two-regime gate. The masking ablation (Figure 6b–c) partially addresses this by showing that the *pattern* of masking matters (NEG-MASK ≠ POS-MASK), but neither NEG-MASK nor POS-MASK is a random or fixed baseline — they both use the same difficulty-conditional gating, just one-sided. A random masking baseline would directly test the "sparsity is all you need" hypothesis.

Additionally, the **POS-MASK + NEG-MASK combination** is not tested. This combination should exactly equal QAE (since POS-MASK masks positives on easy queries, NEG-MASK masks negatives on hard queries, and together they implement the full two-regime gate). Verifying this equality would serve as a sanity check that the two masks are independent and that there is no interaction effect. Its absence is a minor gap in the ablation design.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The masking ablation (Figure 6b–c) provides the strongest evidence that the specific masking pattern matters — NEG-MASK outperforms POS-MASK in the explosion regime, and vice versa in the collapse regime. This shows that *how* you mask interacts with the dominant failure mode, which weakens the "any sparsification works" hypothesis. However, the lack of a random or fixed baseline means we cannot quantify *how much* the adaptive gate matters relative to simple sparsification. The K-sensitivity analysis (Figure 9) shows that performance degrades at K = 0.2 and K = 0.8 relative to K = 0.4, which suggests the threshold location matters, but K = 0.2 and K = 0.8 still implement the two-regime gate — they just shift the boundary.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss these missing comparisons or acknowledge the alternative hypothesis that sparsification alone drives the gains. The conclusion does not propose testing simpler baselines in future work. A practitioner evaluating whether to adopt QAE might reasonably wonder whether they could achieve similar stability by simply randomly dropping 80% of gradient contributions (which would be even simpler to implement than a quantile baseline) or by using a fixed baseline of 0.5 (which eliminates the group-adaptivity but also eliminates the mean's sensitivity to outliers). The paper provides no evidence to answer this question.
