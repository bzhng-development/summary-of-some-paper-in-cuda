# WHEN REASONING MEETS ITS LAWS

**ArXiv:** [2512.17901](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.17901)

## 🎯 Pitch

The paper introduces LORE (Laws of Reasoning), a theoretical framework and benchmark (LORE-BENCH) that formalize how Large Reasoning Models should allocate test-time reasoning compute (compute law) and how accuracy should decay with problem complexity (accuracy law), operationalized via monotonicity and compositionality. It shows existing LRMs are broadly monotonic but fail at compositionality, and presents SFT-Compo, a simple supervised fine-tuning method that enforces compute compositionality and yields consistent gains in compositional behavior and downstream reasoning accuracy—improving both efficiency and robustness of model reasoning.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces the **Laws of Reasoning (LORE)**, a theoretical framework that formalizes how Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) should allocate reasoning compute and accuracy as functions of question complexity—specifically positing a **compute law** (reasoning compute should scale linearly with complexity) and an **accuracy law** (accuracy should decay exponentially with complexity). The authors approximate these laws through two tractable properties—monotonicity (more complex questions should receive more compute) and compositionality (the compute for two independent sub-questions should equal the sum of solving each individually)—and evaluate 10 LRMs on a custom benchmark, LORE-BENCH, finding that most models satisfy monotonicity but fail compositionality, with nMAD values as high as 0.528 on reasoning compute. To address this gap, they develop **SFT-Compo**, a supervised fine-tuning method that enforces compute-law compositionality by selecting training examples where reasoning lengths are additive, which yields consistent Pass@1 improvements across six benchmarks—for instance, a +5.0 average gain on the 8B model—while also producing synergistic improvements in monotonicity and accuracy compositionality, establishing that better compliance with reasoning laws strengthens general reasoning capacity even when the laws are enforced through tractable proxies rather than directly measured complexity.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Reasoning Models Allocate Compute Arbitrarily, Not Systematically

The central problem this paper addresses is a fundamental misalignment between how Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) *actually* behave and how they *ought* to behave if they were reasoning efficiently. Despite the impressive performance of models like DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI o1, and Phi-4-Reasoning on complex mathematical and scientific problems, these models exhibit **counterintuitive reasoning patterns** that deviate from basic principles of efficient thinking.

The paper opens with a striking motivating example using DeepSeek-R1 (Figure 1). When asked to compute the sum of 1 through 10, the model uses 525 reasoning tokens and achieves 75% accuracy. When asked to square the number 55, it uses 1,197 reasoning tokens with 38% accuracy. The composite question—"compute the sum of 1 through 10, then square that sum"—would logically require approximately 817 reasoning tokens (the sum of the individual reasoning budgets) and accuracy no worse than the harder sub-question (38%). Instead, DeepSeek-R1 generates only 525 reasoning tokens and achieves just 50% accuracy. This means the model is **thinking less about the harder composite question than it did about the simpler squaring sub-question**—a pattern that violates basic intuitions about how reasoning effort should scale with problem difficulty.

This is not an isolated anomaly. The paper demonstrates that such behavior is widespread across current LRMs (Section 3.3, Figure 5), where composite questions consistently receive reasoning budgets far from the sum of their sub-questions' budgets. The underlying cause is identified as a training data problem: current Chain-of-Thought (CoT) data are "heuristically curated by human annotators or generated through online rollout, rarely constrained by explicit rules, e.g., how much thinking budget to allocate for a given problem" (Section 1). In other words, **models are never taught how long they should think**—they learn reasoning lengths as an artifact of whatever examples happen to appear in their training data, not as a principled function of problem difficulty.

This gap matters because it leads to two well-documented failure modes in LRMs:

- **Overthinking**: The model allocates excessive compute to simple problems, wasting computational resources and potentially introducing errors through unnecessary reasoning steps. This is documented in works like Chen et al. (2024b) and Sui et al. (2025), which show that LRMs often produce lengthy reasoning chains for trivial questions where a direct answer would suffice.

- **Underthinking**: The model allocates insufficient compute to hard problems, failing to explore enough reasoning steps to reach the correct solution. Su et al. (2025), Yang et al. (2025), and Wang et al. (2025) identify this as a significant limitation, where models truncate their reasoning prematurely on complex multi-step problems.

Both failure modes directly harm performance (Stechly et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025a), and they stem from the same root cause: **the absence of a principled framework for reasoning budget allocation**. Without such a framework, empirical heuristics dominate, and models learn suboptimal allocation strategies that are brittle and inconsistent.

### Why This Problem Is Important: Practical, Theoretical, and Safety Implications

The paper's motivation operates on three interconnected levels:

**Practical deployment efficiency.** LRMs are expensive to run. Every reasoning token consumes compute, memory, and wall-clock time. If a model over thinks on simple queries or underthinks on hard ones, the result is either wasted resources or degraded accuracy—both of which harm user experience and cost-effectiveness in production systems. A principled understanding of how reasoning compute should scale with complexity would enable more efficient inference, potentially reducing costs while maintaining or improving accuracy by allocating the right amount of thinking to each query.

**Theoretical understanding of reasoning capabilities.** The field has made enormous empirical progress in building LRMs through reinforcement learning and distillation, but the underlying mechanisms governing their reasoning behavior remain poorly understood. Shojaee et al. (2025) take a step toward examining reasoning through the lens of problem complexity, but their analysis is limited to a constrained puzzle-solving setting. The paper argues that we need **formal laws** that characterize optimal reasoning behavior—analogous to how scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) characterize optimal pretraining compute allocation—to move from ad-hoc empirical improvements to principled design.

**Safety and reliability.** Models that allocate reasoning compute unpredictably are harder to audit, debug, and trust. If a model sometimes overthinks simple arithmetic and sometimes underthinks complex algebra, it becomes difficult to predict its failure modes or establish reliability guarantees. Formalizing desired reasoning behaviors provides a normative standard against which models can be evaluated and improved, which has direct implications for deploying LRMs in high-stakes domains.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper situates its contribution against two broad categories of prior work on controlling reasoning behavior:

**Adaptive post-training techniques.** One line of research attempts to modulate reasoning through supervised fine-tuning with variable-length CoT data. For example, Aggarwal and Welleck (2025) and Team et al. (2025) fine-tune models on examples with different reasoning lengths, aiming to teach the model to match its thinking to problem difficulty. However, these approaches rely on **ad-hoc heuristics** to determine what constitutes appropriate reasoning length for a given problem. They lack a formal principle specifying how compute *should* scale with complexity—they simply expose the model to varied examples and hope it generalizes appropriately. As the paper notes (Section 1), these methods "still behave undesirably in our studies," suggesting that example-based learning without explicit principles is insufficient.

The other post-training strategy involves RL-based length penalty or reward shaping. Approaches like those in Zhang et al. (2025a), Fang et al. (2025), and Liu et al. (2025) add terms to the reward function that penalize or encourage reasoning length. However, these methods treat length as a single global knob—penalize long reasoning to reduce overthinking, or reward long reasoning to encourage thoroughness—without conditioning on problem difficulty. The paper evaluates two length-control models, Thinkless-1.5B-RL-DeepScaleR and AdaptThink-7B-delta0.05, and finds that they still exhibit "considerable deviations" from compositionality (Section 3.3, Table 2), demonstrating that global length control does not inherently produce difficulty-appropriate allocation.

**Test-time modulation.** Another line of work controls reasoning at inference time. Muennighoff et al. (2025) and Fan et al. (2025) dynamically adjust reasoning behavior during generation, while Qiao et al. (2025) and Liu and Wang (2025) use confidence signals to decide when to stop thinking. However, these methods again rely on **empirical heuristics** (e.g., "stop when confidence exceeds a threshold") rather than a principled relationship between complexity and compute. Zhang et al. (2025b) explore test-time compute scaling strategies, but the allocation policies are learned from data rather than derived from formal principles about how reasoning should behave.

The fundamental limitation shared by all prior approaches is that they **lack a theoretical framework** that specifies what optimal reasoning behavior looks like. Without such a framework:

- It is impossible to evaluate whether a model's reasoning allocation is genuinely efficient or merely an artifact of training data biases.
- There is no principled target for training—we can penalize long chains or reward short ones, but we cannot say with confidence what the *right* length is for a given problem.
- The field lacks standardized benchmarks for measuring whether models exhibit desirable reasoning properties, making it difficult to compare methods or track progress.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper makes three fundamental moves that distinguish it from prior work:

**1. From ad-hoc heuristics to formal laws.** Rather than proposing another empirical technique for controlling reasoning length, the paper develops a theoretical framework—the Laws of Reasoning (LORE)—that formalizes how reasoning compute and accuracy should relate to question complexity. The compute law (Hypothesis 1) postulates that $C_\theta(x) \propto \kappa(x)$: for an optimal reasoning model, the reasoning compute scales linearly with the problem's intrinsic complexity (measured as the minimal number of primitive steps needed to solve it). The accuracy law (Hypothesis 2) postulates that $A_\theta(x) = \exp(-\lambda_\theta \kappa(x))$: accuracy decays exponentially with complexity, reflecting the intuition that each reasoning step has some failure probability and all must succeed for the final answer to be correct.

This is a **normative framework**: it specifies how models *should* behave, not how they currently behave. The paper explicitly draws on human reasoning as an inspiration, noting that "human[s] generally adapt their thinking based on problem complexity" (Section 1), and seeks to formalize this intuition mathematically.

**2. From unmeasurable complexity to tractable proxies.** A key challenge is that the theoretical framework depends on $\kappa(x)$—the minimal number of primitive steps to solve a problem—which is "generally intractable" to compute (Section 2.1). The paper solves this by identifying two **properties that are both tractable to measure and theoretically sufficient to imply the laws** (Propositions 1 and 2, with formal proofs in Appendix D):

- **Monotonicity**: If question A is more complex than question B, then the model should allocate at least as much compute to A as to B ($\kappa(x_1) \leq \kappa(x_2) \implies C_\theta(x_1) \leq C_\theta(x_2)$). This requires only relative complexity comparisons, not absolute values.

- **Compositionality**: For two independent questions (defined operationally as questions from disjoint mathematical concept sets), the compute for their composition should equal the sum of solving each individually ($C_\theta(x_1 \oplus x_2) \approx C_\theta(x_1) + C_\theta(x_2)$), and the accuracy should be multiplicative ($A_\theta(x_1 \oplus x_2) = A_\theta(x_1) \cdot A_\theta(x_2)$). This requires only that we can construct composite questions from independent sub-questions.

These properties serve as **empirical litmus tests**: if a model satisfies them, it is behaving consistently with the laws (even though we cannot directly verify the laws themselves due to the intractability of $\kappa(x)$). If a model fails them, it is demonstrably not reasoning optimally.

**3. From evaluating performance to evaluating compliance with principles.** The paper introduces LORE-BENCH, a benchmark designed not to measure raw accuracy but to measure whether models adhere to the desired reasoning properties. LORE-MONO constructs question variants with known complexity orderings (e.g., "apply this operation N times" for N = 1, 2, ..., 30) to test monotonicity. LORE-COMPO constructs composite questions from independent MATH500 sub-questions to test compositionality. This represents a shift in evaluation philosophy: rather than asking "how accurate is the model?" it asks "does the model reason according to principled laws?"

**4. From observing violations to enforcing compliance.** After demonstrating that current LRMs fail compositionality, the paper develops SFT-Compo—a fine-tuning method that directly enforces the compositionality property on reasoning compute. Critically, this demonstrates that the framework is not merely descriptive but **prescriptive**: by enforcing compliance with the laws (even through a tractable proxy like compositionality), the model's general reasoning capability improves. This closes the loop from theory (the laws specify desired behavior) to evaluation (LORE-BENCH measures compliance) to intervention (SFT-Compo enforces compliance).

The paper's positioning can be understood through the three research questions it poses in Section 1: **(Q1) Can we theoretically formalize model reasoning to ensure desirable behavior?** (Yes—the LORE framework.) **(Q2) How can we evaluate whether popular LRMs follow these principles?** (Via LORE-BENCH, measuring monotonicity and compositionality.) **(Q3) Does enforcing these principles further improve general reasoning capabilities?** (Yes—SFT-Compo yields consistent gains across benchmarks.) This three-part structure—theory, evaluation, intervention—organizes the paper's contribution as a complete cycle from formal principles to practical improvements, with the LORE framework serving as the unifying thread.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper develops **LORE (Laws of Reasoning)**, a theoretical framework that prescribes how Large Reasoning Models *should* allocate their reasoning compute and accuracy as functions of question complexity, along with **SFT-Compo**, a supervised fine-tuning method that enforces one of the framework's key properties (compositionality) to improve general reasoning capabilities. The paper solves the problem that current LRMs allocate reasoning compute arbitrarily—overthinking simple problems and underthinking hard ones—by providing a formal principle (compute should scale linearly with complexity) and a practical training intervention that makes models behave closer to this principle, even though the underlying complexity metric is intractable to measure directly.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has three interconnected components operating at different levels of abstraction:

1. **Theoretical Framework (LORE)** — Two laws (compute law and accuracy law) that specify how reasoning compute $C_\theta(x)$ and accuracy $A_\theta(x)$ should relate to question complexity $\kappa(x)$. These laws are formalized in terms of the minimal number of primitive Turing machine steps needed to solve a problem, a quantity that is well-defined but computationally intractable. Two tractable *properties*—monotonicity and compositionality—serve as empirical proxies that are theoretically sufficient to imply the laws and practically measurable without knowing $\kappa(x)$.

2. **Evaluation Benchmark (LORE-BENCH)** — A two-part benchmark that measures whether models satisfy the tractable properties:
   - **LORE-MONO** tests monotonicity by constructing question variants with known complexity orderings (30 variants per seed question, 40 seed questions across math, science, language, and code domains).
   - **LORE-COMPO** tests compositionality by constructing composite questions from pairs of independent MATH500 sub-questions (250 triplets of $(x_1, x_2, x_1 \oplus x_2)$) and measuring the mean absolute deviation from additivity.

3. **Enforcement Method (SFT-Compo)** — A supervised fine-tuning procedure that takes a base LRM and a training dataset, constructs composite question triplets, samples multiple outputs from a stronger teacher model, selects the combination of (sub-question 1 reasoning, sub-question 2 reasoning, composite question reasoning) that best satisfies length additivity *among correct solutions*, and fine-tunes the base model on these selected trajectories to internalize compositional reasoning behavior.

Information flows as follows: the theoretical framework defines what optimal behavior looks like → LORE-BENCH measures whether current models exhibit that behavior → the gap between desired and actual behavior motivates SFT-Compo → SFT-Compo produces training data that enforces the desired property → fine-tuning on this data improves both compliance with the laws and general reasoning accuracy.

#### Formal Definitions: Complexity, Reasoning Compute, and Reasoning Accuracy

The paper builds its framework on three foundational concepts defined in Section 2.1. These definitions establish the theoretical primitives that the laws govern and the properties measure.

**Complexity $\kappa(x)$.** The paper defines question complexity in terms of an idealized computational model: a fixed deterministic Turing machine with a binary verifier.

Specifically, let a **unit-cost primitive step** be a single valid transition of this Turing machine. Let $\tau$ be any finite sequence of such primitive steps, with length $\ell(\tau) \in \mathbb{N}$. Let $v(x, \tau) \in \{0, 1\}$ be a binary verifier that accepts $(x, \tau)$ if and only if $\tau$ is a valid solution sequence for question $x$.

Then the complexity of question $x$ is:

$$\kappa(x) \triangleq \min \{\ell(\tau) : v(x, \tau) = 1\} \in \mathbb{N} \cup \{\infty\}$$

where the minimization runs over all possible sequences $\tau$, and $\kappa(x) = \infty$ if no valid solution sequence exists (i.e., the question is unsolvable).

**What it computes:** the minimal number of unit-cost Turing machine transitions needed to produce a solution that the verifier accepts as correct. Conceptually, this captures the intrinsic difficulty of a question—how many primitive computational operations must be performed, in the best case, to reach the answer. For example, a question requiring two sequential arithmetic operations would have higher $\kappa$ than one requiring a single operation.

**Why this form:** the definition uses a Turing machine rather than, say, a higher-level abstraction (like "number of mathematical steps") because Turing machines provide a universal, well-defined notion of primitive computation that is independent of any particular model's architecture or tokenization. The minimization over all possible sequences ensures $\kappa(x)$ captures the *inherent* complexity of the problem—the information-theoretic minimum—not the complexity of any particular solution approach. The binary verifier formalization separates the question of "what counts as a valid solution" (the verifier) from "how efficiently can we produce one" (the complexity). The key practical note is that **computing $\kappa(x)$ is generally intractable** because verifying the minimal solution length requires a global search over a potentially exponential space of candidate solution sequences. This intractability is what motivates the use of proxy properties later.

**Reasoning Compute $C_\theta(x)$.** For a given model $M_\theta$ with parameters $\theta$, the reasoning compute is defined as the expected number of reasoning tokens generated:

$$C_\theta(x) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{r \sim p_\theta(\cdot|x)}[\ell(r)]$$

where $r$ is the reasoning chain (the model's internal "thinking" before producing its final answer), $p_\theta(r|x)$ is the model's distribution over reasoning chains given the question $x$, and $\ell(r)$ is the length of the reasoning chain in tokens.

**What it computes:** the average number of reasoning tokens the model generates per question, accounting for the randomness in the model's sampling. This is measured empirically by sampling multiple outputs (8 per question in the paper's evaluations) and averaging the token counts of the reasoning portions.

**Why this form:** reasoning compute is defined in terms of expected token count rather than, say, FLOPs or wall-clock time, because token count is a model-agnostic, easily measurable proxy that is directly proportional to the actual computation performed (each token requires one forward pass through the model). The expectation over $p_\theta(r|x)$ captures that the same model may produce different-length reasoning chains on the same question due to sampling stochasticity, and the average is the relevant quantity for understanding the model's *typical* reasoning behavior.

**Reasoning Accuracy $A_\theta(x)$.** Accuracy is defined as the probability that the model produces the correct final answer:

$$A_\theta(x) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{(r, y) \sim p_\theta(\cdot|x)}[\mathbf{1}\{\text{ans}(y) = a^\star(x)\}] \in [0, 1]$$

where $y$ is the final answer extracted from the output (after the reasoning chain $r$), $a^\star(x)$ is the ground-truth correct answer for question $x$, and $\text{ans}(y)$ extracts the answer portion from the full model output.

**What it computes:** the fraction of sampled outputs where the model's final answer matches the correct answer. For the paper's evaluations with 8 samples per question, this is the empirical pass@1 rate—does the model get the question right, on average, across multiple attempts?

**Why this form:** accuracy is the standard metric for reasoning benchmarks, but defined here in expectation to match the probabilistic treatment used throughout the framework. The indicator function $\mathbf{1}\{\text{ans}(y) = a^\star(x)\}$ provides a binary correctness signal that works for closed-form answer problems (math, code) where exact string matching or equivalent answer checking is possible. The expectation over the full distribution $(r, y) \sim p_\theta(\cdot|x)$ captures that the same question may be answered correctly on some attempts and incorrectly on others.

**Independence and Composition.** The paper defines the composition of two questions $x_1$ and $x_2$ as their concatenation with a connector prompt $c \in \mathcal{V}^*$:

$$x_1 \oplus x_2 = \text{concat}(x_1, c, x_2)$$

Two questions $x_1$ and $x_2$ are **independent** if the complexity of their composition is additive:

$$\kappa(x_1 \oplus x_2) = \kappa(x_1) + \kappa(x_2)$$

Since exact $\kappa$ values are unavailable in practice, the paper **operationally defines independence** through concept sets: each question $x \in \mathcal{X}$ is associated with a set of mathematical concepts $\mathcal{S}(x) \subseteq \mathcal{S}$ (e.g., derivatives for Calculus, group theory for Algebra, logic for Discrete mathematics) relevant to solving it. Two questions are considered independent if their concept sets are disjoint: $\mathcal{S}(x_1) \cap \mathcal{S}(x_2) = \emptyset$.

**Why this operational definition:** the concept-set approach provides a tractable proxy for true independence that can be applied systematically to benchmark construction. Questions from different MATH subjects (e.g., Algebra vs. Geometry) are assumed to require disjoint reasoning, so solving them together should require the sum of the individual reasoning efforts. This is not a formally rigorous guarantee of independence—there could be cases where two questions from nominally different subjects share underlying reasoning structures—but it is a reasonable and scalable approximation that makes benchmark construction feasible at the scale of 250 triplets.

#### The Compute Law and Its Proxy Properties

Hypothesis 1 (Section 2.2) formalizes the paper's central claim about how reasoning compute should scale:

> **Hypothesis 1 (Compute Law).** For an optimal reasoning model $M_\theta$ and a question $x$ with complexity $\kappa(x)$, there exists $\alpha_\theta > 0$ such that:
>
> $$C_\theta(x) = \alpha_\theta \kappa(x) + o(\kappa(x))$$

where $C_\theta(x)$ is the expected reasoning compute (from Definition 2), $\kappa(x)$ is the question complexity (from Definition 1), $\alpha_\theta$ is a positive constant that depends only on the model $M_\theta$ and its decoding strategy (representing the model's "thinking speed" in tokens per primitive step), and $o(\kappa(x))$ denotes a sublinear term that becomes negligible relative to $\kappa(x)$ as complexity grows large ($o(\kappa)/\kappa \to 0$ when $\kappa \to \infty$).

**What it computes:** the model's reasoning compute is predicted to be approximately proportional to the problem's intrinsic complexity—double the complexity, double the reasoning tokens. The sublinear $o(\kappa(x))$ term captures "introductory and transition tokens during the reasoning process" (Section 2.2) that are roughly constant per problem regardless of complexity (e.g., restating the question, formatting, concluding phrases), and which constitute a diminishing fraction of total reasoning as problems get harder.

**Why this form:** the linear relationship is motivated by the intuition that an optimal reasoner should allocate compute directly proportional to the work required—each primitive step in the solution requires some constant amount of "thinking" from the model, and the total thinking should be the sum of thinking per step. A sublinear relationship ($C_\theta(x)$ growing slower than linearly with $\kappa(x)$) would imply the model is cutting corners on harder problems; a superlinear relationship would imply inefficiency where each additional step requires disproportionately more thinking. The $o(\kappa(x))$ overhead term allows for constant per-problem costs while preserving the asymptotic linear relationship. Critically, the paper does not claim current models satisfy this law—it is a **normative hypothesis** about how optimal models *should* behave, to be tested through the proxy properties.

**Proxy Property 1: Compute-Complexity Monotonicity.** Since $\kappa(x)$ is intractable to measure directly, the paper uses monotonicity as a testable implication of the compute law:

> **Property 1 (Compute-Complexity Monotonicity).** For any two questions $x_1, x_2 \in \mathcal{X}$:
> $$\kappa(x_1) \leq \kappa(x_2) \implies C_\theta(x_1) \leq C_\theta(x_2)$$

**What it states:** if question $x_2$ is intrinsically more complex than $x_1$, then the model should allocate at least as much reasoning compute to $x_2$ as to $x_1$. The compute does not need to grow with any particular functional form—it just needs to be monotonically non-decreasing with complexity.

**Why this property is useful:** monotonicity can be tested **without knowing the exact values of $\kappa(x)$**—it only requires knowing which of two questions is more complex. This is exactly what LORE-MONO provides by constructing question variants where complexity is guaranteed to increase with the variant index. If a model satisfies monotonicity, its reasoning compute will show a positive correlation with the variant index. If it violates monotonicity (as the motivating DeepSeek-R1 example does—allocating fewer tokens to the harder composite question), it cannot be satisfying the compute law.

**Proxy Property 2: Compute-Complexity Compositionality.** The second property tests the additivity of compute across independent sub-questions:

> **Property 2 (Compute-Complexity Compositionality).** For any two independent questions $x_1, x_2 \in \mathcal{X}$, their composition $x_1 \oplus x_2$ satisfies:
> $$C_\theta(x_1 \oplus x_2) = C_\theta(x_1) + C_\theta(x_2) + o(\kappa(x_1) + \kappa(x_2))$$
> and therefore approximately:
> $$C_\theta(x_1 \oplus x_2) \approx C_\theta(x_1) + C_\theta(x_2)$$

**What it states:** when a model solves two independent sub-questions together as a composite, the total reasoning compute should be (approximately) the sum of what it would use to solve each sub-question individually. The $o(\kappa(x_1) + \kappa(x_2))$ term allows for small constant overheads (e.g., transition phrases like "now let's solve the second question") but the dominant behavior should be additive.

**Why this property is useful:** compositionality can be tested without knowing $\kappa(x)$ at all—it only requires measuring $C_\theta$ on individual sub-questions and their composition, and checking whether they add up. The construction of LORE-COMPO (pairing questions from different MATH subjects) provides the independence condition operationally. Critically, Proposition 1 establishes that **monotonicity plus compositionality implies the compute law**: if a model satisfies both properties, then $C_\theta(x) \propto \kappa(x)$ must hold (under mild technical conditions formalized in Appendix D). The proof works by showing that the two properties force $C_\theta$ to be additive over independent questions and monotonic in complexity, which together force a linear relationship (a standard result from the theory of functional equations—a monotonic additive function on the natural numbers must be linear).

**Why monotonicity alone is not sufficient:** a model could be monotonic (more complex → more compute) but have any monotonic functional form—quadratic, exponential, logarithmic—and still pass a monotonicity test. Compositionality provides the additional constraint that the function must be additive over independent sub-problems, which rules out non-linear forms. Together, the two properties uniquely identify linear scaling.

#### The Accuracy Law and Its Proxy Properties

The accuracy law (Hypothesis 2, Section 2.3) complements the compute law by specifying how correctness should relate to complexity:

> **Hypothesis 2 (Accuracy Law).** For an optimal reasoning model $M_\theta$ and a question $x$ with complexity $\kappa(x)$, when $0 < A_\theta(x) \leq 1$, there exists $\lambda_\theta \geq 0$ such that:
>
> $$A_\theta(x) = \exp\left(-\lambda_\theta \kappa(x)\right)$$
>
> Equivalently, $\log A_\theta(x) \propto -\kappa(x)$, where $\lambda_\theta \geq 0$ is a decay rate.

**What it computes:** the probability of answering correctly is predicted to decrease exponentially as problem complexity increases. Each additional primitive step multiplies the success probability by a constant factor $\exp(-\lambda_\theta)$, where $\lambda_\theta$ is the model's per-step failure rate. If $\lambda_\theta = 0.1$, a 5-step problem has accuracy $\exp(-0.5) \approx 0.61$, while a 10-step problem has accuracy $\exp(-1.0) \approx 0.37$.

**Why this form:** the exponential form follows from modeling each primitive step as an independent Bernoulli trial: each step either succeeds or fails, all steps must succeed for the final answer to be correct, and steps succeed independently with some constant probability $p = \exp(-\lambda_\theta)$. This is the standard reliability model for systems with serial independent components. A sub-exponential decay (slower than exponential) would imply the model recovers from or corrects mid-reasoning errors, which would require additional mechanisms beyond what the definition of primitive steps assumes. A super-exponential decay would imply errors compound, making later steps more likely to fail given earlier steps succeeded—possible in practice (a mistake early in reasoning may cascade), but not what the idealized independent-step model predicts. The paper treats this as a normative hypothesis to be tested, not an empirical claim about current models.

**Proxy Property 3: Accuracy-Complexity Monotonicity.**

> **Property 3 (Accuracy-Complexity Monotonicity).** For $x_1, x_2 \in \mathcal{X}$:
> $$\kappa(x_1) \leq \kappa(x_2) \implies A_\theta(x_1) \geq A_\theta(x_2)$$

**What it states:** more complex questions should have equal or lower accuracy. This is the natural complement to compute monotonicity—more complex problems require more thinking (Property 1) and are less likely to be solved correctly (Property 3).

**Proxy Property 4: Accuracy-Complexity Compositionality.**

> **Property 4 (Accuracy-Complexity Compositionality).** For independent $x_1, x_2 \in \mathcal{X}$:
> $$A_\theta(x_1 \oplus x_2) = A_\theta(x_1) \cdot A_\theta(x_2)$$

**What it states:** the accuracy on a composite of two independent questions should be the product of the individual accuracies. If the model has an 80% chance of getting $x_1$ right and a 70% chance of getting $x_2$ right, it should have a $0.8 \times 0.7 = 0.56$ (56%) chance of getting both right in the composite. This follows from the same independence reasoning as the compute compositionality: the sub-problems involve disjoint reasoning, so successes on each are independent events, and the probability of both events occurring is the product.

**Why this form rather than additive accuracy:** probability theory dictates that for independent events, probabilities multiply, not add. If accuracy were additive ($A_\theta(x_1 \oplus x_2) = A_\theta(x_1) + A_\theta(x_2)$), it could exceed 1 for sufficiently many sub-questions, which is impossible. Multiplicative compositionality is the only form that respects the mathematical structure of probability.

As with the compute law, Proposition 2 (Appendix D) establishes that monotonicity plus compositionality for accuracy imply the accuracy law ($\log A_\theta(x) \propto -\kappa(x)$) under mild conditions.

**Relationship between the two laws.** The compute law and accuracy law are presented as complementary: the compute law specifies how much thinking *effort* the model should expend, while the accuracy law specifies the *outcome* quality that results. They are linked by the underlying complexity $\kappa(x)$: harder problems both require more compute and yield lower accuracy. However, the paper does not claim that compute causally determines accuracy—a model could use the "right" amount of compute per the law but still have low accuracy if its per-step reasoning quality is poor (high $\lambda_\theta$). The two laws characterize different aspects of optimal behavior, and the paper evaluates them independently.

#### LORE-MONO: Benchmark Construction for Testing Monotonicity

Testing monotonicity requires pairs of questions where one is known to be more complex than the other, without needing to know their absolute complexity values. LORE-MONO (Section 3.1) solves this by constructing question *variants* with a known complexity ordering through a three-stage process.

**Stage 1: Seed Question Curation.** The authors select four domains that require extensive reasoning—math, science, language, and code—and curate 10 diverse seed questions per domain, yielding 40 seed questions total. Each seed question defines a **problem template**: a parameterized scenario where a single operation can be applied repeatedly, with each application increasing the required computation.

Examples provided in Appendix E.1.1 illustrate the templates:
- **Math seed question:** a second-order recurrence relation with alternating update rules (parity-dependent linear combinations with a nonlinear term), where the answer is the $n$-th element $x_n$ after applying $n-1$ updates from initial values.
- **Science seed question:** a batch bioreactor simulation where an enzyme converts substrate to product using a recyclable cofactor, with discrete ticks applying reaction rules and periodic cofactor regeneration. The output is the product count after exactly $n$ ticks.
- **Language seed question:** a letter maze on a toroidal grid where the agent moves right for vowels and down for consonants, appending target cell letters to a string and mutating the grid (vowels rotate columns, consonants rotate rows). The output is the last $k$ letters of the accumulated string after $n$ moves.
- **Code seed question:** a Python code execution task where a string transformation function $f(s, i)$ applies a position-dependent rotation and a vowel-shift cipher, iterated $N$ times. The output is the final string value.

**Stage 2: From Seed Questions to Variants.** For each seed question, the authors create a series of 30 variants by varying a single integer parameter $N$ that controls how many times the core operation is applied. Variant 1 applies the operation once, variant 2 applies it twice, and variant 30 applies it thirty times. Since the operation is identical across applications (the same update rule, same reaction, same movement rule, same string transformation), each additional application adds the same amount of intrinsic computational work.

**Why this guarantees monotonic complexity:** by construction, variant $N+1$ requires all the computation of variant $N$ plus one additional application of the same operation. Therefore $\kappa(\text{variant } N) < \kappa(\text{variant } N+1)$ for all $N \in \{1, \dots, 29\}$. The absolute complexity values are unknown, but the relative ordering is guaranteed by the construction. This is exactly what monotonicity testing requires: a partial order on question complexity that is known with certainty.

**Stage 3: Program-based Generation and Manual Verification.** All variants are generated through Python scripts to ensure correctness and scalability. The authors explicitly note that they "manually verify each seed question and review sampled variants" to prevent unintended shortcuts, particularly **periodic answer patterns**. For example, if a recurrence relation produces answers that cycle (e.g., all even-$N$ variants yield answer 1, all odd-$N$ yield answer 0), a model could exploit the pattern without performing the intended computation. Such seed questions are identified during manual review and excluded.

**Measurement Protocol.** For each model being evaluated, the authors sample 8 outputs per question (each variant of each seed question) at a fixed decoding temperature (0.6 for the DeepSeek family, 0.8 for the Phi-4 family, as specified in the respective technical reports) with a maximum generation length of 20,480 tokens. For each variant index (1 through 30), they:

1. Average the reasoning compute $C_\theta$ across all 40 questions (10 per domain × 4 domains) at that variant index, applying **max-min normalization** to the reasoning compute of each question series "to prevent any single item from dominating the results." This normalization scales each question's compute values to $[0, 1]$ based on its own min and max across variants, so a question whose compute varies from 100 to 10,000 tokens and one whose compute varies from 50 to 500 tokens contribute equally to the cross-question average.
2. Average the log accuracy $\log A_\theta$ across all 40 questions at that variant index.
3. Compute the **Spearman rank correlation coefficient** $\rho \in [-1, 1]$ between the variant index and (a) the averaged reasoning compute, and (b) the averaged log accuracy.

**Why Spearman correlation:** Spearman's $\rho$ measures monotonic association without assuming linearity. A high positive $\rho$ for compute means that as the variant index (complexity) increases, compute tends to increase—exactly what Property 1 requires. A high negative $\rho$ for log accuracy means that as complexity increases, log accuracy tends to decrease—exactly what Property 3 requires. The paper reports $\rho$ separately for each domain (math, science, language, code) and for all domains combined.

**Design choices and their justifications:**
- **30 variants per seed question:** provides sufficient resolution to detect monotonic trends while keeping generation costs manageable (30 variants × 40 questions × 8 samples = 9,600 outputs per model).
- **Four diverse domains:** ensures the monotonicity findings are not domain-specific. The domains span symbolic math, scientific simulation, linguistic pattern-following, and code execution—different reasoning modalities that might stress different model capabilities.
- **Max-min normalization per question:** prevents questions with inherently large token counts from dominating the cross-question average, since a question about matrix operations might naturally produce thousands of tokens while a simpler recurrence produces hundreds. The normalization ensures each question contributes equally to the measured correlation regardless of its absolute compute scale.
- **Fixed decoding temperature:** matches the sampling configuration used in the models' original technical reports, ensuring the evaluation reflects the models' intended operating regimes rather than an arbitrary temperature choice.

#### LORE-COMPO: Benchmark Construction for Testing Compositionality

Testing compositionality requires pairs of independent questions and their composition. LORE-COMPO (Section 3.2) constructs these from the MATH500 dataset through a systematic sampling and pairing procedure.

**Dataset Source and Independence Definition.** The paper uses MATH500 (Lightman et al., 2023), a dataset of 500 competition-level math problems, where each question is labeled by subject category (e.g., Algebra, Geometry, Number Theory, Counting & Probability, Precalculus, Prealgebra). The paper **operationally defines independence** through these subject labels: two questions $x_1$ and $x_2$ are considered independent if they come from **distinct subjects**, i.e., their concept sets $\mathcal{S}(x_1)$ and $\mathcal{S}(x_2)$ are disjoint. The intuition is that questions from different mathematical domains require disjoint reasoning capabilities—solving an Algebra problem and a Geometry problem together should not involve overlapping reasoning steps.

**Triplet Construction.** The construction procedure works as follows:

1. Randomly sample pairs of questions $(x_1, x_2)$ from distinct pre-defined subjects within MATH500.
2. Form the composite question $x_{12} = x_1 \oplus x_2$ by concatenating the two sub-questions with a fixed connector prompt. The connector used is: "Answer the following questions in order: Q1. {Q1}\nQ2. {Q2}", which ensures the model addresses the sub-questions sequentially in a known order.
3. Each original MATH500 question is used **at most once** across all triplets, preventing data leakage where the same question appears in multiple composite pairs.
4. This procedure yields **250 triplets**, each consisting of $(x_1^{(i)}, x_2^{(i)}, x_{12}^{(i)})$ for $i = 1, \dots, 250$. The full benchmark is thus: $\mathcal{D}_{\text{LoRe-Compo}} = \{(x_1^{(i)}, x_2^{(i)}, x_{12}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{250}$.

**Why 250 triplets:** MATH500 contains 500 questions. Using each question at most once gives a maximum of 250 disjoint pairs (since each pair consumes two questions and each triplet needs two distinct questions). This is the largest possible benchmark under the "use at most once" constraint.

**Measurement Protocol.** For each triplet $(x_1, x_2, x_{12})$, the paper measures reasoning compute $C_\theta$ and log accuracy $\log A_\theta$ for each of the three questions (two sub-questions, one composite) by sampling 8 outputs per question with the same decoding configuration as LORE-MONO. Compositionality predicts that $C_\theta(x_{12}) \approx C_\theta(x_1) + C_\theta(x_2)$ and $\log A_\theta(x_{12}) \approx \log A_\theta(x_1) + \log A_\theta(x_2)$ (since the accuracy law operates in log space).

**Metric: Normalized Mean Absolute Deviation (nMAD).** To quantify how closely a model satisfies compositionality, the paper defines the **Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD)** for a function $f_\theta$ (where $f_\theta$ is either $C_\theta$ or $\log A_\theta$):

$$\text{MAD}_f = \sum_{(x_1, x_2, x_{12}) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{LoRe-Compo}}} \left| f_\theta(x_{12}) - \left(f_\theta(x_1) + f_\theta(x_2)\right) \right|$$

where the sum runs over all 250 triplets, and each term is the absolute difference between the composite measurement and the sum of the individual measurements.

**What it computes:** the total absolute deviation from perfect additivity across all triplets. If the model perfectly satisfies compositionality, $f_\theta(x_{12}) = f_\theta(x_1) + f_\theta(x_2)$ for every triplet, and MAD = 0. Larger MAD values indicate larger systematic deviations from additive behavior.

**Why MAD is scale-dependent and why nMAD fixes this:** MAD is measured in the same units as $f_\theta$—tokens for compute, log-probability for accuracy. A model that generates longer reasoning chains overall will have a larger MAD even if its relative deviation from additivity is the same. To normalize for scale, the paper defines the **Normalized MAD (nMAD)**:

$$\text{nMAD}_f = \frac{\text{MAD}_f}{S_f}, \quad S_f = \sum_{(x_1, x_2, x_{12}) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{LoRe-Compo}}} |f_\theta(x_1) + f_\theta(x_2)|$$

where $S_f$ is the sum of the absolute values of the predicted sums across all triplets—essentially the total "expected" magnitude if all compute were additive.

**What nMAD represents:** nMAD is a dimensionless ratio between 0 and (potentially) ∞. An nMAD of 0 means perfect additivity. An nMAD of, say, 0.5 means the total absolute deviation from additivity is 50% of the total expected additive value—a substantial violation. The paper reports nMAD values in Table 2; for DeepSeek-R1-1.5B, $\text{nMAD}_{C_\theta} = 0.528$, meaning the absolute deviation is over half the expected additive value.

**Why this metric:** nMAD provides a single scalar summary of compositionality violations that is comparable across models of different sizes (which produce different absolute token counts) and across different functions ($C_\theta$ vs. $\log A_\theta$, which are in different units). It is analogous to a coefficient of variation for the residuals from the additive model.

**Visualization Protocol.** The paper visualizes compositionality by plotting $C_\theta(x_{12})$ on the y-axis against $C_\theta(x_1) + C_\theta(x_2)$ on the x-axis for each of the 250 triplets (Figures 5, 6b, 9, 10). If the model perfectly satisfies compositionality, all points lie on the $y = x$ line. Systematic deviation above the line indicates overthinking on composite questions; systematic deviation below indicates underthinking. Scatter around the line indicates noisy, inconsistent allocation.

**Design choices and their justifications:**
- **Subject-based independence:** using MATH subject labels as a proxy for independence is an approximation—two Algebra questions could share underlying reasoning patterns, and a Geometry question might involve algebraic manipulation. However, this proxy is "motivated by the practical difficulty of formalizing independence between questions in an actionable and general way" (Appendix C). It is systematic, reproducible, and scalable to 250 triplets, which manual independence judgments would not be.
- **Sequential ordering in composite questions:** the connector prompt explicitly instructs the model to answer questions "in order," which gives a predictable structure to the composite—the model should solve Q1 first, then Q2. This is important because if the order were unspecified, the model might interleave reasoning from the two sub-questions, making it harder to interpret deviations from additivity.
- **Use-at-most-once constraint:** prevents any single question from appearing in multiple composite pairs, which would create statistical dependencies between triplets and potentially bias the MAD computation if certain questions are systematically easier or harder.
- **8 samples per question:** matches the LORE-MONO protocol and provides reasonable estimates of expected compute and accuracy without excessive computational cost. With 250 triplets × 3 questions × 8 samples = 6,000 outputs per model, plus 40 seed questions × 30 variants × 8 samples = 9,600 outputs for LORE-MONO, the total evaluation budget is 15,600 outputs per model.

#### SFT-Compo: Enforcing Compositionality Through Supervised Fine-Tuning

SFT-Compo (Section 4) is the paper's proposed intervention for improving reasoning by enforcing the compositionality property on reasoning compute. It is a supervised fine-tuning method that constructs training examples where the reasoning lengths of sub-questions and their composite are additive, then fine-tunes the base model on these examples to internalize compositional behavior.

**Why enforce compositionality specifically on compute rather than accuracy:** the paper states that "accuracy compositionality is not easy to enforce directly, as it does not specify which reasoning path should be selected for supervision" (Section 4). Accuracy compositionality tells us what the *outcome* probabilities should be, but during fine-tuning we need to select a specific reasoning trajectory (a sequence of tokens) to train on. Compute compositionality provides a **selection criterion on reasoning paths**: among correct solutions, pick the one where the composite reasoning length is closest to the sum of the sub-question reasoning lengths. This is an operational, actionable criterion that can be applied to concrete model outputs.

**Step 1: Training Data Construction.** The procedure uses a training dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ (a subset of DeepScaler; Luo et al., 2025b) and follows these steps:

1. **Select question pairs.** Choose pairs of questions $(x_1, x_2)$ from $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ that come from distinct categories to ensure (operational) independence. Since DeepScaler does not come with predefined categories, the paper first annotates each question using GPT-4.1-mini to assign it to one of: Algebra (Prealgebra), Counting & Probability, Geometry, Number Theory, or Calculus (Precalculus). Pairs are then formed from questions in different categories.

2. **Form composite questions.** Construct $x_{12} = x_1 \oplus x_2$ using the same connector prompt as LORE-COMPO.

3. **Sample from a teacher model.** For each question in the triplet (both sub-questions and the composite), sample $K = 8$ outputs $o = (r, y)$ from a **stronger teacher model**. The paper uses DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B as the teacher when fine-tuning smaller models (1.5B, 7B, 8B, and Phi-4-mini). Each output contains a reasoning chain $r$ (the thinking tokens) and a final answer $y$. This yields:
   - $\{o_1^{(k)} = (r_1^{(k)}, y_1^{(k)})\}_{k=1}^K$ for sub-question $x_1$
   - $\{o_2^{(k)} = (r_2^{(k)}, y_2^{(k)})\}_{k=1}^K$ for sub-question $x_2$
   - $\{o_{12}^{(k)} = (r_{12}^{(k)}, y_{12}^{(k)})\}_{k=1}^K$ for composite $x_{12}$

4. **Select the compositionality-optimal combination.** Among all $K^3 = 512$ possible combinations $(o_1^{(i)}, o_2^{(j)}, o_{12}^{(k)})$ (one output for each question in the triplet), the method considers only those where **all three reasoning paths lead to correct answers**. Among these correct combinations, it selects the one that best satisfies compute additivity:

$$(r_1^*, r_2^*, r_{12}^*) = \arg\min_{r_1, r_2, r_{12}} |\ell(r_1) + \ell(r_2) - \ell(r_{12})|$$
$$\text{s.t. } r_1, r_2, r_{12} \text{ each yielding a correct final answer}$$

where $\ell(r)$ is the length (in tokens) of a reasoning chain, and the minimization is over all triples of reasoning paths (one from each question) that all lead to correct answers.

**What this selects:** the combination of reasoning trajectories where (1) all three answers are correct (ensuring the fine-tuning data teaches correct reasoning), and (2) the composite reasoning length is as close as possible to the sum of the two sub-question reasoning lengths (enforcing the compositionality property). If the teacher model naturally produced a composite reasoning chain of exactly the right length, the deviation $|\ell(r_1) + \ell(r_2) - \ell(r_{12})|$ would be zero; in practice, it selects the closest match among the $K^3$ combinations.

**Why minimize absolute deviation rather than squared error:** the L1 loss is more robust to outliers and directly measures the deviation in tokens. A squared-error minimization would disproportionately penalize large deviations, potentially selecting combinations with slightly worse overall length additivity but dramatically more balanced deviation across triplets—which is not what compositionality requires.

5. **Construct supervised examples.** Each triplet yields three supervised examples: $(x_1, o_1^*)$, $(x_2, o_2^*)$, and $(x_{12}, o_{12}^*)$, where $o_i^* = (r_i^*, y_i^*)$ with $y_i^*$ being the final answer paired with the selected reasoning chain $r_i^*$.

6. **Aggregate across triplets.** Repeating this procedure across all selected question pairs in $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ yields the compositional supervision dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{comp}} = \{(x_1, o_1^*), (x_2, o_2^*), (x_{12}, o_{12}^*) \mid (x_1, x_2) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\}$. The paper reports that the final dataset contains approximately **3,900 question-output pairs**.

**Step 2: Supervised Fine-Tuning.** The base model $M_\theta$ is fine-tuned on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{comp}}$ using standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT, also called next-token prediction with teacher forcing). The training configuration:
- **Epochs:** 5
- **Batch size:** 16 (effective batch size of 8 with gradient accumulation of 2)
- **Learning rate:** grid-searched over $\{1 \times 10^{-6}, 5 \times 10^{-6}, 5 \times 10^{-5}\}$
- **Warmup ratio:** 0 (no learning rate warmup)
- **Optimizer:** AdamW (implicit from standard SFT practice, though not explicitly stated)

**What the model learns:** by fine-tuning on trajectories where the composite reasoning length approximately equals the sum of the sub-question reasoning lengths, the model learns to **allocate reasoning compute additively** across independent sub-problems. When it encounters a composite question at test time, it has been trained to produce a reasoning chain whose length matches what it would produce for each sub-question individually, plus the sum.

**Why SFT rather than RL:** the paper uses supervised fine-tuning (behavioral cloning on selected teacher outputs) rather than reinforcement learning because (1) the compositionality criterion provides a direct way to select correct trajectories, making RL unnecessary for this specific goal, and (2) SFT is simpler, more stable, and computationally cheaper than RL fine-tuning. The selection step (picking the best combination from $K^3$ possibilities) performs the "credit assignment" that RL would otherwise need to learn through trial and error.

**Control Baseline: SFT without compositionality selection.** To isolate whether gains come from compositionality enforcement versus simply distilling the stronger teacher model, the paper introduces a control baseline called **SFT**. This baseline constructs the training dataset identically to SFT-Compo except that instead of selecting the compositionality-optimal combination, it **uniformly samples one correct combination**:

$$(r_1^*, r_2^*, r_{12}^*) \sim \text{Unif}\left(\{(r_1, r_2, r_{12}) \mid r_1, r_2, r_{12} \text{ each yield a correct final answer}\}\right)$$

SFT and SFT-Compo use the same teacher model, the same question triplets, the same $K=8$ sampling budget, and the same fine-tuning configuration. The only difference is the **selection criterion**: SFT-Compo selects for compositionality, while SFT selects uniformly at random from correct combinations. Any performance difference between SFT-Compo and SFT can therefore be attributed to the compositionality enforcement, not to distillation from the teacher.

**Design choices and their justifications:**
- **$K=8$ samples:** provides a reasonable search space ($8^3 = 512$ combinations per triplet) for finding compositionality-aligned trajectories without excessive teacher model computation. Larger $K$ would increase the chance of finding near-perfect additive combinations but cost proportionally more.
- **Teacher model selection (14B for 1.5B–8B students):** using a model approximately 2–10× larger than the student ensures the teacher produces high-quality (often correct) reasoning chains from which to select. This is standard distillation practice—the teacher needs to be strong enough that correct solutions are available in the sampled outputs.
- **Correctness constraint:** requiring all three reasoning paths to be correct prevents the model from learning to satisfy compositionality at the expense of accuracy—a degenerate solution where the model produces empty reasoning for both sub-questions would trivially satisfy additivity but yield wrong answers.
- **GPT-4.1-mini for category annotation:** since DeepScaler lacks subject labels, automated annotation with a capable but inexpensive model provides the category information needed for the operational independence definition without prohibitive manual labeling costs.

**Why SFT-Compo should improve general reasoning (the paper's hypothesis).** The logic connecting compositionality enforcement to general reasoning improvement is indirect but rests on the theoretical framework: if the compute law is a property of optimal reasoning models (Hypothesis 1), and compositionality plus monotonicity imply the compute law (Proposition 1), then enforcing compositionality should push the model toward a more optimal reasoning strategy. Even though SFT-Compo only directly enforces compositionality on reasoning *length* (not on the *content* of reasoning), the training process exposes the model to examples where the reasoning budget is appropriately scaled to the composite problem's complexity. This teaches the model a generalizable principle—"when problems combine, reasoning effort should add"—that transfers to novel problems beyond the training distribution, improving how the model allocates its thinking across all problems and thereby improving accuracy.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Formalizing Reasoning Behavior as a Set of Scientific Laws, Not Empirical Heuristics

The paper's foundational conceptual move is to treat reasoning behavior not as an engineering problem to be optimized through trial and error, but as a **scientific phenomenon governed by discoverable laws**. This reframes the entire conversation around LRM reasoning from "how can we make models think better?" to "what are the universal principles that optimal reasoning must obey?"

Before LORE, the field approached reasoning inefficiency through ad-hoc interventions. Post-training methods like variable-length SFT (Aggarwal & Welleck, 2025; Team et al., 2025) exposed models to examples with different reasoning lengths and hoped they would generalize appropriately. RL-based approaches like length penalties (Zhang et al., 2025a; Fang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025) treated reasoning length as a global knob—penalize long chains to reduce overthinking, reward long chains to encourage thoroughness—without conditioning on problem difficulty. Test-time methods like confidence-based early stopping (Qiao et al., 2025; Liu & Wang, 2025) applied heuristics without a principled grounding for when stopping is appropriate. None of these approaches could answer the question: **what *should* the reasoning behavior be?**

LORE answers this by proposing two normative hypotheses—the compute law ($C_\theta(x) \propto \kappa(x)$) and the accuracy law ($A_\theta(x) = \exp(-\lambda_\theta \kappa(x))$)—that specify how optimal models *should* allocate compute and how their accuracy *should* degrade with complexity, regardless of how current models actually behave. This is analogous to how physics posits conservation laws that real systems may approximate but rarely satisfy exactly: the laws provide a target to aim for and a lens through which to diagnose deviations. The paper explicitly draws this parallel through its terminology—"Laws of Reasoning"—positioning itself not as another training recipe but as a theoretical framework for understanding reasoning itself.

The intellectual distinctiveness lies in **separating the normative from the descriptive**. The paper does not claim current models satisfy these laws (the evaluation in Section 3 shows they emphatically do not). Instead, it argues that the laws characterize an ideal toward which models should be pushed, and that measuring compliance with the laws provides a more principled evaluation metric than raw accuracy alone. This is a fundamentally different intellectual stance from prior work, which focused on modifying behavior without first defining what optimal behavior means. The paper thus provides the field with something it lacked: a **target specification for reasoning behavior** that is grounded in computational complexity theory (via the Turing machine definition of $\kappa(x)$), satisfies internal mathematical consistency (via the formal proofs in Appendix D), and is empirically tractable (via the proxy properties).

### Innovation 2: Proving That Tractable Proxy Properties Imply Intractable Laws

The paper identifies and solves a critical theoretical challenge: the central quantity in the framework—problem complexity $\kappa(x)$—is "generally intractable" to compute (Section 2.1). If the laws are stated in terms of an unmeasurable quantity, they risk being philosophically elegant but practically useless. The paper's solution—proving that monotonicity plus compositionality *implies* the linear compute law (Proposition 1) and the exponential accuracy law (Proposition 2)—is a non-trivial theoretical contribution that bridges the gap between formal principles and empirical testing.

The proof strategy (formalized in Appendix D) leverages a classic result from functional equations: a monotonic additive function on the natural numbers must be linear. The compositionality property forces $C_\theta$ to be additive over independent questions: $C_\theta(x_1 \oplus x_2) = C_\theta(x_1) + C_\theta(x_2)$. The monotonicity property forces $C_\theta$ to be non-decreasing with $\kappa$. Together, these force the functional form $C_\theta(x) = \alpha_\theta \kappa(x)$—the linear relationship posited by the compute law. The accuracy proof follows the same structure in log space: multiplicative compositionality ($A_\theta(x_1 \oplus x_2) = A_\theta(x_1) \cdot A_\theta(x_2)$) and monotonicity force $\log A_\theta(x) \propto -\kappa(x)$.

What makes this innovative is not the mathematical difficulty of the proof (the functional equation result is well-established) but the **conceptual architecture it enables**. By proving the equivalence between (monotonicity + compositionality) and the laws, the paper provides:

1. **Sufficiency**: if a model satisfies the two tractable properties, it *must* satisfy the corresponding law, even though we can never directly verify the law (since we cannot measure $\kappa(x)$). This means the proxy properties are not merely correlated with the laws—they are theoretically sufficient to guarantee them.

2. **Necessity in practice**: violations of the proxy properties constitute definitive evidence that a model fails the laws. If a model allocates less compute to a harder composite question than to an easier sub-question (violating monotonicity), or if its composite compute deviates substantially from the sum of sub-question computes (violating compositionality), it cannot be satisfying the compute law, regardless of what $\kappa(x)$ actually is. This is what gives LORE-BENCH its diagnostic power: the benchmark measures tractable quantities that have a formal logical relationship to the intractable ideal.

3. **A target for intervention**: to make a model better comply with the laws, one only needs to enforce the proxy properties—which are operationally measurable and can be optimized via training (as SFT-Compo demonstrates). The proof guarantees that improving compositionality is not merely an aesthetic preference but moves the model toward the theoretically optimal behavior.

This is a fundamental advance over prior work on reasoning length control, none of which established any formal relationship between their training objectives and an underlying notion of optimal reasoning. Methods that penalize or reward reasoning length optimize a heuristic metric without a guarantee that the resulting behavior converges to any well-defined optimum. LORE provides that guarantee: enforce additivity and monotonicity, and the linear law follows.

### Innovation 3: Shifting Evaluation from Accuracy to Law-Compliance as a Diagnostic Framework

The paper's introduction of LORE-BENCH represents a shift in evaluation philosophy that moves beyond the standard paradigm in ML benchmarking. Traditional reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, AIME) ask: "how often does the model get the right answer?" LORE-BENCH asks: "does the model's *behavior* obey principled regularities?" This is a fundamentally different evaluation goal with different implications for model development.

Consider what LORE-MONO measures. Rather than asking whether a model correctly solves the 30th variant of a recurrence relation, it asks whether the model *allocates more compute* to variant 30 than to variant 1. A model could achieve perfect accuracy on all 30 variants but still fail the monotonicity test—for instance, by using the same reasoning length for every variant, which would be efficient for easy variants but represent *underthinking* on hard ones (it got lucky despite insufficient reasoning). Conversely, a model could pass monotonicity (compute scales with variant index) but have low accuracy on the hardest variants—which would indicate appropriate effort allocation but insufficient per-step reasoning quality. These distinct failure modes are invisible to accuracy-only benchmarks but are directly surfaced by LORE-BENCH.

LORE-COMPO extends this philosophy further. The nMAD metrics (Table 2) reveal that even models equipped with reasoning length control mechanisms—Thinkless-1.5B with nMADCθ = 0.339 and AdaptThink-7B with nMADCθ = 0.327—exhibit substantial compositionality violations, with deviations nearly as large as uncontrolled models (DeepSeek-R1-7B at 0.337). This is a diagnostic finding that accuracy benchmarks would miss entirely: these length-controlled models may achieve reasonable accuracy on individual benchmarks, but their underlying reasoning allocation strategy is not fundamentally more principled than uncontrolled models. The length control is cosmetic—it changes the overall scale of reasoning but not its structure.

The diagnostic power is most evident in the contrast between monotonicity and compositionality results (Tables 1 and 2). Most models achieve Spearman correlations near 1.0 for compute monotonicity (e.g., 0.991 for DeepSeek-R1-7B overall), indicating that they *do* allocate more compute to harder problems within a question family. Yet the same models show nMADCθ values of 0.3–0.5 on compositionality—indicating that their compute allocation *across* independent sub-problems is chaotic. A model that thinks longer on harder variants but fails to add thinking when problems combine is satisfying one aspect of the law but violating another. This decomposition—monotonicity satisfied, compositionality violated—is a specific, actionable diagnosis that points to exactly where the model's reasoning strategy breaks down. No prior benchmark provides this level of behavioral insight.

This evaluation philosophy has practical implications for model development. If a new training method improves AIME accuracy by 3%, it is ambiguous whether the improvement comes from better reasoning allocation or from memorized patterns in the training data. If the same method also reduces nMADCθ from 0.4 to 0.2 on LORE-COMPO, that provides evidence of a *structural* improvement in how the model approaches problems—evidence that is more likely to generalize to novel problem distributions. LORE-BENCH thus provides a complement to accuracy benchmarks: a behavioral assay that measures the *process* of reasoning, not just its outcomes.

### Innovation 4: Demonstrating That Enforcing One Proxy Property Produces Synergistic Improvements Across All Properties and Laws

The most empirically striking finding in the paper is not that SFT-Compo improves compositionality (which is tautological—the method is designed to do exactly that), but that enforcing compositionality on reasoning compute produces **cascading improvements across properties and laws that were not directly optimized**. This synergy (Section 5.3) transforms LORE from a purely descriptive framework into one with prescriptive force: the laws are not independent desiderata but interconnected aspects of optimal reasoning, such that improving compliance with one law pulls the model toward compliance with others.

The specific synergies documented are:

**Compute compositionality → compute monotonicity (Figure 7a).** SFT-Compo is never trained to improve monotonicity—the training data only enforces length additivity across composite question triplets. Yet on the 1.5B model, the overall Spearman correlation for compute monotonicity rises from 0.875 to 0.977, and in the code domain—where the base model showed essentially no monotonic relationship (ρ = 0.151, near zero)—it jumps to 0.914. This is not a small refinement; it transforms a near-total failure of monotonicity into a strong positive correlation. The mechanism is indirect: by learning to allocate compute additively across sub-problems, the model internalizes a general principle that compute should scale with the amount of work required, which transfers to within-question-family scaling even though the training data contained no such examples.

**Compute compositionality → accuracy compositionality (Figure 7b).** This is perhaps the most surprising synergy because SFT-Compo's selection criterion (Equation 1) explicitly optimizes only for reasoning *length* additivity, with correctness as a binary constraint (all selected paths must be correct). There is no term in the objective encouraging multiplicative accuracy. Yet nMADlog Aθ drops dramatically—from 2.368 to 0.685 on the 1.5B model (a 71.1% reduction), and from 1.170 to 0.756 on the 7B model (a 35.4% reduction). The accuracy compositionality improves because the model, having learned to allocate reasoning compute proportionally to the composite problem's total work, is now spending the *right amount* of thinking on each sub-problem within the composite, which makes it more likely to get both sub-answers correct. The additive compute allocation enables multiplicative accuracy as an emergent property rather than a directly trained behavior.

**Compute compositionality → general reasoning accuracy (Table 3).** This is the practical payoff. SFT-Compo yields consistent Pass@1 improvements across all six evaluation benchmarks and all four model sizes, with the 8B model gaining +5.0 average points and the 1.5B model gaining +4.8. The SFT control baseline—which uses the same teacher model and training data but selects correct trajectories uniformly rather than optimizing for compositionality—consistently underperforms SFT-Compo (e.g., 1.5B: SFT +1.7 vs. SFT-Compo +4.8; 8B: SFT +3.0 vs. SFT-Compo +5.0). This isolates the compositionality enforcement as the causal factor: distilling a stronger teacher helps somewhat, but *how* you select which teacher outputs to learn from matters substantially more. The compositionality criterion acts as a quality filter that selects trajectories reflecting a more principled reasoning strategy.

The intellectual significance of these synergies extends beyond the empirical gains. They provide **evidence that the laws are not arbitrary formalisms but reflect genuine structure in optimal reasoning**. If the compute law and accuracy law were independent hypotheses with no deeper connection, enforcing one would not improve the other. The fact that it does—and that the improvements propagate from a single proxy property (compute compositionality) to all three other properties (compute monotonicity, accuracy compositionality, and accuracy monotonicity implicitly through the accuracy improvements)—suggests that the laws capture a coherent, interconnected characterization of efficient reasoning. This transforms LORE from a collection of plausible hypotheses into a framework with internal consistency that is validated by the non-trivial prediction that enforcing one property generates spillover effects on others.

### Innovation 5: The Difficulty-as-Complexity Framing Grounded in Computation Theory, Not Dataset Labels

The paper's definition of complexity $\kappa(x)$ via minimal Turing machine steps (Definition 1) is a conceptual innovation that breaks from how difficulty is typically operationalized in ML benchmarking. Standard practice labels questions as "easy" or "hard" based on either human judgments (e.g., MATH dataset difficulty levels 1–5) or model accuracy statistics (e.g., pass@1 quintiles as in the compute-optimal test-time scaling work). Both approaches are fundamentally circular for the purpose of studying reasoning: they define difficulty in terms of either human perception or the very model behavior being studied, making it impossible to ask whether the model's reasoning scales *appropriately* with difficulty—it will always scale appropriately by construction if difficulty is defined by the model's own accuracy.

The Turing machine definition breaks this circularity. $\kappa(x)$ is defined independently of any model: it is the minimal number of primitive computational steps required by a fixed deterministic Turing machine with a binary verifier. This is a model-agnostic, information-theoretic notion of problem complexity grounded in computability theory. A question has high $\kappa(x)$ not because humans find it hard or because current models score poorly on it, but because any computational process—human, machine, or otherwise—must execute at least $\kappa(x)$ elementary operations to solve it. This provides an **Archimedean point** outside the ML training-and-evaluation loop from which to define what difficulty *means*, allowing the paper to ask a genuinely non-circular question: do current models allocate compute in proportion to this intrinsic difficulty?

The practical intractability of computing $\kappa(x)$ is acknowledged upfront (Section 2.1: "verifying the minimal solution length requires a global search over a potentially exponential space"), but the theoretical value of having a well-defined, model-independent complexity notion persists even when it cannot be directly measured. It serves as the conceptual foundation that justifies the proxy properties: monotonicity and compositionality are derived as *consequences* that must hold if the model's compute scales with the intrinsic $\kappa(x)$, even though $\kappa(x)$ itself is hidden. Without this grounding, the proxy properties would appear arbitrary—why should compute be additive? Because if compute is proportional to intrinsic complexity, and intrinsic complexity is additive over independent sub-problems (Definition 4), then compute must be additive. The reasoning is: $\kappa(x_1 \oplus x_2) = \kappa(x_1) + \kappa(x_2)$ (by definition of independence), and $C_\theta(x) \propto \kappa(x)$ (by the compute law), therefore $C_\theta(x_1 \oplus x_2) \propto \kappa(x_1) + \kappa(x_2) \propto C_\theta(x_1) + C_\theta(x_2)$. The proxy properties are grounded in the complexity definition, not pulled from intuition alone.

This also distinguishes LORE from prior work that uses difficulty-conditioned strategies. The compute-optimal test-time scaling paper (referenced in the prior sections) bins questions by pass@1 rate—a model-dependent difficulty metric that changes if the model improves. In that framework, a question can move from the "hard" bin to the "easy" bin as the model gets better, making difficulty a moving target. LORE's $\kappa(x)$ is fixed for a given question and verifier, independent of which model is being evaluated. This enables **cross-model comparisons of law compliance**: we can ask whether a 14B model satisfies the laws better than a 1.5B model, and the answer reflects something about the models' reasoning strategies, not about which questions happen to be in which difficulty bins for each model. The fact that the paper finds similar nMAD patterns across model sizes (Table 2)—all models show substantial compositionality violations regardless of scale—is a meaningful finding precisely because the metric is grounded in the same $\kappa(x)$ for all models.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on multiple datasets. LORE-MONO is a custom synthetic benchmark with 40 seed questions (10 each across math, science, language, and code domains), each generating 30 complexity-ordered variants (1,200 questions total). LORE-COMPO is constructed from MATH500 (Lightman et al., 2023), yielding 250 triplets of (sub-question 1, sub-question 2, composite). For general reasoning evaluation, six benchmarks are used: GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), MATH500, AIME 2024, AIME 2025 (Mathematical Association of America, 2025), AMC 2023 (AI-MO, 2024), and OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024). The SFT-Compo training data uses a subset of DeepScaler (Luo et al., 2025b) with questions annotated into five categories by GPT-4.1-mini.

- **Base model(s).** Ten LRMs are evaluated on LORE-BENCH: DeepSeek-R1-Distill variants (Qwen-1.5B, Qwen-7B, Llama-8B, Qwen-14B) (Guo et al., 2025), Phi-4-mini-reasoning (Xu et al., 2025a), OpenReasoning-Nemotron-14B (Ahmad et al., 2025), Sky-T1-32B-Preview (Team, 2025a), Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Thinking (Team, 2025b), and two length-control models—Thinkless-1.5B-RL-DeepScaleR (Fang et al., 2025) and AdaptThink-7B-delta0.05 (Zhang et al., 2025a). For SFT-Compo, four models are fine-tuned: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, and Phi-4-mini-reasoning. DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B serves as the teacher model for sampling. The models span a wide parameter range (1.5B to 80B) and include both standard LRMs and those with explicit reasoning length control, allowing analysis of whether length-control techniques inherently promote law-compliant behavior.

- **Metrics.** The primary LORE-BENCH metrics are: (1) **Spearman rank correlation** ρ between variant index and reasoning compute (for monotonicity, Property 1) or between variant index and log accuracy (for Property 3), computed across the 30 variant indices after averaging over 40 questions per index; and (2) **nMAD (Normalized Mean Absolute Deviation)**, defined as nMAD_f = MAD_f / S_f where MAD_f = Σ|f_θ(x_12) − (f_θ(x_1) + f_θ(x_2))| and S_f = Σ|f_θ(x_1) + f_θ(x_2)|, summed over 250 triplets for f_θ ∈ {C_θ, log A_θ}. For general reasoning, the metric is **Pass@1 accuracy (%)**, averaged over 8 sampled outputs per question. Reasoning compute C_θ(x) is the expected number of reasoning tokens (averaged across samples), and accuracy A_θ(x) is the fraction of samples where the extracted final answer matches ground truth.

- **Baselines.** The paper evaluates 10 standard LRMs on LORE-BENCH with no modifications, establishing pre-intervention behavior. For the SFT-Compo comparison (Table 3), three conditions are compared: (1) **Base**: the unmodified pre-trained LRM; (2) **SFT**: the control baseline that constructs training data identically to SFT-Compo (same teacher model, same question triplets, same K=8 sampling budget) but selects one correct combination uniformly at random rather than optimizing for compositionality—this isolates the effect of compositionality-based selection from general distillation; (3) **SFT-Compo (Ours)**: the proposed method that selects the combination minimizing |ℓ(r_1) + ℓ(r_2) − ℓ(r_12)| among all correct triples. No external prior methods are used as baselines since the paper is evaluating a newly proposed theoretical framework rather than comparing against established reasoning-length control methods on standard benchmarks.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For LORE-BENCH evaluation, each model generates 8 outputs per question at a fixed decoding temperature (0.6 for DeepSeek family, 0.8 for Phi-4 family, as specified in their technical reports) with a maximum generation length of 20,480 tokens. For general reasoning evaluation, the maximum generation length is 10,240 tokens. For SFT-Compo training, the teacher model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B) samples K=8 outputs per question in each triplet. The paper does not report FLOPs or wall-clock time; "compute" is measured exclusively in token counts. There is no constraint on total inference budget—the evaluation measures the model's natural reasoning allocation rather than optimizing within a fixed budget.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For LORE-MONO, reasoning compute is max-min normalized per question series before averaging across questions at each variant index, preventing any single question from dominating the Spearman correlation. The Spearman correlation is computed separately per domain (math, science, language, code) and overall (all 40 questions combined). For LORE-COMPO, nMAD is computed over all 250 triplets without cross-validation since the metric is evaluated on a fixed benchmark rather than used for model selection. For SFT-Compo, the learning rate is grid-searched over {1e-6, 5e-6, 5e-5}, and the best configuration is selected. The general reasoning evaluation uses 8 samples per question and reports Pass@1 as the average across these samples. No confidence intervals or statistical significance tests are reported for any metric.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### LORE-BENCH: Monotonicity Evaluation (LORE-MONO)

The headline finding from LORE-MONO (Table 1) is that **most current LRMs largely satisfy compute-complexity monotonicity, with overall Spearman correlations between reasoning compute and variant index approaching 1.0**, but the weakest model (DeepSeek-R1-1.5B) shows domain-specific failures where reasoning compute does not systematically increase with complexity.

**Compute monotonicity results (Table 1, "Reasoning Compute ↑" columns).** Nine of ten evaluated models achieve overall Spearman correlations ≥ 0.967 between variant index (complexity) and reasoning compute. The strongest performers are AdaptThink-7B and DeepSeek-R1-7B (both ρ = 0.995 overall), indicating near-perfect monotonic ordering of compute allocation within question families. Even the length-control models—Thinkless-1.5B (ρ = 0.976) and AdaptThink-7B (ρ = 0.995)—show strong monotonicity, suggesting that global length penalties do not disrupt the basic ability to scale compute with within-family complexity.

The notable outlier is DeepSeek-R1-1.5B, with an overall ρ of 0.875—substantially lower than all other models. The domain breakdown reveals the source: in the **language domain**, its correlation is −0.346 (compute *decreases* with increasing complexity, a direct violation of monotonicity), and in the **code domain**, the correlation is 0.151 (near zero, indicating no systematic relationship). The paper provides a case study (Appendix E.2.2) showing that this model occasionally "fails to think"—producing empty reasoning chains on certain code questions—which explains the broken monotonicity in code. In contrast, the same model achieves ρ = 0.861 in math and ρ = 0.910 in science, demonstrating that the monotonicity failure is domain-specific rather than universal.

**Accuracy monotonicity results (Table 1, "Log Accuracy ↓" columns).** All models show negative Spearman correlations between log accuracy and variant index, consistent with Property 3 (accuracy should decrease as complexity increases). DeepSeek-R1-7B achieves the strongest negative correlation (ρ = −0.978 overall), while DeepSeek-R1-1.5B again shows the weakest (ρ = −0.868 overall). The accuracy monotonicity results are generally less variable across domains than compute monotonicity, with most domain-specific correlations falling in the −0.7 to −0.98 range. Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Thinking shows an interesting pattern: strong accuracy monotonicity in math (ρ = −0.403, much weaker than other models' −0.8 to −0.95) but strong in all other domains (−0.774 to −0.973), suggesting domain-specific difficulty scaling differences even in the largest model tested.

**Visual evidence for the 1.5B model's failures (Figure 4).** The plots reveal that for math, science, and the "all" aggregate, the 1.5B model's reasoning compute generally increases with variant index, though with high variance (wide shaded regions). For the language domain, the compute curve is erratic—rising and falling unpredictably—while log accuracy shows a weak downward trend inconsistent with the exponential decay predicted by the accuracy law. For code, the compute curve is essentially flat with high variance, confirming the near-zero correlation. These visualizations make concrete the pattern behind the correlation numbers: the model breaks monotonicity not through a systematic reversal but through chaotic, unpredictable allocation where complexity provides essentially no signal about how much the model will think.

**Additional model visualizations (Figure 8 in Appendix).** The remaining nine models all show qualitatively different behavior from the 1.5B. Reasoning compute curves are smooth and monotonically increasing across all four domains, with tighter variance. Log accuracy curves show consistent downward trends. This suggests that the monotonicity property is satisfied by all models with sufficient capability (roughly 3.8B parameters and above), and the 1.5B model's failures reflect a capacity floor below which even basic complexity-aware allocation breaks down.

**What monotonicity does and does not show.** A critical interpretive point: achieving ρ ≈ 1.0 for compute monotonicity means the model allocates more tokens to harder *variants within the same seed question family*. It does not mean the model allocates compute proportionally to absolute complexity across different question families. A model could pass monotonicity with ρ = 0.99 while using 100 tokens for variant 1 of a math question and 10,000 tokens for variant 1 of a language question—the correlation only requires that within each family, the ordering is preserved, not that the scaling slopes are consistent or that the absolute allocation is appropriate. This is why compositionality testing (LORE-COMPO) is necessary: it measures whether the model's compute allocation respects additive structure *across* question boundaries, which monotonicity alone cannot assess.

#### LORE-BENCH: Compositionality Evaluation (LORE-COMPO)

The headline finding from LORE-COMPO (Table 2) is that **all current LRMs fail to exhibit compositionality**, with nMAD values for reasoning compute ranging from 0.322 (Phi-4-mini) to 0.528 (DeepSeek-R1-1.5B), and nMAD values for log accuracy ranging from 0.694 (Thinkless-1.5B) to 2.368 (DeepSeek-R1-1.5B). These values indicate that the absolute deviation from additive behavior is a substantial fraction (32–53% for compute, 69–237% for log accuracy) of the expected additive values.

**Compute compositionality results (Table 2, "nMAD_Cθ ↓" column).** Phi-4-mini achieves the best (lowest) nMAD for reasoning compute at 0.322, meaning its composite reasoning length deviates from the sum of sub-question lengths by about 32% of the expected additive value on average. DeepSeek-R1-1.5B shows the worst violation at 0.528. The two length-control models—Thinkless-1.5B (0.339) and AdaptThink-7B (0.327)—show nMAD values in the same range as standard LRMs, with Thinkless-1.5B actually performing *worse* than Phi-4-mini (0.322) and roughly comparable to uncontrolled models of similar scale (DeepSeek-R1-7B at 0.337). This is a critical finding: **global reasoning length control does not inherently produce compositional behavior**. These models have been trained to modulate overall reasoning length (via RL-based penalties or adaptive stopping), but they have not learned to make that modulation proportional to problem structure—when two independent problems combine, they do not add their thinking budgets.

The scale trends are not monotonic. The largest model (Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Thinking, 0.411) has worse compute compositionality than several 7–8B models, while the 1.5B model has the worst. This suggests that compositionality is not simply a function of model capability—even very large models have not internalized additive allocation from their training data.

**Visual evidence for compute compositionality (Figure 5, Figure 9 in Appendix).** The scatter plots of C_θ(x_12) against C_θ(x_1) + C_θ(x_2) reveal the nature of the violations. For DeepSeek-R1-1.5B (Figure 5, left), most points fall substantially below the y=x line, indicating **systematic underthinking on composite questions**: the model allocates far fewer tokens to the composite than to the sum of its parts. For DeepSeek-R1-8B (Figure 5, right), the scatter is more symmetric around the line but with very high variance—some composite questions get far too many tokens, others far too few. The common pattern across all models (Figure 9) is not a consistent bias in one direction but rather **highly variable, unpredictable allocation**: the model's reasoning length on a composite question is only weakly predictable from its behavior on the individual sub-questions.

For Thinkless-1.5B (Figure 9), the scatter shows a distinctive compressed pattern—points cluster at lower values on both axes compared to standard models, reflecting the length penalty's effect of reducing overall token counts, but the deviation from y=x remains substantial (nMAD = 0.339). For AdaptThink-7B, the pattern is similar to DeepSeek-R1-7B (both around 0.33), confirming that length control compresses the scale but does not improve the structural alignment.

**Accuracy compositionality results (Table 2, "nMAD_log A_θ ↓" column).** The nMAD values for log accuracy are substantially larger than for compute, ranging from 0.694 (Thinkless-1.5B) to 2.368 (DeepSeek-R1-1.5B). An nMAD of 2.368 means the absolute deviation from multiplicative accuracy is over twice the expected additive log-accuracy value—a massive violation. Thinkless-1.5B's relatively better accuracy compositionality (0.694 vs. 2.368 for the base 1.5B) is a notable exception: the length-controlled variant shows dramatically better accuracy structure even though its compute compositionality is only marginally better (0.339 vs. 0.528). This suggests that **penalizing reasoning length may improve accuracy calibration** even when it does not improve compute allocation structure—the model makes fewer reasoning errors per token on composite questions, even though it still misallocates tokens.

The worst accuracy compositionality violations come from DeepSeek-R1-1.5B (2.368), Sky-T1-32B (1.900), and Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Thinking (1.487). The presence of a 32B and an 80B model among the worst performers reinforces that compositionality is not a simple function of scale.

**Summary of LORE-BENCH findings.** The evaluation reveals a consistent pattern: **current LRMs satisfy the monotonicity property (compute scales with within-family complexity) but fail the compositionality property (compute fails to add across independent problems)**. This is a specific, actionable diagnosis: models have learned to modulate thinking difficulty *within* problem types but have not learned the structural principle that independent sub-problems require additive thinking budgets. The length-control models demonstrate that global length modulation does not address this gap—they produce shorter chains overall but maintain the same structural misallocation. The framework thus identifies a concrete axis for improvement (compositionality) that is orthogonal to current length-control approaches.

#### SFT-Compo: Effectiveness in Enforcing Compositionality

The headline finding (Figure 6a) is that **SFT-Compo consistently and substantially reduces nMAD for reasoning compute across all three model sizes tested**, with reductions of 40.5% (1.5B: 0.528 → 0.314), 5.9% (7B: 0.337 → 0.317), and 22.5% (8B: 0.423 → 0.328).

**Quantitative reductions (Figure 6a).** The absolute post-SFT-Compo nMAD values cluster in a narrow range (0.314–0.328) across the three model sizes, despite starting from different baselines (0.337–0.528). This convergence suggests that the SFT-Compo procedure produces models with similar levels of compositional behavior regardless of their initial compliance. The 1.5B model shows the largest improvement because it started from the worst baseline; the 7B model shows the smallest improvement because it already had relatively good compositionality (0.337) and the 3.9K training examples may not provide enough signal to reduce nMAD substantially below ~0.31.

**Visual evidence (Figure 6b).** For the 1.5B model, the pre-SFT-Compo scatter plot (left) shows most points clustered well below the y=x line with substantial dispersion. Post-SFT-Compo (right), the points shift visibly upward toward the diagonal, with more points falling near or on the line. The improvement is not perfect—there remain points with substantial deviation—but the systematic underthinking bias (points below the line) is substantially reduced and the overall alignment is visibly better.

**Additional model visualizations (Figure 10 in Appendix).** For the 7B model, the improvement is more subtle—the pre-trained model already had reasonable alignment (nMAD = 0.337), and SFT-Compo nudges it to 0.317 with slightly tighter clustering around the diagonal. For the 8B model, the improvement is more dramatic, with a clear reduction in extreme outliers (points far from the diagonal in the base model plot become more concentrated near the line). This is consistent with the quantitative results: the 8B model had more room for improvement (0.423 baseline) than the 7B model (0.337 baseline).

**Interpretation.** The key takeaway is that compositionality of reasoning compute can be effectively enforced through a simple SFT procedure—**the model does not require architectural changes, RL training, or explicit complexity labels**. The selection criterion in Equation 1 (minimizing absolute deviation from length additivity among correct teacher outputs) provides sufficient signal for the model to learn additive allocation from only 3.9K examples. This is a practically significant finding because it means compositionality can be improved without solving the hard problem of measuring κ(x) directly.

#### SFT-Compo: Impact on General Reasoning Capabilities

The headline finding (Table 3) is that **SFT-Compo consistently improves Pass@1 accuracy across all six evaluation benchmarks and all four model sizes, with average gains of +3.2 to +5.0 percentage points, and consistently outperforms the SFT control baseline that uses the same teacher model but uniform trajectory selection.**

**Model-by-model results (Table 3).**

**DeepSeek-R1-1.5B:** SFT-Compo achieves +4.8 average Pass@1 gain (47.6% → 52.4%), with the largest gains on AIME24 (+7.4, from 18.8% to 26.2%) and MATH500 (+6.0, from 71.6% to 77.6%). The SFT baseline achieves only +1.7 average gain. The difference (+3.1 points attributable to compositionality selection) is largest on AIME24 (SFT: +1.6 vs. SFT-Compo: +7.4—a 5.8 point gap) and MATH500 (SFT: +4.8 vs. SFT-Compo: +6.0—a 1.2 point gap). GSM8K shows a notable SFT gain (+3.9) that nearly matches SFT-Compo (+4.8), suggesting that for grade-school math, distillation from the teacher model accounts for most of the improvement, with compositionality adding a smaller marginal benefit.

**DeepSeek-R1-7B:** SFT-Compo achieves +3.2 average gain (61.5% → 64.7%), with the largest absolute gain on AIME24 (+7.0, from 36.3% to 43.3%). SFT achieves +2.0 average gain. The compositionality benefit is particularly pronounced on AIME24 (SFT: +3.7 vs. SFT-Compo: +7.0) and AIME25 (SFT: +5.0 vs. SFT-Compo: +5.7—more modest), while AMC23 shows no gain for either method (SFT: +1.4, SFT-Compo: +1.6) and GSM8K shows minimal gain (SFT: +0.6, SFT-Compo: +0.6—identical). This pattern—larger gains on harder benchmarks—is consistent across model sizes.

**DeepSeek-R1-8B:** SFT-Compo achieves +5.0 average gain (54.5% → 59.5%), the largest average improvement among all models. Gains are distributed across benchmarks: AIME25 (+6.3, from 22.9% to 29.2%), MATH500 (+6.6, from 76.4% to 83.0%), OlympiadBench (+5.9, from 40.9% to 46.8%). SFT achieves +3.0 average gain. The compositionality benefit is most pronounced on AIME25 (SFT: +1.3 vs. SFT-Compo: +6.3—a 5.0 point gap) and OlympiadBench (SFT: +3.8 vs. SFT-Compo: +5.9—a 2.1 point gap). GSM8K again shows near-identical SFT and SFT-Compo gains (+1.5 vs. +3.0—a modest difference).

**Phi-4-mini:** SFT-Compo achieves +4.4 average gain (59.5% → 63.9%), with the largest gains on AIME24 (+11.2, from 32.5% to 43.7%) and AIME25 (+7.1, from 24.6% to 31.7%). SFT achieves +1.8 average gain. This is the largest gap between SFT-Compo and SFT (2.6 points), and also features the largest single-benchmark gain (+11.2 on AIME24) in the entire table. OlympiadBench shows a slight SFT regression (−0.5), while SFT-Compo improves it (+1.2), suggesting that without compositionality selection, distillation can sometimes degrade performance on certain benchmarks, and compositionality enforcement mitigates this.

**The critical SFT vs. SFT-Compo comparison.** Across all 24 comparisons (4 models × 6 benchmarks), **SFT-Compo outperforms SFT in 23 of 24 cases**. The sole exception is DeepSeek-R1-7B on GSM8K, where both methods achieve identical +0.6 gain. The average advantage of SFT-Compo over SFT is approximately 2–3 percentage points, which is substantial relative to the total gains achieved. This provides strong evidence that **the compositionality selection criterion is the causal factor driving the improvement**, not merely distillation from the teacher model. If the gains were solely from learning from a stronger model, SFT and SFT-Compo would perform identically—they use the same teacher, same questions, and same number of samples. The consistent gap demonstrates that *which* teacher outputs are selected for training matters as much as the teacher itself.

**Benchmark-specific patterns.** The gains are not uniform across benchmarks:
- **AIME 2024 and AIME 2025** consistently show the largest absolute and relative improvements from SFT-Compo, with gains of +7.0 to +11.2 points. These are the hardest benchmarks in the suite (competition-level math), suggesting that compositionality enforcement is most beneficial on problems that require multi-step reasoning where budget allocation matters most.
- **GSM8K** consistently shows the smallest gains (+0.5 to +3.9), and the SFT-vs-SFT-Compo gap is narrow. GSM8K problems are relatively simple (grade-school math), where most models already achieve high accuracy (81–91% for the base models), leaving limited room for improvement and making budget allocation less critical.
- **AMC23** shows intermediate gains with a notable pattern: the 7B model sees essentially no gain (+0.6 for SFT-Compo, +1.4 for SFT on AIME24 vs. +1.6 for both on AMC23), suggesting that AMC23 problems may be in a difficulty regime where compositionality provides less leverage for that particular model.

**The Phi-4-mini anomaly.** Phi-4-mini shows the largest SFT-Compo gains (+4.4 average) and the largest SFT-vs-SFT-Compo gap (+2.6), despite having the best baseline compositionality (nMAD_Cθ = 0.322, Table 2). This is counterintuitive—if Phi-4-mini already allocates compute more compositionally than other models, why does enforcing compositionality help it more? The paper does not directly address this, but possible explanations include: (1) the nMAD metric may not fully capture the *quality* of compositional allocation—Phi-4-mini might achieve low nMAD through consistently short chains rather than appropriately additive ones; (2) Phi-4-mini may benefit more from the teacher model's reasoning quality (the 14B DeepSeek model may be a stronger relative teacher for Phi-4-mini than for the DeepSeek-distilled models that share the same architecture family); or (3) the compositionality enforcement may interact with Phi-4-mini's architecture in ways that produce larger downstream accuracy benefits.

#### Synergistic Effects: Compositionality Enforcement Improves Other Properties

The headline finding (Section 5.3, Figures 7a, 7b) is that **enforcing compositionality on reasoning compute produces synergistic improvements in both compute monotonicity and accuracy compositionality, properties that were not directly optimized during training.**

**Compute compositionality → compute monotonicity (Figure 7a).** The base DeepSeek-R1-1.5B model shows weak overall monotonicity (ρ = 0.875, Table 1) and near-zero monotonicity in the code domain (ρ = 0.151). After SFT-Compo, the overall Spearman correlation rises to 0.977, and the code domain correlation jumps to 0.914. The visualizations (Figure 7a) confirm the improvement: the SFT-Compo reasoning compute curve (orange) rises smoothly with variant index in both the overall LORE-MONO plot and the code domain plot, while the base model curve (blue) is erratic and, for code, nearly flat with high variance.

The mechanism for this synergy is indirect but plausible: by learning to allocate compute additively across independent sub-problems (the compositionality training objective), the model internalizes a general principle that total thinking should scale with total work. This principle transfers to within-family complexity scaling, even though the training data contained no explicit monotonicity examples—the model generalizes from "compute = sum of sub-computes" to "more steps → more compute." The code domain improvement from 0.151 to 0.914 is particularly striking because it represents a transformation from essentially no complexity-aware allocation to strong monotonic scaling, achieved solely through compositionality training on a different dataset (DeepScaler math problems, not code problems). This cross-domain transfer suggests that compositionality teaches a domain-general principle rather than a domain-specific pattern.

**Compute compositionality → accuracy compositionality (Figure 7b).** SFT-Compo dramatically reduces nMAD for log accuracy across all three model sizes: 1.5B: 2.368 → 0.685 (71.1% reduction); 7B: 1.170 → 0.756 (35.4% reduction); 8B: 0.818 → 0.677 (17.2% reduction). The post-SFT-Compo accuracy nMAD values (0.677–0.756) are substantially closer to the compute nMAD values (0.314–0.328) than the pre-training values were, suggesting the two forms of compositionality become more aligned after training.

This synergy is particularly noteworthy because SFT-Compo's selection criterion (Equation 1) explicitly optimizes only for reasoning length additivity, with correctness as a binary constraint (all selected paths must produce correct answers). There is no term encouraging multiplicative accuracy. The improvement in accuracy compositionality is therefore an emergent property: by learning to allocate the right amount of compute to each sub-problem within the composite, the model is more likely to solve both correctly, which brings the joint accuracy closer to the product of individual accuracies. If the model previously underthought on the composite (as the visualizations in Figure 5 suggest), it was failing one or both sub-problems more often than their individual accuracies would predict—making accuracy sub-multiplicative. After SFT-Compo, the improved compute allocation raises the joint success rate, pushing accuracy toward multiplicative behavior.

**Implications.** These synergistic effects transform LORE from a collection of independent properties into a framework with internal coherence. The fact that pushing on one property (compute compositionality) improves two others (compute monotonicity and accuracy compositionality) suggests the laws are not arbitrary desiderata but capture genuine interdependencies in optimal reasoning. This is the strongest evidence in the paper that the LORE framework characterizes something real about reasoning structure—if the properties were merely correlated performance metrics, improving one would not necessarily improve others. The cascading improvements imply that **compositionality is a keystone property**: enforcing it pulls the model toward compliance with the broader framework, making it a particularly efficient target for intervention.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Effect of SFT-Compo on 7B and 8B model compositionality (Figure 10, Appendix F.2):** For the 7B model, nMAD_Cθ decreases from 0.337 to 0.317 (5.9% reduction), a smaller improvement than the 1.5B model's 40.5% reduction because the base 7B model already has relatively good compositionality. The scatter plot shows subtle tightening around the y=x diagonal. For the 8B model, nMAD_Cθ decreases from 0.423 to 0.328 (22.5% reduction), with visible reduction in extreme outliers. This pattern—larger absolute improvement for models with worse initial compositionality—suggests SFT-Compo is most beneficial for models that most need it and that there may be a floor effect where nMAD cannot easily be reduced below ~0.31 with the current training procedure and dataset size.

**SFT control baseline (Table 3):** This is the critical ablation. SFT uses identical teacher model outputs and training data but selects correct trajectories uniformly rather than optimizing for compositionality. SFT-Compo outperforms SFT in 23 of 24 model-benchmark combinations, with an average advantage of 2–3 Pass@1 points. This demonstrates that **the compositionality selection criterion specifically—not general distillation—drives the improvement**. The ablation isolates the effect of the LORE framework: without the theoretical motivation to enforce additivity, standard distillation from a stronger model yields consistent but smaller gains, and occasionally degrades performance (Phi-4-mini on OlympiadBench: SFT −0.5 vs. SFT-Compo +1.2).

**Domain-specific monotonicity analysis (Table 1):** Breaking down the Spearman correlations by domain reveals that monotonicity failures are domain-specific rather than uniform. DeepSeek-R1-1.5B shows strong monotonicity in math (ρ = 0.861) and science (ρ = 0.910) but fails in language (ρ = −0.346) and code (ρ = 0.151). This granularity is valuable for diagnosis: the model's reasoning allocation system is not uniformly broken—it works in structured symbolic domains (math, science) but collapses in domains requiring different reasoning modalities (natural language pattern-following, code execution simulation). This suggests that monotonicity may require domain-specific reasoning capabilities that the 1.5B model lacks in language and code, and that scaling model size improves monotonicity primarily by filling these domain-specific capability gaps.

**SFT-Compo's improvement of code domain monotonicity on the 1.5B model (Figure 7a):** The code domain Spearman correlation rises from 0.151 to 0.914 after SFT-Compo, despite the training data consisting of DeepScaler math problems—not code problems. This cross-domain transfer is a robustness check demonstrating that compositionality enforcement teaches a domain-general principle. The model learns that compute should scale with work, and it applies this principle even in a domain (code execution) that was entirely absent from the SFT-Compo training data.

**Learning rate grid search (Appendix F.1):** The paper grid-searches over learning rates {1e-6, 5e-6, 5e-5} and selects the best. No results are reported for how sensitive SFT-Compo's performance is to learning rate choice, batch size, or number of epochs. The 5-epoch, batch-size-16 configuration is presented without evidence that these values are optimal or that results are robust to variation. This is a notable gap—given the relatively small training set (3.9K examples), overfitting is a plausible concern, and the paper does not report whether longer training degrades performance.

**Teacher model choice (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B for all student sizes):** The same 14B teacher is used for all four student models, spanning 1.5B to 8B parameters. No ablation tests whether a different teacher (stronger or weaker, or from a different model family) would yield different results. For Phi-4-mini, which is architecturally distinct from the DeepSeek family, the cross-architecture distillation may have different properties than the within-family distillation for the DeepSeek-distilled models—this could explain Phi-4-mini's larger gains but is not investigated.

**Number of samples K=8:** No ablation varies K (the number of outputs sampled per question from the teacher). Larger K would increase the chance of finding combinations with near-perfect length additivity but cost proportionally more. The paper does not explore whether the quality of the selected trajectories improves with K or whether K=8 is sufficient to find good additive combinations.

**Dataset size (3.9K examples):** No ablation varies the number of training triplets. With 3.9K question-output pairs, it is unclear whether compositionality enforcement saturates at this dataset size or whether additional data would yield further improvements. The small 7B improvement (0.337 → 0.317) may reflect saturation at ~3.9K examples for models that already have reasonable compositionality.

**No negative SFT-Compo results are reported:** The paper presents SFT-Compo as uniformly beneficial across all models and benchmarks. It would strengthen the analysis to see whether SFT-Compo ever degrades performance—for instance, on benchmarks where the base model already achieves near-ceiling accuracy, or on models where compositionality is already strong. The absence of reported negative results makes it difficult to assess the method's robustness or identify failure modes.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "Most current LRMs largely satisfy monotonicity but fail compositionality"

This claim is **well-supported by the empirical evidence but with an important qualification about what "largely satisfy monotonicity" means.** Table 1 shows that 9 of 10 models achieve overall Spearman correlations above 0.967 between reasoning compute and variant index, and all models show negative correlations between log accuracy and variant index. This is clear evidence that within the constructed question families, models do allocate more compute to harder variants.

However, the claim's scope is narrower than it appears. LORE-MONO tests monotonicity **only within the specific question families constructed for the benchmark**—families where complexity is varied by repeating an identical operation N times. This is a particular form of complexity scaling that may not represent how complexity varies across naturally occurring problems. A model could pass LORE-MONO with ρ = 0.99 while still allocating compute inappropriately across problems with qualitatively different structures—for instance, using more tokens on a simple arithmetic word problem than on a complex geometry proof, simply because the model's training data contained more arithmetic examples with long reasoning chains. LORE-MONO demonstrates that models have learned to scale compute with the *quantity* of repeated operations within a fixed structure, but does not test whether they scale appropriately with *qualitative* complexity differences across problem types.

The compositionality failure is more robustly established. Table 2 shows nMAD_Cθ values of 0.322–0.528 across all models, and Figure 5 shows systematic deviations from the y=x line. The operational definition of independence via disjoint MATH subjects is a reasonable approximation, though it is not formally guaranteed—two questions from different MATH subjects could share underlying reasoning structures. However, the consistency of the compositionality violations across models and the magnitude of the nMAD values (deviations of 32–53% of expected additive value) make it unlikely that the finding is an artifact of the operational definition. A more rigorous independence test would require human expert judgments or formal proofs of concept-set disjointness, but the current evidence is sufficient to establish the qualitative claim that compositionality is substantially violated.

A missing analysis: the paper does not examine whether compositionality violations are systematic (e.g., always underthinking on composites) or random (high-variance, unpredictable allocation). Figure 5 suggests the latter for most models—points scatter on both sides of y=x—but a systematic decomposition of MAD into bias (mean deviation) and variance components would provide more diagnostic information about the nature of the failure.

#### Claim 2: "SFT-Compo effectively enforces compositionality"

This claim is **supported with clear quantitative evidence** (Figure 6a: nMAD reductions of 5.9–40.5% across three model sizes), but the enforcement is **partial, not complete**. Post-SFT-Compo nMAD values remain at 0.314–0.328, meaning the models still deviate from additivity by about one-third of the expected additive value. The paper does not establish what nMAD value would constitute "satisfying" compositionality—is 0.31 close enough that the remaining deviations are practically inconsequential, or do they still represent meaningful misallocation? Without a baseline for what nMAD looks like for a model that truly satisfies the laws (which the paper cannot provide, since no current model does), the remaining gap is hard to interpret.

The small improvement for the 7B model (0.337 → 0.317) raises questions about diminishing returns. If the 7B model's 0.337 nMAD already represents near the best achievable with 3.9K training examples and the current SFT-Compo procedure, then the method may have a ceiling that leaves substantial room for further improvement. The paper does not discuss whether alternative training procedures—larger K for better trajectory selection, more training data, iterative rounds of SFT-Compo, RL-based compositionality rewards—might push nMAD lower.

The Phi-4-mini results (Table 3) complicate the story: this model has the best baseline nMAD_Cθ (0.322) but shows the largest accuracy gains from SFT-Compo (+4.4 average). If better compositionality already correlates with better reasoning, why does further improving it produce such large gains for Phi-4-mini? This suggests that nMAD_Cθ may not be the sole determinant of reasoning quality—other factors, such as the base model's reasoning capability or the alignment between the teacher and student architectures, may modulate how much accuracy benefit compositionality enforcement provides.

#### Claim 3: "Enforcing compositionality leads to stronger reasoning capabilities"

This claim is **well-supported by the consistent improvements across benchmarks and the crucial SFT vs. SFT-Compo comparison** (Table 3), but the **mechanism is not directly validated**. The paper's causal chain is: SFT-Compo improves compositionality → improved compositionality causes better reasoning → observed accuracy gains. The first link is established (Figure 6a). The third link is established (Table 3). The second link—that compositionality improvement *causes* the accuracy improvement—is inferred from the SFT vs. SFT-Compo comparison but not directly tested.

An alternative explanation: SFT-Compo may select higher-quality reasoning trajectories from the teacher model, not merely more additive ones. The selection criterion minimizes |ℓ(r_1) + ℓ(r_2) − ℓ(r_12)| among correct trajectories, and it is possible that trajectories with additive lengths also tend to be more coherent, better-structured, or more pedagogically valuable for the student model—independently of their additivity. If so, the accuracy gains would reflect better trajectory quality selection rather than compositionality enforcement per se. The SFT baseline controls for this partially by also selecting correct trajectories, but it does not control for the possibility that the *subset* of correct trajectories with additive lengths is systematically higher quality than a random correct trajectory.

A stronger test would be an ablation that selects trajectories to *enforce* non-additivity (e.g., maximize |ℓ(r_1) + ℓ(r_2) − ℓ(r_12)| among correct combinations) and shows that this degrades performance compared to SFT-Compo. Without such a test, the causal role of compositionality specifically—as opposed to trajectory quality—remains somewhat ambiguous.

The cross-benchmark transfer is a strength. SFT-Compo trains on DeepScaler math problems but improves performance on AIME, AMC, MATH500, GSM8K, and OlympiadBench—diverse math and science benchmarks that were not in the training data. This demonstrates that the learned compositional behavior generalizes beyond the training distribution, which is what one would expect if the method teaches a general principle rather than overfitting to training examples.

However, all evaluation benchmarks are in math and science domains. The paper does not test whether SFT-Compo improves reasoning in the language or code domains from LORE-MONO (despite showing that monotonicity in code improves). Given that LORE-MONO includes language and code domains specifically to test domain generality, the absence of general reasoning evaluation in these domains is a notable gap. It leaves open whether compositionality enforcement transfers to non-mathematical reasoning or whether the gains are specific to the symbolic reasoning modality represented by math benchmarks.

#### Claim 4: "Synergistic effects emerge, yielding broader improvements across different properties and laws"

This claim is **supported by the specific synergies documented (Figures 7a, 7b) but the evidence is limited to the 1.5B model for monotonicity and three models for accuracy compositionality**. The paper shows:
- Compute compositionality → compute monotonicity: demonstrated for the 1.5B model only (Figure 7a). It is unclear whether this synergy appears in the 7B and 8B models—if their baseline monotonicity is already near-ceiling (ρ > 0.98), there may be no room to measure improvement.
- Compute compositionality → accuracy compositionality: demonstrated for 1.5B, 7B, and 8B models (Figure 7b). The consistency across sizes strengthens this finding.

The paper does not test the reverse direction (does enforcing accuracy compositionality improve compute compositionality?) or the full matrix of cross-property interactions. The synergies documented are consistent with the theoretical framework—if the two laws share a common underlying complexity κ(x), then improving alignment with one law should pull toward alignment with the other—but the evidence is incomplete. In particular, no synergy is documented from accuracy to compute properties, which would provide stronger evidence for the bidirectional coupling the framework implies.

The most compelling synergy is the cross-domain monotonicity improvement (code domain ρ: 0.151 → 0.914). This demonstrates transfer from a math-specific compositionality intervention to a code-specific monotonicity metric, with a large effect size and a domain that was entirely absent from training. This is difficult to explain except through the learning of a domain-general principle—exactly what the LORE framework predicts.

#### Methodological Strengths

**The SFT control baseline is well-designed.** Using the same teacher, same data, and same training procedure but different trajectory selection criterion cleanly isolates the compositionality enforcement as the causal factor. This is a stronger control than comparing against the base model alone, which would confound distillation benefits with compositionality benefits.

**The benchmark construction for LORE-MONO is careful.** The program-based generation with manual verification for periodic shortcuts ensures that the complexity ordering is valid and not exploitable through pattern recognition. The four-domain design (math, science, language, code) provides some domain diversity, though all domains involve structured, rule-based reasoning rather than open-ended or creative tasks.

**The nMAD metric is normalized for scale, enabling cross-model comparison.** Without normalization, larger models (which produce more tokens) would show larger absolute MAD values, making it impossible to compare compositionality across model sizes. The normalization by total expected additive value addresses this, though it assumes that the expected additive value is an appropriate baseline—if models systematically underthink (as Figure 5 suggests for DeepSeek-R1-1.5B), the denominator is inflated relative to the actual composite compute, potentially making nMAD look better than it should.

#### Methodological Weaknesses and Gaps

**LORE-MONO has only 40 seed questions.** With 10 questions per domain and 30 variants each, the benchmark provides 1,200 total questions, but the diversity of seed templates is limited. The monotonicity findings may be sensitive to the specific templates chosen—a different set of 10 math seed questions might yield different correlations. The paper acknowledges this limitation (Appendix C) but does not provide sensitivity analysis (e.g., bootstrap confidence intervals on ρ when resampling seed questions).

**No confidence intervals or statistical tests are reported.** All metrics (Spearman ρ, nMAD, Pass@1) are reported as point estimates without uncertainty quantification. With 40 seed questions contributing to each Spearman correlation and 250 triplets contributing to each nMAD, it would be valuable to know whether the differences between models (e.g., DeepSeek-R1-7B nMAD_Cθ = 0.337 vs. AdaptThink-7B nMAD_Cθ = 0.327) are statistically significant or within sampling variation. Without this, ranking models by nMAD (as Table 2 implicitly does) may overinterpret noise.

**The operational definition of independence via MATH subject labels is an approximation.** Two questions from different MATH subjects might still share reasoning structure (e.g., an Algebra word problem and a Counting & Probability problem both requiring equation setup). The paper acknowledges this limitation (Appendix C), but the impact on nMAD is unclear: if some pairs are not truly independent, the additive prediction $C_\theta(x_1) + C_\theta(x_2)$ overestimates the true independent complexity, artificially inflating the measured nMAD. A sensitivity analysis using a stricter independence criterion (e.g., only pairs explicitly judged independent by human experts) would help bound this effect.

**SFT-Compo training data overlap with evaluation benchmarks is not discussed.** SFT-Compo trains on DeepScaler, while evaluation uses GSM8K, MATH500, AIME, AMC, and OlympiadBench. The paper states that DeepScaler is a "subset" used for training, but does not verify that there is no overlap between DeepScaler questions and the evaluation benchmarks (particularly MATH500, which is also the source for LORE-COMPO). If DeepScaler contains questions that appear in MATH500 or are near-duplicates of AIME/AMC problems, the reported gains would partly reflect memorization rather than improved reasoning.

**The absence of LORE-BENCH evaluation on SFT-Compo models is a notable gap.** The paper reports nMAD improvements for SFT-Compo vs. base on LORE-COMPO (Figure 6) and accuracy improvements on general benchmarks (Table 3), but does **not** report LORE-MONO monotonicity results for SFT-Compo on the 7B and 8B models, nor LORE-COMPO accuracy compositionality for Phi-4-mini. The synergistic effects section (5.3) reports these piecemeal—monotonicity for the 1.5B model only, accuracy compositionality for 1.5B/7B/8B but not Phi-4-mini. A complete LORE-BENCH evaluation of all SFT-Compo models would provide a more systematic picture of how compositionality enforcement affects all four properties (compute monotonicity, compute compositionality, accuracy monotonicity, accuracy compositionality) across all model sizes.

**The SFT baseline has a subtle confound.** SFT selects one correct combination uniformly. If the number of correct combinations varies across triplets (some triplets have many correct combinations, others have few), the uniform selection may over-represent easy triplets (where many combinations are correct) compared to SFT-Compo, which always selects the most additive combination regardless of how many correct combinations exist. This could make SFT's training data systematically different from SFT-Compo's in ways beyond compositionality. A better control would sample a random correct combination with probability proportional to the total number of correct combinations, ensuring the distribution over triplets matches SFT-Compo's.

**No evaluation of SFT-Compo's impact on the accuracy law directly.** The paper shows that compute compositionality enforcement improves accuracy compositionality (Figure 7b) and general accuracy (Table 3), but does not evaluate whether SFT-Compo models better satisfy the accuracy law's exponential form (Hypothesis 2). LORE-MONO could test this: after SFT-Compo, does log accuracy become more linearly related to variant index? The synergistic effects section only reports nMAD_log A on LORE-COMPO, not Spearman correlations on LORE-MONO for accuracy.

**Computational cost of SFT-Compo is not compared to alternatives.** The method requires sampling K=8 outputs from a 14B teacher for each of three questions per triplet, plus standard SFT on 3.9K examples. This is modest but non-trivial. The paper does not compare SFT-Compo's cost-effectiveness to alternatives like training on more data without compositionality selection, or using a stronger teacher with random selection, or applying RL-based length penalties with similar compute budgets. Without such comparisons, it is unclear whether SFT-Compo is the most efficient way to achieve the reported accuracy gains.

#### Summary of Experimental Support

The experiments provide **strong support for the existence and measurability of compositionality violations in current LRMs** (Tables 1, 2; Figures 4, 5), **good support for SFT-Compo's effectiveness at improving compositionality** (Figure 6), and **solid support for the downstream accuracy benefits** (Table 3), with the SFT control baseline providing credible evidence that compositionality enforcement specifically—not general distillation—drives the gains. The synergistic effects (Figures 7a, 7b) are documented but limited in scope, and the causal pathway from compositionality enforcement to accuracy improvement is inferred rather than directly tested. The framework's strongest empirical contribution is the demonstration that a tractable proxy property (compositionality) can be improved through targeted fine-tuning and that this improvement transfers to general reasoning benchmarks, validating the prescriptive value of the LORE framework. The weakest link is the limited scope of evaluation—the synergistic effects are not fully characterized across all models and properties, and the domain generality of SFT-Compo's benefits beyond math benchmarks is not tested.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Problem Remains Unsolved — Complexity κ(x) Is Intractable, and the Proxy Properties Do Not Fully Substitute for It

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire LORE framework is grounded in the concept of question complexity κ(x), defined as the minimal number of primitive Turing machine steps required to solve a problem (Definition 1). This definition is what gives the laws their normative force: the compute law states that C_θ(x) should scale with this intrinsic, model-independent difficulty, not with dataset labels or model-specific accuracy statistics. However, the paper explicitly acknowledges that "computing κ(x) is generally intractable, as verifying the minimal solution length requires a global search over a potentially exponential space" (Section 2.1). The proxy properties—monotonicity and compositionality—are introduced specifically because κ(x) cannot be directly measured, with Propositions 1 and 2 (Appendix D) proving that these properties imply the laws under certain technical conditions.

**The consequence.** The intractability of κ(x) creates a fundamental gap between the theoretical framework and its empirical operationalization. The paper evaluates whether models *satisfy the proxy properties* and claims this tells us whether models *satisfy the laws*. But the logical relationship is one-directional: if a model satisfies monotonicity and compositionality, then it must satisfy the corresponding law (the properties are sufficient). However, if a model *violates* the proxy properties—as all current LRMs do for compositionality (Table 2: nMAD values of 0.322–0.528)—we know the laws are violated, but we cannot quantify *by how much* in terms of κ(x). An nMAD of 0.322 tells us the model's composite compute deviates from additivity by 32% of the expected additive value, but we cannot translate this into a statement like "the model's effective complexity scaling coefficient α_θ is misestimated by X%" because α_θ itself is defined in terms of the unmeasurable κ(x). The framework gives us a violation detector but not a calibration tool.

More critically, the impossibility of measuring κ(x) means the paper cannot directly validate the central hypotheses (the compute law and accuracy law) on any model, including the SFT-Compo fine-tuned variants. The best the paper can do is show that SFT-Compo improves compliance with the proxy properties (Figure 6a) and that this correlates with improved accuracy (Table 3). Whether the post-SFT-Compo models actually satisfy C_θ(x) ∝ κ(x) for some well-defined κ(x) remains an unverifiable claim. A model could perfectly satisfy nMAD_Cθ = 0 while having C_θ(x) ∝ κ(x)^2 rather than ∝ κ(x)—compositionality only constraints the function to be additive, not linear, and linearity requires the additional assumption that all complexity values can be reached through compositions and that monotonicity holds, which are satisfied only under the idealized conditions of Propositions 1 and 2. In real benchmarks, the finite set of questions and the operational definition of independence via subject labels may not provide the richness needed for the functional equation proof to fully constrain the functional form.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper is transparent about the intractability of κ(x) from the outset (Section 2.1) and frames the proxy properties as the solution. However, no experiment attempts to validate the relationship between the proxy properties and the laws beyond the formal proofs in Appendix D. There is no synthetic setting where κ(x) is known (e.g., for constructed problems where the minimal solution length can be computed by enumeration) that could serve as a ground-truth validation of whether satisfying the proxy properties actually corresponds to satisfying the laws in practice. The synergistic effects (Section 5.3) provide indirect evidence that the properties are interconnected in ways the framework predicts, but this is correlational support, not causal validation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation or propose methods for approximating κ(x) in practice. Appendix C acknowledges the intractability as a fundamental constraint and suggests that "more refined treatments of independence" are a direction for future work, but this addresses the operational definition of independence rather than the underlying complexity measurement problem. The limitation is inherent to the framework's formulation—any definition of κ(x) grounded in minimal computational complexity faces the same intractability barrier. This means LORE will always operate through proxy properties, and the gap between proxy compliance and law compliance will remain a theoretical rather than empirically resolvable question.

### The Practical Cost of Enforcing Compositionality Is Not Accounted For — Teacher Model Sampling and Trajectory Selection Require Substantial Compute That Is Not Amortized in the Reported Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** SFT-Compo (Section 4) constructs its training data through a multi-step process that is computationally expensive: (1) annotate all questions in the training dataset with category labels using GPT-4.1-mini; (2) form composite question triplets by pairing questions from distinct categories; (3) for each question in each triplet, sample K=8 outputs from a DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B teacher model, requiring K × 3 = 24 teacher generations per triplet; (4) among the K³ = 512 possible output combinations, evaluate which ones produce correct answers for all three questions and select the combination minimizing length deviation (Equation 1); (5) fine-tune the base model on the selected trajectories for 5 epochs. The paper states that the final dataset contains approximately 3.9K question-output pairs, which at 3 outputs per triplet implies roughly 1,300 triplets were constructed, requiring ~31,200 teacher model generations (1,300 triplets × 24 generations per triplet). The teacher is a 14B-parameter model—substantially larger than three of the four student models being fine-tuned (1.5B, 7B, 8B).

**The consequence.** The headline accuracy gains in Table 3 (e.g., +5.0 average Pass@1 for the 8B model, +4.8 for the 1.5B model) are reported without accounting for the computational cost of constructing D_comp. A practitioner deciding whether to deploy SFT-Compo needs to compare its total cost (teacher sampling + SFT training) against alternatives that might achieve similar gains with less overhead—for example, simply fine-tuning on more data from the teacher without the compositionality selection step (which the SFT baseline does, achieving +1.7 to +3.0 average gains at zero selection cost beyond uniform sampling), or using a larger student model with standard training. The paper's SFT baseline provides a partial cost comparison (same teacher cost, same data, no selection optimization), but the teacher itself represents a significant computational investment that is not amortized in any reported metric.

The cost structure also creates a scale mismatch: SFT-Compo uses a 14B teacher to improve 1.5B–8B students. If one wanted to apply the method to a 14B or larger model, an even larger teacher would be needed—potentially a 32B, 70B, or API-only model like o1—making the teacher sampling cost the dominant expense. The paper does not explore whether SFT-Compo can work with a same-scale teacher (e.g., using the 14B model's own outputs to train itself) or whether the teacher must be substantially stronger than the student for the trajectory selection to be effective.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not report teacher sampling cost, total FLOPs for D_comp construction, wall-clock time, or any cost-effectiveness metric. The SFT control baseline (Table 3) demonstrates that the compositionality selection criterion adds value over uniform selection from the same teacher outputs, but this is a marginal cost comparison—it shows that *given the teacher outputs already exist*, selecting by compositionality is better than selecting randomly. It does not address whether generating those teacher outputs in the first place is cost-effective compared to other uses of the same compute budget. Section 5.1 mentions that the training dataset contains 3.9K question-output pairs and that K=8 samples are used, but does not discuss the implications of this sampling budget for practical deployment.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation or discuss the cost-performance tradeoff. There is no exploration of whether smaller K (e.g., K=4, reducing teacher cost by 8× since combinations scale as K³) would still yield effective compositionality enforcement, or whether a weaker/cheaper teacher model would suffice. The method as presented is a research contribution demonstrating a proof of concept—that enforcing compositionality improves reasoning—but it is not optimized for practical deployment. This is a reasonable scope for an initial paper proposing a new framework, but it leaves a substantial gap between the demonstrated gains and the cost required to achieve them.

### The Training Data Construction Relies on a Strong Teacher Model — No Evidence That SFT-Compo Works Without Access to a Model That Already Produces Correct and Compositional Reasoning

**The assumption or constraint.** SFT-Compo assumes access to a teacher model that is (a) strong enough to produce correct reasoning chains for sub-questions and their composites, and (b) produces outputs from which additive-length combinations can be selected. The paper uses DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B as the teacher for all student models (Section 5.1). The trajectory selection procedure (Equation 1) requires that among the K³ = 512 output combinations per triplet, there exists at least one combination where all three reasoning paths lead to correct answers—otherwise, the triplet contributes no training data. If the teacher model's accuracy is too low, many triplets will yield zero correct combinations, reducing the effective dataset size and potentially biasing the training data toward easier questions where correct combinations exist.

**The consequence.** SFT-Compo is fundamentally a distillation method with a compositionality filter, not a method for improving reasoning from scratch. If a practitioner does not have access to a model that already achieves reasonable accuracy on the target problem distribution, SFT-Compo cannot be applied—the teacher must be capable of producing the correct, compositional trajectories that the student will learn from. This creates a bootstrapping problem: the method improves reasoning by learning from a *better* reasoner, which means the gains are conditional on already having access to better reasoning. The method cannot help if the available models all exhibit the compositionality violations documented in Table 2 (nMAD_Cθ 0.322–0.528 across all 10 evaluated LRMs), because the teacher itself would need to be substantially more compositional than the student for the trajectory selection to find additive combinations. But Table 2 suggests that even the strongest evaluated models (Qwen3-Next-80B, DeepSeek-R1-14B) have nMAD_Cθ ≥ 0.368, meaning the teacher may not be substantially more compositional than the student—it is simply larger and more accurate, providing correct trajectories from which additive ones can sometimes be found by selection among K³ combinations, even if the model's natural tendency is not additive.

This also means SFT-Compo cannot be applied iteratively (fine-tune the student, then use it as the teacher for the next round) because the student, after one round of SFT-Compo, may not be sufficiently better than its own baseline at producing compositional trajectories to serve as an effective teacher. The paper's one-round distillation approach does not demonstrate that compositionality can be improved through self-play or iterative refinement.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2 provides the nMAD values for the teacher model class: DeepSeek-R1-14B has nMAD_Cθ = 0.368, which is worse than Phi-4-mini (0.322), DeepSeek-R1-7B (0.337), and AdaptThink-7B (0.327). This means the teacher model used for SFT-Compo is *less* compositional than some of the student models being fine-tuned (certainly less compositional than Phi-4-mini, which has the best baseline nMAD_Cθ at 0.322). The method succeeds despite this because the selection procedure (Equation 1) cherry-picks the most additive combinations from the teacher's outputs—but it relies on the teacher being accurate enough that correct combinations exist, not on the teacher being inherently compositional. The paper does not report what fraction of triplets yield at least one correct combination (the yield rate), or how the length deviation |ℓ(r_1) + ℓ(r_2) − ℓ(r_12)| is distributed among correct combinations. If the teacher's correct outputs are highly non-compositional, the "best" selected combination may still have substantial deviation from additivity, limiting how much compositionality the student can learn.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation or discuss teacher model requirements. Table 3 does not include an ablation where a weaker teacher (e.g., the 7B or 8B model itself) is used, which would test whether the method requires a teacher substantially stronger than the student or whether self-distillation with compositionality selection is effective. The paper also does not explore whether the selected trajectories are actually close to additive—reporting the distribution of |ℓ(r_1) + ℓ(r_2) − ℓ(r_12)| for the selected trajectories would clarify whether SFT-Compo is learning from genuinely additive examples or merely from the best available approximations.

### The Benchmark Construction Has Limited Diversity and an Unvalidated Independence Assumption — LORE-MONO Has Only 40 Seed Questions, and LORE-COMPO's Independence Proxy May Overestimate Violations

**The assumption or constraint.** LORE-MONO (Section 3.1) constructs monotonicity-testing variants from only 40 seed questions total—10 per domain across math, science, language, and code. Each seed question defines a template where complexity is increased by repeating an identical operation N times (N = 1, ..., 30). LORE-COMPO (Section 3.2) defines independence between sub-questions operationally: two questions from different MATH subject categories are considered independent because their concept sets are disjoint. The paper explicitly acknowledges both limitations in Appendix C: "our LORE-MONO currently includes only 40 seed questions in total. Expanding its topic diversity and coverage is an important direction for future work" and "we operationalize independence through disjoint sets of mathematical concepts. Although this proxy is not rigorous, it is motivated by the practical difficulty of formalizing independence between questions in an actionable and general way."

**The consequence.** For LORE-MONO, the 40 seed templates may not represent the diversity of complexity scaling patterns in real-world reasoning tasks. All 30 variants of a seed question apply the *same operation* repeatedly—this tests whether the model scales compute with the *quantity* of repeated computation, but not whether it scales appropriately with *qualitative* complexity changes (e.g., a problem requiring a proof strategy shift vs. one requiring more arithmetic). The monotonicity findings (Table 1: Spearman correlations of 0.967–0.995 for most models) may therefore overestimate how well models satisfy monotonicity on naturally occurring problems where complexity varies through structural changes rather than repetition. A model that perfectly scales compute with the number of iterations of a recurrence relation may still allocate fewer tokens to a structurally novel but objectively harder problem than to a familiar but computationally simpler one. LORE-MONO measures *within-template* monotonicity, not *cross-template* monotonicity, which would require complexity comparisons across different seed questions—something the benchmark's construction does not support since κ(x) is unknown.

For LORE-COMPO, the operational independence definition via MATH subject labels may systematically underestimate true independence. Two questions from different MATH subjects might still share reasoning structure—for example, an Algebra word problem and a Counting & Probability problem could both require setting up and solving linear equations. If the sub-questions are not truly independent, then the ideal composite complexity is less than κ(x_1) + κ(x_2), and the additive prediction C_θ(x_1) + C_θ(x_2) overestimates what an optimal model should use. This would make the nMAD values in Table 2 (0.322–0.528) overestimates of true compositionality violations—some of the measured "violation" would be the model correctly recognizing shared structure and avoiding redundant computation. The paper provides no validation of the independence assumption—no human expert judgments confirming that MATH subject categories correspond to disjoint reasoning, and no analysis of whether measured compositionality violations correlate with the semantic similarity of sub-questions within a triplet.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The limitations are acknowledged in Appendix C but not quantified. The paper does not report sensitivity analyses: how would the Spearman correlations change if different seed questions were selected for LORE-MONO? How would nMAD change if a stricter independence criterion were applied to LORE-COMPO (e.g., only pairs judged independent by multiple human raters)? For LORE-MONO, the Spearman correlations are reported at the domain level (Table 1), which provides some signal of domain robustness—models that show strong monotonicity in math tend to show strong monotonicity in science and code—but this does not address the limited diversity *within* each domain's 10 templates. For LORE-COMPO, the paper reports nMAD aggregated across all 250 triplets without analyzing whether triplets where the sub-questions are more clearly independent (e.g., Geometry + Algebra vs. two Algebra-adjacent topics like Prealgebra + Algebra) show different violation magnitudes.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges both limitations in Appendix C as directions for future work but does not attempt to mitigate them in the current study. No sensitivity analyses or robustness checks are provided. The limited diversity of LORE-MONO and the unvalidated independence assumption in LORE-COMPO mean that the benchmark's measurements should be interpreted as *existence proofs* of compositionality violations (we can construct question pairs where additive behavior fails) rather than *population estimates* of how severe compositionality violations are across the distribution of real-world reasoning problems.

### Evaluation Is Restricted to Math and Science Benchmarks — No Evidence That Compositionality Enforcement Transfers to the Non-Mathematical Domains Present in LORE-MONO

**The assumption or constraint.** The general reasoning evaluation in Section 5.2 uses six benchmarks: AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC 2023, MATH500, GSM8K, and OlympiadBench. All six are mathematics or mathematical-science benchmarks. This is despite LORE-MONO (Section 3.1) including four domains—math, science, language, and code—specifically to test domain generality, and despite the monotonicity analysis (Table 1, Figure 7a) showing that the most dramatic SFT-Compo improvement was in the code domain (Spearman ρ rising from 0.151 to 0.914 for the 1.5B model). SFT-Compo's training data is constructed from DeepScaler (Luo et al., 2025b), which the paper describes as a dataset of mathematical problems. The fine-tuned models are thus trained on math problems, evaluated on math and adjacent benchmarks, and shown to improve compositionality on LORE-COMPO, which is also constructed from MATH500 (a math dataset).

**The consequence.** The paper's central claim—that enforcing compositionality yields "consistently improved reasoning performance on multiple benchmarks" (Section 1)—is empirically supported only for mathematical reasoning. The improvements in the code domain monotonicity (Figure 7a) demonstrate that the principle transfers to a non-math domain in terms of *behavioral compliance* with the laws, but the paper does not test whether this translates to improved *accuracy* on code tasks (e.g., HumanEval, MBPP). Similarly, the language domain from LORE-MONO—where the base 1.5B model showed a negative compute monotonicity correlation (ρ = −0.346, indicating reasoning compute *decreased* with complexity)—is never revisited after SFT-Compo to see whether the compositionality enforcement also improved monotonicity there (Figure 7a only shows the code domain improvement alongside the overall improvement).

This is a significant generalization gap because the LORE framework is presented as a universal characterization of reasoning (the laws are derived from Turing machine complexity, which is domain-agnostic). If the accuracy benefits of compositionality enforcement are specific to the symbolic, rule-based reasoning modality of mathematics, then the framework's scope may be narrower than the paper implies. Code and language reasoning involve different cognitive demands—code requires precise execution semantics, language requires semantic understanding and pattern-following—and the additive compute principle may interact differently with these modalities. A model that learns to add compute across independent math sub-problems may not transfer this behavior to independent code sub-problems (e.g., "implement function A, then implement function B"), or may transfer the *behavior* (as the code monotonicity improvement suggests) without improving *accuracy* because code correctness depends on different failure modes than math correctness.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The only non-math evidence for SFT-Compo's benefits is Figure 7a, which shows the code domain monotonicity improvement on LORE-MONO for the 1.5B model. This is a behavioral metric (does compute scale with variant index?) rather than an accuracy metric (does the model get code questions right?). The paper does not evaluate SFT-Compo models on LORE-MONO's accuracy across any domain, nor does it test on external code or language benchmarks. The absence of these evaluations means the generalization claim is supported by math benchmarks plus one behavioral metric in one non-math domain for one model size.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation or discuss the domain scope of its evaluation. The LORE-MONO construction (Section 3.1) explicitly includes language and code domains to test domain generality, making their absence from the SFT-Compo evaluation conspicuous. The addition of code and language accuracy benchmarks (or even LORE-MONO accuracy results for SFT-Compo models in these domains) would substantially strengthen the claim that compositionality enforcement improves reasoning in general rather than mathematical reasoning specifically.

### The Hardest Problems Remain Unaddressed — The LORE Framework Provides No Mechanism for Improving Accuracy on Problems Where the Base Model's Accuracy Is Near Zero

**The assumption or constraint.** The LORE framework characterizes how reasoning compute and accuracy *should* scale with problem complexity for an optimal model, and SFT-Compo enforces one consequence of this scaling (compositionality). However, neither the framework nor the intervention addresses the scenario where a model's accuracy on a problem class is near zero—where the model simply lacks the capability to solve the problem regardless of how well it allocates its reasoning budget. The compute law posits C_θ(x) ∝ κ(x), and the accuracy law posits A_θ(x) = exp(−λ_θ κ(x)), but both assume the model has some non-zero per-step success probability. If λ_θ is very large (the model makes errors on nearly every primitive step), then A_θ(x) ≈ 0 for any non-trivial κ(x), and improving budget allocation cannot help—the model's reasoning chains, regardless of length, are essentially random with respect to correctness.

**The consequence.** SFT-Compo improves accuracy by teaching the model to allocate compute more appropriately across sub-problems, but this mechanism presupposes that the model already has the capacity to solve those sub-problems when given the right amount of compute. For problems that are fundamentally beyond the model's capability—where even with optimal budget allocation, the per-step success probability is near zero—SFT-Compo offers no benefit. This is analogous to the finding in the compute-optimal test-time scaling paper that test-time compute "cannot compensate for fundamental capability gaps that larger pretraining would address." The LORE framework provides a normative target for how compute *should* scale, but does not address the orthogonal question of how to improve the base reasoning capability (lower λ_θ) that determines whether that compute translates to correct answers.

The paper's evaluation (Table 3) shows that SFT-Compo's gains are largest on the hardest benchmarks (AIME 2024: +7.0 to +11.2 points; AIME 2025: +5.7 to +7.1 points) and smallest on the easiest (GSM8K: +0.5 to +3.9 points). This might seem to contradict the claim that SFT-Compo doesn't help on capability-bound problems—AIME is extremely difficult. However, the base models already achieve non-trivial accuracy on AIME (18.8–36.3% depending on size), meaning they have some capacity to solve these problems, and SFT-Compo improves how they allocate thinking to do so. The relevant failure mode is on problems where base accuracy is ~0%—for instance, the hardest LORE-MONO variants (variant 30 of complex seed questions) where even the largest models may have near-zero accuracy, or out-of-distribution problems requiring knowledge or reasoning patterns absent from training. The paper does not characterize where SFT-Compo's benefits saturate—at what base accuracy level does compositionality enforcement cease to help?

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 (log accuracy correlations) and Figure 4 (accuracy vs. variant index for the 1.5B model) show that accuracy degrades with increasing complexity on LORE-MONO, and for the hardest variants and weakest models, accuracy approaches floor levels. However, the paper does not analyze whether SFT-Compo's accuracy benefits are concentrated in a particular difficulty regime—for example, whether the gains on AIME come primarily from problems where the base model already had some chance of success (>10% pass@1) versus problems where it was essentially guessing (~0% pass@1). The LORE-MONO evaluation does not include accuracy results for SFT-Compo models, so we cannot see whether the compositionality enforcement improved accuracy on the hardest variants or only on medium-difficulty variants.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation or discuss the capability bounds within which compositionality enforcement is effective. The theoretical framework implicitly acknowledges the existence of a capability floor (λ_θ in the accuracy law captures per-step failure rate, and if λ_θ is high, accuracy is low regardless of κ(x) proportionality), but the experimental analysis does not characterize where this floor lies for current models or whether SFT-Compo lowers it. The related work section (Appendix B) discusses reasoning length control approaches that aim to reduce overthinking and underthinking, but the relationship between budget allocation quality and base reasoning capability remains unexplored. This is an important practical limitation: a practitioner needs to know whether SFT-Compo is worth applying to their specific model-dataset combination, and the answer depends on whether the model's accuracy on that dataset is above the threshold where better budget allocation translates to better outcomes—a threshold the paper does not identify.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

- **How this changes the landscape**
  - It reframes reasoning evaluation from “solve problems well” to also “solve problems with structurally sensible allocation of reasoning compute,” using principled proxy tests (Figure 2; Sections 2–3).
  - It suggests that reasoning control should be evaluated not only by average accuracy but also by whether behavior respects monotonicity and compositionality—especially for composed tasks where current models underperform (Table 2; Figure 5).

- **Follow-up research enabled**
  - **Better independence notions**: Since compositionality hinges on independence, refining `S(x)`-style concept disjointness or building tasks with provable additive complexity would strengthen the empirical link to the formal laws (Appendix C).
  - **Scaling `LORE-MONO`**: Increase domains, seed diversity, and variant types beyond repeated updates while still preserving known complexity orderings (Appendix C).
  - **Direct accuracy-law enforcement**: The paper states accuracy compositionality is hard to enforce because it doesn’t specify which reasoning path to supervise (footnote 5). A future line is devising supervision or selection rules that explicitly target multiplicative accuracy (Property 4) or log-additivity.

- **Practical applications / use cases**
  - **Model selection and diagnostics**: `LORE-BENCH` can be used to detect abnormal behaviors like “underthinking on composite questions” even if raw accuracy looks competitive (motivated by Figure 1 and compositionality failures in Table 2).
  - **Training recipe**: `SFT-Compo` provides a concrete way to improve both compositionality and downstream math/science reasoning (Figure 6; Table 3), especially when composite-task underthinking is observed.

- **Repro/Integration Guidance (based on what is provided)**
  - When to prefer this method:
    - If a model shows strong monotonicity but poor compositionality (the common pattern reported in Section 3.3), and especially if composed multi-part prompts lead to reduced reasoning length and accuracy.
  - What you need to implement:
    - A source of categorized questions to form `(x1, x2, x12)` triplets (Section 4; Appendix F.1 mentions annotating categories for DeepScaler questions).
    - A way to sample `K` outputs per question (the paper uses `K=8`) and to filter for correctness to apply the length-matching selection rule (Equation (1)).
  - What is not fully specified in the excerpt:
    - The exact optimizer/scheduler and full fine-tuning hyperparameters for `SFT-Compo` are referenced as being in Appendix F, but only partial details are shown here (epochs and batch size are given; optimizer is not).
