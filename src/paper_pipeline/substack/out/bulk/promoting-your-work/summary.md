Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (the author himself sharing his playbook for promoting engineering work to get recognized):

**Why promoting your work matters: the promotion-committee reality**

Ryan opens by naming the structural reason self-promotion isn't optional: "People decide promotions (usually committees of managers and staff+ engineers)." In a perfect world, doing impactful work would be enough, but "your work can't be recognized unless people know about it." He paints the ideal end-state—"imagine how smooth a promotion would be if everyone on the committee already thought your work was at the next level"—and frames everything that follows as the lightweight, non-sleazy path to that outcome. The goal isn't to become a personal-branding machine; it's to make sure the right people have the evidence they need to advocate for you.

**How much time to spend: the launch-post habit**

His core mechanism is simple and deliberately low-overhead: always write a summary of results from notable projects. He estimates spending "an hour or two" to put together a launch post, and this is the main way people learned about his work. The minimalism is the point—you don't need a constant drip of self-promotional content, just a disciplined habit of documenting wins after they ship. An important secondary benefit: these posts double as performance-review artifacts, so you can just link to them later instead of reconstructing impact from memory, making review season "less work."

**Who to share with: relevance over strategy**

He used two distribution channels. First, he **tagged relevant people**—the engineers and managers directly involved in the project and their managers. Not a targeted list of influencers, just the people who naturally cared about the work. Second, his org had a **biweekly highlights process**—a post aggregating top wins that many managers read—and his launch posts were often pulled into it organically. The through-line: he never strategized about who to get in front of. "I just shared it with who I thought it was relevant to and if the work was impactful it often spread further." Sometimes he'd get asked to speak at team meetings to share knowledge about the changes. The one non-negotiable: always include your manager, because "it will help them represent your work during performance reviews."

**The launch post template: lead with wins**

The writing advice echoes his broader philosophy on communication—"make the first line interesting." For a launch post, that means the first line should be "a summary of the biggest wins." If the impact is compelling enough, people will be curious to read the whole thing. The structure is wins-first, then the details of what changed. This gets the critical message across to skimmers and increases the chance someone reads the entire post. And he closes with a guardrail that undergirds the whole approach: **"Self-promotion is never a replacement for quality work."** Focus almost all your energy on doing great work. Once you have results, just do the incremental work to document and share them—the promotion committee already wants to say yes; your job is to hand them the receipts.

---

His entire playbook rests on a quiet inversion of how engineers often think about visibility: the **launch-post habit** makes promotion not a persuasion problem but a documentation problem. By targeting relevance rather than influence and letting the biweekly highlights process do ambient distribution, he sidesteps the discomfort of "self-promotion" entirely—he's just telling the people who already care what happened. And the structure itself (wins-first, then mechanics) ensures that even a skimming committee member absorbs the signal, which is all they need to advocate for you in the room.
