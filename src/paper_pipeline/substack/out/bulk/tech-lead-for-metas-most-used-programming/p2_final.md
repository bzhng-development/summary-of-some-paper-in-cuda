Here are the takeaways from this Dwayne Reeves interview (Tech Lead for Meta's most-used programming language Hack, went from new grad to Senior Staff Engineer):

**Joining Facebook (as interview practice)**

Dwayne Reeves had no intention of working at Facebook. He was interested in Google (for East Coast offices) and Twitter (for Scala), but a friend already at Facebook convinced him to interview as practice for his Google interviews. He made it to the final rounds, and visiting campus completely changed his view. He had assumed Facebook was “just a website” with nothing for someone interested in core CS problems, but hearing engineers talk about memcache scaling challenges and a custom PHP runtime made him realize “they're actually doing some real engineering work here.” What sealed it was the level of care. After he asked for time to decide, his recruiter emailed a week later with an unsolicited increased offer — “wow, I didn't ask for this” — while Amazon had flatly told him “we don't negotiate with new grads.” Then CTO Bret Taylor took all the accepted MIT new grads to lunch at a local café. “I couldn't imagine any C-suite level person at Google actually taking the time to talk with us personally.” At the time Facebook had roughly 1,000–1,500 employees — small relative to today.

**MIT opened doors, but the education wasn't the real differentiator**

Reeves grew up in Bridgeport, Connecticut: inner city, public schools, child of Jamaican immigrants. Working at Apple or Microsoft “was the dream — I never thought real people actually worked there.” At MIT, recruiters from every tech company came to career fairs and were eager to talk to him starting freshman year. His freshman Microsoft internship alone was enough to get an Apple offer. He's since worked with many engineers just as capable who didn't go to school at all and almost missed Facebook's radar by chance. “I just had more opportunities because MIT had this name recognition.” The actual education was excellent, but the advantage was the surface area for luck it created.

**First team and the early PHP-to-DSL translation grind**

His first team was building a service to evaluate code and move computation outside the main PHP codebase, using a custom DSL they were developing. He spent about two years largely translating PHP into that DSL, then started adding features to the language. The project was ultimately canceled. He pivoted to privacy infrastructure, working on representing privacy policies in more manageable ways than scattered if-statements. When Hack (Meta's gradually-typed PHP dialect) was introduced, his team went all-in on it. “Types, which is what I love. I grew up working in statically typed languages. I just thought they were superior. So PHP was always weird.”

After that project, he spent a quarter migrating a core API — something no one had attempted — using “a lot of hacks, a lot of bash scripts, a lot of ingenuity.” The result: roughly 20% of call sites moving to the Hack API revealed some kind of error, usually around null handling. This became the proof point that Hack wasn't just a cool tool — “it's actually finding mistakes in the code base at scale.” It made Hack be taken much more seriously as a replacement for PHP.

**Why static typing wins at scale (and the uncanny valley problem)**

Static typing, Reeves argues, is an information-communication tool. As codebases and teams grow, keeping everything in your head breaks down; types communicate intent, enable tooling (auto-complete, search), and allow proving that whole classes of errors are absent. “You write some text in the file and because you write it in this way, we have developed ways of proving that this text does not exhibit these classes of errors at all — and you could just rely on it. That's really powerful.” For hobbyists working alone, dynamic languages might be faster; at a hundred engineers, you need help.

He coined the **uncanny valley of type systems** — borrowing from computer graphics, where near-human renders become off-putting. As you migrate from dynamic to static typing, engineers without static-typing intuition start seeing types that don't behave as expected, and the experience gets *worse* before it gets better. The post he wrote on this framed the team's strategy for years: avoid that valley by making the language *sound*, even if it meant breaking some legacy PHP behaviors that were incompatible with a reliable type checker. “If you have a language where everything is typed but the types can't be relied upon, that creates a less-than-ideal state for development.”

**Senior promotion (IC5): writing almost no code and getting the highest review ever**

After the successful API migration, he was asked to scale Hack adoption more broadly. He spent a half talking to people, giving ideas to the Hack team and other engineers, and writing almost no code himself. He thought he was having his worst half ever. His manager initially told him to find time to write code, then reversed course the next one-on-one: keep focusing on outcomes. Hack coverage went from 20–30% to 60–70%; people stopped writing PHP-first. He still worried his performance review would suffer from low code output. Instead, he got his highest review ever, was redefined at the half, and got promoted to IC5 — without even being in the conversation.

“It really solidified to me that writing code is just not what my job as a software engineer is. My job is to identify and solve problems. Sometimes the best way is writing code; sometimes it's bringing clarity to a problem and the path forward. If others end up writing the code, then that's fine.” It marked the shift from valuing himself as a coded-output machine to valuing strategic direction.

**Staff promotion (IC6): forced to let go of “my project”**

He joined the Hack team in 2015, unexpectedly named tech lead just as most existing engineers left. He spent a year redesigning Hack's collection/array system, convinced the runtime team to implement it, and began the rollout. He saw this as his IC6 project. His manager told him to hand it off and work on something else. Reeves was upset — “this is my project. This is my path.” The manager gave him a **rocket-ship analogy**: the first-stage engines get you out of orbit; once you've escaped, you fire different jets. “I only cared about you getting us out of orbit. Now that we've done that, pass this off to someone else and find something else to do.” He wouldn't let Reeves leave the one-on-one until he agreed.

The half ended and Reeves was ready to discuss his *path* to IC6 — only to be told he'd already been promoted. In both IC5 and IC6, the promotions were surprises because his mental model of what the next level required — direct control and personal code output — was wrong. “Each time it felt like being okay with having less direct control on the outcome. The real important thing I did was aligning what the right strategy and right problems were in the first place.” He also credits a strong manager relationship as the through-line — “the times when I felt most connected is when I felt I was doing my best work.”

**The reluctant manager (and why he switched back)**

He didn't want to be a manager. His manager raised it because the team was growing fast; Reeves said no. When he later heard about the TLM (tech lead manager) role, he thought it might be a nice transition — still coding, some management. His manager warned him TLM is two jobs and not recommended, then reversed course (likely after talking to the director) and said “actually, we should try this.” Reeves agreed because he'd noticed a pattern: every time he did something uncomfortable, it led to better results.

It went well for a couple of years — from three reports up to 12–13 — and he started having managers report to him. The tension emerged as the org grew: the best thing for the 35–40-person language team (his technical vision) diverged from what was best for the six or seven ICs he directly managed. At one PSC, he was told he'd be a GE as an IC — his tech lead work for 35+ people was excellent — but as a manager he was merely good, not exceeding. He faced a choice: replace himself as tech lead to focus on being an org manager, or go back to IC so his time could freely serve the larger org's technical needs. The choice was obvious: “it was just easier to find a manager for six to seven people than me trying to find someone who can be an effective tech lead for our group.”

**Managing ICs vs. managing EMs**

The difference, he says, is supporting someone through technical problems versus people problems. With ICs, the feedback loop is short — you coach, they take on larger work, you see results. As a manager, “the things I attempt to do in one meeting, I have no idea if it helps. It might be six months, nine months down the line where something clicks.” With EMs, the loop is even longer, and he found his brain was still 80% focused on technical challenges. He was a self-described “vibe manager,” not actively optimizing the org — “fine if the org just ran and I was able to do technical work.” The thing he enjoyed most was coaching, particularly IC coaching, because it aligned with his natural IC mindset.

**Senior Staff promotion (IC7): crossing the uncanny valley at scale**

If you asked anyone on the team what they were doing, they'd say “we're trying to cross the uncanny valley.” The vision he'd articulated in that post drove the team for years. The promotion came as execution accumulated enough results attributable to that vision — it was treated as “just a matter of time.” When he joined in 2015, maybe 5–10% of Hack files were in strict mode; by the promotion, essentially the entire Hack codebase was strict, plus language-runtime changes had paid off. He was still officially a manager when he got the promo (TLM didn't formally begin until M2 equivalent), but the promo was based on technical contributions, not org leadership.

**Working with brilliant engineers and overcoming imposter syndrome**

Reeves joined Hack with “no prior experience with languages or compilers. Honestly, I didn't even know what a lexer was.” He credits two colleagues for surviving: Paul Bisset, who was his sounding board for any question, no matter how dumb, and Kendall Hopkins, who brought the product-developer perspective on what changes were actually needed. Another colleague, Andrew Kennedy, had “literally written foundational papers on type theory.” Reeves would research type-theory topics and find Kennedy's name already there. “When I first met him, I did not know how amazing his pedigree was, and he's one of the most humble guys I've met.” Working with Kennedy humanized prestige — Reeves would make observations and Kennedy would say “that's an interesting viewpoint I hadn't thought about.” It taught him that “everyone has their strengths and there's something you can always add.” Now 14 years at the company, Reeves is sensitive to the same dynamic from the other side: “every once in a while someone will say ‘wait, you're *the* Dwayne, the Hack guy’ — and I think, don't think of me that way. I want to be approachable.” The humility he saw in senior ICs is something “all senior ICs should try to emulate.”

**Why stay at Meta for 14 years**

He has considered leaving and even received offers, but each time he asked: “Is my motivation for moving trying to solve a temporary problem? Am I unhappy with a project for two months — is that worth changing over?” If he didn't feel valued or faced a toxic environment, he would have left; but the signs of being valued were always there, and the discomforts were resolvable. He also had a voice that carried weight, opportunities to bring in diverse talent, and frankly, “how many opportunities are there to work on a programming language?” His principle: **don't make permanent decisions based on temporary circumstances.** “I feel I'm a bit privileged and fortunate. There's just been so many great people. It's let me have a career that goes beyond my wildest dreams. When I was first learning to code as a high school student, child of Jamaican immigrants, I would never have thought I'd end up with the opportunities I've had.”

**Advice to his younger self**

“When someone tells you ‘write code to do this,’ stop and ask: how do they know that's the right code to write? You should aim to be the person making those decisions about what's the right thing to do.” He had years of imposter syndrome that could have been short-circuited by understanding that his value wasn't measured in keystrokes. “I'm more than what I can type on the keyboard. Focus on being in the rooms deciding what's the right thing to do.” He still remembers being the younger engineer unsure if he belonged, and now sees his role as making sure others don't feel that way about him.

---

Reeves’s arc is a long-running demonstration of his **don't make permanent decisions based on temporary circumstances** principle — applied both to staying at Meta and to pushing through the discomfort of management. That persistence let him fully execute the **uncanny valley of type systems** vision, driving Hack from 5–10% strict to nearly the entire codebase. His manager’s **rocket-ship analogy** (escape orbit, then fire different jets) forced him to repeatedly decouple his identity from direct code output, a lesson that ripples through his advice to “aim to be the person deciding what’s the right thing to do.” The through-line is clear: the value isn't in the keystrokes, but in the clarity of the strategy — and in making yourself approachable enough that others can carry it forward.
