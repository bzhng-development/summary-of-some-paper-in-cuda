# Efficient RLHF: Reducing the Memory Usage of PPO

**ArXiv:** [2309.00754](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00754)

## 🎯 Pitch

PPO for RLHF typically needs over 3× the memory of SFT, but Hydra-PPO flips this: by sharing a single base model with dynamic LoRA switching, it uses *less* memory than SFT while improving alignment. The trick? Turning off the LoRA adapters instantly recovers the frozen reference and reward models, eliminating three full model copies from GPU memory.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Hydra-RLHF**, a set of memory-reduction techniques for the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) stage of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, and evaluates them on Llama 7b and OPT 1.3b across four public benchmarks (GPT-4-LLM, Open-Source Assistant, Learning to Summarize, and StackExchange). The core mechanisms are model-sharing via a multi-headed architecture that integrates the SFT and Reward models into a single base model, and **Dynamic LoRA**, which recovers the frozen reference and reward models from the actor and critic by simply turning off their LoRA adapters during inference (e.g., π_ref ← LO(π_θ) by ignoring LoRA parameters). Hydra-PPO reduces the memory footprint of PPO below that of Supervised Fine-Tuning while improving alignment—achieving a 55.52% aggregate expected win-rate against all competing methods versus 50.68% for standard LoRA-PPO on Llama 7b—and reduces per-sample latency by up to 65% when the memory savings are reinvested into larger batch sizes. On the smaller OPT 1.3b model, however, Hydra-PPO underperforms standard LoRA-PPO (52.33% vs. 56.6% aggregate win-rate), establishing that the benefits of combining language and reward models into a single multi-headed architecture depend critically on sufficient model capacity.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RLHF Is Too Memory-Expensive for Most Practitioners

The paper addresses a stark practical bottleneck in the standard RLHF pipeline: **the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) stage requires over 3× the memory of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**. This memory explosion makes RLHF infeasible for researchers and engineers without access to very large GPU clusters, effectively locking out a substantial portion of the community from alignment research and deployment.

To understand why this is a problem, it's helpful to understand what memory is being consumed. During standard PPO, at minimum *four separate models* must be loaded into GPU memory simultaneously (Section 2, Stage 3):

1. **The Reference model (π_ref):** A frozen copy of the SFT model, kept in memory to compute a KL-divergence penalty that prevents the actor from diverging too far from its supervised starting point.

2. **The Actor (π_θ):** The trained generative model — the model actually being updated by PPO. It generates completions for prompts, and those completions are scored by the reward model.

3. **The Reward model (r_φ(x,y)):** A frozen copy of the trained reward model, used to score the actor's outputs and provide the reward signal that drives alignment.

4. **The Critic or Value Function (V(x,y)):** An additional trained model, initialized from the reward model, that estimates the expected return (cumulative future reward) for a given partial sequence. This enables Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), which reduces the variance of the PPO policy gradient updates.

Each of these models, for a large language model like Llama 7b, occupies tens of gigabytes of GPU memory just for the parameters alone — before accounting for activations, optimizer states, and intermediate tensors. The paper reports that a standard full-fine-tuning PPO run on Llama 7b would require approximately **220 GB of GPU memory** per A100 80GB GPU (Table 1), even at a batch size of 1. This is a scaled-up estimate from OPT 1.3b because the authors note that "Full PPO overflows our setup" on Llama 7b (Appendix B) — the memory requirements exceed what their 8×A100 80GB nodes can handle.

### Why This Problem Matters: Alignment as Infrastructure

RLHF has become, as the paper puts it, "an integral part for creating safe and controllable foundation models" (Section 1). The conversational quality of ChatGPT, the helpfulness of GPT-4, and the harmlessness of models like Claude are all substantially attributable to alignment through some form of RLHF or preference optimization. The paper cites a series of landmark results — InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022), Anthropic's Helpful & Harmless assistant (Bai et al., 2022a), Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022b), and the Llama 2 family (Touvron et al., 2023) — each of which used RLHF as a critical component.

But there's a tension: RLHF is simultaneously described as essential and as "limited in usage, being both highly complex and demanding a massive amount of memory" (Section 1). The paper points out that "the use of RLHF is in its infancy" and that "there is a strong need to evaluate its variations in terms of speed and performance" (Section 1). In other words, the field knows alignment matters, but the practical barriers to entry are so high that only well-resourced organizations can meaningfully experiment with PPO-based alignment.

This has downstream consequences beyond academic research. If alignment techniques are only accessible to large industrial labs, the broader community cannot audit, improve, or adapt these methods for domain-specific applications (e.g., medical QA, legal summarization, educational tools). The democratization of alignment — making RLHF feasible on consumer hardware or small academic clusters — is therefore not just an engineering convenience; it's a prerequisite for building safe and controllable language models outside of a handful of organizations.

### The Gap: No Systematic Analysis of Memory-Saving Techniques for PPO

The paper identifies a specific analytical gap: while various memory-efficient techniques exist — parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA (Hu et al., 2021), model-sharing strategies, and multi-task architectures — there has been **no comprehensive comparison of how these techniques affect the memory usage, training speed, and alignment quality** of the PPO stage specifically.

This is not a trivial gap because PPO imposes unique constraints that differ from standard supervised training:

- **PPO requires both a frozen reference model and a frozen reward model** during training. These are not needed during SFT. The memory they occupy is essentially dead weight from a gradient perspective (they don't need optimizer states or gradients), but it still consumes significant parameter memory.

- **PPO training is notoriously unstable** (Engstrom et al., 2020; Rafailov et al., 2023; Schulman et al., 2017). Any modification that changes the optimization dynamics — such as sharing parameters between the actor and critic, or combining the language modeling and reward modeling objectives — risks destabilizing training in ways that aren't obvious from supervised fine-tuning experiments.

- **The inference-to-update ratio in PPO is high.** PPO alternates between generating sequences (inference) and updating model parameters (training). This means memory savings that reduce the number of models loaded can also enable larger inference batch sizes, which directly improves throughput — but this interaction between memory savings and throughput isn't obvious from looking at parameter counts alone.

Prior work had treated these methods in isolation: LoRA had been studied for SFT and for standard supervised tasks, but its interaction with the multi-model architecture of PPO hadn't been characterized. Multi-headed architectures were well-known in multi-task learning (Ruder, 2017; Crawshaw, 2020) and reinforcement learning (Mnih et al., 2016; Silver et al., 2016; Flet-Berliac and Preux, 2020), but hadn't been systematically evaluated in the context of RLHF's specific requirements. The paper explicitly positions itself as filling this gap: "we delve into the training process and model architectures of standard RLHF-PPO. Through this investigation, we identify substantial opportunities for memory/computation cost reduction through the implementation of model-sharing" (Section 1).

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper characterizes the limitations of existing approaches along several axes:

**Full Fine-Tuning PPO is prohibitively expensive.** The default approach — training all parameters of both the actor and critic, while keeping full copies of the reference and reward models — is described as "costly to run" and "infeasible for most practitioners" (Section 1). The paper doesn't even attempt full fine-tuning PPO experiments on Llama 7b because of the memory requirements. The 220 GB per GPU estimate in Table 1 makes this explicit: this is beyond what even high-end GPU clusters can support at reasonable batch sizes.

**Standard LoRA-PPO reduces but doesn't solve the memory problem.** Applying LoRA to the actor and critic — which replaces full parameter updates with low-rank adapter training — reduces the trainable parameter count dramatically. However, it doesn't address the fact that *four separate base models* must still be loaded. LoRA-PPO still requires loading the full parameters of the reference model, the actor's base model, the reward model, and the critic's base model. The paper reports that LoRA-PPO on Llama 7b uses **68 GB of GPU memory** at batch size 1 (Table 1). While this is dramatically better than 220 GB, it still represents over 3× the memory of SFT, and at batch size 1 it yields a throughput of only 0.053 samples per second (18.75 seconds per sample). This is too slow for practical research iteration.

**Recent alignment alternatives to PPO don't address memory.** The paper acknowledges a growing body of work that aims to replace PPO entirely by folding preference optimization into supervised training — RAFT (Dong et al., 2023), RRHF (Yuan et al., 2023), PRO (Song et al., 2023), and notably DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), which reframes the RLHF objective as a direct preference classification loss. While these methods are promising and address the *complexity* of PPO, the paper argues they are "orthogonal" to its contribution: "our work is orthogonal to these methods, aiming not to replace RLHF, but rather to make it more widely usable" (Section 5). In other words, even if direct alignment methods eventually supersede PPO, the reality is that PPO remains the dominant approach in production systems (InstructGPT, Claude, Llama 2), and making it more accessible has immediate practical value.

**No prior work addresses the structural redundancy in PPO's model architecture.** This is the paper's key observation: the reference model and the actor share the same base parameters (both are initialized from π_SFT), and the reward model and the critic share the same base parameters (the critic is initialized from the reward model). In standard PPO, these pairs are loaded as four independent copies in GPU memory. The paper identifies this as "substantial opportunities for memory/computation cost reduction through the implementation of model-sharing between Reference/Reward Models and Actor/Critic Models" (Section 1). Prior work had not systematically exploited this redundancy because it requires architectural changes (multi-headed models, dynamic LoRA switching) that go beyond simply applying PEFT methods to the existing PPO pipeline.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning has two key aspects:

**First, it frames itself as a systematic analysis, not just a method proposal.** The introduction explicitly states the goal: provide "a comprehensive analysis of the memory usage, performance, and training time of memory-savings techniques for PPO" (Abstract). This is an empirical study of tradeoffs, not just a novel architecture. The paper compares five approaches (SFT, LoRA-PPO, Hydra-SFT, J-Hydra-PPO, Hydra-PPO) across four datasets and two model sizes, measuring both alignment quality (via GPT-4 evaluation and ROUGE scores) and computational efficiency (memory usage, latency per sample, throughput). This breadth is intentional — the paper wants to establish not just that Hydra-PPO works, but *where* it works (Llama 7b) and *where it doesn't* (OPT 1.3b), providing actionable guidance for practitioners.

**Second, it positions the contribution as an enabler rather than a replacement.** The paper explicitly states that Hydra-RLHF "opens up the possibility for the community to apply RLHF for a wider variety of models and applications" (Section 6). The goal is to lower the barrier to entry so that researchers can experiment with PPO-based alignment without requiring massive GPU clusters. This is distinct from papers that propose entirely new alignment algorithms (like DPO) — Hydra-RLHF is designed to work *within* the existing PPO framework, making it a drop-in improvement rather than a paradigm shift.

The paper also acknowledges a specific limitation of its scope: it assumes the SFT and Reward Model training datasets are the same, since the multi-headed Hydra architecture requires pairwise preference data for training the SFT head (the model needs both a preferred and dispreferred completion to learn from, since the SFT objective is trained on the winning response while the reward model head is trained on the preference comparison). The authors note that "Hydra-RLHF could be extended to handle exceptions when data is limited" (Section 5), but this constraint means the method currently requires datasets structured in a specific way.

Finally, the paper positions its Dynamic LoRA technique as a simple but previously overlooked optimization: "Rather than loading π_SFT twice, π_ref can be recovered from the actor by 'turning off' LoRA" (Section 3). This is not a complex algorithmic innovation — it's a straightforward consequence of how LoRA works (the adapter weights can be disabled to recover the original model) — but it had not been systematically applied to the multi-model PPO architecture before this work.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This is primarily an **empirical systems paper** whose core idea is that a multi-headed transformer architecture combined with dynamic adapter switching can eliminate the redundant model copies in standard PPO-based RLHF, reducing memory usage below SFT levels while maintaining (and sometimes improving) alignment quality. The paper builds an end-to-end RLHF pipeline, called **Hydra-RLHF**, that replaces the conventional four-model PPO architecture with a single base model carrying two task-specific heads and two independently switchable sets of LoRA adapters — one set for the actor and one for the critic — so that the frozen reference and reward models are recovered *on-the-fly* by simply disabling the LoRA weights rather than loading separate copies into memory.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The Hydra-RLHF system has five major structural components:

1. **A multi-headed base model (π_hydra)** — a single decoder-based transformer with two output heads: (a) a standard causal language modeling (CLM) head that predicts the next token, and (b) a reward modeling (RM) head that outputs a scalar value for a given input sequence. This single model replaces the conventionally separate SFT and Reward models.

2. **Two independent sets of LoRA adapters** — low-rank update matrices (rank 128) applied to all linear layers of the base model. One set belongs to the actor (the generative policy being trained), and the other set belongs to the critic (the value function being trained). These adapters are the *only* parameters updated during PPO; the base model weights remain frozen.

3. **Dynamic LoRA switching** — a runtime mechanism that toggles which (if any) LoRA adapters are active. When LoRA is **on**, the base model plus active adapters forms either the actor (π_θ) or the critic (V(x,y)). When LoRA is **off**, the bare base model serves as either the reference model (π_ref) or the reward model (r_φ(x,y)), depending on which head is queried. The operation is denoted `LO(·)`, meaning "ignore LoRA parameters."

4. **Two-stage training pipeline** — Stage 1 (Hydra-SFT) jointly trains the multi-headed model on both the language modeling and reward modeling objectives using pairwise preference data. Stage 2 (Hydra-PPO) freezes the base model, attaches the two LoRA adapter sets, and runs PPO optimization with dynamic LoRA switching.

5. **Evaluation harness** — GPT-4-based grading on a 0–9 scale with step-by-step reasoning, applied across four benchmark datasets (GPT-4-LLM, Open-Source Assistant, Learning to Summarize, StackExchange), plus ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L for the summarization task.

Information flows as follows during PPO: a prompt enters the system → the actor (base model + actor LoRA) generates a completion of length T → the critic LoRA is swapped in and computes value estimates for the generated sequence → LoRA is turned off, and the bare base model computes (a) reference log-probabilities using the CLM head and (b) a reward scalar using the RM head → Generalized Advantage Estimation computes advantage estimates Â from the reward and value estimates → the actor and critic LoRA parameters are updated via the PPO clipped surrogate objective (actor) and squared-error loss (critic) in alternating minibatch steps → repeat for the next batch of prompts.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the standard RLHF-PPO pipeline (Section 2 of the paper) — because Hydra-RLHF's modifications are best understood as a systematic transformation of this baseline architecture, and each design choice targets a specific memory redundancy in the standard approach.
- **Second**, the Hydra-SFT training objective and the dynamic LoRA mechanism — since these are the two foundational building blocks that enable the memory savings in Hydra-PPO.
- **Third**, the Hydra-PPO training procedure — walking through the generation, scoring, and update phases with explicit model-switching mechanics.
- **Fourth**, the Joined-Hydra-PPO ablation — since sharing LoRA weights between actor and critic is the natural next compression step, and understanding why it fails highlights important properties of PPO optimization.
- **Fifth**, the design choices and hyperparameter selection — covering the specific settings that make Hydra-PPO work (LoRA rank, learning rates, batch size scaling, dataset pairing strategy) and what would happen if they were changed.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

The paper is a **systems contribution** that restructures the multi-model RLHF-PPO pipeline into a single-model architecture with dynamically-switched adapters, then empirically characterizes the resulting memory, latency, and alignment-quality tradeoffs. The central technical insight is that the four models required during PPO — (1) actor, (2) reference, (3) reward, and (4) critic — share their base parameters in pairs: the actor and reference are both initialized from π_SFT, and the critic is initialized from the reward model r_φ. By parameterizing the actor and critic as a shared frozen base plus separate low-rank updates, and by combining the language model and reward model into a multi-headed architecture, each pair collapses to a single set of base parameters with switchable adapter weights and heads.

#### Standard RLHF-PPO as the Baseline (Section 2)

Before modifying the architecture, the paper establishes the conventional three-stage RLHF pipeline that Hydra-RLHF transforms. Understanding the baseline's memory structure is essential because every Hydra design decision targets a specific redundancy in this structure.

**Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).** An input language model is trained using the standard causal language modeling objective:

$$L_{\text{xent}} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y \mid x)\right]$$

where $x$ is a prompt, $y$ is the target completion, and $\pi$ is the language model producing a probability distribution over the completion tokens. The dataset $\mathcal{D}$ consists of prompt-completion pairs. This training yields the model π_SFT. The paper uses the notation **FFT-SFT** when all parameters are trained and **LoRA-SFT** when using Low-Rank Adaptation.

**Stage 2: Reward Model (RM) Training.** The head of the SFT model is replaced with a scalar output layer. This modified model, denoted r_φ(x, y), is trained to predict human preference. The training data consists of preference pairs: for a prompt x, two completions y_w (the preferred or "winning" answer) and y_l (the "losing" answer) are provided. The reward model is trained with the Bradley-Terry preference loss:

$$L_R(r_\phi, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log\left(\sigma(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l))\right)\right]$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid function, $r_\phi(x, y_w)$ is the scalar reward predicted for the winning completion, and $r_\phi(x, y_l)$ is the scalar reward predicted for the losing completion.

**What it computes:** the negative log-likelihood that the reward model assigns a higher score to the preferred completion than to the dispreferred one. The sigmoid function $\sigma(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l))$ squashes the difference in predicted rewards into a probability between 0 and 1, and the loss pushes this probability toward 1 (the reward model should be confident that y_w > y_l).

**Why this form:** the Bradley-Terry model assumes that the probability of preferring y_w over y_l follows a logistic function of the difference in their latent reward scores. This is the standard maximum-likelihood objective for pairwise preference data, and it is the same loss used in InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022) and essentially all subsequent RLHF work. An alternative would be a margin-based ranking loss, but the Bradley-Terry formulation has the advantage of producing calibrated probability estimates that can serve as a reward signal for PPO.

After training, the reward model's outputs are normalized such that:

$$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)}\left[r_\phi(x)\right] = 0$$

This normalization is applied to improve PPO stability — it ensures that the reward signal is centered, preventing the policy gradient from being dominated by a constant offset.

**Stage 3: PPO.** The SFT model π_SFT and the reward model r_φ are used to initialize and subsequently train two new models with Proximal Policy Optimization. Four models are now resident in GPU memory simultaneously:

- **π_ref (Reference):** a frozen copy of π_SFT. Its purpose is to compute a KL-divergence penalty that prevents the actor from diverging too far from its supervised starting point. Without this penalty, the actor could exploit imperfections in the reward model by generating high-scoring but incoherent or unnatural text (reward hacking).

- **π_θ (Actor):** the trained generative model, initialized as a copy of π_SFT. This is the model being aligned; it generates completions for prompts, receives reward scores, and is updated to maximize reward while staying close to π_ref.

- **r_φ (Reward):** a frozen copy of the trained reward model. It scores the actor's generated completions to provide the reward signal.

- **V(x, y) (Critic or Value Function):** a trained model initialized from the reward model r_φ. It predicts the expected cumulative future reward (return) for a given partial sequence. This enables Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), which constructs low-variance advantage estimates for the PPO policy gradient.

The PPO objective is expressed in terms of the probability ratio:

$$r(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y \mid x)}$$

where $\pi_\theta$ is the current policy (actor), $\pi_{\text{old}}$ is the policy at the start of the current update epoch, and $r(\theta)$ measures how much the policy has changed for a given action y given prompt x.

The PPO clipped surrogate objective is:

$$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\min\left(r(\theta)\hat{A}, \text{clip}(r(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon)\hat{A}\right)\right]$$

where $\hat{A}$ is the advantage estimate (computed via GAE from the reward and critic predictions), $\epsilon$ is the clipping threshold (typically 0.2), and $\text{clip}(r(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon)$ constrains the probability ratio to the interval $[1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon]$.

**What it computes:** the expected clipped advantage-weighted probability ratio. The min operation selects either the unclipped ratio $r(\theta)\hat{A}$ or the clipped version, whichever is smaller (more conservative). When the advantage is positive (the action was good), the ratio is clipped at $1 + \epsilon$ to prevent the policy from increasing the probability of that action too aggressively. When the advantage is negative (the action was bad), the ratio is clipped at $1 - \epsilon$ to prevent the policy from decreasing the probability too aggressively.

**Why this form:** the clipping mechanism is the key innovation of PPO over earlier trust-region methods like TRPO. It approximates a trust-region constraint (keeping the new policy close to the old policy) without requiring a second-order optimization step. The clip range $[1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon]$ means the policy update is bounded: no single action's probability can change by more than a factor of approximately 20% in either direction per update epoch. This stabilizes training by preventing the catastrophic policy collapse that can occur with unconstrained policy gradient methods.

The advantage estimate $\hat{A}$ is constructed using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE):

$$\hat{A}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma \lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}$$

where $\gamma$ is the discount factor (set to 1.0 in all experiments), $\lambda$ is the GAE trace-decay parameter (set to 0.95), and $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$ is the temporal difference error at step $t$.

**What it computes:** a weighted sum of future temporal difference errors, with exponentially decaying weights controlled by $\lambda$. When $\lambda = 0$, GAE reduces to the standard one-step TD error. When $\lambda = 1$, it becomes Monte Carlo returns (minus the baseline). Intermediate values produce a bias-variance tradeoff: higher $\lambda$ reduces bias but increases variance.

**Why this form:** the exponentially-weighted sum allows GAE to smoothly interpolate between high-bias low-variance one-step estimates and low-bias high-variance Monte Carlo estimates. The choice of $\lambda = 0.95$ means the estimate is dominated by the first several steps of reward but still incorporates long-term information, which is appropriate for language tasks where the reward signal (a scalar at the end of the sequence) is sparse.

The critic V(x, y) is trained with a standard squared-error regression loss on the empirical returns:

$$L_V = \mathbb{E}\left[(V(x, y) - R)^2\right]$$

where $R$ is the return (cumulative reward) for the sequence, computed from the reward model's scores.

**The memory problem (Stage 3, cont'd).** The critical observation for this paper is that the four models — π_ref, π_θ (actor), r_φ (reward), V(x, y) (critic) — contain substantial parameter redundancy. The reference model π_ref is a frozen copy of π_SFT, and the actor π_θ is *also* a copy of π_SFT that gets updated during PPO. If the actor is trained with LoRA, then the only difference between π_ref and π_θ is the LoRA adapter weights — the base model parameters are identical. Similarly, the critic V(x, y) is initialized from the reward model r_φ, and both share the same base model structure (they differ only in the output head and any training-induced parameter changes). Despite this redundancy, standard PPO loads all four as independent parameter sets into GPU memory, resulting in the 3× memory overhead relative to SFT.

#### Hydra-SFT: The Multi-Headed Base Model (Section 3, Stage 1)

Hydra-RLHF's first structural modification is to train a single model with two output heads that simultaneously learns both the causal language modeling task and the reward prediction task. The model is denoted π_hydra — a decoder-based transformer with:

1. **A causal language modeling (CLM) head** — the standard linear layer plus softmax that predicts the next token in a sequence, producing a probability distribution over the vocabulary.

2. **A reward model (RM) head** — a completely separate linear layer that maps the final hidden state of the decoder to a single scalar, representing the predicted reward or preference score for the input sequence.

**Training objective.** The Hydra-SFT model is trained to minimize:

$$L_{\pi_{\text{hydra}}}(x, y_w, y_l) = L_{\text{xent}}(x, y_w) + \gamma L_R(x, y_w, y_l)$$

where $L_{\text{xent}}(x, y_w)$ is the standard causal language modeling cross-entropy loss on the winning completion only, $L_R(x, y_w, y_l)$ is the Bradley-Terry reward modeling loss on the preference pair, and $\gamma$ is a weighting multiplier that balances the two objectives.

**What it computes:** a weighted sum of two losses. The first term encourages the model to generate the preferred completion y_w token-by-token (this is the language modeling component). The second term encourages the model's RM head to assign a higher scalar score to y_w than to y_l (this is the preference learning component). The multiplier $\gamma$ controls the relative importance — with $\gamma = 0.1$ in all reported Llama 7b experiments, the language modeling loss dominates by a factor of 10.

**Why this form:** a simple unweighted sum (γ = 1) would give equal weight to the RM objective, which could interfere with language modeling performance because the two tasks have fundamentally different loss scales and optimization landscapes. The language modeling loss involves predicting tens or hundreds of tokens per sequence (each contributing to the loss), while the RM loss is a single scalar pairwise comparison. Setting γ = 0.1 was chosen empirically — the paper notes "in practice, we find γ = 0.1 generally works well" — and it reflects the heuristic that reward modeling should inform the representation without dominating it. An alternative would be alternating optimization (train language modeling for N steps, then reward modeling for M steps), but the joint weighted loss is simpler to implement and doesn't require tuning an alternation schedule.

**Dataset constraint: pairwise preference data required.** A critical requirement introduced by this architecture is that the training data must contain pairwise preference annotations. Unlike standard SFT, which only needs prompt-completion pairs, Hydra-SFT needs triples (x, y_w, y_l) for each training example. The language modeling head is trained exclusively on y_w (the winning completion), while the RM head is trained on the comparison between y_w and y_l. The paper explicitly notes this constraint: "L_π_hydra(x, y_w, y_l) requires pairwise comparison data to train both heads, making standard SFT datasets unusable."

**Handling multi-way rankings.** When the preference dataset contains rankings with more than two completions (e.g., y₁ > y₂ > y₃), the standard RM training approach forms all possible pairwise comparisons: (y₁, y₂), (y₁, y₃), (y₂, y₃). However, for Hydra-SFT, this would be problematic because the SFT head would be trained on y₂ and y₃ in pairs where they appear as the "winning" completion (e.g., in the pair (y₂, y₃), y₂ would be the target for language modeling). To avoid this, the paper restricts pair formation to only include the **best-ranked sample** as the winning completion: "Only pairs containing the sample with the best ranking should be considered to avoid training the SFT head on other samples." So for a ranking y₁ > y₂ > y₃, only the pairs (y₁, y₂) and (y₁, y₃) are used — y₁ is always the SFT target, and y₂ and y₃ serve only as losing completions for the RM head.

**Hyperparameters for Hydra-SFT.** All Hydra-SFT training uses the AdamW optimizer with a cosine-decaying learning rate scheduler and weight decay of 0.1 (Appendix B). The reward head multiplier γ is set to 0.1 for most experiments (0.07 for StackExchange due to the specific characteristics of that dataset, where the most up-voted answers are on average longer, creating a risk of trivial length-based reward models). Specific hyperparameters vary by dataset (Table 12):

| Hyperparameter | GPT-4-LLM | Open-Source Asst. | Learning to Sum. | StackExchange |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Learning Rate | 3e-6 | 1e-5 | 5e-7 | 5e-7 |
| Batch Size (per GPU) | 4 | 4 | 1 | 1 |
| Gradient Accum. Steps | 1 | 1 | 10 | 6 |

These numbers reflect dataset-specific tuning: the learning rates were selected by sweeping {5e-5, 5e-6, 5e-7} for 4 epochs, and the batch sizes were adjusted to maximize GPU memory utilization on the 8×A100 80GB node while providing stable training. The total effective batch size is $8 \times \text{batch\_size} \times \text{gradient\_accumulation\_steps}$ (8 GPUs, with gradient accumulation providing larger effective batches without exceeding memory).

#### Dynamic LoRA: Recovering Frozen Models by Disabling Adapters (Section 3)

The second foundational mechanism is **Dynamic LoRA**, a technique that eliminates the need to maintain separate copies of the reference and reward models in GPU memory during PPO. The insight is straightforward: when training the actor and critic with LoRA, the base model parameters are frozen — only the low-rank adapter matrices are updated. This means that **disabling the LoRA adapters recovers the original base model exactly**.

**How LoRA works (recap).** Low-Rank Adaptation (Hu et al., 2021) parameterizes weight updates as the product of two low-rank matrices. For a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ in the base model, the LoRA update is:

$$W' = W + BA$$

where $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$ are the low-rank adapter matrices, and $r \ll \min(d, k)$ is the rank. Only $A$ and $B$ are trained; $W$ remains frozen. The paper applies LoRA with rank $r = 128$ to **all linear layers** of the actor and critic. This is a crucial design choice — higher rank means more parameters and more expressivity, but also more memory. Rank 128 is relatively high for LoRA (many applications use ranks 8–64), reflecting the fact that alignment through PPO may require more substantial updates to the model's behavior than standard domain adaptation tasks.

**Dynamic LoRA operation.** At runtime, the paper defines two states for the model:

- **LoRA ON:** the adapter matrices A and B are active, so the effective weight is $W + BA$. This is used when the model needs to act as the actor π_θ (trained generative policy) or critic V(x, y) (trained value function).
- **LoRA OFF:** the adapter matrices are ignored, so the effective weight is $W$ (the frozen base model). This recovers the reference model (when using the CLM head) and the reward model (when using the RM head). The paper denotes this operation as π_ref ← LO(π_θ), where LO(·) means "ignoring LoRA parameters."

**Why this works.** The actor π_θ is initialized as a copy of π_SFT — its base weights are exactly π_SFT. After some amount of LoRA-based PPO training, the actor's effective weights are π_SFT + LoRA_updates. Turning off LoRA recovers π_SFT, which is precisely the reference model π_ref. Similarly, the critic V(x, y) is initialized from the reward model r_φ, so its base weights (with LoRA off) recover r_φ exactly.

**Memory savings.** Standard PPO requires loading π_SFT twice (once for the actor's base and once as π_ref) and r_φ twice (once as the critic's base and once as the reward model). With Dynamic LoRA, only one copy of each base model is needed. The paper reports that this saves "about 20% of memory while maintaining performance equivalent to LoRA-PPO" (Section 3).

**The LoRA adapter memory footprint.** It's important to note that the LoRA adapters themselves consume memory — specifically, the $B$ and $A$ matrices for every linear layer, plus their optimizer states (moments in Adam). However, the number of parameters in the adapters is dramatically smaller than the base model. For Llama 7b (approximately 7 billion parameters), applying rank-128 LoRA to all linear layers adds roughly 100–200 million trainable parameters, compared to the 7 billion frozen base parameters. This is why LoRA-PPO's memory usage (68 GB at batch size 1, Table 1) is already far below full fine-tuning PPO (estimated 220 GB). Dynamic LoRA further reduces this by removing the duplicated base model parameters for the reference and reward.

#### Hydra-PPO: The Complete Training Procedure (Section 3, Stage 2)

Hydra-PPO combines the multi-headed architecture (Hydra-SFT) with Dynamic LoRA to run the entire PPO stage using a single base model and two sets of LoRA adapters. The training procedure is detailed in Algorithm 2 of Appendix A.

**Model initialization.** After Hydra-SFT training, the base model π_hydra contains both the CLM head (trained on the winning completions) and the RM head (trained on preference pairs). This base model is frozen. Two independent sets of LoRA adapters are added to all linear layers of this base model:

- **Actor LoRA:** one set of low-rank matrices per linear layer, which will be trained to maximize the PPO objective. When these adapters are active, the model functions as the policy π_θ — it generates completions and computes log-probabilities for the policy gradient.

- **Critic LoRA:** a separate, independent set of low-rank matrices per linear layer, which will be trained to predict value estimates. When these adapters are active, the model functions as the value function V(x, y) — it predicts the expected return for a given state.

The two adapter sets are completely independent: the actor LoRA weights are updated only by the actor loss, and the critic LoRA weights are updated only by the critic loss. They share the same base model but occupy different memory and are optimized separately.

**Why two sets of LoRA weights?** A natural question is whether the actor and critic could share a single set of LoRA adapters — after all, they both start from the same base model, and their objectives (maximizing reward-penalized by KL vs. predicting expected returns) might benefit from shared representations. The paper tests this configuration as Joined-Hydra-PPO (or J-Hydra-PPO), using a single set of LoRA adapters for both the actor and critic. The results show it "performs significantly worse than two separate sets" (Section 4), which the authors attribute to the combined actor/critic optimization amplifying the already unstable nature of PPO. This is a key empirical finding: sharing adapters between actor and critic creates destructive interference in the update directions, likely because the policy gradient and value function regression pull the shared parameters in opposing directions.

**Training loop (per iteration).** Algorithm 2 in Appendix A provides pseudocode. Each iteration consists of:

**Phase 1: Sequence Generation (rollout).** For a batch of N prompts:

1. The **actor LoRA is turned on**, and the critic LoRA is turned off. The model, now functioning as π_θ, generates completions of length T tokens for each prompt. The generation is done autoregressively (token-by-token), and the log-probabilities of each generated token under the policy are recorded for later ratio computation.

2. **Immediately after generation**, while the generated sequences are still in memory, the **critic LoRA is turned on** (and actor LoRA remains off or is turned off depending on implementation — the pseudocode specifies turning on critic LoRA and using it to "calculate critic values on the generated sequence"). The model, now functioning as V(x, y), computes the value estimate for each timestep of each generated sequence. This value represents the expected future return from that state.

**Phase 2: Reward and Reference Computation.** For each generated sequence:

1. **Both LoRA adapter sets are turned off** — the model reverts to the frozen Hydra-SFT base. Using only the base parameters:

    a. The **CLM head** computes the reference log-probability of each generated token under π_ref. This is the probability the frozen SFT model would have assigned to the same tokens, and it is used to compute the KL-divergence penalty:
    
    $$\text{KL}_t = \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(a_t \mid s_t)$$
    
    where $\pi_\theta$ uses the actor LoRA (from the generation phase) and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ uses the bare base model with LoRA off.

    b. The **RM head** computes the scalar reward r for the complete generated sequence (or at the final timestep). This reward represents the model's assessment of how well the completion aligns with human preferences.

**Phase 3: Advantage Estimation.** Using the reward signal and the critic's value estimates, Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) computes the advantage $\hat{A}_t$ for each timestep t:

$$\hat{A}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{T-t} (\gamma \lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}$$

where $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$, with $r_t = r - \beta \cdot \text{KL}_t$ (the reward is penalized by the KL divergence from the reference model, with $\beta = 0.02$ controlling the penalty strength). Note that for language tasks, the reward r is typically only available at the final step of the sequence; intermediate steps have $r_t = 0$ (except for the KL penalty, which applies at every step).

**Phase 4: Parameter Update.** The actor and critic LoRA weights are updated for K epochs (the paper uses K = 1, meaning one pass through the data per iteration) with minibatch size M ≤ NT:

1. **Actor update:** With the actor LoRA active, the PPO clipped surrogate objective is minimized:
    
    $$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta_{\text{LoRA}}^{\text{actor}}) = \mathbb{E}\left[\min\left(r(\theta)\hat{A}, \text{clip}(r(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon)\hat{A}\right)\right]$$
    
    where $r(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)}$ uses the current actor LoRA weights for the numerator and the weights from the start of the iteration for the denominator. Only the actor LoRA adapters are updated; the base model and critic LoRA remain frozen.

2. **Critic update:** With the critic LoRA active, the squared-error regression loss is minimized:
    
    $$L_V(\theta_{\text{LoRA}}^{\text{critic}}) = \mathbb{E}\left[(V(s_t) - R_t)^2\right]$$
    
    where $R_t$ is the empirical return (cumulative reward from step t). Only the critic LoRA adapters are updated.

3. **After K epochs:** The old policy $\pi_{\text{old}}$ is updated to the current policy for the next iteration of sequence generation.

**Key design choice: separate forward passes for different model roles.** A subtle implementation detail is that the actor generation, critic evaluation, and reference/reward computation require *different configurations* of the same base model, but they operate on the same data. The pseudocode in Appendix A clarifies the ordering:

1. Generate using actor LoRA only (critic LoRA off).
2. Compute critic values on the generated sequence using critic LoRA only.
3. Turn LoRA off entirely to compute reference log-probabilities and reward.

This ordering minimizes the number of forward passes: the generation produces the sequence; the critic pass reuses the sequence to compute values; and the no-LoRA pass reuses it again for reference probabilities and reward. Without model sharing, each of these would require loading a separate model from memory and running a separate forward pass, which is both memory-intensive and slow.

**Memory footprint of Hydra-PPO.** Table 1 reports the memory usage for Llama 7b on the StackExchange dataset with a total sequence length of 800 tokens per A100 80GB GPU:

| Method | Batch Size | Model (GB) | Activation (GB) | Total (GB) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PPO (full FT, est.) | 1 | 111.8 | 101.3 | ~220 |
| LoRA-PPO | 1 | 53.2 | 12.5 | 68.0 |
| J-Hydra-PPO | 4 | 14.3 | 51.4 | 67.9 |
| Hydra-PPO | 4 | 15.9 | 52.8 | 71.1 |

The critical numbers: Hydra-PPO uses 71.1 GB total at batch size 4, while LoRA-PPO uses 68.0 GB at batch size **1**. The model memory (parameters + optimizer states) drops from 53.2 GB to 15.9 GB because only one base model is loaded instead of four. However, activations increase from 12.5 GB to 52.8 GB because Hydra-PPO processes a 4× larger batch simultaneously. The total memory is similar (71.1 vs. 68.0), but Hydra-PPO processes 4× more samples per GPU per forward pass, directly translating to throughput improvements.

**The batch size increase is the key to latency reduction.** The paper explicitly states: "With the excess memory, Hydra-RLHF may use a higher batch size and therefore train with up to 65% faster per-sample latency" (Section 6). This is not a direct computational speedup per operation — it's an efficiency gain from amortizing the overhead of generation and parameter updates across more samples processed in parallel. The per-sample latency numbers in Table 1 quantify this:

| Method | Batch Size | Inference Latency (s/sample) | Update Latency (s/sample) | Total Latency (s/sample) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LoRA-PPO | 1 | 17.23 | 1.52 | 18.75 |
| Hydra-PPO | 4 | 4.88 | 1.59 | 6.47 |

The inference latency drops from 17.23 to 4.88 seconds per sample — a 3.5× reduction — because four samples are processed in parallel during generation (the generation cost is roughly divided by the batch size). The update latency remains similar (1.52 vs. 1.59) because parameter updates are computed on the same total amount of data per optimizer step. The net result is a 65% reduction in total per-sample latency (from 18.75 to 6.47 seconds).

**Latency scaling with sequence length.** Figure 2 shows that Hydra-PPO's latency advantage grows as sequence length increases. At sequence length 256, all methods are within a factor of ~2 in per-sample latency. At sequence length 1024, Hydra-PPO and J-Hydra-PPO converge (around 6–7 seconds per sample) while LoRA-PPO is above 15 seconds. At sequence length 2048, LoRA-PPO cannot fit in memory on the authors' setup at all, while Hydra-PPO continues to operate. The paper explains this scaling behavior: "As we can see from the figure, Hydra-PPO saves exponentially more time as sequence length increases." The exponential advantage comes from the interaction between activation memory (which grows linearly with sequence length) and batch size: LoRA-PPO at batch size 1 has activation memory growing proportionally to sequence length but no parallelism to amortize it; Hydra-PPO at batch size 4 can always process 4× more tokens per forward pass regardless of sequence length, providing a multiplicative speedup that becomes more valuable as per-sequence computation increases.

**KL-divergence penalty (β = 0.02).** A critical hyperparameter shared across all PPO variants is the KL penalty coefficient β = 0.02 (Appendix B). This controls the tradeoff between reward maximization and staying close to the reference model:

$$r_t^{\text{effective}} = r_{\text{RM}} - \beta \cdot \text{KL}(\pi_\theta \parallel \pi_{\text{ref}})$$

where $r_{\text{RM}}$ is the raw reward from the RM head and KL is the per-token Kullback-Leibler divergence between the actor's output distribution and the reference model's output distribution.

**Why β = 0.02 specifically?** The paper does not provide an ablation study for this hyperparameter, but the choice reflects established practice in the RLHF literature (Ouyang et al., 2022; Stiennon et al., 2022). The KL penalty serves two purposes: (1) it prevents reward hacking, where the actor learns to generate text that the reward model scores highly but that is unnatural or nonsensical (exploiting imperfections in the reward model), and (2) it prevents catastrophic forgetting, where the actor loses the language modeling capabilities acquired during SFT. Too low a β leads to reward over-optimization and degraded generation quality; too high a β prevents the actor from moving far enough from the SFT model to achieve meaningful alignment. The value 0.02 represents a balance that the RLHF community has converged on empirically.

**Other shared PPO hyperparameters.** All PPO runs share: GAE γ = 1.0 (no discount factor — all future rewards are weighted equally, which is appropriate for episodic language tasks where the sequence has a clear end), GAE λ = 0.95 (moderate bias-variance tradeoff for advantage estimation), 100 warmup steps (learning rate linearly increases from zero during the first 100 optimizer steps), 1 epoch per iteration (the data generated in one rollout batch is used for exactly one pass of actor and critic updates), LoRA rank 128 for both actor and critic (the same rank as used in SFT LoRA training), and reward model normalization to the range (-1, 1) (meaning that after training, the RM head's outputs are scaled and shifted to fall within [-1, 1] for PPO stability).

**Checkpoint selection for PPO.** Since PPO can be unstable — the reward can increase initially and then collapse as the actor learns to exploit the reward model — the paper uses a specific checkpoint selection strategy: "We track the mean of the reward from the last 20 steps, and use the model with the best reward throughout training" (Appendix B). This means that the model evaluated in Tables 3–8 is not necessarily the final model after training, but rather the intermediate checkpoint that achieved the highest smoothed reward. This is a practical necessity for fair comparison, but it also means the reported results represent an upper bound on what a practitioner could achieve without such oracle checkpoint selection.

#### Joined-Hydra-PPO: The Single-LoRA Ablation (Section 4)

As an ablation to understand the necessity of separate actor and critic adapters, the paper introduces **Joined-Hydra-PPO (J-Hydra-PPO)**. This variant uses only **one set of LoRA weights** that is shared between the actor and the critic. The training procedure (Algorithm 1 in Appendix A) is otherwise identical: sequences are generated using the single LoRA set as the actor, values are computed using the same LoRA set as the critic (the critic head is still separate from the CLM head — only the LoRA adapters are shared), and updates to the single LoRA set are computed from both the actor's PPO loss and the critic's squared-error loss.

**The combined loss.** In J-Hydra-PPO, the shared LoRA weights are updated by minimizing:

$$L_{\text{joint}} = L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta_{\text{LoRA}}^{\text{shared}}) + \alpha \cdot L_V(\theta_{\text{LoRA}}^{\text{shared}})$$

where α is a **critic loss multiplier** — an additional hyperparameter that controls the relative weight of the value function loss versus the policy gradient loss. The paper reports different α values per dataset: for GPT-4-LLM, α = 0.1; for Learning to Summarize, α = 3; for Open-Source Assistant, α = 0.1; for StackExchange, α = 3 (Table 12).

**Why α varies so dramatically.** The wide range of α values (0.1 to 3) indicates that the optimal balance between the actor and critic losses is highly dataset-dependent when they share parameters. A small α (0.1) means the critic loss has minimal influence on the shared parameters — the policy gradient dominates. A large α (3) means the critic loss is weighted 3× more heavily than the actor loss. The authors don't explain the reason for these specific choices, but the variation suggests that on some datasets (GPT-4-LLM, Open-Source Assistant), the critic's value predictions should only weakly influence the shared representation, while on others (Learning to Summarize, StackExchange), stronger value function regularization is needed to stabilize training.

**Why J-Hydra-PPO underperforms.** The paper reports that J-Hydra-PPO "performs significantly worse than two separate sets" and speculates that "combining actor and critic model amplified the unstable nature of PPO" (Section 4). The mechanism is likely gradient interference: the policy gradient for the actor pushes the shared parameters in a direction that increases the probability of high-advantage actions, while the critic's regression loss pushes the same parameters in a direction that more accurately predicts returns. These directions may conflict — a parameter update that increases a token's probability (good for the actor) might worsen the value prediction (bad for the critic), and vice versa. With separate adapter sets, each set can specialize to its own objective without interference.

The J-Hydra-PPO results also reveal instability in practice: "In general, we find J-Hydra-PPO to be highly unstable, taking multiple attempts to find solid hyperparameters" (Appendix B). This contrasts with the authors' experience that "Hydra-PPO is generally as stable as LoRA-PPO," suggesting that the instability is specifically caused by the shared adapter architecture, not by the multi-headed model itself.

**Why test this at all if it fails?** The J-Hydra-PPO ablation serves an important scientific purpose: it establishes that the memory reduction from model sharing (one base model instead of four) is separable from the further compression of sharing LoRA adapters (one set instead of two). The fact that model sharing works (Hydra-PPO matches or exceeds LoRA-PPO) but adapter sharing doesn't (J-Hydra-PPO underperforms) tells us that the actor and critic need independent *updates*, not just independent *heads*. The shared base representation is beneficial (it saves memory and provides a common feature extractor), but separate adaptation pathways are necessary for stable PPO optimization.

#### Dataset-Specific Modifications to the RLHF Pipeline

The paper makes several dataset-specific adjustments to the standard RLHF pipeline that are important for understanding the experimental setup:

**GPT-4-LLM dataset (Section 4.1).** This dataset consists of instruction-following prompts with responses sampled from multiple foundation models (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, OPT-IML), where the responses are ranked by GPT-4 itself. The paper states: "To our knowledge, we are the first to attempt full RLHF (including PPO) on this dataset." The key modification: "We pair only the highest-scoring response with each other response" — for each prompt, the response ranked best by GPT-4 is used as y_w, and each of the other responses individually serves as y_l in separate training pairs. This creates multiple (y_w, y_l) pairs per prompt, one for each non-winning response, but always with the same y_w. This is the same strategy used in the base Hydra-SFT to avoid training the SFT head on non-winning responses.

**StackExchange dataset (Section 4.4).** The StackExchange dataset contains questions with multiple completions ranked by user votes. The paper modifies the standard approach in two ways. First: "we pair only the best answer with up to 3 other answers" — unlike the standard RM training that would form all pairwise combinations, Hydra-RLHF restricts pairs to those containing the best-ranked answer, capping the number of losing completions per question at 3. Second: "we find that the most up-voted answers are on average longer than the other answers, leading to trivial reward models" — if the reward model learns that longer = better, it fails to capture actual quality. By capping at 3 losing answers and only using the best as the SFT target, the model is forced to distinguish quality beyond simple length heuristics.

**Learning to Summarize dataset (Section 4.3).** The Reddit TL;DR dataset, as modified by Stiennon et al. (2022), is used "without modification" to the data format — each prompt has exactly one preference completion pair, naturally satisfying Hydra-SFT's requirement for pairwise data.

**Open-Source Assistant Datasets (Section 4.2).** The paper uses a combination of four datasets hosted on HuggingFace: "Dahoas/rm-static", "Dahoas/full-hh-rlhf", "Dahoas/synthetic-instruct-gptj-pairwise", and "yitingxie/rlhf-reward-datasets". These are various open-source ChatBot and Assistant-style preference datasets, including Anthropic's Helpful & Harmless data (Bai et al., 2022a). The paper trains on them without modification, using the default preprocessing from DeepSpeed-Chat (the codebase the paper is forked from).

**Summary of dataset usage and constraints.** Across all datasets, the common thread is that each training example must provide at minimum one prompt x, one preferred completion y_w, and one dispreferred completion y_l. This is a strict requirement of the Hydra architecture — it cannot train on standard SFT data (x, y) without preference annotations because the RM head would have no loss signal. The paper acknowledges this limitation: "Hydra-RLHF requires that the SFT and RM training datasets be the same" and "Hydra-RLHF could be extended to handle exceptions when data is limited" (Section 5, "Dataset Formation").

#### GPT-4 Evaluation Protocol (Section 4 and Appendix C)

The paper evaluates alignment quality using GPT-4 as a judge, a now-common approach in the RLHF literature (Zheng et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023). The evaluation protocol is described in detail in Appendix C.

**Scoring methodology.** Each model's output is graded on a **0–9 integer scale** by GPT-4, with the model instructed to "first, give an extremely short, 1-sentence evaluation of the answer" and then provide "the integer score out of 9." The system prompt positions GPT-4 as "an unbiased and harsh critic who judges the quality of answers from an assistant." Each output is graded **3 times at temperature 1**, and the three scores are averaged for a final score. The paper notes that this multi-sample averaging approach was chosen after discovering that simpler methods (e.g., "out of answer one or two, which is better") suffer from order bias: "we found that simply switching the order of the answers would switch the preferred answer by a large margin" (Appendix C).

**Why 0–9 with three samples?** The 0–9 scale provides finer granularity than a binary preference judgment, and averaging across three independent evaluations at temperature 1 (non-deterministic) reduces the variance from any single GPT-4 judgment. The paper doesn't report inter-evaluation agreement statistics, but the choice of three samples is a practical heuristic for reducing noise in LLM-based evaluation.

**Output cleanup.** Before evaluation, the paper applies several preprocessing steps to model outputs: removing end-of-text tokens, stopping generation upon detection of the model speaking for the "other side" (e.g., generating a new instruction and response within its answer), and stopping generation upon detection of a 5-gram phrase sequence that is already present earlier in the same sequence. These cleanup steps prevent degenerate outputs (repetition, hallucinated user-assistant loops) from contaminating the evaluation.

**Evaluation prompt templates.** Two separate grading prompts are used:

1. **For GPT-4-LLM, Open-Source Assistant, and StackExchange:** GPT-4 is asked to judge based on whether the answer is "more helpful, answers the question more precisely, is more concise, is grammatically correct, is kinder and more friendly, and does not contain any rude or confrontational speech."

2. **For Learning to Summarize:** GPT-4 is asked to judge based on whether the summary "is more concise, captures more of the sentiment of the input text, does not contain any false information about the input, and copies minimal text directly from the input."

These criteria reflect the different goals of each task: assistant-style datasets prioritize helpfulness, precision, and politeness; summarization prioritizes conciseness, faithfulness, and avoidance of extractive copying.

**Win-rate calculation.** To compare methods, the paper computes **pairwise win-rates**: for each pair of methods (A, B), the fraction of evaluation samples where A's averaged score exceeds B's averaged score (with ties when scores are equal). The "expected aggregate win-rate" in Tables 3 and 9 is computed by taking each method's total wins against all other methods (counting ties as half a win for each side) and dividing by the total number of comparisons. This produces a single number representing how often a method would be expected to win against a randomly chosen opponent from the set of all methods.

**ROUGE evaluation for summarization.** In addition to GPT-4 evaluation, the Learning to Summarize task is evaluated with ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L scores (Table 4). ROUGE measures n-gram overlap between the generated summary and a reference summary. The paper reports precision, recall, and F-measure for both metrics. The observation that "SFT-based approach typically yields better precision performance, whereas PPO-based methods substantially enhance recall" is attributed to PPO encouraging longer text generation — longer summaries naturally have higher recall (they cover more of the reference content) but lower precision (they include more extraneous content).

#### Model Size and Family: The OPT 1.3b Results (Section 4.5)

The paper extends experiments to OPT 1.3b (Zhang et al., 2022) to test whether the findings generalize across model scales. The results (Table 9) tell a different story at this smaller scale:

| Method | GPT-4-LLM | Open-Source Asst. | Average |
|---|---|---|---|
| SFT | 45.65 | 52.5 | 49.08 |
| LoRA-PPO | 59.5 | 53.7 | 56.6 |
| Hydra-SFT | 44.4 | 42.58 | 43.49 |
| Hydra-PPO | 50.2 | 54.45 | 52.33 |

**Key observations.** Hydra-SFT underperforms standard SFT for OPT 1.3b (43.49% vs. 49.08% aggregate), whereas for Llama 7b, the two were comparable (48.50% vs. 48.55%). This suggests that the multi-headed architecture's joint language-modeling and reward-modeling objective degrades the base model's generation quality when model capacity is insufficient. The paper speculates: "We speculate this difference to be due to the capacity of the model. For the smaller 1.3b model, combining language and reward models may be more difficult."

Furthermore, LoRA-PPO outperforms Hydra-PPO for OPT 1.3b (56.6% vs. 52.33%), the opposite of the Llama 7b result where Hydra-PPO wins (55.52% vs. 50.68%). The paper doesn't provide a detailed explanation, but the implication is clear: the multi-headed architecture imposes a capacity bottleneck that becomes costly at smaller model sizes. The reward modeling head and language modeling head compete for the same hidden representations, and when the model's representational capacity is limited, this competition harms both tasks. At 7 billion parameters, the model has sufficient capacity to support both heads without degradation; at 1.3 billion, it does not.

This finding establishes an important boundary condition for Hydra-RLHF: it is not a universally applicable technique but rather one that becomes beneficial above some minimum model capacity. The paper doesn't determine where that threshold lies (it could be anywhere between 1.3B and 7B parameters), but the OPT 1.3b results serve as a clear caution against applying the method to smaller models without validation.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Model-Sharing in PPO Is a *Structural* Optimization, Not Just a Compression Trick

The dominant assumption in RLHF practice — visible in InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022), DeepSpeed-Chat (Yao et al., 2023), and essentially every PPO implementation referenced in the paper — is that the four models required during PPO (actor, reference, reward, critic) must be loaded as independent copies in GPU memory. This assumption is so ingrained that the 3× memory overhead relative to SFT has been treated as an unavoidable cost of doing RLHF — a fact of nature rather than a design choice to be questioned.

Hydra-RLHF's key intellectual move is to recognize that this redundancy is **architectural, not algorithmic**. The actor and reference share the same base parameters (both are initialized from π_SFT), and the critic and reward model share the same base (the critic is initialized from r_φ). These are not independent models that happen to be similar — they are identical at the parameter level, differing only in whether the parameters have been updated by training (actor vs. reference) or which output head is active (reward vs. critic). The paper's framing of Dynamic LoRA — recovering the reference model from the actor by "turning off" LoRA adapters — recasts memory duplication as a failure to exploit the mathematical relationship between these model instances.

This is not an incremental compression technique like 8-bit quantization or gradient checkpointing, which trade accuracy for memory. It is a **lossless structural optimization**: the reference model *is* the actor's base, exactly, without approximation. The reward model *is* the critic's base, exactly, without approximation. The memory savings come from removing genuine redundancy, not from degrading precision. The paper demonstrates this losslessness implicitly — Hydra-PPO matches or exceeds LoRA-PPO's alignment quality across all Llama 7b benchmarks (Table 3: 55.52% vs. 50.68% aggregate win-rate) — but the more fundamental contribution is the conceptual reframing that makes the redundancy visible in the first place.

Prior work had applied parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) to PPO (LoRA-PPO), which reduces the *trainable* parameter count but leaves the four-model loading architecture intact. Dynamic LoRA is categorically different: it reduces the *loaded* parameter count by exploiting the fact that PEFT creates frozen-base + adapter structures that can be toggled at runtime. This is only possible because LoRA keeps the base model frozen — a property that was designed for parameter efficiency during training, not for memory efficiency during inference, but which Hydra-RLHF repurposes for the latter.

The significance goes beyond the memory numbers in Table 1 (71.1 GB at batch size 4 vs. 68.0 GB at batch size 1). The deeper point is that PPO's memory overhead was never a fundamental requirement of the algorithm — it was an artifact of a specific implementation pattern that the field had accepted without scrutiny. By identifying and eliminating this redundancy, Hydra-RLHF opens the conceptual space for other structural optimizations that ask not "how can we compress this model?" but "why are we loading this model at all?"

---

### Innovation 2: The Multi-Headed Architecture Enables Joint SFT and RM Training Without Degrading Generation Quality — With a Sharp Capacity Cliff

The paper's second conceptual contribution is a controlled empirical demonstration that a single decoder-based model can simultaneously learn to generate high-quality text (through the CLM head) and evaluate preference pairs (through the RM head) **without sacrificing either capability — provided the model has sufficient capacity**. The Hydra-SFT model achieves comparable generation quality to standard SFT on Llama 7b (Table 3: 48.50% vs. 48.55% aggregate win-rate) while producing a reward model head that is *better* than the separately-trained RM on three of four datasets (Appendix F, Table 16: Hydra RM accuracy exceeds standard RM accuracy on GPT-4-LLM, Open-Source Assistant, and StackExchange for Llama 7b).

This is not an obvious result. Multi-task learning has a long history of showing that sharing representations across tasks can improve performance on each (Ruder, 2017; Crawshaw, 2020), but it can also cause **destructive interference** — the gradients from one task push the shared parameters in directions that hurt the other task. In the context of RLHF, the two tasks are in some tension: the language modeling objective encourages the model to assign high probability to specific token sequences, while the reward modeling objective encourages the model to distinguish between sequences based on a scalar preference signal. The fact that these objectives coexist without degradation at 7B parameters but *do* cause degradation at 1.3B parameters (Table 9: Hydra-SFT at 43.49% vs. SFT at 49.08% aggregate win-rate for OPT 1.3b) establishes a **capacity cliff** that was not previously documented for joint language-and-preference training.

The finding is significant because it reframes when and why multi-headed architectures work for alignment. The dominant narrative from multi-task learning is that task relatedness determines success — related tasks benefit from shared representations, unrelated tasks suffer. But language modeling and reward modeling are arguably quite related (both require understanding text quality), yet the 1.3B model fails to combine them. The paper's results suggest that **absolute capacity** is the dominant factor, not task relatedness: the 7B model has enough representational bandwidth to support both heads without interference, while the 1.3B model exhausts its capacity, forcing a tradeoff. This is a practical diagnostic for practitioners: if your base model is below some threshold (somewhere between 1.3B and 7B parameters, per this paper's evidence), a multi-headed Hydra approach will likely degrade generation quality; above that threshold, it may improve both tasks through shared representations.

The improved reward model accuracy from the multi-headed architecture (Appendix F, Table 16) is also conceptually interesting: it suggests that training the RM head alongside the language modeling objective acts as a form of **representation regularization** — the model cannot overfit to the preference signal because it must simultaneously maintain high-quality next-token prediction. This is the opposite of standard RM training, where the model can specialize entirely to preference discrimination and potentially lose the broader language understanding that makes reward predictions generalizable. The paper doesn't make this regularization argument explicitly, but the consistent Hydra RM accuracy advantage across datasets (except Learning to Summarize, where accuracy is nearly identical: 69.36% vs. 69.31%) is evidence for it.

### Innovation 3: Adapter Sharing Between Actor and Critic Systematically Destabilizes PPO — A Diagnostic Negative Result

Negative results are rarely published, and when they are, they are rarely informative. The J-Hydra-PPO ablation — which tests whether the actor and critic can share a single set of LoRA adapters — stands out as a genuinely useful negative finding because it isolates **gradient interference** as a specific failure mode in PPO optimization, and because it empirically distinguishes the *architecture* decision (sharing base model parameters) from the *training dynamics* decision (sharing adapter parameters).

The natural next step after combining the SFT and RM into a single model is to ask whether the actor and critic also need independent parameters. They are both initialized from the same base, they process the same sequences, and their objectives — maximizing reward-penalized by KL vs. predicting expected returns — are conceptually complementary. It is reasonable to hypothesize (as the authors clearly did, given that they tested J-Hydra-PPO) that sharing adapters would provide additional memory savings with minimal performance cost.

The data reject this hypothesis decisively. J-Hydra-PPO achieves a 46.50% aggregate win-rate on Llama 7b (Table 3) — worse than SFT (48.55%), LoRA-PPO (50.68%), and Hydra-PPO (55.52%). On specific datasets, the gaps are stark: 40.38% vs. 55.38% on StackExchange, 43.13% vs. 61.58% on Learning to Summarize (Table 3). The paper also notes that J-Hydra-PPO is "highly unstable, taking multiple attempts to find solid hyperparameters" in contrast to Hydra-PPO, which "is generally as stable as LoRA-PPO" (Appendix B).

The conceptual value of this result is that it refutes the premise that the actor and critic are "complementary" in the shared-parameter sense. The two losses — the PPO clipped surrogate and the squared-error regression on returns — pull the shared parameters in directions that conflict, and this conflict is severe enough to cause training instability and degraded final performance even when the loss balance is carefully tuned (α ranges from 0.1 to 3 across datasets, reflecting how dataset-dependent this conflict is). The actor needs to increase the probability of high-advantage tokens; the critic needs to accurately predict the value of states, which may require reducing sensitivity to tokens that are spuriously correlated with reward. These are not just different objectives — they are actively competing for representational resources.

This finding has practical implications beyond Hydra-RLHF. It suggests that any approach that shares parameters between the policy and value function in PPO (a common design pattern in deep RL more broadly) should be viewed with skepticism for language model alignment. The instability is not a quirk of the Hydra architecture — it is a fundamental property of joint actor-critic optimization in the high-dimensional, high-variance setting of RLHF. The paper doesn't propose a solution to this interference problem (it simply avoids it by using separate adapters), but it clearly identifies it as a barrier that future work must address if further memory compression is desired.

The contrast between the Hydra architecture (which successfully combines language modeling and reward modeling) and the J-Hydra architecture (which fails to combine actor and critic training) is itself a useful conceptual distinction: **multi-task pretraining** (learning two output heads from static data) is fundamentally different from **multi-task reinforcement learning** (learning two heads from online, correlated, policy-dependent data). The former works at sufficient capacity; the latter fails even when capacity is abundant, because the problem is not representational — it is the dynamics of simultaneously optimizing a policy and its value estimate from the same rolling data distribution.

### Innovation 4: LoRA-PPO Produces Comparable or Better Alignment Than Full Fine-Tuning SFT — A Baseline That Challenges the Scaling Assumption

The final contribution is not a method but an **empirical baseline** with theoretical implications: LoRA-PPO consistently and substantially improves alignment over both full fine-tuning SFT (the standard supervised baseline) and LoRA-SFT (the parameter-efficient SFT baseline). This is visible across datasets and model sizes:

- Llama 7b: LoRA-PPO achieves 50.68% aggregate win-rate vs. SFT's 48.55% (Table 3), and in pairwise comparisons, LoRA-PPO wins against SFT on GPT-4-LLM (Table 5: 43.6% row win vs. 43.4% column win) and Learning to Summarize (Table 7: 31.6% vs. 44.8% — actually losing here, but see next point).
- OPT 1.3b: LoRA-PPO achieves 56.6% aggregate win-rate vs. SFT's 49.08% (Table 9), with dominant pairwise wins on GPT-4-LLM (Table 10: 41.2% row win vs. 44.0% column win — but aggregate favors LoRA-PPO due to wins against other methods).

This is significant because LoRA is often assumed (or feared) to underperform full fine-tuning for complex tasks — a concern the paper acknowledges (Appendix E: "While efficient, LoRA does not always match FFT-SFT performance") and that is supported by prior work (Sun et al., 2023; Pu et al., 2023). The fact that LoRA-PPO *surpasses* FFT-SFT suggests that the alignment gains from PPO are robust to the parameter-efficiency constraint — the policy can learn meaningful alignment behaviors through low-rank updates alone, without needing to modify the full 7 billion parameters.

The ROUGE results for Learning to Summarize (Table 4) add nuance to this finding. While LoRA-PPO wins on recall (14.70 vs. 13.12 for ROUGE-1 recall; 12.25 vs. 11.35 for ROUGE-L recall), it loses on precision (88.93 vs. 90.69 for ROUGE-1 precision; 71.46 vs. 75.56 for ROUGE-L precision). This tradeoff — PPO increases recall at the cost of precision — is attributed to PPO encouraging longer generations, and it demonstrates that alignment through RLHF changes *what* the model generates, not just *how well* it generates. The PPO objective rewards summaries that capture more of the source content (higher recall) even if they are less concise (lower precision), reflecting the preference patterns in the training data.

The conceptual implication is that PPO-based alignment should not be viewed as a uniform quality improvement over SFT — it is a **distribution shift** in the model's outputs toward the preferences expressed in the reward model's training data. When those preferences value conciseness, PPO will produce more concise outputs. When they value coverage, PPO will produce longer, more comprehensive outputs. The ROUGE precision/recall decomposition makes this shift visible and quantifiable in a way that aggregate win-rates obscure. It also serves as a warning: if the reward model encodes undesirable preferences (like "longer is better"), PPO will amplify them, which is exactly the reward hacking concern that the KL penalty is designed to prevent.

The practical significance is that LoRA-PPO represents a viable, lower-cost entry point to RLHF. If LoRA-based PPO can match or exceed the alignment quality of full fine-tuning SFT (let alone full fine-tuning PPO), then practitioners can achieve meaningful alignment gains without the extreme memory requirements of full PPO — even without adopting the full Hydra architecture. The paper's 68.0 GB at batch size 1 (Table 1) is still beyond consumer hardware, but it is dramatically below the estimated 220 GB for full fine-tuning PPO, making LoRA-PPO feasible on clusters of 4–8 A100s rather than requiring dozens. This finding — that PPO itself is more compressible than previously assumed — is a contribution that stands independently of the Hydra architecture.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on four public benchmarks: (1) **GPT-4-LLM** (Peng et al., 2023), consisting of instruction-following prompts with responses from GPT-4, GPT-3.5, and OPT-IML ranked by GPT-4 — the paper uses only pairs containing the highest-scoring response as the winning completion to satisfy Hydra-SFT's pairwise data requirement; (2) **Open-Source Assistant Datasets**, a combination of four HuggingFace-hosted ChatBot/Assistant preference datasets including Anthropic's Helpful & Harmless data, used without modification; (3) **Learning to Summarize**, the Reddit TL;DR dataset (Völske et al., 2017) as modified by Stiennon et al. (2022), where each prompt contains exactly one preference completion pair; and (4) **StackExchange** (Lambert et al., 2023), consisting of questions with multiple completions ranked by user votes, with the modification that only the best answer is paired with up to 3 other answers. Evaluation uses 500 samples from the validation set of each dataset (Appendix C).

- **Base model(s).** Primary experiments use **Llama 7b** (Touvron et al., 2023), a 7-billion-parameter decoder-only transformer. Extension experiments use **OPT 1.3b** (Zhang et al., 2022) to test generalization across model families and scales. Llama 7b is chosen because it "is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" and sits at a scale where full fine-tuning PPO is infeasible (estimated 220 GB per GPU at batch size 1, Table 1), making memory efficiency a practical necessity rather than a theoretical exercise. OPT 1.3b is chosen to probe whether the multi-headed architecture's benefits are capacity-dependent.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **GPT-4-evaluated win-rate** on a 0–9 integer scale. Each model output is graded three times at temperature 1 with step-by-step reasoning, scores are averaged, and pairwise win-rates are computed (fraction of evaluation samples where method A's average score exceeds method B's, with ties counted as half a win for each side). The "expected aggregate win-rate" (Tables 3, 9) summarizes each method's total wins against all other methods divided by total comparisons. For Learning to Summarize, **ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L** precision, recall, and F-measure (Lin, 2004) supplement the GPT-4 evaluation to capture surface-level summary quality. Appendix D additionally reports **reward model win-rates** (how often the trained RM prefers the PPO model's outputs over the base model's outputs) as a measure of optimization success against the learned reward signal.

- **Baselines.** Five methods are compared: (1) **SFT** — full fine-tuning supervised fine-tuning on the winning completions from the preference data, representing the standard pre-alignment baseline; (2) **LoRA-PPO** — standard PPO where the actor and critic are trained with LoRA (rank 128 on all linear layers) while the reference and reward models remain as separate frozen copies in memory, representing the state-of-the-art memory-efficient PPO baseline; (3) **Hydra-SFT** — the multi-headed model trained with the joint language modeling + reward modeling objective (γ = 0.1), representing the base model for the Hydra-PPO pipeline; (4) **J-Hydra-PPO** — Joined-Hydra-PPO, using a single set of shared LoRA adapters for both actor and critic, representing the maximum-compression ablation; (5) **Hydra-PPO** — the full method with separate actor and critic LoRA adapters on the multi-headed base model with Dynamic LoRA switching. All baselines within a model family (Llama 7b or OPT 1.3b) share the same pretrained checkpoint before Stage 1 training. An additional LoRA-SFT baseline appears in Appendix E (Table 15) for comparison against LoRA-PPO, but this is not part of the main five-method comparison.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures computational cost in three dimensions: (1) **GPU memory usage** (GB), broken down into model memory (parameters + optimizer states) and activation memory, measured via PyTorch during a training run on StackExchange with 800-token total sequence length (Table 1); (2) **latency per sample** (seconds), decomposed into inference latency (generation + forward passes) and update latency (gradient computation + parameter updates); and (3) **throughput** as a function of sequence length (Figure 2), where latency per sample is plotted for sequence lengths 256, 512, 1024, and 2048 at the maximum batch size that fits in memory for each method. All latency measurements are taken on an 8×A100 80GB node. The paper does not measure FLOPs directly but uses wall-clock latency and memory consumption as the practically relevant cost metrics, arguing that memory constraints — not raw FLOPs — are the binding constraint that prevents practitioners from running PPO.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not use cross-validation for hyperparameter selection in the conventional sense. Instead, it uses a held-out validation set within each dataset to select model checkpoints: "We track the mean of the reward from the last 20 steps, and use the model with the best reward throughout training" (Appendix B). This means the final model evaluated on the test set is the PPO checkpoint with the highest smoothed reward on the validation portion of training, not necessarily the model at the end of training. This introduces a subtle oracle selection: the model is chosen based on a metric (reward) that correlates with but is not identical to the evaluation metric (GPT-4 win-rate), partially addressing but not fully eliminating the risk of overfitting to the validation reward signal. No statistical significance tests or confidence intervals are reported for the win-rate comparisons. The 500-sample evaluation per dataset means each pairwise win-rate comparison has an effective sample size of 500, but the paper does not compute standard errors or perform hypothesis tests on the differences between methods.

### Main Quantitative Results

Organized by the paper's primary axes of investigation: (1) aggregate alignment quality across all methods, (2) per-dataset pairwise win-rates, (3) ROUGE-based evaluation for summarization, (4) memory usage and latency, (5) latency scaling with sequence length, (6) reward model optimization evidence, and (7) model size generalization to OPT 1.3b.

#### Aggregate Alignment Quality: Hydra-PPO Outperforms All Methods on Llama 7b

Table 3 reports the expected aggregate win-rate for each method against all other methods on Llama 7b, averaged across the four datasets. The headline results:

- **Hydra-PPO: 55.52%** — the highest aggregate win-rate, meaning this method is expected to win against a randomly chosen opponent from the set of all five methods more than half the time.
- **LoRA-PPO: 50.68%** — second-highest, showing a clear improvement over SFT.
- **Hydra-SFT: 48.50%** — statistically indistinguishable from SFT (48.55%), confirming that the multi-headed architecture does not degrade base model quality at this scale.
- **SFT: 48.55%** — the supervised baseline.
- **J-Hydra-PPO: 46.50%** — the worst-performing method, worse even than SFT, confirming that sharing LoRA adapters between actor and critic degrades alignment quality.

The gap between Hydra-PPO (55.52%) and LoRA-PPO (50.68%) is approximately 4.84 percentage points in aggregate win-rate, which is a substantial effect size. This means Hydra-PPO not only reduces memory usage but also produces better-aligned models on average across datasets. The paper attributes this improvement to the Hydra-SFT reward model being more accurate than the separately-trained reward model (Table 16: Hydra RM accuracy exceeds standard RM accuracy on 3 of 4 datasets for Llama 7b), providing a better reward signal for PPO optimization. However, Table 3 is an average across four datasets with different characteristics, and the per-dataset breakdowns (Tables 5–8) reveal that Hydra-PPO's advantage is not uniform.

#### Per-Dataset Pairwise Win-Rates: Hydra-PPO Dominates on Summarization, Mixed on Others

Tables 5–8 provide the full pairwise win-rate matrices for each Llama 7b method on each dataset, as judged by GPT-4. Each cell shows "Row Win % / Column Win %" with the remainder as ties.

**GPT-4-LLM (Table 5).** Hydra-PPO achieves the highest win-rate against SFT (39.0% row win vs. 47.0% column win — note that Hydra-PPO actually *loses* to SFT in this pairwise, with 47.0% of evaluations favoring SFT and 39.0% favoring Hydra-PPO, the remainder being ties). The largest pairwise advantage for Hydra-PPO is against Hydra-SFT (38.8% vs. 49.2% in Hydra-PPO's favor as the row method). Against LoRA-PPO, Hydra-PPO wins 47.6% vs. 40.0% as the row method. The aggregate win-rate for Hydra-PPO on this dataset is 54.13% (Table 3), but the pairwise breakdown shows it is not uniformly dominant — it loses to SFT head-to-head and has close matchups with LoRA-PPO.

**Open-Source Assistant (Table 6).** Hydra-PPO's aggregate win-rate is 51.00% (Table 3), a narrower advantage. Against SFT, Hydra-PPO wins 45.4% vs. 39.4% as the row method. Against LoRA-PPO, the matchup is nearly even: 42.8% vs. 42.6%. Hydra-PPO's largest advantage is against Hydra-SFT (38.6% vs. 43.6% as row). J-Hydra-PPO notably wins against Hydra-PPO in this dataset: 45.6% vs. 42.4% (J-Hydra-PPO as row, Hydra-PPO as column) — the only dataset where J-Hydra-PPO is competitive. The paper does not explain this anomaly, but the Open-Source Assistant dataset's heterogeneous composition (four different datasets combined) may introduce variability that favors different architectural choices.

**Learning to Summarize (Table 7).** This is Hydra-PPO's strongest dataset. Hydra-PPO achieves a 61.58% aggregate win-rate (Table 3), the highest for any method on any dataset. Against SFT, Hydra-PPO wins decisively: 31.4% vs. 47.2% as row (Hydra-PPO as column wins 47.2% of the time). Against LoRA-PPO, Hydra-PPO wins 36.8% vs. 42.6% as row. Against J-Hydra-PPO, the dominance is stark: 23.7% vs. 52.6% as row (J-Hydra-PPO wins only 23.7% of comparisons against Hydra-PPO). The summarization task, which has a clear objective quality signal (factual accuracy, conciseness, coverage), appears to benefit most strongly from the Hydra architecture's improved reward model.

**StackExchange (Table 8).** Hydra-PPO achieves a 55.38% aggregate win-rate (Table 3). The pairwise against SFT is close: 42.4% vs. 48.6% as row. Against LoRA-PPO, Hydra-PPO wins: 36.8% vs. 51.8% as row (Hydra-PPO as column wins 51.8%). Against J-Hydra-PPO, Hydra-PPO wins: 35.2% vs. 56.6% as row. The paper notes that StackExchange is "the most difficult dataset we test, containing extremely diverse and specific questions" and that "during PPO, models often learn to repeat their answers" (Section 4.4) — a form of reward hacking where repetition receives spuriously high reward scores. Hydra-PPO notably avoids this repetition issue while both standard PPO and J-Hydra-PPO encounter it, suggesting that the Hydra architecture's reward model is less susceptible to this particular failure mode.

**Key pattern across datasets.** Hydra-PPO's advantage is largest on Learning to Summarize (61.58%) and smallest on Open-Source Assistant (51.00%). The variability across datasets — from a 3.87 percentage point aggregate advantage over LoRA-PPO on GPT-4-LLM to a 6.10 point advantage on Learning to Summarize (computed from Table 3 per-dataset values) — indicates that the benefits of the Hydra architecture are task-dependent. The paper does not systematically analyze which task characteristics (e.g., output length, diversity, presence of clear correctness criteria) predict Hydra-PPO's relative performance, leaving this as an open question for practitioners porting the method to new domains.

#### ROUGE Evaluation: PPO Shifts the Precision-Recall Tradeoff Toward Recall

Table 4 reports ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L scores for all Llama 7b methods on the Learning to Summarize dataset. The key findings:

- **ROUGE-1 F-measure:** Hydra-PPO achieves 29.31, substantially outperforming SFT (21.69), LoRA-PPO (23.95), and Hydra-SFT (21.42). J-Hydra-PPO scores 25.00, beating LoRA-PPO on this metric despite its poor GPT-4 win-rate (43.13% aggregate on this dataset, Table 3). This discrepancy between ROUGE (surface-level n-gram overlap) and GPT-4 evaluation (semantic quality) illustrates that these metrics capture different aspects of summary quality.

- **ROUGE-L F-measure:** Hydra-PPO achieves 24.73 vs. SFT's 18.59 and LoRA-PPO's 19.77. The same ranking holds: Hydra-PPO > J-Hydra-PPO > LoRA-PPO > SFT ≈ Hydra-SFT.

- **Precision vs. Recall tradeoff:** SFT achieves the best ROUGE-1 precision (90.69) and ROUGE-L precision (75.56), but the worst recall (13.12 ROUGE-1 recall, 11.35 ROUGE-L recall). Hydra-PPO achieves the best recall (19.21 ROUGE-1, 16.43 ROUGE-L) but lower precision (88.91 and 72.45, respectively). This pattern — PPO methods consistently increase recall at the cost of precision — is observed across all PPO variants. The paper's interpretation: "This trend could potentially be attributed to the encouragement of longer text generation during the PPO stage." Longer summaries naturally capture more of the reference content (higher recall) but include more extraneous tokens (lower precision). The reward model, trained on human preferences for the Reddit TL;DR dataset, appears to value comprehensive coverage over conciseness, and PPO amplifies this preference.

- **Hydra-SFT vs. SFT:** Hydra-SFT (21.42 ROUGE-1 F1, 18.27 ROUGE-L F1) is slightly worse than SFT (21.69 and 18.59), consistent with the aggregate win-rate comparison (48.50% vs. 48.55% in Table 3) — the joint training does not harm summary quality but doesn't improve it either at this scale.

- **J-Hydra-PPO anomaly:** J-Hydra-PPO achieves higher ROUGE scores (25.00 ROUGE-1 F1, 21.81 ROUGE-L F1) than LoRA-PPO (23.95 and 19.77) despite its much worse GPT-4 win-rate (43.13% vs. 55.48% aggregate on this dataset). This reinforces that n-gram overlap metrics do not capture the semantic quality dimensions that GPT-4 evaluation (and human preference) values — J-Hydra-PPO's summaries may have high lexical overlap with references while being less coherent or less faithful to the source, which GPT-4 penalizes but ROUGE does not detect.

#### Memory Usage and Latency: Hydra-PPO Processes 4× the Batch Size at Similar Total Memory

Table 1 provides the core systems comparison for Llama 7b on StackExchange with 800-token total sequence length, measured on a single A100 80GB GPU:

| Method | Batch Size | Model Memory (GB) | Activation Memory (GB) | Total GPU Memory (GB) | Inference Latency (s/sample) | Update Latency (s/sample) | Total Latency (s/sample) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PPO (full FT, est.) | 1 | 111.8 | 101.3 | ~220 | — | — | — |
| LoRA-PPO | 1 | 53.2 | 12.5 | 68.0 | 17.23 | 1.52 | 18.75 |
| J-Hydra-PPO | 4 | 14.3 | 51.4 | 67.9 | 4.63 | 0.38 | 5.01 |
| Hydra-PPO | 4 | 15.9 | 52.8 | 71.1 | 4.88 | 1.59 | 6.47 |

**Memory analysis.** The critical comparison is between LoRA-PPO and Hydra-PPO:

- **Model memory** drops from 53.2 GB to 15.9 GB — a 3.3× reduction. This is the direct consequence of loading one base model instead of four. The remaining 15.9 GB includes: the frozen Hydra base model parameters (~7B parameters at 2 bytes each in FP16 ≈ 14 GB), the actor LoRA adapters (rank 128 on all linear layers, estimated ~100–200M parameters plus Adam optimizer states at ~8 bytes per parameter ≈ 1–2 GB), and the critic LoRA adapters (same size). The 15.9 GB figure is within what a single A100 80GB can handle with substantial room for activations.

- **Activation memory** increases from 12.5 GB to 52.8 GB — a 4.2× increase. This is the direct consequence of increasing the batch size from 1 to 4. Activation memory scales roughly linearly with batch size for transformer models (each sample requires storing intermediate activations for the backward pass during the update phase).

- **Total memory** is similar: 68.0 GB (LoRA-PPO) vs. 71.1 GB (Hydra-PPO). The memory savings from model sharing are fully reinvested into batch size, keeping total memory utilization near the 80 GB GPU limit.

**Latency analysis.** The per-sample latency breaks down into:

- **Inference latency** drops from 17.23 s/sample (LoRA-PPO) to 4.88 s/sample (Hydra-PPO) — a 3.5× reduction. The generation phase processes 4 samples in parallel on each GPU, dividing the forward-pass cost approximately evenly. The reduction is not exactly 4× because generation is memory-bandwidth-bound rather than compute-bound — 4× the batch size increases memory traffic, and there is fixed overhead from kernel launches and communication that doesn't scale with batch size.

- **Update latency** remains similar: 1.52 s/sample (LoRA-PPO) vs. 1.59 s/sample (Hydra-PPO). The update phase processes the same total number of tokens per optimizer step (the effective batch size is kept constant via gradient accumulation), so the computational work is comparable. Hydra-PPO's update latency is marginally higher (1.59 vs. 1.52), likely due to the additional overhead of managing multiple LoRA adapter sets and the multi-headed architecture's slightly more complex backward pass.

- **Total latency** drops from 18.75 s/sample to 6.47 s/sample — a 65% reduction, corresponding to a 2.9× speedup in samples per second. This is the headline "up to 65% faster per-sample latency" claim from the abstract.

**J-Hydra-PPO latency advantage.** With only one set of LoRA adapters, J-Hydra-PPO achieves even lower latency: 5.01 s/sample total, with both inference (4.63 s) and update (0.38 s) being faster than Hydra-PPO. The update latency is particularly striking — 0.38 s vs. 1.59 s, a 4.2× reduction — because only one set of adapter gradients needs to be computed instead of two. However, this latency advantage comes at the cost of alignment quality (46.50% aggregate win-rate vs. 55.52% for Hydra-PPO, Table 3), making it a poor tradeoff in practice.

**Full fine-tuning PPO estimate.** The paper cannot run full fine-tuning PPO on Llama 7b directly because it overflows memory. The 220 GB estimate is obtained by running full PPO on OPT 1.3b and scaling up by the ratio of LoRA-PPO memory between Llama 7b and OPT 1.3b (Appendix B): "Because Full PPO overflows our setup, we repeat the experiment using OPT 1.3b, and use the ratio between LoRA-PPO on Llama 7b to scale the numbers up." This is a rough estimate — it assumes that the memory ratio between full PPO and LoRA-PPO is constant across model scales, which is approximately true if LoRA reduces trainable parameters by a consistent factor regardless of model size. The 220 GB figure should be interpreted as an order-of-magnitude estimate rather than a precise measurement.

#### Latency Scaling with Sequence Length: Hydra-PPO's Advantage Grows with Context Length

Figure 2 plots latency per sample (log scale) against total sequence length (256, 512, 1024, 2048 tokens, also log scale) for LoRA-PPO, Hydra-PPO, and J-Hydra-PPO on Llama 7b. The paper does not provide exact numerical values for each point, but the trends are visible from the log-log plot:

- **All methods show roughly linear scaling in log-space**, meaning latency grows as a power law of sequence length. This is expected: transformer self-attention has quadratic complexity in sequence length, and both generation (autoregressive decoding) and forward/backward passes scale with sequence length.

- **Hydra-PPO and J-Hydra-PPO are consistently faster than LoRA-PPO at all sequence lengths.** At sequence length 256, the gap is modest — all methods are within roughly a factor of 2. At sequence length 1024, the gap widens substantially: LoRA-PPO is above 15 seconds per sample while Hydra-PPO and J-Hydra-PPO converge to approximately 6–7 seconds per sample. The paper states: "Hydra-PPO saves exponentially more time as sequence length increases" — this is visible as the widening vertical gap between the LoRA-PPO line and the Hydra-PPO/J-Hydra-PPO lines on the log-log plot, indicating a multiplicative rather than additive advantage.

- **At sequence length 2048, LoRA-PPO cannot fit in memory** on the authors' 8×A100 80GB setup. The LoRA-PPO line in Figure 2 terminates between 1024 and 2048. In contrast, both Hydra-PPO and J-Hydra-PPO continue to operate at sequence length 2048, with J-Hydra-PPO and Hydra-PPO converging to similar latency (around 10–15 seconds per sample, based on extrapolation from the plot's trend). This is the practical consequence of memory savings: longer sequences require more activation memory, and LoRA-PPO's model memory overhead leaves insufficient room for activations at long context lengths, while Hydra-PPO's reduced model memory can accommodate the larger activation footprint.

- **J-Hydra-PPO and Hydra-PPO converge at sequence length 1024.** The paper notes: "Hydra-PPO and J-Hydra-PPO converge at sequence length 1024 as the inference increase overtakes update latency." At short sequence lengths, J-Hydra-PPO's faster update phase (0.38 s vs. 1.59 s per sample, Table 1) gives it a latency advantage. As sequence length increases, the inference phase (generation + forward passes, which scales with sequence length) dominates the total latency, and the update phase becomes a negligible fraction. Since both methods process the same batch size (4), their inference latencies converge, and the total latencies converge as well.

**Interpretation.** Figure 2 demonstrates that Hydra-PPO's advantage is not a fixed speedup factor but scales with task complexity. For short-generation tasks (256 tokens), the advantage is modest (~2×). For long-generation tasks (1024+ tokens), the advantage is large (2–3×) and in some regimes represents the difference between feasible and infeasible (at 2048 tokens, only Hydra variants can run at all). This has practical implications for practitioners: if your use case involves long-form generation (summarization of long documents, multi-turn dialogue, chain-of-thought reasoning), the memory savings from Hydra-PPO are not just a convenience but a necessity for fitting PPO within typical GPU memory budgets.

#### Reward Model Optimization Evidence: PPO Methods Successfully Optimize Against Their Respective Reward Models

Appendix D, Table 14 reports win-rates of each PPO method against its respective base model as judged by the reward model used during training. Specifically:
- LoRA-PPO vs. FFT-SFT, judged by the separately-trained RM.
- J-Hydra-PPO vs. Hydra-SFT, judged by the Hydra RM head.
- Hydra-PPO vs. Hydra-SFT, judged by the Hydra RM head.

The results show that all PPO methods overwhelmingly optimize their actors toward their reward models:

| Dataset | Model | LoRA-PPO vs. SFT | J-Hydra-PPO vs. Hydra-SFT | Hydra-PPO vs. Hydra-SFT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-4-LLM | Llama 7b | 82.6% PPO wins | 62.2% PPO wins | 69.2% PPO wins |
| Open-Source Asst. | Llama 7b | 14.8% PPO wins | 40.2% PPO wins | 64.4% PPO wins |
| Learning to Sum. | Llama 7b | 53.8% PPO wins | 31.0% PPO wins | 40.8% PPO wins |
| StackExchange | Llama 7b | 89.8% PPO wins | 45.6% PPO wins | 89.0% PPO wins |
| GPT-4-LLM | OPT 1.3b | 70.2% PPO wins | 79.6% PPO wins | 86.6% PPO wins |
| Open-Source Asst. | OPT 1.3b | 78.6% PPO wins | 41.2% PPO wins | 40.8% PPO wins |

**Key findings:**

- **LoRA-PPO achieves high RM win-rates on most datasets** (82.6% on GPT-4-LLM, 89.8% on StackExchange), confirming that standard PPO with LoRA successfully optimizes the policy against the learned reward signal. The exception is Open-Source Assistant at 14.8% — this means the RM actually *prefers* the SFT model's outputs over the LoRA-PPO outputs 85.2% of the time. This is a striking case of PPO failing to optimize the reward despite the RM being trained on the same data distribution. The paper does not explain this anomaly, but possible causes include severe reward over-optimization (the PPO model finds outputs that score highly on the RM during training but look worse to the RM when evaluated independently), distribution shift between the RM's preference judgments during PPO (on policy-generated text) and during evaluation (on SFT-generated text), or unusual characteristics of the Open-Source Assistant dataset mixture.

- **Hydra-PPO achieves higher RM win-rates than J-Hydra-PPO** on Open-Source Assistant (64.4% vs. 40.2%) and GPT-4-LLM (69.2% vs. 62.2%) for Llama 7b, consistent with Hydra-PPO's better GPT-4 evaluation scores. On StackExchange, both Hydra-PPO (89.0%) and LoRA-PPO (89.8%) achieve very high RM win-rates, suggesting that the RM signal is easier to optimize on this dataset, but GPT-4 evaluation (Table 8) reveals that LoRA-PPO's high RM optimization comes with degraded generation quality (the repetition problem), while Hydra-PPO achieves high RM scores without this degradation.

- **J-Hydra-PPO shows variable RM optimization success.** On GPT-4-LLM (62.2%) and StackExchange (45.6%), it achieves moderate RM win-rates, but on Learning to Summarize (31.0%), it is actively worse than the base Hydra-SFT model — the RM prefers Hydra-SFT outputs 69.0% of the time. This is consistent with the instability noted in Appendix B ("J-Hydra-PPO [is] highly unstable") and the poor aggregate win-rate (46.50%, Table 3). The shared actor-critic LoRA adapters apparently struggle to simultaneously optimize the policy and value function, leading to degraded policy performance that even the in-distribution RM can detect.

- **For OPT 1.3b on Open-Source Assistant**, both J-Hydra-PPO (41.2%) and Hydra-PPO (40.8%) achieve low RM win-rates, meaning the RM prefers the base Hydra-SFT outputs over the PPO-optimized outputs in approximately 60% of comparisons. This is consistent with the finding that Hydra-PPO underperforms LoRA-PPO for OPT 1.3b (Table 9: 52.33% vs. 56.6% aggregate win-rate) — when the reward model itself indicates that PPO optimization is failing to improve the base model, GPT-4 evaluation will likely agree.

**Interpretation.** Table 14 serves as a manipulation check: it verifies that PPO is actually doing what it is designed to do (optimize the policy against the reward signal). When the RM win-rate is high but GPT-4 win-rate is low (as with LoRA-PPO on StackExchange, where RM win-rate is 89.8% but aggregate GPT-4 win-rate is only 49.4%), this indicates **reward over-optimization** — the policy exploits the RM's blind spots, producing outputs that the RM scores highly but that GPT-4 (and presumably humans) find lacking. When the RM win-rate is low (as with LoRA-PPO on Open-Source Assistant, 14.8%), this indicates fundamental PPO training failure on that dataset. Hydra-PPO achieves high RM win-rates (64–89% on 3 of 4 Llama 7b datasets) without the over-optimization symptoms seen in LoRA-PPO, suggesting that the Hydra architecture's reward model provides a more robust optimization signal.

#### Model Size and Family Generalization: OPT 1.3b Results

Table 9 reports the expected aggregate win-rates for OPT 1.3b across two datasets (GPT-4-LLM and Open-Source Assistant; Learning to Summarize and StackExchange are not tested at this scale). The results reveal a different performance ordering than Llama 7b:

| Method | GPT-4-LLM | Open-Source Asst. | Average |
|---|---|---|---|
| SFT | 45.65 | 52.5 | 49.08 |
| LoRA-PPO | 59.5 | 53.7 | 56.6 |
| Hydra-SFT | 44.4 | 42.58 | 43.49 |
| J-Hydra-PPO | 48.2 | 46.78 | 47.49 |
| Hydra-PPO | 50.2 | 54.45 | 52.33 |

**Key differences from Llama 7b results:**

- **LoRA-PPO is the best method** with 56.6% aggregate, surpassing Hydra-PPO (52.33%). This is the opposite of the Llama 7b result where Hydra-PPO wins. The paper speculates: "We speculate this difference to be due to the capacity of the model. For the smaller 1.3b model, combining language and reward models may be more difficult."

- **Hydra-SFT underperforms SFT substantially** (43.49% vs. 49.08% aggregate), whereas on Llama 7b they were comparable (48.50% vs. 48.55%). This confirms the capacity hypothesis: at 1.3B parameters, the joint language modeling and reward modeling objectives compete for limited representational resources, degrading the base model's generation quality by approximately 5.59 percentage points in aggregate win-rate. The Hydra-SFT model is a worse starting point for PPO, which partially explains Hydra-PPO's underperformance relative to LoRA-PPO (which starts from the stronger standard SFT model).

- **Hydra-PPO still improves over Hydra-SFT** (52.33% vs. 43.49%), demonstrating that PPO optimization works even when starting from a degraded base model — the alignment gains from PPO (+8.84 percentage points) compensate for some of the Hydra-SFT quality loss. However, the final Hydra-PPO model still trails LoRA-PPO (52.33% vs. 56.6%) because the initial quality gap is larger than what PPO can recover.

- **J-Hydra-PPO is no longer the worst method** on OPT 1.3b — it achieves 47.49% aggregate, beating Hydra-SFT (43.49%) and nearly matching SFT (49.08%). On GPT-4-LLM (Table 10), J-Hydra-PPO achieves 48.2% vs. LoRA-PPO's 59.5% and Hydra-PPO's 50.2%. The pairwise matrices (Tables 10–11) show that J-Hydra-PPO is competitive with SFT on GPT-4-LLM (52.6% row win vs. 39.4% column win) but loses to LoRA-PPO (50.8% row win vs. 41.2% column win). The shared-adapter architecture appears less harmful at smaller model scales — possibly because the smaller model has less capacity to support conflicting gradient directions, so the interference effect is proportionally smaller. This is speculative; the paper does not provide a mechanism.

**Pairwise details for OPT 1.3b (Tables 10–11).** On GPT-4-LLM (Table 10), LoRA-PPO dominates most matchups: it wins against Hydra-FFT (53.4% column win), against J-Hydra-PPO (41.2% row win vs. 50.8% column win for LoRA-PPO), and is essentially even with Hydra-PPO (46.2% vs. 46.6%). SFT is competitive with LoRA-PPO (41.2% vs. 44.0%). On Open-Source Assistant (Table 11), LoRA-PPO shows strong wins against Hydra-SFT (53.0% column win), J-Hydra-PPO (53.0% row win vs. 35.8% column win), and Hydra-PPO (52.0% row win vs. 33.6% column win). The dominance of LoRA-PPO over the Hydra variants is consistent across datasets at this model scale.

**Interpretation.** The OPT 1.3b results establish a clear boundary condition for Hydra-RLHF: the method is beneficial when model capacity is sufficient to support the multi-headed architecture without degrading generation quality (≥7B parameters for Llama-family models), but harmful when capacity is insufficient (<7B parameters, with the exact threshold lying somewhere between 1.3B and 7B). This capacity cliff is the paper's most important negative finding, and it transforms Hydra-RLHF from a universally-applicable technique into a scale-dependent one. Practitioners considering Hydra-RLHF for models below 7B parameters should first validate that Hydra-SFT does not substantially degrade generation quality relative to standard SFT on their domain.

This is a cross-comparison: the base model for LoRA-PPO is FFT-SFT (the standard approach), while LoRA-SFT is evaluated directly as a generation model. The results:

| Dataset | Model | LoRA-SFT Wins / LoRA-PPO Wins |
|---|---|---|
| GPT-4-LLM | OPT 1.3b | 42.0 / 45.2 |
| Open-Source Asst. | OPT 1.3b | 29.2 / 61.0 |

This is presented as a direction for future work rather than a solved problem.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Hydra-SFT reward head multiplier γ:** The paper sweeps γ values during Hydra-SFT training and reports that γ = 0.1 "generally works well" (Section 3), with γ = 0.07 used for StackExchange due to the dataset's tendency toward length-biased reward models. No formal ablation table is provided; the choice is described as empirically determined. The sensitivity of results to γ is not quantified — we do not know whether γ = 0.05 or γ = 0.2 would produce substantially different Hydra-SFT quality or downstream PPO performance.

**Joined-Hydra-PPO critic loss multiplier α:** Tables 12 and 13 show that α (the weight on the critic's squared-error loss when sharing LoRA adapters with the actor) varies dramatically across datasets — from 0.1 on GPT-4-LLM and Open-Source Assistant to 3 on Learning to Summarize and StackExchange. The paper does not provide an ablation study showing how J-Hydra-PPO performance varies with α, making it unclear whether the reported α values are optimal or even near-optimal. The wide range (0.1 to 3, a 30× difference) and the dataset-specific tuning suggest that J-Hydra-PPO is highly sensitive to this hyperparameter, which is consistent with the reported instability but makes the method difficult to deploy without extensive tuning.

**Dynamic LoRA as a standalone technique:** The paper claims that applying Dynamic LoRA to standard LoRA-PPO (without the multi-headed architecture) saves "about 20% of memory while maintaining performance equivalent to LoRA-PPO" (Section 3). However, the paper does not report a separate ablation comparing LoRA-PPO with and without Dynamic LoRA. The 20% figure appears to be an estimate based on the memory footprint of the reference and reward models relative to the total, but without an explicit "LoRA-PPO + Dynamic LoRA" experiment, we cannot verify that no subtle performance degradation occurs from the runtime model switching. The claim is plausible — the base model parameters are identical whether loaded once or twice — but the absence of a direct ablation is a gap.

**LoRA rank for actor and critic:** All experiments use rank 128 for both actor and critic LoRA adapters (Appendix B). The paper does not test alternative ranks (e.g., 64, 256) to determine whether the alignment quality or memory tradeoff is sensitive to this choice. Rank 128 is relatively high for LoRA (many applications use ranks 8–64), and the memory footprint of the adapters scales linearly with rank (the number of parameters in A and B matrices is r × (d_in + d_out) per layer). A rank ablation would establish whether the full rank-128 expressivity is necessary for PPO alignment or whether lower ranks could provide further memory savings with minimal quality loss.

**Hydra-SFT architecture choice — separate vs. shared heads:** The paper uses two completely separate linear heads (CLM and RM) on top of the shared decoder backbone. It does not test alternative architectures such as having the RM head share the CLM head's hidden representation (e.g., using the CLM head's pre-softmax logits as input to the RM head) or having the RM head operate on intermediate layer representations rather than the final hidden state. It is possible that different head architectures could reduce the capacity burden on smaller models or improve reward model accuracy at all scales.

**Reward model normalization range:** All RM heads are normalized to (-1, 1) (Appendix B). The paper does not test alternative normalization ranges (e.g., (0, 1) or unnormalized) or investigate whether Hydra-PPO's stability is sensitive to this choice. In standard RLHF, RM normalization is known to affect PPO stability (Ziegler et al., 2020), but whether the multi-headed architecture changes this sensitivity is unexplored.

**KL penalty coefficient β:** All PPO experiments use β = 0.02 (Appendix B). While this is standard in the RLHF literature, the paper does not test whether the Hydra architecture benefits from a different β. Since the Hydra reward model is trained jointly with the language model head and achieves different accuracy than the separately-trained RM (Table 16), the optimal KL penalty for balancing reward optimization against staying close to the reference model might differ.

**Evaluation protocol robustness — GPT-4 grading consistency:** The paper grades each output 3 times at temperature 1 and averages scores (Appendix C). It does not report inter-evaluation agreement metrics (e.g., standard deviation of the three scores, correlation between individual evaluations, or the fraction of samples where all three evaluations agree on the winner in a pairwise comparison). Without these statistics, we cannot assess how much of the reported win-rate differences are attributable to evaluation noise rather than genuine quality differences. For example, if a typical pairwise comparison involves a 51%–49% split in GPT-4 preferences but the evaluation noise is ±3%, the measured winner could easily reverse across evaluation runs. The paper's finding that score-based evaluation (0–9 scale) was more consistent than direct pairwise comparison ("we found that simply switching the order of the answers would switch the preferred answer by a large margin") is suggestive that evaluation stability is a concern, but the degree of remaining noise in the 3-sample averaged protocol is unquantified.

**Dataset preprocessing for StackExchange:** The paper modifies the StackExchange dataset by pairing only the best answer with up to 3 other answers, rather than forming all pairwise combinations. It also notes that "the most up-voted answers are on average longer than the other answers, leading to trivial reward models" (Section 4.4). The paper does not test whether this preprocessing strategy — capping at 3 losing answers and restricting to pairs containing the best answer — is sufficient to prevent the RM from learning a length-based heuristic. The Hydra-SFT RM achieves 64.20% accuracy on StackExchange (Table 16), which is only marginally better than the standard RM's 63.90%, suggesting that the length bias may still be influencing the learned reward signal. An ablation comparing different numbers of losing completions per question (1, 3, 5, all) and different pairing strategies would clarify whether the preprocessing choices affect downstream PPO quality.

**Cross-dataset generalization:** Each model is trained and evaluated on the same dataset. The paper does not test whether a Hydra-PPO model trained on one dataset (e.g., GPT-4-LLM) generalizes to other datasets (e.g., Open-Source Assistant) better or worse than a LoRA-PPO model trained on the same source dataset. Cross-dataset generalization is a practically important property — if Hydra-PPO's shared representations produce more robust alignment that transfers across domains, this would be an additional advantage beyond memory efficiency. This experiment is not attempted.

### Critical Assessment

The paper makes two central claims: (1) Hydra-RLHF reduces PPO memory usage below SFT levels while maintaining or improving alignment quality, and (2) the memory savings can be reinvested into larger batch sizes, reducing per-sample latency by up to 65%. The experimental evidence supports these claims for Llama 7b but reveals important boundary conditions that the paper's framing sometimes understates.

**On the memory reduction claim (Claim 1):** The evidence in Table 1 is direct and persuasive: Hydra-PPO uses 71.1 GB total at batch size 4 compared to LoRA-PPO's 68.0 GB at batch size 1. Model memory drops from 53.2 GB to 15.9 GB, a 3.3× reduction. However, the claim that Hydra-PPO's memory usage is "smaller than SFT" (from the abstract) requires clarification. The paper does not directly measure SFT memory usage in Table 1 — the comparison is to LoRA-PPO. SFT with full fine-tuning on Llama 7b would consume substantially less than 68 GB (no reference, reward, or critic models; no PPO-specific data structures). The claim appears to be based on the fact that Hydra-PPO's model memory (15.9 GB) is dominated by a single base model plus two small adapter sets, which is architecturally similar to SFT (one model). But the activation memory of Hydra-PPO at batch size 4 (52.8 GB) would exceed SFT activation memory at typical SFT batch sizes. The claim is true in spirit — the model parameter footprint is SFT-like — but misleading if read as "total GPU memory < SFT total GPU memory at the same batch size," which the paper does not and cannot demonstrate because it maxes out memory for Hydra-PPO while LoRA-PPO is constrained to batch size 1.

**On the alignment quality claim (Claim 2):** Hydra-PPO's 55.52% aggregate win-rate on Llama 7b (Table 3) convincingly outperforms LoRA-PPO's 50.68%. This is the paper's strongest result because it demonstrates that memory savings need not come at the cost of alignment — in fact, they can coincide with alignment improvements. However, the per-dataset breakdowns (Tables 5–8) reveal substantial variance. Hydra-PPO's advantage is concentrated on Learning to Summarize (61.58% vs. 55.48% for LoRA-PPO, a 6.1-point gap) and StackExchange (55.38% vs. 49.4%, a 5.98-point gap). On GPT-4-LLM, the gap is smaller (54.13% vs. 48.8%, a 5.33-point gap). On Open-Source Assistant, the gap is negligible (51.00% vs. 49.03%, a 1.97-point gap that may be within evaluation noise given the unquantified GPT-4 grading variance). A practitioner working primarily with assistant-style datasets (the most common RLHF use case) might find that Hydra-PPO offers little to no alignment benefit over LoRA-PPO, though the memory savings would still justify its use.

The paper's explanation for Hydra-PPO's alignment advantage — that Hydra-SFT produces a better reward model (Table 16) — is partially supported. The Hydra RM outperforms the standard RM on GPT-4-LLM (95.37% vs. 93.50%), Open-Source Assistant (85.51% vs. 76.75%), and StackExchange (64.20% vs. 63.90%), but is essentially tied on Learning to Summarize (69.36% vs. 69.31%). The largest RM accuracy gap is on Open-Source Assistant (+8.76 points), yet this is precisely the dataset where Hydra-PPO's GPT-4 win-rate advantage over LoRA-PPO is smallest (+1.97 points). The relationship between RM accuracy and downstream PPO alignment quality is not monotonic, suggesting that other factors — perhaps the nature of the reward signal (smooth vs. brittle), the KL penalty's effectiveness at preventing over-optimization, or the Hydra architecture's inherent regularization — also contribute.

**On the latency claim (Claim 3):** The 65% per-sample latency reduction (from 18.75 s to 6.47 s, Table 1) is well-supported for the specific configuration tested (Llama 7b, StackExchange, 800-token sequences, batch size 4 vs. batch size 1). However, this is a comparison between two configurations that are not on equal footing in one respect: LoRA-PPO at batch size 1 has a total batch size of 8 (8 GPUs × 1 sample × gradient accumulation steps = 8), while Hydra-PPO at batch size 4 has a total batch size of 32 (8 GPUs × 4 samples × gradient accumulation steps = 32, or possibly adjusted via gradient accumulation to match). The paper states that gradient accumulation is used to "ensure the effective total batch size is the same" (Section 4, Throughput Comparison paragraph), which means Hydra-PPO's larger per-GPU batch is compensated by fewer gradient accumulation steps, keeping the optimizer's effective batch size constant across methods. With this control, the latency comparison is fair: both methods process the same total amount of data per optimizer step, but Hydra-PPO does so with fewer sequential forward/backward passes because each pass processes more samples in parallel.

The latency scaling result (Figure 2) is robust and practically important: at sequence length 2048, LoRA-PPO cannot run at all on the authors' hardware, while Hydra-PPO can. This is a qualitative advantage (feasibility vs. infeasibility) that matters more than any quantitative speedup factor.

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

1. **Single evaluation protocol dependency.** All alignment quality claims rest on GPT-4 evaluation with a specific prompting strategy (0–9 scale, 3-sample averaging). While GPT-4 evaluation is common in the RLHF literature, it introduces unknown biases — GPT-4 may prefer certain stylistic features (e.g., longer responses, certain politeness formulas) that do not reflect genuine human preference. The paper does not validate GPT-4's judgments against human evaluations on any dataset, nor does it report inter-evaluator agreement statistics for the 3-sample averaging protocol. If GPT-4's evaluation noise is comparable to the win-rate differences between methods (2–6 percentage points on several comparisons), the claimed ranking of methods could be unreliable.

2. **No comparison to non-PPO alignment methods.** The paper compares Hydra-PPO only to other PPO variants and SFT. It does not compare against Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023) or other PPO-alternative methods despite acknowledging them in the Related Work section. This is defensible given the paper's stated goal (improving PPO, not replacing it), but it means the reader cannot assess whether the alignment quality achieved by Hydra-PPO (55.52% aggregate win-rate) is competitive with simpler, more memory-efficient methods that bypass PPO entirely. If DPO achieves comparable alignment with the memory footprint of SFT, then Hydra-PPO's memory savings, while real, may be solving a problem that the field is already moving past.

3. **Single model family for the main results.** All Llama 7b experiments use the same pretrained model. The OPT 1.3b extension is valuable but tests a different scale, not a different architecture at the same scale. We do not know whether Hydra-RLHF works on other 7B-class models (e.g., Mistral 7B, Falcon 7B, MPT 7B) or whether the capacity cliff is specific to the OPT architecture versus a general property of model scale. The paper's claim that Llama 7b "is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4) is asserted, not demonstrated.

4. **No human evaluation.** GPT-4 evaluation is used throughout as a proxy for human preference. The paper does not conduct any human evaluation to validate that GPT-4's judgments align with human preferences on these specific datasets and model outputs. This is a pragmatic choice (human evaluation is expensive) but weakens the claim that Hydra-PPO "improves alignment" — we only know that it improves GPT-4-assessed alignment, which may or may not correspond to human-assessed alignment.

5. **Small effective sample size for latency measurements.** The latency numbers in Table 1 appear to be from a single training run configuration. There is no report of variance across multiple runs or across different stages of training (PPO latency can vary as sequence lengths change during training). The 65% speedup claim is precise but should be understood as a point estimate from one measurement, not a statistically characterized result.

6. **No test of Hydra-PPO with a separately-trained RM.** The paper's hypothesis that Hydra-PPO's alignment advantage comes from the improved Hydra-SFT reward model could be tested by running standard LoRA-PPO using the Hydra RM head as its reward model (loading the Hydra base model's RM head as a separate model, without the multi-headed architecture). This would isolate whether the alignment gain is due to the better reward signal or due to the shared architecture's regularization during PPO. This experiment is not performed.

7. **The capacity cliff is documented but not characterized.** The paper shows that Hydra-RLHF fails at 1.3B parameters and succeeds at 7B parameters, but does not test any intermediate scales (e.g., 2.7B, 3B, 6B) to determine where the transition occurs. The "cliff" might be a gradual degradation or a sharp threshold; practitioners with models in the 1.3B–7B range have no guidance.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **LoRA-PPO + Dynamic LoRA (without Hydra-SFT):** A head-to-head comparison isolating Dynamic LoRA's memory savings and verifying no performance degradation.
- **Hydra-PPO with varying LoRA ranks:** Determining whether rank 128 is necessary or whether rank 64 or 32 could provide further memory savings with acceptable quality loss.
- **Hydra-PPO vs. DPO at matched memory:** Since DPO is a leading PPO-alternative, a direct comparison would contextualize Hydra-PPO's practical value.
- **Human evaluation on a subset of comparisons:** Even 100 human judgments on a single dataset would substantially strengthen the GPT-4 evaluation's credibility.
- **Training dynamics analysis:** Plotting reward, KL divergence, and GPT-4 win-rate over the course of PPO training for Hydra-PPO vs. LoRA-PPO would reveal whether Hydra-PPO's advantage comes from faster convergence, higher peak performance, or better stability throughout training.
- **Cross-dataset generalization:** Testing whether alignment learned via Hydra-PPO on one dataset transfers to others, which would test the hypothesis that the shared architecture produces more general alignment.

**Where the claims hold and where they are conditional:**

- **The memory reduction claim holds unconditionally** for the tested configurations: Hydra-PPO always uses less model memory than LoRA-PPO because one base model is always smaller than four. This is a structural guarantee, not an empirical finding.

- **The alignment improvement claim holds conditionally:** Hydra-PPO improves over LoRA-PPO on Llama 7b (aggregate win-rate 55.52% vs. 50.68%) but the effect size varies substantially across datasets (from +1.97 points on Open-Source Assistant to +6.10 points on Learning to Summarize). On OPT 1.3b, Hydra-PPO underperforms LoRA-PPO (52.33% vs. 56.6%), so the claim does not hold at smaller scales. The alignment advantage is conditional on sufficient model capacity and on dataset characteristics that the paper does not fully characterize.

- **The latency reduction claim holds conditionally:** The 65% speedup is measured at a specific configuration (batch size 4 for Hydra-PPO vs. batch size 1 for LoRA-PPO, 800-token sequences). At shorter sequence lengths (256 tokens, Figure 2), the speedup is smaller (~2×). At longer sequence lengths (2048 tokens), the advantage is qualitative (feasibility) rather than quantitative. The speedup also depends on having sufficient GPU memory to reinvest the model memory savings into larger batches — on GPUs with less than 80 GB, the optimal batch size increase might be smaller, reducing the latency advantage.

**Overall assessment of experimental support:** The paper's core system-level claims about memory reduction and latency improvement are well-supported by direct measurement (Table 1, Figure 2). The alignment quality claims are supported for Llama 7b in aggregate but show meaningful variance across datasets, and the reliance on GPT-4 evaluation without human validation introduces uncertainty about whether "alignment" as measured corresponds to genuine human preference improvement. The identification of the capacity cliff (1.3B vs. 7B) is a valuable negative result that adds scientific rigor by establishing a clear boundary condition. The paper would be strengthened by ablations that isolate the contributions of Dynamic LoRA versus the Hydra architecture, by evaluation protocol validation (human judgments, inter-rater statistics), and by comparison against non-PPO alignment methods that represent the practical alternative for memory-constrained practitioners.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Capacity Cliff: Hydra-RLHF Degrades Generation Quality Below a Certain Model Scale

**The assumption or constraint.** The abstract and introduction present Hydra-RLHF as a general solution for reducing PPO memory usage. However, the paper's own OPT 1.3b experiments (Section 4.5, Table 9) reveal a sharp boundary condition: the multi-headed Hydra-SFT architecture degrades generation quality on smaller models. On OPT 1.3b, Hydra-SFT achieves only a 43.49% aggregate win-rate compared to 49.08% for standard SFT — a 5.59 percentage point drop. The paper explicitly attributes this to model capacity: "We speculate this difference to be due to the capacity of the model. For the smaller 1.3b model, combining language and reward models may be more difficult."

**The consequence.** Practitioners using models in the 1.3B–7B parameter range (a common regime for academic research and resource-constrained deployment) have no guidance on whether Hydra-RLHF will help or harm their alignment pipeline. The capacity cliff means that Hydra-RLHF is not a drop-in replacement for standard RLHF — it requires empirical validation that the joint language-and-reward training does not degrade the base model before committing to the full PPO pipeline. Furthermore, Hydra-PPO on OPT 1.3b underperforms standard LoRA-PPO (52.33% vs. 56.6% aggregate win-rate, Table 9), meaning that even if practitioners could tolerate the Hydra-SFT quality loss, the downstream alignment gains are insufficient to recover. The paper's headline claim — that Hydra-RLHF "reduces PPO memory usage to be smaller than SFT while improving alignment" — is only validated at 7B parameters and above, and the OPT 1.3b results directly contradict it at smaller scales.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The OPT 1.3b results are reported in Table 9 (aggregate win-rates), Table 10 (GPT-4-LLM pairwise), Table 11 (Open-Source Assistant pairwise), and Table 16 (pre-PPO perplexity and RM accuracy). The performance gap is unambiguous: on GPT-4-LLM, LoRA-PPO achieves 59.5% vs. Hydra-PPO's 50.2% (Table 9). On Open-Source Assistant, LoRA-PPO achieves 53.7% vs. Hydra-PPO's 54.45% — the one dataset where Hydra-PPO edges ahead at this scale, though the 0.75-point gap is within plausible GPT-4 evaluation noise given the unquantified inter-evaluation variance. The paper does not test any intermediate model sizes (e.g., 2.7B, 3B, 6B), so the exact transition point is unknown.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation. It acknowledges the capacity issue in the OPT 1.3b discussion (Section 4.5) but does not propose architectural modifications (e.g., separate vs. shared hidden representations for the two heads, capacity-expanding techniques like wider hidden dimensions, or progressive unfreezing of the reward head during SFT) that might extend Hydra-RLHF to smaller models. The authors also do not frame this as a methodological limitation in Section 6 (Conclusion) — the conclusion simply states that Hydra-RLHF "opens up the possibility for the community to apply RLHF for a wider variety of models and applications" without caveating the model-size boundary. A practitioner reading only the abstract and conclusion would reasonably assume the method works at any scale, which the paper's own data refutes.

---

### The Reward Model-Only Evaluation Does Not Validate Against Human Judgments

**The assumption or constraint.** All alignment quality claims in the paper rest on GPT-4 evaluation using a specific 0–9 scoring protocol with 3-sample averaging (Appendix C). The paper explicitly positions GPT-4 as the arbiter of alignment quality: "We employ GPT-4 to evaluate model performance in general" (Section 4). No human evaluation is conducted to validate that GPT-4's quality judgments align with actual human preferences on these datasets and model outputs. The paper does not report inter-evaluation agreement statistics for the 3-sample averaging protocol (e.g., standard deviation across evaluations, correlation between individual GPT-4 judgments, or the fraction of samples where all three evaluations agree on the winner).

**The consequence.** The entire ranking of methods — Hydra-PPO > LoRA-PPO > SFT ≈ Hydra-SFT > J-Hydra-PPO for Llama 7b — is contingent on GPT-4's preferences matching human preferences. If GPT-4 has systematic biases (e.g., favoring longer responses, penalizing certain stylistic features, or being insensitive to factual errors that humans would catch), the reported alignment improvements may not translate to human-perceived quality. This is particularly concerning given the paper's own observation about GPT-4's sensitivity to evaluation format: "we found that simply switching the order of the answers would switch the preferred answer by a large margin" (Appendix C). If a trivial change like answer ordering flips preferences, the evaluation protocol may be more fragile than the reported win-rate precision (to two decimal places, e.g., 55.52%) suggests. Several pairwise comparisons have margins of 2–4 percentage points (e.g., Hydra-PPO vs. LoRA-PPO on Open-Source Assistant: 42.8% vs. 42.6%, Table 6), which could easily fall within the noise of GPT-4's evaluation variance if that variance were quantified.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports only final win-rates without any measure of evaluation uncertainty. Appendix C describes the evaluation protocol in detail — the 0–9 scale, the step-by-step reasoning requirement, the 3-sample averaging, and the output cleanup procedure — but provides no statistics on how consistent these evaluations are. We do not know: the mean absolute deviation of the three scores per sample, the percentage of samples where all three evaluations agree on the better of two answers, or whether GPT-4's preference ranking correlates with ROUGE scores (on Learning to Summarize) in a way that would provide convergent validity. The ROUGE results in Table 4 do not cleanly validate the GPT-4 rankings: Hydra-PPO dominates both GPT-4 and ROUGE on summarization, but J-Hydra-PPO achieves higher ROUGE scores (25.00 ROUGE-1 F1, 21.81 ROUGE-L F1) than LoRA-PPO (23.95 and 19.77) despite its much lower GPT-4 win-rate (43.13% vs. 55.48% aggregate on this dataset, Table 3). This discrepancy suggests ROUGE and GPT-4 capture different quality dimensions, making neither a reliable proxy for the other.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. No human evaluation is conducted or proposed for future work. The authors justify the GPT-4 evaluation by citing prior work that uses LLMs as evaluators (Peng et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2022a; Kim et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023), but this prior work does not validate GPT-4 on the specific datasets, model outputs, and evaluation prompts used in this paper. The 3-sample averaging at temperature 1 is presented as a noise-reduction technique, but without reporting the actual variance, the reader cannot assess whether it achieves sufficient precision to support the claimed performance ordering.

### The Hydra Architecture Requires Pairwise Preference Data for All Training — Standard SFT Datasets Are Unusable

**The assumption or constraint.** Hydra-SFT trains the language modeling head exclusively on the winning completion y_w from preference pairs, using the joint loss L_π_hydra(x, y_w, y_l) = L_xent(x, y_w) + γ L_R(x, y_w, y_l) (Section 3, Stage 1). This means every training example must include a dispreferred completion y_l to provide a loss signal for the reward model head. The paper explicitly acknowledges this constraint: "L_π_hydra(x, y_w, y_l) requires pairwise comparison data to train both heads, making standard SFT datasets unusable" and "Hydra-RLHF requires that the SFT and RM training datasets be the same" (Section 5, "Dataset Formation").

**The consequence.** This is a severe data constraint in practice. Many RLHF pipelines use separate datasets for SFT and RM training: a large corpus of high-quality prompt-completion pairs for SFT (often hundreds of thousands of examples, curated from human demonstrations), and a separate, typically smaller dataset of human preference annotations for RM training. Hydra-RLHF cannot use these separate datasets — it needs a single dataset where every example has both a winning and a losing completion. This means either (a) the SFT training is restricted to examples that happen to have preference annotations (reducing the available SFT data by potentially orders of magnitude), or (b) preference annotations must be collected for the existing SFT data (prohibitively expensive). The four datasets used in the paper all naturally contain pairwise preference data (GPT-4-LLM rankings, TL;DR preferences, Helpful & Harmless comparisons, StackExchange upvotes), so this constraint is invisible in the experimental results — but it would immediately surface for any practitioner whose SFT data and preference data come from different sources.

Furthermore, the restriction on multi-way rankings — "only pairs containing the sample with the best ranking should be considered" (Section 3) — means that for datasets with k > 2 ranked completions per prompt, only k−1 of the possible k(k−1)/2 pairwise comparisons are used. This reduces the effective training data for the reward model head. On StackExchange, the authors cap the losing completions at 3 ("we pair only the best answer with up to 3 other answers"), deliberately discarding preference data to avoid training the SFT head on suboptimal completions and to prevent length-based reward model heuristics. The paper does not quantify how much preference data is lost due to these restrictions or how the lost data affects reward model accuracy.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The constraint is stated in Section 3 and Section 5 ("Dataset Formation"). Table 16 shows that despite the data pairing restrictions, the Hydra RM head achieves comparable or better accuracy than the standard RM on three of four datasets for Llama 7b (GPT-4-LLM: 95.37% vs. 93.50%; Open-Source Assistant: 85.51% vs. 76.75%; StackExchange: 64.20% vs. 63.90%; Learning to Summarize: 69.36% vs. 69.31%). This suggests the data loss is not catastrophic for RM accuracy on these specific datasets. However, the paper does not test Hydra-RLHF on a scenario where the SFT and RM datasets are genuinely separate — the four benchmarks all provide preference pairs natively. The paper's claim that "Hydra-RLHF could be extended to handle exceptions when data is limited" (Section 5) is an aspiration, not a validated claim.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation (Section 5) but does not attempt to mitigate it experimentally. The suggestion that "Hydra-RLHF could be extended to handle exceptions when data is limited" is left as future work without any concrete proposal (e.g., using the RM head's predictions to pseudo-label dispreferred completions for SFT-only data, or using a separate SFT warmup phase before joint training, or incorporating an unsupervised language modeling loss on the SFT-only data alongside the pairwise losses). Practitioners with separate SFT and RM datasets currently cannot use Hydra-RLHF without either discarding SFT data or synthesizing preference labels, neither of which is evaluated in the paper.

### The Reward Over-Optimization Problem Is Not Addressed — PPO Still Exploits the Reward Model on Some Datasets

**The assumption or constraint.** PPO's central instability — the tendency to over-optimize the reward model, producing outputs that score highly under the RM but are low-quality according to humans — is well-documented in the RLHF literature (Ouyang et al., 2022; Stiennon et al., 2022) and is acknowledged in this paper: "PPO can even reduce performance by exploiting flaws in the reward model, which we observe in the StackExchange dataset" (Section 4). The paper relies on the standard defense — a KL-divergence penalty with β = 0.02 (Appendix B) — to prevent reward hacking, and does not introduce any architectural or algorithmic innovations to make the reward signal more robust to exploitation.

**The consequence.** Hydra-PPO achieves better alignment than LoRA-PPO on three of four Llama 7b datasets (Tables 3, 5–8), but this improvement comes from a better reward model (the Hydra RM head, Table 16), not from a more robust optimization procedure. If the Hydra RM head also contains exploitable flaws — which it almost certainly does, given that reward model imperfection is universal — Hydra-PPO remains vulnerable to the same over-optimization pathologies as standard PPO. The paper provides direct evidence of ongoing reward hacking in the StackExchange results: "During PPO, models often learn to repeat their answers. Despite multiple attempts, both the PPO and J-Hydra-PPO models encounter this issue while Hydra-PPO does not" (Section 4.4). Hydra-PPO avoids this specific failure mode (repetition) but the paper does not analyze whether it avoids all forms of reward exploitation or simply avoids this particular one while remaining vulnerable to others.

The Appendix D results (Table 14) further illustrate the over-optimization problem: LoRA-PPO achieves an 89.8% RM win-rate on StackExchange (the reward model strongly prefers PPO outputs over SFT outputs), but its GPT-4 aggregate win-rate is only 49.4% (Table 3) — meaning the model strongly optimizes the reward signal while achieving below-average alignment quality as judged by GPT-4. Hydra-PPO also achieves an 89.0% RM win-rate on StackExchange with a much better GPT-4 win-rate (55.38%), suggesting its reward model is less exploitable. But the RM win-rates in Table 14 show that on GPT-4-LLM and Open-Source Assistant for OPT 1.3b, Hydra-PPO achieves 86.6% and 40.8% RM win-rates respectively (vs. 70.2% and 78.6% for LoRA-PPO), indicating that the reward optimization dynamics are complex and not uniformly improved by the Hydra architecture.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence for ongoing reward exploitation is most visible on StackExchange, where Section 4.4 explicitly describes the repetition failure mode. Table 14 (RM win-rates) provides quantitative evidence that both LoRA-PPO and Hydra-PPO achieve high reward optimization (89.8% and 89.0% respectively on StackExchange) but with very different downstream quality (49.4% vs. 55.38% aggregate GPT-4 win-rate, Table 3). The paper does not provide qualitative examples of Hydra-PPO outputs on StackExchange to verify that they are free of other quality degradations (e.g., generic responses, hallucinated facts, unnatural phrasing) that might escape GPT-4 detection. The paper also does not analyze the training dynamics (e.g., plotting reward, KL divergence, and generation length over the course of training) to identify whether Hydra-PPO eventually begins to over-optimize at higher step counts.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation at all. The fact that Hydra-PPO avoids the specific StackExchange repetition failure is presented as an empirical finding without analysis of why. The KL penalty (β = 0.02) is inherited from prior work without testing whether the Hydra architecture benefits from a different coefficient. The paper does not propose any techniques for detecting or preventing reward over-optimization (e.g., early stopping based on validation set quality, dynamic KL penalty adjustment, or ensembling multiple reward models). The checkpoints are selected based on the highest smoothed reward during training ("We track the mean of the reward from the last 20 steps, and use the model with the best reward throughout training," Appendix B), which is the opposite of an over-optimization defense — it actively selects for the checkpoint that most successfully exploited the RM, with the hope that the Hydra RM's exploitation correlates with genuine quality improvement. This gamble pays off on some datasets (Learning to Summarize, StackExchange for Hydra-PPO) but clearly fails on others (LoRA-PPO on StackExchange, J-Hydra-PPO on several datasets), and there is no principled way to know in advance which outcome will occur.

### Memory Savings Are Only Partially Characterized — No Comparison to Non-RLHF Alignment Methods or 4-bit Quantization

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper frames its contribution around making PPO-based RLHF more memory-efficient: "LoRA-PPO reduces memory usage to be smaller than SFT" (Abstract) and "Hydra-PPO reduces the number of trained and static models in memory during PPO" (Section 1). However, the paper only compares Hydra-PPO against other PPO variants — LoRA-PPO, J-Hydra-PPO, and full fine-tuning PPO (estimated). It does not compare against alignment methods that bypass PPO entirely, such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Rafailov et al., 2023), RAFT (Dong et al., 2023), or RRHF (Yuan et al., 2023), despite acknowledging them in Section 5 (Related Work): "our work is orthogonal to these methods, aiming not to replace RLHF, but rather to make it more widely usable."

**The consequence.** The practical value of Hydra-PPO's memory savings depends on whether PPO-based alignment is even necessary. If DPO — which trains a single model with a preference classification loss, requiring no reference model, reward model, or critic — achieves comparable alignment quality with the memory footprint of SFT alone, then Hydra-PPO's reduction from 4 models to 1 is solving a problem that the field may already have moved past. The paper provides no evidence that Hydra-PPO's alignment quality (55.52% aggregate GPT-4 win-rate on Llama 7b, Table 3) is better than what DPO or RAFT would achieve on the same datasets with the same base model. A practitioner choosing between DPO (simpler, memory the same as SFT, no PPO instability) and Hydra-PPO (more complex, memory higher than SFT due to activations from large batch sizes, PPO instability partially mitigated) has no data from this paper to inform the decision.

Similarly, the paper does not compare against applying aggressive quantization (e.g., 4-bit or 8-bit) to the standard LoRA-PPO pipeline. If loading the reference and reward models in 4-bit precision reduces their memory footprint by 75%, the effective memory of LoRA-PPO might approach Hydra-PPO's without requiring the multi-headed architecture or the pairwise data constraint. The paper's claim that Hydra-RLHF is "a simple and promising solution for enabling more widespread usage of RLHF" (Abstract) is uncalibrated against these practical alternatives.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 5 (Related Work) acknowledges RAFT, RRHF, PRO, and DPO as methods that "combine preference data in some way with supervised fine-tuning" and notes that "the latter two seek to wholly replace PPO by re-framing the supervised training objective." The paper explicitly states its work is "orthogonal to these methods" but does not discuss what this orthogonality means for practitioners — whether Hydra-PPO and DPO could be combined (using the Hydra architecture for PPO when PPO is preferred, using DPO otherwise), or whether DPO makes Hydra-PPO's memory savings obsolete for many use cases. No experiments compare Hydra-PPO against any PPO-alternative method on any dataset. The hardware comparison (Table 1) only includes PPO variants and does not show what SFT memory usage looks like, making the "smaller than SFT" claim in the abstract difficult to verify directly.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The authors' framing — that Hydra-RLHF is "orthogonal" to PPO-alternative methods — is true in the sense that Hydra-RLHF modifies PPO rather than replacing it, but it dodges the practical question of whether PPO itself is the right target for memory optimization. A brief statement in the conclusion that "we also see potential for future improvements" does not constitute a plan for evaluating Hydra-PPO against the broader alignment landscape. The paper's memory measurements would be substantially more informative if they included a reference line for SFT memory usage and a comparison against a representative non-PPO method (e.g., DPO) on at least one dataset.

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost in a Broader Sense: Training Hydra-SFT Introduces Overhead Not Accounted for in PPO Comparisons

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's memory and latency comparisons (Table 1, Figure 2) measure only the PPO stage of RLHF. However, Hydra-RLHF modifies Stage 1 (SFT) as well: the Hydra-SFT model must be trained with a joint language modeling and reward modeling objective using pairwise preference data, requiring careful balancing of the loss weighting γ and — on datasets with multi-way rankings — deliberate restriction of training pairs to only those containing the best completion. The paper acknowledges that Hydra-SFT training requires the SFT and RM datasets to be the same (Section 5) and that standard SFT uses "5e-5, 5e-6, and 5e-7" learning rate sweeps for 4 epochs (Appendix B), but does not report the computational cost of this Stage 1 training or compare it to the cost of training separate SFT and RM models.

**The consequence.** The headline claim that Hydra-PPO reduces PPO memory usage below SFT levels omits the fact that getting to PPO in the first place requires Hydra-SFT training, which may be more expensive or more finicky than standard SFT + RM training. If Hydra-SFT requires more hyperparameter tuning (the paper sweeps learning rates for all Stage 1 models, but Hydra-SFT introduces the additional γ hyperparameter that must also be set), stricter data requirements (pairwise data only, best-answer pairing restrictions), and potentially more epochs to converge (the two objectives may optimize at different rates), then the total developer time and compute cost of the RLHF pipeline might increase even as PPO memory decreases. The paper reports Hydra-SFT hyperparameters in Table 12 — learning rates range from 5e-7 to 3e-6 depending on dataset, and the reward head multiplier γ ranges from 0.07 to 0.1 — but does not report how many hyperparameter configurations were tested to arrive at these values, nor how sensitive Hydra-SFT quality is to suboptimal γ.

Furthermore, Hydra-SFT underperforms standard SFT on OPT 1.3b (43.49% vs. 49.08% aggregate win-rate, Table 9), meaning that for smaller models, the Stage 1 modification is not just an overhead concern but actively degrades the base model. If a practitioner needs to run Hydra-SFT, discover that it underperforms on their model scale, and then fall back to standard SFT + separate RM training, the attempt cost is wasted.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 16 reports pre-PPO perplexity and RM accuracy for both the standard pipeline (separate SFT and RM) and the Hydra pipeline (Hydra-SFT). On Llama 7b, Hydra-SFT achieves comparable causal perplexity and better RM accuracy on three of four datasets, suggesting the Stage 1 overhead pays off in reward model quality. On OPT 1.3b, Hydra-SFT achieves better perplexity on GPT-4-LLM (1.66 vs. 1.71) and Open-Source Assistant (1.72 vs. 2.00), but worse GPT-4 win-rates (Table 9), indicating that perplexity improvement does not translate to generation quality improvement — a discrepancy the paper does not analyze. The paper does not report Hydra-SFT training time, memory usage, or convergence behavior relative to standard SFT + RM training. The hyperparameter tables (Tables 12–13) show that Hydra-SFT training epochs (3–6) are comparable to separate SFT (3–7) and RM (3–6) training, but this is training duration, not wall-clock time, and does not account for the sequential nature of standard training (SFT then RM, possibly using the SFT checkpoint) vs. joint training (one model, one training run).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss the Stage 1 cost. The contributions are framed entirely around PPO-stage memory and latency (Table 1, Figure 2), with Hydra-SFT presented as a one-time cost that enables the downstream savings. This is a reasonable framing given the paper's focus, but it means a practitioner cannot compute the total RLHF pipeline cost (Stage 1 + Stage 3) from the reported numbers. The paper also does not provide guidance on when the joint Hydra-SFT training is expected to converge — whether the two losses converge at similar rates, whether one head overfits while the other improves, or whether early stopping should be based on language modeling validation loss, reward model validation accuracy, or a combination. The checkpoint selection strategy for Hydra-SFT ("We use the model with the best validation performance across epochs," Appendix B) does not specify which validation metric is used, leaving ambiguity about how to trade off language modeling quality against reward model accuracy during Stage 1.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

Hydra-RLHF makes a targeted but practically significant intervention in the RLHF pipeline: it demonstrates that the four-model architecture of standard PPO — long treated as an unavoidable memory tax — is in fact a structural redundancy that can be eliminated without degrading, and in some cases while improving, alignment quality. This is not a paradigm shift in the sense that DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) represents — it does not reframe the alignment objective or propose an alternative to PPO. Rather, it is a **refactoring of the existing PPO machinery** that removes genuine redundancy (the actor's base model *is* the reference model; the critic's base *is* the reward model) through a combination of multi-headed architecture and dynamic adapter switching. The conceptual contribution is making this redundancy visible: prior work accepted loading four models as a cost of doing PPO; Hydra-RLHF shows that with LoRA freezing the base parameters and two task-specific output heads, only one base model ever needs to reside in GPU memory.

The **magnitude of the practical impact** is concrete and well-measured: on Llama 7b, Hydra-PPO reduces model memory from 53.2 GB to 15.9 GB (Table 1) — a 3.3× reduction — while achieving a higher aggregate GPT-4 win-rate than standard LoRA-PPO (55.52% vs. 50.68%, Table 3). When the freed memory is reinvested into a 4× larger batch size, per-sample latency drops from 18.75 to 6.47 seconds (a 65% reduction). At sequence length 2048, LoRA-PPO cannot fit in memory at all on the authors' 8×A100 80GB hardware, while Hydra-PPO continues to operate (Figure 2) — a qualitative feasibility threshold that matters more than any speedup factor for practitioners working with long-context tasks.

The paper resolves a **specific tension in prior PEFT-for-alignment work**: the concern that parameter-efficient methods like LoRA might underperform full fine-tuning for complex tasks like RLHF (a concern the paper acknowledges in Appendix E, citing Sun et al., 2023 and Pu et al., 2023). The LoRA-PPO baseline (50.68% aggregate, Table 3) demonstrates that LoRA-based PPO *can* improve over full fine-tuning SFT (48.55%), establishing that low-rank adaptation is sufficient for meaningful alignment gains. Hydra-PPO then shows that pushing the parameter-efficiency logic further — to the point where only LoRA adapters distinguish the actor from the reference model — does not degrade and can actually improve alignment. This shifts the burden of proof: rather than assuming full-model separation is necessary for PPO stability, future PPO implementations should justify why they are loading redundant model copies.

The paper also introduces **verifier over-optimization as a visible failure mode in RLHF** (distinct from the better-known reward hacking in the RLHF literature). The StackExchange results (Section 4.4), where LoRA-PPO and J-Hydra-PPO learn to repeat answers to exploit the reward model but Hydra-PPO avoids this behavior, provide a concrete case study of how reward model quality and architecture interact with over-optimization tendencies. The RM win-rates in Table 14 — where LoRA-PPO achieves 89.8% RM preference on StackExchange but only 49.4% GPT-4 aggregate (Table 3) — quantify the gap between reward optimization and genuine alignment improvement. This makes reward model robustness a first-class concern in PPO system design, not just a hyperparameter tuning afterthought.

**Research directions that become more attractive:** improving reward model robustness specifically for PPO optimization (since the Hydra architecture shows that better RMs directly improve downstream alignment), exploring dynamic adapter-switching strategies beyond the simple on/off mechanism of Dynamic LoRA (e.g., interpolating between actor and critic adapters, or using the reference model as a regularizer during updates rather than just at scoring time), and investigating whether the multi-headed architecture's beneficial representation learning (Hydra RM outperforms separate RM on 3 of 4 datasets for Llama 7b, Table 16) can be extended to other reward-related tasks like constitutional AI classifiers or multi-objective reward models.

**Research directions that become less critical:** developing ever-more-sophisticated PPO variants that treat the four-model architecture as fixed. The paper shows that collapsing redundant models is a simpler and more impactful intervention than algorithmic improvements to PPO's update rule. Similarly, work focused on compressing individual models in the PPO pipeline (e.g., 8-bit quantization of the reference model) is partially obviated by the observation that the reference model doesn't need to be stored as a separate copy at all.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Cheap difficulty estimation for Hydra-PPO training stability.** The paper identifies J-Hydra-PPO's instability (Appendix B: "highly unstable, taking multiple attempts to find solid hyperparameters") but does not diagnose *when* during training the instability emerges or whether early warning signals exist. A concrete follow-up would instrument Hydra-PPO training runs to track the gradient cosine similarity between the actor and critic objectives on the shared base representations (even though Hydra-PPO uses separate adapters, the base model processes both tasks). If gradient conflict in the base model's representations predicts downstream PPO divergence, a lightweight "alignment health" metric — computable from a small validation batch without running full PPO to convergence — could guide hyperparameter selection (β, learning rate, batch size) or trigger early stopping. This would directly address the paper's acknowledged limitation that PPO checkpoint selection depends on oracle reward tracking ("use the model with the best reward throughout training," Appendix B). The experiment would run Hydra-PPO and LoRA-PPO on the GPT-4-LLM dataset (the most stable in the paper's experience) and Open-Source Assistant (where LoRA-PPO's RM win-rate collapses to 14.8%, Table 14), logging gradient conflict metrics at each step and testing whether they predict the final GPT-4 win-rate better than the training reward does.

**Cross-dataset generalization of alignment learned via Hydra-PPO vs. LoRA-PPO.** The paper evaluates each method only on the dataset it was trained on, but a central (and untested) claim implicit in the multi-headed architecture is that joint language-and-reward training produces more general representations that transfer better across domains. A direct experiment: train Hydra-PPO and LoRA-PPO on GPT-4-LLM (the cleanest, most stable dataset), then evaluate both on Open-Source Assistant and StackExchange *without further PPO training*. If Hydra-PPO's shared representations produce alignment that generalizes better (measured by GPT-4 win-rate against the in-domain SFT baseline on the target dataset), this would be a major additional advantage beyond memory efficiency — the multi-headed training would act as a form of alignment regularization. Conversely, if both methods transfer equally poorly, the representational benefits of the Hydra architecture are confined to the training distribution, and practitioners should not expect out-of-domain alignment improvements. This experiment uses the existing checkpoints from the paper's training runs and only requires running evaluation on held-out datasets, making it immediately executable.

**Determining the Hydra capacity cliff threshold.** The paper shows Hydra-SFT degrades generation quality at 1.3B parameters (OPT 1.3b: 43.49% vs. SFT's 49.08%, Table 9) but succeeds at 7B parameters (Llama 7b: 48.50% vs. 48.55%, Table 3). Where is the threshold? A systematic experiment would run Hydra-SFT and standard SFT on a series of decoder-only models at intermediate scales — e.g., OPT 2.7B, Pythia 2.8B, Pythia 6.9B, Llama 2 7B (to control for architecture differences vs. OPT), and possibly a 13B model to test for diminishing returns — on a single dataset (GPT-4-LLM, for consistency with the paper). For each scale, measure both GPT-4 win-rate and RM accuracy. The hypothesis: there is a sharp threshold (not gradual degradation) at some parameter count where the model's residual stream dimensionality becomes sufficient to support two independent output projections without interference. Identifying this threshold would transform Hydra-RLHF from a "try it and see" method to a principled one with a clear applicability criterion. It would also contribute to the broader multi-task learning literature by providing evidence on whether task interference in transformers is primarily about absolute capacity or about per-task representational demands.

**Combining Hydra-PPO with Direct Preference Optimization on the same base model.** The paper explicitly positions itself as orthogonal to PPO-alternative methods like DPO (Section 5), but the Hydra architecture could enable a novel hybrid: use Hydra-SFT to produce a strong multi-headed base model, run Hydra-PPO to get the benefits of online RL (exploration, reward optimization), and then distill the resulting policy into a single-head model using DPO's preference loss on the PPO-generated comparison data. This would combine PPO's ability to explore the output space (generating novel completions that may be better than any in the static preference dataset) with DPO's simplicity and stability. A concrete experiment: on the Learning to Summarize dataset (where Hydra-PPO achieves its strongest results — 61.58% aggregate win-rate, Table 3), collect the Hydra-PPO model's completions for the training prompts, pair them with the SFT model's completions to form preference pairs, and train a DPO model on these pairs. Compare the resulting model against Hydra-PPO, standard DPO (trained on the original static preference data), and LoRA-PPO. The hypothesis: Hydra-PPO explores productively and generates better "winning" completions than are present in the static dataset, so DPO trained on PPO-generated data should outperform DPO trained on static data, potentially matching Hydra-PPO's quality with SFT-level memory requirements at deployment.

**Ablation of the LoRA rank for actor vs. critic in Hydra-PPO.** All experiments use rank 128 for both actor and critic LoRA adapters (Appendix B). The actor and critic have fundamentally different objectives — the actor learns a policy over a discrete action space (vocabulary), while the critic learns a scalar value function — and may require different adapter capacities. A systematic experiment: run Hydra-PPO on GPT-4-LLM and Learning to Summarize with actor rank ∈ {32, 64, 128, 256} and critic rank ∈ {32, 64, 128, 256} independently (a 4×4 grid), measuring both GPT-4 win-rate and memory usage. The hypothesis: the actor requires high rank (it must shift a probability distribution over 32K+ tokens) while the critic can succeed with lower rank (it outputs a single scalar). If the critic achieves comparable value prediction accuracy with rank 32 as with rank 128, the memory savings from reducing critic adapter size could be reinvested into larger batch sizes or longer sequence lengths, further improving the throughput advantage. This ablation would also establish whether the rank-128 choice — which is on the high end of typical LoRA usage — is necessary or merely conservative.

**Hydra-PPO with a smaller, separately-trained reward model head.** The paper claims Hydra-PPO's alignment advantage comes partly from the Hydra RM being more accurate (Table 16), but this conflates two factors: the RM's accuracy and the fact that the RM shares the base model's representations with the actor. A controlled experiment would train a standard LoRA-PPO where the reward model is the Hydra RM head from a separately-trained Hydra-SFT model (loading the entire Hydra-SFT checkpoint but only using its RM head), removing the shared-representation aspect while keeping the improved RM accuracy. If this variant matches Hydra-PPO's GPT-4 win-rates, then the Hydra architecture's benefit is purely from a better reward signal, and practitioners could achieve the same alignment quality by simply investing more in RM training without adopting the multi-headed architecture. If it underperforms Hydra-PPO, then the shared representations during PPO provide an additional benefit (perhaps the actor and reward model's aligned representations make the reward signal more robust to distribution shift during PPO). This experiment directly tests the paper's implicit claim that model-sharing during PPO matters beyond memory savings.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Enabling RLHF on academic GPU clusters and consumer hardware.** The most immediate practical impact is lowering the hardware barrier for PPO-based alignment. Standard LoRA-PPO on Llama 7b requires 68 GB of GPU memory at batch size 1 (Table 1) — barely fitting on a single A100 80GB, and impossible on consumer GPUs (RTX 4090: 24 GB; RTX 3090: 24 GB). Hydra-PPO's model memory drops to 15.9 GB, and while the paper maxes out the remaining memory with larger batch sizes (reaching 71.1 GB total), a practitioner with a 24 GB consumer GPU could run Hydra-PPO at batch size 1 with model memory ~16 GB and reduced activation memory (since batch size 1 activations are ~13 GB based on LoRA-PPO's numbers). This makes PPO-based alignment feasible on hardware costing ~$1,500 rather than ~$15,000 per GPU — a 10× reduction in capital barrier. University labs, independent researchers, and startups could run alignment experiments that were previously restricted to well-resourced industrial labs. The paper's open-source code (forked from DeepSpeed-Chat) and detailed hyperparameter tables (Tables 12–13) provide a concrete recipe for replication.

**Long-context PPO alignment for summarization and document QA.** Figure 2 shows that LoRA-PPO cannot run at all at sequence length 2048 on the authors' 8×A100 80GB setup, while Hydra-PPO and J-Hydra-PPO continue to operate. For applications requiring long-context generation — summarization of long documents, multi-turn dialogue alignment, chain-of-thought reasoning where the model generates hundreds of tokens of intermediate steps — this is not a speedup but an enablement. A team building a long-form summarization system (e.g., summarizing legal documents, scientific papers, or meeting transcripts) using RLHF could use Hydra-PPO to train on full-length examples without truncation, where standard LoRA-PPO would be forced to either truncate (losing alignment signal on long outputs) or use model parallelism (increasing complexity and communication overhead). The paper's ROUGE results on Learning to Summarize (Table 4) already demonstrate that PPO improves recall (19.21 vs. 13.12 ROUGE-1 recall for SFT) while Hydra-PPO achieves the best overall F-measure (29.31 ROUGE-1 F1 vs. 23.95 for LoRA-PPO), suggesting that long-context PPO alignment with Hydra-PPO produces summaries that are both more comprehensive and higher-quality by GPT-4 judgment.

**Cost-efficient RLHF for iterative model improvement cycles.** In production RLHF pipelines (e.g., the InstructGPT or Llama 2 training recipes), alignment is typically an iterative process: collect human preference data → train RM → run PPO → deploy → collect more preference data on the deployed model's outputs → retrain RM → run PPO again → repeat. Each PPO cycle currently requires substantial GPU cluster time. The paper's latency reduction — 18.75 s/sample to 6.47 s/sample for Llama 7b on StackExchange (Table 1) — means each PPO iteration completes 2.9× faster, enabling more cycles within a fixed compute budget. For a team running weekly PPO updates, this could mean the difference between 1 cycle per week (standard LoRA-PPO) and ~3 cycles per week (Hydra-PPO), or between running PPO on 100K examples vs. 300K examples in the same wall-clock time. Faster iteration enables tighter human-in-the-loop feedback, where human evaluators can review PPO outputs between cycles and adjust the reward model training data to correct systematic errors before the next PPO run. The paper's observation that Hydra-PPO avoids specific reward exploitation patterns (StackExchange repetition, Section 4.4) further supports its use in iterative pipelines where reward model robustness is critical.

**Alignment of domain-specific models where preference data is naturally paired.** The Hydra architecture's constraint — requiring pairwise preference data for all training — is a limitation for general-purpose RLHF but is actually well-matched to domains where preference data is abundant and naturally structured. Medical QA: physicians routinely compare treatment plans or diagnostic reasoning, producing implicit preference rankings. Code generation: unit test pass/fail provides a natural preference signal where the passing solution is the "winning" completion. Educational feedback: teachers comparing student answers produce preference data. In each of these domains, the Hydra architecture's joint language-and-reward training could produce domain-specific aligned models without requiring separate SFT data curation — the preference data serves both purposes simultaneously. The paper's GPT-4-LLM results (54.13% aggregate for Hydra-PPO, Table 3) already demonstrate this on an instruction-following domain where synthetic preferences (GPT-4 rankings) suffice, suggesting the approach generalizes to domains where preference annotations can be automated through strong evaluator models or objective metrics.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not explicitly articulate a decision rule choosing Hydra-PPO over named alternatives beyond the memory/latency comparison with LoRA-PPO. However, the experimental results imply practical guidance that the paper itself does not state as a formal tradeoff matrix. I provide this as a **conditional section based on evidence in the paper** rather than a generic preference matrix.

Based on the paper's own experimental boundaries, Hydra-PPO should be preferred over standard LoRA-PPO when:

- **Model capacity is at least 7B parameters** (or the practitioner has validated that Hydra-SFT does not degrade generation quality on their specific model — the OPT 1.3b results in Table 9 show a 5.59 percentage point aggregate drop from Hydra-SFT at insufficient capacity, while Llama 7b shows no drop, Table 3).

- **The training data naturally contains pairwise preferences** (all four benchmarks in the paper meet this criterion; practitioners with separate SFT and RM datasets will need to either merge them or synthesize preference labels, which the paper does not evaluate).

- **Sequence lengths exceed 1024 tokens** (Figure 2 shows that Hydra-PPO's latency advantage grows with context length and that standard LoRA-PPO becomes infeasible at 2048 tokens on typical hardware — for short-generation tasks, the speedup is modest).

- **Throughput (samples/second) matters more than peak memory reduction alone** — if the goal is to run PPO on a single small GPU with minimal memory, the paper's configuration maxes out memory with larger batches rather than minimizing absolute memory. A memory-minimized Hydra-PPO configuration (batch size 1) would use ~29 GB (16 GB model + ~13 GB activations, extrapolating from Table 1), still requiring a data-center GPU, not a consumer card.

- **Reward model quality improvement is desired**, since Hydra-SFT produces a more accurate RM than standard RM training on 3 of 4 datasets for Llama 7b (Table 16: +1.87 points on GPT-4-LLM, +8.76 points on Open-Source Assistant, +0.30 points on StackExchange, essentially tied on Learning to Summarize).

Standard LoRA-PPO should be preferred when:

- **Model capacity is below ~7B parameters** — the Hydra-SFT base model degrades at 1.3B (Table 9) and the threshold is unknown between 1.3B and 7B.

- **The SFT and RM datasets are separate** with no straightforward way to add preference annotations to the SFT data.

- **PPO stability is paramount and the practitioner cannot invest in dataset-specific hyperparameter tuning** — Hydra-PPO's reward advantage is dataset-dependent (largest on Learning to Summarize at 61.58% vs. 55.48%, Table 3; negligible on Open-Source Assistant at 51.00% vs. 49.03%), and J-Hydra-PPO's instability shows the architecture can be sensitive to loss balance (α ranging from 0.1 to 3 across datasets, Tables 12).

- **Wall-clock time per PPO iteration is less important than implementation simplicity** — Hydra-PPO requires implementing dynamic LoRA switching and managing two adapter sets; LoRA-PPO uses standard PPO infrastructure with four separate model objects, which may be easier to integrate with existing RLHF codebases.
