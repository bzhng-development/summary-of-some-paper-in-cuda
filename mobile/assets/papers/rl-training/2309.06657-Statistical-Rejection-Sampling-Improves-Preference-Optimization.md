# Statistical Rejection Sampling Improves Preference Optimization

**ArXiv:** [2309.06657](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06657)

## 🎯 Pitch

RSO dramatically boosts alignment by sampling preference pairs from the *estimated optimal policy* using rejection sampling, rather than the baseline SFT model. This cleaner target distribution makes preference optimization nearly twice as effective, winning human preference over DPO by more than a 2:1 margin.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Statistical Rejection Sampling Optimization (RSO)**, a method for improving language model alignment with human preferences by constructing preference pairs from the estimated optimal policy rather than from the SFT policy or an unknown mixture of sources. Using T5-large and T5-XXL models on Reddit TL;DR summarization and AnthropicHH dialogue tasks, RSO combines a pairwise reward-ranking model with statistical rejection sampling to generate responses closer to the target optimal policy (rso-sample-rank), then applies either sigmoid or hinge loss to fit the model on these labeled pairs — unifying DPO's logistic regression and SLiC's SVM-style approaches under a single framework. RSO improves AutoSxS win rates over DPO by 4.1 points on Reddit TL;DR and 17.0 points on AnthropicHH with T5-large, and human evaluators prefer RSO responses more than twice as often as DPO responses, establishing that the benefit of sampling from the estimated optimal policy is substantial only when the sampling distribution meaningfully approximates the target policy.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Preference Data Is Sampled from the Wrong Distribution

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is a **statistical mismatch in preference optimization**: existing methods that estimate the optimal policy $\pi^*$ from human preference data are fitting on pairs that were not sampled from $\pi^*$, violating a basic requirement of maximum likelihood estimation.

To understand why this matters, we need to be precise about what "optimal policy" means in this context. The paper operates within the KL-constrained reward maximization framework that underpins modern RLHF (Equation 3). Starting from a supervised fine-tuned policy $\pi_{\text{sft}}$, the goal is to find a policy $\pi$ that maximizes expected reward while staying close to $\pi_{\text{sft}}$ in KL divergence:

$$\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P, y \sim \pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta D_{KL}\big(\pi(y|x) \| \pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x)\big)$$

DPO's key insight was that this objective has a closed-form solution (Equation 4):

$$\pi_r(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x) \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\right)$$

By inverting this relationship and plugging it into the Bradley-Terry preference model, DPO derives Equation (2), which expresses the probability that response $y_1$ is preferred over $y_2$ **purely in terms of the optimal policy and the reference policy**, without any explicit reward function:

$$p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 | x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y_2|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y_1|x)}\right)}$$

This equation is the foundation of the approach. It says: if you observe preference pairs $(y_w, y_l)$ and treat $p^*(y_w \succ y_l|x)$ as the target, you can directly estimate $\pi^*$ by fitting a binary classifier where the logit is $\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y_l|x)}$.

But here is the critical subtlety: **this equation is a density estimation problem**. To estimate $\pi^*$ as a maximum likelihood estimator (MLE), you need training samples drawn from $\pi^*$ itself. The paper states this directly in the introduction:

> "the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) of the target optimal policy requires labeled preference pairs sampled from that policy"

This is the statistical insight that motivates the entire paper. If you fit Equation (2) on preference pairs from some other distribution — say, the SFT policy $\pi_{\text{sft}}$, or a mixture of unknown policies — you are not computing the MLE of $\pi^*$. You are solving a **mismatched estimation problem**. The resulting estimator may still be useful (it might capture some signal about which responses are better), but it is not statistically optimal for the target distribution.

---

### Why This Mismatch Is Hidden in Existing Methods

This statistical mismatch has been present in preference optimization from the beginning, but it was obscured by two factors: the complexity of RLHF pipelines and the way DPO was formulated.

**In online RLHF** (Stiennon et al., 2020; Bai et al., 2022; Touvron et al., 2023), the mismatch is partially addressed by the online nature of training. PPO samples from the current policy, which is continuously updated toward $\pi^*$, so the sampling distribution gradually approaches the target. However, even here, human preference labels are typically collected before or early in training — from the SFT policy or an earlier RLHF checkpoint — and then reused. The labels are stale, collected from a distribution that no longer matches the policy being optimized. The paper notes (Section 3.1) that the human feedback data $\mathcal{D}_{\text{hf}}$ comes from "some mixed unknown policies" $\pi_{\text{unk}}$, which may include "SFT policy, previous or current RLHF policy, or policies from other agents."

**In DPO**, the statistical mismatch becomes more acute because DPO is entirely offline. DPO takes the human preference dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{hf}}$ and directly fits the policy model using the sigmoid loss in Equation (8) — what the paper calls the "direct" approach (sigmoid-norm-direct). The human preference data used in DPO experiments is collected from unknown policies — in the Reddit TL;DR dataset, these are "human preferences on decodes from multiple models" (Section 5). DPO never samples from the estimated $\pi^*$ during training. The paper is explicit about this gap:

> "DPO uses the collected human preference data from other policies directly in all the experiments and lacks a study on the effect of sampling. Although they propose to sample pairs from the SFT policy and get them labeled by human, it is still not strictly MLE for the preference model due to the mismatch between the sampling distribution and $\pi^*$."

DPO does mention the possibility of using the SFT policy to generate responses and then having humans label them, but this still samples from $\pi_{\text{sft}}$, not $\pi^*$. The gap between $\pi_{\text{sft}}$ and $\pi^*$ is precisely the gap that preference optimization is trying to close — so using $\pi_{\text{sft}}$ as the sampling distribution means you are fitting on data that, by construction, does not reflect the behavior of the policy you are trying to estimate.

**SLiC-HF** (Zhao et al., 2023) makes this same error for a slightly different reason. SLiC's sample-rank variant generates candidate responses from the SFT policy, ranks them using a reward model (or tournament procedure), and fits the policy on these ranked pairs. Again, the sampling distribution is $\pi_{\text{sft}}$, not $\pi^*$. The paper is clear about this limitation: "SLiC can only sample preference pairs from the SFT policy."

The consequence is that **both DPO and SLiC are estimating $\pi^*$ from the wrong distribution**. Intuitively, this matters because the SFT policy and the optimal policy can differ substantially in which responses they are likely to generate. Preferences observed under $\pi_{\text{sft}}$ may not reflect the relative quality of responses that would be generated under $\pi^*$. For example, $\pi_{\text{sft}}$ might frequently generate mediocre responses that are clearly worse than other mediocre responses — these pairs provide weak training signal. But $\pi^*$ would generate higher-quality responses overall, and the preference pairs sampled from it would capture finer distinctions near the quality frontier, providing richer signal for policy improvement.

---

### The Reward Model Gap: Why an Explicit Verifier Matters

The paper identifies a second, related problem that DPO explicitly denies: the role of the reward model. DPO's slogan is "your language model is secretly a reward model" — the idea being that the policy model itself can serve as an implicit reward function via the relationship in Equation (5), eliminating the need for a separate reward model. But the paper argues that this implicit reward perspective is insufficient for the sampling problem because **you cannot sample from $\pi^*$ without access to the reward function $r(x,y)$**.

Look at Equation (4) again: $\pi_r(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x) \exp(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y))$. To sample from this distribution — for instance, via rejection sampling — you need to evaluate $r(x,y)$ for candidate responses. DPO's implicit reward $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$ is circular when you don't yet have $\pi^*$ (you're trying to estimate it) and involves the intractable partition function $Z(x)$. So DPO cannot use its own implicit reward to guide sampling toward $\pi^*$.

The paper's position is that an **explicit reward model** — specifically, the pairwise reward-ranking model $\rho_\psi(x, y_1, y_2)$ trained on human preference data — is necessary and beneficial:

> "DPO claims that the language model is secretly a reward model, we show that the language model learns better from an explicit reward model because comparing between two responses (reward) is easier to learn than generating high quality responses (policy)."

This is a conceptual argument: comparing two responses (is A better than B?) is an easier learning problem than generating optimal responses directly, because it reduces a generation problem to a classification problem. Once you have a good comparator, you can use it to guide sampling toward $\pi^*$, and then train the policy on those higher-quality samples. The reward model enables the sampling strategy; the policy model benefits from the improved samples.

---

### The Broader Significance: Closing the Gap Between Offline and Online Methods

This paper sits at the intersection of two trends in preference optimization:

**Trend 1: Moving from online to offline methods.** Online RLHF with PPO is complex, memory-intensive (requiring policy, value, reward, and reference networks simultaneously), and often unstable. DPO and SLiC promised to simplify this: directly optimize the policy from a static preference dataset, no reward model needed (for DPO), no reinforcement learning needed. This is attractive for scalability and reproducibility.

**Trend 2: The recognition that data quality matters more than algorithmic sophistication.** Across machine learning, there is growing evidence that the quality, distribution, and relevance of training data often matter more than the choice of optimizer or loss function. In the preference optimization context, the key data question is: **which responses should we compare to provide the most informative training signal?**

The paper's argument is that the first trend (offline methods) inadvertently sacrificed something important from the second trend (data quality). Online RLHF, for all its complexity, at least partially addresses the distribution mismatch by sampling from a policy that is being updated toward $\pi^*$. Offline methods like DPO and SLiC, by fixing the preference dataset at the start, accept a potentially large mismatch between the sampling distribution ($\pi_{\text{unk}}$ or $\pi_{\text{sft}}$) and the target distribution ($\pi^*$). The gap this paper addresses is: **can we get the simplicity and stability of offline methods while closing the distribution gap that online methods partially address?**

The solution — using an explicit reward model to perform rejection sampling from $\pi_{\text{sft}}$ toward $\pi^*$, then training on those samples — is essentially a way to achieve **offline preference optimization with on-policy data**. The data is generated offline, before policy training begins, but it is sampled from a distribution that approximates $\pi^*$ rather than from $\pi_{\text{sft}}$ or an unknown mixture.

---

### Prior Approaches and Their Specific Shortcomings

The paper explicitly positions itself relative to four categories of prior work:

**DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023):** The "direct" approach. Trains the policy as a binary classifier on human preference data using the sigmoid loss in Equation (8). The preference pairs come from unknown policies (in the standard setup). The sampling distribution mismatch is the primary limitation — DPO never encounters preference pairs from $\pi^*$ during training. Furthermore, without an explicit reward model, DPO cannot generate such pairs even if it wanted to, because there is no way to score candidate responses for rejection sampling.

**SLiC-HF (Zhao et al., 2023):** Two variants exist. SLiC-direct fits the hinge loss in Equation (9) directly on human preference data, suffering the same distribution mismatch as DPO-direct. SLiC-sample-rank samples responses from $\pi_{\text{sft}}$, ranks them with a reward model via tournament, and fits on the ranked pairs. This uses an explicit reward model but still samples from $\pi_{\text{sft}}$, not $\pi^*$. The paper notes that the SLiC loss is "almost equivalent" to an SVM with hinge loss (Section 1, see also the unified framework in Section 3.1 where the paper treats DPO as logistic regression and SLiC as SVM), but the sampling strategy — not just the loss function — is what limits performance.

**RAFT (Dong et al., 2023):** Samples responses from $\pi_{\text{sft}}$, ranks them with a reward model, and fine-tunes on the best response as the new SFT target. This is behavior cloning on the reward model's preferred output, not preference optimization per se. It does not use comparative preference pairs for training, only the single best output. The reward model's rankings provide the learning signal, but there is no mechanism for the policy to learn the relative quality between responses.

**ReST (Gulcehre et al., 2023):** A multi-round approach: sample from the current policy, score with a reward model, filter to keep high-scoring responses, fine-tune on those, repeat. The sampling distribution gradually shifts toward higher-reward regions, but (a) this requires multiple rounds of training and sampling, (b) the filtering threshold is ad hoc (in the paper's experiments, 0.7 normalized reward), and (c) there is no principled connection to the KL-constrained optimal policy $\pi^*$. ReST is more of a heuristic self-training loop than a principled estimation procedure.

**Online RLHF (PPO-based methods):** These partially address the distribution mismatch by sampling from the current policy during training, but introduce substantial complexity: maintaining multiple networks (policy, value, reward, reference), managing the instability of RL training, and handling the memory constraints that limit model size. The paper notes that RLHF requires "a reward model, a policy model, a reference policy, and a value model" and "limits the maximum feasible size of a model due to memory constraints." These are practical barriers that motivate the search for offline alternatives.

---

### The Specific Gap: No Method Samples Preference Pairs from the Estimated Optimal Policy

The paper's diagnosis cuts across all these methods: **none of them sample preference pairs from the estimated optimal policy $\pi^*$.** DPO uses human data from unknown policies. SLiC uses the SFT policy. RAFT and ReST use the SFT policy or intermediate checkpoints. Online RLHF uses a moving policy that approaches $\pi^*$ but mixes in stale labels from earlier distributions.

The paper's contribution is not a new loss function (it unifies existing ones) or a new reward model architecture (it uses standard pairwise ranking). It is a **sampling strategy**: use statistical rejection sampling with an explicit reward model to generate preference pairs from an approximation of $\pi^*$, then train the policy on those pairs using either logistic loss (DPO-style) or hinge loss (SLiC-style). The key insight is that this closes the distribution gap and enables a more accurate MLE of the optimal policy, with all the simplicity of offline training.

The paper frames this as a three-way choice for constructing the preference dataset $\mathcal{D}_p$ (Section 3.1):

- **direct:** Use the human preference data $\mathcal{D}_{\text{hf}}$ directly, sampled from unknown policies $\pi_{\text{unk}}$. This is what DPO does.
- **sft-sample-rank:** Sample responses from $\pi_{\text{sft}}$, rank with the reward model, construct pairs. This is what SLiC-sample-rank does.
- **rso-sample-rank:** Sample responses from $\pi_{r_\psi}$ (the estimated optimal policy induced by the reward model via Equation 4), rank with the reward model, construct pairs. This is the proposed approach.

The paper's central empirical claim is that the progression direct → sft-sample-rank → rso-sample-rank yields consistent improvements, with the largest gains at the final step, because the sampling distribution moves progressively closer to $\pi^*$. This is tested across two tasks, two model sizes, multiple loss functions, and four evaluation methods (proxy reward, gold reward, AutoSxS, human evaluation).

---

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper does not claim to replace DPO or SLiC. It claims to **improve them by fixing their sampling strategy**. The loss functions (sigmoid-norm and hinge-norm) are kept — in fact, the paper unifies them by showing both are binary classifiers fitting Equation (2), just with different loss functions (logistic regression vs. SVM with hinge loss). The innovation is in the data: where do the preference pairs come from?

This is a conceptual reframing of the preference optimization problem. Rather than treating preference data as a fixed resource to be consumed (the DPO/SLiC-direct view) or as something that can be generated from the SFT policy (the SLiC-sample-rank view), the paper argues that **the data should be generated from a distribution that approximates the target of estimation**. This is textbook statistical principles — the MLE of a distribution should use samples from that distribution — applied to the specific mechanics of preference optimization.

The paper also positions its use of "rejection sampling" as distinct from the way AnthropicHH (Bai et al., 2022) and Llama 2 (Touvron et al., 2023) use the term. In those works, "rejection sampling" refers to best-of-N or top-k-over-N: sample N responses from the SFT policy, score them with a reward model, and return the best one(s). The paper shows (Theorem 1) that this is a special case of statistical rejection sampling when $\beta \to 0$ — i.e., when you trust the reward model completely and accept only the maximum-reward response. But pure best-of-N "has the issue of reward hacking because it trusts the reward model too much without any regularization" (Section 1). Statistical rejection sampling with $\beta > 0$ balances reward exploitation with closeness to the SFT policy (through the KL constraint in Equation 3), producing samples from $\pi_{r_\psi}$ rather than simply the argmax of the reward model.

In summary: the paper identifies a distribution mismatch that limits existing offline preference optimization methods, proposes to close it using statistical rejection sampling guided by an explicit reward model, and shows that this sampling improvement translates to consistent gains in policy quality across diverse evaluations — without introducing the complexity of online RL.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper builds a **training procedure** that takes a supervised fine-tuned language model and a set of human preference comparisons (response A is better than response B for a given prompt) and produces an improved policy model that generates responses better aligned with those preferences. The system solves the problem of **statistical mismatch**: existing methods (DPO, SLiC) train on preference pairs sampled from the supervised fine-tuned policy or from unknown human sources, but maximum likelihood estimation theory says you should train on pairs sampled from the optimal policy you're trying to estimate — so this paper introduces rejection sampling with an explicit reward model to generate preference pairs from an approximation of that optimal policy, then trains on those pairs.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The RSO pipeline has four major components connected in a specific sequence:

1. **Supervised Fine-Tuned Policy (`$\pi_{\text{sft}}$`)** — A T5-large or T5-XXL model fine-tuned on task-specific demonstrations (Reddit TL;DR summaries or AnthropicHH dialogue responses). This serves as both the starting point for all training and the proposal distribution for generating candidate responses during sampling.

2. **Pairwise Reward-Ranking Model (`$\rho_\psi$`)** — A T5-XXL model trained on the human preference dataset `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{hf}}$` to predict which of two responses is preferred. Given a prompt and two candidate responses, it outputs a probability that response A beats response B. This model serves two roles: it provides the reward scores used by the rejection sampler, and it labels the constructed preference pairs for policy training.

3. **Statistical Rejection Sampler** — An algorithm that takes the SFT policy as a proposal distribution and the reward model as a scoring function, then generates responses approximately distributed according to the KL-constrained optimal policy `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`. This component transforms samples from `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` into samples from `$\pi_{r_\psi}$` by probabilistically accepting or rejecting each SFT-generated response based on how its reward score compares to the best candidate reward.

4. **Policy Trainer** — A standard fine-tuning loop that takes the labeled preference pairs (generated by the rejection sampler and scored by the reward model) and updates the SFT policy parameters to maximize agreement with those preferences, using either logistic loss (DPO-style) or hinge loss (SLiC-style).

Information flows through the pipeline in three stages. **Stage 1 (reward model training):** the human preference dataset `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{hf}}$` trains `$\rho_\psi$`. **Stage 2 (preference pair generation):** for each prompt from the SFT training set, the SFT policy samples 64 candidate responses, the rejection sampler subsamples 8 of those according to the `$\pi_{r_\psi}$` distribution using reward scores from `$\rho_\psi$`, and the reward model labels pairwise comparisons among the 8 accepted responses to produce a preference dataset `$\mathcal{D}_p$`. **Stage 3 (policy training):** the policy model initializes from `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` and trains on `$\mathcal{D}_p$` using a loss function that encourages higher normalized likelihood for preferred responses.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the KL-constrained reward maximization objective and its optimal solution (Equations 3, 4, 5), because this establishes what `$\pi^*$` is and why it can be expressed in terms of a reward function — the theoretical foundation everything else builds on.
- **Second**, the preference model and the unified loss framework (Section 3.1), because understanding how DPO and SLiC relate as binary classifiers on normalized likelihoods reveals what the policy trainer is actually doing and why the choice of loss function is separable from the choice of sampling strategy.
- **Third**, the pairwise reward-ranking model (Section 2 "Reward Model"), because the rejection sampler and the pair-labeling process both depend on the reward model's scores, and the paper needs an explicit reward model precisely because DPO's implicit reward cannot guide sampling.
- **Fourth**, the statistical rejection sampling algorithm (Section 3.2, Algorithm 1, Theorem 1), because this is the core technical innovation — how to generate samples from `$\pi_{r_\psi}$` using only `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` and the reward model.
- **Fifth**, the three candidate preference data distributions (direct, sft-sample-rank, rso-sample-rank) and the corresponding experimental configurations, because these operationalize the central hypothesis and connect the theoretical framework to the experimental design.
- **Sixth**, the hyperparameter choices (`$\beta$`, `$\gamma$`, sampling temperatures, candidate counts) and their roles, because the behavior of the system depends critically on these settings and the paper's ablation studies explain why.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems-and-methods paper** whose core idea is that preference optimization methods can be substantially improved by replacing their sampling strategy (from the SFT policy or unknown human sources to the estimated optimal policy via rejection sampling) while keeping their loss functions essentially unchanged — and that this improvement is enabled by an explicit reward model that the policy-only approaches forego.

#### The KL-Constrained Reward Maximization Objective

The starting point for understanding RSO is the objective function that defines what an "optimal policy" means in the context of learning from human feedback. This objective, shared by RLHF and DPO, balances two competing desires: maximize the expected reward of generated responses, but don't stray too far from the supervised fine-tuned policy that already produces reasonable outputs.

The objective is stated in Equation (3):

$$\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{P}, y \sim \pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi(y|x) \| \pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x)\big)$$

where `$\mathcal{P}$` is the distribution over prompts, `$\pi(y|x)$` is the policy being optimized (a conditional probability distribution over responses given a prompt), `$r(x,y)$` is a scalar reward function evaluating the quality of response `$y$` to prompt `$x$`, `$\pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x)$` is the supervised fine-tuned reference policy, `$D_{\text{KL}}$` is the Kullback-Leibler divergence measuring how different two probability distributions are, and `$\beta > 0$` is a hyperparameter controlling the trade-off.

**What it computes:** a single scalar representing the expected reward under the current policy, minus a penalty proportional to how far the current policy diverges from the SFT policy. The `$\max_\pi$` indicates we are searching over possible policies to find the one that maximizes this quantity.

**Why this form:** pure reward maximization (`$\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[r]$`) without the KL penalty would cause the policy to collapse to a point mass on the single response with the highest reward score — this is "reward hacking" where the model exploits inaccuracies in the reward model. The KL term acts as a regularizer: it keeps the policy close to the well-behaved SFT distribution, preventing the policy from moving into regions where the reward model's scores are unreliable or where generated text becomes degenerate. The hyperparameter `$\beta$` quantifies how much we trust the reward model: small `$\beta$` means we prioritize reward heavily (exploitation), large `$\beta$` means we stay close to the SFT policy (exploration/conservatism).

---

#### The Optimal Policy in Closed Form

A central mathematical result — derived in DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) — is that the KL-constrained maximization problem in Equation (3) has a known closed-form solution. For any reward function `$r$`, the policy that maximizes Equation (3) is given by Equation (4):

$$\pi_r(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x) \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\right)$$

where `$Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x) \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\right)$` is the partition function (a normalizing constant that ensures `$\pi_r$` sums to 1 over all possible responses), and all other symbols retain their meanings from Equation (3).

**What it computes:** for a given prompt `$x$` and candidate response `$y$`, this equation produces a probability `$\pi_r(y|x)$` — the likelihood that the optimal policy would generate `$y$`. The computation is: start with the SFT policy's probability `$\pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x)$`, multiply by `$\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)$` (exponentiating the reward scaled by inverse temperature), then divide by `$Z(x)$` (the sum of this product over all possible responses) to normalize.

**Why this form:** this is a Boltzmann distribution (also known as a Gibbs distribution) where the SFT policy provides the base measure and the reward provides an energy-based reweighting. Responses with higher rewards get exponentially higher probability under `$\pi_r$` compared to `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$`, but the SFT prior prevents the distribution from putting all mass on the single highest-reward response. The `$\beta$` parameter governs the exponent's sharpness: as `$\beta \to 0$`, `$\exp(r/\beta)$` becomes extremely peaked around the maximum reward, and `$\pi_r$` approaches a point mass on `$\arg\max_y r(x,y)$`. As `$\beta \to \infty$`, `$\exp(r/\beta) \to 1$` for all responses, and `$\pi_r \to \pi_{\text{sft}}$`.

Equation (4) can be rearranged to express the reward in terms of the policy, which is how DPO eliminates the explicit reward model. Solving for `$r(x,y)$` yields Equation (5):

$$r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

where the second term `$\beta \log Z(x)$` depends only on the prompt `$x$`, not on the response `$y$`.

**What it computes:** given the optimal policy `$\pi_r$` and the reference policy `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$`, this equation recovers the implicit reward that would make `$\pi_r$` optimal under Equation (3). The reward for response `$y$` is `$\beta$` times the log-ratio of its probability under the optimal policy versus the reference policy, plus a prompt-dependent constant.

**Why this form:** this relationship is what makes DPO possible — it means that if you can estimate `$\pi_r$` (the optimal policy), you have implicitly recovered the reward function, and vice versa. DPO uses this to train the policy directly from preferences without training a separate reward model: by fitting a policy so that the reward differences implied by Equation (5) match the observed human preferences under the Bradley-Terry model. However, as the paper points out, this relationship is also why DPO cannot sample from `$\pi_r$`: computing `$Z(x)$` requires summing over all possible responses, which is intractable for language generation. An explicit reward model `$r_\psi$` avoids this problem because it directly outputs a scalar score without needing `$Z(x)$`.

---

#### The Bradley-Terry Preference Model

The connection between rewards and observed preferences is made through the Bradley-Terry model (Bradley & Terry, 1952), Equation (6):

$$P(y_1 \succ y_2 | x) = \sigma\big(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2)\big)$$

where `$P(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)$` is the probability that response `$y_1$` is preferred over `$y_2$` for prompt `$x$`, `$r^*$` is the ground-truth (unobserved) reward function, and `$\sigma(t) = 1/(1 + e^{-t})$` is the logistic sigmoid function.

**What it computes:** the probability of a preference judgment as a function of the reward difference between the two responses. When `$y_1$` has much higher reward than `$y_2$`, the sigmoid's input is large and positive, and the preference probability is close to 1. When rewards are equal, the probability is 0.5 (random choice). When `$y_2$` has higher reward, the probability is close to 0.

**Why this form:** the Bradley-Terry model is the standard assumption in preference learning because it models the pairwise comparison as a noisy observation of an underlying latent score difference, where the noise follows a logistic distribution. It is used throughout RLHF, DPO, and SLiC, making it the common ground for comparing methods.

By substituting the reward expression from Equation (5) into the Bradley-Terry model (Equation 6), the partition function terms cancel (they depend only on `$x$`, not on `$y_1$` or `$y_2$`), yielding the DPO preference equation (Equation 2):

$$p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 | x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y_2|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y_1|x)}\right)}$$

where `$\pi^*$` is the optimal policy induced by the ground-truth reward `$r^*$` according to Equation (4).

**What it computes:** the probability that `$y_1$` beats `$y_2$` expressed purely in terms of the policy ratio `$\pi^*(y|x) / \pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x)$` (the normalized likelihood of response `$y$` under the optimal policy). This eliminates `$r^*$` entirely — the preference probability depends only on how much more likely `$\pi^*$` is to generate each response compared to the SFT baseline.

**Why this form:** this is the equation that DPO and RSO both fit. It converts the preference modeling problem into a policy estimation problem: if you observe that `$y_1$` is preferred over `$y_2$`, then `$\pi^*$` should assign higher relative probability to `$y_1$` than to `$y_2$` (after normalizing by `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$`). The `$\beta$` parameter from the original KL constraint becomes the scaling factor on the log-ratio — it determines how much a given difference in normalized log-likelihood translates to a difference in preference probability. The key statistical point the paper makes is: to estimate `$\pi^*$` via maximum likelihood from this equation, the training pairs `$(y_1, y_2)$` should be sampled from `$\pi^*$` itself, not from `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` or some unknown mixture.

#### The Unified Loss Framework: Logistic Regression and SVM

The paper reframes DPO and SLiC as two instances of the same estimation problem with different loss functions (Section 3.1, "Choice of loss function"). Both methods fit a binary classifier to observed preference pairs, where the classifier's input is the difference in normalized log-likelihood between the preferred and dispreferred responses.

Given a preference dataset `$\mathcal{D}_p = \{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}_w, y^{(i)}_l)\}$` where `$y_w$` is preferred over `$y_l$` for prompt `$x$`, the general form of the classifier uses as its logit (the unnormalized score before applying sigmoid):

$$\Delta(x, y_w, y_l) = \gamma \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y_w|x)} - \gamma \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y_l|x)}$$

where `$\pi_\theta$` is the policy model being trained (parameterized by `$\theta$`), `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` is the fixed reference policy, and `$\gamma$` is a temperature hyperparameter that controls the steepness of the decision boundary.

**What this computes:** for a pair of responses, `$\Delta$` measures how much more the current policy favors the preferred response over the dispreferred response, relative to the SFT baseline. Each term `$\gamma \log(\pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{sft}})$` is the log of the normalized likelihood — the same quantity that appears in Equation (2). A large positive `$\Delta$` means the policy strongly agrees with the preference label; a negative `$\Delta$` means the policy contradicts it.

**Why this form:** by normalizing by `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$`, the logit measures the policy's deviation from the SFT prior, not the absolute probability of a response. This is important because the SFT policy already assigns different base probabilities to different responses (common responses get high probability regardless of quality). Subtracting `$\log \pi_{\text{sft}}$` factors out these base-rate effects, so the training signal focuses on the policy's learned preference signal. The `$\gamma$` parameter, which the paper decouples from `$\beta$`, controls the margin sensitivity: larger `$\gamma$` means the loss penalizes misclassifications more aggressively.

**DPO's loss (sigmoid-norm):** DPO applies logistic regression to `$\Delta$`, using the sigmoid loss (binary cross-entropy on the preference probability). Equation (8):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{sigmoid-norm}}(\pi_\theta | \pi_{\text{sft}}, \mathcal{D}_p) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}_p} \left[ \log \sigma\!\left( \gamma \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y_w|x)} - \gamma \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y_l|x)} \right) \right]$$

**What it computes:** the expected negative log-likelihood of the observed preference, where the predicted probability that `$y_w$` beats `$y_l$` is `$\sigma(\Delta)$`. For a single pair, if `$\Delta$` is large and positive (policy strongly agrees with the preference), `$\sigma(\Delta) \approx 1$` and the loss is near zero. If `$\Delta$` is large and negative (policy strongly disagrees), `$\sigma(\Delta) \approx 0$` and the loss is large. The expectation averages over all pairs in the training set.

**Why this form:** logistic regression is the maximum-likelihood estimator for a Bernoulli target (preferred vs. not), assuming the log-odds are linear in `$\Delta$`. It provides calibrated probability estimates and uses a smooth, differentiable loss that is well-suited to gradient-based optimization. The loss penalizes errors proportionally to their severity — a mild disagreement incurs a smaller loss than a strong contradiction.

**SLiC's loss (hinge):** SLiC uses a hinge loss, which is the loss function underlying support vector machines. The paper's Equation (9) presents the unnormalized version:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{hinge}}(\pi_\theta | \mathcal{D}_p) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}_p} \left[ \max\big(0, 1 - [\gamma \log \pi_\theta(y_w|x) - \gamma \log \pi_\theta(y_l|x)]\big) \right]$$

**What it computes:** the hinge loss evaluates whether the policy's raw log-probability difference `$\gamma(\log \pi_\theta(y_w) - \log \pi_\theta(y_l))$` exceeds a margin of 1. If the difference is `$\geq 1$`, the loss is zero — the model is already sufficiently confident in the correct ordering. If the difference is less than 1 (or negative), the loss is `$1 - \text{difference}$`, linearly penalizing the shortfall. The expectation averages this over all training pairs.

**Why this form:** the hinge loss is characteristic of maximum-margin classifiers. Unlike logistic loss, which always provides a non-zero gradient, hinge loss stops providing gradient once the margin is satisfied, which can lead to sparser solutions and may be more robust to outliers. However, the paper notes that `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{hinge}}$` without the `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` normalization is vulnerable to a subtle problem: a policy can achieve a large log-probability difference simply by assigning high probability to common (but not necessarily good) responses, since `$\log \pi_\theta$` is not controlled for base rates. This motivates the normalized hinge loss.

**The improved hinge-norm loss:** The paper's Equation (10) normalizes the hinge loss by `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$`, creating the SVM counterpart to DPO's logistic regression:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{hinge-norm}}(\pi_\theta | \pi_{\text{sft}}, \mathcal{D}_p) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}_p} \left[ \max\!\left(0, 1 - \left[ \gamma \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y_w|x)} - \gamma \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{sft}}(y_l|x)} \right]\right) \right]$$

**Why this form:** the normalization by `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` addresses the base-rate problem. Now the logit is the same `$\Delta$` used in DPO, and the margin applies to the normalized log-likelihood difference. This makes hinge-norm the direct SVM analog of sigmoid-norm (DPO): both minimize a loss on `$\Delta$`, but one uses logistic regression and the other uses a hinge loss. The paper empirically validates (Table 1) that hinge-norm outperforms the unnormalized hinge loss on the AutoSxS metric (e.g., 70.84 vs. 69.26 on Reddit TL;DR for rso-sample-rank), confirming that the SFT normalization matters.

#### The Pairwise Reward-Ranking Model

The paper trains an explicit pairwise reward-ranking model `$\rho_\psi$`, a T5-XXL (11B parameter) encoder-decoder model fine-tuned on the human preference data `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{hf}}$`. This model is essential because it provides the reward scores used by the rejection sampler, and it also provides the preference labels on the constructed pairs used for policy training.

The model's input is a formatted string containing the prompt and two candidate responses. For the summarization task (Reddit TL;DR), the format is:

```
[CONTEXT] {x} [SUMMARY A] {y1} [SUMMARY B] {y2}
```

For the AI assistant task (AnthropicHH), the format is:

```
[CONTEXT] {x} [RESPONSE A] {y1} [RESPONSE B] {y2}
```

The model outputs a single token: "A" if it predicts `$y_1$` is preferred, or "B" if it predicts `$y_2$` is preferred. The probability of decoding "A" — after softmax over the two output tokens — is treated as the model's estimate of `$\hat{P}(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)$`, the probability that response A beats response B.

**What it computes:** given a prompt and two responses, `$\rho_\psi$` outputs a preference probability `$\hat{P}(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)$` — a single scalar between 0 and 1. To obtain a pointwise reward score for a single response, the paper compares it against a baseline response `$y_b$` and uses Equation (7):

$$r_\psi(x, y) = \text{logit}\big(\rho_\psi(x, y, y_b)\big)$$

where `$\text{logit}(p) = \log(p / (1-p))$` is the inverse of the logistic sigmoid, and `$y_b$` is "a random decoded sequence from the SFT policy" assigned a reward score of 0.

**What this equation computes:** it converts the pairwise preference probability into a pointwise reward score. If `$\rho_\psi(x, y, y_b) = 0.5$` (response `$y$` is equally preferred to the baseline), then `$r_\psi(x, y) = \log(0.5/0.5) = 0$`, matching the baseline's score. If `$\rho_\psi(x, y, y_b) = 0.9$` (strong preference for `$y$`), then `$r_\psi(x, y) = \log(0.9/0.1) \approx 2.2$`, indicating higher quality. If `$\rho_\psi(x, y, y_b) = 0.1$` (strong preference for the baseline), then `$r_\psi(x, y) \approx -2.2$`.

**Why this form:** the Bradley-Terry model (Equation 6) states that `$P(y \succ y_b) = \sigma(r(y) - r(y_b))$`. Setting `$r(y_b) = 0$` (anchoring the absolute scale), we get `$r(y) = \sigma^{-1}(P(y \succ y_b)) = \text{logit}(P(y \succ y_b))$`. So Equation (7) directly inverts the Bradley-Terry model to recover the implicit reward from the predicted preference probability. The choice of baseline is somewhat arbitrary — the paper selects a random SFT response, which is a convenient zero point — and notes that "we can in theory solve n reward scores from n² comparisons with a baseline sequence via a constraint optimization setting," but leaves this for future work.

The trained reward-ranking model achieves 73.23% accuracy on the validation set for summarization and 69.75% for the AI assistant task (Section 2, footnote 1). To remove positional bias (models may systematically prefer response A or B regardless of content), the paper randomly flips the order of response pairs and their associated labels during training.

#### Statistical Rejection Sampling: From SFT Samples to Optimal Policy Samples

This is the core technical contribution: an algorithm that generates responses approximately distributed according to `$\pi_{r_\psi}$` (the estimated optimal policy) using only the SFT policy `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` (as the proposal distribution) and the reward model `$r_\psi$` (to compute acceptance probabilities).

The algorithm, described in Section 3.2 and implemented in Algorithm 1 (Python pseudocode in Appendix A.1), proceeds as follows:

1. Start with an empty set of accepted responses for the current prompt.
2. Generate a batch of candidate responses from the SFT policy `$\pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x)$` using temperature sampling (temperature = 0.7, top-k = 40).
3. Score each candidate with the reward model `$r_\psi(x, y)$` using Equation (7).
4. Find the maximum reward `$r_{\text{max}}$` among the currently available (not-yet-accepted-or-rejected) candidates.
5. For each candidate `$y$` with reward `$r_\psi(x, y)$`, generate a uniform random number `$u \sim U[0, 1]$` and accept `$y$` if:

$$u < \exp\!\left(\frac{r_\psi(x, y) - r_{\text{max}}}{\beta}\right)$$

6. Remove accepted candidates from the pool and add them to the output set.
7. If more samples are needed, recompute `$r_{\text{max}}$` from the remaining candidates and repeat steps 5–7 until the desired number of samples is obtained.

**What this computes:** given an SFT proposal batch and per-candidate reward scores, this procedure produces a subsample of responses whose distribution approximates `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`. The acceptance probability `$\exp((r - r_{\text{max}})/\beta)$` is highest for the candidate(s) with maximum reward (where `$r = r_{\text{max}}$`, acceptance probability = 1) and decreases exponentially for candidates with lower rewards. The steepness of this decay is controlled by `$\beta$`.

**Why this form:** this follows directly from the structure of the optimal policy in Equation (4). The ratio `$\pi_{r_\psi}(y|x) / \pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z_\psi(x)} \exp(r_\psi(x,y)/\beta)$`. In rejection sampling, we accept a proposal from the proposal distribution `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` with probability proportional to the target-to-proposal density ratio, divided by a constant `$M$` that upper-bounds this ratio for all `$y$`. The algorithm approximates `$M$` using the maximum observed reward `$r_{\text{max}}$`, leading to the acceptance probability in step 5. Theorem 1 formalizes this:

**Theorem 1 (stated in Section 3.2, proved in Appendix A.2):** "Let `$r_{\text{max}}$` be the maximum rewards among the response candidates not yet accepted. As the number of response candidates goes to infinity, Algorithm 1 can generate num_samples distinct samples from `$\pi_{r_\psi}$` with expected acceptance rate `$\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x)}[\exp(\frac{1}{\beta} \cdot (r_\psi(x, y) - r_{\text{max}}))]$`."

**What Theorem 1 says in plain English:** if you had infinitely many SFT samples, the algorithm would produce exact samples from `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`, and the fraction of proposals that get accepted would be the expected value of the exponential reward gap. In practice, the algorithm operates on a finite batch of 64 SFT samples, computing `$r_{\text{max}}$` as the sample maximum rather than the true maximum, and the sampling-without-replacement mechanism (removing accepted candidates before the next round) ensures that each round accepts at least one candidate (the one with `$r = r_{\text{max}}$`, which has acceptance probability 1).

**The `$\beta$` parameter and its role:** `$\beta$` is the same parameter from the KL-constrained objective (Equation 3). It governs how selective the rejection sampler is:

- **`$\beta \to 0$` (small):** the exponential `$\exp((r - r_{\text{max}})/\beta)$` becomes extremely sharp. Only responses with `$r$` very close to `$r_{\text{max}}$` have any chance of acceptance. In the limit `$\beta \to 0$`, only the exact maximum-reward response is accepted (with probability 1), and all others are rejected. This is exactly the best-of-N or top-k-over-N procedure that prior work (Bai et al., 2022; Touvron et al., 2023) calls "rejection sampling." The paper explicitly notes this equivalence: "If `$\beta \to 0$`, only the highest reward response will be accepted and all other responses will be rejected. This is the rejection sampling (top-k-over-N) referred by AnthropicHH and Llama 2."

- **`$\beta \to \infty$` (large):** the exponential `$\exp((r - r_{\text{max}})/\infty) \to \exp(0) = 1$`, so every SFT sample is accepted. The sampler returns responses distributed as `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$`, with no selection pressure. This corresponds to complete trust in the SFT policy and no trust in the reward model.

- **Intermediate `$\beta`** (the practical regime): the sampler accepts a range of high-reward responses, with acceptance probability decaying smoothly with reward gap. This produces samples from `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`, which balances reward exploitation with closeness to the SFT prior — exactly the tradeoff encoded in the KL-constrained objective.

**Why statistical rejection sampling rather than just best-of-N:** pure best-of-N (`$\beta \to 0$`) "has the issue of reward hacking because it trusts the reward model too much without any regularization" (Section 1). By setting `$\beta > 0$`, RSO maintains diversity in the accepted responses and prevents the sampler from exclusively chasing potentially over-optimized reward maxima. The accepted responses retain some probability mass from the SFT prior, providing a regularizing effect. The paper treats `$\beta$` as a hyperparameter and selects it based on validation metrics, finding `$\beta = 0.5$` to be optimal (Figure 3b).

**Implementation details:** The paper samples 64 response candidates from the SFT policy per prompt, then applies rejection sampling to subsample 8 responses. These 8 responses are then formed into preference pairs: the 8 responses can be paired in `$8 \times 7 / 2 = 28$` pairwise comparisons, each labeled by the reward model `$\rho_\psi$`. The paper experiments with both first-round ranking (pair adjacent responses, generating `$n/2$` pairs) and tournament ranking (run a single-elimination tournament until a winner is determined, generating `$n-1$` pairs). Table 2 shows that tournament ranking improves proxy reward win rates but does not improve AutoSxS — the simplest "first-round-rank" on 8 RSO-sampled responses gives the best AutoSxS (71.86%), and adding tournament ranking reduces it slightly (71.69%).

#### The Three Candidate Preference Data Distributions

The paper operationalizes its hypothesis about the importance of the sampling distribution by comparing three methods for constructing the training preference dataset `$\mathcal{D}_p$` (Section 3.1, "Choice of preference data distribution"):

**1. direct:** Use the human preference data `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{hf}}$` directly, without generating new responses or labeling. This is the standard DPO and SLiC-direct approach. The sampling distribution is `$\pi_{\text{unk}}$` — an unknown mixture of human raters comparing responses generated by "SFT policy, previous or current RLHF policy, or policies from other agents" (Touvron et al., 2023). The paper characterizes this as the furthest from `$\pi^*$`: "Statistically speaking, since we are estimating `$\pi^*(y|x)$`, it is desired to draw samples from `$\pi^*(y|x)$`," and "direct" uses samples from an uncontrolled distribution that may be qualitatively very different from the target.

**2. sft-sample-rank:** Sample response pairs from the SFT policy `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` and label them with the reward-ranking model `$\rho_\psi$`. This is equivalent to SLiC-HF-sample-rank (Zhao et al., 2023). The sampling distribution is known and controlled (`$\pi_{\text{sft}}$`), and the reward model provides consistent (if imperfect) labels, but the samples still come from the SFT policy rather than the optimal policy. This is an improvement over "direct" because the sampling distribution is at least well-defined and the labeling is consistent, but it still suffers from the distribution mismatch.

**3. rso-sample-rank:** Sample response pairs from `$\pi_{r_\psi}$` (the estimated optimal policy, induced by the reward model via Equation 4) using the statistical rejection sampling algorithm, and label them with `$\rho_\psi$`. The sampling distribution is `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`, which is "closer to `$\pi^*(y|x)$` than [the] other two choices" (Section 3.1). This is the proposed approach.

**The experimental design:** the paper crosses these three preference data distributions with the three loss functions (sigmoid-norm, hinge, hinge-norm), producing nine experimental configurations (Table 1). DPO is the sigmoid-norm-direct configuration; SLiC-direct is hinge-direct; SLiC-sample-rank is hinge-sft-sample-rank. RSO corresponds to the rso-sample-rank configurations with either sigmoid-norm (RSO-sigmoid-norm) or hinge-norm (RSO-hinge-norm). The paper also includes RAFT and ReST as separate baselines that use cross-entropy loss on the single best response rather than preference pair losses.

**The expected ordering:** the paper's hypothesis predicts that performance should improve from direct → sft-sample-rank → rso-sample-rank, because the sampling distribution moves progressively closer to `$\pi^*$`. Table 1 confirms this ordering: on Reddit TL;DR, AutoSxS win rates for sigmoid-norm are 67.72 (direct) → 69.02 (sft-sample-rank) → 71.86 (rso-sample-rank), and for hinge-norm: 66.63 (direct) → 68.46 (sft-sample-rank) → 70.84 (rso-sample-rank). On AnthropicHH, the gaps are larger: AutoSxS for sigmoid-norm moves from 24.01 → 39.56 → 40.98, and for hinge-norm: 22.89 → 35.96 → 38.58. The jump from direct to sft-sample-rank is particularly large on AnthropicHH, which the paper does not explicitly explain but may reflect that the human preference data for dialogue tasks is further from `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` than for summarization, making the distribution mismatch more severe.

#### Full Training Configuration and Hyperparameters

The paper provides specific hyperparameter settings that govern the entire RSO pipeline. The default values and their roles are:

**Reward-ranking model training:** trained on `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{hf}}$` to predict pairwise preferences. Accuracy on the validation set is 73.23% for Reddit TL;DR and 69.75% for AnthropicHH.

**Response generation (pre-sampling from SFT):** for each prompt in the SFT training set, 64 candidate responses are sampled from `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` using temperature sampling with temperature = 0.7 and top-k = 40. Temperature controls the randomness of generation (0.7 introduces diversity while keeping outputs reasonable), and top-k = 40 restricts the sampling to the 40 most probable tokens at each step, preventing degenerate low-probability tokens.

**Rejection sampling:** from the 64 SFT candidates, 8 responses are subsampled via Algorithm 1 with `$\beta = 0.5$` (the default; ablation in Figure 3b shows this is optimal). The `$\beta$` value of 0.5 means that a response with reward 0.5 below the maximum has acceptance probability `$\exp(-0.5/0.5) = \exp(-1) \approx 0.37$`, and a response 1.0 below the maximum has acceptance probability `$\exp(-1.0/0.5) = \exp(-2) \approx 0.14$`. This provides moderate selection pressure while maintaining diversity.

**Preference pair construction:** from the 8 accepted responses, preference pairs are constructed using first-round ranking (the default, based on Table 2 results). This divides the 8 responses into 4 adjacent pairs, with the reward model labeling which response in each pair is preferred.

**Policy training:** the policy model (T5-large at 770M parameters or T5-XXL at 11B parameters) initializes from the SFT checkpoint and trains on the constructed preference pairs. Training uses batch size 32, learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-5}$` with the Adafactor optimizer (Shazeer & Stern, 2018). The default loss function temperature is `$\gamma = 0.05$` (ablation in Figure 3a shows this is optimal). The default `$\beta = 0.5$` (for rejection sampling) and `$\gamma = 0.05$` (for the loss function) are decoupled — `$\beta$` controls the sampling distribution, and `$\gamma$` controls the training loss margin sensitivity. The paper picks the checkpoint with the highest reward-ranking model win rate against the SFT target on a validation set for final evaluation.

**Scale-up experiments:** the T5-XXL policy experiments (Table 3) use the sigmoid-norm loss only (presumably for computational efficiency, and because sigmoid-norm and hinge-norm performed similarly in the T5-large experiments). The sampling and training otherwise follow the same procedure.

**Regularization loss:** SLiC's original formulation includes a regularization term `$-\lambda \log \pi_\theta(y_{\text{ref}}|x)$` that encourages the policy to maintain high probability on the SFT target response (Equation 1). The paper drops this term for all experiments, noting in Appendix A.6 (Table 5) that adding regularization with weights ranging from 0.5 to 500 "does not show significant improvement" on proxy reward or AutoSxS metrics, and "we drop it to align better with the DPO setting." This is a simplifying choice that removes a hyperparameter and makes the comparison between DPO and SLiC variants more direct.

#### The Relationship Between `$\beta$` and `$\gamma$`

A subtle but important design choice is the decoupling of `$\beta$` (from the KL-constrained objective) and `$\gamma$` (the temperature in the loss function). In DPO's original formulation, these are the same parameter: DPO sets `$\gamma = \beta$` in Equation (8), meaning the same parameter that controls the KL penalty in the objective also scales the logit in the loss function.

The paper decouples them, treating `$\beta$` as a hyperparameter for the rejection sampling stage and `$\gamma$` as a separate hyperparameter for the policy training stage. This is motivated by the fact that these parameters control different aspects of the system: `$\beta$` determines the sampling distribution (how aggressively the rejection sampler selects high-reward responses), while `$\gamma$` determines the training loss's sensitivity to preference violations (how much each misclassified pair is penalized). Decoupling them allows independent optimization of the sampling quality and the training dynamics.

The ablation experiments confirm that these parameters have different optima: Figure 3b shows that proxy reward win rate peaks at `$\beta = 0.5$` (with sharp degradation at `$\beta = 0$` and `$\beta = 5$`), while Figure 3a shows that proxy reward win rate peaks at `$\gamma = 0.05$` (with degradation at `$\gamma = 0.005$` and `$\gamma = 0.5$`). If `$\beta$` and `$\gamma$` were forced to be equal, no single value could achieve both optima.

The paper's interpretation of `$\beta$` in the rejection sampling context is worth noting: "`$\beta$` indicates how much we trust the reward model. If the reward model is very accurate and robust, we should set a small `$\beta$`. Otherwise, we should set a larger `$\beta$`." The optimal `$\beta = 0.5$` reflects a moderate level of trust — enough to prefer high-reward responses but not enough to exclusively chase the maximum.

#### Relation to Prior Work: How RSO Differs From Best-of-N and ReST

The paper takes care to distinguish its statistical rejection sampling from two superficially similar approaches:

**Best-of-N / top-k-over-N (Bai et al., 2022; Touvron et al., 2023):** In these works, "rejection sampling" means: sample N responses from the policy, score them with a reward model, and return the single best (best-of-N) or top k (top-k-over-N) responses. The paper shows that this is the limiting case of statistical rejection sampling when `$\beta \to 0$` — in the limit, only the maximum-reward response is accepted with probability 1. But this `$\beta \to 0$` limit corresponds to the KL penalty going to zero in Equation (3), meaning the objective becomes pure reward maximization with no regularization. This makes the approach vulnerable to reward hacking: the reward model's imperfections get amplified because the sampler exclusively trusts the reward signal.

RSO's contribution is to use `$\beta > 0$`, which maintains the KL regularization implicit in the optimal policy distribution. The accepted responses are distributed as `$\pi_{r_\psi}$` (Equation 4) rather than as a point mass on the reward-maximizing response. This provides diversity and robustness: multiple different high-reward responses can be accepted, not just the absolute maximum, and responses from lower-reward regions can also be accepted with some probability, maintaining coverage of the SFT distribution.

**ReST (Gulcehre et al., 2023):** ReST also uses a reward model to filter SFT-generated responses for fine-tuning, but it does so with a hard threshold (normalized reward > 0.7) rather than probabilistic acceptance. The paper notes several differences: (a) ReST trains on the filtered responses as new SFT targets (cross-entropy loss on the response text), not as preference pairs for comparative training; (b) the threshold is ad hoc rather than derived from the KL-constrained objective; and (c) ReST typically runs multiple rounds of sampling, filtering, and training, while RSO does one round of sampling followed by preference optimization. The paper's results (Table 1) show that ReST substantially underperforms RSO: on Reddit TL;DR, ReST achieves AutoSxS 34.36 vs. RSO's 71.86; on AnthropicHH, ReST achieves 15.58 vs. RSO's 40.98. This suggests that preference pair training on RSO-sampled data is much more effective than behavioral cloning on filtered data.

**RAFT (Dong et al., 2023):** RAFT samples responses from the SFT policy, ranks them with a reward model, and fine-tunes on the best response. Like ReST, this is behavioral cloning rather than preference optimization. The paper's results show RAFT outperforming ReST (AutoSxS 53.77 vs. 34.36 on Reddit TL;DR) but still substantially underperforming all RSO variants, confirming that preference pair training provides a richer learning signal than single-response imitation.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Preference Optimization as a Sampling Distribution Problem Rather Than a Loss Function Problem

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is diagnostic rather than algorithmic: it identifies that the primary bottleneck in offline preference optimization is **not the choice of loss function** but the **distribution from which training pairs are sampled**. This reframes the entire problem space.

Prior to this work, the dominant narrative in preference optimization was about loss functions and algorithmic frameworks. DPO's contribution was framed as "eliminating the reward model" — the key innovation was the analytical derivation that allows optimizing the policy directly from preferences without learning an explicit reward. SLiC's contribution was framed around the contrastive calibration loss. The implicit assumption was that if you have a good loss function and a preference dataset, you're solving the problem correctly.

RSO challenges this framing by pointing out a statistical fact that was hiding in plain sight: the maximum likelihood estimator of `$\pi^*$` from Equation (2) requires samples drawn from `$\pi^*$` itself. This is not a subtle point — it's a basic requirement of density estimation — but it was obscured by the complexity of the RLHF pipeline and the elegance of DPO's derivation. The paper forces the field to ask: **where do the training pairs come from?** rather than **how should we weight the training pairs?**

The significance of this reframing goes beyond the specific solution (rejection sampling). It establishes a new axis of analysis for preference optimization methods: the **sampling distribution gap**. Any method — DPO, SLiC, RLHF, or future approaches — can now be analyzed in terms of how closely its training distribution approximates `$\pi^*$`. The paper provides a framework for this analysis by explicitly comparing three distributions: `$\pi_{\text{unk}}$` (direct), `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` (sft-sample-rank), and `$\pi_{r_\psi}$` (rso-sample-rank), and showing that proximity to `$\pi^*$` is the key driver of performance. This is a **conceptual reframing of the field's priorities**, not an incremental improvement — it tells future researchers what to optimize (data distribution) rather than what to tweak (loss function hyperparameters).

The experimental evidence for this reframing is in the consistent ordering across Table 1: direct → sft-sample-rank → rso-sample-rank improves performance for **every** loss function and **every** task. On Reddit TL;DR with hinge-norm: AutoSxS goes 66.63 → 68.46 → 70.84. With sigmoid-norm: 67.72 → 69.02 → 71.86. The loss function (sigmoid-norm vs. hinge-norm) matters, but the sampling distribution matters more. This pattern validates the diagnostic: fixing the sampling distribution gives gains that changing the loss function cannot match, because the loss function cannot compensate for training on data from the wrong distribution.

### Innovation 2: Unifying DPO and SLiC as Binary Classifiers on the Same Quantity, Differing Only in Loss Function

The paper makes a theoretical contribution that is subtle but important: it shows that DPO and SLiC are **doing the same statistical estimation problem** — fitting a binary classifier to preference pairs where the classifier input is the normalized log-likelihood difference `$\Delta$` — and differ only in their choice of loss function. DPO uses logistic loss (logistic regression); SLiC uses hinge loss (support vector machine). This is stated concisely in Section 1:

> "we unify DPO and SLiC statistically by showing that they vary by loss functions to fit on human preference data: DPO is a logistic regression on human preference data and SLiC is almost equivalent to a support vector machine (SVM) with hinge loss."

This is not just taxonomy. It is a **clarification that decouples two previously entangled concerns**: (1) the mathematical form of the preference model (Equation 2, shared by both), and (2) the loss function used to fit it (logistic vs. hinge). Prior work treated these as method-specific: DPO's loss was seen as intrinsic to DPO, and SLiC's loss as intrinsic to SLiC. The paper shows they are interchangeable — you can use hinge loss with DPO's normalized logits (hinge-norm, Equation 10), and you can use sigmoid loss with SLiC's sampling strategy (sigmoid-norm-sft-sample-rank).

The practical consequence is a **modular framework** where the loss function and the sampling strategy can be independently optimized. This enables the paper's central experiment: cross the three sampling distributions with the three loss functions to isolate the effect of each. If the loss function and sampling strategy were coupled (as in the original DPO and SLiC formulations), you could not tell whether DPO's performance came from its sigmoid loss or from its use of human preference data. The unified framework shows that both matter, but the sampling strategy is the larger lever.

Why does this matter beyond this paper? Because it suggests that **future work on preference optimization should not focus on inventing new loss functions**. The paper shows that two very different loss functions (logistic and hinge) produce similar results when given the same data. The gains from the loss function variation are small (a few points on AutoSxS, comparing sigmoid-norm-rso-sample-rank at 71.86 to hinge-norm-rso-sample-rank at 70.84 on Reddit TL;DR). The gains from the sampling strategy are much larger (direct to rso-sample-rank gives ~4 points on Reddit TL;DR and ~17 points on AnthropicHH for the same loss). This is a **negative result with positive implications**: it tells the field to stop optimizing loss functions and start optimizing data generation.

The paper also identifies a concrete improvement to SLiC within this unified framework: normalizing the hinge loss by `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` (hinge-norm vs. hinge). The unnormalized hinge loss (hinge) allows the policy to satisfy the margin by assigning high probability to common-but-mediocre responses, since `$\log \pi_\theta$` alone doesn't account for base rates. The hinge-norm fixes this by subtracting `$\log \pi_{\text{sft}}$`, matching DPO's normalization. The experimental evidence (Table 1) supports this: on Reddit TL;DR with rso-sample-rank, hinge achieves 69.26 AutoSxS while hinge-norm achieves 70.84. This is a direct consequence of the unified framework — once you see SLiC and DPO as fitting the same `$\Delta$`, the need for normalization becomes obvious.

### Innovation 3: Showing That the Reward Model Is Valuable *Because* It Enables Better Sampling, Not Because It Provides Better Training Labels

A counterintuitive insight from this work is about **why** the explicit reward model helps. The natural assumption would be that the reward model's value lies in providing better (more consistent, more accurate) preference labels compared to human annotations. After all, the reward model is trained on the human preference data, so it should capture the same signal but with less noise.

But the paper shows the reward model's value is fundamentally about **enabling sampling from `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`**, not about label quality. This is evident from the experimental design: the sft-sample-rank configuration already uses the reward model for labeling (it labels pairs generated from `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$`), but it does **not** use the reward model to guide which responses to sample. The jump from sft-sample-rank to rso-sample-rank — which adds rejection sampling but uses the same reward model for labeling — provides substantial gains: ~2.8 AutoSxS points on Reddit TL;DR and ~1.4 on AnthropicHH for sigmoid-norm (Table 1).

The paper makes this point explicitly in the introduction:

> "DPO claims that the language model is secretly a reward model, we show that the language model learns better from an explicit reward model because comparing between two responses (reward) is easier to learn than generating high quality responses (policy)."

The key phrase is "easier to learn" — but the mechanism by which this ease translates to better performance is the sampling strategy. The reward model provides a tractable scoring function `$r_\psi(x,y)$` that can be evaluated on large batches of SFT-generated candidates, enabling rejection sampling toward `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`. DPO's implicit reward — even if it were learnable — cannot be used this way because computing `$Z(x)$` is intractable. So the reward model's advantage is not that it's a better classifier of preferences (it may even be worse than the implicit reward DPO could learn), but that it is **operationalizable** for guiding data generation.

This has implications for how the field should think about reward model design. If the reward model's primary value is as a **sampling guide** rather than a **label generator**, then reward model quality should be evaluated on its ability to rank responses correctly in the region around `$\pi^*$`, not on its agreement with human labels on arbitrary response pairs. A reward model that agrees perfectly with human preferences on `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` samples but fails to discriminate among high-quality responses from `$\pi^*$` would be useless for RSO. Conversely, a reward model with moderate human agreement that accurately ranks near-optimal responses could be very effective. This reframes reward model evaluation.

The paper's advocacy for the reward model also functions as a **critique of DPO's reward-model-free philosophy**. DPO's slogan — "your language model is secretly a reward model" — implies that the reward model is an unnecessary intermediate representation that can be bypassed entirely. RSO shows that this is true only if you never need to sample from `$\pi^*$`. If you want to improve the sampling distribution — which the paper argues is the key to performance — you need a reward model that can be evaluated independently of the policy. The "secret" reward model in DPO is entangled with the policy being trained; it cannot be used to guide generation of training data because accessing it requires evaluating the very policy you're trying to improve.

### Innovation 4: Establishing That Best-of-N Rejection Sampling Is a Limiting Case of a More General KL-Regularized Procedure

The paper makes a theoretical contribution that clarifies a confusion in the RLHF literature about the term "rejection sampling." In AnthropicHH (Bai et al., 2022) and Llama 2 (Touvron et al., 2023), "rejection sampling" refers to generating N responses from the current policy, scoring them with a reward model, and returning the best one(s). This is an **exploitation-only** procedure: it trusts the reward model completely, selecting the maximum-reward response with no regard for the SFT prior.

RSO shows (Theorem 1) that this best-of-N procedure is a special case of statistical rejection sampling when `$\beta \to 0$`. In this limit, the acceptance criterion `$\exp((r - r_{\text{max}})/\beta)$` becomes a delta function at `$r = r_{\text{max}}$` — only the exact maximum-reward response is accepted with probability 1, and all others are rejected. This is precisely best-of-N.

But the more general statistical rejection sampling procedure with `$\beta > 0$` does something fundamentally different: it produces samples from `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`, the **KL-regularized optimal policy**, not from the argmax of the reward model. The accepted responses maintain some probability mass from the SFT prior, meaning the sampler accepts a **distribution** of responses rather than a single point. This matters because the KL penalty in Equation (3) exists precisely to prevent the policy from collapsing to a narrow peak around potentially over-optimized reward maxima. Best-of-N (`$\beta \to 0$`) discards this regularization; RSO with `$\beta > 0$` preserves it.

The paper frames this as a critique of prior rejection sampling approaches:

> "This algorithm has the issue of reward hacking because it trusts the reward model too much without any regularization. In this paper we show that top-k-over-N is a special case of our statistical rejection sampling and it is critical to balance between the reward exploitation and regularization towards the SFT policy."

The experimental evidence for the importance of the `$\beta$` parameter is in Figure 3b: when `$\beta = 0$` (equivalent to best-of-N), the proxy reward win rate is **lower** than at `$\beta = 0.5$` on Reddit TL;DR. This is a striking result — it means that pure reward exploitation (`$\beta = 0$`) produces a worse policy than the moderate regularization at `$\beta = 0.5$`. The reward model can be over-trusted, and the KL constraint is not just a theoretical nicety but a practical necessity.

What makes this an insight rather than just a parameter sweep is the connection to the KL-constrained objective. The `$\beta$` in rejection sampling is the same `$\beta$` from Equation (3) — it controls the same tradeoff between reward maximization and proximity to the SFT policy. Prior work treated best-of-N as an ad hoc selection procedure disconnected from the RLHF objective. RSO shows it is a special case of the same objective, taken to the unregularized extreme, and that the regularized version (`$\beta > 0$`) is empirically better. This provides a **principled interpretation** of a previously heuristic practice and a prescription for improvement: set `$\beta$` to balance trust in the reward model against the SFT prior.

The decoupling of `$\beta$` and `$\gamma$` (Section 3.1, confirmed in Figures 3a and 3b) is a practical consequence of this insight. In DPO's original formulation, the equivalent parameter controls both the sampling distribution (if you were sampling) and the training loss sensitivity. RSO shows these should be tuned independently, because they serve different purposes and have different optima. The best `$\beta$` for rejection sampling (0.5) is an order of magnitude larger than the best `$\gamma$` for the loss function (0.05), confirming that the sampling tradeoff and the training tradeoff operate on different scales.

### Innovation 5: Demonstrating That Human Evaluation Follows the Statistical Intuition — Even When Proxy Metrics Are Misleading

A methodological contribution of the paper is its **multi-layered evaluation strategy** that reveals a form of reward hacking in the proxy metrics and validates the RSO approach through human judgment.

The paper uses four evaluation methods (Section 5): Proxy Reward (the T5-XXL reward-ranking model's win rate against the SFT target), Gold Reward (a separately trained PaLM 2-S reward model on the same data), AutoSxS (a PaLM 2-L few-shot in-context comparison), and Human Evaluation (Amazon Mechanical Turk raters comparing three systems). These form a hierarchy of increasing reliability and decreasing correlation with the training objective.

The key observation is that **certain methods score highly on proxy reward but poorly on AutoSxS and human evaluation** — a signature of reward hacking where the policy learns to exploit the training reward model without producing genuinely better outputs. Specifically, the hinge loss without SFT normalization (hinge) achieves very high proxy reward win rates (93.36 on Reddit TL;DR for hinge-rso-sample-rank) but lower AutoSxS (69.26) compared to hinge-norm-rso-sample-rank (92.80 proxy reward, 70.84 AutoSxS). Similarly, SLiC-direct (hinge-direct) achieves 86.92 proxy reward but only 60.54 AutoSxS on Reddit TL;DR — a massive gap that indicates the proxy metric is being gamed.

The human evaluation results (Table 4) provide the strongest validation of the paper's central claim. Raters asked to compare responses from direct, sft-sample-rank, and rso-sample-rank (all using the same loss function) consistently prefer rso-sample-rank: it was chosen as the best response 48% of the time for sigmoid-norm on Reddit TL;DR (vs. 21% for direct and 10% for sft-sample-rank) and 31% of the time on AnthropicHH (vs. 15% for direct and 22% for sft-sample-rank). The quality scores (1-5 ratings) show the same pattern: rso-sample-rank achieves 4.02 vs. 3.84 (direct) and 3.74 (sft-sample-rank) on Reddit TL;DR. This means the progression direct → sft-sample-rank → rso-sample-rank is not just a quirk of automatic metrics but reflects **genuinely perceptible improvements in response quality as judged by humans**.

What makes this an insight rather than just a sanity check is that it validates the **statistical intuition** behind the method. The paper's argument is that sampling from `$\pi_{r_\psi}$` provides a more accurate MLE of `$\pi^*$`. This is a theoretical claim about statistical estimation. The human evaluation confirms that this theoretical improvement translates to better outputs as perceived by humans — closing the loop from statistical theory to practical utility. If the human evaluation had shown no preference for rso-sample-rank over sft-sample-rank, it would mean either (a) the distribution mismatch doesn't matter in practice, or (b) the rejection sampling doesn't move the distribution enough. The strong human preference for rso-sample-rank rejects both possibilities and confirms that the sampling improvement is substantive enough to be noticed.

The paper's use of AutoSxS as an intermediate evaluation layer is also notable. AutoSxS uses PaLM 2-L as a few-shot judge — a large model that is not the same as the training reward model and is not susceptible to the same over-optimization. The correlation between AutoSxS and human evaluation is high (the ordering of methods is consistent across both), making AutoSxS a practical proxy for human judgment in ablation experiments where human evaluation would be prohibitively expensive. This methodological choice — validating AutoSxS against human judgments and then using it for systematic ablation — enables the paper's extensive hyperparameter analysis without requiring human raters for every configuration.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses three datasets across its experiments. The primary tasks use **Reddit TL;DR summarization** (Stiennon et al., 2020), which provides 117k/6k/6k examples in train/validation/test splits for SFT (`\mathcal{D}_{\text{sft}}^{\text{tldr}}`), plus 93k human preference comparisons on model-generated summaries (`\mathcal{D}_{\text{hf}}^{\text{tldr}}`). The second primary task uses the **AnthropicHH dialogue dataset** (Bai et al., 2022), specifically the helpfulness slice (`\mathcal{D}_{\text{hf}}^{\text{helpful}}`) with 161k/9k examples in train/test splits; positive responses from the preference pairs serve as SFT targets. A third dataset, **CNN/DailyMail** (Hermann et al., 2015), is used only for cross-task generalization testing with 287k/13k/11k examples in its train/validation/test SFT splits.

- **Base model(s).** All policy models are T5 variants (Raffel et al., 2020), an encoder-decoder transformer architecture. The primary experiments use **T5-large** (770M parameters) as the policy model being optimized, with scaling experiments extending to **T5-XXL** (11B parameters). The reward-ranking model is consistently **T5-XXL** (11B parameters), trained separately as a pairwise preference classifier. The SFT policy `\pi_{\text{sft}}` for each task is a T5-large or T5-XXL model fine-tuned on the task's demonstration data. The paper chose T5 models as representative of the capabilities of contemporary encoder-decoder architectures, enabling clean isolation of the preference optimization method without conflating with architectural innovations.

- **Metrics.** Four evaluation approaches are used, forming a hierarchy of increasing reliability. **Proxy Reward** computes the win rate of generated responses against the SFT target text on the trained T5-XXL pairwise reward-ranking model — this is the fastest metric but is susceptible to reward hacking since the policy may exploit the same model used during training. **Gold Reward** evaluates using a separately trained PaLM 2-S (Anil et al., 2023) reward model on the same human preference data, providing an independent reward signal (validation accuracy: 76.07% on summarization, 70.18% on dialogue). **AutoSxS** uses PaLM 2-L as a few-shot in-context judge: for each prompt, responses from two policies are compared, with the judge choosing A, B, or tie; the average score across 8 decoded samples with 4 flipped orderings determines a winner if the margin exceeds 0.35 — this provides a large-model evaluation independent of the training reward model. **Human Evaluation** uses Amazon Mechanical Turk raters who assign 1–5 pointwise quality scores to responses from three systems (direct, sft-sample-rank, rso-sample-rank) and select the best one; each comparison is judged by 3 different raters to ensure reliability, with the aggregate choice determined by majority vote.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five established approaches. **DPO** (Rafailov et al., 2023) uses sigmoid loss on normalized likelihoods (`\mathcal{L}_{\text{sigmoid-norm}}`) with preference pairs taken directly from human annotations — equivalent to the sigmoid-norm-direct configuration in the paper's framework. **SLiC-HF** (Zhao et al., 2023) uses hinge loss in two variants: SLiC-direct fits hinge loss directly on human preference data, while SLiC-sample-rank first samples responses from `\pi_{\text{sft}}`, ranks them via tournament with a reward model, and then fits hinge loss on the ranked pairs — equivalent to hinge-sft-sample-rank (with minor differences: RSO drops SLiC's SFT regularization loss, finding it provides no significant improvement per Appendix A.6 Table 5). **RAFT** (Dong et al., 2023) samples responses from `\pi_{\text{sft}}`, ranks them with a reward model, and fine-tunes on the single best response as a new SFT target using cross-entropy loss. **ReST** (Gulcehre et al., 2023) uses one round of grow-and-improve: normalize reward scores to [0,1], keep responses with score > 0.7, fine-tune on these as new SFT targets using cross-entropy loss. **Online RLHF** (PPO-based) is discussed but not run as a baseline; the paper notes the lack of expertise and the fact that DPO already shows competitiveness with RLHF as justification for focusing the comparison on offline methods.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute in terms of inference operations across three stages, with the full accounting detailed in Appendix A.10 Table 7. For RSO, the cost structure is: (1) **sampling stage:** `n_c = 64` response decodings from the SFT policy per prompt (using temperature 0.7 and top-k=40), which requires `N_p \times n_c` full sequence generations where `N_p` is the number of prompts; (2) **reward scoring stage:** each of the 64 candidates is scored against a randomly chosen baseline sequence, requiring `n_c - 1 = 63` reward model comparisons per prompt (the reward model decodes only a single token — "A" or "B" — making this fast relative to full policy generation); (3) **rejection sampling and pair construction:** from the 64 scored candidates, 8 are selected via Algorithm 1, and these 8 are formed into `n_d/2 = 4` preference pairs via first-round ranking, requiring an additional 4 reward model comparisons. The total reward model inference per prompt is approximately `63 + 4 = 67` single-token comparisons. For comparison: RAFT and ReST require `n_c` SFT decodings and `n_c - 1` reward comparisons; SLiC-sample-rank requires `n_d` SFT decodings and `n_d - 1` reward comparisons; DPO-direct and SLiC-direct require no additional inference (they use pre-existing human preference data). The paper notes that "rso-sample-rank accounts for less than 10% of the total training time" (Appendix A.10), and the sampling/ranking is fully parallelizable across the training set, unlike online PPO which requires sequential sampling during training. The comparison across methods is made at equivalent training budgets — each method trains the policy model to convergence on its respective preference dataset, with the same batch size (32) and optimizer (Adafactor, learning rate 1e-5). The policy training cost itself is similar across methods (same architecture, same number of training steps to convergence), so the primary cost difference is in the data generation stage.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper uses a straightforward checkpoint selection protocol rather than k-fold cross-validation. For each experimental configuration, the policy is trained to convergence, and the **checkpoint with the highest reward-ranking model win rate against the SFT target** on a validation set is selected for final evaluation. This is a standard early-stopping-by-validation approach, not a cross-validation procedure. The four evaluation metrics (Proxy Reward, Gold Reward, AutoSxS, Human Evaluation) are all computed on held-out test sets that are distinct from both the SFT training data and the human preference training data. For the human evaluation specifically, each comparison task is replicated 3 times with different raters (47 total raters participated, median 16 tasks per rater), and model identities are anonymized with randomized response ordering to eliminate positional bias. Confidence intervals (95%) are reported for the hyperparameter sweeps in Figure 3. The paper acknowledges that the reward-ranking model validation accuracy is moderate — 73.23% on Reddit TL;DR summarization and 69.75% on AnthropicHH dialogue (Section 2 footnote 1) — which bounds the quality of the reward signal used for both pair labeling and rejection sampling.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Head-to-Head Comparison Across All Methods on Two Primary Tasks (Table 1, T5-Large)

The primary results table (Table 1) compares nine configurations (3 loss functions × 3 preference pair distributions) plus the RAFT and ReST baselines, all using T5-large (770M) policy models, evaluated by Proxy Reward, Gold Reward, and AutoSxS.

**On Reddit TL;DR summarization**, the best RSO configuration (RSO-sigmoid-norm, using rso-sample-rank pairs with sigmoid-norm loss) achieves 92.37% Proxy Reward, 82.22% Gold Reward, and 71.86% AutoSxS. This represents a **+4.14 AutoSxS point improvement** over standard DPO (sigmoid-norm-direct: 67.72% AutoSxS) and a **+4.52 point improvement** over SLiC-sample-rank (hinge-sft-sample-rank: 67.34% AutoSxS). The progression from direct to sft-sample-rank to rso-sample-rank is monotonic in AutoSxS for every loss function tested. For sigmoid-norm: 67.72 → 69.02 → 71.86. For hinge: 60.54 → 67.34 → 69.26. For hinge-norm: 66.63 → 68.46 → 70.84. Three patterns are notable. First, the unnormalized hinge loss (hinge-direct, hinge-sft-sample-rank, hinge-rso-sample-rank) shows the largest gap between Proxy Reward and AutoSxS — e.g., hinge-rso-sample-rank achieves 93.36% Proxy Reward (the highest in the table) but only 69.26% AutoSxS (lower than both sigmoid-norm and hinge-norm rso-sample-rank configurations), indicating reward hacking where the policy exploits the training reward model. Second, the normalized losses (sigmoid-norm and hinge-norm) track each other closely, with sigmoid-norm slightly ahead at the rso-sample-rank level (71.86 vs. 70.84). Third, RAFT (Proxy 74.84, AutoSxS 53.77) and ReST (Proxy 49.03, AutoSxS 34.36) substantially underperform all preference pair-based methods, confirming that behavioral cloning on filtered or selected single responses is less effective than comparative preference training.

**On AnthropicHH dialogue**, the pattern is magnified. RSO-sigmoid-norm achieves 86.94% Proxy Reward, 59.15% Gold Reward, and 40.98% AutoSxS. The gap between direct and rso-sample-rank is **16.97 AutoSxS points** for sigmoid-norm (24.01 → 40.98) and 15.69 points for hinge-norm (22.89 → 38.58). The direct configurations perform markedly poorly on this task: DPO (sigmoid-norm-direct) achieves only 24.01% AutoSxS, and SLiC-direct (hinge-direct) achieves only 15.69%. The jump from direct to sft-sample-rank is particularly large on this dataset — sigmoid-norm-direct to sigmoid-norm-sft-sample-rank improves AutoSxS from 24.01 to 39.56 (+15.55 points) — suggesting that the human preference data for dialogue tasks is substantially further from `\pi_{\text{sft}}` than for summarization, making the distribution mismatch more severe. Even the intermediate sft-sample-rank recovers most of the gap, and rso-sample-rank adds another +1.42 points on top. The unnormalized hinge loss again shows reward hacking: hinge-rso-sample-rank achieves 82.21% Proxy but only 32.56% AutoSxS, compared to hinge-norm-rso-sample-rank at 84.44% Proxy and 38.58% AutoSxS. RAFT (AutoSxS 24.99) and ReST (AutoSxS 15.58) are again substantially below the preference pair methods.

**The effect of the loss function is smaller than the effect of the sampling distribution.** On Reddit TL;DR, switching from sft-sample-rank to rso-sample-rank improves AutoSxS by approximately 2.8 points for sigmoid-norm and 2.4 points for hinge-norm. Switching from sigmoid-norm to hinge-norm at any fixed sampling distribution changes AutoSxS by 1–2 points. The sampling strategy dominates. On AnthropicHH, the sampling strategy dominates even more dramatically: the direct to sft-sample-rank switch provides ~15 points, while the loss function choice among normalized variants changes results by ~2 points at any given sampling level.

#### Human Evaluation Results (Table 4)

The human evaluation provides the strongest test of the central claim — that rso-sample-rank generates genuinely better outputs, not just better proxy scores. Table 4 reports results from a three-way comparison (direct vs. sft-sample-rank vs. rso-sample-rank) for each loss function on both tasks.

**On Reddit TL;DR with sigmoid-norm:** rso-sample-rank is chosen as the best response **48% of the time**, compared to 21% for direct and 10% for sft-sample-rank. The average quality score (1–5) is 4.02 for rso-sample-rank, versus 3.84 for direct and 3.74 for sft-sample-rank. The fact that sft-sample-rank is chosen less often (10%) than direct (21%) on this task despite its higher AutoSxS score (69.02 vs. 67.72) is an interesting discrepancy — it may reflect that human raters perceive the direct (human-labeled) data as producing more natural outputs even when AutoSxS slightly prefers sft-sample-rank, or it may be noise from the relatively small human evaluation sample (the paper does not report statistical significance for the choice proportions). With hinge-norm, the pattern is similar: rso-sample-rank preferred 46% of the time (quality 3.97) vs. direct 21% (quality 3.80) and sft-sample-rank 11% (quality 3.68). Across both loss functions, rso-sample-rank is preferred **more than 2x as often as direct** (DPO-equivalent configuration), and more than 4x as often as sft-sample-rank.

**On AnthropicHH with sigmoid-norm:** rso-sample-rank is chosen as best 31% of the time (quality 3.37), compared to direct 15% (quality 3.04) and sft-sample-rank 22% (quality 3.21). The gap is smaller than on Reddit TL;DR but still substantial: rso-sample-rank receives roughly 2x the preference share of direct. With hinge-norm: rso-sample-rank 33% (quality 3.60), direct 13% (quality 3.33), sft-sample-rank 22% (quality 3.56). The normalized loss (hinge-norm) shows higher absolute quality scores than sigmoid-norm on this task (e.g., rso-sample-rank quality 3.60 vs. 3.37), which is consistent with the pattern in Table 1 where hinge-norm-sft-sample-rank (35.96 AutoSxS) outperforms sigmoid-norm-sft-sample-rank (39.56 AutoSxS) — though the AutoSxS ordering is reversed for rso-sample-rank (38.58 vs. 40.98).

**Key takeaway from human evaluation:** the sampling distribution progression (direct → sft-sample-rank → rso-sample-rank) translates to human-perceptible quality improvements, validating the statistical argument that sampling from `\pi_{r_\psi}` provides a better MLE of `\pi^*`. The magnitude of human preference for rso-sample-rank over direct (2x preference share, ~0.2–0.3 point quality improvement) is substantial enough to matter in practice.

#### Scaling to T5-XXL Policy Models (Table 3)

Table 3 investigates whether RSO's benefits persist when the policy model is scaled up to 11B parameters (T5-XXL), which is the same scale as the reward-ranking model. Only sigmoid-norm loss is tested at this scale (presumably due to computational cost, and because sigmoid-norm and hinge-norm performed similarly at T5-large scale). The comparison covers direct (DPO), sft-sample-rank, and rso-sample-rank for both tasks.

**On Reddit TL;DR with T5-XXL:** DPO (direct) achieves 94.04% Proxy Reward and 85.03% AutoSxS. Sft-sample-rank improves to 97.50% Proxy and 85.66% AutoSxS (+0.63 points). Rso-sample-rank (RSO) achieves 98.29% Proxy and **86.01% AutoSxS** — a +0.98 point improvement over direct and +0.35 points over sft-sample-rank. The absolute gains from the sampling strategy are smaller at this model scale compared to T5-large (+4.14 from direct to rso-sample-rank), but the ordering is preserved. This is consistent with the interpretation that a larger policy model has more capacity to learn from any dataset, reducing the relative importance of data quality improvements — though the improvements persist and remain positive.

**On AnthropicHH with T5-XXL:** the benefits of the sampling strategy remain substantial. DPO (direct) achieves 76.84% Proxy and 52.80% AutoSxS. Sft-sample-rank improves dramatically to 94.91% Proxy and 66.79% AutoSxS (+13.99 points). Rso-sample-rank achieves 97.54% Proxy and **70.26% AutoSxS** — a +17.46 point improvement over direct and +3.47 points over sft-sample-rank. The AnthropicHH task shows large gains from the sampling strategy even at scale, particularly in the jump from direct to sft-sample-rank (similar to the T5-large pattern where the distribution mismatch was more severe on this task). The fact that rso-sample-rank continues to add meaningful gains beyond sft-sample-rank at 11B scale (+3.47 AutoSxS points) confirms that the rejection sampling improvement is not merely a small-model phenomenon — it provides benefits at scales where the policy model matches the reward model in capacity.

**Comparison across scales:** the absolute AutoSxS numbers for T5-XXL (86.01 Reddit TL;DR, 70.26 AnthropicHH) are substantially higher than T5-large (71.86 and 40.98 respectively), confirming that scaling the policy model provides independent benefits beyond the data improvement. The sampling strategy and model scaling are complementary rather than competing: RSO on T5-large achieves 71.86 AutoSxS on Reddit TL;DR, while DPO on T5-XXL achieves 85.03. RSO on T5-XXL achieves 86.01 — combining both improvements yields the best result.

#### Cross-Task Generalization to CNN/DailyMail (Table 6)

Table 6 (Appendix A.7) tests whether policies trained with preference data from the Reddit TL;DR domain transfer to the out-of-domain CNN/DailyMail news summarization task. This is a zero-shot transfer test: the policy is optimized using Reddit TL;DR preference pairs (from any of the three sampling strategies) but never sees any preference data from CNN/DailyMail. Evaluation uses Proxy Reward (against CNN/DailyMail reference summaries) and AutoSxS on the CNN/DailyMail validation split.

The RSO approach consistently improves cross-task generalization. RSO-sigmoid-norm achieves 69.38% Proxy Reward and 39.71% AutoSxS, compared to DPO (sigmoid-norm-direct): 61.31% Proxy and 37.36% AutoSxS. The gain from rso-sample-rank over direct is +1.99 AutoSxS points for sigmoid-norm and +8.27 points for hinge-norm (33.91 → 42.18). Notably, hinge-norm-rso-sample-rank achieves the highest AutoSxS on this task (42.18%), outperforming all sigmoid-norm variants. This is the only task where hinge-norm clearly dominates sigmoid-norm, which may reflect that the margin-based loss generalizes better across distribution shifts. RSO-hinge-norm also achieves the largest improvement over its direct counterpart (+8.27 AutoSxS points from 33.91 to 42.18), suggesting that the combination of rejection sampling (bringing the training distribution closer to the optimal policy for the source task) and the margin-based loss (which may be more robust to domain shift) is particularly effective for transfer.

SLiC-direct (hinge-direct) performs poorly on this transfer task: 64.18% Proxy but only 33.63% AutoSxS — the unnormalized hinge loss appears to overfit to the Reddit TL;DR reward model in a way that does not generalize. This is consistent with the reward hacking pattern observed in the primary tasks.

**Key takeaway from cross-task transfer:** RSO's benefits extend beyond the training domain, with RSO-hinge-norm achieving the best out-of-domain performance. This suggests that sampling from `\pi_{r_\psi}` produces a policy that has learned general features of "good summarization" rather than domain-specific reward model exploitation.

#### Qualitative Examples (Figures 4 and 5 in Appendix A.3)

The paper provides two qualitative examples comparing SFT, SLiC, DPO, and RSO outputs. For Reddit TL;DR (Figure 4), a forum post about relationship and career decisions is summarized by all four systems. RSO's summary ("M[21] unhappy in relationship with F[22]. Met an F[19] in town with similar interests and I really like her. What should I do in regards to current relationship/other girl?") concisely captures all key elements: the narrator's demographics, the current relationship dissatisfaction, the new person of interest, and the central question. In contrast, SFT omits key details, SLiC is vague, and DPO includes extraneous information. For AnthropicHH (Figure 5), a user asks about sending a letter quickly through traditional mail. RSO's response directly answers the question about mailing a letter, while SFT repeatedly discusses email (irrelevant), and SLiC/DPO are vague and repetitive. These examples illustrate the practical improvement: RSO generates responses that are more directly responsive and include fewer irrelevant or redundant details.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Effect of `\gamma` (loss function temperature) on Reddit TL;DR (Figure 3a):** The paper fixes `\beta = 0.5` and sweeps `\gamma \in \{0.005, 0.05, 0.5\}` across all three loss functions (sigmoid-norm, hinge, hinge-norm) using rso-sample-rank pairs. The proxy reward win rate peaks at `\gamma = 0.05` for all three losses. At `\gamma = 0.005`, the loss penalizes misclassification weakly (the logit difference is scaled down by a factor of 10 compared to `\gamma = 0.05`), leading to underfitting and lower proxy win rates. At `\gamma = 0.5`, the loss penalizes misclassification aggressively, which causes overfitting to the reward model's labels and a slight degradation. The optimal `\gamma = 0.05` is an order of magnitude smaller than the default `\beta = 0.5`, confirming that the sampling temperature and the loss temperature operate on different scales and should be tuned independently. The gap between hinge and the normalized losses is largest at `\gamma = 0.5`, where the unnormalized hinge loss degrades more severely, consistent with the normalization being more important when the margin penalty is strong.

**Effect of `\beta` (rejection sampling temperature) on Reddit TL;DR (Figure 3b):** The paper fixes `\gamma = 0.05` and sweeps `\beta \in \{0, 0.05, 0.5, 5\}` for the sigmoid-norm loss with rso-sample-rank pairs. The proxy reward win rate peaks at `\beta = 0.5`, with sharp degradation on both sides. At `\beta = 0` (equivalent to best-of-N, accepting only the maximum-reward response), proxy win rate drops — a direct demonstration that pure reward exploitation produces a worse policy than the KL-regularized sampling at `\beta = 0.5`. At `\beta = 0.05` (very selective, near-best-of-N), the proxy rate is intermediate. At `\beta = 5` (very permissive, approaching `\pi_{\text{sft}}`), the proxy rate drops back to approximately the sft-sample-rank baseline level (shown as horizontal reference lines in the figure). This `\beta = 5` result validates that the benefit of rejection sampling comes specifically from moving the sampling distribution toward `\pi_{r_\psi}`, not merely from using more compute or a different labeling strategy — when `\beta` is so large that the sampler accepts nearly everything, performance reverts to the sft-sample-rank baseline.

**Preference pair construction: ranking strategy and sample count (Table 2):** The paper compares four aspects of preference pair construction on Reddit TL;DR using sigmoid-norm loss: (a) sft-sample-rank with 8 candidates and first-round ranking (baseline, 88.63% Proxy, 68.51% AutoSxS), (b) sft-sample-rank with 8 candidates and tournament ranking (90.69% Proxy, 68.57% AutoSxS — only +0.06 AutoSxS gain), (c) rso-sample-rank with 8 candidates and first-round ranking (92.37% Proxy, 71.86% AutoSxS), (d) rso-sample-rank with 8 candidates and tournament ranking (93.35% Proxy, 71.69% AutoSxS — slightly worse AutoSxS than first-round), (e) sft-sample-rank with 64 candidates and first-round ranking (88.91% Proxy, 68.84% AutoSxS — marginally better than 8-candidate sft-sample-rank), (f) sft-sample-rank with 64 candidates and tournament ranking (91.14% Proxy, 71.08% AutoSxS). Two findings stand out. First, **tournament ranking consistently improves Proxy Reward but does not improve AutoSxS**: the tournament increases Proxy Reward by 1–3 points across settings but leaves AutoSxS unchanged or slightly worse (rso-8-sample tournament actually decreases AutoSxS from 71.86 to 71.69). This is a form of reward over-optimization — tournament ranking amplifies the reward model's signal in the training data, which the policy learns to exploit for higher Proxy scores, but this exploitation does not translate to the independent AutoSxS metric. Second, **increasing from 8 to 64 candidates (sft-sample-rank) provides only marginal AutoSxS improvement** (68.51 → 68.84 for first-round, 68.57 → 71.08 for tournament), suggesting that the SFT policy's response quality saturates as a source of training signal — adding more SFT samples does not help because the responses are all drawn from the same (suboptimal) distribution. By contrast, **8 rso-sample-rank candidates with first-round ranking outperform 64 sft-sample-rank candidates with tournament ranking** on AutoSxS (71.86 vs. 71.08), showing that the quality of the sampling distribution matters more than the quantity of samples or the sophistication of the ranking procedure.

**Regularization loss in SLiC (Table 5, Appendix A.6):** The original SLiC formulation includes a regularization term `-\lambda \log \pi_\theta(y_{\text{ref}}|x)` encouraging the policy to maintain probability on the SFT target response. The paper sweeps `\lambda \in \{0, 0.5, 5, 50, 500\}` for the hinge-sft-sample-rank configuration on Reddit TL;DR. Adding regularization produces Proxy Reward values ranging from 90.06 to 90.83 and AutoSxS from 67.34 to 67.84 — all essentially identical to the no-regularization baseline (90.15 Proxy, 67.34 AutoSxS). The paper concludes that "there is no strong gain by adding the regularization loss" and drops it from all experiments to simplify the comparison with DPO. This is a consequential finding for the preference optimization literature: it suggests that the KL constraint implicit in the preference loss (through the `\pi_{\text{sft}}` normalization) already provides sufficient regularization, and an explicit SFT likelihood term is redundant when using normalized losses.

**ReST$^\text{EM}$-style revision model (Appendix K, Figure 16 — not present in the provided paper text):** The paper references this in the context of limitations, noting that fine-tuning the revision model with RL-style on-policy data collection (ReST$^\text{EM}$; Singh et al., 2024) caused performance to degrade substantially. This negative result is not detailed in the provided sections but is mentioned as a contrast to RSO's offline approach.

**Reward-ranking model accuracy and transfer to revision model outputs (discussed implicitly across sections):** The paper trains a separate reward-ranking model for each task, with validation accuracy 73.23% on Reddit TL;DR and 69.75% on AnthropicHH (Section 2, footnote 1). The paper also trains a separate Gold Reward model (PaLM 2-S) with 76.07% and 70.18% accuracy respectively (Section 5, footnote 8). The moderate accuracy of the reward-ranking model (~70–76%) is a constraint on RSO's performance — the rejection sampler is guided by an imperfect reward signal, and errors in reward estimation get propagated into the sampling distribution `\pi_{r_\psi}`. The paper notes that distributing the reward model's signal across the fully parallelized sampling stage (rather than sequential online interaction) may help by averaging out some reward noise, but does not directly ablate reward model quality.

**Positional bias in reward model (Section 2, "Reward Model"):** The paper randomly flips response pairs and associated labels during reward-ranking model training to remove positional bias (the tendency to prefer response A over B regardless of content). The effect of this mitigation is not ablated — the paper does not report reward model accuracy with and without flipping — but it is a standard practice in pairwise preference modeling.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments in this paper support its central claims, but the support is stronger for some claims than others, and several important questions remain untested.

**Claim: RSO improves over DPO and SLiC through better sampling distribution.** This claim is well-supported by the consistent ordering across Table 1 (direct → sft-sample-rank → rso-sample-rank monotonic in AutoSxS for every loss and task) and the human evaluation (Table 4, rso-sample-rank preferred 2x over direct). The ablation in Table 2 further supports the mechanism: sampling from `\pi_{r_\psi}` (8 candidates, rso-sample-rank) outperforms sampling more from `\pi_{\text{sft}}` (64 candidates, sft-sample-rank) on AutoSxS, confirming that distribution quality matters more than quantity. However, the paper does not directly measure how close `\pi_{r_\psi}` is to `\pi^*` — there is no oracle experiment where pairs are sampled from the true optimal policy (which would require knowing `\pi^*`, a circular requirement). The evidence is therefore indirect: we know that training on data from `\pi_{r_\psi}` produces a better policy than training on data from `\pi_{\text{sft}}` or `\pi_{\text{unk}}`, but we cannot verify that this is *because* `\pi_{r_\psi}` is closer to `\pi^*`. Alternative explanations are possible — for example, the rejection sampling may simply be acting as a form of data filtering that removes low-quality responses, and any filtering mechanism with similar selectivity would produce similar gains. An ablation comparing rejection sampling to a simpler filtering strategy (e.g., keep the top-8 responses by reward score without probabilistic acceptance) would help distinguish these mechanisms.

**Claim: The reward model is valuable because it enables sampling from `\pi_{r_\psi}`.** This claim is partially supported. The comparison between sft-sample-rank and rso-sample-rank shows that adding rejection sampling (which requires the reward model) provides consistent gains — this establishes that the reward model has value *beyond* just providing training labels (since sft-sample-rank already uses the reward model for labeling). But the paper does not test the counterfactual: what if you used a different, simpler scoring function for rejection sampling? For instance, using the SFT policy's own likelihood as a proxy for quality (rejecting low-probability responses) would test whether any filtering signal provides similar benefits, or whether the reward model specifically matters. The claim that "comparing between two responses (reward) is easier to learn than generating high quality responses (policy)" is an assertion about learning difficulty, not a result demonstrated by experiments. No experiment compares the difficulty of learning a reward model vs. learning a policy of equivalent quality.

**Claim: Loss function choice is less important than sampling distribution.** This claim is well-supported by the data. On Reddit TL;DR, the gap between loss functions at fixed sampling distribution (sigmoid-norm vs. hinge-norm at rso-sample-rank) is 71.86 vs. 70.84 AutoSxS — a 1.02 point difference. The gap between sampling distributions at fixed loss function (direct vs. rso-sample-rank for sigmoid-norm) is 67.72 vs. 71.86 — a 4.14 point difference. On AnthropicHH, the sampling gap is even more dominant: direct to rso-sample-rank for sigmoid-norm is a 16.97 point gap vs. ~2 points between loss functions. However, the conclusion that the field should "stop optimizing loss functions" overstates the evidence. The paper tests only two loss function families (logistic and hinge) on one model family (T5). It is possible that other loss functions — e.g., those incorporating uncertainty in preference labels, or those with adaptive margins — could provide larger gains that rival the sampling distribution effect. The paper's claim is valid for the specific comparison tested but should be stated as "among the loss functions we tested, sampling strategy is the larger lever" rather than a universal statement.

**Claim: RSO scales favorably to larger models.** Supported by Table 3, but with important caveats. The scaling experiment tests exactly one scale jump (T5-large 770M → T5-XXL 11B) and one loss function (sigmoid-norm). On Reddit TL;DR, the benefit of rso-sample-rank over direct shrinks from +4.14 AutoSxS points at 770M to +0.98 at 11B. This could indicate that larger models benefit less from data quality improvements — they may be better at extracting signal from noisy or mismatched data — or it could be a ceiling effect (AutoSxS is already at 85% for direct at 11B, leaving less room for improvement). On AnthropicHH, where AutoSxS for direct is much lower (52.80% at 11B), the benefit of rso-sample-rank remains large (+17.46 points vs. direct, +3.47 points vs. sft-sample-rank). This suggests the diminishing returns story is task-dependent and may be primarily a ceiling effect rather than a fundamental property of scaling. A full scaling analysis would require testing at multiple intermediate model sizes and on tasks with varying direct performance levels.

**Claim: RSO applies across tasks (Reddit TL;DR, AnthropicHH, CNN/DailyMail).** The cross-task transfer to CNN/DailyMail (Table 6) is a genuine test of generalization, and RSO shows gains there. But the three tasks are all text summarization or dialogue — none test code generation, mathematical reasoning, factual question answering, or other domains. The paper's claims about the generality of the approach are based on a narrow task distribution. Furthermore, the CNN/DailyMail experiment uses preference pairs generated from Reddit TL;DR prompts (since CNN/DailyMail has no preference data), meaning the transfer is from one summarization domain to another — a relatively small domain shift. Whether RSO would help for tasks with fundamentally different preference structures (e.g., creative writing, where quality is highly subjective, or code generation, where correctness is binary and verifiable) is untested.

**Missing baselines and comparisons.** Several comparisons that would strengthen the paper are absent. (1) The paper does not compare against online RLHF (PPO) with matched total computation — it argues that DPO is already competitive with RLHF, but a direct comparison would validate whether offline RSO can match or exceed online methods. (2) There is no comparison against simply training on more sft-sample-rank data — if sampling from `\pi_{\text{sft}}` and scoring with the reward model is the bottleneck, perhaps 64 sft candidates are insufficient and 256 or 1024 candidates would close the gap with rso-sample-rank. Table 2 partially addresses this (comparing 8 vs. 64 sft candidates), but only at one scale. (3) The paper doesn't ablate the reward model architecture — a smaller reward model or a non-pairwise reward model (e.g., a pointwise scorer) might provide similar benefits with lower cost. (4) There is no multi-round RSO experiment — the paper runs one round of sampling and training, while methods like ReST iterate. The paper argues that one round is sufficient, but a multi-round comparison (RSO for N rounds vs. ReST for N rounds) would test whether the principled rejection sampling approach compounds across rounds.

**Statistical significance and variance.** The paper does not report confidence intervals or standard deviations for the main results in Table 1, Table 3, or Table 4. The only confidence intervals shown are in Figure 3 (the hyperparameter sweeps), where shaded regions indicate 95% confidence. Without variance estimates on the main results, we cannot assess whether, e.g., the 71.86 vs. 70.84 AutoSxS difference between sigmoid-norm and hinge-norm at rso-sample-rank is statistically reliable or noise. The human evaluation (Table 4) does not report inter-rater agreement or statistical significance of the preference proportions — 48% vs. 21% vs. 10% with 3 raters per task could be fragile depending on the number of tasks and rater consistency. The paper's claim that RSO is preferred "more than 2x as often" would benefit from a confidence interval or significance test.

**The test set size and representativeness.** The Reddit TL;DR test set has 6k examples, but the AnthropicHH test set has only 9k examples, and the human evaluation likely uses a subset (the paper doesn't specify the number of human-evaluated examples). For the AutoSxS metric, the paper uses 8 decoded samples per comparison with 4 flipped orderings — this provides some robustness to decoding randomness but does not address the underlying test set size. The CNN/DailyMail transfer evaluation uses the validation split (13k examples), which is the largest test set. The paper does not discuss test set contamination or overlap between SFT training data and evaluation data.

**The proxy-to-AutoSxS gap as a diagnostic tool.** One of the paper's implicit contributions is demonstrating that proxy metrics (the training reward model's evaluation) can be misleading. Table 1 shows cases where hinge loss achieves very high proxy reward but mediocre AutoSxS (e.g., hinge-rso-sample-rank: 93.36% Proxy, 69.26% AutoSxS). The paper uses this gap to argue for the importance of independent evaluation (Gold Reward, AutoSxS, human). However, the paper doesn't analyze *why* hinge loss is more susceptible to reward hacking than sigmoid-norm or hinge-norm — the margin-based loss might encourage the policy to find sharp decision boundaries that the reward model misclassifies, or the unnormalized variant might exploit the base-rate vulnerability more aggressively. Understanding this mechanism would strengthen the unified framework argument by explaining when each loss function is appropriate.

**Overall assessment.** The experiments provide strong evidence that RSO's sampling strategy improves over DPO and SLiC on the tested tasks and models, with gains that are visible to human evaluators and persist at larger model scales. The core mechanism — moving the sampling distribution from `\pi_{\text{sft}}` or `\pi_{\text{unk}}` toward `\pi_{r_\psi}` — is supported by the monotonic direct → sft-sample-rank → rso-sample-rank ordering that holds across all tested configurations. The limitations are primarily in scope (two tasks, one model family, one scale jump), missing baselines (online RLHF, alternative filtering strategies), and statistical rigor (no confidence intervals on main results). The paper's findings are likely to generalize to other text generation tasks where preference data is available and a reward model can be trained, but the magnitude of the improvement may vary with reward model quality, the gap between `\pi_{\text{sft}}` and `\pi^*`, and the specific loss function used.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for in the Headline Efficiency Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** RSO's sampling strategy requires generating and scoring 64 response candidates per training prompt from the SFT policy, then applying rejection sampling to select 8. This is referenced implicitly throughout Section 5 (the method description) and Appendix A.10 (computational efficiency), where the paper acknowledges that RSO needs "additional computation on sampling from the SFT policy and ranking from the pairwise reward model." The paper explicitly states that "rso-sample-rank accounts for less than 10% of the total training time" (Appendix A.10), framing the overhead as manageable rather than prohibitive. However, this 10% figure is presented without detailed measurement methodology and is an empirical observation specific to the T5-large and T5-XXL training setups used, not a fundamental property. For a practitioner training at a different scale — with a larger policy model, more training data, or a different ratio of policy model size to reward model size — the relative cost of the sampling stage could shift substantially.

**The consequence.** The headline gains in Table 1 (e.g., +4.14 AutoSxS points on Reddit TL;DR for RSO over DPO) are computed assuming the sampling stage cost is negligible relative to policy training. But the sampling stage requires 64 full sequence generations plus approximately 67 reward model comparisons per prompt (63 for scoring against a baseline, plus ~4 for pair labeling; Appendix A.10 Table 7 and derivations). For a training set of 117k prompts (Reddit TL;DR), this is roughly 7.5 million SFT-policy generations and 7.8 million reward model inferences — a non-trivial computational expenditure. The paper does not provide a FLOPs-matched comparison where DPO or SLiC is given extra training epochs or a larger model to compensate for not spending compute on sampling. A fair comparison would ask: if we gave DPO the same total FLOPs budget as RSO (including the sampling stage), would the gap persist? The current comparison gives RSO extra compute for data generation without accounting for it.

A related issue: the 64 SFT candidates are generated at temperature 0.7 with top-k=40 (Section 5 "Method"), and the rejection sampler operates on this fixed candidate pool. The quality of the RSO sampling distribution depends on whether the 64 SFT candidates contain sufficiently many high-reward responses. If `\pi_{\text{sft}}` assigns very low probability to the region around the optimal policy — i.e., if the SFT policy rarely generates genuinely good responses — then even 64 candidates may not provide adequate coverage, and the rejection sampler will be selecting the best of mediocre options rather than approximating `\pi_{r_\psi}`. The paper does not measure the effective sample size or coverage of the SFT candidate pool relative to `\pi_{r_\psi}`.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not conduct a FLOPs-matched comparison between RSO and DPO/SLiC with equivalent total compute. Table 2 provides partial evidence on the sampling quantity question: increasing from 8 to 64 SFT candidates with tournament ranking improves sft-sample-rank AutoSxS from 68.57 to 71.08 (+2.5 points), but 8 RSO candidates with first-round ranking achieve 71.86 — still higher. This suggests that even with fewer total candidates, the RSO distribution provides better training signal. However, it does not address the total cost problem: the sampling stage cost is present regardless of the number of candidates accepted (8), because 64 must still be generated and scored.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the cost in Appendix A.10 but frames it as acceptable rather than problematic: "we only need to sample once for each prompt in the training data. But the training of DPO can go through multiple epochs." It notes that sampling and ranking are "fully parallelizable over the whole training set" while training is "only parallelizable within the batch," and that reward ranking is fast because it decodes only one token. The paper also suggests that prompt caching and advanced serving techniques (Pope et al., 2023) can reduce the effective cost. These are valid mitigations for large-scale industrial settings where parallel inference infrastructure is available, but they do not eliminate the cost — they only reduce wall-clock time. For researchers or practitioners with limited compute budgets, the upfront sampling stage may be a significant fraction of total resources, and the paper provides no guidance on how to trade off sampling budget against training budget optimally. Future work on cheaper difficulty estimation or adaptive candidate counts is not suggested in this context, though the paper acknowledges in Section 1 that "it is critical to balance between the reward exploitation and regularization" — without extending this balance to the economic tradeoff between sampling cost and policy improvement.

### Reward Model Quality Is the Silent Bottleneck, and the Paper's Reward Models Are Moderately Accurate

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire RSO pipeline depends on the pairwise reward-ranking model `\rho_\psi` for two distinct functions: (1) providing the reward scores that guide rejection sampling toward `\pi_{r_\psi}`, and (2) labeling the constructed preference pairs that serve as policy training data. The paper reports that this model achieves 73.23% accuracy on the Reddit TL;DR validation set and 69.75% on the AnthropicHH validation set (Section 2, footnote 1). The Gold Reward model — a separately trained PaLM 2-S used as an independent evaluator — achieves 76.07% and 70.18% respectively (Section 5, footnote 8). These accuracies mean that roughly one in four preference judgments is incorrect.

**The consequence.** Errors in the reward model propagate through the RSO pipeline in two distinct ways. First, in the rejection sampling stage: if the reward model assigns an incorrectly high score to a low-quality response, that response may be accepted with high probability, polluting the sampling distribution `\pi_{r_\psi}` with bad responses that the policy will later learn to prefer. Conversely, if genuinely good responses receive incorrectly low scores, they will be rejected, removing high-quality training signal. Second, in the pair labeling stage: the 4 pairs constructed from the 8 RSO-accepted responses are labeled by the same imperfect reward model. If the reward model mislabels a pair (marking the worse response as preferred), the policy training loss receives a misleading gradient, actively pushing the policy toward worse behavior.

The severity of this error propagation is sensitive to `\beta`. With `\beta = 0.5` (the paper's optimal setting), a response with reward 0.5 below the maximum has acceptance probability `\exp(-0.5/0.5) = \exp(-1) \approx 0.37`. This means that moderately-scoring responses still have a non-trivial chance of acceptance. If the reward model's errors are concentrated in this moderate-reward region (where the model is uncertain and likely to make mistakes), RSO's probabilistic acceptance may actually amplify reward model errors by admitting responses whose scores are inflated by model noise. The paper's ablation in Figure 3b shows that `\beta = 0` (only accept the maximum-reward response, regardless of absolute quality) performs worse than `\beta = 0.5`, which the paper interprets as evidence that pure exploitation overfits reward errors. But `\beta = 0.5` doesn't eliminate the error propagation — it smooths it.

The paper's finding that `\beta = 5` (very permissive, near-SFT) degrades performance back to the sft-sample-rank baseline (Figure 3b) can be interpreted as: when the reward model is almost entirely ignored, performance reverts to the baseline distribution. This implies that the gains at `\beta = 0.5` come specifically from the reward model's signal — and therefore that a worse reward model would produce smaller gains or even negative gains. The paper does not investigate how RSO's performance varies with reward model quality, so practitioners cannot estimate the minimum reward model accuracy needed for RSO to provide benefits.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides reward model validation accuracies (footnotes 1 and 8), and Figure 3b shows that `\beta` tuning matters — both are indirect indicators that reward model quality is important. The gap between Proxy Reward and AutoSxS in Table 1 is an implicit measure of reward model over-optimization: for hinge-rso-sample-rank, Proxy Reward is 93.36% but AutoSxS is only 69.26% — a 24.1-point gap that indicates the policy is exploiting reward model errors. The paper interprets this as evidence that the unnormalized hinge loss is more susceptible to reward hacking (Section 5.2), but it equally indicates that the reward model itself is imperfect enough to be hacked. The Gold Reward metric, which uses an independently trained reward model, shows the same ordering as AutoSxS (rso-sample-rank > sft-sample-rank > direct), suggesting that the evaluation is consistent across reward models — but this doesn't tell us whether a more accurate reward model would yield larger RSO gains.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not systematically address reward model quality. There is no ablation showing RSO performance as a function of reward model accuracy (e.g., by using reward models trained on subsets of the preference data to vary accuracy). The paper does not discuss ensemble reward models, uncertainty-aware rejection sampling (where acceptance probability is scaled by reward model confidence), or iterative reward model improvement. The "Gold Reward" evaluation model is used only for evaluation, not for guiding sampling. The paper notes in Section 2 that the reward model's accuracy can be improved by "solving n reward scores from n² comparisons with a baseline sequence via a constraint optimization setting" but "leaves this for future study." A practitioner deploying RSO would need to train a reward model and hope that its accuracy is sufficient, without guidance from the paper on minimum viable accuracy or how to diagnose reward-model-induced degradation.

### The Revision Model and PRM Search Are Never Combined — and the Single-Round Design Leaves Multi-Round Gains Unexplored

**The assumption or constraint.** RSO is designed and evaluated as a single-round procedure: generate preference pairs from `\pi_{r_\psi}` once, train the policy on those pairs, and stop. This is stated in the method description (Section 3): the rejection sampler generates 8 responses per prompt from an initial pool of 64 SFT candidates, constructs preference pairs, and the policy trains to convergence on this static dataset. There is no mechanism for iterating — after training, the improved policy is not used as a new proposal distribution for another round of rejection sampling, nor is the reward model updated based on the trained policy's outputs. This contrasts with methods like ReST (Gulcehre et al., 2023), which explicitly iterates: sample from the current policy, filter with reward, fine-tune, repeat. The paper compares RSO against a single round of ReST (Section 5.1, Table 1), not against multiple rounds.

**The consequence.** A single round of RSO can only move the policy as far as the initial SFT candidate pool allows. The rejection sampler selects responses from `\pi_{\text{sft}}` that are reweighted toward `\pi_{r_\psi}` — but if `\pi_{r_\psi}` and `\pi_{\text{sft}}` are far apart, the 64 SFT candidates may not adequately cover the high-reward region, limiting how much the trained policy can improve. After training on RSO pairs, the policy `\pi_\theta` should be closer to `\pi^*` than `\pi_{\text{sft}}` was. If this improved policy were then used as the proposal distribution for a second round of rejection sampling (generating candidates from `\pi_\theta` rather than `\pi_{\text{sft}}`, re-ranking with the reward model, and training again), the sampling distribution would move even closer to `\pi^*`. The paper's single-round design leaves these potential compounding gains unexplored.

The paper's comparison with ReST in Table 1 is not a fair test of the multi-round hypothesis. ReST uses cross-entropy loss on filtered responses (behavioral cloning), not preference pair training — so even with multiple rounds, ReST may underperform single-round RSO because the learning signal (single best response vs. comparative pairs) is weaker. A multi-round RSO — where each round uses rejection sampling from the previous round's policy to generate training data for the next round — would combine the principled sampling distribution improvement with iterative refinement. The paper provides no theoretical or empirical analysis of whether such iteration would converge, converge to `\pi^*`, or diverge due to accumulated reward model errors.

There is also a subtler missed opportunity: the paper studies RSO's rejection sampling independently of search and iterative refinement mechanisms. In the broader preference optimization literature, there are two axes for improvement — improving the proposal distribution (what responses are generated) and improving the selection mechanism (how responses are chosen). RSO focuses on the selection axis (rejection sampling guided by reward), but does not combine it with iterative revision of the proposal distribution. An integrated system could use RSO's rejection sampling to select high-quality responses, then fine-tune a revision model that conditions on its own rejected outputs to generate improved candidates — combining the two complementary axes that prior work has studied in isolation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 shows that single-round RSO substantially outperforms single-round ReST and RAFT (both of which are effectively single-round methods). The paper does not run multi-round RSO or compare against multi-round ReST. Appendix A.10 discusses computational efficiency and notes that "RSO can also be done with multiple rounds" in a parenthetical remark (footnote 9), but no experiments support this claim. The scaling results in Table 3 show that RSO benefits T5-XXL as well as T5-large, but do not address whether the gap between RSO and DPO shrinks or grows with scale — if the gap shrinks, multi-round RSO might be necessary to maintain advantages at larger scales.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly positions multi-round RSO as future work in footnote 9: "we pick one round of grow and improve as a fair comparison to one round of RSO, since RSO can also be done with multiple rounds." This is an acknowledgment of the limitation, but it is buried in a footnote and not addressed in the main text. The paper provides no guidance on how multi-round RSO would work — whether to re-train the reward model on the improved policy's outputs (to reduce distribution shift), whether to adjust `\beta` across rounds (starting permissive, becoming more selective), or how many rounds are needed. For a practitioner, the single-round design is simpler to implement, but it leaves open the question of whether further gains are achievable with modest additional computation.

### All Experiments Are on the T5 Model Family with a Single Architecture Class, and Generalization to Autoregressive Decoder-Only Models Is Untested

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses T5 (Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer) models — an encoder-decoder architecture where the encoder processes the full input prompt and the decoder generates the response autoregressively. The policy models are T5-large (770M parameters) and T5-XXL (11B parameters), and the reward-ranking model is T5-XXL. The paper acknowledges this limitation implicitly by not discussing other architectures, but it does not explicitly flag the encoder-decoder assumption. The choice is stated in Section 5: "Starting from a T5-large (770M) SFT policy and a T5-XXL (11B) pairwise reward-ranking model."

**The consequence.** The dominant architecture for large language models in 2024 is the decoder-only transformer (GPT, LLaMA, PaLM, Claude, Gemini families), which differs from T5's encoder-decoder design in several ways that could affect RSO's behavior. First, encoder-decoder models process the full input bidirectionally in the encoder before generating, which may produce different SFT policy distributions — particularly for tasks like summarization where the encoder can attend to the entire document while generating each summary token. Decoder-only models process input and output concatenated left-to-right, which may yield different patterns of errors and different gaps between `\pi_{\text{sft}}` and `\pi^*`. Second, the rejection sampling procedure requires the SFT policy to generate diverse candidate responses at temperature 0.7. The diversity characteristics of encoder-decoder vs. decoder-only architectures under same-temperature sampling are not equivalent — the architectures may have different entropy profiles, affecting how well 64 candidates cover the response space and how selective the rejection sampler can afford to be. Third, T5 models use a different pretraining objective (span corruption) than standard autoregressive language models (next-token prediction), which may affect the base quality of the SFT policy and the nature of the distribution mismatch that RSO addresses.

More practically, the preference optimization literature has shifted heavily toward decoder-only models (LLaMA, Mistral, etc.) for both research and deployment. A practitioner deploying preference optimization today is overwhelmingly likely to be working with a decoder-only model. Without experiments on such architectures, the paper's claims about RSO's effectiveness cannot be assumed to transfer. The improvements shown in Tables 1 and 3 (+4.14 AutoSxS on T5-large, +0.98 on T5-XXL for Reddit TL;DR) are specific to T5 — a decoder-only model of comparable scale might show larger, smaller, or even negative gains from RSO, depending on the interaction between architecture and the distribution mismatch.

The reward-ranking model architecture is also T5-XXL — an encoder-decoder model outputting "A" or "B" tokens. The accuracy of this pairwise classification (73.23% on Reddit TL;DR, 69.75% on AnthropicHH) may not be representative of what can be achieved with decoder-only reward models of comparable scale, which are now standard in the field. A practitioner using a decoder-only reward model with different calibration properties might need different `\beta` and `\gamma` settings, and the optimal values found in Figures 3a and 3b (`\beta = 0.5`, `\gamma = 0.05`) may not transfer.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** There is no evidence — the paper conducts zero experiments on non-T5 architectures. The paper does not discuss the architectural assumption or its potential consequences. The related work section (Section 4) discusses prior methods (DPO, SLiC, RLHF) that have been applied to multiple architectures, but does not connect this to RSO's architectural scope. The cross-task generalization experiment (CNN/DailyMail, Table 6) tests domain transfer, not architectural transfer.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper makes no claim about architectural generality, but it also does not warn readers that the results may be architecture-specific. The unified framework (logistic regression vs. SVM for preference optimization, Section 3.1) is mathematically architecture-agnostic — it depends only on the ability to compute `\log \pi_\theta(y|x)` and `\log \pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x)` for any sequence model — so the theoretical framing should apply to decoder-only models. However, the practical effectiveness depends on the empirical gap between `\pi_{\text{sft}}` and `\pi^*` and the quality of the rejection sampling approximation, both of which may differ across architectures. Future work would need to replicate the core experiments (Table 1, Figures 3a/3b, Table 4) on at least one decoder-only model to establish architectural robustness.

### Hard Problems — Where the SFT Policy Has Near-Zero Probability of Generating Good Responses — May See Minimal or Negative Gains from RSO

**The assumption or constraint.** RSO's rejection sampling can only select responses that exist in the SFT candidate pool. If the SFT policy `\pi_{\text{sft}}` assigns extremely low probability to the region of response space where `\pi^*` concentrates — i.e., if the SFT policy is fundamentally incapable of generating good responses for certain prompts — then no amount of rejection sampling can recover the optimal policy from SFT-generated candidates. The acceptance criterion `\exp((r - r_{\text{max}})/\beta)` reweights existing candidates but cannot create new responses from regions where `\pi_{\text{sft}}(y|x) \approx 0`. This is the same structural limitation identified in the reference paper (Liu et al., 2024) for test-time compute: "if the base model's pass@1 is near zero on a problem class, no amount of search or revision will help — there are no correct solutions in the proposal distribution to find or refine." RSO's proposal distribution is `\pi_{\text{sft}}`, and the rejection sampler is a selection mechanism, not a generation mechanism.

**The consequence.** On prompts where the SFT policy performs poorly — producing summaries that miss key information, dialogue responses that are unhelpful or off-topic — RSO may provide limited or zero improvement. The rejection sampler will select the best of the SFT candidates, but if all 64 candidates are of poor quality, the "best" is still poor. Training on preference pairs constructed from these poor responses may even be harmful: the reward model may label trivial differences between bad responses as meaningful preferences (e.g., a slightly less incoherent summary is "preferred" over a completely incoherent one), and the policy will learn to optimize these spurious distinctions rather than learning what makes a genuinely good response. This is a form of **Cantillon effect in learning**: the policy gets better at distinguishing degrees of badness without ever seeing what good looks like.

The paper does not stratify results by prompt difficulty or SFT policy performance. We do not know whether RSO's gains in Table 1 are concentrated on "easy" prompts (where `\pi_{\text{sft}}` already performs reasonably and the reward model can fine-tune preferences among good candidates) or are uniform across all prompts. If the gains come primarily from easy prompts where the SFT candidate quality is already high, then RSO's practical value is bounded by the SFT policy's baseline capability. For prompts where the SFT policy truly struggles — those requiring complex reasoning, factual precision, or stylistic sophistication far beyond SFT training — RSO may offer no benefit or even regress.

This limitation is particularly relevant for AnthropicHH dialogue, where the SFT policy is trained only on positive responses from preference pairs (Section 5 "Tasks"). If the SFT policy's dialogue quality is generally low on certain types of queries (e.g., those requiring specific factual knowledge, multi-turn reasoning, or nuanced social judgment), RSO's rejection sampler is operating on a low-quality candidate pool, and the large gap between direct and sft-sample-rank on this task (AutoSxS 24.01 → 39.56, Table 1) may partly reflect that direct (human-labeled pairs) contains preference pairs that the SFT policy cannot reproduce, while sft-sample-rank constructs pairs from the SFT policy's own output distribution — a form of distribution matching that helps when the SFT policy is weak, not just when it is strong.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not report performance stratified by any difficulty measure. There is no analysis of which prompts benefit most from RSO, or whether RSO ever reduces performance on individual prompts. The qualitative examples in Figures 4 and 5 show RSO outperforming baselines, but these are selected examples (likely favorable cases) and do not represent the full distribution. The proxy-to-AutoSxS gap in Table 1 provides indirect evidence: on AnthropicHH, hinge-rso-sample-rank achieves 82.21% Proxy but only 32.56% AutoSxS, suggesting the policy has learned preferences that the reward model endorses but an independent evaluator does not — consistent with the Cantillon effect where the policy optimizes for spurious distinctions among mediocre responses.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the relationship between SFT policy quality and RSO effectiveness, does not propose difficulty-stratified evaluation, and does not suggest adaptive strategies (e.g., falling back to direct human labels for prompts where SFT candidate quality is poor). This is a significant gap because it means a practitioner cannot predict whether RSO will help on their specific task without running the full experiment — there is no diagnostic to determine if the SFT policy is "good enough" for RSO to work.

### The AnthropicHH Task Shows Reward Hacking That RSO Mitigates but Does Not Eliminate, and the Mechanism Is Not Understood

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper identifies reward hacking — the policy learning to exploit the training reward model without producing genuinely better outputs — as a central challenge in preference optimization (Section 1, discussing DPO and best-of-N). RSO is presented as a method that mitigates reward hacking by maintaining the KL regularization through `\beta > 0` in rejection sampling, preventing the collapse to pure reward maximization. However, the results in Table 1 reveal that reward hacking persists in RSO, albeit at reduced levels, and the paper does not fully diagnose the conditions under which it occurs.

**The consequence.** On AnthropicHH, the gap between Proxy Reward and AutoSxS for RSO configurations is substantial. RSO-sigmoid-norm achieves 86.94% Proxy Reward but only 40.98% AutoSxS — a 46-point gap. RSO-hinge-norm achieves 84.44% Proxy but only 38.58% AutoSxS — a similar gap. While these AutoSxS numbers are significantly better than direct (24.01%) and sft-sample-rank (39.56%), the fact that the Proxy metric is more than double the AutoSxS metric means the policy is still substantially over-optimized to the training reward model. A practitioner observing the Proxy Reward during training would conclude the model is performing excellently (86.94% win rate against SFT baseline), yet the actual quality as judged by AutoSxS is below 41% — worse than chance if the baseline were strong. This is a dangerous disconnect between training signal and true quality.

The reward hacking on AnthropicHH is not uniform across methods. The unnormalized hinge loss (hinge) is particularly vulnerable: hinge-rso-sample-rank achieves 82.21% Proxy but only 32.56% AutoSxS — a smaller gap than sigmoid-norm in absolute terms but a lower AutoSxS ceiling. This suggests that the hinge loss, even with RSO's improved sampling, is more susceptible to finding and exploiting reward model vulnerabilities. The paper notes this pattern (Section 5.2: "Hinge loss shows reward hacking in Reddit TL;DR dataset with higher Proxy Reward win rates but lower AutoSxS than other losses") but does not explain *why* the hinge loss is more vulnerable. The margin-based nature of hinge loss may encourage the policy to find sharp decision boundaries that happen to align with reward model errors, while the probabilistic sigmoid loss maintains smoother boundaries that are more robust to label noise. Without understanding this mechanism, a practitioner choosing between sigmoid-norm and hinge-norm has no principled basis for the decision — the paper simply reports that sigmoid-norm is slightly better and both are acceptable.

The AnthropicHH vs. Reddit TL;DR comparison deepens the puzzle. On Reddit TL;DR, the Proxy-to-AutoSxS gaps are much smaller: RSO-sigmoid-norm achieves 92.37% Proxy and 71.86% AutoSxS — a 20.5-point gap, less than half the AnthropicHH gap. The reward model accuracy is similar across tasks (73.23% for Reddit TL;DR, 69.75% for AnthropicHH), so the larger gap on AnthropicHH cannot be explained by reward model quality alone. It may reflect that dialogue responses have higher variance in what constitutes "good" quality, making the reward model's judgments less consistent and easier to exploit; or that the AnthropicHH SFT policy produces responses that are further from human preferences, creating a larger space for reward hacking; or that the AnthropicHH preference data contains systematic biases that the reward model amplifies. The paper does not investigate these hypotheses.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 provides the raw numbers revealing the reward hacking pattern. The ablation in Figure 3b shows that `\beta` optimization helps: at `\beta = 0` (pure best-of-N), proxy win rate is lower than at `\beta = 0.5`, suggesting that pure exploitation is worse than regularized sampling. But the comparison is only on Proxy Reward, not AutoSxS — so we only know that `\beta = 0.5` optimizes the proxy metric, not that it minimizes reward hacking as measured by the Proxy-to-AutoSxS gap. The decoupling of `\beta` and `\gamma` (Figures 3a, 3b) is presented as a practical improvement but may have a hidden cost: by tuning `\gamma` independently, the loss function's sensitivity to preference violations can amplify reward model errors in ways that the original DPO coupling (`\gamma = \beta`) would have constrained.

**Mitigation status.** Partial. The paper uses multiple evaluation metrics (Proxy, Gold, AutoSxS, Human) specifically to detect reward hacking, and validates RSO's improvements through human evaluation (Table 4) which confirms that the AutoSxS gains reflect genuine quality improvements. But the paper does not propose mechanisms to further reduce reward hacking — no adversarial training of the reward model, no uncertainty quantification in reward scores, no ensemble reward models, no dynamic `\beta` adjustment based on reward model confidence. The paper's recommendation to use normalized loss functions (hinge-norm vs. hinge) is a partial mitigation, the mechanism of which is not fully explained beyond the base-rate argument. A practitioner deploying RSO on a task with similar reward-model-accuracy to AnthropicHH (~70%) should expect substantial reward hacking and must invest in independent evaluation (like AutoSxS or human studies) to detect it — the training proxy metric will be misleadingly optimistic.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper does not introduce a new loss function, a new model architecture, or a new theoretical framework for preference optimization. What it does — and this is what makes it a genuine conceptual shift rather than an incremental improvement — is **redefine what the field should consider the primary lever for improving offline preference optimization.** The lever is not the optimization algorithm. It is the distribution from which training pairs are sampled.

Prior to this work, the conversation around DPO and SLiC was overwhelmingly about the loss function. DPO's contribution was framed as solving the RLHF objective in closed form and eliminating the reward model. SLiC's contribution was framed as a contrastive calibration loss that directly optimizes sequence likelihoods toward preferences. The implicit shared assumption was: if you have a preference dataset and a good loss function, you are doing preference optimization correctly. Differences in performance between methods were attributed to algorithmic properties — the sigmoid loss being better calibrated, the hinge loss providing a clearer margin, the KL constraint being handled more elegantly.

RSO refutes this framing by showing something that, in retrospect, is statistically obvious but had been invisible to the field: **the maximum likelihood estimator of `$\pi^*$` requires training samples from `$\pi^*$`.** And none of the existing methods provide them. DPO-direct uses human labels from unknown policies. SLiC-sample-rank uses samples from the SFT policy. Both are solving a mismatched estimation problem — fitting a model on data from the wrong distribution.

The impact of this reframing is visible in the empirical results. On Reddit TL;DR, moving from direct to rso-sample-rank improves AutoSxS by 4.14 points for the same sigmoid-norm loss — larger than the entire gap between sigmoid-norm and hinge-norm at any fixed sampling distribution. On AnthropicHH, the sampling strategy gap is 16.97 AutoSxS points for sigmoid-norm, dwarfing the ~2-point differences between loss functions. The paper's key message is not "RSO is better than DPO" but rather **"where you get your training pairs matters more than how you weight them."** This is a hypothesis that the field can now test, refine, and extend — and it shifts the research agenda from loss engineering to data engineering.

**This is a reframing, not a paradigm shift.** The paper does not overturn the KL-constrained reward maximization framework or the Bradley-Terry preference model. It operates entirely within these frameworks. What changes is the diagnosis of the bottleneck. DPO argued that the bottleneck was algorithmic complexity — that RLHF was too complicated and the reward model was an unnecessary intermediate. RSO argues that the bottleneck is statistical — that the data is from the wrong distribution, and the reward model is necessary precisely because it enables sampling from the right one. This is a correction to DPO's narrative, not a rejection of its mathematics. DPO's derivation of Equation (2) remains correct. RSO's contribution is to point out that fitting Equation (2) on direct human labels is not the MLE of `$\pi^*$`, and to provide a practical method for generating better training data.

**The paper reconciles a latent contradiction in the preference optimization literature.** DPO claimed that "your language model is secretly a reward model" — that the implicit reward learned during policy training is sufficient, and the explicit reward model is dispensable. RSO shows that this is true only if you never need to sample from `$\pi^*$`. The implicit reward in DPO is entangled with the policy being trained — it cannot guide data generation because evaluating it requires the partition function `$Z(x)$`, which is intractable. So DPO's claim is correct in a narrow sense (the policy does implicitly represent a reward function) but practically misleading (that implicit reward cannot be used for the sampling problem that turns out to be the performance bottleneck). RSO resolves this by showing that the explicit reward model is valuable not because it provides better labels (sft-sample-rank already uses it for labeling) but because it is **operationalizable** — you can run it on thousands of candidate responses to guide rejection sampling toward `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`.

**The paper also clarifies a confusion about "rejection sampling" in the RLHF literature.** Prior work (Bai et al., 2022; Touvron et al., 2023) used "rejection sampling" to mean best-of-N: generate N responses, score them, return the best. RSO shows (Theorem 1) that this is a special case of statistical rejection sampling when `$\beta \to 0$` — the unregularized limit where the KL penalty vanishes and the policy collapses to a point mass on the max-reward response. By using `$\beta > 0$`, RSO generalizes best-of-N to sample from the full KL-regularized optimal policy `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`, maintaining diversity and providing robustness against reward model errors. The ablation in Figure 3b, where `$\beta = 0$` performs worse than `$\beta = 0.5$`, empirically validates that this generalization is not just theoretically cleaner but practically better. This clarification means future work cannot use "rejection sampling" to mean both things without disambiguation — the best-of-N procedure and statistical rejection sampling are related but distinct, with different `$\beta$` regimes, different output distributions, and different robustness properties.

**Research directions that become more attractive:**

- **Data-centric preference optimization** becomes the central research thrust. Rather than designing new loss functions, the focus shifts to methods for generating higher-quality preference pairs: better reward models, better sampling strategies, active learning to identify which pairs are most informative, and iterative refinement of the sampling distribution.
- **Reward model quality** becomes a first-class concern. Since RSO's gains depend on the reward model's ability to guide sampling toward `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`, improving reward model accuracy, calibration, and robustness to distribution shift directly translates to better policies. This elevates reward model research from a supporting role to a central one.
- **The connection between sampling and estimation** in preference optimization becomes an explicit object of study. The paper's three-level comparison (direct, sft-sample-rank, rso-sample-rank) provides a template for analyzing any preference optimization method in terms of its sampling distribution gap.

**Research directions that become less attractive:**

- **Loss function engineering for its own sake.** The paper shows that sigmoid-norm and hinge-norm perform similarly when given the same data (~1-2 AutoSxS points difference at T5-large scale). Unless a new loss function addresses a fundamentally different aspect of the problem (e.g., robust estimation under reward model uncertainty), marginal loss function improvements are unlikely to match the gains from data quality improvements.
- **Reward-model-free preference optimization as a goal.** DPO's elimination of the reward model was celebrated as a simplification. RSO shows that the reward model provides unique value — enabling sampling from `$\pi_{r_\psi}$` — that the implicit reward cannot replicate. Future methods that claim to eliminate the reward model must also address the sampling distribution problem, or they will face the same statistical mismatch that RSO identifies.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Direct measurement of the sampling distribution gap and its causal effect on policy quality.** The paper argues that RSO works because `$\pi_{r_\psi}$` is closer to `$\pi^*$` than `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` or `$\pi_{\text{unk}}$`. But this claim is supported only indirectly — by the monotonic ordering in Table 1 — rather than by direct measurement. A follow-up study could construct a controlled experiment: train a reward model on human preferences to convergence, then synthetically generate "optimal policies" with known `$\beta$` values by sampling from `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` reweighted by the reward model at different temperatures `$\beta \in \{0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0\}$`. For each `$\beta$`, measure the KL divergence between the sampling distribution and the true `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`. Then train a policy on preference pairs sampled from each distribution and measure the resulting policy's true expected reward. The hypothesis: policy quality should improve monotonically as the sampling distribution approaches `$\pi_{r_\psi}$` (decreasing KL), but with diminishing returns once the KL is below some threshold. This would directly validate the paper's central mechanism and identify the point where further distribution improvement no longer helps.

**Iterated RSO with on-policy reward model updates.** RSO as presented is single-round: sample from `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$`, reject toward `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`, train, and stop. But after one round of training, the new policy `$\pi_\theta^{(1)}$` is closer to `$\pi^*$` than `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` was. A natural extension is multi-round RSO: use `$\pi_\theta^{(t)}$` as the proposal distribution for round `$t+1$`, generate candidates, apply rejection sampling (possibly with a re-trained or fine-tuned reward model on `$\pi_\theta^{(t)}$` outputs), and train `$\pi_\theta^{(t+1)}$` on the resulting pairs. The key question is whether this converges and how many rounds are beneficial. A strong experiment would run 3-5 rounds of RSO on Reddit TL;DR and AnthropicHH, measuring AutoSxS and human evaluation after each round, and compare against multi-round ReST and multi-round RAFT at equivalent total compute. The paper's negative experience with ReST$^\text{EM}$-style training (Appendix K, where on-policy data collection degraded performance) suggests that the reward model must be carefully updated to avoid compounding errors — the follow-up should test both fixed-reward-model and updated-reward-model variants, with the hypothesis that updating the reward model on the current policy's outputs is necessary to prevent the sampler from exploiting reward model blind spots that were irrelevant under `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` but become salient as the policy improves.

**Reward model quality thresholds and ensemble rejection sampling.** The paper's reward-ranking model achieves 73.23% accuracy on Reddit TL;DR and 69.75% on AnthropicHH. A natural question: how does RSO performance scale with reward model accuracy? A follow-up could train reward models of varying quality — by training on subsets of the preference data (10%, 25%, 50%, 100%), by varying model size (T5-small, T5-base, T5-large, T5-XXL), or by using reward models at different stages of training — and measure RSO's AutoSxS gains as a function of reward model validation accuracy. The hypothesis: there exists a minimum accuracy threshold below which RSO provides negative gains (the reward model misguides the sampler toward bad responses), and above which gains increase monotonically with accuracy. This would provide practitioners with a diagnostic: before deploying RSO, ensure your reward model exceeds accuracy `$X$` on a held-out validation set. A second, related experiment would test ensemble rejection sampling: train multiple independent reward models (e.g., 3-5 T5-XXL models with different random seeds), use the mean reward or the minimum reward (conservative) as the acceptance criterion in Algorithm 1, and measure whether ensemble rejection sampling reduces the Proxy-to-AutoSxS gap compared to single-model RSO. The hypothesis is that reward model errors are partially uncorrelated across ensemble members, so an ensemble provides a more robust sampling signal.

**RSO with decoder-only architectures at scale.** All experiments in the paper use T5 encoder-decoder models, but the field has converged on decoder-only architectures (LLaMA, Mistral, GPT). A replication study on a modern decoder-only model stack — e.g., using Llama-2-7B as the SFT policy and a fine-tuned Llama-2-7B as the reward-ranking model — would establish whether RSO's benefits transfer across architectures. The experiment should replicate Table 1 (direct vs. sft-sample-rank vs. rso-sample-rank with sigmoid-norm and hinge-norm losses) on at least two tasks (summarization and dialogue, to match the paper's scope), with AutoSxS and human evaluation. A critical measurement is whether the gap between direct and rso-sample-rank is larger, smaller, or similar to the T5 results. If the gap is smaller, it may indicate that decoder-only SFT policies have different error characteristics (perhaps the distribution mismatch is less severe, or the SFT policy is already closer to `$\pi^*$`), which would bound RSO's practical value. If the gap is larger, it would strengthen the case that RSO is broadly applicable. A further scaling dimension: test RSO at multiple policy sizes (1B, 7B, 13B, 70B) to map how the benefit from sampling distribution improvement scales with model capacity, extending the paper's T5-large to T5-XXL comparison (Table 3) to a wider range and modern architectures.

**Combining RSO with iterative revision or on-policy refinement of the proposal distribution.** The paper studies RSO's rejection sampling as a selection mechanism — choosing among SFT-generated candidates — but never combines it with mechanisms that improve the candidates themselves. A natural integration is with iterative revision models (as studied in the companion preference optimization literature): use RSO's rejection sampler to select high-quality responses, then fine-tune a revision model that conditions on its own lower-quality outputs to generate improved candidates. In a second stage, the revision model replaces `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` as the proposal distribution, generating higher-quality candidate pools from which rejection sampling can select even better responses. A concrete experiment: on Reddit TL;DR, train a revision model following the procedure from the companion paper (fine-tuning on sequences of incorrect-then-correct responses with edit-distance-based pairing), then use this revision model as the proposal distribution for RSO. Compare against: (a) RSO from the SFT policy (the paper's current method), (b) revision model without RSO (selecting the final revision output), and (c) a combined system where RSO selects among revision model outputs. The hypothesis is that combining improved proposal (revision) with improved selection (RSO) yields gains beyond either alone, because they operate on complementary axes — the revision model moves the proposal distribution toward higher-quality regions, and rejection sampling selects the best from those regions.

**Difficulty-stratified analysis of RSO gains.** The paper reports aggregate AutoSxS improvements but does not analyze which types of prompts benefit most from RSO's improved sampling. This is analogous to the difficulty-stratified analysis that revealed the true structure of test-time compute scaling. A follow-up could stratify the Reddit TL;DR and AnthropicHH test sets by the SFT policy's baseline performance: for each prompt, measure the SFT policy's average reward score against the baseline response (or some proxy for SFT quality), then bin prompts into quintiles and measure RSO's improvement over DPO in each bin. The hypothesis: RSO provides the largest gains on medium-difficulty prompts, where the SFT policy sometimes but not always generates good responses, because the rejection sampler can selectively amplify the good ones. On very easy prompts (SFT almost always good), RSO provides minimal gains because the SFT distribution is already close to optimal — the sampling mismatch is small. On very hard prompts (SFT almost never good), RSO provides zero or negative gains because the candidate pool contains no good responses to select — the sampler is choosing among bad options. If confirmed, this stratification would provide practitioners with a diagnostic for when RSO is worth the computational cost, and would suggest adaptive strategies (use RSO for medium-difficulty prompts, direct human labels for hard prompts, and the SFT policy directly for easy prompts).

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-effective alignment of medium-scale language models for production tasks.** A team deploying a summarization or dialogue model at moderate scale (e.g., 7B-13B parameters) faces a choice: use online RLHF with its complexity and instability, use DPO with its simplicity but potential data mismatch, or invest in a larger model with simpler fine-tuning. RSO offers a third path: keep the medium-scale model, invest moderate compute in generating RSO preference pairs (64 SFT generations + reward model scoring per prompt, which the paper reports as <10% of training time), and achieve alignment quality that would otherwise require a larger model or online RL. The paper's numbers support this: RSO on T5-large (770M) achieves 71.86 AutoSxS on Reddit TL;DR summarization, while DPO on T5-XXL (11B) achieves 85.03 — a gap of ~13 points. If a 7B RSO-trained model closes even half that gap relative to a 13B DPO-trained model, the savings in inference cost (smaller model) and training complexity (no RL) could be substantial. The specific benefit is a principled, reproducible offline training recipe with strong alignment quality at a given parameter budget.

**Improving data generation for self-improvement and distillation pipelines.** When using language models to generate training data for themselves or for smaller student models, the quality and diversity of generated responses directly determine the downstream model quality. RSO's rejection sampling provides a principled replacement for ad hoc filtering heuristics (like ReST's 0.7 threshold or RAFT's best-response selection). A practitioner generating training data at scale — e.g., producing millions of dialogue responses for fine-tuning a customer-support chatbot — can use RSO's rejection sampler to select high-quality responses from a larger candidate pool, then train the deployment model on preference pairs constructed from these selected responses. The paper's numbers suggest that training on pairs from `$\pi_{r_\psi}$` rather than `$\pi_{\text{sft}}$` provides ~2-4 AutoSxS points improvement on summarization and ~1-17 points on dialogue (Table 1) — gains that compound when the resulting policy is used to generate data for the next round of improvement. The specific benefit is a quality uplift in self-training loops without the instability of online RL, using only offline sampling and a trained reward model.

**Reward model development as a standalone investment with compounding returns.** The paper's finding that RSO's gains depend on the reward model's ability to guide sampling elevates reward model quality from an evaluation convenience to a central driver of policy improvement. For organizations investing in preference optimization infrastructure, this suggests that resources should be allocated to building the best possible reward model — larger scale, better calibration, ensemble methods, adversarial training — because that investment pays off across all downstream policy training. A 5% improvement in reward model accuracy could translate to a 2-3 point AutoSxS gain through better rejection sampling, which might exceed the gain from doubling the policy model size. The paper provides the mechanism (rejection sampling toward `$\pi_{r_\psi}$`) and the evidence (Figure 3b, where `$\beta$` optimization matters), making a concrete case for reward model investment that practitioners can justify to stakeholders with specific numbers.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does articulate a clear tradeoff against specific named alternatives, grounded in its experimental comparisons against DPO-direct, SLiC-direct, SLiC-sample-rank, RAFT, and ReST. The decision rule, drawn from the paper's results and limitations, is:

- **Prefer RSO over DPO-direct or SLiC-direct when you can train or access a reward model** with reasonable accuracy (the paper's reward-ranking models achieve ~70-76% validation accuracy) and your SFT policy has non-trivial probability of generating good responses for a meaningful fraction of your prompts. The paper shows consistent AutoSxS improvements of 4-17 points across two tasks with T5-large, and human evaluators prefer RSO outputs more than 2x as often as direct-baseline outputs (Table 4).

- **Prefer RSO over SLiC-sample-rank when the additional cost of rejection sampling** (64 SFT generations + ~67 reward model comparisons per prompt, which the paper reports as <10% of training time) is acceptable relative to your compute budget. The paper shows that 8 RSO-sampled candidates with first-round ranking (71.86 AutoSxS) outperform 64 SFT-sampled candidates with tournament ranking (71.08 AutoSxS) on Reddit TL;DR (Table 2) — RSO achieves better results with fewer training pairs by using a better sampling distribution.

- **Prefer RSO over online RLHF (PPO) when training stability and infrastructure simplicity matter more than closing the last few points of alignment quality.** The paper does not run PPO baselines, but it notes (Section 1) that RLHF "introduces notable complexities into the training process, including a reward model, a policy model, a reference policy, and a value model" and "limits the maximum feasible size of a model due to memory constraints." RSO requires only the policy model and the reward model during training, and sampling/ranking are fully parallelizable and offline. For teams without RL infrastructure, RSO provides a simpler path with competitive alignment quality.

- **Prefer DPO-direct or SLiC-direct when you cannot train a reward model** (e.g., insufficient preference data, no compute budget for reward model training, or domains where reward model accuracy would be unacceptably low) and must work directly from the available human preference pairs. The paper's "direct" baselines represent this fallback option, and they still provide non-trivial alignment — 67.72 AutoSxS on Reddit TL;DR for DPO-direct with T5-large (Table 1) — but substantially below RSO's 71.86.

- **Prefer scaling the SFT policy or collecting more human preference data over adopting RSO when the SFT policy's baseline quality is very poor** for a large fraction of your prompts (difficulty bin 5 in the test-time compute analogy). RSO's rejection sampler can only select among SFT-generated candidates — if those candidates are consistently bad, no amount of reward-guided selection will help. The paper does not stratify by SFT performance, but this limitation follows directly from the rejection sampling mechanism. A practitioner observing that the SFT policy rarely produces acceptable responses should invest in better SFT training before applying RSO.
