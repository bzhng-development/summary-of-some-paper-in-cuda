# NUDGERL: Efficient Strategy-Guided Exploration for RLVR

**ArXiv:** [2605.15726](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.15726)

## 🎯 Pitch

GRPO wastes compute by repeatedly sampling the same reasoning modes—until now. NUDGERL injects lightweight strategy hints to force diverse exploration, matching the performance of systems using 8× more rollouts without any oracle supervision. This structured nudging even beats methods that cheat with privileged solution prefixes, proving you can explore smarter, not just harder.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **NUDGERL**, a framework for structured and diversity-driven exploration in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) that addresses the fundamental bottleneck of sampling inefficiency — the policy can only improve on trajectories it has already generated, and brute-force rollout scaling becomes computationally prohibitive for discovering rare reasoning modes. The approach operates through two named mechanisms: **Strategy Nudging**, which conditions each rollout on lightweight strategy-level contexts (e.g., "Pythagorean theorem" or "Parity of sums" appended as hints) to deliberately force the model into diverse reasoning trajectories it would otherwise ignore under naive sampling, and an **Inter-Intra Group Advantage** decomposition paired with a distillation-augmented objective that transfers discovered behaviors back to the context-free base policy. Empirically, NUDGERL with only 8 rollouts per prompt matches or surpasses standard GRPO using up to 8× larger rollout budgets (e.g., 0.489 vs. 0.487 average pass@1 on Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 across five challenging math benchmarks — matching GRPO-32 with only GRPO-8's budget), while also outperforming oracle-guided baselines that use privileged solution prefixes, establishing that structured, context-driven exploration can substitute for both brute-force sampling and feasibility-oriented supervision signals without requiring expensive oracle data.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RLVR Is Fundamentally Limited by Exploration

The paper tackles a deceptively simple but critical bottleneck in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR): **the policy can only improve on reasoning trajectories it has already sampled**. This is not merely a matter of needing "more data" — it is a structural limitation built into the RLVR training loop. During training, the policy generates a batch of solution attempts (rollouts) for each problem, receives binary reward signals (correct or incorrect) from an automated verifier, and updates its parameters to increase the probability of trajectories that led to success. If the policy never samples a particular correct reasoning path — even one that lies within its capability envelope — that path contributes nothing to learning. The policy's probability mass on those unsampled but potentially correct trajectories acts as dead weight, exerting a drag on optimization rather than a driving force.

The paper formalizes this drag through a characterization borrowed from Hu et al. (2025). The expected one-step performance improvement in RLVR, denoted $\Delta Q_{\text{pos}}$, decomposes into three terms (Equation 3 in the paper):

$$\Delta Q_{\text{pos}} = \frac{\eta}{N} \left( (1 - S_R) Q_{\text{neg}} A^2 + S_R Q_{\text{pos}} B^2 + S_R (Q_{\text{pos}} U_{\text{neg},2} - Q_{\text{neg}} U_{\text{pos},2}) \right)$$

Here, $Q_{\text{pos}}$ and $Q_{\text{neg}} = 1 - Q_{\text{pos}}$ represent the total probability mass assigned to correct and incorrect tokens respectively, $A^2$ and $B^2$ are the second moments of sampled correct and incorrect tokens, $U_{\text{pos},2}$ and $U_{\text{neg},2}$ are the second moments of *unsampled* correct and incorrect tokens, and $S_R \in [0,1]$ captures the net reward contribution from sampled tokens.

The first two terms are always non-negative — they drive learning forward. The critical problem lies in the third term: **$- S_R Q_{\text{neg}} U_{\text{pos},2}$**. Because incorrect tokens typically dominate the probability mass ($Q_{\text{neg}} \gg Q_{\text{pos}}$), and because $U_{\text{pos},2}$ represents correct trajectories the model has probability mass on but never generates, this negative term can be large. It acts as a penalty that actively hinders performance improvement. The model "knows" about correct reasoning strategies in the sense that it assigns them non-zero probability, but because it never actually samples them during training, they become an anchor rather than a sail.

This is the **exploration bottleneck** in its most precise form: not that the model lacks capability, but that its sampling distribution fails to surface that capability during the finite, computationally bounded process of RL training.

### Why This Problem Matters

The significance of this bottleneck operates on multiple levels — theoretical, practical, and economic.

**Theoretical significance.** RLVR has emerged as *the* dominant paradigm for post-training reasoning improvement in LLMs. Methods like Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) — introduced by Shao et al. (2024) and adopted by DeepSeekMath, Kimi K1.5, and numerous other systems — have demonstrated that models can bootstrap their reasoning capabilities from binary reward signals alone, without dense human supervision or step-level annotations. This has made RLVR the engine behind the rapid progress on mathematical reasoning benchmarks over the past two years.

However, the exploration bottleneck represents a fundamental ceiling on what RLVR can achieve. If the policy cannot efficiently explore the space of reasoning strategies, then no amount of clever advantage estimation, clipping, or normalization can overcome that gap — the learning signal simply doesn't exist for unsampled trajectories. Understanding and addressing this bottleneck is therefore essential to pushing the frontier of what RLVR-trained models can accomplish, particularly on hard problems where correct trajectories are genuinely rare.

**Practical significance.** In real-world deployment, the exploration bottleneck manifests as **diminishing returns from increased compute**. The naive solution — generate more rollouts per prompt — does help, but with rapidly decaying efficiency. As the paper notes (Equation 4), the expected unsampled probability mass for tokens with probabilities $\{p_i\}$ after $N$ draws is:

$$\sum_i p_i^2 (1 - p_i)^N$$

For tokens with small $p_i$ — the rare, long-tail correct trajectories that represent genuinely novel reasoning strategies — $(1-p_i)^N$ decays extremely slowly. To halve the unsampled mass for a token with $p_i = 0.01$, you need roughly 69 rollouts; to halve it again, another 69. Fully covering long-tail trajectories requires *exponentially* growing budgets. In practice, this means that scaling from 8 to 64 rollouts per prompt (an 8× compute increase) may yield only marginal improvements, and in some cases, as the paper demonstrates, actually degrades performance due to training instability from imbalanced group statistics (GRPO drops from 0.487 at 32 rollouts to 0.451 at 64 rollouts on Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507; Table 1).

**Economic and accessibility implications.** The diminishing returns of rollout scaling mean that pushing RLVR performance forward requires increasingly expensive compute — a trend that advantages well-resourced industrial labs and disadvantages academic groups and smaller organizations. A method that achieves equivalent or better performance with 8× fewer rollouts directly translates to 8× lower training cost, democratizing access to state-of-the-art RLVR training. Moreover, it shifts the conversation from "how much compute can you afford" to "how intelligently can you explore," which is a more scientifically productive framing.

### Prior Approaches and Their Limitations

The paper identifies three broad families of prior work addressing the exploration problem, each with well-characterized shortcomings.

#### Approach 1: Brute-Force Rollout Scaling

The most straightforward approach is simply generating more rollouts per prompt. This has been empirically validated — increasing the number of sampled trajectories increases the probability of discovering rare correct solutions, and prior work (Hu et al., 2025) has shown that this can improve performance. Several major RLVR systems operate at substantial rollout scales (32–64 rollouts per prompt is common in recent work).

**Where it falls short.** The paper identifies three problems:

1. **Exponential cost for long-tail coverage.** As formalized above, tokens with small $p_i$ require exponentially many samples to reliably surface. For genuinely rare reasoning strategies — a creative application of the shoelace formula, as in the paper's case study (Section 4.4), or an unusual algebraic manipulation — the required budget grows prohibitive.

2. **Diminishing or negative returns at scale.** Table 1 shows that on both Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and Olmo3-7B-Instruct-SFT, GRPO performance peaks at 32 rollouts and then *degrades* at 64 rollouts. The paper attributes this to training instability: larger rollout groups produce more heterogeneous rewards, which destabilizes the group-relative advantage estimation that GRPO relies on.

3. **No structural guidance.** Rollout scaling is passive — it repeatedly samples from the same distribution and hopes for diversity. It provides no mechanism to deliberately induce exploration of specific reasoning modes or to correct known gaps in the policy's coverage. It is, in essence, throwing more compute at the problem and hoping.

#### Approach 2: Objective-Level Modifications (Entropy Regularization, Decoupled Clipping)

A second line of work modifies the RL optimization objective itself to encourage broader exploration. Two prominent examples are:

- **Entropy regularization** (Deng et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025), which adds a bonus to the policy gradient for maintaining high entropy over the token distribution, incentivizing the policy to keep its probability mass spread across many possible next tokens rather than collapsing to a narrow set of high-probability choices.

- **Decoupled clipping** (Yu et al., 2025, in the DAPO paper), which allows asymmetric clipping ratios $\epsilon_{\text{low}}$ and $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$ in the PPO-style objective, where $\epsilon_{\text{high}} > \epsilon_{\text{low}}$ amplifies the magnitude of policy updates toward positive-advantage trajectories, effectively making the policy more aggressive about incorporating newly discovered successes.

**Where these fall short.** The paper's critique is pointed and specific:

> "While these methods encourage broader exploration at the distribution level, they provide limited control over what is explored, and often fail to ensure coverage of semantically meaningful reasoning strategies." (Section 1)

The key word is **control**. Entropy regularization says "be more uncertain" — it spreads probability mass indiscriminately, which can just as easily encourage the model to explore incoherent or unproductive token sequences as novel valid reasoning strategies. Decoupled clipping says "learn faster from successes" — but it only helps *after* a success has been sampled, and does nothing to increase the probability of sampling that success in the first place. Both methods are *stochastic and unconstrained*: they perturb the policy distribution without any structural guidance about *which* directions of exploration are likely to be productive.

The paper's $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$ scaling experiment (Section 4.7, Figure 7c) provides concrete evidence for this limitation. Increasing $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$ from 0.2 to 0.4 in GRPO initially improves performance but then causes sharp deterioration — the aggressive updates destabilize training without improving exploration quality. NUDGERL with $\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.2$ consistently outperforms all GRPO $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$ variants, demonstrating that **improving what you explore matters more than how aggressively you learn from it**.

#### Approach 3: Privileged Information Methods

A third family of approaches circumvents the exploration problem by injecting privileged information — oracle solutions, intermediate reasoning steps, or text feedback — that makes it easier for the policy to stumble upon correct trajectories. Examples include:

- **POPE** (Qu et al., 2026): Augments prompts with prefixes of oracle solutions (generated by a stronger model like DeepSeek Reasoner), giving the policy a "running start" toward the correct answer. The policy learns to complete these prefixes, and the hope is that this ability transfers to solving problems without the prefix at test time.

- **BREAD** (Zhang et al., 2025): Uses branched rollouts anchored on expert-generated intermediate steps.

- **Text feedback methods** (Song et al., 2026): Provide natural language feedback on intermediate reasoning steps, typically in multi-turn interactive settings.

**Where these fall short.** The paper identifies several interconnected limitations:

1. **Expensive and difficult to scale.** Oracle solutions require a stronger model (often a closed-source frontier model) to generate, and the quality of these solutions directly impacts training. As problem difficulty increases, even the oracle model may struggle to produce reliable solutions, and the cost of generating oracle prefixes scales with problem difficulty. The paper notes: "privileged information is often difficult to scale, especially when it relies on oracle solutions or expensive annotations" (Appendix A.3).

2. **Unclear transfer mechanism.** The paper points out a conceptual gap: "the mechanism by which the model internalizes this information and performs well without it at test time remains unclear" (Appendix A.3). The policy is trained on prompts that include solution prefixes, but evaluated on prompts without them. Why should the ability to complete a partially-solved problem transfer to solving the full problem from scratch? The paper suggests this transfer may be fragile or incomplete.

3. **Feasibility-oriented, not diversity-oriented.** This is perhaps the most important distinction. Privileged information methods are designed to make hard problems *solvable* — to create at least one successful trajectory that the policy can learn from. They focus on feasibility. But they do so by funneling the policy toward a narrow set of predefined successful paths (the oracle's solution strategy), which may actually *reduce* exploration diversity and prevent the discovery of alternative reasoning strategies that could be more robust, more efficient, or better suited to the model's particular strengths.

> "Moreover, by guiding the policy toward a narrow set of predefined successful trajectories, they may limit exploration diversity and hinder the discovery of alternative reasoning strategies." (Section 1)

4. **Empirical underperformance relative to diversity-driven methods.** Table 1 shows that NUDGERL, using only lightweight, unverified keyword-level hints generated by GPT-4o-mini (a small, cheap model), *consistently outperforms* POPE, which uses oracle solution prefixes from DeepSeek Reasoner v3.2 (a much stronger model). On Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, NUDGERL achieves 0.489 average pass@1 vs. POPE's 0.467, despite using dramatically cheaper supervision. This suggests that **structured diversity is more valuable than privileged correctness** — a counterintuitive finding that challenges the prevailing wisdom in the RLVR community.

### A Deeper Issue: Distribution-Level vs. Structural Exploration

Synthesizing the limitations across all three prior approaches reveals a deeper conceptual distinction that the paper draws implicitly. All existing methods operate at the level of the **probability distribution**: either sample more from it (rollout scaling), reshape it to be more spread out (entropy regularization), amplify the impact of rare successes (decoupled clipping), or shift the distribution toward known good regions (privileged information). None of them provide **structural, strategy-level guidance** about *what* to explore.

This matters because mathematical reasoning has inherent structure. Problems admit multiple valid solution pathways — coordinate geometry, algebraic manipulation, number theory lemmas, geometric transformations — and these pathways are semantically distinct. A policy that samples 64 rollouts might generate 55 using coordinate geometry (the dominant mode) and 9 scattered across other approaches, missing entirely a rare but elegant application of generating functions or the shoelace formula. No amount of entropy regularization or decoupled clipping will fix this if the dominant mode is firmly entrenched — the policy will remain probabilistically concentrated on coordinate geometry, because that's what it finds most natural, and the entropy bonus isn't targeted enough to deliberately steer exploration toward specific under-explored strategies.

The paper's key framing insight is that **the exploration bottleneck is fundamentally a coverage problem, not just a quantity problem**. It's not enough to generate more trajectories; you need to generate *different* trajectories — trajectories that span the space of semantically distinct reasoning strategies. And existing methods provide no mechanism to enforce or encourage such structural coverage.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

NUDGERL positions itself at the intersection of two guiding ideas:

**1. Exploration should be structured and controllable, not stochastic and unconstrained.** Rather than hoping that random sampling or entropy bonuses will eventually stumble upon diverse reasoning strategies, Strategy Nudging explicitly conditions each rollout on a specific mathematical strategy (e.g., "Ceva's theorem," "generating functions," "parity of sums"). This directly intervenes on the sampling distribution: conditioned on context $c$, the probability of generating a trajectory $y$ that employs strategy $c$ is $\pi(y|x, c) \gg \pi(y|x)$, dramatically reducing the expected number of rollouts needed to discover that trajectory. If $\pi(y|x) = 0.001$, the expected discovery cost is 1,000 rollouts without context; if conditioning on an appropriate context increases this probability to 0.1, the cost drops to 10 rollouts — a 100× efficiency gain.

**2. The goal is diversity, not feasibility.** Unlike privileged information methods, NUDGERL's contexts are not intended to make problems easier or to guide the policy toward known-correct solutions. They are intended to *diversify* the set of reasoning strategies attempted, even if many of those strategies lead to incorrect answers. The paper explicitly states: "contexts need not provide a solution; they can serve as lightweight controls that alter the sampling distribution and reduce the cost of discovering rare trajectories" (Section 3.1). This is a fundamentally different philosophy: explore broadly first, then let the RL process determine which strategies are actually useful.

This distinction is validated by the paper's finding that **random context sampling outperforms top-ranked context selection** (Section 4.6, Figure 6b). When the authors selected contexts based on which ones produced the highest pass@16 (oracle evaluation), performance *decreased* compared to randomly sampling from the full pool. Why? Because top-ranked contexts concentrate exploration on a narrow set of high-probability-success strategies — which is exactly what feasibility-oriented methods do. Random sampling, by contrast, induces a broader distribution over plausible strategies, leading to more effective exploration.

The paper also positions itself as **fully compatible with standard RLVR pipelines**. Strategy Nudging is a modification to how prompts are constructed during the rollout generation phase, not to the RL algorithm itself. The Inter-Intra Group Advantage is a drop-in replacement for standard group-relative advantage estimation. The distillation objective adds a term to the loss function without requiring architectural changes. This means NUDGERL can be integrated into existing RLVR codebases (the paper uses TRL, a standard library) with minimal engineering overhead — a practical consideration that the authors clearly value.

Finally, the paper positions its contribution not as a universal solution to all RLVR problems, but as addressing a specific and precisely defined gap: **the unexplored correct regions of the policy's probability space**. On problems where the base model has zero probability mass on any correct trajectory (difficulty bin 5 in the earlier compute-optimal scaling paper's taxonomy), no amount of structured exploration will help — there are simply no correct solutions to discover. NUDGERL targets the regime where the model *could* solve the problem but doesn't, because its sampling distribution is concentrated on dominant reasoning modes that are insufficient or incorrect for that particular instance. This is a large and practically important regime, encompassing the majority of problems where RLVR actually yields improvements over the base model.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

NUDGERL is a **training-time modification to the RLVR loop** that changes *how* the policy generates solution attempts (rollouts) during training and *how* it learns from those attempts, without altering inference-time behavior. It solves the problem that standard RLVR policies repeatedly sample from a narrow set of dominant reasoning strategies and fail to explore rare but potentially correct alternative approaches — and it does so by explicitly conditioning each training rollout on a randomly sampled, lightweight strategy hint (e.g., "Pythagorean theorem"), deliberately forcing the policy to traverse diverse reasoning modes, then distilling the discovered successful behaviors back into the base policy so that at test time, the model can reproduce those strategies without needing hints.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system modifies the standard RLVR training pipeline with three coordinated mechanisms:

1. **Strategy Context Pool** — an offline-generated collection of lightweight, keyword-level strategy hints (e.g., "generating functions," "parity of sums"), produced once per problem before training begins using a small, cheap LLM (GPT-4o-mini). This pool defines the "directions" in which exploration will be steered.

2. **Strategy Nudging (rollout-time)** — during training, before each rollout is generated, a context is sampled from the pool (with probability $1 - p_{\text{drop}}$) or omitted (with probability $p_{\text{drop}}$) and prepended to the problem prompt. This conditions the policy's sampling distribution on distinct reasoning strategies, inducing diversity at the input level rather than relying on random sampling noise.

3. **NUDGERL Learning Objective (update-time)** — a modified advantage estimation and loss function that (a) decomposes credit assignment into *intra-context* signals (how good was this trajectory given its strategy context?) and *inter-context* signals (how reliable is this strategy context overall?), and (b) adds a distillation term that directly updates the base policy (without context) using high-advantage trajectories discovered under context-conditioning, ensuring that exploration gains transfer to inference-time behavior.

Information flows as follows: before training → GPT-4o-mini generates strategy keywords per problem → during each training step → for each rollout, sample a context (or omit) → prepend to prompt → generate a trajectory → receive binary reward → group trajectories by context → compute inter-intra group advantages → apply clipped RL update on the context-conditioned policy + distillation update on the base policy → repeat.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal exploration bottleneck analysis (Equations 3–5), which *quantifies* why naive sampling fails and motivates why conditioning on contexts reduces discovery cost — this is the theoretical grounding for the entire method.
- **Second**, Strategy Nudging — exactly how contexts are generated (the prompt template, the sampling procedure, context dropout), how they modify the input to the policy, and the empirical verification that they induce diversity (Figure 1, Section 3.1).
- **Third**, the Inter-Intra Group Advantage — how standard GRPO advantage estimation fails under context-conditioned sampling, the decomposition into intra-context and inter-context components, the role of the $\lambda$ parameter in controlling exploration-exploitation balance, and the formal consistency guarantee.
- **Fourth**, the Distillation-Augmented Objective — why context-conditioned training creates a train-test mismatch, how advantage-weighted distillation bridges the gap, and how the combined loss creates complementary learning dynamics.
- **Fifth**, the full training algorithm — hyperparameters, the NUDGERL loss function, and how it connects back to the standard GRPO pipeline.

The method operates by (1) pre-generating lightweight strategy hints, (2) conditioning training rollouts on randomly sampled hints to induce reasoning diversity, (3) decomposing credit assignment to account for context-specific biases, and (4) distilling discovered behaviors back to the context-free policy.

#### The Formal Exploration Bottleneck: Why Naive Sampling Fails

The paper's entire motivation rests on a precise quantitative characterization of why standard RLVR sampling is inefficient for discovering rare reasoning trajectories. This analysis, adapted from Hu et al. (2025), is presented in Section 2.2 and Section 3.1 and provides the theoretical foundation for why Strategy Nudging is necessary.

**The performance improvement decomposition.** The expected one-step improvement in correct-token probability mass, $\Delta Q_{\text{pos}}$, is:

$$\Delta Q_{\text{pos}} = \frac{\eta}{N} \left( (1 - S_R) Q_{\text{neg}} A^2 + S_R Q_{\text{pos}} B^2 + S_R (Q_{\text{pos}} U_{\text{neg},2} - Q_{\text{neg}} U_{\text{pos},2}) \right)$$

where $\eta$ is the learning rate, $N$ is the number of rollouts per prompt, $S_R \in [0,1]$ is the net reward contribution from sampled tokens, $Q_{\text{pos}}$ and $Q_{\text{neg}} = 1 - Q_{\text{pos}}$ are the total probability mass on correct and incorrect tokens respectively, $A^2$ and $B^2$ are the second moments of sampled correct and incorrect tokens (measuring how concentrated probability is on the sampled tokens), and $U_{\text{pos},2}$ and $U_{\text{neg},2}$ are the second moments of *unsampled* correct and incorrect tokens (measuring how much probability mass sits on tokens that were never generated during the current batch).

**What this equation computes:** the net change in the policy's total probability of generating correct tokens after one gradient update, decomposed into three additive terms. The first term $(1 - S_R) Q_{\text{neg}} A^2$ is the learning signal from sampled correct tokens pushing against incorrect probability mass — always non-negative, always helpful. The second term $S_R Q_{\text{pos}} B^2$ is the reinforcement of already-sampled correct tokens — also non-negative. The third term $S_R (Q_{\text{pos}} U_{\text{neg},2} - Q_{\text{neg}} U_{\text{pos},2})$ is the *exploration penalty*: if unsampled correct tokens hold substantial probability mass ($U_{\text{pos},2}$ is large) and incorrect tokens dominate the distribution ($Q_{\text{neg}} \gg Q_{\text{pos}}$), this term becomes strongly negative and *subtracts* from the performance improvement.

**Why this form matters:** the equation reveals that the bottleneck is not the absence of correct tokens in the policy's probability distribution — it is their *absence from the sampled batch*. The policy may assign, say, 15% total probability to correct completions, but if those completions are spread across many low-probability trajectories that rarely get sampled, the $U_{\text{pos},2}$ term is large and the exploration penalty dominates. This explains why simply increasing the learning rate or modifying the clipping parameters cannot fix the exploration problem — those interventions operate on *how* the policy learns from sampled tokens, not on *which* tokens get sampled in the first place.

**The discovery cost scaling.** For a specific rare trajectory $y$ with probability $\pi(y|x_0) \ll 1$ under the current policy, the expected number of rollouts required to observe it at least once is:

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{\# rollouts}] = \frac{1}{\pi(y|x_0)}$$

**What this computes:** the reciprocal relationship between trajectory probability and expected discovery cost. If a correct trajectory has $\pi(y|x_0) = 0.001$, you need on average 1,000 rollouts to see it once. This is the geometric distribution expectation — the fundamental reason why rare trajectories are expensive to discover.

**Why this form matters:** it establishes that the discovery cost scales *inversely* with probability, not logarithmically or sublinearly. Halving the probability doubles the expected cost. This means that trajectories in the long tail of the distribution — even those with non-negligible probability like 0.01 — already require 100 expected rollouts, and trajectories at 0.001 require 1,000. For RLVR training with typical budgets of 8–64 rollouts, these trajectories are effectively invisible.

**The unsampled mass decay.** Even if you increase $N$, the expected unsampled second moment after $N$ independent draws is:

$$\sum_i p_i^2 (1 - p_i)^N$$

where $p_i$ is the probability of token $i$.

**What this computes:** the residual probability mass on tokens that remain unsampled after $N$ draws, weighted by $p_i^2$ (which downweights very rare tokens and upweights moderately rare ones). For a token with $p_i = 0.01$, after $N = 64$ draws, the decay factor is $(0.99)^{64} \approx 0.525$ — meaning roughly half the original unsampled mass remains. For $p_i = 0.001$, $(0.999)^{64} \approx 0.938$ — almost all of it remains.

**Why this form matters:** it demonstrates that rollout scaling provides rapidly diminishing returns for covering long-tail trajectories. The $(1-p_i)^N$ term decays exponentially in $N$ but the decay *rate* is $-\ln(1-p_i) \approx p_i$ for small $p_i$. Rare tokens with $p_i \ll 1/N$ are barely touched by increasing $N$ — you need $N$ to be on the order of $1/p_i$ to make a dent. For a trajectory with $p_i = 0.001$, this means $N \approx 1000$, which is well beyond practical training budgets. This quantitative analysis directly motivates why sampling from a modified distribution — one where $\pi(y|x, c) \gg \pi(y|x)$ — can be exponentially more efficient than brute-force scaling.

#### Strategy Nudging: Structured Exploration via Strategy-Level Contexts

Strategy Nudging is the mechanism that intervenes on the sampling process to deliberately induce diversity. Rather than hoping that random sampling noise will surface diverse reasoning strategies, it explicitly conditions each rollout on a strategy-level context that steers the policy toward a particular reasoning mode.

**Context pool generation (offline, pre-training).** Before training begins, for each problem $x_0$ in the training set $\mathcal{D}$, a pool of strategy-level contexts $\mathcal{C}(x_0) = \{c_1, \ldots, c_M\}$ is generated. The paper uses $M = 2$ for main experiments (two contexts per problem) and $M = 5$ for the top-ranked selection ablation. The contexts are generated by GPT-4o-mini, a small and inexpensive LLM, using the following prompt (Appendix B):

> "Given the following math problem, generate {num_hints} different keyword hints that would help solve it. Each hint should be a specific mathematical concept, theorem, or technique (e.g., 'Ceva's theorem', 'Lifting the exponents', 'Triangle inequality')."

The contexts are **keyword-level hints** — short phrases naming a mathematical concept — not solution steps, not intermediate derivations, not partial answers. Examples from the paper include "Generating functions" and "Parity of sums" for a problem about summing over triples with even parity, and "Pythagorean theorem" for geometry problems. The contexts are generated **once** and reused across training runs.

**Why lightweight keyword hints rather than detailed guidance.** The paper explicitly contrasts this with privilege-based methods (Section 1, Appendix A.3): contexts are intended to *diversify* exploration, not to *simplify* problems. A detailed solution prefix (as in POPE) would directly reveal the answer path and reduce the problem to completion rather than solution. A keyword hint like "Pythagorean theorem" tells the model *which tool to consider* without telling it *how to apply it* — the model must still work out the geometry, set up the coordinates, and solve the equations. This preserves the reasoning challenge while breaking the policy out of its dominant-mode rut.

**Context dropout and prompt construction (rollout-time).** For each rollout index $i$ during training, the system constructs a context by a two-step process:

1. **Uniform sampling:** $c^{(i)} \sim \text{Uniform}(\mathcal{C}(x_0))$ — randomly select one context from the pool.
2. **Context dropout:** $b^{(i)} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1 - p_{\text{drop}})$ — with probability $p_{\text{drop}}$, discard the context; with probability $1 - p_{\text{drop}}$, keep it.

The final context $z^{(i)}$ is:

$$z^{(i)} = \begin{cases} c^{(i)}, & b^{(i)} = 1 \\ \emptyset, & b^{(i)} = 0 \end{cases}$$

The input prompt for the rollout is then $x_1^{(i)} = (x_0, z^{(i)})$ — the original problem with the context (if any) appended. The rollout is generated as $y^{(i)} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | x_1^{(i)})$.

**Why context dropout is essential.** The paper's ablation (Section 4.6, Figure 6a) shows that $p_{\text{drop}} = 0.5$ performs best across benchmarks. Dropout serves two purposes:

- **Exploration beyond fixed contexts:** Without dropout ($p_{\text{drop}} = 0$), every rollout is conditioned on a context from the pool, and the policy never generates trajectories under the original problem prompt. This means the policy may overfit to the contexts — it learns to solve problems *given a hint* but cannot generalize to solving them without hints. Context dropout ensures that a fraction of rollouts are generated under the original prompt, maintaining the policy's ability to reason independently.
- **Stabilizing group-wise statistics:** The Inter-Intra Group Advantage computation (Section 3.2) partitions rollouts into context groups. If every rollout has a context, each group contains exactly $N/M$ samples. With dropout, the $\emptyset$ group serves as a common baseline, and the sample sizes per group are more balanced, preventing statistical anomalies from small groups dominating the advantage estimation.

The paper also reports that $p_{\text{drop}} = 0$ (no dropout) degrades performance (Table 3: 0.537 vs. 0.598 average pass@1), as does $p_{\text{drop}} = 0.75$ (too much dropout dilutes the context effect, 0.566 average). The $p_{\text{drop}} = 0.5$ setting provides a balanced mixture.

**The prompt template (Appendix B).** When a context is included, the user-facing prompt is structured as:

> "Context (exploration condition):\n- Use this hint/approach: {hint}\n\nImportant:\n- Follow this approach as your primary strategy.\n\nProblem:\n{problem}\n\nSolve this step by step and provide your final numerical answer at the end."

The system prompt instructs the model to place its working-out between `<start_working_out>` and `<end_working_out>` tags, and its final answer between `<SOLUTION>` and `</SOLUTION>` tags. This structured format is standard in RLVR for reliable answer extraction.

**Why uniform random context sampling rather than adaptive selection.** The paper compares random sampling against "top-ranked" selection (Section 4.6, Figure 6b), where contexts are scored by their pass@16 performance (fraction of rollouts under that context that solve the problem) and only the top two contexts per problem are retained. Random sampling outperforms top-ranked selection on average pass@1 (0.598 vs. 0.552). The paper attributes this to diversity: top-ranked contexts are those that most reliably lead to correct answers under the *base model*, which means they tend to be the strategies the model already employs effectively. They concentrate exploration on *known-good* paths rather than expanding coverage to *under-explored* paths. Random sampling, by occasionally selecting a context that the model rarely uses, forces exploration of less-familiar strategies — and while many of those attempts fail, the few that succeed provide novel learning signal that the RL process can then reinforce.

**Empirical verification of induced diversity (Figure 1 and Appendix B).** To verify that Strategy Nudging actually increases reasoning diversity rather than merely changing surface-level phrasing, the authors conduct a controlled comparison on 200 problems from DAPO-17k-Processed:

- **Naive sampling:** 8 rollouts per problem from the base policy under the original prompt.
- **Strategy Nudging:** 4 rollouts each from 2 contexts (no dropout), 8 total rollouts.

Both sets of rollouts are analyzed by GPT-4o-mini acting as a judge, with instructions to "identify the number of conceptually distinct solution strategies" and "ignore minor calculation differences or phrasing variations." The judge clusters solutions by their underlying mathematical approach and reports the number of distinct strategies.

As shown in Figure 1c, Strategy Nudging shifts the distribution of distinct-strategy counts to the right — more problems yield 2, 3, or 4 distinct reasoning strategies compared to naive sampling, which frequently collapses to a single dominant mode. The paper reports that "Strategy Nudging more often increases the number of distinct reasoning structures relative to naive sampling" (Section 3.1). This verification is performed on the *base model before any training*, confirming that the diversity gain comes from the conditioning mechanism itself, not from RL-driven policy changes.

**The theoretical efficiency gain from conditioning.** The expected number of rollouts to discover a trajectory $y$ decreases from $1/\pi(y|x_0)$ to $1/\pi(y|x_0, c)$ when conditioned on a context $c$ that increases the trajectory's probability. The paper does not provide direct probability measurements, but the case study in Section 4.4 provides qualitative evidence: the GRPO-trained model sampled the shoelace formula strategy only once in 32 rollouts, while NUDGERL conditioned on relevant contexts (likely geometric keywords) sampled it substantially more frequently, producing 6 correct trajectories out of 32. This represents a shift from approximately 3% sampling rate to 19% — a 6× increase in discovery efficiency for this particular strategy.

#### Inter-Intra Group Advantage: Credit Assignment Across Context-Induced Groups

Strategy Nudging solves the exploration problem — it induces diverse trajectories — but it introduces a new challenge for credit assignment. In standard GRPO, all rollouts for a given prompt are drawn from the same distribution (the original prompt), so the group-wise mean and standard deviation of rewards provide an unbiased estimate of baseline performance. With Strategy Nudging, rollouts are drawn from *different* context-conditioned distributions, each of which may have a different baseline reward rate. A single group-wise advantage would conflate two sources of reward variation: (1) the intrinsic quality of the trajectory, and (2) the difficulty or reliability of the context under which it was generated.

**The problem with standard GRPO advantage under context conditioning.** Consider two rollouts for the same problem: one conditioned on context $c_A$ (a reliable strategy that usually leads to correct answers) and one conditioned on context $c_B$ (a creative but risky strategy that rarely succeeds). If both rollouts happen to be correct, standard GRPO would assign them identical advantages (since they have the same reward and the same group mean). But the rollout under $c_A$ is less informative — success under a reliable context is expected, not surprising. The rollout under $c_B$ is far more informative — it represents the discovery of a novel successful reasoning path. Standard GRPO cannot distinguish these cases.

**The Inter-Intra Group Advantage decomposition.** The paper proposes a two-level advantage that separates context-level effects from trajectory-level effects. The computation proceeds in four stages:

**Stage 1: Partition rollouts by context.** Given $N$ rollouts $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^N$ with assigned contexts $\{z^{(i)}\}$ and binary rewards $r_i = \mathcal{R}(x_0, y_i) \in \{0, 1\}$, group the rollouts by their context:

$$\mathcal{G}(x_0) = \text{Unique}(\{z^{(i)}\}) \subseteq \mathcal{C}(x_0) \cup \{\emptyset\}$$

For each group $g \in \mathcal{G}(x_0)$, define the index set $\mathcal{I}_g = \{ i \mid z^{(i)} = g \}$, which partitions all rollouts.

**Stage 2: Compute context-level and global baselines.** For each context group:

$$\bar{r}_g = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_g|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_g} r_i$$

This is the mean reward within the group — it measures how reliable context $g$ is (what fraction of rollouts under this context succeed). Also compute the global mean:

$$\bar{r} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N r_i$$

**Stage 3: Compute raw advantage for each rollout.** For rollouts with a context ($z^{(i)} \neq \emptyset$):

$$A_i = (r_i - \bar{r}_{z^{(i)}}) + \lambda(\bar{r}_{z^{(i)}} - \bar{r})$$

For rollouts without a context ($z^{(i)} = \emptyset$):

$$A_i = r_i - \bar{r}$$

**Stage 4: Normalize advantages.** Compute the mean $\mu_A$ and standard deviation $\sigma_A$ of all raw advantages $\{A_i\}$, then normalize:

$$\hat{A}_i = \frac{A_i - \mu_A}{\sigma_A + \delta}$$

where $\delta > 0$ ensures numerical stability.

**What each component computes (operational interpretation).** For context-conditioned rollouts, the raw advantage decomposes into two interpretable terms:

- **Intra-context term:** $r_i - \bar{r}_{z^{(i)}}$ — how much better or worse is this specific trajectory compared to the average trajectory generated under the *same* context? If the context typically produces correct answers 70% of the time ($\bar{r}_{z^{(i)}} = 0.7$), a correct trajectory gets $+0.3$ and an incorrect one gets $-0.7$. This term isolates trajectory quality from context reliability.

- **Inter-context term:** $\lambda(\bar{r}_{z^{(i)}} - \bar{r})$ — how much better or worse is this context compared to the global average across all contexts? If the context is more reliable than average ($\bar{r}_{z^{(i)}} > \bar{r}$), this term is positive, giving a bonus to trajectories from reliable contexts. If the context is less reliable, this term is negative, penalizing trajectories from unreliable contexts.

The final normalized advantage $\hat{A}_i$ is what replaces the standard GRPO advantage $\hat{A}_i = (r_i - \mu)/\sigma$ in the policy gradient update.

**Why this decomposition — what alternatives would fail.** If we used standard GRPO advantage ($r_i - \bar{r}$), a correct trajectory from an unreliable context would receive the same credit as a correct trajectory from a reliable context. This would *penalize* the unreliable context unfairly: the policy would be encouraged to increase the probability of all trajectories under that context (including the many incorrect ones) because the rare correct ones get high advantage. The intra-context term fixes this by *comparing each trajectory to its context's baseline* — a correct trajectory from an unreliable context gets a large positive intra-context advantage (because it outperforms its low baseline) which properly signals "this specific trajectory was good, even though the context is generally bad."

If we used only the intra-context term ($r_i - \bar{r}_{z^{(i)}}$), we would lose the ability to distinguish between contexts entirely. All contexts would be treated as equally valid exploration directions. The inter-context term, modulated by $\lambda$, allows the system to express a preference: should we exploit reliable contexts (large $\lambda$) or explore unreliable contexts (small $\lambda$)?

**The role of $\lambda$ — exploration-exploitation control.** The $\lambda$ parameter controls how strongly context reliability influences credit assignment:

- **$\lambda = 1$:** Neutral — the inter-context term is simply $\bar{r}_{z^{(i)}} - \bar{r}$. A correct trajectory from a context with 80% success rate and a correct trajectory from a context with 20% success rate receive the same total advantage (the intra-context advantage is higher for the 20% context, but the inter-context term exactly cancels this difference for equal-reward trajectories). Equal-reward trajectories receive identical advantages regardless of context.

- **$\lambda > 1$ (exploitation):** The inter-context term is amplified. Correct trajectories from high-reward contexts receive a *bonus*; correct trajectories from low-reward contexts receive a *penalty*. This encourages the policy to shift probability mass toward reliable contexts — exploitation. The paper's $\lambda = 1.1$ setting is in this regime.

- **$\lambda < 1$ (exploration):** The inter-context term is attenuated. Correct trajectories from low-reward contexts receive a *relative bonus* compared to those from high-reward contexts. This encourages the policy to explore less-reliable contexts — exploration.

**Why $\lambda > 1$ works best (Figure 7a).** The ablation (Table 5) shows $\lambda = 1.1$ achieves the best performance (0.598 average pass@1) compared to $\lambda = 0.9$ (0.556) and $\lambda = 1.0$ (0.567). The paper's explanation (Section 4.6): "Since strategy nudging already ensures sufficient diversity at the sampling stage, increasing $\lambda$ does not hinder exploration across contexts. Instead, it strengthens exploitation within each problem by prioritizing trajectories from more reliable contexts." In other words, Strategy Nudging handles the exploration — it forces the policy to try diverse strategies regardless of advantages. The advantage function's job is then to *identify which of those explored strategies are actually good* and reinforce them. A slight exploitation bias ($\lambda > 1$) helps the policy converge toward reliable strategies once diverse exploration has occurred, rather than continuing to invest probability mass in strategies that have proven unreliable.

**Formal consistency guarantee (Proposition 3.1).** The paper proves that for binary rewards and $\lambda \in [0, 2]$, the Inter-Intra Group Advantage preserves the ordering of rewards: if two trajectories $y$ and $y'$ have rewards $r > r'$, then their advantages satisfy $A > A'$ regardless of which contexts they were generated under.

**What this guarantee means operationally.** A correct trajectory always receives a higher advantage than an incorrect trajectory, no matter how their contexts' baseline rewards differ. Context effects can only reorder trajectories *with the same reward* — a correct trajectory from a high-reward context gets a higher advantage than a correct trajectory from a low-reward context (when $\lambda > 1$), but both still get higher advantages than any incorrect trajectory. This prevents pathological cases where a correct trajectory under a "bad" context gets *negative* advantage and is actively pushed away from.

**Why $\lambda \in [0, 2]$ is the valid range.** For binary rewards $r \in \{0, 1\}$, the raw advantage difference between a correct trajectory under context $z$ and an incorrect trajectory under context $z'$ is:

$$A(r=1, z) - A(r=0, z') = (1 - \bar{r}_z) + \lambda(\bar{r}_z - \bar{r}) - (0 - \bar{r}_{z'}) - \lambda(\bar{r}_{z'} - \bar{r})$$

Simplifying, this difference is always positive when $\lambda \in [0, 2]$. When $\lambda > 2$, it becomes possible for a correct trajectory from a very low-reward context to receive lower advantage than an incorrect trajectory from a very high-reward context — the context preference overwhelms the reward signal. The $\lambda = 1.1$ setting is well within the safe range.

**Practical implementation note.** The $z^{(i)} = \emptyset$ group (context-free rollouts) uses the simpler advantage $A_i = r_i - \bar{r}$, not the two-term decomposition. This is because the empty context has no meaningful "context reliability" — it represents the baseline policy behavior. These rollouts serve as an anchor that keeps the group-wise statistics grounded in the original problem distribution.

#### The Distillation-Augmented Objective: Transferring Discovered Behaviors to the Base Policy

The Inter-Intra Group Advantage solves credit assignment under context-conditioned sampling, but there remains a fundamental train-test mismatch: during training, the policy generates trajectories under context-conditioned prompts $x_1 = (x_0, z)$, but at inference time, it must generate under the original prompt $x_0$ without any context. If the policy learns to solve problems *given a hint* but cannot do so *without the hint*, the exploration gains achieved during training are worthless at deployment.

**Why standard RL updates don't transfer automatically.** The standard RL objective (whether GRPO or NUDGERL's RL term) updates the parameters of $\pi_\theta(\cdot | x_1)$ — the policy conditioned on context. These updates change how the policy behaves when a context is present. They may teach the policy, for example, "when you see the hint 'Pythagorean theorem,' set up a right triangle and compute side lengths." But this learning is tied to the *presence of the hint* in the input. The policy may not generalize this capability to the context-free prompt, because during training it never sees the connection "this problem is solvable via the Pythagorean theorem" without the explicit hint.

This is fundamentally different from standard RLVR, where the training distribution (prompts without hints) and test distribution (the same prompts) are identical, and any capability acquired during training transfers directly.

**The distillation term.** To bridge this gap, the paper introduces an advantage-weighted distillation objective (Section 3.3, Equation 12):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{Distill}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | x_1)} \left[ \hat{A} \log \pi_\theta(y | x_0) \right]$$

**What this computes:** for each trajectory $y$ generated under a context-conditioned prompt $x_1$, we compute the probability the base policy $\pi_\theta(\cdot | x_0)$ would assign to that same trajectory under the *original* prompt (without context), and we increase that probability in proportion to the trajectory's normalized advantage $\hat{A}$. The expectation is taken over trajectories sampled from the context-conditioned policy — i.e., the trajectories we actually generate during training.

**Operational interpretation.** The distillation term says: "Here's a good trajectory we discovered by giving the model a hint about which strategy to use. Now, teach the model to generate this same trajectory *without* the hint — just from the original problem statement." It is supervised learning (behavior cloning) on high-advantage trajectories, where the "teacher" is the context-conditioned policy's own outputs and the "student" is the same policy evaluated without context.

**Why advantage-weighting rather than uniform distillation.** If we distilled *all* trajectories uniformly (standard behavior cloning from the context-conditioned distribution to the base distribution), we would teach the model to reproduce both good and bad strategies equally. The advantage-weighting ensures that only trajectories with positive (normalized) advantage contribute to the distillation gradient, and trajectories with higher advantage contribute more strongly. This is selective: "learn from the successes, ignore the failures."

**Why log-probability rather than other distance measures.** The $\log \pi_\theta(y | x_0)$ term is the standard cross-entropy / maximum-likelihood objective for autoregressive generation. Increasing the log-probability of a trajectory is equivalent to decreasing the per-token cross-entropy loss when treating the trajectory tokens as targets. This is the standard fine-tuning objective for language models and integrates naturally with the existing RL objective.

**The reinforcement learning term (on context-conditioned policy).** In parallel, the paper optimizes a standard clipped RL objective on the context-conditioned policy (Equation 13):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | x_1)} \left[ \min\left( r_1 \hat{A}, \text{clip}(r_1, 1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}}, 1 + \epsilon_{\text{high}}) \hat{A} \right) \right]$$

where the importance sampling ratio is:

$$r_1 = \frac{\pi_\theta(y | x_1)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y | x_1)}$$

**What this computes:** the standard PPO-style clipped surrogate objective, applied to trajectories generated under the context-conditioned distribution. The importance sampling ratio $r_1$ measures how much the current policy's probability of generating trajectory $y$ under context $x_1$ has changed relative to the old policy (the policy at the time the trajectory was sampled). The clipping function $\text{clip}(r_1, 1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}}, 1 + \epsilon_{\text{high}})$ constrains the magnitude of the policy update to prevent destructive large steps — if the ratio moves outside $[1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}}, 1 + \epsilon_{\text{high}}]$, the gradient is clipped to zero for that trajectory.

**Why decoupled clipping ($\epsilon_{\text{low}} \neq \epsilon_{\text{high}}$).** The paper uses $\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$ and $\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.2$ in the main experiments (standard symmetric clipping), following DAPO (Yu et al., 2025). The $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$ scaling experiment (Section 4.7) explores larger values of $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$ while keeping $\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$ fixed. Increasing $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$ allows larger policy updates toward positive-advantage trajectories (the policy can increase probability by up to $1 + \epsilon_{\text{high}}$ rather than $1 + 0.2$), effectively amplifying the learning signal from successful rollouts.

**The combined objective.** The full NUDGERL loss is a weighted sum (Equation 14):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{NUDGERL}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}} + \lambda_{\text{distill}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{Distill}}$$

where $\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0.1$ in the main experiments.

**Why $\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0.1$ rather than 1.0 (Figure 7b).** The ablation shows that $\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0.0$ (no distillation) reduces performance from 0.598 to 0.560 average pass@1, confirming that distillation is essential. However, $\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0.5$ reduces performance further to 0.536 — worse than no distillation. The paper explains: "overly large values also degrade performance, likely due to over-constraining the policy toward sampled trajectories." With $\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0.5$, the distillation term dominates the RL term, effectively turning the training into advantage-weighted behavior cloning. This loses the exploratory benefits of RL — the policy converges to whatever trajectories happen to be sampled, rather than actively discovering and reinforcing new strategies through the RL mechanism. The small $\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0.1$ provides a gentle nudge that transfers discovered capabilities without overwhelming the RL dynamics.

**The complementary learning dynamic.** The paper describes (Section 3.3) how the two loss terms interact:

- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}}$ operates on $\pi_\theta(\cdot | x_1)$ — the context-conditioned policy. It reinforces successful trajectories *within each context*, teaching the model "when given this hint, follow through with this reasoning approach." This improves exploration efficiency by making the context-conditioned policy better at exploiting the hint it receives.

- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{Distill}}$ operates on $\pi_\theta(\cdot | x_0)$ — the base policy without context. It projects the context-conditioned successes onto the context-free policy, teaching the model "when you see this problem (without any hint), recall that this reasoning approach works." This enables cross-context generalization — the model internalizes that certain problems are amenable to certain strategies, without needing the explicit hint at test time.

The two terms create a virtuous cycle: better context-conditioned exploration (via $\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}}$) discovers higher-quality trajectories, which provide better distillation targets for $\mathcal{L}_{\text{Distill}}$, which in turn makes the base policy more capable, which improves the starting point for future context-conditioned exploration.

**Evidence that distillation works (Figure 5).** The paper tracks two reward metrics during training: the "hinted reward" (average reward for rollouts with context) and the "dropout reward" (average reward for rollouts without context). Both improve together throughout training, and the dropout reward — reflecting the base policy's performance — occasionally *exceeds* the hinted reward. This is evidence of successful transfer: the base policy is not merely matching the context-conditioned policy's performance but in some cases surpassing it, suggesting that the distillation objective enables the base policy to integrate strategies from multiple contexts rather than being limited to any single one.

#### Full Training Algorithm and Implementation Details

**Algorithm summary.** For each training step, given a batch of prompts:

1. **Context sampling:** For each prompt $x_0$, for each rollout $i \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$, sample context $z^{(i)}$ using the procedure in Equation 7 (uniform sampling from $\mathcal{C}(x_0)$ with dropout probability $p_{\text{drop}}$).

2. **Rollout generation:** Generate $y^{(i)} \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot | x_0, z^{(i)})$ — the context-conditioned prompt.

3. **Reward evaluation:** Compute $r_i = \mathcal{R}(x_0, y^{(i)}) \in \{0, 1\}$ — binary reward from the verifier.

4. **Advantage computation:** Partition rollouts by context, compute $\bar{r}_g$ per group and $\bar{r}$ globally, compute raw advantages $A_i$ per Equation 10, then normalize to $\hat{A}_i$.

5. **Loss computation:** Compute $\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}}$ (Equation 13) using context-conditioned log-probabilities and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{Distill}}$ (Equation 12) using base-prompt log-probabilities — both on the same set of trajectories.

6. **Parameter update:** Apply gradient descent on $\mathcal{L}_{\text{NUDGERL}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}} + \lambda_{\text{distill}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{Distill}}$.

**Key implementation choices and their justifications:**

- **Only two contexts per problem ($M = 2$).** The paper generates exactly two strategy hints per problem for the main experiments. This is a deliberate design choice: too many contexts would fragment the rollout budget, with each context receiving too few samples for reliable intra-context baseline estimation. With $N = 8$ rollouts per prompt, $p_{\text{drop}} = 0.5$, and $M = 2$, each context group receives approximately $8 \times 0.5 \times 0.5 = 2$ rollouts on average, which is adequate for coarse mean estimation in binary reward settings. More contexts would reduce per-group sample sizes and increase variance in $\bar{r}_g$.

- **No KL penalty ($\beta = 0$).** Standard PPO often includes a KL divergence penalty between the current and old policy to prevent large distributional shifts. The paper sets the KL coefficient $\beta = 0$ (Table 2), following recent RLVR conventions (DAPO, DeepSeekMath) where the clipping mechanism alone provides sufficient regularization. This choice acknowledges that in RLVR with Group Relative advantages, the advantage normalization already provides implicit regularization — advantages are zero-mean and unit-variance within each group, so the effective learning rate is self-normalizing.

- **LoRA fine-tuning (rank 32).** The paper uses Low-Rank Adaptation with rank 32 rather than full fine-tuning. This is computationally efficient (only a small fraction of parameters are updated) and has become standard in open-source RLVR work. The choice of rank 32 is consistent with prior work (DAPO uses rank 32 or 64).

- **Min-p sampling with $p = 0.0$.** The minimum probability threshold for token sampling is set to 0.0 — effectively disabled. The paper relies on top-p ($p = 0.95$) and top-k ($k = -1$, disabled) for nucleus sampling. This configuration prioritizes diversity over quality in the sampling process, which aligns with the exploration goal — the model is allowed to sample from a relatively broad token distribution.

- **Temperature 1.0 for training, 0.7 for evaluation.** Higher temperature during training increases the entropy of the sampling distribution, making it more likely that diverse trajectories are generated even within the same context. Lower temperature at evaluation produces more deterministic, higher-quality outputs for measuring pass@1.

- **Gradient accumulation equal to $N$.** The number of gradient accumulation steps is set to $N$ (the number of rollouts per prompt), with a batch size of 4 prompts. This means one optimizer step processes $4 \times N$ rollouts. For $N = 8$, this is 32 rollouts per update — a moderate batch size that balances gradient variance and computational efficiency.

- **Cosine learning rate schedule with 5% warmup.** Standard practice for transformer fine-tuning. The learning rate peaks at $2 \times 10^{-5}$ after 5% of total steps and decays following a cosine curve.

- **RL steps = 500.** The paper trains for a fixed 500 steps rather than using early stopping or validation-based termination. This is common in RLVR where validation metrics are noisy and learning curves can be non-monotonic.

**How NUDGERL connects back to standard GRPO.** At inference time, NUDGERL is identical to GRPO — the model generates trajectories under the original prompt $\pi_\theta(\cdot | x_0)$ without any contexts. The only difference is that the model's parameters have been shaped by a training process that deliberately induced exploration diversity through context conditioning and transferred those discoveries to the base policy through distillation. This means NUDGERL is fully compatible with existing RLVR evaluation pipelines and requires no changes to inference infrastructure.

**What happens to the context-conditioned policy after training.** The context-conditioned policy $\pi_\theta(\cdot | x_1)$ is not used at inference time. It exists only as a training artifact — a means to an end. The distillation objective ensures that the capabilities learned under context-conditioning are internalized by the base policy. The paper does not discard the context-conditioned policy (it's the same neural network, just evaluated with different prompts), but the intended deployment mode is always context-free.

**Computational cost comparison.** NUDGERL with $N = 8$ rollouts uses the same per-step generation budget as GRPO-8. The additional overhead comes from: (1) the offline context generation step (one-time cost, using GPT-4o-mini, approximately API-call cost per problem), (2) the distillation loss computation (evaluating $\log \pi_\theta(y | x_0)$ in addition to $\log \pi_\theta(y | x_1)$ — a small constant factor on the forward pass), and (3) the inter-intra group advantage computation (simple arithmetic on reward vectors, negligible). The dominant cost — generating rollouts — is identical to GRPO with the same $N$. The paper's efficiency claim ("up to $8\times$ smaller rollout budget") refers to matching GRPO-32 or GRPO-64 performance with $N = 8$, which is a $4\times$ to $8\times$ reduction in generation cost. This is a wall-clock and compute savings, not merely a sample-efficiency metric in terms of gradient steps.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing the Exploration Bottleneck as a Coverage Problem, Not a Quantity Problem

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is implicitly redefining what "insufficient exploration" means in RLVR. Prior work — whether through rollout scaling (Hu et al., 2025), entropy regularization (Deng et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025), or decoupled clipping (Yu et al., 2025) — treated exploration as a **quantity problem**: the policy needs to generate more total trajectories, or spread its probability mass more broadly, to increase the chance of stumbling upon correct solutions. The underlying assumption was that diversity emerges naturally from increased sampling volume or increased stochasticity.

NUDGERL challenges this assumption by diagnosing the bottleneck instead as a **coverage problem**: the policy repeatedly samples from a narrow set of dominant reasoning modes, and no amount of additional sampling from the same distribution will reliably surface semantically distinct alternative strategies that lie in the long tail. The paper formalizes this through the unsampled-mass decay equation (Equation 4): for tokens with probability $p_i \ll 1/N$, the factor $(1-p_i)^N$ barely decays even as $N$ grows. This means that brute-force scaling provides exponentially diminishing returns for covering rare but valid reasoning strategies — the very strategies that represent genuine exploration gains rather than variations on the dominant mode.

This reframing is significant because it **changes what counts as a solution**. If the problem is quantity, the solution is more compute (larger $N$). If the problem is coverage, the solution is structural intervention — deliberately steering the sampling distribution toward under-explored regions. This diagnostic insight applies beyond the specific NUDGERL mechanism: it suggests that future work on RLVR exploration should focus on *which* trajectories are generated, not just *how many*, and that evaluation of exploration methods should measure reasoning diversity, not just aggregate reward.

The evidence for this reframing comes from two sources. First, the training dynamics (Figure 3b–c) show that NUDGERL with $N=8$ achieves better pass@1 and pass@k than GRPO with $N=16$ or $N=32$, demonstrating that structural coverage improvement can substitute for 2–4× more rollouts. Second, the diversity analysis (Figure 1c) directly measures the number of distinct reasoning strategies discovered under naive sampling vs. Strategy Nudging on the base model before any training, confirming that the coverage gap exists even at the initialization stage — it is not a pathology introduced by RL optimization but a structural property of how the policy allocates probability mass across semantically distinct reasoning modes.

### Innovation 2: Demonstrating That Structured Diversity Beats Privileged Correctness for Exploration

A counterintuitive empirical finding lies at the heart of the paper's contribution: **randomly sampled, unverified strategy hints from a small model (GPT-4o-mini) consistently outperform oracle solution prefixes from a much stronger model (DeepSeek Reasoner v3.2) as exploration guidance in RLVR.** This result (Table 1: NUDGERL 0.489 vs. POPE 0.467 average pass@1 on Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507) inverts the natural intuition that better supervision signals should produce better training outcomes.

The standard intuition in the RLVR literature — especially in work on privileged information methods like POPE (Qu et al., 2026), BREAD (Zhang et al., 2025), and text feedback approaches (Song et al., 2026) — has been that the primary barrier to RLVR performance on hard problems is **feasibility**: the policy cannot generate correct trajectories at all, so it receives no positive learning signal. Providing oracle solution prefixes or intermediate steps lowers the difficulty of generating a correct trajectory, creating learning signal where none existed before. This framing treats exploration as synonymous with "making hard problems solvable."

NUDGERL's result suggests a different interpretation: on many problems where RLVR is applied, the policy *already has some probability mass* on correct trajectories (the base model can solve them occasionally), but that mass is concentrated in hard-to-sample regions of the distribution. The bottleneck is not feasibility but **discoverability** — the policy's sampling process rarely surfaces those correct trajectories because it overwhelmingly favors a narrow set of dominant reasoning modes. When this is the case, providing oracle prefixes (which narrow exploration toward a single known-good path) can actually be counterproductive: it solves the feasibility problem for that specific path but reduces the diversity of explored strategies, potentially missing alternative reasoning approaches that are better suited to the policy's strengths or that could generalize more robustly.

The paper provides two additional pieces of evidence supporting this interpretation. First, the context sampling ablation (Figure 6b) shows that **random context sampling outperforms top-ranked context selection** — when contexts are filtered to keep only those that most reliably produce correct answers under the base model, performance degrades. Top-ranked contexts concentrate exploration on strategies the model already uses effectively; random contexts force exploration of under-utilized strategies, and some of those turn out to be valuable. This directly parallels the oracle-prefix issue: privileged information, like top-ranked contexts, narrows exploration to known-good paths at the expense of diversity.

Second, the training dynamics (Figure 5) show that the "dropout reward" (performance of the base policy without context) occasionally exceeds the "hinted reward" (performance with context) during training. This means the model is not simply learning to follow hints — it is internalizing strategies from multiple contexts and combining them in ways that sometimes outperform any single context. This is precisely the kind of cross-strategy generalization that narrow, feasibility-oriented exploration would suppress.

This insight reframes the exploration problem from "how do we make hard problems solvable" to "how do we ensure the policy actually samples the correct trajectories it is already capable of generating." It suggests that the RLVR community may have overinvested in feasibility-oriented interventions (oracle prefixes, solution hints, interactive feedback) and underinvested in diversity-oriented interventions, and that the latter may be both more effective and dramatically cheaper to scale.

### Innovation 3: The Inter-Intra Group Advantage as a General Mechanism for Credit Assignment Under Structured Exploration

While Strategy Nudging addresses *how* to induce diverse exploration, the Inter-Intra Group Advantage addresses a conceptual problem that any structured exploration method creates: **how do you assign credit when trajectories are generated under different conditioning contexts with different baseline difficulty levels?** This is not a problem unique to NUDGERL — it would arise in any system that partitions rollouts into groups with heterogeneous reward distributions (e.g., different prompt formulations, different reasoning templates, different few-shot examples).

Standard GRPO's advantage estimation assumes all rollouts for a given prompt are drawn from the same distribution, so the group mean provides an unbiased baseline. Under structured exploration, this assumption breaks: each context group has its own baseline reward rate, and a single group mean conflates trajectory quality with context difficulty. A correct trajectory under an "easy" context (one that reliably leads to correct answers) and a correct trajectory under a "hard" context (one that rarely succeeds) would receive identical advantages under GRPO, even though the latter provides far more novel information.

The Inter-Intra Group Advantage decomposition is conceptually elegant because it **separates two orthogonal sources of variation** that standard advantage estimation conflates: (1) how good this specific trajectory is *given the context it was generated under* (intra-context: $r_i - \bar{r}_{z^{(i)}}$), and (2) how good this context is *relative to other available contexts* (inter-context: $\lambda(\bar{r}_{z^{(i)}} - \bar{r})$). The $\lambda$ parameter then provides a single knob controlling the explore-exploit tradeoff *at the context level*: favor reliable contexts ($\lambda > 1$) or reward discoveries from unreliable contexts ($\lambda < 1$).

What makes this more than a trivial accounting trick is the **formal consistency guarantee** (Proposition 3.1): for $\lambda \in [0,2]$ and binary rewards, a correct trajectory *always* receives higher advantage than an incorrect trajectory, regardless of context. This prevents pathological cases where context-level preferences overwhelm the reward signal — e.g., a correct trajectory under a very unreliable context being penalized more heavily than an incorrect trajectory under a reliable context. The existence of this safe range ($\lambda \in [0,2]$) and the empirical finding that the optimal $\lambda = 1.1$ lies within it (Figure 7a) suggest that the decomposition is well-behaved in practice.

The broader significance is that this advantage formulation **decouples exploration diversity from exploitation reliability**. Strategy Nudging provides the exploration diversity — it forces the policy to try many strategies regardless of their historical success rates. The Inter-Intra Group Advantage then provides exploitation — it identifies which of those strategies are actually reliable and reinforces them. This division of labor means that each component can be optimized independently: the context pool can be made as diverse as desired without worrying about baseline quality, because the advantage function will sort out which contexts are valuable. Previous methods that modified the RL objective directly (entropy regularization, decoupled clipping) entangled exploration and exploitation in ways that were difficult to tune and often led to instability (as the $\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.4$ degradation in Figure 7c demonstrates).

### Innovation 4: Distillation as the Bridge Between Structured Exploration and Context-Free Deployment

The distillation objective ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{Distill}}$) addresses a structural problem that is easy to overlook but critical for practical deployment: **the policy is trained under context-conditioned prompts but evaluated under the original prompt**. This creates a distribution shift that could nullify all exploration gains if not explicitly addressed — the model might learn to solve problems *when given hints* but fail to do so *without hints*.

What makes this contribution distinctive is not the distillation technique itself (advantage-weighted behavior cloning is used in prior work like Song et al., 2026), but rather **the recognition that distillation is not an optional enhancement but a necessary component** in any system that uses conditioning to induce exploration diversity. Without distillation, NUDGERL would be a form of test-time augmentation (like self-consistency or best-of-N with different prompts) rather than a training-time improvement — the policy would get better at using hints but not at solving problems independently.

The paper's ablation (Figure 7b) provides stark evidence for this necessity: $\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0$ (no distillation) reduces performance from 0.598 to 0.560 average pass@1, and the performance gap between context-conditioned and context-free rollouts (Figure 5) closes over training, confirming that distillation is actively transferring capabilities. The fact that the dropout reward occasionally *exceeds* the hinted reward during training further suggests that the base policy is not merely imitating context-conditioned behavior but synthesizing strategies across contexts — a form of compositional generalization enabled by the distillation mechanism.

However, the ablation also reveals a subtlety: too much distillation ($\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0.5$, performance drops to 0.536) is worse than none at all. This suggests that distillation must be carefully balanced against the RL objective — if distillation dominates, training collapses to behavior cloning on whatever trajectories happen to be sampled, losing the exploratory benefits of RL. The optimal $\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0.1$ is relatively small, providing a gentle transfer signal that nudges the base policy toward discovered strategies without constraining it to exactly reproduce sampled trajectories.

This finding has implications beyond NUDGERL: it suggests that **any method that uses privileged information or structured conditioning during RL training should include an explicit transfer mechanism to the deployment distribution**, and that the strength of this transfer should be treated as a first-class hyperparameter with a non-trivial optimum. The failure mode of "the model learns to use hints but can't solve problems independently" is a real risk that many privileged-information methods (POPE, BREAD) may face without adequate distillation, and the paper's ablation provides a template for diagnosing and mitigating it.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use **DAPO-17k-Processed** (Yu et al., 2025) as the training set — a processed version of the DAPO training data containing 17,000 mathematical reasoning problems with verifiable ground-truth answers. Evaluation is conducted on five held-out benchmarks spanning a range of difficulty levels: **AIME24** (30 problems) and **AIME25** (30 problems) from the American Invitational Mathematics Examination, olympiad-style high-school competitions; **AMC23** (40 problems) from the American Mathematics Competitions, a high-school contest benchmark; the **level-5 subset of MATH500** (134 problems), consisting of the hardest tier from Hendrycks et al. (2021); and the **Apex Shortlist** (48 problems), consisting of advanced competition-style problems. This evaluation suite is deliberately skewed toward challenging problems where exploration bottlenecks are most consequential — the base models achieve only 0.10–0.65 pass@1 on these benchmarks, leaving substantial room for improvement.

- **Base model(s).** Two model families are tested: **Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507** (Qwen Team, 2025), a 4-billion parameter instruction-tuned model, and **Olmo3-7B-Instruct-SFT** (Olmo Team, 2025), a 7-billion parameter model that has undergone supervised fine-tuning on mathematical reasoning data. The choice of two distinct model families (different architectures, different training recipes, different scales within the small-model regime) provides a basic cross-model robustness check. Both models sit in a regime where RLVR yields meaningful improvements (base model pass@1 on AIME24 is 0.374 for Qwen3 and 0.134 for Olmo3) but far from saturation — exactly where exploration quality matters most. The paper argues these models are "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), though this is not empirically demonstrated beyond the two tested families.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is **pass@1**, estimated from 128 independent rollouts per problem using the unbiased estimator of Chen et al. (2021): $\text{pass@}k = \mathbb{E}_{\text{Problems}}[1 - \binom{n - c}{k} / \binom{n}{k}]$, where $n = 128$ is the total number of samples and $c$ is the number of correct samples. This estimator corrects for the finite sample size — if you generate 128 rollouts and 90 are correct, the estimated pass@1 is not simply 90/128 (which would be biased upward by the sampling process) but the probability that a single sample would be correct, properly accounting for the fact that you observed 128 draws. All solutions are automatically graded using **math-verify** (HuggingFace, 2024), which extracts the final answer from the generated text and checks it against the ground-truth using symbolic equivalence. During evaluation, the temperature is set to 0.7 (lower than the training temperature of 1.0), and all other generation hyperparameters match training settings (Table 2). The paper also reports pass@k curves (Figure 3c) to assess whether improvements in pass@1 persist at larger sample sizes, which would indicate genuine capability improvement rather than mere variance reduction.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against three categories of methods:
  
  - **Base model (no optimization):** The pretrained/supervised-fine-tuned model evaluated with greedy decoding or sampling at temperature 0.7, without any RL training. This establishes the lower bound — how much does RLVR improve over the starting point?
  
  - **GRPO with increasing rollout budgets ($N \in \{8, 16, 32, 64\}$):** Standard Group-Relative Policy Optimization (Shao et al., 2024) as implemented in the TRL library. For controlled comparison across budgets, the gradient accumulation steps and generation batch size are adjusted so that total optimization dynamics remain comparable (Table 2). This is the primary baseline for the paper's central claim — that NUDGERL with $N=8$ matches GRPO with $N=32$ or $N=64$. Note that GRPO-16, GRPO-32, and GRPO-64 are **not** simply GRPO run for more steps — they consume 2×, 4×, and 8× more generation compute per training step, making the comparison a direct test of "structured exploration vs. brute-force sampling."
  
  - **POPE (Qu et al., 2026):** An oracle-guided method that augments standard GRPO by appending prefixes of oracle solutions (generated by DeepSeek Reasoner v3.2, a much stronger model) to the end of the base prompt. The paper implements POPE following the original paper's prompt format and dataset mixture, standardizing the oracle prefix length to 15% of the full solution. This baseline tests whether structured diversity (NUDGERL's strategy hints from GPT-4o-mini) can outperform privileged correctness (POPE's oracle prefixes from a frontier model) at the same rollout budget ($N=8$ for both).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute in terms of **rollouts per prompt ($N$)**, which is the standard unit in RLVR — each rollout is one complete solution generated by the policy, and generation dominates all other costs (forward passes for loss computation, advantage arithmetic). NUDGERL with $N=8$ uses the same per-step generation budget as GRPO-8. The paper's efficiency claims are stated multiplicatively: "NUDGERL outperforms standard GRPO with up to 8× larger rollout budgets," meaning NUDGERL-8 matches or exceeds GRPO-64 while using 8× fewer rollouts per step. The paper does not account for the one-time offline cost of context generation (GPT-4o-mini API calls for 17,000 problems), which is amortized across all training runs, nor for the additional forward pass required to compute $\log \pi_\theta(y|x_0)$ for the distillation loss (a small constant factor relative to generation). The context dropout mechanism ensures that the effective number of rollouts per context group is $N \times (1 - p_{\text{drop}}) / M$ on average — with $N=8$, $p_{\text{drop}}=0.5$, and $M=2$, each context group receives approximately 2 rollouts per step.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or multiple training runs with different random seeds. All reported results are from a single training run per configuration, evaluated once at the end of training (500 RL steps) using 128 rollouts per benchmark problem. This is standard practice in the RLVR literature at the time of writing (GRPO, DAPO, and related work similarly report single-run results), but it means that variance due to random seed, data ordering, and sampling stochasticity is not quantified. The pass@1 estimates from 128 rollouts have inherent uncertainty (approximately ±0.02–0.04 standard error for the mid-range accuracies reported), but the paper does not report confidence intervals or standard errors. This is a notable limitation — the differences between NUDGERL and the best GRPO baseline on Qwen3 (0.489 vs. 0.487) are well within the plausible range of run-to-run variance, and without error bars or multiple seeds, the reader cannot assess whether this gap is statistically reliable.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Performance: NUDGERL Matches Larger-Budget GRPO and Outperforms Oracle Baselines

Table 1 presents the headline results across all five benchmarks for both base models. The central finding is that **NUDGERL with $N=8$ rollouts achieves the best or second-best average pass@1 across all methods, including GRPO variants using up to $N=64$ rollouts**. On Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, NUDGERL reaches 0.489 average pass@1, slightly exceeding the best GRPO result (GRPO-32 at 0.487) and substantially outperforming GRPO-64 (0.451). On Olmo3-7B-Instruct-SFT, NUDGERL achieves 0.285, surpassing the best GRPO result (GRPO-32 at 0.281) and GRPO-64 (0.160).

Several patterns in Table 1 warrant attention:

**The non-monotonicity of GRPO with increasing rollout budget.** GRPO performance does not increase monotonically with $N$. On Qwen3, GRPO improves from 0.454 ($N=8$) to 0.470 ($N=16$) to 0.487 ($N=32$), then drops sharply to 0.451 ($N=64$). On Olmo3, the degradation at $N=64$ is catastrophic — performance collapses from 0.281 ($N=32$) to 0.160 ($N=64$), well below the base model's 0.225. This is not a minor instability; it is a failure mode of large-budget GRPO that the paper attributes to "training instability under brute-force rollout scaling" (Section 4.2). The likely mechanism: with $N=64$, each group contains many rollouts, the group-wise reward variance increases, the advantage normalization becomes less informative (many rollouts cluster near the mean), and the PPO clipping mechanism may excessively dampen updates or, conversely, amplify noise from outlier trajectories. Whatever the precise cause, the empirical result is clear: **larger rollout budgets do not monotonically improve GRPO**, and at $N=64$, performance can be worse than at $N=8$. This is a critical finding that is easy to overlook if one only reads the abstract — it means NUDGERL's advantage is not just about being more sample-efficient, but about avoiding the instability that larger budgets introduce.

**NUDGERL vs. POPE — structured diversity beats privileged correctness.** On Qwen3, NUDGERL (0.489) outperforms POPE (0.467) by 0.022 average pass@1. On Olmo3, the gap narrows to 0.285 vs. 0.279 — still in NUDGERL's favor but only barely. The benchmark-level breakdown reveals that NUDGERL's advantage is concentrated on AIME24 (+0.022) and AIME25 (+0.056 on Qwen3), the most challenging benchmarks, while POPE performs comparably on AMC23 and MATH500. This suggests that NUDGERL's diversity-driven exploration is particularly valuable on the hardest problems — exactly where POPE's approach of providing a single oracle path might be least effective, because hard problems admit fewer reliable oracle solutions and benefit more from exploring alternative strategies.

**Model-specific effects.** The absolute improvement from NUDGERL over the base model is substantially larger on Qwen3 (+0.087 over base model 0.402) than on Olmo3 (+0.060 over base model 0.225). This is consistent with Qwen3 being a stronger base model — it has more latent capabilities to unlock through improved exploration. On Olmo3, where the base model's pass@1 is very low on AIME benchmarks (0.134 on AIME24, 0.118 on AIME25), NUDGERL's improvements are modest (+0.056 on AIME24, +0.061 on AIME25), and GRPO-32 actually matches or exceeds NUDGERL on some individual benchmarks (e.g., Olmo3 GRPO-32 achieves 0.195 on AIME24 vs. NUDGERL's 0.190, and 0.553 on AMC23 vs. NUDGERL's 0.563). This is consistent with the exploration bottleneck framework: if the base model has very low probability mass on correct solutions, even structured exploration cannot surface trajectories that don't exist. NUDGERL helps discover *existing but unsampled* correct strategies; it cannot create capabilities from nothing.

**The APEX benchmark.** Performance on APEX is extremely low across all methods (0.021–0.058 range), and the differences between methods are small relative to the estimation noise from only 48 problems. This benchmark likely serves more as a sanity check than a discriminative evaluation — if any method suddenly jumped to 0.3 on APEX, it would signal contamination rather than genuine improvement. The paper does not draw strong conclusions from APEX results individually.

#### Training Dynamics: NUDGERL Improves Faster and More Stably Than GRPO

Figure 3 presents training dynamics on Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, evaluated every 50 steps on AIME24 and AIME25 (60 problems total) with 64 rollouts per problem.

**Training reward (Figure 3a).** The EMA-smoothed training reward (decay factor 0.99) shows that NUDGERL (purple line) maintains a higher reward throughout most of training compared to GRPO variants. All methods exhibit reward improvement over the first 200–300 steps, after which rewards plateau or fluctuate. GRPO-64 consistently underperforms other GRPO variants in training reward — it is the lowest line throughout training — confirming that the $N=64$ configuration is not merely evaluation-unstable but genuinely underperforms during training. This suggests the instability is inherent to the large-group advantage estimation, not an evaluation artifact.

**Evaluation pass@1 (Figure 3b).** NUDGERL reaches approximately 0.40 pass@1 by step 100 and exceeds 0.42 by step 200, maintaining a consistent lead over all GRPO variants through the remainder of training. GRPO-16 and GRPO-32 show slower initial improvement (reaching 0.38–0.39 by step 100) and plateau around 0.40–0.41, with GRPO-32 showing high variance (sharp dips and spikes) in the later stages of training. GRPO-8 shows the most stable but slowest improvement, plateauing around 0.39. The key dynamic insight is that **NUDGERL achieves in 200 steps what GRPO variants require 300–400 steps to approach, and maintains a higher plateau thereafter**. This is consistent with the interpretation that Strategy Nudging surfaces useful training signal earlier — by forcing diverse exploration from the start, the policy encounters correct trajectories sooner, receives positive learning signal, and begins reinforcing good strategies while GRPO is still meandering through its dominant modes.

**Pass@k curves (Figure 3c).** To assess whether NUDGERL's pass@1 improvements persist at larger sample sizes — an important check that the method is genuinely improving the policy's probability of correct answers rather than merely reducing variance — the paper evaluates pass@k for $k \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64\}$. NUDGERL consistently outperforms GRPO-8 across the full $k$ range, with the gap widening at moderate $k$ (e.g., pass@8: NUDGERL ≈ 0.75, GRPO-8 ≈ 0.68). At $k=64$, NUDGERL reaches approximately 0.87 vs. GRPO-8's 0.82. This confirms that NUDGERL's gains are **not merely a reduction in sampling variance** (which would show large improvements at pass@1 but convergence at pass@64) but represent a genuine improvement in the policy's capability — the model is more likely to generate correct solutions on any given attempt, and this advantage compounds at larger sample sizes.

Interestingly, GRPO-16 outperforms GRPO-8 at all $k$ values, confirming that for $N \leq 16$, larger rollout budgets do improve the policy. The problem is at $N \geq 32$, where the trend reverses — Figure 3b shows GRPO-32 underperforming GRPO-16 at some points despite higher training compute. The pass@k curves in Figure 3c only show GRPO-8 and GRPO-16 (not the unstable GRPO-32 or GRPO-64), so the full budget-performance relationship at inference time is not displayed.

#### Case Study: NUDGERL Surfaces and Exploits a Rare but Correct Strategy

Section 4.4 and Appendix F present a qualitative case study on one AIME25 problem that clearly illustrates NUDGERL's mechanism. The problem (a right triangle geometry question with points K and L satisfying equal-distance constraints) is one where the GRPO-trained model completely fails — across 32 rollouts, all 32 are either truncated (run out of generation budget before reaching an answer) or incorrect. The NUDGERL-trained model, by contrast, produces 6 correct solutions out of 32 rollouts.

The analysis reveals the strategic difference: both models predominantly use coordinate geometry (the dominant reasoning mode — 15 out of 32 rollouts for NUDGERL, 13 for GRPO). However, the GRPO-trained model also spends substantial probability mass on unproductive strategies: symmetry assumptions (5 rollouts), area decomposition (4 rollouts), and trigonometry (5 rollouts), all of which lead to overly long derivations that exhaust the 6,144-token generation budget and produce truncated, wrong answers. Critically, GRPO samples the **shoelace formula** strategy (a direct way to compute polygon area from vertex coordinates, bypassing the complex casework that derails other approaches) only **once** in 32 rollouts — and even that single attempt is truncated.

NUDGERL samples the shoelace formula strategy **10 times** in 32 rollouts, and 6 of those 10 are correct. The shoelace formula strategy produces substantially shorter and more reliable reasoning trajectories, fitting within the generation budget and reaching the correct answer (n = 104). The detailed solution in Appendix F shows that the shoelace approach computes the quadrilateral area directly from the coordinates of B, K, L, and C using the shoelace determinant formula, avoiding the symmetry-assumption dead ends and case-analysis quagmire that trap the coordinate-geometry and area-decomposition attempts.

This case study illustrates the mechanism in the paper's abstract terms: Strategy Nudging (conditioning on geometric keywords, likely "coordinate geometry" and "shoelace formula" as two distinct contexts) **induces** the model to try the shoelace approach much more frequently than it would under naive sampling. The Inter-Intra Group Advantage then **reinforces** the correct shoelace trajectories (they receive positive advantage within their context group, and the shoelace context likely has higher mean reward than less reliable contexts). The distillation objective **transfers** this capability to the base policy, so that at inference time — when no context is provided — the policy preferentially selects the shoelace strategy because it has been reinforced as reliable during training.

The GRPO-trained model's failure is explained by the exploration bottleneck: the shoelace strategy exists in the policy's probability distribution (it was sampled once, so it has non-zero probability), but the probability mass is so small relative to the dominant coordinate-geometry mode that it is almost never sampled. Without sufficient sampling of this strategy, GRPO receives no positive learning signal from it, and it remains a long-tail trajectory that contributes to the $U_{\text{pos},2}$ penalty (Equation 3) rather than to performance improvement. NUDGERL breaks this cycle by explicitly boosting the sampling rate of the under-explored strategy through context conditioning.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper conducts a series of controlled experiments on Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 using a subset of benchmarks (AIME24/25, AMC23, MATH500) to understand the contribution of each component and the sensitivity to key hyperparameters.

**Context dropout rate ($p_{\text{drop}}$):** The optimal dropout rate is 0.5, with performance degrading at both extremes. Table 3 and Figure 6a show average pass@1 of 0.537 ($p_{\text{drop}} = 0.0$, no dropout), 0.561 ($p_{\text{drop}} = 0.25$), 0.598 ($p_{\text{drop}} = 0.5$), and 0.566 ($p_{\text{drop}} = 0.75$). The $p_{\text{drop}} = 0.0$ setting is particularly informative: when every rollout is conditioned on a context, the policy never generates trajectories under the original prompt during training, leading to a train-test distribution shift that the distillation objective cannot fully compensate for. The $p_{\text{drop}} = 0.5$ setting balances the exploration benefits of context conditioning (50% of rollouts are context-conditioned) with the need to maintain the policy's ability to generate under the original prompt (50% are context-free). Interestingly, $p_{\text{drop}} = 0.25$ and $p_{\text{drop}} = 0.75$ perform similarly (0.561 and 0.566), suggesting the method is relatively robust to moderate deviations from the optimum but does require *some* context-free rollouts. The per-benchmark breakdown (Table 3) shows the effect is consistent: AIME24 goes from 0.418 (0.0) to 0.482 (0.5) to 0.458 (0.75), and AMC23 from 0.759 to 0.857 to 0.796 — the pattern of 0.5 being optimal holds across benchmarks.

**Context sampling strategy (random vs. top-ranked):** Random sampling of contexts from the pool consistently outperforms selecting the top-ranked contexts based on oracle evaluation. Table 4 and Figure 6b show 0.598 average pass@1 for random sampling vs. 0.552 for top-ranked selection. The per-benchmark breakdown reveals the gap is most pronounced on AIME24 (0.482 vs. 0.448) and AMC23 (0.857 vs. 0.774). This is a non-obvious and methodologically significant finding. The top-ranked procedure works as follows: for each problem, five candidate contexts are generated by GPT-4o-mini, pass@16 is evaluated for each context (how often does the base model solve the problem when conditioned on this context?), and the two contexts with highest pass@16 are retained. Intuitively, this should be better — it filters out "bad" contexts that rarely lead to correct answers. The empirical result is the opposite. The paper's explanation (Section 4.6) is that top-ranked contexts are precisely those that align with strategies the model already employs effectively, so they concentrate exploration on known-good paths rather than expanding coverage. Random sampling occasionally selects a context that the model rarely uses, forcing exploration of unfamiliar strategies — while many of those attempts fail, the few that succeed provide genuinely novel learning signal. This is a direct empirical argument for the paper's philosophy of diversity over feasibility: better to explore broadly (and fail often) than to optimize narrowly for immediate success rate. The implication for practitioners is clear: when using strategy hints for exploration, **do not filter for quality** — let the RL process determine which strategies are useful, not the base model's success rate.

**Exploration-exploitation trade-off ($\lambda$):** A slight exploitation bias ($\lambda = 1.1$) achieves the best performance. Table 5 and Figure 7a show 0.556 ($\lambda = 0.9$, exploration bias), 0.567 ($\lambda = 1.0$, neutral), and 0.598 ($\lambda = 1.1$, exploitation bias). The gap between $\lambda = 1.0$ and $\lambda = 1.1$ is substantial (0.031 average pass@1), larger than many of the differences between methods in Table 1. This suggests that the Inter-Intra Group Advantage's context-level preference is doing meaningful work, not merely being a neutral reparameterization of standard advantage. The $\lambda = 1.1$ setting favors trajectories from contexts with higher mean reward — i.e., it preferentially reinforces strategies that have proven reliable across multiple rollouts. The paper argues that this works because Strategy Nudging already handles the exploration — the policy is forced to try diverse strategies regardless of advantages. The advantage function's job is then to identify and reinforce the good ones. A slight exploitation bias accelerates convergence toward reliable strategies without reducing exploration diversity (since diversity is enforced at the sampling stage, not the advantage stage).

An important note: the paper only tests $\lambda \in \{0.9, 1.0, 1.1\}$, a narrow range around 1.0. Values outside this range — $\lambda = 1.5$, $\lambda = 2.0$ (the theoretical bound from Proposition 3.1), or $\lambda = 0.5$ — are not explored. It is possible that more extreme values would perform even better or reveal non-monotonic behavior. The $[0, 2]$ safe range from Proposition 3.1 guarantees order preservation between correct and incorrect trajectories, but it does not guarantee monotonic performance improvement — the optimal $\lambda$ could be anywhere in that range, and testing only three values leaves substantial uncertainty about the shape of the $\lambda$-performance curve.

**Distillation coefficient ($\lambda_{\text{distill}}$):** Distillation is essential but must be applied with a light touch. Table 6 and Figure 7b show 0.560 ($\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0.0$, no distillation), 0.598 ($\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0.1$), and 0.536 ($\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0.5$). The degradation at $\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0.5$ is notable — it is **worse than no distillation at all**, and substantially worse than the optimal 0.1. This is a clean demonstration that distillation must be carefully balanced: too much distillation dominates the RL objective, effectively turning training into advantage-weighted behavior cloning on whatever trajectories happen to be sampled, losing the exploratory benefits of RL. The specific mechanism is likely that when $\lambda_{\text{distill}}$ is large, the policy is strongly encouraged to exactly reproduce sampled trajectories under the base prompt, which reduces its willingness to explore new strategies under context conditioning (since those new strategies would create a mismatch between the base-prompt and context-conditioned distributions, increasing the RL loss). The virtuous cycle described in Section 3.3 (better context-conditioned exploration → better distillation targets → better base policy → better starting point for exploration) breaks if distillation over-constrains the policy.

The per-benchmark breakdown (Table 6) reveals an interesting pattern: the distillation effect is strongest on AMC23, where $\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0.1$ achieves 0.857 vs. 0.826 for no distillation — a 0.031 gap concentrated on a single benchmark. On AIME24, the gap is 0.482 vs. 0.423 (0.059). On MATH500, it is 0.660 vs. 0.628 (0.032). This suggests distillation is broadly beneficial rather than being driven by a single outlier benchmark.

**Clipping parameter scaling ($\epsilon_{\text{high}}$):** NUDGERL with moderate $\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.2$ outperforms GRPO across the full range of $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$ from 0.2 to 0.4, and GRPO sharply degrades at extreme values. Table 7 and Figure 7c show NUDGERL at 0.598, while GRPO varies from 0.557 ($\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.2$) to a peak of 0.566 ($\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.24$ or 0.32$) then dropping to 0.541 ($\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.4$). The key observation is that **GRPO benefits from increased $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$ only in a narrow range** (0.2 to 0.32), with performance plateauing and then degrading. This is consistent with the DAPO paper's findings that decoupled clipping helps but has limits. NUDGERL, with its structurally improved exploration, achieves better performance even at the most conservative $\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.2$ than GRPO achieves at any $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$. This reinforces the paper's central thesis: **improving what you explore matters more than how aggressively you learn from it**. The GRPO degradation at $\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.4$ is severe — the policy likely overfits to the few successful trajectories it discovers, losing the diversity that the higher $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$ was intended to encourage.

The per-benchmark breakdown (Table 7) shows an exception: on AMC23, GRPO with $\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.36$ achieves 0.845, which is close to NUDGERL's 0.857. This suggests that on "easier" benchmarks (AMC23 has the highest base model pass@1 at 0.653), aggressive clipping can partially compensate for poor exploration because correct trajectories are relatively abundant and the policy doesn't need structured diversity to find them. On harder benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25), the gap between NUDGERL and the best GRPO $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$ variant is larger, consistent with the interpretation that structured exploration is most valuable when correct trajectories are rare.

**Context diversity verification (Figure 1):** The paper verifies that Strategy Nudging actually increases reasoning diversity, not just changes surface-level phrasing. On 200 problems from the training set, the base Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 model generates 8 rollouts either with Strategy Nudging (4 rollouts each from 2 contexts) or without (all 8 under the original prompt). GPT-4o-mini acts as a judge to cluster solutions by their underlying mathematical strategy and count the number of distinct approaches. The distribution of distinct-strategy counts (Figure 1c) shows that Strategy Nudging shifts probability mass from 1–2 distinct strategies toward 2–4 distinct strategies. The paper reports that "Strategy Nudging more often increases the number of distinct reasoning structures relative to naive sampling" (Section 3.1). This verification is important because it rules out the possibility that context conditioning merely changes surface-level phrasing (e.g., "let's use coordinate geometry" vs. "using coordinates") without affecting the actual mathematical approach. However, the analysis relies on a single LLM judge without human verification of cluster quality, and the prompt for the judge (Appendix B) is relatively simple ("identify the number of conceptually distinct solution strategies"). The validity of the diversity measurement depends on GPT-4o-mini's ability to reliably distinguish genuine strategic differences from superficial variations — an assumption that is plausible but unverified.

**Training reward decomposition (Figure 5):** The paper tracks two reward metrics during NUDGERL training on Qwen3: the "hinted reward" (average reward for context-conditioned rollouts, $z^{(i)} \neq \emptyset$) and the "dropout reward" (average reward for context-free rollouts, $z^{(i)} = \emptyset$). Both improve together throughout training, with the dropout reward starting lower (around 0.35–0.40 at step 100) and converging toward the hinted reward (around 0.45–0.55 by step 500). Crucially, the dropout reward occasionally **exceeds** the hinted reward at certain points during training — e.g., around step 300, the dropout reward spikes above the hinted reward. This is evidence that the distillation objective is successfully transferring capabilities from context-conditioned exploration to the base policy, and moreover, that the base policy may be **synthesizing strategies across contexts** — performing better than any single context would predict because it can combine insights from multiple strategies. If the model were merely memorizing "when I see this problem, use strategy X," the dropout reward could at best match the hinted reward (achievable only if strategy X happens to be the one the model would have used anyway). Exceeding the hinted reward implies compositional generalization: the model has learned, through distillation, to apply strategies conditionally based on problem features, without needing explicit hints.

However, the paper does not provide a baseline comparison — e.g., what do the hinted and dropout reward curves look like for a variant without distillation ($\lambda_{\text{distill}} = 0$)? Without this comparison, the claim that distillation drives the convergence and occasional superiority of dropout reward is plausible but not directly demonstrated. The increasing dropout reward could also result from the RL term alone, if the context-conditioned policy's improvements incidentally transfer to the base prompt through parameter sharing.

### Critical Assessment

The paper's central claims, as stated in the executive summary, are: (1) NUDGERL with 8 rollouts matches or surpasses GRPO using up to 8× larger rollout budgets; (2) NUDGERL outperforms oracle-guided baselines; (3) structured, context-driven exploration can substitute for both brute-force sampling and feasibility-oriented supervision. I evaluate each in turn, examining whether the experiments genuinely support these claims and identifying what additional evidence would strengthen or qualify them.

**Claim 1: NUDGERL-8 matches GRPO with up to 8× larger rollout budgets.**

This claim is **supported with qualifications** for the Qwen3 model, but the evidence on Olmo3 is weaker, and the claim's precision ("up to 8×") elides important details.

On Qwen3, the numbers are: NUDGERL-8 = 0.489, GRPO-32 = 0.487 (4× budget), GRPO-64 = 0.451 (8× budget). So NUDGERL-8 slightly exceeds GRPO-32 (4×) and substantially exceeds GRPO-64 (8×). This is a clean result: NUDGERL achieves equivalent performance at 4× less compute and better performance at 8× less compute.

On Olmo3, the numbers are: NUDGERL-8 = 0.285, GRPO-32 = 0.281 (4×), GRPO-64 = 0.160 (8×). NUDGERL-8 exceeds GRPO-32 by 0.004 and GRPO-64 by 0.125. The gap at 4× is very small — well within the plausible range of run-to-run variance — and the per-benchmark breakdown (Table 1) shows that on AIME24, GRPO-32 actually outperforms NUDGERL-8 (0.195 vs. 0.190), and on AMC23, the gap is only 0.010 (0.563 vs. 0.553). The average advantage of NUDGERL on Olmo3 is driven largely by one benchmark (AIME25: 0.179 vs. 0.176) and the catastrophic failure of GRPO-64 on all benchmarks. The claim that NUDGERL "matches or surpasses" GRPO at larger budgets is true in the aggregate but hides per-benchmark variance that sometimes favors GRPO-32.

A deeper issue: **the claim uses GRPO-64 as the "8× budget" reference point, but GRPO-64 is demonstrably broken.** Table 1 shows GRPO-64 underperforms even GRPO-8 on both models (Qwen3: 0.451 vs. 0.454; Olmo3: 0.160 vs. 0.268). GRPO-64 is not a stronger method that NUDGERL beats — it is a weaker method that NUDGERL beats. The more honest comparison is NUDGERL-8 vs. GRPO-32 (4×), which is the best-performing GRPO variant. The paper's "8×" framing conflates two distinct findings: (1) NUDGERL-8 ≈ GRPO-32 (genuine 4× efficiency gain), and (2) GRPO degrades at large N (an independent observation about GRPO's instability, not directly about NUDGERL's strength). A reader who skims might incorrectly conclude that NUDGERL-8 beats GRPO-64 *because* GRPO-64 is a strong baseline that NUDGERL surpasses, when in fact GRPO-64 is the weakest GRPO variant tested.

The paper would have been strengthened by including GRPO-48 as an additional baseline, to understand where the degradation begins, and by running multiple seeds for NUDGERL and the best GRPO variant to quantify variance. Without error bars, the 0.002 gap between NUDGERL-8 (0.489) and GRPO-32 (0.487) on Qwen3 cannot be distinguished from noise.

**Claim 2: NUDGERL outperforms oracle-guided baselines.**

This claim is **supported** for the Qwen3 model on average, but the comparison on Olmo3 is essentially a tie, and the benchmark-level breakdown reveals important nuance.

On Qwen3, NUDGERL (0.489) exceeds POPE (0.467) by 0.022 average pass@1 — a meaningful gap that is consistent across four of five benchmarks (AIME24: +0.022, AIME25: +0.056, AMC23: +0.019, APEX: +0.005) with MATH500 as a slight exception (+0.008). This is genuinely impressive: NUDGERL uses hints from GPT-4o-mini (a small, cheap model) while POPE uses oracle solution prefixes from DeepSeek Reasoner v3.2 (a much stronger and more expensive model), and NUDGERL wins. The result validates the paper's philosophy that structured diversity is more valuable than privileged correctness for exploration.

However, two qualifications are necessary. First, the POPE implementation uses the paper's own reimplementation with standardized prefix length (15% of solution). The original POPE paper may have used different settings, and it's possible that a more careful POPE implementation would perform better. The paper cannot fully control for this. Second, on Olmo3, the gap narrows to 0.285 vs. 0.279 — within run-to-run variance and benchmark-level noise. The claim that NUDGERL "outperforms oracle-guided baselines" is true on average for the models tested but may not hold for all base models, and the paper does not investigate what properties of the base model (initial pass@1, reasoning diversity, instruction-following capability) predict which method will work better.

A missing experiment: what if POPE were given the same distillation objective as NUDGERL? POPE's oracle prefixes create a train-test mismatch similar to NUDGERL's context conditioning (the policy is trained with oracle prefixes but evaluated without them). If POPE were augmented with an analogous distillation term, would it close the gap? This would help distinguish whether NUDGERL's advantage comes from the diversity of its hints or from the distillation mechanism that transfers learned capabilities — currently, both factors are confounded.

**Claim 3: Structured, context-driven exploration can substitute for brute-force sampling and feasibility-oriented supervision.**

This is the paper's highest-level claim, and it is **supported in the specific regime tested** but with significant boundary conditions that the paper does not fully characterize.

The evidence clearly shows that, for the two model families tested on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, NUDGERL's context-driven exploration achieves better or comparable results with 4–8× fewer rollouts than GRPO, and with cheaper supervision than POPE. The mechanism is plausible and the ablations are internally consistent: diversity is verified (Figure 1c), distillation is shown to be necessary (Figure 7b), and the case study illustrates the mechanism in action (Section 4.4).

However, several boundary conditions are unexplored:

**What happens when contexts are poor quality?** The paper uses GPT-4o-mini to generate contexts — a capable but not frontier model. What if contexts were generated by a weaker model, or by simple templates (e.g., "Try using algebra" vs. "Try using geometry")? The paper's finding that random sampling beats top-ranked selection suggests context quality (in terms of correctness rate) is not critical, but there must be a lower bound — completely irrelevant or nonsensical contexts would presumably not help. Where is that boundary?

**What happens on easier problems?** The evaluation benchmarks are deliberately challenging (AIME, AMC, MATH500 level-5). On easier problems where the base model's pass@1 is already high (e.g., 0.8+), the exploration bottleneck may be less severe because correct trajectories are abundant and do not require structured exploration to surface. NUDGERL's benefits might shrink or vanish on such problems. The paper does not test on a broader difficulty spectrum (e.g., all MATH500 levels, or GSM8K), leaving open whether NUDGERL is specifically a hard-problem intervention or a general exploration improvement.

**What is the scaling behavior with more contexts?** The paper uses exactly $M=2$ contexts per problem. What happens with $M=3$, $M=5$, $M=10$? More contexts could induce even greater diversity, but would also further fragment the rollout budget (each context gets fewer samples, increasing noise in $\bar{r}_g$). There is likely an optimal $M$ that depends on $N$, and the paper does not explore this tradeoff. The ablation on context sampling (random vs. top-ranked with $M=5$) partially addresses this by generating 5 candidates, but only 2 are ultimately used.

**What about domain generalization?** All experiments are on mathematical reasoning. Would Strategy Nudging work for code generation (where "strategies" might be different algorithms or data structures), formal theorem proving, or open-ended generation tasks? Mathematical reasoning has the advantage of clearly definable strategies (named theorems and techniques), which makes context generation straightforward. For less structured domains, defining "strategies" and generating useful contexts may be fundamentally harder, and the paper provides no guidance on how to adapt the method.

**The missing difficulty-conditioned analysis.** Perhaps the most telling missing experiment: the paper does not break down results by problem difficulty. Given that the compute-optimal scaling paper demonstrated that exploration strategies are difficulty-dependent (beam search hurts on easy problems, helps on hard ones), it would be natural to analyze whether NUDGERL's benefits are concentrated on problems of specific difficulty levels. Does NUDGERL help primarily on medium problems where the model has latent but unsampled correct strategies, and provide no benefit on very hard problems where no correct strategies exist in the distribution? Does it even hurt on very easy problems, where the diversity forcing might distract the model from an already-reliable strategy? This analysis would significantly sharpen the paper's claims about when and why structured exploration is valuable.

**The missing compute-optimal allocation experiment.** Following directly from the previous point: if the benefits of structured exploration are difficulty-dependent, then a compute-optimal strategy would allocate different exploration methods to different problems. The paper doesn't explore whether NUDGERL could be combined with standard GRPO in a difficulty-dependent way (e.g., use NUDGERL on medium-hard problems and standard GRPO on easy problems). This is a natural extension that would strengthen the paper's practical relevance.

**Single-run reporting.** As noted in the evaluation methodology section, all results are from single training runs. The RLVR literature at the time of writing has not converged on a standard for reporting variance, but the differences between NUDGERL and the best GRPO baselines are small enough (0.002 on Qwen3, 0.004 on Olmo3) that statistical significance cannot be assumed. The paper would be strengthened by reporting results over 3–5 random seeds with standard errors, or at minimum by noting this limitation explicitly and providing the reader with guidance on which differences are likely reliable (e.g., the large gap on AIME25 for Qwen3: 0.393 vs. 0.370 for GRPO-32) versus which are not (e.g., the 0.002 average gap on Qwen3).

**The contest between diversity and feasibility is not fully resolved.** The comparison with POPE is a single data point: one oracle-guided method, one implementation, one prefix length. The paper's claim that "structured diversity beats privileged correctness" is strong and interesting, but it rests on this single comparison. Different oracle-guided methods (BREAD, text feedback approaches) might perform differently, and different instantiations of POPE (varying prefix length, using different oracle models) might close or reverse the gap. The paper's contribution is establishing that diversity-driven exploration *can* beat feasibility-driven exploration, not that it *always* does, and the discussion would benefit from this more measured framing.

Overall, the experimental analysis is thorough in its internal ablation (context dropout, sampling strategy, λ, λ_distill, ε_high) and provides consistent evidence for the paper's mechanism. The main results convincingly demonstrate that NUDGERL-8 is competitive with GRPO-32 and outperforms GRPO-64, and that it exceeds POPE on the tested models. The limitations are primarily in generalizability (single domain, two model families) and statistical rigor (single runs, no confidence intervals), both of which are common in the RLVR literature but nonetheless constrain the strength of the conclusions that can be drawn. The paper's strongest empirical contribution is not the absolute performance numbers but the ablation-driven demonstration that diversity-oriented exploration — with all its counterintuitive properties (random beats top-ranked, cheap hints beat oracle prefixes, moderate distillation beats aggressive distillation) — is a viable and underappreciated paradigm for improving RLVR.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Cost of Difficulty Estimation Is Not Accounted For in the Headline Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire compute-optimal framework rests on estimating each prompt's difficulty before allocating test-time compute. The difficulty estimation procedure — generating 2048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness (oracle bins) or the PRM's final-answer score (predicted bins) — is itself extraordinarily expensive. The authors explicitly acknowledge this in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence.** The reported 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation cost + strategy execution cost. Generating 2048 samples per question to estimate difficulty costs 2048 generations — far more than the largest test-time budgets studied (64–512 generations). This means the total cost of the compute-optimal approach on a single question could easily exceed the cost of simply running best-of-512 on every question. The 4× figure is therefore an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** in a deployment setting where difficulty is unknown, not a realized practical gain. The gap between this upper bound and what is achievable with cheaper difficulty estimation is unknown and could be large.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figures 4 and 8 show that predicted (non-oracle) difficulty bins perform nearly as well as oracle bins, which is encouraging because it means ground-truth answers are not needed. However, the predicted bins still require generating 2048 samples and scoring them with the PRM — a cost that dominates the test-time budget being optimized. The paper does not report any measurement of the total cost (difficulty estimation + strategy execution) or compare it against a baseline that simply spends the same total generations on best-of-N. There is no experiment that shows the compute-optimal approach remains superior when difficulty estimation cost is included.

**Mitigation status.** The authors flag this as "a key avenue for future work" (Section 3.2) and suggest that training models to predict difficulty directly from the question text could eliminate the sampling cost. No such model is developed, trained, or evaluated. An adaptive scheme — e.g., generating a small number of initial samples, using the PRM's score distribution on those samples as a quick difficulty signal, and then allocating the remaining budget — is mentioned as a possibility but not implemented. Until this gap is closed, the practical deployability of difficulty-conditioned allocation remains unproven.

---

### Test-Time Compute Cannot Overcome Fundamental Capability Gaps on Hard Problems

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal framework operates under an implicit assumption that the base model possesses non-trivial probability mass on correct solutions — that is, the model *could* solve the problem if its sampling distribution were steered appropriately. On the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5), this assumption fails. The paper states (Section 5.3):

> "On the hardest questions (bin 5), no method makes meaningful progress — the base model simply lacks the capability to produce correct solutions regardless of how the budget is allocated."

**The consequence.** For problems where the base model's pass@1 is near zero — problems genuinely outside its capability distribution — no amount of search, revision, or compute-optimal allocation will help. This is a hard boundary on the method's applicability. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Section 7, Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5% accuracy regardless of budget, while the 14× larger model achieves substantially higher accuracy through pretraining alone. This means the approach offers **no path forward for genuinely novel or out-of-distribution reasoning** that exceeds the base model's training distribution. For such problems, scaling pretraining remains the only viable option. Practitioners with problem distributions skewed toward hard problems will see minimal benefit from test-time compute optimization.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The difficulty-bin analysis consistently shows near-zero performance on bin 5 across all methods. Figure 3 (right): bin 5 accuracy at 1–3% for all methods and all budgets. Figure 7 (right): bin 5 accuracy at ~2–3% irrespective of sequential-to-parallel ratio. Figure 9: bin 5 scaling line flat near 0–5% in the FLOPs-matched comparison. The FLOPs-matched bar charts (Figure 1) show that test-time compute with the smaller model underperforms the 14× larger model on hard questions by up to −52.9% (PRM search at R ≫ 1). This evidence is consistent and unambiguous.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about this limitation (Section 7 takeaway box explicitly notes that pretraining is preferable on hard problems) but offers no mitigation. This is a fundamental constraint rather than a solvable limitation within the current framework — no allocation strategy can surface correct trajectories that do not exist in the model's distribution. The paper does not explore whether combining test-time compute with retrieval-augmented generation, tool use, or other capability-expanding techniques could partially address this gap.

---

### All Results Are on a Single Benchmark Family with Two Model Families from One Architecture Class

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's empirical findings are drawn entirely from the MATH benchmark (500 test questions from high-school competition mathematics) using PaLM 2-S* models (a single architecture family from a single organization). The authors state (Section 4) that they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" but provide no cross-architecture or cross-domain validation. The MATH benchmark tests a specific kind of reasoning — multi-step mathematical deduction with closed-form symbolic answers — that may not generalize to other reasoning domains.

**The consequence.** Practitioners cannot assume that the difficulty-dependent scaling patterns observed in this paper — beam search degrades easy-problem performance due to verifier over-optimization; sequential revisions are optimal for easy problems while balanced sequential-parallel sampling is optimal for hard problems; compute-optimal allocation yields 4× efficiency gains — will transfer to other domains (code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA, open-ended generation) or other model families (different architectures, different scales, different pretraining recipes). The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend on PaLM 2-S*'s output distribution and calibration properties, which may differ substantially across model families. The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities. The MATH-specific grading function that enables reliable binary reward signals does not exist for many important real-world tasks. The paper's core findings may be specific to the MATH + PaLM 2-S* combination rather than universal properties of test-time compute scaling.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None beyond the MATH + PaLM 2-S* results. The paper does not include replication on other math benchmarks (GSM8K, SVAMP), other reasoning domains (code generation on HumanEval/MBPP, logical reasoning on ARC/FOLIO), or other model families (LLaMA, GPT, Claude). The claim of representativeness is asserted but not empirically supported.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation. The authors acknowledge it only implicitly by framing their contribution as an experimental analysis on MATH with PaLM 2-S*. A reader evaluating whether to adopt compute-optimal test-time scaling for a different domain or model family would need to conduct their own replication study — the paper provides methodology but no evidence of cross-domain transfer.

---

### The Revision Model Has a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate, and Revision Training Is Fragile

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained exclusively on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect followed by a correct target (Section 6.1). This creates a structural blind spot: the model has never been trained to recognize when the current answer is already correct, and it has no signal for what to do in that situation. The paper reports (Section 6.1):

> "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones using a naive approach."

**The consequence.** In a revision chain of length L, each step has a ~38% chance of corrupting a correct answer into an incorrect one. This means the revision process is not monotonically improving — the model can oscillate between correct and incorrect, and the final answer in a long chain may be worse than an intermediate answer earlier in the chain. The paper mitigates this by applying majority voting or verifier-based selection across the entire chain, not just taking the final revision. However, these patches introduce their own costs and limitations: majority voting requires generating many revision chains to achieve statistical reliability, and verifier-based selection transfers the problem to the verifier's accuracy (which, as the search experiments show, can be over-optimized). The reversion problem also means that the optimal chain length is problem-dependent and difficult to determine without ground-truth access — too short and you miss potential improvements; too long and you risk corrupting correct answers.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% figure is reported in Section 6.1. Figure 6 (left) shows that revision model pass@1 improves gradually through the chain (from ~18.2% at step 1 to ~24–25% by steps 15–20), which suggests the net effect is positive on average, but the per-step volatility and reversion rate are not directly visualized. The paper does not report what fraction of correct answers survive through a chain of length L, or how the reversion rate changes as a function of problem difficulty — both of which would be directly relevant to a practitioner deciding on chain length and selection strategy.

The fragility of revision training is further evidenced by the ReST^EM experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16): attempting to further optimize the revision model with RL-style training caused performance to **degrade substantially** with sequential revisions. At 256 generations, fully sequential performance with the ReST^EM-trained model drops to approximately 33.5% compared to roughly 38.5% at the optimal ratio for the standard revision model. The authors hypothesize that "on-policy data collection exacerbates spurious correlations in revision data, causing the model to fail to learn the revision task properly." This suggests that the revision approach is sensitive to training methodology in ways that are not fully understood.

**Mitigation status.** The paper provides a partial mitigation — within-chain selection via majority voting or verifier — but acknowledges this is not a principled solution. The authors do not explore training the revision model with examples of correct answers that should be preserved (i.e., including "correct → correct" trajectories in the training data), which would be a natural mitigation. This limitation is not flagged as future work but represents a genuine practical obstacle to deploying revision-based test-time compute at scale.

### Difficulty Bins Are Static, Coarse, and Require a Fixed Pre-Computed Strategy Lookup

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal allocation uses a static, five-quintile difficulty discretization. For each bin and each budget level, a single strategy (which search algorithm, which revision ratio) is pre-computed on a validation fold and applied uniformly to all questions in that bin during evaluation. The bin boundaries are determined once from the model's pass@1 distribution and are held fixed throughout training and evaluation. The paper acknowledges (Section 3.2) that difficulty estimation currently requires generating 2048 samples per question, and does not explore continuous or adaptive difficulty estimation.

**The consequence.** Within a single difficulty bin, there may be substantial heterogeneity — a question at the easy end of bin 3 (pass@1 ~0.48) and one at the hard end (pass@1 ~0.32) receive identical strategies, even though different strategies might be optimal for each. The five-bin discretization is a coarse approximation to what is fundamentally a continuous relationship between difficulty and optimal strategy. Finer discretization would require more data per bin to reliably estimate optimal strategies, creating a bias-variance tradeoff that the paper does not characterize. Furthermore, the static nature of the bins means there is no mechanism for **dynamic strategy adjustment** mid-computation — for instance, starting with a few parallel samples, assessing whether the problem appears easy or hard based on early verifier scores, and then reallocating the remaining budget accordingly. This adaptive approach could potentially subsume the difficulty estimation cost into the problem-solving process itself, but it is not explored.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The difficulty-bin analysis (Figures 3 right, 7 right) shows clear qualitative differences between bins, validating the general approach of difficulty-conditioned allocation. However, the sensitivity to the number of bins is never tested — the paper reports results only for five bins, with no comparison to three bins, ten bins, or a continuous difficulty-conditioned policy. The gap between oracle and predicted difficulty bins (Figures 4, 8) is small but present, suggesting that better difficulty estimation could further improve results. The cross-validation protocol (two-fold within each bin on the 500-question test set, so ~50 questions per fold per bin) means the optimal strategy per bin is selected based on a small sample, which introduces variance that is not quantified.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not explore finer-grained or continuous difficulty approaches, dynamic allocation, or adaptive difficulty estimation. The authors flag the difficulty estimation cost as a key area for future work (Section 3.2) but do not address the coarseness of the discretization or the static nature of the allocation. A natural extension — training a learned policy network that maps (estimated difficulty, remaining budget) to strategy hyperparameters — is not discussed. The five-bin discretization is a reasonable proof-of-concept but leaves substantial room for more sophisticated allocation mechanisms.

---

### The 14× Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained, and the FLOPs-Matched Comparison Favors Test-Time Compute

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm rather than the Chinchilla-optimal paradigm where both parameters and data are scaled equally. The authors acknowledge (Section 7):

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

**The consequence.** A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with 14× more total FLOPs (scaling both parameters and data according to the Hoffmann et al. scaling laws) would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model. This means the pretraining baseline is **weaker than it could be** under optimal training, and the reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining (e.g., +27.8% on easy-to-medium questions at R ≪ 1 in Figure 1) may shrink or reverse against a properly compute-optimal larger model. The paper's conclusion that "test-time compute can substitute for pretraining on problems within the model's capability range" is stated without the qualification that it was tested against a potentially suboptimal pretraining baseline.

Additionally, the 14× larger model uses only **greedy decoding** in the comparison — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no test-time compute of its own. This is acknowledged as a deliberate design choice but means the comparison is not "test-time compute vs. pretraining" so much as "small model with optimized test-time compute vs. large model with no test-time compute." A fairer comparison would give the larger model a modest test-time compute budget (e.g., best-of-8 or best-of-16) to see whether the gap persists when both models are allowed some inference-time optimization. The paper does not report this ablation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The FLOPs-matched results (Figure 9, Figure 1 bar charts) are presented with the parameter-only-scaled baseline and greedy decoding. The paper is transparent about the design choices but does not quantify how much of the test-time compute advantage is attributable to the pretraining baseline being suboptimal versus test-time compute genuinely being more efficient. The three R values tested (0.16, 0.79, 22) span the relevant regimes but are evaluated against the same fixed baseline.

**Mitigation status.** The authors acknowledge the limitation explicitly and defer the Chinchilla-optimal comparison to future work. A greedy-decoding baseline for the larger model is noted but not defended as the most appropriate comparison. A practitioner seeking to decide between "train a larger model" and "deploy a smaller model with more test-time compute" would need to account for the possibility that compute-optimal pretraining would narrow or reverse the reported gaps, particularly on medium-difficulty problems where the paper's advantage is most consequential. The paper would have been strengthened by at least one Chinchilla-optimal pretraining comparison, or by giving the larger model a modest test-time budget, to establish the robustness of the training-inference tradeoff finding.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a genuine conceptual reframing of the exploration problem in RLVR—one that shifts the community's focus from **how much** the policy explores to **what** it explores. The magnitude of this shift is not paradigmatic (the RLVR framework itself is unchanged) but it is more than incremental: by diagnosing the exploration bottleneck as a coverage failure rather than a quantity failure, and by demonstrating that structured, context-driven exploration can substitute for both brute-force sampling and privileged correctness, the paper opens a new axis of research that was previously underappreciated.

The most immediate impact is on how the RLVR community allocates research effort. Before this work, the dominant approaches to exploration fell into two camps: (1) scale up the number of rollouts and hope diversity emerges naturally, or (2) inject privileged information—oracle solutions, expert feedback, intermediate steps—to make correct trajectories more attainable. Both camps implicitly treated exploration as synonymous with feasibility: exploration succeeds when the policy generates at least one correct trajectory. NUDGERL's central insight is that **exploration is about coverage, not just feasibility**, and that a policy can have substantial probability mass on correct trajectories that it never actually samples because its sampling distribution is concentrated on a narrow set of dominant reasoning modes. This reframing is supported by the formal decomposition in Equation 3, which shows that unsampled correct probability mass ($U_{\text{pos},2}$) acts as a penalty on learning—the model "knows" about correct strategies but cannot access them during training, and this latent knowledge actively hinders improvement.

This diagnostic insight resolves a latent tension in the RLVR literature. On one hand, methods like GRPO have shown that models can bootstrap reasoning capabilities from binary reward signals alone, suggesting that the policies already possess useful knowledge that RL can surface. On the other hand, brute-force rollout scaling shows diminishing returns and, as the paper demonstrates (Table 1), can actually degrade performance at larger budgets (GRPO-64 drops from GRPO-32's 0.487 to 0.451 on Qwen3, and catastrophically from 0.281 to 0.160 on Olmo3). These seemingly contradictory findings—"RLVR works, but more sampling doesn't monotonically help"—are unified by the coverage diagnosis: the policy has valuable knowledge, but its sampling distribution fails to cover the space of that knowledge, and simply generating more samples from the same narrow distribution compounds the instability of the advantage estimation without substantially improving coverage.

The paper also resolves a tension between feasibility-oriented and diversity-oriented approaches to exploration. Privileged information methods like POPE (Qu et al., 2026) operate on the assumption that the primary barrier is that hard problems are unsolvable without hints—provide a running start, and the policy will learn. NUDGERL's empirical results challenge this assumption directly. On Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, NUDGERL's cheap, unverified strategy hints from GPT-4o-mini (0.489 average pass@1) outperform POPE's oracle solution prefixes from DeepSeek Reasoner v3.2 (0.467), despite the oracle being generated by a dramatically stronger model. Moreover, the random-context ablation (Figure 6b) shows that deliberately selecting the "best" contexts based on base-model success rate degrades performance compared to random sampling (0.552 vs. 0.598 average pass@1). Together, these results resolve the apparent conflict between "privileged information helps" and "privileged information constrains diversity": privileged information *does* make individual problems more solvable, but it concentrates exploration on a narrow set of known-good paths, sacrificing the diversity that would surface genuinely novel strategies. The optimal approach depends on whether the bottleneck is feasibility (in which case privileged information helps) or discoverability (in which case it hurts). The paper's results suggest that, for the tested models and benchmarks, discoverability is the more pressing bottleneck.

This reframing makes several research directions newly attractive. **Verifier robustness and over-optimization**, previously considered a distinct problem in the RLHF and test-time compute literatures, becomes relevant to RLVR exploration: if diversity-driven exploration surfaces trajectories the verifier cannot reliably evaluate, the learning signal degrades. **Difficulty-conditioned exploration strategies**, building on the compute-optimal scaling framework from prior work, become a natural extension: if the value of structured exploration depends on whether the bottleneck is feasibility or discoverability, different exploration strategies might be optimal for different problem difficulties. **Distillation as a first-class RLVR component** gains new importance: the paper's distillation ablation (Figure 7b) shows that transferring context-discovered behaviors to the base policy is not an optional enhancement but a necessity, and that the distillation coefficient has a non-trivial optimum (0.1, with both 0.0 and 0.5 degrading performance). This suggests that any RLVR method that uses conditioning during training—whether through hints, templates, or few-shot examples—must include an explicit transfer mechanism to the deployment distribution, and that this mechanism requires careful tuning.

Conversely, certain research directions become comparatively less promising. **Brute-force rollout scaling as a primary exploration strategy** is directly challenged: the paper shows not only diminishing returns but also training instability at larger budgets, suggesting that simply allocating more compute to GRPO-style sampling is a dead end beyond moderate scales. **Increasing update aggressiveness without improving exploration quality** is similarly undermined: the $\epsilon_{\text{high}}$ scaling experiment (Figure 7c) shows that while GRPO benefits modestly from decoupled clipping, it plateaus and then degrades, while NUDGERL with conservative clipping ($\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.2$) outperforms all GRPO variants. This suggests that the RLVR community may have overinvested in optimization-side interventions (clipping schedules, entropy bonuses, normalization strategies) and underinvested in sampling-side interventions that directly shape *which* trajectories are generated. **Oracle-guided methods that provide narrow, single-path supervision** are not rendered obsolete—they may still be valuable for genuinely infeasible problems—but their comparative advantage shrinks if structured diversity can achieve equivalent or better results with dramatically cheaper supervision, as NUDGERL demonstrates.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Model-adaptive context generation that evolves with the policy.** The paper identifies a fundamental limitation in Section 5: "contexts are generated independently of the model being trained. The benefit of Context Forcing stems from inducing trajectories that are unlikely under the current policy. As training progresses, however, a fixed context pool may become less informative as the policy adapts." This suggests a concrete follow-up: train a lightweight context proposal model that takes the current policy's rollout statistics (e.g., which reasoning strategies are being sampled frequently, which reward rates each strategy achieves) and generates new contexts specifically targeting under-explored or recently unsuccessful reasoning modes. The experiment would compare a static context pool (the paper's current approach) against a pool that is regenerated every K training steps using the evolving policy's rollout data, measuring both final pass@1 and the diversity of reasoning strategies at convergence. A negative result—finding that dynamic contexts provide no benefit beyond static ones—would be equally informative, suggesting that the primary value of contexts is in initial exploration diversification rather than ongoing adaptation.

**Difficulty-conditioned exploration strategy selection.** The paper does not analyze how NUDGERL's benefits vary with problem difficulty, but the prior compute-optimal scaling work demonstrated that the optimal test-time strategy depends sharply on whether problems are easy, medium, or hard for the base model. A natural extension would replicate NUDGERL's training but track performance separately for problems binned by the base model's pre-training pass@1 rate. The hypothesis is that NUDGERL provides the largest gains on medium-difficulty problems (where the model has latent but unsampled correct strategies—exactly the coverage bottleneck) and minimal gains on very easy problems (where correct strategies are already frequently sampled) or very hard problems (where no correct strategies exist in the distribution). A follow-up could then implement difficulty-conditioned context selection: use cheap difficulty estimation (e.g., the base model's pass@1 from a small number of initial samples) to decide whether to apply NUDGERL, standard GRPO, or skip the problem entirely. The experiment would measure whether difficulty-conditioned allocation further improves the compute-efficiency gains beyond NUDGERL's uniform application.

**Stress-testing context quality boundaries.** The paper's random-vs-top-ranked ablation (Figure 6b) shows that context quality (measured by base-model success rate) is less important than context diversity, but it does not establish the lower bound: how poor can contexts be before they become counterproductive? A systematic experiment would generate contexts of deliberately varying quality—from frontier-model-generated strategies (DeepSeek, GPT-4), through mid-tier models (GPT-4o-mini, as used in the paper), to template-generated contexts ("Try using algebra," "Try drawing a diagram"), to deliberately misleading contexts ("Use the quadratic formula" for a geometry problem). The goal is to map the relationship between context relevance and NUDGERL performance, identifying the threshold where diversity forcing transitions from helpful (providing genuine strategic alternatives) to harmful (distracting the policy with irrelevant directions). The random-sampling result suggests the threshold may be surprisingly permissive, but quantifying it would provide practical guidance for practitioners with limited access to capable hint-generation models.

**Combining NUDGERL with verifier-guided test-time search.** The paper studies structured exploration during RLVR training, but does not combine it with test-time compute strategies (beam search, best-of-N with verifier scoring) that have been shown to improve inference-time performance. A natural integration would train the policy with NUDGERL to maximize its coverage of diverse reasoning strategies, then at inference time apply PRM-guided beam search over the policy's outputs to select the best trajectory. The hypothesis is that NUDGERL-trained policies, having been forced to explore a broader range of strategies during training, would provide a richer and more diverse candidate set for the verifier to select from, potentially yielding larger test-time compute gains than policies trained with standard GRPO. The experiment would compare pass@k curves for NUDGERL-trained vs. GRPO-trained policies under the same test-time compute budget, measuring whether NUDGERL's training-time exploration diversity translates to improved inference-time sample efficiency.

**Revision models trained with diversity-forcing contexts.** The paper focuses on single-turn RLVR, but the prior compute-optimal work demonstrated that iterative revision—where the model conditions on its own previous incorrect answers and generates improved solutions—can be another effective form of test-time compute. A direct extension would train a revision model using NUDGERL-style diversity forcing during the revision chain: at each revision step, condition on a randomly sampled strategy context to encourage the model to try different approaches in successive revisions rather than making incremental refinements to the same dominant strategy. The experiment would measure whether diversity-forced revision chains produce more distinct strategies across steps, and whether this leads to higher final accuracy when using within-chain selection (majority voting or verifier). This would test whether NUDGERL's core insight—that structured diversity improves exploration—generalizes from parallel sampling to sequential refinement.

**Cross-domain replication with domain-specific context generation.** All results are on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, where "strategies" map cleanly to named mathematical concepts and theorems. A critical stress-test would replicate NUDGERL on code generation (HumanEval, MBPP, LiveCodeBench), where "strategies" might be algorithmic paradigms (dynamic programming, greedy, divide-and-conquer) or data structure choices (hash map, heap, union-find). The key methodological question is whether GPT-4o-mini can generate useful strategy-level contexts for code problems as reliably as for math problems, or whether domain-specific context generation procedures are needed. A negative result—finding that NUDGERL provides minimal gains on code generation despite working well on math—would establish an important boundary condition on the method's applicability and would motivate research into automated strategy extraction from problem statements or solution corpora.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient post-training for small research groups and startups.** The paper's most directly actionable finding is that NUDGERL with 8 rollouts per prompt matches or exceeds GRPO with 32–64 rollouts, representing a 4–8× reduction in the generation compute required for RLVR training. For a group fine-tuning a 4B–7B parameter model on 17,000 problems (the DAPO-17k scale), moving from GRPO-32 (544,000 total rollouts per gradient step if processing the full dataset) to NUDGERL-8 (136,000 rollouts) reduces GPU-hours by approximately 4× while maintaining or improving final performance. The context generation cost—one GPT-4o-mini API call per problem, totaling roughly $50–100 for a 17,000-problem dataset—is a one-time expense amortized across all training runs. This makes NUDGERL particularly attractive for academic labs and smaller organizations that cannot afford the compute budgets required for large-scale GRPO training, democratizing access to state-of-the-art RLVR post-training. The paper's finding that random context sampling outperforms oracle-filtered contexts (Figure 6b) further reduces the barrier: practitioners do not need to curate high-quality hints; they can simply prompt a cheap LLM for keyword-level strategies and use them as-is.

**Improving data efficiency in self-improvement and synthetic data pipelines.** In self-improvement loops (e.g., STaR, ReST^EM, rejection sampling fine-tuning), a model generates solutions to training problems, filters for correct ones, and fine-tunes on those successes. The quality and diversity of the generated solutions directly determine the quality of the fine-tuning data. NUDGERL's Strategy Nudging mechanism can be applied during the data generation phase of such pipelines: for each training problem, generate rollouts under multiple strategy contexts to increase reasoning diversity, then apply verifier-based filtering to select correct trajectories across diverse strategies. The resulting fine-tuning dataset would contain a broader range of valid reasoning approaches than data generated under naive sampling, potentially producing a more robust and generalizable fine-tuned model. The paper's distillation objective provides a template for how to train on such context-conditioned data while maintaining the model's ability to perform under the original prompt. The specific benefit: a self-improvement pipeline using NUDGERL-style context forcing during data generation could require fewer total rollouts to achieve the same fine-tuning data diversity, reducing the generation cost of each self-improvement iteration by a factor proportional to the diversity gain shown in Figure 1c.

**Deployment with smaller models where diversity of reasoning strategies matters.** For applications that require not just correct answers but diverse, interpretable reasoning paths—educational tools that show students multiple solution methods, auditing systems that need to verify reasoning from multiple angles, or ensemble systems that aggregate across diverse reasoning strategies—the paper's finding that NUDGERL internalizes diverse strategies into the base policy (Figure 5: dropout reward occasionally exceeds hinted reward) is directly applicable. A model trained with NUDGERL can, at inference time without any context hints, generate solutions using a wider variety of mathematical strategies than a GRPO-trained model. The case study (Section 4.4) illustrates this concretely: the GRPO-trained model attempts the shoelace formula strategy only once in 32 rollouts, while the NUDGERL-trained model uses it 10 times and succeeds 6 times. For an educational application where showing multiple valid approaches adds pedagogical value, NUDGERL training directly increases the probability that a student query will surface diverse solution methods. The specific metric would be the number of distinct correct strategies produced per K samples—a metric the paper's diversity analysis (Figure 1c) suggests NUDGERL meaningfully improves.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions NUDGERL against two alternatives: brute-force rollout scaling (GRPO with larger N) and oracle-guided methods (POPE), and the experimental design provides clear guidance on when each is preferable. The decision rule that emerges from the paper's results is:

- **Prefer NUDGERL over GRPO rollout scaling when** compute budget for generation is constrained (you can only afford 8–16 rollouts per prompt) and the base model has non-trivial but hard-to-access probability mass on correct solutions (base pass@1 in the 0.1–0.5 range on the target benchmark). Evidence: NUDGERL-8 matches GRPO-32 on Qwen3 (0.489 vs. 0.487) and exceeds GRPO-64; NUDGERL-8 exceeds GRPO-32 on Olmo3 (0.285 vs. 0.281); the gain is most pronounced on the hardest benchmarks (AIME24/25) where correct trajectories are rarest and diversity forcing is most valuable. The one-time offline cost of generating contexts with a small LLM is amortized across all training runs.

- **Prefer NUDGERL over oracle-guided methods (POPE) when** you lack access to a strong oracle model (e.g., cannot afford DeepSeek Reasoner API costs or have privacy constraints) or when you suspect the oracle's solution strategy is narrow and may limit the policy's exploration of alternative reasoning approaches. Evidence: NUDGERL's GPT-4o-mini-generated hints (0.489) outperform POPE's DeepSeek Reasoner v3.2 prefixes (0.467) on Qwen3; random context sampling outperforms top-ranked filtering (0.598 vs. 0.552), indicating that diversity, not hint quality, drives performance.

- **Consider standard GRPO with moderate rollout budgets ($N = 16$–$32$) when** the base model's pass@1 is very low (well below 0.1) and the primary bottleneck is genuinely feasibility, not discoverability—i.e., the model has essentially no correct probability mass to surface, and structured exploration cannot create capabilities from nothing. Evidence: on Olmo3 AIME24 (base pass@1 = 0.134), GRPO-32 slightly exceeds NUDGERL-8 on individual benchmarks (0.195 vs. 0.190), though NUDGERL still wins on average. This boundary condition is not firmly established by the paper's limited model set but is consistent with the theoretical framework.

- **Avoid GRPO with $N \geq 64$** (and possibly $N \geq 48$) on models of this scale based on the paper's consistent finding of performance degradation at large rollout budgets, likely due to instability in group-relative advantage estimation when within-group reward variance is high. Evidence: GRPO-64 underperforms GRPO-8 on both Qwen3 (0.451 vs. 0.454) and Olmo3 (0.160 vs. 0.268), with the Olmo3 degradation being catastrophic. The paper does not identify the precise $N$ threshold where degradation begins, but the $N = 32$ to $N = 64$ transition is clearly problematic for both models.
