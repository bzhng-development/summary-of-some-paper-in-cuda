Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman speedrunning guide (a tactical playbook for accelerating from Mid-level to Senior Engineer in big tech):

**Logistics refresher: the lagging-promo mechanic**

Promotions in big tech are not immediate; you need at least six months of consistent performance at the next level before the system will recognize you. This **lagging promotion** structure means your review packet is always looking backward, not forward. Your manager builds that packet and represents you to the promotion committee, but as you climb, they face increasing pushback—higher IC levels carry more ambiguity about what “meets expectations” even means ("What does an IC8 even look like?"). Consequently, you have to work *with* your manager to gather feedback from the committee, treating the manager as the messenger and the committee as the ultimate source of truth.

**The strategy: find L5 scope and shift your time**

The core playbook has three pillars. First, **find L5 scope as soon as possible**—your manager or tech lead can help confirm whether the work qualifies, and once it does, you can “just obsess about delivering it” to guide you in the right direction. Second, **focus on team leadership and influence**: raw code output is no longer the most important thing. You must deliberately shift time away from personal execution toward leading workstreams and influencing your team. Third, **uplift your teammates**—seek out opportunities to mentor others early, because mentorship relationships cannot be rushed and are a hard requirement for the Senior bar.

Promotion timelines from this level are mapped in three tiers: one half is exceptional but rare (usually someone who was under-leveled and just got adjusted); two halves is a “great” and ambitious target if you find L5 scope in your first half; three halves is a steady, realistic pace for many. The extra ~$120k in compensation and access to more impactful work make the behavior changes worth it.

**Ryan’s own growth path: three halves to Senior**

In **H1** (L4, Exceeds Expectations), he wrapped up the workstream that had earned him the L4 promo, then picked up another L4 project and spent a ton of time on engineering craft—deprecating legacy systems nobody else would touch because they were “dangerous and not that impactful.” He exhibited zero L5 behaviors that half. In **H2** (L4, No Rating due to COVID cancellation), his manager handed him an L5 workstream to cut video-messaging latency in half, leading roughly six engineers. He also began a side project that turned into a multi-half collaboration with another team and took on an intern who “did a phenomenal job” helping execute both roadmaps. The pandemic wiped out performance reviews that cycle, but the behaviors had started. In **H3** (L4 → L5 promotion, Greatly Exceeds), he doubled down on the cross-org scope from H2, developed a multi-half roadmap, influenced another team to invest several engineers in revamping the Instagram video-ads pipeline, built out a second workstream, and mentored another engineer to deliver it. His impact that half “could’ve met expectations at the L6 level.” The combination of massive impact, team-level influence, and mentorship sealed the promotion.

**What he learned along the way**

The **skill of tech leading** is an extension of strong execution. Ryan felt unsure leading an initiative with only two years of experience, but leaning on his ability to land code—the same skill that got him to L4—made him comfortable helping others do the same: “The L5 behavior of team-level influence is just helping others do the same.” He also discovered that **working hard created a portfolio effect**: he had a ton of workstreams in flight simultaneously, which derisked the promo by increasing the odds that one would produce outsized impact. He wasn’t trying to design a derisking strategy; he was “just throwing myself at any problems that came my way,” and in hindsight that turned out to be an effective approach.

The biggest tactical regret was his first half as an L4, where he chased time-intensive, low-impact tech-debt cleanups because he loved the craft. “I would’ve had more impact if I influenced someone else to do them while I found L5 scope instead.” The shift from L4 to L5 is fundamentally a pivot from being the person who *does* the work to the person who *makes the work happen through others*—practicing communication, shifting time from execution to leadership and influence, and once you find an L5 workstream, obsessing over shipping it “no matter what it takes.”

---

His **L5 scope** playbook—find it, confirm it, then obsess over delivery—sits alongside his accidental **portfolio effect** derisking strategy, where throwing himself at every problem increased his surface area for outsized impact. Together they point to a meta-lesson that threads through the whole guide: the **lagging promotion** mechanic rewards consistent, demonstrated behaviors over time, which is why mentorship relationships “can’t be rushed” and why the pivot from doing to leading must be deliberate.
