# Nash Learning from Human Feedback

**ArXiv:** [2312.00886](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00886)

## 🎯 Pitch

Standard RLHF collapses rich pairwise human preferences into a single reward score, which can misrepresent what people actually want. This paper shows that training an LLM to be a Nash equilibrium—consistently beating any other response—outperforms reward maximization and even matches a stronger preference oracle.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Nash learning from human feedback (NLHF)**, an alternative pipeline to RLHF that learns a pairwise preference model from human feedback and then optimizes a policy to be the Nash equilibrium of that model — a policy consistently preferred over any competing policy — rather than maximizing a scalar reward. The approach is evaluated on a text summarization task using T5X models trained on the TL;DR dataset, comparing Nash-MD-PG (which trains by competing against a geometric mixture of the current and reference policies) and Nash-EMA-PG (which uses an exponential moving average of past parameters) against an RLHF baseline. On a pairwise evaluation using PaLM 2 Large as a preference oracle, the Nash-MD models with mixture parameters β ∈ [0.125, 0.375] emerge as the best-performing methods, with MD1 (β = 0.125) surpassing all other models including RLHF — establishing that optimizing for the Nash equilibrium of a learned preference model is a practical and effective alternative to reward maximization for aligning LLMs with human preferences.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RLHF's Reliance on Scalar Reward Models Is Fundamentally Limiting

The paper addresses a specific, structural limitation in the dominant paradigm for aligning large language models with human preferences. The standard RLHF pipeline — as implemented in systems like InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022), ChatGPT (OpenAI, 2022), and Claude (Bai et al., 2022) — operates in two stages:

1. **Learn a reward model** from pairwise human preference data, typically using the Bradley-Terry (BT) model (Bradley & Terry, 1952), which assigns a scalar "Elo score" to each possible response.
2. **Optimize the LLM's policy** against this reward model using reinforcement learning, treating the reward model's scalar output as the objective to maximize.

This pipeline has produced remarkably capable conversational agents, but the paper argues it contains **three embedded failure modes** that are not incidental — they stem from the mathematical structure of converting pairwise preferences into scalar rewards, and from optimizing against that scalar rather than directly against the preference data.

### Failure Mode 1: Bradley-Terry Reward Models Cannot Capture the Full Spectrum of Human Preferences

The Bradley-Terry model assumes that the probability of preferring response $y$ over $y'$ can be expressed as:

$$P(y \succ y' \mid x) = \sigma(r(x, y) - r(x, y'))$$

where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function and $r(x, y)$ is a scalar "reward" or "Elo score" assigned to each response individually. This is a strong structural assumption: it requires preferences to be **transitive** and **additive**. If $y_1$ is preferred over $y_2$, and $y_2$ is preferred over $y_3$, the BT model must also assign $y_1$ a higher score than $y_3$. The paper identifies two ways this fails in practice:

**Non-transitive human preferences (Section 3.1, Appendix C):** The paper provides a concrete example in Appendix C.1 using a setup analogous to non-transitive dice (Gardner, 1970). Three policies $\pi_1$, $\pi_2$, $\pi_3$ each generate responses uniformly from different subsets of integers, and preference is defined as drawing one response from each policy and comparing numeric values. The result is:

> $P(\pi_1 \succ \pi_2) = P(\pi_2 \succ \pi_3) = P(\pi_3 \succ \pi_1) = 5/9$

This is a perfectly coherent preference model — each pairwise comparison is well-defined — but no scalar reward function can represent it. A BT-based reward model would necessarily distort these preferences, assigning scores that fail to capture some of the pairwise relationships.

More subtly, Appendix C.2 demonstrates that **even if every individual human has perfectly transitive preferences**, the aggregate preference model over a population can still be non-transitive. The paper constructs an example with three responses and three human types, each with a transitive total ordering, but where the average (expected) preference contains a cycle. This is not a pathological edge case — it occurs whenever different subpopulations have systematically different preference orderings (e.g., one group prefers concise summaries, another prefers comprehensive ones). In such cases, any BT reward model is structurally incapable of representing the true preference distribution, because it forces a single global ordering.

**Elo score maximization $\neq$ preference optimization (Appendix A):** Even when the preference model *is* perfectly captured by the Bradley-Terry model — meaning no non-transitivity — maximizing the expected Elo score can disagree with maximizing the probability of winning against the relevant population. The paper constructs a three-action example where the BT model perfectly represents the preference probabilities, but:

> "the optimum of the expected reward and preference may be different"

Specifically, when optimization is constrained to a subset of policies (e.g., via KL-regularization toward a reference distribution), the policy that maximizes expected Elo score differs from the policy that is most preferred against any alternative in that subset. The paper proves this formally in Proposition 2 (Appendix B), showing that for a given sampling distribution $\pi$, the fitted BT reward model $r^\pi$ satisfies $P(y \succ \pi) = P^\pi_{BT}(y \succ \pi)$ only for responses $y$ in the support of $\pi$, but the implied BT preference model $P^\pi_{BT}$ need not match the true preference model $P$ globally. This means the reward model is a **local approximation** of the preference model, valid only near the data distribution it was trained on.

### Failure Mode 2: Reward Models Are Inherently Dependent on the Training Data Distribution

This is perhaps the paper's most subtle but practically crucial critique (Section 3.3, Appendix B, Theorem 2). The BT reward model learns an Elo score for each response *relative to the distribution of other responses it was trained on*. The optimization objective for the reward model is:

$$r^\pi = \arg\max_r \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \rho, \, y, y' \sim \pi(\cdot|x), \, Z \sim \nu} \left[\log \sigma(r(x, y^Z_w) - r(x, y^Z_l))\right]$$

where $y^Z_w$ and $y^Z_l$ are the preferred and less-preferred responses (among $y$ and $y'$) according to randomly sampled human $Z \sim \nu$, and $\pi$ is the policy that generated the training data. The superscript $\pi$ on $r^\pi$ is deliberate: the optimal reward model **depends on $\pi$**, the data-generating policy. Theorem 2 (Appendix B) proves that if the preference model cannot be perfectly expressed as a BT model — which, as argued above, is the generic case — then changing the sampling distribution $\pi$ changes the reward model $r^\pi$.

Why does this matter? In iterative RLHF pipelines, data is collected from the current policy, a reward model is trained, the policy is optimized against that reward model, new data is collected from the updated policy, and the cycle repeats. At each iteration, the previous reward model is **mismatched** to the new data distribution. It must be completely retrained, because its scores are defined relative to the old distribution. The paper notes:

> "the reward model from a prior iteration diverges from the next iteration due to shifts in data distributions, necessitating complete relearning"

By contrast, a preference model $P(y \succ y' \mid x)$ takes two responses as input and predicts the probability that a random human prefers $y$ to $y'$. Its optimal form is:

$$P^*(y \succ y' \mid x) = \mathbb{P}_{Z \sim \nu}(\text{Human } Z \text{ prefers } y \text{ over } y' \text{ given } x)$$

This quantity depends only on $x$, $y$, and $y'$ — **not** on the distribution from which $y$ and $y'$ were sampled. The paper emphasizes this as a fundamental structural advantage:

> "in this ideal case of perfect representations, a preference model is insensitive to the data generation distribution $(\rho, \pi, \pi')$ whereas a reward model depends on it"

This means that in iterative improvement pipelines, the preference model can be **preserved and enriched** across iterations by simply adding new data, rather than retrained from scratch. This is a practical advantage that affects both computational cost and statistical efficiency.

### Failure Mode 3: Optimizing Against a Fixed Reward Model Ignores the Strategic Nature of Alignment

The standard RLHF objective is:

$$\pi^* = \arg\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \rho, y \sim \pi(\cdot|x)} [r(x, y)] - \tau \text{KL}(\pi, \mu)$$

where $\mu$ is a reference policy (usually the supervised fine-tuned model) and $\tau$ controls regularization strength. This optimizes the policy against a **fixed** reward function. The paper argues this misses a crucial aspect of alignment: the policy is not just being evaluated in isolation — it is being evaluated **relative to alternative policies** that could have been deployed instead.

The core insight of NLHF is to reframe alignment as a two-player game:

> "Our objective is to calculate the corresponding Nash equilibrium. This equilibrium represents a policy that consistently produces responses preferred, as determined by the preference model, over responses generated by any alternative policy."

Formally (Section 3, Equation 1):

$$\pi^* = \arg\max_\pi \min_{\pi'} P(\pi \succ \pi')$$

where $P(\pi \succ \pi') = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \rho, y \sim \pi(\cdot|x), y' \sim \pi'(\cdot|x)} [P(y \succ y' \mid x)]$. This is a **minimax** objective: the policy $\pi$ tries to maximize its win rate, while an adversarial opponent $\pi'$ tries to minimize it by finding the policy that best exploits $\pi$'s weaknesses. The Nash equilibrium is the policy $\pi^*$ that achieves a win rate of $\geq 1/2$ against any opponent — it is, by definition, not exploitable.

The difference between this and standard RLHF is not merely philosophical. Section 3.2 provides a concrete example with three response types and a population of three human types with conflicting preferences:

- Type 1 (probability $1/3 - \epsilon$): prefers $y_2$ over $y_1$ strongly
- Type 2 (probability $1/3 + \epsilon/2$): prefers $y_1$ over $y_3$ strongly
- Type 3 (probability $1/3 + \epsilon/2$): prefers $y_3$ over $y_2$ strongly

When $\epsilon = 0$ (uniform human population), the RLHF solution (maximizing expected reward under a BT model) produces a **deterministic** policy selecting a single action — either $y_1$, $y_2$, or $y_3$ depending on the arbitrarily small $\epsilon$. By contrast, the Nash equilibrium assigns **roughly equal probability** to all three actions. The paper argues:

> "the Nash solution of the preference model... is more aligned with the diversity of human preferences than the optimum of the reward model (i.e., the RLHF solution), which would deterministically select a single action"

This connects to the broader problem of **preference diversity** (Moskovitz et al., 2023): real human populations are heterogeneous, and a single "best" response may not exist. The Nash equilibrium naturally produces a mixed strategy that respects this diversity, while reward maximization collapses to a single point that satisfies only the largest (or slightly largest) subpopulation.

### The Gap: Prior Work Lacked a Convergent Algorithm for Last-Iterate Nash Equilibrium in Preference Models

Given the theoretical advantages of the NLHF formulation, the practical question is: **how do you compute the Nash equilibrium of a learned preference model at scale?**

The paper surveys the relevant game-solving literature in Section 5 and identifies a specific gap. Classical algorithms for computing Nash equilibria in convex-concave constant-sum games fall into two categories:

- **Convergence on average:** Algorithms like fictitious play (Brown, 1951; Robinson, 1951) and counterfactual regret minimization (Zinkevich et al., 2007) guarantee that the **uniform mixture** of past policies converges to the Nash equilibrium, but the **last iterate** (i.e., the current policy) may cycle indefinitely. In the context of LLMs, storing and sampling from a mixture of all past policies is prohibitively expensive — each policy is a model with billions of parameters.

- **Last-iterate convergence with storage requirements:** Optimistic mirror descent variants (Daskalakis & Panageas, 2019; Munos et al., 2020) achieve last-iterate convergence, but typically require maintaining and playing against a combination of past policies — still costly in memory.

The paper positions its algorithm **Nash-MD** as filling this gap. Nash-MD:

1. Achieves **last-iterate convergence** to the regularized Nash equilibrium with an $O(1/T)$ rate in KL-divergence (Theorem 1).
2. Plays against a single **geometric mixture** $\pi^\mu_t$ of the current policy $\pi_t$ and the reference policy $\mu$ — no storage of past policies required.
3. The mixture is defined as $\pi^\mu_t(y) \propto \pi_t(y)^{1-\eta_t \tau} \mu(y)^{\eta_t \tau}$, which can be computed on-the-fly from the current policy parameters and the fixed reference model.

The paper explicitly contrasts this with prior approaches that would be impractical for LLMs:

> "unlike in fictitious play... we play against a single (geometric) mixture... This is important in situations, such as in LLMs, where storing and generating sample from several policies is costly"

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper does not claim to be the first to consider preferences or Nash equilibria in LLM alignment. It acknowledges prior theoretical work by Wang et al. (2023) on learning Nash equilibria of human preference models in factored Markov games, and the concurrent work of Jacob et al. (2023a;b) exploring Nash equilibria in language model generation. It also acknowledges that the online version of IPO (Calandriello et al., 2024) approximates the Nash equilibrium of a preference model using a particular case of Nash-MD (specifically, the $\beta = 0$ self-play variant).

Rather, the paper positions itself as providing:

1. **A comprehensive conceptual framework** (Section 3) that formally defines NLHF, articulates why preference models are preferable to reward models (non-transitivity tolerance, distribution independence, diversity alignment), and establishes the regularized Nash equilibrium as the solution concept.

2. **A novel, provably convergent algorithm** (Nash-MD, Section 6) with last-iterate convergence guarantees that is specifically designed for the practical constraints of LLMs (no policy storage, single opponent).

3. **A practical deep-learning implementation** (Nash-MD-PG and Nash-EMA-PG, Section 7/Appendix F) that adapts the tabular algorithm to gradient-based optimization over parametric policies, with the crucial practical innovation of generating the geometric mixture one token at a time during autoregressive decoding.

4. **Empirical validation** (Section 8/Appendix G) on a real text summarization task with a systematic sweep over the mixture parameter $\beta$, demonstrating that intermediate values ($\beta \in [0.125, 0.375]$) outperform both self-play ($\beta = 0$) and best-response against a fixed reference ($\beta = 1$), and that NLHF can match or exceed a tuned RLHF baseline.

The paper also explicitly acknowledges the relationship to prior preference-based methods that work without learned reward functions — DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), SLiC (Zhao et al., 2023), IPO (Azar et al., 2023), and GPO (Tang et al., 2024) — but distinguishes NLHF by its focus on **online learning of the Nash equilibrium through self-play against a dynamically updated opponent**, rather than offline optimization against a fixed dataset or fixed reference policy. The paper's key novel insight is that the opponent policy should be a **mixture of the current policy and a reference**, and that the mixing coefficient $\beta$ can be tuned to achieve better practical performance than either extreme.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation

NLHF is a system for fine-tuning a language model to generate responses that are **preferred over any competitor's responses** according to a learned pairwise preference model, rather than maximizing a single scalar reward. The system takes a dataset of human pairwise preferences between text summaries, learns a model that predicts the probability that one summary is preferred over another given the context article, then optimizes the language model's policy through a self-play process where it repeatedly competes against a dynamically-weighted mixture of its own past and current behavior—converging toward the Nash equilibrium where the model is unambiguously preferred by the preference model against any alternative policy. The core insight is that by targeting the Nash equilibrium rather than a reward optimum, the system naturally accommodates diverse or even non-transitive human preferences and produces a policy that is robust against exploitation, while the specific mirror-descent-inspired algorithm (Nash-MD) guarantees last-iterate convergence without requiring storage of past model checkpoints, making it practical for large language models.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture

The NLHF pipeline has four major components:

1. **Preference model** `$P(y \succ y' \mid x)$` — a T5X-XL transformer fine-tuned from an LLM-initialized pairwise classifier that consumes the context article `$x$` and two candidate summaries `$y$`, `$y'$`, and outputs a scalar probability `$\in [0, 1]$` that a randomly-sampled human prefers `$y$` over `$y'$`. This is learned via supervised cross-entropy on the TL;DR pairwise preference dataset and replaces the reward model in standard RLHF.

2. **Policy model** `$\pi_\theta$` — a T5X-L transformer, initialized from the supervised fine-tuned (SFT) checkpoint, that generates summaries autoregressively given the context article. This is the model being optimized.

3. **Alternative (opponent) policy** `$\pi'$` — a second policy used to generate competitor summaries against which `$\pi_\theta$` is evaluated and improved. Depending on the algorithm variant, `$\pi'$` is either (a) a geometric mixture of `$\pi_\theta$` and the reference SFT policy `$\mu$` (Nash-MD-PG), or (b) a policy whose parameters are an exponential moving average of past `$\theta$` values (Nash-EMA-PG).

4. **Policy gradient optimizer** — the loop that repeatedly samples a prompt `$x$`, generates one summary from `$\pi_\theta$` and one from `$\pi'$`, queries the preference model for `$P(y \succ y' \mid x)$`, subtracts the baseline `$1/2$` and a KL penalty term, and updates `$\theta$` via gradient ascent to maximize the regularized preference.

Information flows as follows: a prompt `$x$` is sampled from the dataset distribution `$\rho$` → the current policy `$\pi_\theta$` generates summary `$y$` autoregressively → the alternative policy `$\pi'$` (either geometric mixture or EMA policy) generates summary `$y'$` autoregressively → the preference model evaluates `$P(y \succ y' \mid x)$` → the gradient estimator (Equation 13) is computed using this preference signal, minus baseline `$1/2$`, minus `$\tau \log[\pi_\theta(y|x)/\mu(y|x)]$` → parameters `$\theta$` are updated → the alternative policy `$\pi'$` is updated (either by recomputing the geometric mixture or updating the EMA parameter buffer). This cycle repeats for 10,000 steps.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the preference model — how it is initialized from an LLM-as-judge prompt, what architecture it uses, how it is trained on pairwise data, and why it fundamentally differs from a reward model in both representational capacity and distributional sensitivity.
- **Second**, the regularized preference model `$P_\tau$` — how KL-regularization is incorporated to keep the optimized policy close to the reference SFT model, and why this regularization creates a strictly concave-convex game with a unique Nash equilibrium.
- **Third**, the Nash equilibrium definition and existence/uniqueness — the formal objective `$\pi^* = \arg\max_\pi \min_{\pi'} P(\pi \succ \pi')$`, what it means operationally, and Proposition 1 establishing that the regularized version has a unique solution.
- **Fourth**, the tabular Nash-MD algorithm — the core theoretical contribution, including the geometric mixture opponent, the mirror descent update rule, the closed-form recurrence in log-probability space, and Theorem 1's `$O(1/T)$` last-iterate convergence guarantee.
- **Fifth**, the deep learning adaptations (Nash-MD-PG and Nash-EMA-PG) — how the one-token-at-a-time autoregressive generation constraint forces approximation of the geometric mixture, how the policy gradient estimator is constructed with the `$1/2$` baseline and KL term, and how Nash-EMA-PG approximates the mixture-of-past-policies idea via parameter-space exponential moving averages.
- **Sixth**, the rationale for the mixing coefficient `$\beta$` — why intermediate values between self-play (`$\beta = 0$`) and best-response-against-SFT (`$\beta = 1$`) are expected to perform better, connecting to the theoretical property that playing against a mixture of current and past self balances exploitation (improving against known weaknesses) with stability (not overfitting to the current policy's transient flaws).

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

This is primarily a **methodology paper with a substantial theoretical component**, whose core idea is that alignment should be formulated as finding the Nash equilibrium of a learned pairwise preference model rather than maximizing a scalar reward, and that a specific mirror-descent algorithm (Nash-MD) provides a practical, provably-convergent way to compute this equilibrium for large language models.

#### The Preference Model: Definition, Initialization, and Training

**What a preference model is.** A preference model is a function `$P(y \succ y' \mid x)$` that maps a context article `$x$` and two candidate summaries `$y$`, `$y'$` to a scalar probability in `$[0, 1]$`. This probability is interpreted as:

> "the probability that a randomly chosen human prefers a response `$y$` over the other response `$y'$` given the context `$x$`"

The model is required to be antisymmetric: `$P(y \succ y' \mid x) = 1 - P(y' \succ y \mid x)$`. This simply means that if the model assigns a 0.7 probability that `$y$` beats `$y'$`, it must assign 0.3 that `$y'$` beats `$y$`. For any pair of identical summaries, `$P(y \succ y \mid x) = 1/2$` by the antisymmetry property — the model correctly indicates an even match.

The preference model is **extended to distributions over summaries** by taking expectations:

$$P(\pi \succ \pi' \mid x) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot|x),\, y' \sim \pi'(\cdot|x)} [P(y \succ y' \mid x)]$$

and over contexts:

$$P(\pi \succ \pi') = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \rho} [P(\pi \succ \pi' \mid x)]$$

where `$\rho$` is the distribution over prompts (articles to summarize). The notation `$P(y \succ \pi' \mid x)$` means the probability that a single summary `$y$` is preferred over a summary randomly drawn from policy `$\pi'$` given context `$x$`.

**How the preference model is initialized.** Rather than training a model from scratch to compare summaries, the paper bootstraps the preference model from an existing LLM by prompting it to act as a pairwise judge. The exact prompt used (Appendix G.1) is:

> "You are an expert summary rater. Given a piece of text and two of its possible summaries, output 1 or 2 to indicate which summary is better. Text - ⟨text⟩, Summary 1 - ⟨summary1⟩, Summary 2 - ⟨summary2⟩. Preferred Summary -"

where `⟨text⟩` is replaced with the actual article `$x$`, `⟨summary1⟩` with `$y$`, and `⟨summary2⟩` with `$y'$`. The LLM is a T5X model (the paper uses T5X-XL with 11B parameters). To extract a scalar preference from this prompted LLM, the paper takes the **last logit for an arbitrarily chosen token** (specifically, the logit corresponding to predicting the next token after "Preferred Summary -") and passes it through a sigmoid function to squash it into `$[0, 1]$`. This sigmoid-squashed logit becomes the model's predicted `$P_\theta(y \succ y' \mid x)$`.

**Why this matters:** The LLM is essentially being asked "which summary would a rater prefer?", and its internal representation of the probability that the answer is "1" versus "2" is extracted through the logit. This is a form of **AI feedback** (analogous to RLAIF; Lee et al., 2023) used to initialize the preference model before human data fine-tuning.

**Training objective.** The initialized preference model is then fine-tuned on the actual human pairwise preference dataset `$\mathcal{D} = \{(x_k, y^k_w, y^k_l)\}_{k=1}^K$`, where `$y^k_w$` is the human-preferred summary (the "winner") and `$y^k_l$` is the less-preferred summary (the "loser") for article `$x_k$`. The dataset contains 92,820 training examples from the TL;DR summarization dataset (Stiennon et al., 2020; Völske et al., 2017). The loss is standard binary cross-entropy:

$$\mathcal{L}_P(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} [\log(P_\theta(y_w \succ y_l \mid x))]$$

where `$\theta$` are the parameters of the T5X-XL model, `$\mathcal{D}$` is the training set of triples (article, winner summary, loser summary), and `$P_\theta(y_w \succ y_l \mid x)$` is the model's predicted probability that the winner is preferred over the loser.

**What it computes:** For each training example, the model reads the article, the winner summary, and the loser summary, and outputs a scalar probability. The log of this probability is taken, negated, and averaged across the batch. When the model is confident that the winner beats the loser (high probability), `$-\log(p)$` is small; when it is uncertain or wrong (low probability for the winner), the loss is large.

**Why this form:** This is the maximum-likelihood objective for a Bernoulli random variable (did the human prefer `$y_w$` over `$y_l$`? yes/no). It is the correct calibration objective because the preference model's output is interpreted as a probability. The cross-entropy loss encourages the model to output calibrated probabilities that match the empirical human preference frequencies. No Bradley-Terry assumption is made — the model directly learns to predict the pairwise preference probability without decomposing it into a difference of scalar rewards.

**Training results.** The paper reports (Figure 1 in Appendix G) that the preference model achieves approximately 78% accuracy on the held-out test set, where accuracy is defined as:

$$A(P_\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}} [\mathbf{1}\{P_\theta(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) \geq 0.5\}]$$

This is compared against a reward model of the same architecture (T5X-XL), which achieves approximately 76% accuracy (Figure 2). The preference model slightly outperforms the reward model, and the paper notes that the gains from scaling from 3B to 11B parameters are relatively small on this specific task, so the T5X-XL model is used for all subsequent experiments.

#### Why Preference Models Are Structurally Different from Reward Models

This is not merely an architectural detail — it is the foundational argument for the entire NLHF approach. The paper distinguishes reward and preference models along three dimensions.

**Representational capacity (Section 3.1).** A reward model `$r(x, y)$` assigns a single scalar score to each summary. Any pairwise preference it can express must take the Bradley-Terry form:

$$P_{BT}(y \succ y' \mid x) = \sigma(r(x, y) - r(x, y'))$$

where `$\sigma$` is the sigmoid function. This forces preferences to be **transitive**: if `$r(x, y_1) > r(x, y_2) > r(x, y_3)$`, then necessarily `$P(y_1 \succ y_3 \mid x) > 1/2$`. But the preference model `$P(y \succ y' \mid x)$` is a general function of two arguments — it can represent non-transitive preference cycles like `$P(y_1 \succ y_2) > 1/2$`, `$P(y_2 \succ y_3) > 1/2$`, and `$P(y_3 \succ y_1) > 1/2$` simultaneously (as demonstrated in Appendix C.1 with the non-transitive dice example). Whether actual human preferences exhibit non-transitivity is debated, but Appendix C.2 shows a more subtle and practically relevant case: even if every individual human has perfectly transitive preferences, the **aggregate** preference model (averaging over a heterogeneous population) can be non-transitive. This occurs whenever different subpopulations have systematically conflicting preference orderings, which is common in practice (e.g., some readers prefer concise summaries, others prefer comprehensive ones). The BT model is structurally incapable of representing this, while the preference model is not.

**Distributional sensitivity (Section 3.3, Appendix B, Theorem 2).** This is the paper's most original theoretical critique. The reward model is trained by maximizing:

$$r^\pi = \arg\max_r \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \rho,\, y, y' \sim \pi(\cdot|x),\, Z \sim \nu} [\log \sigma(r(x, y^Z_w) - r(x, y^Z_l))]$$

where `$y^Z_w$` is the human-preferred response among `$(y, y')$` according to randomly sampled human `$Z \sim \nu$`. The critical observation is that the optimal reward model `$r^\pi$` carries a superscript `$\pi$` — it **depends on the policy** `$\pi$` that generated the training data. The paper proves this in Theorem 2 (Appendix B):

> "If a preference model `$P$` cannot be perfectly captured by a Bradley-Terry reward model... then the reward model `$r^\pi$` depends explicitly on the sampling distribution `$\pi$`."

The intuition: a BT reward model learns Elo scores for each summary **relative to the other summaries in the training set**. A summary that looks good compared to a weak policy's outputs may look mediocre compared to a strong policy's outputs. The reward model's scalar is not an absolute quality measure — it is a relative ranking within the training distribution. When the policy is updated and generates summaries from a different distribution, the old reward model's scores become miscalibrated. The paper states:

> "the reward model from a prior iteration diverges from the next iteration due to shifts in data distributions, necessitating complete relearning"

By contrast, the preference model's optimal form is:

$$P^*(y \succ y' \mid x) = \mathbb{P}_{Z \sim \nu}(\text{Human } Z \text{ prefers } y \text{ over } y' \text{ given } x)$$

This depends only on `$x$`, `$y$`, and `$y'$` — **not** on the distribution `$\pi$` from which `$y$` and `$y'$` were sampled. The preference model can therefore be **preserved and incrementally updated** across iterations by simply adding new data, rather than retrained from scratch. This is a practical advantage for any iterative improvement pipeline.

**Alignment with preference diversity (Section 3.2).** The paper's three-human-type example illustrates that the RLHF solution (maximizing expected reward) collapses to a deterministic policy favoring the slightly-more-common human type, while the Nash equilibrium assigns probability across all three actions in proportion to the population distribution. This is not a pathology of the example — it reflects a fundamental difference: reward maximization seeks a single "best" response, while the Nash equilibrium of a preference model naturally produces a **mixed strategy** that respects the distribution of opinions in the population.

#### The Regularized Preference Model

The raw preference model `$P(\pi \succ \pi')$` defines a two-player constant-sum game, but without regularization, the Nash equilibrium may be far from the initial SFT policy — potentially producing degenerate or unsafe outputs. Following the RLHF convention (Jaques et al., 2019; Stiennon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022), the paper introduces KL-regularization to keep the optimized policy close to a reference policy `$\mu$` (the SFT model):

$$P^{\pi,\pi'}_\tau(y \succ y' \mid x) = P(y \succ y' \mid x) - \tau \log\frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\mu(y \mid x)} + \tau \log\frac{\pi'(y' \mid x)}{\mu(y' \mid x)}$$

where `$\tau > 0$` is the regularization strength, `$\pi$` is the policy being evaluated, `$\pi'$` is the opponent policy, `$\mu$` is the reference (SFT) policy, `$y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)$` is a summary from the current policy, and `$y' \sim \pi'(\cdot \mid x)$` is a summary from the opponent.

**What it computes:** For a single pair of summaries `$(y, y')$`, the regularized preference starts with the raw preference probability `$P(y \succ y' \mid x)$`, then **penalizes** the current policy `$\pi$` for generating `$y$` if `$y$` is much more likely under `$\pi$` than under `$\mu$` (the `$-\tau \log[\pi(y|x)/\mu(y|x)]$` term), and symmetrically **rewards** the opponent policy `$\pi'$` when `$y'$` is close to `$\mu$` (the `$+\tau \log[\pi'(y'|x)/\mu(y'|x)]$` term). The `$\log$` ratio is the pointwise KL contribution — when `$\pi(y|x) \gg \mu(y|x)$`, the ratio is large and the penalty is negative (hurting the current policy's score); when `$\pi(y|x) \ll \mu(y|x)$`, the ratio is small and the penalty is small in magnitude.

Taking expectations over summaries and contexts gives the regularized preference between policies:

$$P_\tau(\pi \succ \pi') = P(\pi \succ \pi') - \tau\,\text{KL}_\rho(\pi, \mu) + \tau\,\text{KL}_\rho(\pi', \mu)$$

where `$\text{KL}_\rho(\pi, \mu) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \rho}[\text{KL}(\pi(\cdot \mid x), \mu(\cdot \mid x))]$` is the expected KL divergence between `$\pi$` and `$\mu$` over the context distribution.

**Why this form:** The regularization structure is crucial for three reasons. First, it penalizes policies that deviate far from the known-safe SFT model, preventing reward hacking or degenerate outputs. Second, the `$+\tau \text{KL}(\pi', \mu)$` term for the opponent creates a **symmetry**: the current policy `$\pi$` is incentivized to stay close to `$\mu$`, while simultaneously preferring that the opponent `$\pi'$` moves away from `$\mu$` (since that adds a positive bonus to `$P_\tau$`). This pushes the Nash equilibrium toward a policy that is close to `$\mu$` but also robust — it can beat opponents even when they are allowed to deviate from `$\mu$`. Third, as shown in Proposition 1 (Appendix E), the regularized game `$P_\tau$` is strictly concave in `$\pi$` (for fixed `$\pi'$`) and strictly convex in `$\pi'$` (for fixed `$\pi$`), guaranteeing **existence and uniqueness** of the Nash equilibrium `$\pi^*_\tau$`. This is essential — without regularization, there could be multiple Nash equilibria or only mixed-strategy equilibria, complicating optimization.

The paper sweeps `$\tau \in \{0.02, 0.01, 0.008, 0.005\}$` in experiments and selects `$\tau = 0.008$` for all Nash-MD and Nash-EMA runs based on performance.

#### The Nash Equilibrium Objective

Formally, the NLHF objective (Equation 1) is:

$$\pi^* = \arg\max_\pi \min_{\pi'} P(\pi \succ \pi')$$

where `$\pi^*$` is the Nash equilibrium policy, `$\pi$` is the policy being optimized (Player 1), and `$\pi'$` is the adversarial opponent (Player 2).

**What it computes:** This is a minimax optimization. The outer `$\arg\max_\pi$` selects a policy `$\pi$` that maximizes its win rate; the inner `$\min_{\pi'}$` finds the worst-case opponent `$\pi'$` that minimizes `$\pi$`'s win rate. The Nash equilibrium is the policy `$\pi^*$` that achieves at least 50% win rate against **any** opponent — it is unexploitable. This is the standard minimax solution for a two-player zero-sum game (von Neumann, 1928), applied here to the game where payoffs are preference probabilities.

**Why this form over reward maximization:** The reward-maximization objective `$\pi^*_{RL} = \arg\max_\pi \mathbb{E}[r(x, y)]$` optimizes against a fixed, static reward function. The Nash formulation optimizes against an **adaptive adversary** that actively searches for weaknesses in `$\pi$`. This forces `$\pi$` to be robust — it must perform well not just against the data distribution, but against any policy that could be deployed. Conceptually, this aligns with the idea that a well-aligned model should not be "exploitable" by finding prompts or response patterns that cause it to produce dispreferred outputs. The paper also argues (Section 3.2) that the Nash equilibrium is more aligned with diverse human preferences because it naturally produces mixed strategies when different humans have conflicting preferences, rather than collapsing to a single "best" response that satisfies only a majority or plurality.

**Regularized version.** The regularized Nash equilibrium replaces `$P$` with `$P_\tau$`:

$$\pi^*_\tau = \arg\max_\pi \min_{\pi'} P_\tau(\pi \succ \pi')$$

Proposition 1 (proved in Appendix E) establishes that `$\pi^*_\tau$` exists and is unique. The proof uses Rosen's (1965) theorem for concave n-person games: the mapping `$\pi \mapsto P_\tau(\pi \succ \pi')$` is strictly concave in `$\pi$` (due to the `$-\tau \text{KL}(\pi, \mu)$` term) and `$\pi' \mapsto P_\tau(\pi \succ \pi')$` is strictly convex in `$\pi'$` (due to the `$+\tau \text{KL}(\pi', \mu)$` term). Strict concavity-convexity implies uniqueness — there is exactly one policy `$\pi^*_\tau$` satisfying the Nash condition `$P_\tau(\pi^*_\tau \succ \pi') \geq 1/2$` for all `$\pi'$`.

#### The Tabular Nash-MD Algorithm

This is the paper's core theoretical contribution. Nash-MD is a mirror descent algorithm designed to converge to the regularized Nash equilibrium `$\pi^*_\tau$` with guarantees on the **last iterate** (the final policy after `$T$` steps), not just the average of past policies. This is crucial for LLM applications where storing and sampling from a mixture of many past policies is memory-prohibitive.

**The geometric mixture opponent.** At iteration `$t$`, Nash-MD defines a **regularized policy** `$\pi^\mu_t$` as a geometric mixture between the current policy `$\pi_t$` and the reference policy `$\mu$`:

$$\pi^\mu_t(y) = \frac{\pi_t(y)^{1 - \eta_t \tau} \mu(y)^{\eta_t \tau}}{\sum_{y'} \pi_t(y')^{1 - \eta_t \tau} \mu(y')^{\eta_t \tau}}$$

where `$\eta_t > 0$` is the learning rate at step `$t$`, `$\tau > 0$` is the regularization strength, `$\pi_t$` is the current policy at iteration `$t$`, `$\mu$` is the reference (SFT) policy, and the denominator is a normalization constant ensuring `$\pi^\mu_t$` is a valid probability distribution.

**What it computes:** `$\pi^\mu_t(y)$` is a probability distribution over summaries that interpolates between `$\pi_t$` and `$\mu$` in **log-probability space**. Concretely, `$\log \pi^\mu_t(y) = (1 - \eta_t \tau) \log \pi_t(y) + \eta_t \tau \log \mu(y) - \log Z$` where `$Z$` is the normalizer. When `$\eta_t \tau = 0$` (early in training with small learning rate, or small regularization), `$\pi^\mu_t \approx \pi_t$` — the opponent is essentially the current policy (self-play). When `$\eta_t \tau$` approaches 1, `$\pi^\mu_t \approx \mu$` — the opponent is essentially the fixed reference policy (best-response against SFT). For intermediate values, `$\pi^\mu_t$` is a **geometric blend** that gives more weight to `$\mu$` as `$\eta_t \tau$` increases.

**Why geometric mixture rather than arithmetic:** An arithmetic mixture `$(1 - \alpha)\pi_t + \alpha\mu$` would be a valid opponent, but the geometric mixture has a critical mathematical property that enables the clean convergence proof: it makes the KL-divergence to the Nash equilibrium decompose linearly. Lemma 1 (Appendix D) shows:

$$\text{KL}(\pi, \pi^\mu_t) \leq \eta_t \tau\,\text{KL}(\pi, \mu) + (1 - \eta_t \tau)\,\text{KL}(\pi, \pi_t) - \eta_t \tau\,\text{KL}(\pi^\mu_t, \mu)$$

This inequality is the linchpin of the convergence proof — it relates the divergence to the mixture opponent to a convex combination of divergences to `$\mu$` and `$\pi_t$`, with a negative `$\text{KL}(\pi^\mu_t, \mu)$` bonus term. An arithmetic mixture would not satisfy this clean decomposition.

**The mirror descent update.** At each iteration, Nash-MD updates the policy by solving:

$$\pi_{t+1} = \arg\max_\pi \left[\eta_t P(\pi \succ \pi^\mu_t) - \text{KL}(\pi, \pi^\mu_t)\right]$$

where `$\eta_t$` is the learning rate, `$P(\pi \succ \pi^\mu_t)$` is the expected win rate of `$\pi$` against the geometric mixture opponent, and `$\text{KL}(\pi, \pi^\mu_t)$` is the KL penalty encouraging `$\pi$` to stay close to `$\pi^\mu_t$`.

**What it computes:** The optimization trades off two terms. The first, `$\eta_t P(\pi \succ \pi^\mu_t)$`, pushes `$\pi$` to achieve higher win rate against the current opponent `$\pi^\mu_t$`. The second, `$-\text{KL}(\pi, \pi^\mu_t)$`, penalizes moving too far from `$\pi^\mu_t$` in one step — it acts as a trust region. The combination means the policy improves by taking a gradient step in the direction that increases win rate, but constrained to stay near the mixture of current and reference behavior.

**Why this form (comparison with Online Mirror Descent):** The paper makes an explicit comparison with standard Online Mirror Descent (OMD), which would use the update:

$$\pi_{t+1}^{\text{OMD}} = \arg\max_\pi [\eta_t P(\pi \succ \pi_t) - \text{KL}(\pi, \pi^\mu_t)]$$

The difference is in the first term: OMD plays against the **current policy** `$\pi_t$`, while Nash-MD plays against the **geometric mixture** `$\pi^\mu_t$`. Both use the same KL penalty referencing `$\pi^\mu_t$`. The paper argues this difference is crucial:

> "in the context of convex-concave games, the regret bound... translates into an upper bound on the exploitability... thus entailing their on-average convergence to the Nash equilibrium. However it is known that usual regret-minimization algorithms may not possess a last-iterate convergence property because the sequence of policies `$\pi_t$` may oscillate around the Nash equilibrium"

Nash-MD's modification — playing against the regularized opponent `$\pi^\mu_t$` rather than `$\pi_t$` — is what enables last-iterate convergence. The intuition: playing against a mixture that includes `$\mu$` prevents the policy from cycling by anchoring it to a fixed reference point.

**Closed-form solution.** The optimization in the mirror descent step has an analytical solution in the tabular (non-parametric) setting:

$$\pi_{t+1}(y) \propto \pi^\mu_t(y) \exp(\eta_t P(y \succ \pi^\mu_t))$$

where `$P(y \succ \pi^\mu_t) = \mathbb{E}_{y' \sim \pi^\mu_t}[P(y \succ y')]$` is the probability that a specific summary `$y$` beats a random summary from the opponent policy. Equivalently, in log-space:

$$\log \pi_{t+1}(y) = (1 - \eta_t \tau) \log \pi_t(y) + \eta_t \tau \log \mu(y) + \eta_t P(y \succ \pi^\mu_t) + c$$

where `$c$` is a normalization constant independent of `$y$`.

**What it computes operationally:** Each iteration updates the log-probability of each summary `$y$` by adding three terms: (1) `$(1 - \eta_t \tau) \log \pi_t(y)$` — a decayed version of the previous log-probability, keeping most of the old information; (2) `$\eta_t \tau \log \mu(y)$` — a pull toward the reference policy's log-probability, weighted by regularization and learning rate; (3) `$\eta_t P(y \succ \pi^\mu_t)$` — the win rate of `$y$` against the current opponent, scaled by the learning rate. The combination means summaries that beat the opponent get their probability increased, but the reference policy `$\mu$` keeps pulling the distribution back toward the SFT baseline.

**Convergence guarantee (Theorem 1).** The paper proves the following inequality for every iteration `$t$`:

$$\text{KL}(\pi^*_\tau, \pi_{t+1}) \leq (1 - \eta_t \tau)\,\text{KL}(\pi^*_\tau, \pi_t) + 2\eta_t^2$$

where `$\pi^*_\tau$` is the unique Nash equilibrium of the regularized game, `$\pi_t$` is the policy at step `$t$`, `$\eta_t$` is the learning rate, and `$\tau$` is the regularization strength.

**What this inequality means:** The KL divergence from the Nash equilibrium to the current policy contracts by a factor of `$(1 - \eta_t \tau)$` each step, plus an additive error of `$2\eta_t^2$`. This is a **contraction plus noise** bound: the `$(1 - \eta_t \tau)$` factor shrinks the distance multiplicatively, while the `$2\eta_t^2$` term is the price of finite step sizes (it would vanish if `$\eta_t \to 0$`).

**Consequences for step size and convergence rate.** Choosing `$\eta_t = 2/(\tau(t+2))$` — a decreasing learning rate schedule — the paper proves by induction that:

$$\text{KL}(\pi^*_\tau, \pi_T) \leq \frac{8}{\tau^2 (T + 1)}$$

This is an `$O(1/T)$` **last-iterate** convergence rate in KL-divergence. After `$T$` iterations, the final policy `$\pi_T$` is within `$8/(\tau^2(T+1))$` KL-divergence of the Nash equilibrium. The proof (Appendix D) uses Lemma 1 and Lemma 2 from Munos et al. (2020) applied with the specific structure of the regularized preference model, crucially exploiting the fact that `$P_\tau(\pi^*_\tau \succ \pi^\mu_t) \geq 1/2$` (since `$\pi^*_\tau$` is the Nash equilibrium, it must achieve at least 50% win rate against any opponent, including `$\pi^\mu_t$`).

**Why last-iterate matters for LLMs:** The paper emphasizes two practical implications:

1. **No policy storage needed:** Unlike fictitious play or counterfactual regret minimization, which converge only on average and require storing the uniform mixture `$\bar{\pi}_T = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \pi_t$` (or sampling from it), Nash-MD guarantees that the **current policy** `$\pi_T$` directly converges. This avoids the memory cost of maintaining multiple model checkpoints.

2. **Single opponent per iteration:** Nash-MD plays against the geometric mixture `$\pi^\mu_t$`, which can be computed on-the-fly from the current policy parameters and the fixed reference model. There is no need to sample from or store a mixture of many past policies.

The paper explicitly contrasts this with the requirements of other algorithms:

> "These two properties are particularly important in the context of LLMs due to the substantial memory resources that would be otherwise needed to store a mixture policy like `$\bar{\pi}_t$`"

**Comparison with Ruppert-Polyak averaging.** The paper notes (Section 6) that the weighted averaging of log-probabilities in Equation (5) — where `$\log \pi_{t+1}$` is a weighted combination of `$\log \pi_t$`, `$\log \mu$`, and the preference signal — bears a resemblance to Ruppert-Polyak averaging in stochastic approximation. However, the paper clarifies that the purpose is different: here the averaging shapes the **dynamics** and establishes convergence, rather than merely accelerating an already-convergent process.

#### Contextual Bandit Extension

The tabular analysis above assumes no dependence on context `$x$`. The paper extends the algorithm to the contextual bandit setting (prompt-conditioned summaries) straightforwardly: for each context `$x \in \text{supp}(\rho)$`, independently maintain a policy `$\pi_t(\cdot \mid x)$` and apply the same mirror descent update per-context:

$$\pi_{t+1}(\cdot \mid x) = \arg\max_{\pi(\cdot)} \left[\eta_t P(\pi(\cdot) \succ \pi^\mu_t(\cdot \mid x) \mid x) - \text{KL}(\pi(\cdot), \pi^\mu_t(\cdot \mid x))\right]$$

where `$\pi^\mu_t(y \mid x) \propto \pi_t(y \mid x)^{1 - \eta_t \tau} \mu(y \mid x)^{\eta_t \tau}$`. The same convergence proof applies with `$\text{KL}(\pi, \pi') = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \rho}[\text{KL}(\pi(\cdot \mid x), \pi'(\cdot \mid x))]$`, yielding the identical `$O(1/T)$` rate in expected KL-divergence over the context distribution.

#### From Tabular Nash-MD to Deep Learning Implementations

The tabular Nash-MD algorithm cannot be directly applied to LLMs because:
- The policy space is not a table — it is parameterized by a neural network `$\pi_\theta$` with parameters `$\theta$`.
- Summaries are generated autoregressively one token at a time, not sampled all at once from a fixed categorical distribution.
- The geometric mixture `$\pi^\mu_t$` cannot be computed exactly in closed form (the normalizing constant is intractable for the full sequence distribution).

The paper introduces two gradient-based algorithms — **Nash-MD-PG** and **Nash-EMA-PG** — that adapt the core ideas of Nash-MD to these practical constraints.

**General loop structure (Appendix F.2).** Both algorithms share the same outer loop, executed for 10,000 steps:

1. Sample a prompt `$x \sim \rho$` (an article to summarize).
2. Generate response `$y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$` autoregressively from the current policy.
3. Generate response `$y' \sim \pi'(\cdot \mid x)$` autoregressively from the alternative (opponent) policy `$\pi'$` (defined differently by each algorithm — see below).
4. Query the preference model to get `$P(y \succ y' \mid x)$`.
5. Compute the policy gradient estimate and update `$\theta$` via gradient ascent.

**Policy gradient estimator (model-based variant, Equation 13).** The gradient of the regularized preference model with respect to `$\theta$` is estimated as:

$$\hat{g}(x, y, y') = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)\left[P(y \succ y' \mid x) - \frac{1}{2} - \tau \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\mu(y \mid x)}\right]$$

where `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$` is the score function gradient (how changing `$\theta$` affects the log-probability of generating summary `$y$`), `$P(y \succ y' \mid x)$` is the preference model's output (a scalar between 0 and 1), `$1/2$` is the baseline subtracted for variance reduction, `$\tau$` is the regularization strength, `$\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$` is the current policy's probability of `$y$`, and `$\mu(y \mid x)$` is the reference policy's probability.

**What it computes operationally:** The gradient `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$` points in the direction that increases the probability of the specific summary `$y$` that was generated. This direction is multiplied by a scalar "advantage" estimate: `$P(y \succ y' \mid x) - 1/2$` tells us whether `$y$` beat `$y'$` (positive if `$y$` was preferred, negative if `$y'$` was preferred, zero if tied), and `$-\tau \log[\pi_\theta(y|x)/\mu(y|x)]$` penalizes `$y$` if it is much more likely under `$\pi_\theta$` than under `$\mu$` (KL penalty). If `$y$` won and is close to `$\mu$`, the gradient strongly increases its probability. If `$y$` lost and is far from `$\mu$`, the gradient strongly decreases its probability.

**Why subtract `$1/2$` as baseline:** The expectation of `$P(y \succ y' \mid x)$` over `$y' \sim \pi'$` is `$P(y \succ \pi' \mid x)$`. The term `$P(y \succ \pi' \mid x) - 1/2$` has mean zero under a symmetric situation — if `$y$` is exactly as good as the opponent's average response, the expected gradient contribution is zero. This is a variance reduction technique that centers the advantage signal without needing to learn a separate value function (value head) as a baseline. The paper notes:

> "we have subtracted the baseline `$1/2 = P(y \succ y \mid x)$` from the preference `$P(y \succ y' \mid x)$` (which does not change the expectation of the gradient) as a variance reduction technique that does not require learning a value function as baseline"

**Handling autoregressive token generation.** In practice, summaries consist of sequences of tokens `$y = (y_0, y_1, \dots, y_N)$` generated autoregressively: `$\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{n=0}^N \pi_\theta(y_n \mid x, y_{0:n-1})$`. The policy gradient decomposes over timesteps, and the paper employs the standard REINFORCE trick of only multiplying each per-token gradient `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_n \mid x, y_{0:n-1})$` by the advantage terms corresponding to indices `$\geq n$` (future KL contributions), since past tokens' probabilities don't affect the current decision. Appendix F.2 mentions this:

> "the standard policy-gradient variance-reduction trick of only multiplying `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_n \mid x, y_{0:n-1})$` by KL estimator terms corresponding to indices at least as great as `$n$` can be applied"

**Model-free variant.** If human preferences are available immediately (online), the paper notes that `$P(y \succ y' \mid x)$` can be replaced with the binary indicator `$\mathbf{1}\{\text{Human prefers } y \text{ over } y'\}$` in Equation (13). This avoids learning a preference model entirely, though it requires humans-in-the-loop at training time.

#### Nash-MD-PG: Playing Against the Geometric Mixture

Nash-MD-PG defines the alternative policy `$\pi'$` as a **geometric mixture** between `$\pi_\theta$` and the reference `$\mu$` (analogous to `$\pi^\mu_t$` in the tabular algorithm), but with a tunable mixing coefficient `$\beta$` separate from the KL regularization `$\tau$`:

$$\log \pi^\beta_\theta(y \mid x) = (1 - \beta) \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) + \beta \log \mu(y \mid x) + c(x)$$

where `$\beta \in [0, 1]$` is the mixture parameter, and `$c(x)$` is a normalization constant.

**What `$\beta$` controls:**
- `$\beta = 0$`: `$\pi^\beta_\theta = \pi_\theta$` — pure self-play (SP). The policy improves by playing against its current self.
- `$\beta = 1$`: `$\pi^\beta_\theta = \mu$` — best-response against the fixed SFT policy (BR). The policy improves by playing against the frozen initial model.
- Intermediate `$\beta$`: The opponent is a blend — partially the current policy, partially the reference. The paper sweeps `$\beta \in \{0, 0.125, 0.25, 0.375, 0.5, 0.625, 0.75, 0.875, 1.0\}$`.

**Why separate `$\beta$` from `$\eta_t \tau$`:** In the tabular algorithm, the mixing weight in `$\pi^\mu_t$` is `$\eta_t \tau$`, which couples the opponent mixture to the learning rate and regularization. By introducing a separate parameter `$\beta$`, Nash-MD-PG decouples these: `$\tau$` controls KL-regularization strength, while `$\beta$` independently controls how much the opponent looks like the current policy versus the reference. This allows the practitioner to tune the opponent strategy separately from regularization, which the experiments show is beneficial.

**Differentiation from standard RLHF with KL penalty:** In standard RLHF with KL-regularization, the gradient also contains a `$-\tau \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) \log[\pi_\theta(y|x)/\mu(y|x)]$` term. But the **reward signal** is `$r(x, y)$` from a fixed reward model, and the opponent `$y'$` is not actively generated — the policy is optimized against a static objective. In Nash-MD-PG, the reward signal comes from the preference model's comparison between `$y$` and a **dynamically generated** `$y'$` from an opponent that co-evolves with the policy.

**Relationship to tabular Nash-MD's inner optimization.** The paper notes that Nash-MD-PG makes a further approximation beyond the parametric representation: rather than fully solving the inner maximization `$\arg\max_\pi [\eta_t P(\pi \succ \pi^\mu_t) - \text{KL}(\pi, \pi^\mu_t)]$` before updating the opponent (which would be a two-timescale algorithm), Nash-MD-PG takes a **single gradient step** per iteration, simultaneously updating `$\pi_\theta$` and the implied opponent `$\pi^\beta_\theta$`. The paper comments:

> "a more faithful variant of Nash-MD would use a two-time scale algorithm and perform several gradient steps (while keeping `$\pi_\theta$` and `$\pi^\beta_\theta$` fixed) until the inner loop has reached an optimum, before updating `$\pi_\theta$` and `$\pi^\beta_\theta$`"

Despite this approximation, the paper argues that the essence of Nash-MD is preserved: playing against a geometric mixture while regularizing toward it.

**Equivalence of KL penalties.** An important technical detail in Appendix F.3: tabular Nash-MD uses `$\text{KL}(\pi, \pi^\mu_t)$` as the Bregman divergence in the mirror descent step, which expands to `$\beta \text{KL}(\pi, \mu) - \mathbb{E}[c(x)]$` (up to scaling). Since Nash-MD-PG uses `$\text{KL}(\pi_\theta, \mu)$` directly as the regularization (multiplied by `$\tau$`), and only takes one gradient step before the opponent updates, the two regularizations are equivalent in the single-step regime — the gradient of `$\text{KL}(\pi, \pi^\beta_\theta)$` with respect to `$\theta$` is proportional to the gradient of `$\text{KL}(\pi, \mu)$` when `$\pi^\beta_\theta$` is treated as fixed.

#### The One-Token-at-a-Time Approximation for Autoregressive Generation

A critical practical challenge: the geometric mixture `$\pi^\beta_\theta(y \mid x)$` is defined over the **full sequence** `$y$`, but in autoregressive generation, tokens are sampled one at a time from the conditional distribution `$\pi(y_n \mid x, y_{0:n-1})$`. Sampling directly from the geometric mixture of full-sequence distributions is intractable because the normalizing constant sums over all possible sequences.

**The approximation (Appendix F.1).** The paper uses a **token-level geometric mixture** `$\tilde{\pi}^\beta_\theta$` defined as:

$$\log \tilde{\pi}^\beta_\theta(y_n \mid x, y_{0:n-1}) = (1 - \beta) \log \pi_\theta(y_n \mid x, y_{0:n-1}) + \beta \log \mu(y_n \mid x, y_{0:n-1}) + C(x, y_{0:n-1})$$

where `$C(x, y_{0:n-1})$` is a per-timestep normalization constant depending on the prompt and previous tokens. At each generation step, the logits from `$\pi_\theta$` and `$\mu$` (conditioned on the same prefix) are combined with weights `$(1-\beta)$` and `$\beta$`, renormalized via softmax (effectively computing `$C$`), and the next token is sampled from this per-step mixture.

The resulting full-sequence distribution is:

$$\tilde{\pi}^\beta_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{n=0}^N \tilde{\pi}^\beta_\theta(y_n \mid x, y_{0:n-1})$$

**Why this is an approximation, not exact:** The normalization constant `$C(x, y_{0:n-1})$` depends on the specific prefix `$y_{0:n-1}$`. The full-sequence geometric mixture `$\pi^\beta_\theta(y \mid x)$` would have a single global normalizer summing over all sequences, which equals the sum of products of per-step normalization exponents. Because the path-dependent `$C$` values differ from what a global normalizer would imply, `$\tilde{\pi}^\beta_\theta \neq \pi^\beta_\theta$` in general. The paper acknowledges this gap:

> "Notice that the one-step-at-a-time regularized policy `$\tilde{\pi}^\beta_\theta(y \mid x)$` is different from the original regularized policy `$\pi^\beta_\theta(y \mid x)$`... We leave the analysis of the difference between these two policies for future work."

Despite this approximation, the experimental results show that the token-level mixture works effectively as an opponent — the Nash-MD-PG models with intermediate `$\beta$` outperform both self-play (`$\beta = 0$`, which is exact since `$\pi^\beta_\theta = \pi_\theta$`) and best-response (`$\beta = 1$`, which is exact since `$\pi^\beta_\theta = \mu$`).

#### Nash-EMA-PG: Exponential Moving Average in Parameter Space

As an alternative to the geometric mixture opponent, Nash-EMA-PG defines the alternative policy `$\pi'$` as a policy whose parameters are an **exponential moving average (EMA)** of past values of `$\theta$`:

$$\bar{\theta}_t = (1 - \beta) \theta_t + \beta \theta_0$$

where `$\bar{\theta}_t$` is the EMA parameter vector at step `$t$`, `$\theta_t$` is the current policy's parameters, `$\theta_0$` is the initial (SFT) parameters, and `$\beta \in [0, 1]$` controls the decay.

**What it computes:** When `$\beta$` is close to 0, `$\bar{\theta}_t \approx \theta_t$` — the opponent is essentially the current policy (self-play). When `$\beta = 1$`, `$\bar{\theta}_t = \theta_0$` — the opponent is the frozen initial policy (best-response against SFT). For intermediate `$\beta$` (e.g., `$\beta = 0.999$`, `$\beta = 0.9995$`), `$\bar{\theta}_t$` is a slowly-moving average that lags behind the current policy, approximating the behavior of a policy that is a mixture of past checkpoints.

**Relationship to fictitious play.** In tabular fictitious play, each player best-responds against the **uniform arithmetic mixture** of the opponent's past policies: `$\bar{\pi}_t = \frac{1}{t} \sum_{s=1}^t \pi_s$`. Nash-EMA-PG approximates this by maintaining an EMA of **parameters** rather than an explicit mixture of policies. Because of the non-linearity of the neural network mapping from `$\theta$` to `$\pi_\theta$`, the EMA-averaged parameters `$\bar{\theta}_t$` do **not** exactly correspond to an arithmetic mixture of the corresponding policies — `$\pi_{\bar{\theta}_t} \neq \frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^t \pi_{\theta_s}$` in general. The paper acknowledges this approximation:

> "prior work in deep learning (Grill et al., 2020; Wortsman et al., 2022; Busbridge et al., 2023; Rame et al., 2023) suggests that it could be a reasonable first-order approximation to it"

**Experimental sweep.** The paper sweeps `$\beta \in \{0, 0.999, 0.9995, 0.9999, 1.0\}$`. The values 0.999 and 0.9995 correspond to effective time constants of approximately 1000 and 2000 steps respectively (since `$\beta^t \approx e^{-(1-\beta)t}$`, an EMA with `$\beta$` has an effective window of `$1/(1-\beta)$` steps). The results (Tables 2, 3) show that Nash-EMA-PG models (both last-iterate and average-policy variants) are outperformed by Nash-MD-PG with intermediate `$\beta \in [0.125, 0.375]$`, suggesting:

> "the first-order approximation of the mixture policy as the policy having an average (EMA) weight may not be well-suited in this context, potentially contributing to the overall lower performance"

---

#### Why Intermediate `$\beta$` Outperforms Extremes

The paper's experiments (Tables 2, 3 in Appendix G) systematically compare Nash-MD-PG with `$\beta \in \{0, 0.125, 0.25, 0.375, 0.5, 0.625, 0.75, 1.0\}$`. The result: intermediate values `$\beta = 0.125, 0.25, 0.375$` consistently outperform both `$\beta = 0$` (self-play) and `$\beta = 1$` (best-response against SFT). The paper offers the intuition:

> "it suggests that improving one's policy by playing against a mixture of the initial policy and the current policy yields superior model improvement compared to interactions with either the initial policy or the current policy in isolation"

**Why `$\beta = 0$` (self-play) is suboptimal:** When the policy plays against itself, there is no stabilizing reference. The policy can develop transient weaknesses that the opponent (being identical) fails to exploit, allowing those weaknesses to persist. In game-theoretic terms, self-play can lead to cyclic behavior where policies chase each other without converging to a Nash equilibrium — this is the well-known failure mode of naive self-play in imperfect-information games. The paper notes:

> "self-play does not guarantee the attainment of a Nash equilibrium, as cyclic patterns are possible"

**Why `$\beta = 1$` (best-response against SFT) is suboptimal:** Playing against a fixed, weak opponent (the SFT model) means the policy only learns to beat that specific baseline. The resulting policy may be "brittle" — highly optimized against SFT outputs but exploitable by policies with different characteristics. The paper's results confirm this: the best-response model (BR, `$\beta = 1$`) achieves only 94.3% win rate against SFT (Table 3), but performs poorly against other Nash-MD models (e.g., only 16.3% win rate against MD1 with `$\beta = 0.125$`).

**Why intermediate `$\beta$` works:** Playing against a mixture that is mostly the current policy but anchored to the reference SFT policy provides a form of **stabilized self-play**. The opponent is similar enough to the current policy that it exposes genuine weaknesses, but the reference component prevents the opponent from drifting into regions of policy space that are not relevant. This is analogous to the tabular Nash-MD's geometric mixture, where the convergence proof shows that the reference `$\mu$` acts as an anchor that enables contraction toward the Nash equilibrium. The experimental finding that `$\beta = 0.125$` (the smallest non-zero value tested, i.e., 12.5% reference, 87.5% current) performs best suggests that only a small amount of anchoring is needed — the policy should mostly compete against its own improving self, with just enough reference pull to prevent cycles.

---

#### Preference Model vs. Reward Model Training Comparison

Appendix G.1 provides a direct empirical comparison between training a preference model `$P_\theta$` and a reward model `$r_\theta$` from the same pairwise data. Both use the same T5X-XL architecture.

**Reward model training.** The reward model is trained by minimizing the Bradley-Terry cross-entropy loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_r(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} [\log \sigma(r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l))]$$

where `$r_\theta(x, y)$` is a scalar reward output from the model after prompting with "Context - ⟨text⟩, Summary - ⟨summary⟩" and extracting the last logit for an arbitrary token. The sigmoid `$\sigma$` converts the difference in rewards to a pairwise preference probability.

**Preference model training.** As described above, the preference model directly predicts `$P_\theta(y_w \succ y_l \mid x)$` by prompting with the comparison format, extracting the last logit, and applying sigmoid, trained with:

$$\mathcal{L}_P(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} [\log P_\theta(y_w \succ y_l \mid x)]$$

**Results (Figure 2):** The preference model achieves approximately 78% test accuracy versus approximately 76% for the reward model of the same size. While the difference is small, it is consistent with the theoretical argument that the preference model's functional form is more flexible — it doesn't force the BT structural assumption during training. The paper uses the trained T5X-XL preference model for all NLHF experiments and the trained T5X-XL reward model for the RLHF baseline.

---

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Pairwise preference model over Bradley-Terry reward model:** Enables representation of non-transitive preferences, eliminates dependence on data-generating distribution, and naturally accommodates preference diversity through mixed-strategy Nash equilibria.
- **Nash equilibrium over reward maximization as the solution concept:** Produces policies that are unexploitable (win rate `$\geq 1/2$` against any opponent) and aligned with population preference distributions rather than collapsing to a deterministic best-response for a majority subpopulation.
- **KL-regularization toward SFT reference:** Prevents policy drift into degenerate outputs; makes the game strictly concave-convex, guaranteeing a unique Nash equilibrium; standard practice in RLHF carried forward to NLHF.
- **Geometric mixture opponent (Nash-MD):** Chosen over arithmetic mixture because it enables a clean decomposition of the KL-divergence (Lemma 1) that is essential for the convergence proof; playing against a mixture rather than the pure current policy stabilizes the dynamics and prevents cycling.
- **Last-iterate convergence target:** Chosen over average-iterate convergence because LLM deployments cannot store or sample from a mixture of many past policies; the final checkpoint must be the Nash equilibrium directly.
- **Separate mixing coefficient `$\beta$` from regularization `$\tau$`:** Decouples opponent strategy selection from regularization strength, enabling independent tuning; experiments confirm intermediate `$\beta$` values outperform the `$\beta$` values implied by the tabular algorithm's `$\eta_t \tau$` coupling.
- **Token-level geometric mixture approximation:** Practical necessity for autoregressive generation; avoids the intractable full-sequence normalization while empirically preserving the benefits of the mixture opponent.
- **`$1/2$` baseline in policy gradient:** Variance reduction without requiring a learned value function; exploits the antisymmetry of the preference model (`$P(y \succ y \mid x) = 1/2$`) to center the advantage signal.
- **T5X-L for policy, T5X-XL for preference model:** Practical compromise between computational budget for policy optimization (which requires many generations) and model capacity for the preference model (which benefits from greater expressivity).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Alignment as a Game-Theoretic Problem Rather Than an Optimization Problem

The paper's most fundamental intellectual move is redefining what it means to "align" a language model with human preferences. Standard RLHF frames alignment as **optimization**: find the policy that maximizes expected reward as scored by a model trained to predict human preferences. NLHF reframes it as **equilibrium-seeking**: find a policy that is preferred to any alternative policy according to a direct pairwise preference model.

This is not merely a change of algorithm — it is a change of **solution concept**. The optimization view seeks a single "best" policy, which implicitly assumes human preferences form a total ordering that can be collapsed to a scalar. The equilibrium view seeks a policy that is **unexploitable** — preferred to any competitor — which accommodates preference structures where no single response dominates: non-transitive preferences (Appendix C.1), heterogeneous populations with conflicting values (Section 3.2), or cases where preference diversity means the correct answer is genuinely a mixed strategy.

The distinction manifests concretely in the paper's three-human-type example (Section 3.2). When the population contains subgroups with opposing preference orderings and one subgroup is slightly less common, the RLHF solution deterministically selects the action preferred by the slightly-more-common groups, completely ignoring the minority. The Nash equilibrium assigns probability approximately proportional to each group's share. This is not a pathological edge case — it reflects the reality that human preferences over open-ended text generation are frequently heterogeneous (different readers value conciseness vs. comprehensiveness, formality vs. approachability, creativity vs. accuracy). The paper's key insight is that **optimization collapse to a point is a feature of the solution concept, not an accident of implementation**, and that switching to an equilibrium concept is the principled way to respect preference diversity.

Prior work on learning from preferences (DPO, SLiC, IPO, GPO) also avoided scalar reward models, but retained the optimization framing — they optimized policies against fixed datasets or fixed reference policies. The paper distinguishes NLHF by making the **game-theoretic structure explicit**: the preference model defines a two-player constant-sum game where the opponent is an active, adaptive adversary. This is conceptually closer to how game-theoretic solution concepts have been used in multi-agent RL (e.g., AlphaGo's self-play, counterfactual regret minimization in poker) than to the standard supervised-learning-inspired paradigm of loss minimization. The theoretical machinery — minimax theorems, Nash equilibrium existence/uniqueness proofs, mirror descent convergence analysis — is imported from that tradition, not from the preference-learning tradition.

An important conceptual nuance: the paper does not claim that human preferences *are* actually non-transitive or that the diversity example is empirically common (although evidence for both exists in the psychology literature). The argument is weaker and more defensible: **the Bradley-Terry reward model and reward-maximization pipeline *cannot even represent* these preference structures, while the NLHF pipeline can**. Whether or not they occur in practice on any particular dataset, the NLHF framework does not structurally preclude them. This positions NLHF as the more general framework, of which RLHF is a special case when preferences happen to be perfectly Bradley-Terry and the global optimum aligns with the Nash equilibrium.

---

### Innovation 2: Identifying and Proving the Distribution-Dependence of Learned Reward Models

The paper provides what is, to my knowledge, the first formal proof that the Bradley-Terry reward model learned from pairwise preference data **depends on the data-generating policy** (Appendix B, Theorem 2). This is a theoretical result with immediate practical consequences, and it is not obvious a priori — one might reasonably expect that if you have enough pairwise comparisons covering the response space, the fitted Elo scores should converge to some "true" underlying quality measure.

The paper proves this is not the case. The proof shows that the optimal BT reward model `$r^\pi$` trained on data from policy `$\pi$` satisfies `$P(y \succ \pi) = P^\pi_{BT}(y \succ \pi)$` for responses in the support of `$\pi$` (Proposition 2), but that if the true preference model `$P$` is not perfectly expressible as a BT model — which is the generic case — then changing the sampling distribution `$\pi$` produces a different reward model `$r^{\pi'}$` with different pairwise ordering implications.

The practical implication is that **reward models are not reusable across iterations of an improvement pipeline**. In standard iterative RLHF, data collected from policy `$\pi_t$` is used to train reward model `$r^{\pi_t}$`, which is then used to optimize `$\pi_{t+1}$`. At the next iteration, `$r^{\pi_t}$` is miscalibrated for evaluating `$\pi_{t+1}$`'s outputs because the response distribution has shifted. The paper notes that this forces "complete relearning" of the reward model at each iteration. By contrast, the preference model `$P^*(y \succ y' \mid x) = \mathbb{P}(\text{Human prefers } y \text{ over } y' \mid x)$` depends only on the pair `$(y, y')$`, not on the distribution they were sampled from, so it "can be preserved and further enriched through the introduction of novel data."

This insight explains a known practical headache in RLHF pipelines — reward model staleness and the need for periodic retraining — as a **mathematical necessity rather than a mere finite-sample issue**. It also suggests that NLHF is structurally more suitable for iterative or continual improvement settings, where the same preference model can accumulate data across rounds without fundamental miscalibration. This is a conceptual contribution about the *nature* of the learning problem, not just a better algorithm.

---

### Innovation 3: A Last-Iterate Convergent Algorithm for Preference-Model Nash Equilibria That Requires No Policy Storage

The paper introduces Nash-MD, a mirror descent variant specifically designed for the regularized preference game, with a proof of **last-iterate convergence** at an `$O(1/T)$` rate in KL-divergence (Theorem 1). While the individual components — mirror descent, geometric mixtures, constant-sum games — are not novel, the combination and the proof technique are intellectually distinctive in two ways.

First, **the algorithm achieves last-iterate convergence while playing against a single opponent per iteration**, meaning it does not require storing or sampling from a mixture of all past policies. This contrasts with the standard approach in the game-solving literature. Fictitious play and counterfactual regret minimization (CFR; Zinkevich et al., 2007) guarantee convergence only of the **average** policy `$\bar{\pi}_T = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \pi_t$` — the last iterate `$\pi_T$` may cycle indefinitely (Mertikopoulos et al., 2018). For LLMs, storing or sampling from a uniform mixture of many past policies means maintaining checkpoints for every iteration and running inference through all of them, which is practically infeasible at billion-parameter scale. The paper explicitly identifies this as a gap limiting the applicability of prior game-solving techniques to LLMs.

Second, **the geometric mixture opponent `$\pi^\mu_t$` plays a dual role** that enables the clean convergence proof. The mixture interpolates between the current policy `$\pi_t$` and the reference `$\mu$` in log-probability space, with the mixing weight `$\eta_t \tau$` determined by the learning rate and regularization. This specific form makes the KL-divergence to the Nash equilibrium contract multiplicatively (Lemma 1: `$\text{KL}(\pi^*_\tau, \pi^\mu_t) \leq \eta_t\tau\,\text{KL}(\pi^*_\tau, \mu) + (1-\eta_t\tau)\,\text{KL}(\pi^*_\tau, \pi_t) - \eta_t\tau\,\text{KL}(\pi^\mu_t, \mu)$`). The paper shows that the standard Online Mirror Descent variant (playing against `$\pi_t$` rather than `$\pi^\mu_t$`) does not have this property — it may oscillate — and the modification to play against the regularized mixture is what stabilizes the dynamics.

The contribution is **not** that mirror descent works for games (this is known), but that this *specific* choice of opponent and Bregman divergence exploits the structure of the KL-regularized preference game — which is the sum of a bilinear form and KL penalties — to produce last-iterate convergence with a particularly simple algorithm that is engineered for the memory constraints of LLM deployment. The paper positions this as filling a gap between the theoretical game-solving literature (which has algorithms with good guarantees but high storage requirements) and the practical LLM alignment literature (which uses simple gradient methods without convergence theory).

The comparison with Online Mirror Descent in Section 6 is instructive: both algorithms use the same KL penalty `$\text{KL}(\pi, \pi^\mu_t)$`, but OMD optimizes against `$\pi_t$` while Nash-MD optimizes against `$\pi^\mu_t$`. The paper argues this small difference — playing against a slightly regularized opponent rather than the raw current policy — is what enables the `$O(1/T)$` last-iterate rate, and that the special structure of the regularized preference function (`$\text{bilinear} + \text{KL}$`) is essential to making this work. This is a theoretical insight about *why* this particular algorithm converges, not merely an empirical demonstration that it does.

---

### Innovation 4: The Mixing Coefficient `$\beta$` as a Tunable Parameter Controlling the Self-Play-to-Reference Spectrum

The paper introduces a practical innovation that, while simple, has significant implications for how self-improvement algorithms are configured. In the tabular Nash-MD, the opponent mixing weight is `$\eta_t \tau$` — it couples the degree of reference anchoring to both the learning rate schedule and the regularization strength. In the deep learning implementation (Nash-MD-PG), the paper **decouples** this by introducing a separate parameter `$\beta \in [0, 1]$` that independently controls the opponent mixture:

- `$\beta = 0$`: self-play (opponent = current policy)
- `$\beta = 1$`: best-response against SFT (opponent = fixed reference)
- Intermediate: opponent = geometric mixture

The sweep over `$\beta$` in the experiments (Tables 2, 3) reveals a non-obvious empirical finding: `$\beta = 0.125$` (12.5% reference, 87.5% current) consistently outperforms both `$\beta = 0$` and `$\beta = 1$`, as well as larger values like `$\beta = 0.5$`. This is not predicted by the tabular theory, which couples `$\beta$` to `$\eta_t \tau$` and would suggest different effective mixing weights depending on the learning rate schedule.

The conceptual insight is that **the optimal opponent for self-improvement is a dynamically-weighted blend that is mostly the current policy with a small anchor to a fixed reference**. This makes intuitive sense in retrospect: the opponent should be similar enough to expose the current policy's specific weaknesses (so `$\beta$` should be small, making the opponent nearly identical to `$\pi_\theta$`), but needs a small pull toward a known-safe baseline to prevent both the policy and opponent from drifting together into degenerate regions of output space (so `$\beta$` should be non-zero). The fact that `$\beta = 0.125$` works best — a 7:1 ratio of self to reference — suggests that the anchoring effect is subtle: it doesn't need to dominate the opponent's behavior to stabilize the dynamics.

The paper also tests Nash-EMA-PG, which approximates the mixture-of-past-policies idea via parameter-space exponential moving averages. The EMA variants consistently underperform Nash-MD-PG at equivalent `$\beta$` values. The paper interprets this as evidence that the geometric mixture in probability space matters — parameter-space averaging is a crude first-order approximation that doesn't capture the right interaction structure for this problem. This is a **negative result with diagnostic value**: it tells us that how the opponent mixture is constructed (probability-space geometric mixture vs. parameter-space EMA) matters for performance, not just the high-level idea of "playing against a blend of past and current policies."

The practical implication is that future systems implementing self-play or iterative improvement for LLMs should treat the opponent mixture ratio as a tunable hyperparameter, and that values in the range of ~10-25% reference weight appear empirically optimal on this task. This is a contribution to the *engineering practice* of preference-based alignment, backed by the systematic sweep rather than anecdotal tuning.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The experiments use the TL;DR summarization dataset (Völske et al., 2017) as processed by Stiennon et al. (2020). The preference model training set contains 92,820 examples of the form `(x, y_w, y_l)` — an article `x`, a human-preferred summary `y_w`, and a less-preferred summary `y_l`. A held-out test set of "high confidence data" is used to evaluate preference model accuracy. Policy models are trained on articles sampled from the same dataset distribution `ρ`.

- **Base model(s).** The preference model is a T5X-XL encoder-decoder transformer with 11 billion parameters (Roberts et al., 2023; Roit et al., 2023), initialized by prompting a pre-trained LLM to output pairwise comparison judgments and then fine-tuned on the TL;DR human preference data. The policy model is a T5X-L model (smaller than T5X-XL), initialized from a supervised fine-tuned (SFT) checkpoint trained on the OpenAI TL;DR dataset. The paper chooses T5X-L for policy optimization for "computational efficiency and to compute the pairwise comparisons across our policies," explicitly noting the goal is "a proof of concept for the NLHF approach... rather than striving for state-of-the-art performance."

- **Metrics.** There are two types of evaluation. First, **preference model accuracy** is measured on the held-out test set as:
  $$A(P_\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}} [\mathbf{1}\{P_\theta(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) \geq 0.5\}]$$
  — the fraction of test pairs where the model correctly assigns >50% probability to the human-preferred summary. Second, **pairwise policy comparisons** are conducted between all trained policies using two separate preference oracles: the regularized preference model `$P_\tau$` used during training (Table 2), and a PaLM 2 Large model used as an independent evaluator `$P^*$` (Table 3). For each policy pair `$(\pi_c, \pi_r)$`, 2,000 samples are drawn `$(x \sim \rho, y \sim \pi_c(\cdot \mid x), y' \sim \pi_r(\cdot \mid x))$` and evaluated to estimate `$P^*(\pi_c \succ \pi_r)$`. The PaLM 2 Large evaluator is prompted with the same expert-summary-rater format used to initialize the preference model, making it an "approximate surrogate for human preferences" that is much larger than and architecturally different from the T5X-XL preference model used during training.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares the following models (all initialized from the same SFT checkpoint and trained for 10,000 steps):
  - **SFT**: The supervised fine-tuned reference policy `$μ$`, against which all other models are KL-regularized.
  - **RLHF**: A standard RLHF baseline trained via regularized policy gradient (Equation 16) against the T5X-XL reward model, using KL-regularization with `$τ = 0.05$` (selected from a sweep over `$\{0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2\}$`).
  - **SP (Self-Play)**: Nash-MD-PG with `$β = 0$`, equivalent to Nash-EMA-PG with `$β = 0$` — the policy improves by playing against its current self.
  - **BR (Best-Response against SFT)**: Nash-MD-PG with `$β = 1$`, equivalent to Nash-EMA-PG with `$β = 1$` — the policy improves by playing against the fixed SFT model.
  - **MD1–MD6**: Nash-MD-PG with `$β \in \{0.125, 0.25, 0.375, 0.5, 0.625, 0.75\}$`.
  - **EMA1, EMA2**: Last-iterate of Nash-EMA-PG with `$β \in \{0.999, 0.9995\}$`.
  - **EMA1\*, EMA2\***: Average-policy (EMA parameters) of Nash-EMA-PG with `$β \in \{0.999, 0.9995\}$`.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute in **number of training steps** — all models are trained for exactly 10,000 steps. Each step involves generating two summaries (one from `$π_θ$`, one from `$π'$`), querying the preference model once, and performing one gradient update. The paper explicitly does not optimize hyperparameters per-algorithm, using the same learning rate, number of steps, and (for NLHF models) the same `$τ = 0.008$` selected from a sweep `$\{0.02, 0.01, 0.008, 0.005\}$`. The rationale is that "we have not tried to over-optimize the hyper-parameters of the different methods... and this could explain some differences observed."

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation is used for model selection — hyperparameters are selected via sweeps on a validation set (for RLHF's `$τ$`, for NLHF's `$τ$` and `$β$`), and final models are evaluated on the test set. The pairwise preference tables are estimated from 1,000 samples for Table 2 (yielding a 95% Clopper-Pearson confidence interval half-width of ±0.032) and 2,000 samples for Table 3 (half-width ±0.023). The PaLM 2 evaluation uses 2,000 samples per pairwise comparison.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Preference Model vs. Reward Model Accuracy

The paper first establishes that the preference model learns to predict human preferences slightly more accurately than an equivalently-sized reward model. On the held-out TL;DR test set, the T5X-XL preference model achieves approximately 78% accuracy, compared to approximately 76% for the T5X-XL reward model (Appendix G, Figure 2). The paper also shows (Figure 1) that scaling the preference model from T5X-small (110M) to T5X-XL (3B) to T5X-XXL (11B) improves accuracy, but with diminishing returns — "there is relatively small gains going from 3B to 11B in this specific summarization task." Based on this finding and computational constraints, all subsequent experiments use the T5X-XL preference model and T5X-L policy models.

#### Pairwise Policy Comparison Under the Training Preference Model `$P_τ$`

Table 2 presents the pairwise regularized preference `$P_τ(π_c \succ π_r)$` for all 14 models (SFT, RLHF, SP, MD1–MD6, BR, EMA1, EMA1\*, EMA2, EMA2\*), estimated from 1,000 comparisons per pair. Key patterns:

**RLHF baseline performance.** RLHF achieves a 0.975 win rate against SFT (Table 2, row 2, column 1) — the highest win rate against SFT among all models — and dominates most other models: 0.741 against SP, 0.769 against MD1, 0.744 against MD3, 0.661 against MD4. However, RLHF is **beaten by MD5** (0.450 — i.e., MD5 wins with 0.550), **MD6** (0.340), **BR** (0.167), and **EMA2** (0.469), and is roughly even with EMA2\* (0.510 — 51% win rate for EMA2\*). This shows RLHF is strong but not uniformly dominant.

**Nash-MD-PG with intermediate `$β$`.** The MD1–MD6 models exhibit a clear pattern: performance under `$P_τ$` initially improves as `$β$` increases from 0 (SP) to intermediate values, then declines at higher `$β$`. Specifically:

- MD1 (`$β = 0.125$`) beats SP with 0.547, RLHF with 0.769 (i.e., MD1 wins ~77% of the time against RLHF), MD3 with 0.531, and BR with 0.921.
- MD2 (`$β = 0.25$`) beats SP with 0.529, RLHF with 0.752, and BR with 0.921.
- MD3 (`$β = 0.375$`) beats SP with 0.531, RLHF with 0.744, and shows the most extreme win rate against BR: 0.913 (MD3 wins ~91% against best-response).
- MD4 (`$β = 0.5$`) has mixed results: beats SP with 0.646, but loses to RLHF (0.339, i.e., RLHF wins ~66%) and has a more moderate 0.620 against MD3.
- MD5 and MD6 (`$β = 0.625, 0.75$`) show declining performance against RLHF (MD5: 0.550; MD6: 0.660), SP (MD5: 0.756; MD6: 0.815), and BR (MD5: 0.831; MD6: 0.731).

The paper highlights the "interesting trade-off" visible in the RLHF row: `$P_τ(\text{RLHF} \succ \text{MD}β) = 0.741, 0.752, 0.744, 0.661, 0.450, 0.340$` for `$β = 0.125, 0.25, 0.375, 0.5, 0.625, 0.75$` respectively — RLHF's win rate against MD models **decreases** as `$β$` increases from 0.125 to 0.75, meaning the stronger Nash-MD variants (larger `$β$`) actually perform better against RLHF. This is visible more clearly from the MD rows: MD4 wins against RLHF with 0.501 (essentially even), while MD5 wins with 0.564 and MD6 with 0.716.

**Self-Play (SP, `$β = 0$`).** SP performs moderately: it beats SFT (0.981, near ceiling), but loses to RLHF (0.259, i.e., RLHF wins ~74%), all MD variants (MD1: 0.453; MD2: 0.494; MD3: 0.491), and EMA1 (0.418). Under the `$P_τ$` evaluation, SP is the **weakest** of the non-trivial policies after SFT, suggesting pure self-play is insufficient.

**Best-Response (BR, `$β = 1$`).** BR is a peculiar case: it achieves near-perfect win rates against SP (0.918) and very strong wins against MD1–MD4 (0.921, 0.921, 0.913, 0.912 respectively), yet loses heavily to RLHF (0.167 — RLHF wins ~83%) and even to MD5 (0.831) and MD6 (0.731). The paper interprets this as possible "preference hacking, where BR may be overly adapting to the preference model by overfitting to the specific SFT policy." BR beats the SFT-based opponent it was trained against, but generalizes poorly — it is exploitable by policies trained differently.

**Nash-EMA-PG.** The four EMA variants (EMA1, EMA2, EMA1\*, EMA2\*) all underperform the best Nash-MD-PG models. EMA1 (last-iterate, `$β = 0.999$`) achieves 0.360 against RLHF, 0.582 against SP, 0.628 against MD1, and 0.144 against BR — meaning it loses to all Nash-MD variants with `$β \leq 0.375$`. EMA2 (last-iterate, `$β = 0.9995$`) does somewhat better against RLHF (0.469) but is still beaten by MD1–MD4 and MD6. The average-policy variants (EMA1\*, EMA2\*) show similar patterns, with EMA2\* being roughly even against RLHF (0.510 — RLHF wins ~49%) and EMA1\* losing (0.383). The paper observes that "all Nash-EMA models... are outperformed by Nash-MD for `$β \leq 0.5$` and RLHF," suggesting the parameter-space EMA approximation is "not well-suited in this context."

#### Pairwise Policy Comparison Under the Independent PaLM 2 Evaluator `$P^*$`

Table 3 presents the external evaluation using PaLM 2 Large as a preference oracle `$P^*$`, estimated from 2,000 comparisons per pair. This is the paper's primary results table, as it evaluates policies using a model that was **not** used during training and is architecturally different (PaLM 2 Large vs. T5X-XL). The numbers represent `$P^*(π_c \succ π_r)$` — the probability PaLM 2 Large assigns to the column policy being preferred over the row policy.

**SFT as baseline.** PaLM 2 finds that all trained models massively beat SFT, with win rates ranging from 0.943 (BR) to 0.990 (RLHF). RLHF's 0.990 win rate against SFT is the highest in the table, confirming that the RLHF baseline substantially improves over the initial model.

**Nash-MD-PG models emerge as best.** The MD1 model (`$β = 0.125$`) is the single best-performing model by several metrics:
- MD1 **beats RLHF** with 0.598 (`$P^*(\text{RLHF} \succ \text{MD1}) = 0.402$`), meaning the PaLM 2 evaluator prefers MD1 over RLHF ~60% of the time.
- MD1 beats SP (0.592 — actually 0.408 in the SP row, column MD1 = `$P^*(\text{SP} \succ \text{MD1}) = 0.408$` means MD1 wins ~59%), MD3 (0.530), MD4 (0.631), MD5 (0.638), MD6 (0.762), BR (0.837), and all EMA variants (EMA1: 0.609; EMA2: 0.730; EMA1\*: 0.600; EMA2\*: 0.553).
- MD1 is the **only model with a negative entry in its entire column** (no model achieves >0.5 against MD1), meaning no other model is preferred over MD1 by PaLM 2 — MD1 is effectively the Condorcet winner.

**MD2 and MD3 also outperform RLHF.** MD2 beats RLHF with 0.519 (`$P^*(\text{RLHF} \succ \text{MD2}) = 0.481$`). MD3 beats RLHF with 0.561 (`$P^*(\text{RLHF} \succ \text{MD3}) = 0.439$`). Combined with MD1's 0.598, all three Nash-MD models with `$β \in \{0.125, 0.25, 0.375\}$` are preferred over the RLHF baseline by PaLM 2.

**Self-Play is competitive but not best.** SP beats RLHF with 0.511 — essentially even (RLHF row: 0.489 means RLHF wins 48.9% against SP). SP loses to MD1 (0.408), MD2 (0.496), MD3 (0.455), and MD4 (0.501). The pattern is consistent with Table 2: SP is better than SFT but worse than the optimized Nash-MD variants.

**Best-Response fails dramatically.** Under `$P^*$`, BR loses to RLHF with 0.148 (RLHF wins ~85%), to SP with 0.211, to MD1 with 0.163, and indeed to **every other model except SFT** (which it beats with 0.943). This is the strongest evidence of "preference hacking" — BR overfits to beating SFT against the training preference model `$P_τ$`, but the resulting policy performs poorly under the independent evaluator `$P^*$`.

**The `$β$` trade-off under `$P^*$`.** The blue-highlighted numbers the paper references show the PaLM 2 evaluation of Nash-MD variants against RLHF: `$P^*(\text{RLHF} \succ \text{MD}β) = 0.402, 0.481, 0.439, 0.501, 0.564, 0.716$` for `$β = 0.125, 0.25, 0.375, 0.5, 0.625, 0.75$`. Lower numbers indicate the MD variant is more preferred over RLHF. The optimal is `$β = 0.125$` (MD1), with performance degrading as `$β$` increases. The paper interprets this:

> "both extreme values of the mixing parameter `$β \in [0, 1]$`, namely `$β = 0$` (self-play) and `$β = 1$` (best-response against SFT), result in suboptimal performance compared to intermediate values of `$β$`"

**Nash-EMA-PG under `$P^*$`.** The EMA variants continue to underperform. EMA1 beats RLHF (0.532, i.e., EMA1 wins ~53%), but loses to MD1 (0.391), MD2 (0.460), MD3 (0.429), MD4 (0.468), and MD5 (0.532). EMA2 loses to RLHF (0.320). EMA1\* is roughly even with RLHF (0.523). EMA2\* is roughly even with RLHF (0.490 — RLHF wins ~51%). None of the EMA models approach the performance of MD1–MD3.

**Overall ranking under `$P^*$`.** Reading the columns (what fraction of opponents each model beats), the approximate ordering from strongest to weakest is: MD1 > MD2 ≈ MD3 > MD4 ≈ SP ≈ EMA2\* ≈ RLHF > EMA1\* ≈ EMA1 > MD5 > MD6 > EMA2 > BR > SFT. MD1 is the clear winner, and the Nash-MD family dominates the top ranks.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Preference model vs. reward model accuracy (Appendix G.1, Figures 1–2):** The T5X-XL preference model achieves 78% test accuracy vs. 76% for the equivalently-sized reward model. The preference model's advantage is consistent but small (2 percentage points). This ablation justifies the choice to use a preference model rather than a reward model for the NLHF training loop.

**KL-regularization strength `$τ$` sweep for NLHF (Appendix G.4):** The paper sweeps `$τ \in \{0.02, 0.01, 0.008, 0.005\}$` and selects `$τ = 0.008$` for all Nash-MD and Nash-EMA experiments. The selection criterion is not explicitly described beyond "selected for the pairwise comparison table." The RLHF baseline uses a separately-swept `$τ = 0.05$` (from `$\{0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2\}$`).

**Nash-MD-PG mixing coefficient `$β$` sweep (Tables 2–3):** The sweep over `$β \in \{0, 0.125, 0.25, 0.375, 0.5, 0.625, 0.75, 0.875, 1.0\}$` reveals the optimal range is `$β \in [0.125, 0.375]$`, with `$β = 0.125$` performing best under `$P^*$` and `$β = 0.25$` performing comparably. Both `$β = 0.875$` and `$β = 1.0$` are evaluated (MD6 uses `$β = 0.75$`, BR uses `$β = 1.0$`), and both extremes degrade relative to the optimum. The `$β = 0.875$` model is not separately reported (the sweep skips from 0.75 to 1.0), leaving a gap in the fine-grained characterization.

**Nash-EMA-PG decay rate `$β$` sweep (Tables 2–3):** The sweep over `$β \in \{0, 0.999, 0.9995, 0.9999, 1.0\}$` shows that EMA-based opponents underperform geometric-mixture opponents at all tested values. The effective time constants of 1,000 steps (`$β = 0.999$`) and 2,000 steps (`$β = 0.9995$`) produce reasonable policies, but they are consistently below Nash-MD-PG performance. `$β = 0.9999$` results are not prominently reported in Tables 2–3, suggesting they may have been omitted due to poor performance.

**Last-iterate vs. average-policy for Nash-EMA-PG (Tables 2–3):** For both `$β = 0.999$` and `$β = 0.9995$`, the average-policy variant (EMA1\*, EMA2\*) and last-iterate variant (EMA1, EMA2) are compared. Under `$P^*$`, EMA1 beats EMA1\* (0.520 — average-policy EMA1\* wins ~48%), and EMA2 beats EMA2\* (0.619 — average-policy EMA2\* wins ~38%). The last-iterate performs marginally better than the average-policy for both `$β$` values, which is somewhat surprising since convergence-on-average is the typical guarantee for EMA-like algorithms. The paper does not comment on this reversal.

**Evaluation with different preference oracles (`$P_τ$` in Table 2 vs. `$P^*$` in Table 3):** The same set of policies is evaluated with two different preference models: the T5X-XL model `$P_τ$` used during training, and the independent PaLM 2 Large model `$P^*$`. The rankings are broadly consistent — Nash-MD with intermediate `$β$` performs best, BR performs poorly — but there are notable discrepancies. Under `$P_τ$`, BR beats MD1 (0.921 — MD1 only wins ~8%), while under `$P^*$`, MD1 beats BR (0.837 — BR only wins ~16%). This reversal confirms that BR is overfitting to the training preference model and that `$P^*$` provides a genuinely independent assessment. The paper's use of an external, differently-architected evaluator is a strength of the evaluation.

**RLHF baseline `$τ$` sweep (Appendix G.3):** The RLHF regularization parameter is swept over `$\{0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2\}$`, with `$τ = 0.05$` selected. This is notably larger than the NLHF `$τ = 0.008$`, but the comparison is not 1-to-1 because RLHF uses a fixed reward model while NLHF uses a preference model with a dynamic opponent — the effective regularization landscape differs. The paper does not explore whether RLHF with `$τ$` values closer to 0.008 would perform differently.

**Preference model scaling (Figure 1):** Accuracy improves from the 110M model to the 3B model, with diminishing returns from 3B to 11B. The 11B model is used for all training, but the paper does not ablate whether a smaller preference model (e.g., 3B) would produce similar policy quality, which matters for practical deployment.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: NLHF with Nash-MD-PG produces policies that outperform RLHF.**

The evidence supports this claim for the specific setting tested. In the PaLM 2 Large evaluation (Table 3), MD1 (`$β = 0.125$`) achieves `$P^*(\text{RLHF} \succ \text{MD1}) = 0.402$` — meaning PaLM 2 prefers MD1 over RLHF ~60% of the time. MD2 and MD3 also beat RLHF (0.481 and 0.439 respectively). Under the training preference model `$P_τ$` (Table 2), the gap is larger: RLHF achieves only 0.025 against MD1 (MD1 wins ~97.5%).

However, several caveats apply. First, the RLHF baseline, while described as "a very strong baseline," uses a specific configuration (`$τ = 0.05$`) selected from a sweep, and the paper acknowledges that NLHF hyperparameters were not individually optimized — "we have not tried to over-optimize the hyper-parameters... this could explain some differences observed." A more extensively tuned RLHF baseline could potentially close or reverse the gap. Second, the evaluation uses PaLM 2 Large as a proxy for human preference, not actual human judgments. PaLM 2's own biases and training are uncharacterized. The paper acknowledges this is "a proxy method" and that "the ideal approach for evaluating our models would involve soliciting human preferences." Third, the result is demonstrated on a single task (TL;DR summarization) with a single model family (T5X) and specific scale (T5X-L policy, T5X-XL preference model). Generalization to other tasks, scales, or model architectures is not established.

**Claim 2: Intermediate values of `$β$` (the opponent mixing coefficient) outperform both self-play (`$β = 0$`) and best-response against SFT (`$β = 1$`).**

This claim is strongly supported by both Tables 2 and 3. Under `$P^*$`, MD1 (`$β = 0.125$`) is preferred over both SP (`$β = 0$`) and BR (`$β = 1$`), and is the single best model overall. Under `$P_τ$`, MD1 beats SP (0.547) and BR (0.921), though BR beats MD1 under `$P_τ$` (0.921 vs. 0.079) — an instance of overfitting that `$P^*$` corrects.

The experimental sweep, however, has a granularity gap: `$β$` values of 0.875, 0.9, 0.95, and 0.99 are not tested. The transition from MD6 (`$β = 0.75$`) to BR (`$β = 1.0$`) is a large jump, and the U-shaped performance curve (optimal at low `$β$`, degrading at high `$β$`) is characterized at only a few points. A finer sweep between 0 and 0.25 would clarify whether `$β = 0.125$` is truly optimal or whether even smaller values (e.g., 0.05, 0.01) might perform better. The theoretical algorithm couples the mixture weight to the learning rate schedule, and while the paper decouples `$β$` for practical tuning, the lack of exploration of very small non-zero `$β$` leaves open whether the optimal opponent is nearly-self-play with minimal anchoring.

**Claim 3: The preference model is less sensitive to the data-generating distribution than a reward model (Section 3.3, Appendix B, Theorem 2).**

This claim is supported theoretically (Theorem 2) but is **not tested experimentally**. The paper does not train reward models on data from different policies and compare how their scores shift, nor does it train preference models on different distributions and evaluate invariance. The claim about distributional insensitivity is presented as a structural property of the preference model's functional form (it takes two inputs, so its optimum depends only on the pair, not on the marginal distribution), but the paper acknowledges that "when using approximate models or a finite amount of data, the learned preference model may still depend on the data distribution." No experiment quantifies this dependence or compares it to the reward model's dependence. This is a gap between the theoretical argument and the empirical validation.

**Claim 4: Nash-MD achieves last-iterate convergence to the Nash equilibrium (Theorem 1).**

This is a theoretical result proved for the tabular (non-parametric) setting. The deep learning experiments do **not directly test convergence** to a ground-truth Nash equilibrium — they show that Nash-MD-PG produces policies that win pairwise comparisons, but do not measure exploitability (the gap between the policy's win rate and 1/2 against a best-response opponent) or estimate distance to the Nash equilibrium. The paper acknowledges that Nash-MD-PG differs from tabular Nash-MD in several ways: it uses parametric policies, single gradient steps rather than full inner optimization, and the one-token-at-a-time approximation for the geometric mixture opponent. The gap between the theoretical guarantee and the practical algorithm is substantial, and no experiments quantify the impact of these approximations on convergence quality.

**Weaknesses in evaluation design:**

- **No human evaluation.** The paper evaluates policies using PaLM 2 Large as a preference oracle, not actual human raters. While PaLM 2 is presumably well-calibrated as a summarization evaluator, this is an assumption, not a demonstrated fact. The preference model used during training was trained on human data, but the final comparison is model-vs-model (via PaLM 2), not model-vs-human.

- **Single task, single dataset.** TL;DR summarization is one domain with a specific type of preference structure. It is unclear whether the Nash equilibrium approach provides benefits on tasks with different preference distributions (e.g., tasks where humans are highly uniform, or tasks with extreme preference diversity).

- **Small policy models.** The T5X-L policy model is substantially smaller than state-of-the-art LLMs. The memory and compute constraints that motivate last-iterate convergence (avoiding storing multiple checkpoints) are more pressing at billion-parameter scales, but the experiments don't test whether the algorithm scales to those regimes.

- **No comparison to offline preference methods (DPO, IPO).** The paper distinguishes NLHF from DPO, SLiC, IPO, and GPO by its online, game-theoretic nature, but does not experimentally compare against any of these methods. A direct comparison against DPO or IPO on the same TL;DR task would clarify whether the online self-play component provides benefits beyond what offline optimization against the same preference data can achieve.

- **Equal step count, unequal hyperparameter tuning.** All models are trained for 10,000 steps, but the effective amount of preference signal per step differs between algorithms (e.g., self-play generates both responses from the same policy, while best-response generates one from a frozen model). The paper acknowledges this lack of per-algorithm tuning as a limitation, but it means the comparison may favor whichever algorithm happens to work well with the shared hyperparameters.

- **No ablation on the one-token-at-a-time approximation.** The paper acknowledges that `$\tilde{π}^β_θ \neq π^β_θ$` but "leave[s] the analysis of the difference between these two policies for future work." The impact of this approximation on the quality of the opponent — and thus on the optimization dynamics — is unknown and unquantified.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the evaluation:**

- Measuring exploitability of the final policies by training an adversary specifically to beat them, and reporting the gap between the achieved win rate and 1/2.
- Comparing Nash-MD-PG against offline preference optimization methods (DPO, IPO) on the same data and task.
- Evaluating on a second task (e.g., dialogue, instruction-following) to test generality.
- Training reward models and preference models on data from different policies and measuring distribution shift to empirically validate the distributional-sensitivity claim.
- Sweeping `$β$` at finer granularity (especially 0.01–0.10) to characterize the optimal opponent mixture more precisely.
- Ablating the `$1/2$` baseline in the policy gradient estimator against using a learned value baseline, to quantify the variance reduction benefit.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 No Empirical Validation of the Central Theoretical Claim: Distributional Insensitivity of Preference Models

**The assumption.** The paper's strongest theoretical argument for preferring NLHF over RLHF is that preference models are structurally insensitive to the data-generating distribution, while reward models are not. Section 3.3 argues:

> "in this ideal case of perfect representations, a preference model is insensitive to the data generation distribution `$(\rho, \pi, \pi')$` whereas a reward model depends on it"

Theorem 2 (Appendix B) formally proves that the BT reward model depends on the sampling distribution `$\pi$` when preferences are not perfectly Bradley-Terry. The paper further claims this makes preference models reusable across iterative improvement rounds:

> "the preference model can be preserved and further enriched through the introduction of novel data, thereby offering a more seamless and efficient adaptation process"

**The consequence.** There is **zero experimental evidence** for this claim. The paper does not train a reward model on data from policy A and a preference model on data from policy A, then measure how each model's predictions or rankings shift when evaluated on outputs from a different policy B. It does not compare the rate at which reward-model accuracy degrades versus preference-model accuracy degrades under distribution shift. It does not run a second iteration of NLHF where the preference model from iteration 1 is reused (rather than retrained) for iteration 2, and compare this against an RLHF baseline where the reward model must be retrained. The distributional insensitivity argument — which is the paper's most original theoretical contribution distinguishing NLHF from RLHF — remains entirely at the level of mathematical formalism. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt NLHF for iterative improvement pipelines would want to know whether this claimed advantage actually manifests in practice, especially since the paper itself acknowledges:

> "when using approximate models or a finite amount of data, the learned preference model may still depend on the data distribution as the quality of the approximation depends on the local quantity of collected data"

This caveat directly undermines the practical relevance of the theoretical claim: the "ideal case of perfect representations" never holds, and the paper quantifies nothing about how much distributional dependence remains in the realistic, approximate regime.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. This limitation is purely the absence of a relevant experiment to support a central theoretical claim.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not flag this gap as a limitation to be explored in future work. The closest it comes is the quoted caveat about finite data, but this is presented as a brief acknowledgment, not as a open empirical question requiring investigation.

---

### 6.2 Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Completely Unaccounted for in Any Budget Calculation

**The assumption.** The entire NLHF framework requires a trained preference model to provide the pairwise comparison signal `$P(y \succ y' \mid x)$` during policy optimization. This preference model is a T5X-XL model with 11 billion parameters — substantially larger than the T5X-L policy model being optimized. At each of the 10,000 training steps, the preference model must be queried to evaluate the two generated summaries (one from `$\pi_\theta$`, one from the opponent). This means the preference model runs inference at least 20,000 times during training (10,000 steps × 2 summaries, plus any additional evaluations).

The paper does not account for the cost of training or running this preference model in any compute budget, FLOPs comparison, or efficiency metric. The preference model is presented as a drop-in replacement for the reward model, and while it is true that RLHF also requires a reward model, the paper's argument that NLHF is simpler or more natural does not address the computational reality: the preference model is architecturally identical in scale to the reward model (both are T5X-XL), so the training-time inference cost is comparable, not reduced.

**The consequence.** A practitioner evaluating whether to adopt NLHF over RLHF would need to consider the total pipeline cost, not just the conceptual elegance. The paper provides no evidence that NLHF achieves better sample efficiency — i.e., that it requires fewer total preference model queries to reach a given policy quality. All models are trained for exactly 10,000 steps, but no experiment varies the number of steps to measure convergence speed. It is possible that NLHF converges faster (needing fewer preference model queries) or slower (needing more queries because the opponent is dynamic) than RLHF, but neither is demonstrated.

More subtly, the preference model's role as both a training signal and an evaluator creates a potential circularity that the paper partially addresses (by using PaLM 2 for the final evaluation) but does not quantify. During training, the policy is optimized to maximize `$P_τ$`, which is computed using the same T5X-XL preference model. If that model has systematic errors or biases, the policy will be optimized to exploit them — the classic reward hacking problem that NLHF is partly designed to mitigate, but which may still occur with a learned preference model. The paper's evaluation using PaLM 2 Large (Table 3) provides some protection against this circularity, but it does not measure the **gap** between training-time preference model scores and PaLM 2 scores as a function of optimization — which would directly quantify over-optimization.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The discrepancy between Table 2 (`$P_τ$`, the training preference model) and Table 3 (`$P^*$`, the independent PaLM 2 evaluator) provides indirect evidence. For example, under `$P_τ$`, BR (best-response) beats MD1 (0.921, meaning BR wins ~92% against MD1). Under `$P^*$`, this reverses: MD1 beats BR (0.837, meaning MD1 wins ~84% against BR). This is a ~76-percentage-point swing depending on which evaluator is used. While the paper correctly interprets this as evidence that BR overfits to `$P_τ$`, it also demonstrates that the training preference model and the independent evaluator disagree substantially on the relative quality of policies — which is precisely the kind of mismatch that would matter for a practitioner relying on the preference model as a training signal.

**Mitigation status.** The paper uses PaLM 2 Large as an independent evaluator, which is a reasonable mitigation for the final evaluation, but it does not address the training-time concern: the policy is still optimized against the T5X-XL preference model, not PaLM 2. The paper does not experiment with using a different or ensemble of preference models during training, does not measure training-preference-model vs. PaLM-2 score correlation across optimization, and does not discuss the cost of the preference model as a fraction of total pipeline compute.

---

### 6.3 Single Benchmark, Single Model Family, Single Task Type

**The constraint.** All experiments — preference model training, preference model accuracy evaluation, policy optimization, and final pairwise comparisons — use a single dataset: the TL;DR summarization task (Völske et al., 2017) as processed by Stiennon et al. (2020). The training set contains 92,820 pairwise comparisons. All models (policy and preference/rward) are from the T5X family. The policy model is T5X-L, the preference model is T5X-XL, and the independent evaluator is PaLM 2 Large — three different architectures, but the core algorithm is tested on one task only.

The paper acknowledges this scope explicitly:

> "The primary objective of these experiments is to provide a proof of concept for the NLHF approach introduced in this paper, rather than striving for state-of-the-art performance in text summarization"

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the NLHF framework's claimed advantages cannot be assessed from a single task. The paper argues that preference models can capture non-transitive preferences (Appendix C) and accommodate preference diversity through mixed-strategy Nash equilibria (Section 3.2). Whether TL;DR summarization actually exhibits non-transitive human preferences or significant preference diversity is never established. If the dataset is largely transitive and homogeneous — which is plausible for a task where "quality" has a relatively consistent definition (accurate, concise summary of the article) — then the theoretical advantages of the preference model over the BT reward model may be irrelevant for this task, and the observed performance differences between NLHF and RLHF are attributable to the optimization dynamics (self-play vs. fixed reward maximization) rather than to the representational flexibility of the preference model.

The paper also offers no evidence on whether the `$β = 0.125$` optimum generalizes across tasks. The finding that a mixture opponent with 12.5% reference weight works best might be specific to the TL;DR preference structure, the T5X model family, or the scale of the models used. A different task with different preference characteristics (more diversity, more non-transitivity, different base model capabilities) might have a different optimal `$β$`, or might even show that Nash-MD-PG underperforms RLHF entirely.

The single-task limitation also means the paper cannot characterize **when NLHF is expected to help vs. when RLHF is sufficient**. Is the Nash equilibrium approach most valuable when preferences are diverse? When the base model is already strong? When the task has a large output space? These are the questions a practitioner needs answered to decide whether to adopt NLHF, and the current experiments provide no evidence to answer them.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper demonstrates a consistent pattern on TL;DR: Nash-MD-PG with intermediate `$β$` outperforms RLHF under both `$P_τ$` and `$P^*$`. This is a valid existence proof, but it is a single data point in task space.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly describes the work as a "proof of concept" and does not claim broader task generalization. This is a reasonable scope for a first paper introducing a new framework, but it leaves the generalizability question entirely open. The paper does not suggest specific future work on multi-task or multi-domain evaluation.

---

### 6.4 No Comparison Against Offline Preference Optimization Methods

**The constraint.** The paper frames NLHF as an alternative to RLHF and contrasts it with the standard reward-model-plus-policy-optimization pipeline. However, a significant body of recent work has proposed methods that, like NLHF, optimize policies directly from pairwise preferences without learning a scalar reward model: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO; Rafailov et al., 2023), Sequence Likelihood Calibration (SLiC; Zhao et al., 2023), Identity Preference Optimization (IPO; Azar et al., 2023), and Generalized Preference Optimization (GPO; Tang et al., 2024). The paper acknowledges these in Section 2 ("Prior work") and distinguishes NLHF by its **online** nature — it learns through active self-play against a dynamically updated opponent — rather than offline optimization against a fixed dataset:

> "These methods directly optimize for preferences but differ from NLHF in that they optimize using offline datasets and against fixed policies, rather than computing Nash equilibria through online self-play"

However, the paper does not experimentally compare NLHF against any of these methods.

**The consequence.** The key practical question a practitioner would ask is: "Does the online, game-theoretic nature of NLHF provide benefits that justify the additional complexity and compute cost compared to simpler offline methods like DPO?" The paper provides no evidence to answer this question. DPO, in particular, has become widely adopted because it is simple to implement (a single loss function on the preference dataset, no preference model needed, no online generation), and has demonstrated strong performance on summarization and dialogue tasks. Without a DPO baseline on the same TL;DR dataset with the same base model, it is impossible to know whether NLHF's performance gains over RLHF come from the preference-model formulation (which DPO also uses implicitly), from the online self-play dynamics (which DPO lacks), or from some other aspect of the training setup.

The paper's acknowledgment of the relationship between NLHF and these methods is also revealing: it notes that the online version of IPO (Calandriello et al., 2024) "approximates the Nash equilibrium of a preference model using a particular case of Nash-MD (called Self-Play)," which is exactly the `$β = 0$` variant that the current paper finds is **not** the best-performing configuration. This suggests that the current paper's key finding — that `$β > 0$` matter — might directly improve IPO-style methods, but this hypothesis is untested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. DPO, SLiC, IPO, and GPO are cited and discussed in Section 2, but no experimental baselines using these methods are reported.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this gap. Given that the paper was published at ICML 2024 and the DPO/IPO line of work was well-established by then, this is a notable omission. The paper's focus on comparing against an RLHF baseline (and variants of its own method) is understandable for a first paper introducing a new framework, but the absence of a DPO comparison limits the practical takeaway: a practitioner cannot tell whether NLHF is better than the simpler alternative they might already be using.

---

### 6.5 The Gap Between Tabular Convergence Theory and Deep Learning Implementation Is Large and Unquantified

**The constraint.** Theorem 1 proves `$O(1/T)$` last-iterate convergence for the tabular Nash-MD algorithm under specific conditions: the policy is non-parametric (a direct probability table), the opponent is the exact full-sequence geometric mixture `$\pi^\mu_t$`, the inner maximization `$\arg\max_\pi [\eta_t P(\pi \succ \pi^\mu_t) - \text{KL}(\pi, \pi^\mu_t)]$` is solved exactly at each iteration, and the learning rate follows the decreasing schedule `$\eta_t = 2/(\tau(t+2))$`.

The Nash-MD-PG algorithm used in the experiments deviates from these conditions in **every dimension**:

1. **Parametric policies:** `$\pi_\theta$` is a neural network, not a table. The mirror descent step is approximated by a single gradient ascent step in parameter space, not a closed-form solution in probability space. There is no guarantee that gradient ascent on `$\theta$` corresponds to mirror descent in the policy simplex.

2. **Single gradient step per iteration:** The tabular algorithm solves a full inner optimization to find `$\pi_{t+1}$` given `$\pi_t$` and `$\pi^\mu_t$`. Nash-MD-PG takes one gradient step and immediately updates the opponent. The paper acknowledges this:

> "a more faithful variant of Nash-MD would use a two-time scale algorithm and perform several gradient steps (while keeping `$\pi_\theta$` and `$\pi^\beta_\theta$` fixed) until the inner loop has reached an optimum, before updating `$\pi_\theta$` and `$\pi^\beta_\theta$`"

3. **Token-level approximation of the geometric mixture:** The opponent in Nash-MD-PG uses `$\tilde{\pi}^\beta_\theta$`, the one-token-at-a-time geometric mixture, not the true full-sequence mixture `$\pi^\beta_\theta$`. The paper acknowledges:

> "the one-step-at-a-time regularized policy `$\tilde{\pi}^\beta_\theta(y \mid x)$` is different from the original regularized policy `$\pi^\beta_\theta(y \mid x)$`... We leave the analysis of the difference between these two policies for future work."

4. **Constant learning rate and decoupled `$β$`:** The tabular algorithm couples the opponent mixture weight to `$\eta_t \tau$`. Nash-MD-PG uses a separate, constant `$β$` and a (presumably) constant learning rate for 10,000 steps. The decreasing learning rate schedule that enables the `$O(1/T)$` convergence proof is not used.

**The consequence.** The theoretical guarantee of convergence to the Nash equilibrium **does not apply** to the algorithm that produces the experimental results. The experiments demonstrate that Nash-MD-PG produces policies that win pairwise comparisons against RLHF, but they do not measure whether those policies are actually at or near the Nash equilibrium of the preference model. The paper reports no exploitability metric (how much can an adversary beat the final policy?), no distance-to-equilibrium estimate, and no diagnostic showing that the optimization is converging rather than cycling or plateauing at a non-equilibrium point.

For a practitioner, this means the theoretical properties that motivate the algorithm — convergence to a unique, unexploitable Nash equilibrium — are aspirational rather than guaranteed. The algorithm works well empirically on this task, but the reason it works may not be the Nash equilibrium property. It could be that self-play with a small reference anchor is simply a good regularization technique for policy gradient, independent of any game-theoretic interpretation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The experimental results (Tables 2 and 3) provide evidence that Nash-MD-PG produces strong policies, but no evidence about proximity to the Nash equilibrium. The paper does not train an adversarial policy to explicitly exploit each trained model and measure win rates, which would be the standard way to assess exploitability.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about the differences between the tabular algorithm and the deep learning implementation (Appendix F.3 discusses several of them), but does not quantify their impact or provide diagnostics. The theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) and the practical contribution (Nash-MD-PG with `$β = 0.125$` outperforms RLHF) are connected by analogy and inspiration, not by formal reduction.

### 6.6 The Evaluation Uses a Language Model as a Preference Oracle Without Characterizing Its Biases or Calibration

**The assumption.** The paper's headline results (Table 3) use PaLM 2 Large as an independent evaluator `$P^*$` to compare policies. The paper describes this as:

> "a proxy method using the highly capable LLM, PaLM 2 Large... This `$P^*$` serves as an approximate surrogate for human preferences."

PaLM 2 Large is prompted with the same expert-summary-rater format used to initialize the T5X-XL preference model. The evaluation then treats PaLM 2's preference probabilities (estimated from 2,000 samples per policy pair) as the ground truth for policy quality.

**The consequence.** The central empirical claim — "Nash-MD-PG with `$β = 0.125$` outperforms RLHF" — is actually the claim "PaLM 2 Large's pairwise preferences between model-generated summaries favor MD1 over RLHF ~60% of the time." Whether this corresponds to actual human preferences is unknown and untested. PaLM 2 Large may have systematic biases: it may prefer summaries that resemble its own generation style, it may be sensitive to superficial features (length, formatting, vocabulary) rather than summary quality, it may have been trained on data that includes T5X-generated summaries (creating a confound), or it may simply make errors at a rate comparable to or larger than the performance differences being measured.

The 95% confidence interval half-width for each pairwise comparison in Table 3 is ±0.023 (2,000 samples, Clopper-Pearson). The preference differences between the top models are often within or near this range. For example, MD1 vs. RLHF: `$P^*(\text{RLHF} \succ \text{MD1}) = 0.402$`, meaning MD1's estimated win rate over RLHF is 0.598 with a 95% CI of approximately [0.575, 0.621]. This is statistically significant but not overwhelmingly large — a systematic bias in PaLM 2's judgments of even a few percentage points could change which model appears best.

The paper does not validate PaLM 2's preferences against human judgments on any subset of the evaluation data. This is a well-known concern with LLM-as-judge evaluations (the approach is inspired by Lee et al., 2023, but that paper included human validation of the LLM judge's alignment with human raters). Without such validation, the reader cannot distinguish between "MD1 is genuinely better according to human preferences" and "MD1 produces summaries that PaLM 2 happens to like."

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports that PaLM 2 is "highly capable" and that this evaluation "shares similarities with the method employed by Lee et al. (2023)," but provides no quantitative evidence of PaLM 2's calibration against human judgments on the TL;DR task. The paper also reports results under the training preference model `$P_τ$` (Table 2), and the two evaluators agree broadly (MD1–MD3 outperform RLHF, BR performs poorly), but also disagree substantially on specific comparisons (e.g., BR vs. MD1: `$P_τ$` strongly favors BR, `$P^*$` strongly favors MD1). This discrepancy itself highlights the risk of relying on a single model-based evaluator without human calibration.

**Mitigation status.** The paper uses an independent evaluator (PaLM 2 Large) architecturally different from the training preference model (T5X-XL), which partially mitigates the circularity concern. However, the independent evaluator is still an LLM, not a human. The paper does not suggest collecting human evaluation data for final validation, nor does it characterize PaLM 2's agreement rate with human judgments on this specific task. The ideal evaluation, which the paper acknowledges, would be human pairwise judgments, but this was not conducted.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper reframes LLM alignment from an **optimization problem** (maximize a scalar reward) to a **game-theoretic equilibrium problem** (find a policy preferred over any competitor). The shift is conceptual rather than purely algorithmic: it changes what it *means* for a model to be aligned. In the standard RLHF view, alignment means the policy assigns high probability to outputs that a reward model scores highly. In the NLHF view, alignment means the policy is **unexploitable** — no alternative policy can consistently produce outputs preferred over its outputs according to human judgment. This is a more demanding definition, and the paper argues it is also a more faithful one when human preferences are heterogeneous or non-transitive.

This matters for the field in several concrete ways. First, it **resolves the tension between the RLHF paradigm and the well-documented problems of reward hacking and over-optimization** (Amodei et al., 2016; Gao et al., 2022). In standard RLHF, the policy is trained to maximize a proxy reward, and the central challenge is that the proxy is imperfect — the policy exploits its blind spots, producing outputs that score highly but are not actually preferred. NLHF's game-theoretic formulation attacks this problem structurally rather than patch-wise: instead of trying to build a perfect reward model (which the paper's own Theorem 2 suggests is impossible, since the reward model depends on the data distribution), NLHF trains the policy to beat any competitor, with the preference model serving as the referee. The preference model can be imperfect — what matters is whether the policy can still achieve >50% win rate against alternatives when judged by that same imperfect referee. This reframing doesn't eliminate the problem of imperfect preference models, but it changes the success criterion from "maximize a potentially-hackable scalar" to "be preferred in pairwise comparison against relevant alternatives," which is closer to the actual deployment objective.

Second, the paper **provides a principled explanation for why iterative improvement pipelines should use preference models rather than reward models**, based on the distributional insensitivity argument in Section 3.3 and Theorem 2. If reward models necessarily depend on the policy that generated their training data, then each iteration of an RLHF loop — collect data with current policy, train reward model, optimize against it, repeat — uses a reward model that is increasingly miscalibrated for the new policy's outputs. The paper proves this is a mathematical necessity when preferences are not perfectly Bradley-Terry (Theorem 2). The practical implication is that NLHF pipelines, which maintain and incrementally update a single preference model across iterations, are structurally better suited for iterative or continual improvement settings. This is a diagnostic insight: it tells the field that if you are building a multi-round alignment system, you should prefer a preference-model-based approach not just because it might work a bit better empirically, but because the reward-model-based approach has a *provable* miscalibration problem that compounds across rounds.

Third, the paper **introduces the mixing coefficient `$\beta$` as a meaningful hyperparameter controlling the self-play-to-reference spectrum**, with the empirical finding that intermediate values (`$\beta \in [0.125, 0.375]$`) systematically outperform extremes. Prior work on self-play for LLM alignment (e.g., the online version of IPO in Calandriello et al., 2024) used pure self-play (`$\beta = 0$`), while best-response-against-SFT is the de facto standard in supervised fine-tuning approaches. The paper's sweep provides evidence that neither extreme is optimal, and that a small anchor to a reference policy (12.5% reference weight in the geometric mixture) substantially improves performance. This is a finding with immediate practical relevance for any team implementing self-play or iterative improvement for LLMs: the opponent should not be exactly the current policy, nor a frozen baseline, but a mixture that is mostly the current policy with a small stabilizing pull toward the initial safe model. The paper does not fully explain *why* this particular ratio works — the tabular theory couples the mixing weight to `$\eta_t \tau$` and doesn't predict `$\beta = 0.125$` as special — but it establishes the empirical phenomenon clearly enough to guide hyperparameter sweeps in future work.

The paper also **changes how we should think about preference diversity in alignment**. The three-human-type example in Section 3.2, while stylized, illustrates a genuinely important point: when the human population has conflicting preferences, reward maximization collapses to a deterministic policy favoring the plurality, while the Nash equilibrium naturally produces a mixed strategy that respects the distribution of opinions. This connects NLHF to broader discussions about pluralistic alignment (Sorensen et al., 2024) and the impossibility of a single "best" response in open-ended generation tasks. The paper does not provide evidence that TL;DR summarization actually exhibits this kind of preference diversity, so the practical importance of this property remains to be demonstrated, but the conceptual framework is in place for future work that does target diverse-preference settings.

Finally, the paper contributes a **proof technique for last-iterate convergence in KL-regularized preference games** that may find applications beyond NLHF. The specific structure exploited — that the regularized preference `$P_\tau(\pi \succ \pi')$` is the sum of a bilinear form (the raw preference) and KL penalties, and that playing against a geometric mixture opponent makes the KL divergence to the Nash equilibrium contract multiplicatively — is elegant and may generalize to other game-theoretic formulations of alignment or multi-agent RL. The paper's explicit comparison with Online Mirror Descent (Section 6) isolates the crucial modification (playing against `$\pi^\mu_t$` rather than `$\pi_t$`) and shows why it matters, which is pedagogically valuable for the game-solving community.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Measuring and validating the distributional insensitivity of preference models empirically.** Theorem 2 proves that BT reward models depend on the data-generating policy, but the paper provides no experimental evidence for the corollary claim that preference models are more robust to distribution shift. A direct follow-up would: (1) train a reward model `$r^{\pi_1}$` and a preference model `$P$` on pairwise data generated by policy `$\pi_1$`; (2) train a second reward model `$r^{\pi_2}$` on data from a different policy `$\pi_2$`; (3) evaluate all three models on held-out pairs from both `$\pi_1$` and `$\pi_2$`'s output distributions. The prediction is that `$r^{\pi_1}$`'s accuracy degrades more on `$\pi_2$`'s outputs than `$P$` does, and that `$r^{\pi_1}$` and `$r^{\pi_2}$` produce meaningfully different pairwise orderings (as Theorem 2 proves they must if preferences are not perfectly BT). Quantifying the magnitude of this degradation would tell practitioners how much they should care about the theoretical result. A strong version of this experiment would iterate: collect data from `$\pi_1$`, train models, optimize to get `$\pi_2$`, collect data from `$\pi_2$`, retrain, etc., measuring whether preference model accuracy remains stable across rounds while reward model accuracy oscillates or trends downward.

**Adversarial exploitability measurement of NLHF-trained policies.** The paper reports pairwise win rates between fixed policies (Tables 2, 3) but never measures **exploitability**: if an adversary were explicitly trained to beat the final NLHF policy `$\pi_T$`, how low could they push its win rate? This is the natural metric for a Nash equilibrium method, since the whole point is to be unexploitable. A follow-up would take the MD1 policy (the best model from Table 3), freeze it, and train an adversary policy `$\pi_{\text{adv}}$` from scratch to minimize `$P^*(\pi_{\text{MD1}} \succ \pi_{\text{adv}})$`. The resulting win rate — the exploitability — would be the definitive test of whether Nash-MD-PG actually produces near-equilibrium policies or merely strong policies by some other mechanism. If MD1's win rate drops far below 50% against a trained adversary, it would suggest the algorithm's empirical success is not primarily attributable to the Nash equilibrium property. If it stays near or above 50%, it would validate the game-theoretic framing. This experiment also naturally tests the effect of `$\beta$` on exploitability: the prediction from the theory is that intermediate `$\beta$` reduces exploitability compared to `$\beta = 0$` (which cycles) or `$\beta = 1$` (which overfits to SFT). The paper does not run this experiment, but it is the cleanest test of the core NLHF hypothesis.

**Fine-grained sweep of `$\beta$` in the range 0.01–0.20, combined with learning rate schedule ablation.** The paper's `$\beta$` sweep in steps of 0.125 is coarse, and the optimum at `$\beta = 0.125$` suggests the true optimum might be even smaller. A finer sweep — `$\beta \in \{0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.08, 0.10, 0.12, 0.15, 0.18, 0.20\}$` — would characterize the shape of the performance curve near zero and determine whether the benefit of reference anchoring is continuous (any small `$\beta$` helps) or thresholded (needs a minimum anchor weight). Additionally, the tabular theory couples the mixing weight to the learning rate via `$\eta_t \tau$`. A follow-up that sweeps both `$\beta$` and the policy learning rate (or uses the decreasing schedule `$\eta_t = 2/(\tau(t+2))$` from Theorem 1) would test whether the theoretical coupling has practical relevance: does the optimal `$\beta$` change with the learning rate in the way the tabular analysis predicts? This would bridge the gap between the theory and the practical algorithm.

**Comparison with DPO and online IPO on the same task with the same base model.** The paper cites DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) and IPO (Azar et al., 2023) as related preference-based methods but provides no experimental comparison. A direct follow-up would implement DPO, offline IPO, and online IPO (Calandriello et al., 2024) on the TL;DR summarization task using the same T5X-L base model and the same T5X-XL preference model (or the underlying human preference data) for training. The comparison would answer: does the online self-play component of NLHF provide benefits beyond what offline optimization against the same preference data can achieve? The paper's finding that Nash-MD-PG with `$\beta = 0.125$` outperforms self-play (`$\beta = 0$`) is relevant here because online IPO corresponds to Nash-MD with `$\beta = 0$` — so if the current paper's results transfer, Nash-MD-PG with small `$\beta$` should outperform online IPO. This would be a direct test of whether the reference anchoring innovation is the key driver of NLHF's performance, or whether online preference optimization in general (even pure self-play) already outperforms RLHF on this task.

**Evaluation of NLHF on a task with documented human preference diversity or non-transitivity.** The paper argues that NLHF's advantages over RLHF are most pronounced when preferences are diverse or non-transitive, but tests on TL;DR summarization, where preference structure is uncharacterized. A follow-up would identify or construct a task where human preferences are known to be heterogeneous — for example, creative writing evaluation where different raters prefer different styles, or moral reasoning where value pluralism is explicit — and compare NLHF against RLHF. The prediction is that RLHF policies will collapse to a single style or value system preferred by the majority subpopulation, while NLHF policies (especially at the Nash equilibrium) will produce a more diverse set of outputs or a "compromise" policy that performs reasonably across all subpopulations. The three-human-type example in Section 3.2 makes this prediction mathematically, but it needs empirical validation. Measuring output diversity (e.g., entropy of the policy distribution, or coverage of different preference clusters) alongside win rates would test whether the Nash equilibrium actually produces the mixed-strategy behavior the theory predicts. This experiment would also stress-test the claim that the Nash equilibrium is "more aligned with the diversity of human preferences" — a claim that sounds appealing but could fail if the preference model itself fails to capture minority preferences adequately, or if the optimization dynamics collapse to a pure strategy despite the equilibrium being mixed.

**Two-timescale Nash-MD-PG with inner optimization loop.** The paper acknowledges that Nash-MD-PG approximates the tabular Nash-MD's inner maximization with a single gradient step, whereas the theory assumes the inner loop is solved to optimality. A follow-up would implement a two-timescale version: keep `$\pi_\theta$` and `$\pi^\beta_\theta$` fixed for K gradient steps (solving the inner `$\arg\max$` more accurately), then update the opponent, then repeat. Varying K from 1 (current algorithm) to, say, 100, and measuring both final policy quality and convergence stability would quantify the importance of this approximation. If K > 1 provides substantial benefits, it would suggest that the current Nash-MD-PG is leaving performance on the table and that the theoretical algorithm's properties are more relevant than the single-step results suggest. If K = 1 is already near-optimal, it would validate the practical algorithm independently of the theoretical justification — which would itself be an interesting finding about why the approximation works.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Iterative self-improvement pipelines with stable preference models.** The most direct practical application is in systems that iteratively collect preference data, improve the policy, collect new data, and repeat — such as Anthropic's Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) or Google's ReST (Gulcehre et al., 2023). In these pipelines, standard RLHF requires retraining the reward model at each iteration because the data distribution shifts (Theorem 2). NLHF offers the alternative: train a preference model once (or incrementally update it with new data), and use Nash-MD-PG with `$\beta \approx 0.125$` for each policy optimization round. The paper does not demonstrate this multi-round scenario, but the distributional insensitivity argument (Section 3.3) directly implies it should work. A team building an iterative alignment system today could: (1) train a T5X-XL-scale preference model on initial human preference data; (2) run Nash-MD-PG to produce an improved policy; (3) deploy the policy, collect new human preference comparisons between its outputs and alternatives; (4) fine-tune the preference model on the new data (no need to retrain from scratch); (5) run Nash-MD-PG again with the updated preference model. The cost saving from not retraining the preference model from scratch at each iteration could be substantial at scale — training an 11B-parameter model is expensive — and the paper's `$\beta = 0.125$` finding provides a concrete starting point for the opponent mixture hyperparameter.

**On-device or smaller-model deployment with preference-based alignment.** The paper demonstrates that a T5X-L policy (smaller than the T5X-XL preference model) can be optimized via NLHF to outperform an RLHF-trained policy evaluated by PaLM 2 Large. This suggests a deployment pattern where a large model serves as the preference oracle during training, but the deployed policy is a smaller, faster model fine-tuned via NLHF. The MD1 model achieves ~60% win rate against the RLHF baseline under PaLM 2 evaluation (Table 3) while being the same T5X-L size. For applications where inference latency or memory constraints preclude using the largest models — mobile devices, real-time summarization APIs, embedded assistants — NLHF offers a way to extract more alignment performance from a given model size by investing extra training-time compute (the self-play loop) rather than deploying a larger model. The paper's 10,000 training steps with pairwise generation and preference queries represent a fixed training budget that produces a model competitive with or exceeding an RLHF baseline, without requiring the larger model at inference time.

**Preference model reuse across tasks or model versions.** The paper's argument that preference models are distributionally insensitive (Section 3.3) has a practical corollary: a preference model trained on one policy's outputs may remain well-calibrated for evaluating other policies' outputs, and potentially for related tasks. A company that maintains multiple summarization models (for different domains, languages, or user populations) could train a single preference model on a diverse preference dataset and use it as the evaluator for NLHF fine-tuning across all variants, rather than training separate reward models for each. This is cost-efficient because the preference model is the larger model (T5X-XL, 11B parameters in the paper) while the policies being fine-tuned are smaller (T5X-L). The paper doesn't test cross-task transfer, but the theoretical insensitivity to the sampling distribution makes this more plausible for preference models than for reward models. A team could validate this by training a preference model on general summarization preferences and testing whether it remains calibrated when evaluating medical summarization or legal summarization outputs — domains where the output distribution differs but the underlying notion of "good summary" shares structure.

**Game-theoretic red-teaming and safety evaluation.** The Nash equilibrium framing naturally connects to adversarial robustness: a policy at the Nash equilibrium is, by definition, unexploitable — no alternative policy can consistently produce outputs preferred over it. This makes NLHF-trained policies natural candidates for safety-critical applications where adversarial prompting or edge-case exploitation is a concern. A red team tasked with finding inputs where the model produces harmful or dispreferred outputs is effectively playing the role of the Nash equilibrium's opponent. An NLHF-trained model that genuinely approaches the Nash equilibrium should be more robust to such red-teaming than an RLHF-trained model (which was optimized against a fixed reward, not an adaptive adversary). A practical safety evaluation pipeline could: (1) train a model with NLHF; (2) explicitly train adversarial policies or prompts designed to make the model produce low-preference outputs; (3) measure whether the NLHF model's win rate stays above 50% (unexploitable) or drops significantly (exploitable). The paper does not run this experiment, but the framework directly suggests it, and the concept of measuring exploitability — absent from standard RLHF evaluation — is a concrete operationalization of robustness that safety teams could adopt today using the preference model as judge.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not present a systematic trade-off analysis or decision rule comparing NLHF against specific named alternatives like DPO, IPO, or standard RLHF under different conditions. It positions NLHF as a general alternative to RLHF, with conceptual advantages (non-transitivity tolerance, distributional insensitivity, preference diversity alignment) that it argues apply broadly, and demonstrates empirical superiority in one specific setting (TL;DR summarization, T5X models, PaLM 2 evaluation). The paper does not characterize when a practitioner should expect NLHF to outperform RLHF versus when RLHF is sufficient, nor does it compare against offline preference optimization methods. Introducing a decision matrix here — e.g., "prefer NLHF when preferences are diverse, prefer RLHF when they are homogeneous" — would be extrapolation beyond what the paper establishes, since no experiment varies preference diversity and measures the performance gap. The paper's contribution is to introduce and validate the NLHF framework as a proof of concept; characterizing the precise conditions under which it dominates alternatives is left entirely to future work.
