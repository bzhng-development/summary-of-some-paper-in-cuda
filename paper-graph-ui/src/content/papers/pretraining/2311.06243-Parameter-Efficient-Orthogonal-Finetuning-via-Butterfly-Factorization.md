# Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Butterfly Factorization

**ArXiv:** [2311.06243](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.06243)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**, a parameter-efficient finetuning method that composes a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices structured according to butterfly factorization — inspired by the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm — to adapt large foundation models while preserving pairwise neuron angles.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**, a parameter-efficient finetuning method that composes a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices structured according to butterfly factorization — inspired by the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm — to adapt large foundation models while preserving pairwise neuron angles. Evaluated across large language models (DeBERTaV3, Llama-2 on GLUE, MMLU, GSM8K, MATH), vision models (DINOv2 on VTAB-1K, SAM on high-quality segmentation), and text-to-image diffusion models (Stable Diffusion for controllable and subject-driven generation), BOFT consistently outperforms LoRA and the original block-diagonal Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) while using fewer trainable parameters — reducing OFT's O(bd) parameterization to O(d log d) for dense orthogonal matrices — and achieving, for example, a landmark error of 5.667 versus OFT's 6.407 and LoRA's 8.038 in controllable face generation at comparable parameter budgets (~20M). The butterfly structure introduces an implicit inductive bias that improves generalization, establishing that structured sparsity within the orthogonal group can simultaneously enhance parameter efficiency and downstream performance across vision and language domains.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: How Do We Preserve Pretraining Structure While Finetuning Efficiently?

The central tension this paper addresses is fundamental to modern deep learning practice: foundation models are becoming too large to retrain from scratch for every downstream task, yet naïvely finetuning all parameters often performs worse than more constrained approaches. This creates a paradox — if full finetuning is too expensive and sometimes harmful, how do we design adaptation methods that are simultaneously **parameter-efficient** (few trainable parameters), **expressive** (capable of adapting to diverse tasks), and **regularized** (preserving valuable pretraining knowledge)?

This tension is not merely about saving GPU memory. As the paper notes in Section 1, model finetuning methods "introduce no inference latency" — unlike adapter or prompt tuning approaches that add computational overhead at deployment time. For production systems serving millions of queries, inference latency is a hard constraint. A method that achieves high accuracy with zero inference overhead and minimal trainable parameters is therefore both economically and practically significant.

But the real difficulty lies deeper than parameter counts. The paper identifies a structural challenge that most prior work sidesteps: how to update a pretrained weight matrix while **provably preserving the relational information encoded in that matrix**. This is not a new question — it connects to a long line of work on hyperspherical energy and neural similarity (Liu et al., 2018, 2019) that argues pairwise angles between neuron vectors capture semantic relationships learned during pretraining. Disturbing these angles arbitrarily during finetuning risks destroying the very knowledge that makes pretrained models valuable.

The paper's framing of this problem is worth quoting directly (Section 1):

> "The fundamental principle behind model finetuning is to ensure that the pretrained model and the finetuned model are similar based on certain measurements, such that the pretraining knowledge is preserved."

This is a more nuanced position than "don't change the weights too much." It specifies a particular geometric measurement — pairwise angles — and demands the finetuning transformation respect that structure exactly, not approximately. The paper's contribution is to ask: **given this geometric constraint, how parameter-efficient can we make the transformation?**

### The Gap: OFT Gets the Principle Right but the Parameterization Wrong

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) (Qiu et al., 2023) established the core insight: multiply the frozen pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ by a learned orthogonal matrix $R$ so that the forward pass becomes $z = (R \cdot W^0)^\top x$. Because orthogonal transformations preserve inner products and therefore pairwise angles, this guarantees — not just encourages — that the geometric relationships among neurons remain intact. There is no gradient signal pushing neurons closer together or further apart; the only degrees of freedom are collective rotations of all neurons in a layer.

OFT showed compelling results on controllable text-to-image generation, demonstrating both stronger generalization and more stable training than LoRA-style low-rank updates. The problem, however, was immediately obvious: a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix requires $O(d^2)$ parameters. For a typical transformer layer with $d = 1024$ or $d = 4096$, this is millions of parameters per layer — often exceeding the total parameter budget of competing methods like LoRA by large margins.

The original OFT paper introduced a **block-diagonal structure** to address this: instead of learning one full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, parameterize $R$ as a block-diagonal matrix $\text{diag}(R_1, R_2, \ldots, R_r)$ where each $R_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is a small orthogonal matrix and $br = d$. This reduces parameters from $O(d^2)$ to $O(bd)$ — linear in $d$ rather than quadratic, with the block size $b$ controlling the parameter-efficiency tradeoff. When $b = d$ ($r=1$), you recover full OFT; when $b = 2$ ($r = d/2$), you get extreme efficiency at the cost of expressiveness.

But this solution creates its own problems, and the paper is explicit about them (Section 1, Section 3):

**First**, the block-diagonal structure imposes a fixed, arbitrary sparsity pattern. Dimensions within the same block can interact through the orthogonal transformation; dimensions in different blocks cannot interact at all. The paper states this bluntly:

> "it makes no sense to divide the dimensions of a neuron into $r$ groups based on their indices"

Why should the 1st and 2nd dimensions of a neuron vector be allowed to rotate together, but not the 2nd and 3rd (if they happen to fall in different blocks)? The block boundaries are determined by index ordering, which is an artifact of the network architecture, not a semantic property of the features. This introduces an **undesirable inductive bias** — a structural assumption about which dimensions should interact that has no theoretical justification.

**Second**, the block-diagonal structure cannot represent certain important linear transformations. The paper notes that block-diagonal orthogonal matrices "cannot approximate classic linear transforms" such as the discrete Fourier transform, discrete cosine transform, or Hadamard transform. These transforms are dense — every output dimension depends on every input dimension — and no block-diagonal matrix (with block size less than $d$) can represent them. From a hypothesis class perspective, block-diagonal OFT restricts the search space to a subset of the orthogonal group that excludes many natural transformations, potentially limiting what the model can learn.

**Third**, and perhaps most subtly, block-diagonal OFT introduces a **hard tradeoff between expressivity and regularity** that is difficult to tune. Full OFT ($b=d$) is maximally expressive but parameter-inefficient. Highly block-diagonal OFT ($b=2$) is parameter-efficient but severely restricted. There is no smooth interpolation between these extremes — you pick a block size and live with its consequences. The paper's insight is that this tradeoff can be made **gradient** rather than **discrete** by using a different structural assumption.

### The Missing Piece: Dense Orthogonal Matrices from Sparse Factors

The paper identifies a specific technical gap that no prior work addressed: **can we construct a dense orthogonal matrix without losing parameter efficiency?** (Section 3). This is the question that motivates the entire BOFT method.

To see why this is challenging, consider the constraints. We want a matrix $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that is:
- **Orthogonal**: $R^\top R = I_d$, so it preserves angles and norms
- **Dense**: every element is potentially non-zero, avoiding arbitrary block structures
- **Parameter-efficient**: requiring far fewer than $O(d^2)$ trainable parameters
- **Initializable to identity**: so finetuning starts from the pretrained model exactly

A single dense orthogonal matrix fails condition 3. A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix fails condition 2. The paper's key insight is that conditions 2 and 3 can be satisfied simultaneously if we **compose** the dense matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices: $R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1$, where each $B_i$ is structurally sparse and orthogonal. This trades computation time (more matrix multiplications) for parameter space (fewer trainable entries), and critically, it means the **structure** of sparsity across factors determines the expressivity of the product.

The paper formalizes this tradeoff through a novel **information transmission framework** (Section 3) that I'll detail extensively in the Technical Approach section. The core idea is that the product $B_m \cdots B_1$ can be viewed as transmitting information through a grid-structured graph where each $B_i$ defines connectivity between adjacent levels, and the goal is to achieve **dense connectivity** (every input can reach every output) with **minimum edges** (fewest trainable parameters). This reframes the algebraic problem of matrix factorization as a combinatorial problem of graph design, which is where the butterfly structure enters.

### Why LoRA — the Dominant Approach — Is Not the Answer

To understand why BOFT matters, we need to contrast it with the method it competes against most directly: **Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)** (Hu et al., 2022).

LoRA reparameterizes weight updates additively: instead of learning a full update matrix $\Delta W$, it learns a low-rank factorization $\Delta W = AB$ where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ with $r \ll \min(d, n)$. The forward pass becomes $z = (W^0 + AB)^\top x$. This is elegant, parameter-efficient (only $r(d+n)$ parameters per layer), and has proven remarkably effective across NLP tasks.

But LoRA has limitations that become apparent when compared to orthogonal methods, and the paper implicitly critiques these throughout:

**No structural preservation guarantee.** LoRA makes no attempt to preserve pairwise angles between neurons. The low-rank update can arbitrarily change the geometry of the weight matrix, potentially destroying relational information that was carefully encoded during pretraining. This is not a bug in LoRA per se — it works well in practice — but it means LoRA relies entirely on the small magnitude of its updates (controlled by learning rate and rank) to avoid catastrophic forgetting, rather than providing a structural guarantee.

**Empirical evidence of weaker generalization.** The paper's experiments consistently show that orthogonal methods (both OFT and BOFT) outperform LoRA at comparable parameter budgets across diverse tasks (Tables 1-6). The performance gaps are not enormous — often 1-2 percentage points — but they are consistent and, as the paper notes, "have passed significant tests with p < 0.05." More importantly, the spectral norm of the weight matrix is perfectly preserved under orthogonal transformations (Section 5, "Spectral properties"), which has been shown to benefit training stability and generalization (Miyato et al., 2018; Yoshida and Miyato, 2017). LoRA's additive updates provide no such guarantee.

**Difficulty controlling the expressivity-regularity tradeoff.** LoRA's only knob is the rank $r$. There is no way to smoothly interpolate between a highly constrained update (small $r$) and a more expressive one (large $r$) without changing the fundamental nature of the parameterization. BOFT, by contrast, can vary both the block size $b$ and the number of butterfly components $m$ to explore a richer space of structured orthogonal matrices, which the paper shows enables finding better tradeoffs.

### The Prior Work Landscape: Where BOFT Fits

The paper positions itself at the intersection of three research threads:

**1. Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT).** The dominant approaches are reparameterization-based (LoRA, OFT, and their variants), adapter-based (adding small trainable modules between layers), and prompt-based (learning continuous prefix tokens). Among these, reparameterization methods have gained popularity because they introduce zero inference latency — the learned parameters can be absorbed back into the pretrained weights after training, unlike adapters or prompts which require additional computation at every forward pass. This is a critical practical advantage that the paper emphasizes repeatedly.

Within reparameterization methods, LoRA's low-rank structure and OFT's block-diagonal structure represent the two main paradigms. The paper explicitly contrasts them in Figure 1: LoRA adds a low-rank matrix additively; OFT multiplies by an orthogonal matrix. BOFT proposes a third paradigm: multiplicative orthogonal transformation via sparse matrix factorization, combining the principled regularization of orthogonality with the parameter efficiency typically associated with low-rank methods.

**2. Butterfly structures in machine learning.** The Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm is one of the most famous algorithms in computer science, and its induced butterfly graph has been used in multiple ML contexts: parameterizing orthogonal matrices for numerical stability (Parker, 1995), stabilizing recurrent neural network training (Jing et al., 2017), learning fast linear transforms (Dao et al., 2019, 2020), and efficient sparse training (Chen et al., 2022; Dao et al., 2022). The paper draws on this lineage but applies butterfly factorization to a fundamentally different problem: not training networks from scratch with butterfly-parameterized weights, but using butterfly-structured orthogonal matrices to **transform** pretrained weights during finetuning. This is a novel application that the prior butterfly literature did not explore.

**3. Orthogonal and geometric approaches to neural network training.** The motivation for preserving pairwise angles traces to work on hyperspherical energy (Liu et al., 2018) and orthogonal over-parameterized training (Liu et al., 2021), which showed that orthogonal constraints on weight matrices improve generalization by regularizing the geometry of the learned representations. OFT (Qiu et al., 2023) was the first to apply this insight to parameter-efficient finetuning, but its adoption was limited by the parameter inefficiency issue that BOFT directly addresses.

### How BOFT Positions Itself

The paper's positioning can be summarized in a single sentence: **BOFT is to OFT what sparse factorizations are to dense matrices — a way to achieve the same structural guarantees with far fewer parameters by exploiting a composable, structured sparsity pattern inspired by the butterfly graph.**

More concretely, the paper makes several explicit positioning moves:

- It frames OFT as a special case of BOFT: when the number of butterfly components $m=1$, BOFT reduces exactly to block-diagonal OFT (Section 4). This is not merely a mathematical note — it means BOFT subsumes OFT's expressivity and adds the ability to interpolate toward dense orthogonal matrices as $m$ increases. The paper uses the notation BOFT(m,b) throughout to emphasize this continuity: BOFT(1,b) is OFT with block size b; BOFT(log₂ d, 2) is a fully dense orthogonal butterfly matrix.

- It claims the butterfly structure introduces a **beneficial inductive bias** that improves generalization beyond what pure parameter efficiency would predict. The argument (Section 5, "Inductive bias and generalization in BOFT") is that butterfly matrices can exactly represent many classic linear transforms (FFT, DCT, Hadamard) that are fundamental to signal processing, and that this structural capacity biases the finetuning toward learning transformations with similar properties — global, multi-scale information mixing — which happen to be useful for adaptation tasks.

- It explicitly positions BOFT as a **general-purpose** finetuning method, not just a replacement for OFT in diffusion models. The paper states this ambitiously:

> "For the very first time, we apply orthogonal finetuning to various tasks beyond controllable text-to-image generation, showing its great potential as a generic model finetuning method."

This is significant because the original OFT paper was narrowly focused on diffusion model control. By demonstrating strong results across GLUE (NLU), MMLU (knowledge), GSM8K/MATH (reasoning), VTAB-1K (visual transfer), SAM (segmentation), and Stable Diffusion (generation), the paper makes the case that orthogonal finetuning — and BOFT specifically — deserves consideration alongside LoRA as a default PEFT method.

The paper's ultimate framing of its contribution is as a solution to a **structural tradeoff**: the block-diagonal structure in OFT trades expressivity for regularity on a discrete spectrum (fewer blocks = more expressivity but more parameters), whereas the butterfly structure provides a **continuous interpolation** between identity matrices (maximum regularity, minimum expressivity) and the full orthogonal group (maximum expressivity, minimum regularity), enabling finer-grained control over the inductive bias. This is encapsulated in a key sentence from Section 5:

> "BOFT uses the butterfly structure to construct a smoother interpolation between matrices from the full orthogonal group (expressivity) and identity matrices (regularity). This enables us to find a smaller hypothesis class within the orthogonal group for downstream tasks."

### Summary of the Motivation Chain

The paper attacks a specific, well-defined technical gap: **OFT provides principled geometric regularization but is parameter-inefficient for dense orthogonal matrices; block-diagonal approximations fix the parameter count but introduce arbitrary structural assumptions**. The solution — factorizing dense orthogonal matrices into products of sparse orthogonal factors using butterfly topology — is motivated by an elegant connection to information transmission on graphs and draws on established properties of the Cooley-Tukey FFT. The broader significance is that this enables orthogonal finetuning to compete with (and consistently outperform) LoRA across a wide range of tasks, establishing it as a viable general-purpose PEFT method rather than a niche technique for diffusion models.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

BOFT is a method for adapting a large pretrained neural network to a new task by inserting a small number of trainable parameters that multiply the frozen pretrained weights — specifically, a composite orthogonal matrix built from many tiny, structured, sparse orthogonal factors — so that the model can learn task-specific transformations while mathematically guaranteeing that the pairwise geometric relationships (angles) between all neurons are preserved.

The problem it solves is that existing orthogonal finetuning (OFT) requires either too many parameters (for a dense orthogonal matrix, $O(d^2)$ per layer) or imposes arbitrary structural restrictions (block-diagonal matrices that forbid cross-block interactions), and BOFT resolves this by factorising a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of $O(\log d)$ sparse orthogonal matrices, each with only $O(d)$ parameters, yielding total $O(d \log d)$ parameters per layer while retaining dense connectivity and the full angle-preservation guarantee. The "shape" of the solution is: take a pretrained weight matrix $W^0$, represent it as frozen, learn an orthogonal matrix $R = \tilde{B}^{(d, 2^m)} \cdot \tilde{B}^{(d, 2^{m-1})} \cdots \tilde{B}^{(d, 2)}$ where each $\tilde{B}^{(d, k)}$ is a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with block size $2b$, multiply them to form the dense orthogonal matrix, and use $R \cdot W^0$ as the adapted weight matrix during the forward pass — at inference time, multiply $R$ into $W^0$ once to eliminate all overhead.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The BOFT system has four major components that interact in a specific pipeline:

**1. Frozen Pretrained Weights ($W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$).** For each linear layer being adapted, the original pretrained weight matrix is kept frozen — it receives no gradient updates. All task-specific learning happens through the multiplicative orthogonal matrix $R$.

**2. Orthogonal Butterfly Matrix ($R(m, b) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$).** This is the learnable component. It is a product of $m$ sparse block-diagonal orthogonal matrices called *butterfly components*, each of which has a block size of $2b$ and is parameterised using Cayley transform to guarantee orthogonality. The product of these sparse matrices yields a dense matrix (when $m$ is large enough), so every input dimension can influence every output dimension. The number of trainable parameters is $\frac{1}{2} (b-1) d m$ per layer — far less than the $O(d^2)$ of a full orthogonal matrix. At initialisation, all butterfly components are identity matrices, so the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained model.

**3. Cayley Parameterisation (for enforcing orthogonality).** Each $2b \times 2b$ diagonal block within every butterfly component must remain orthogonal throughout training. This is enforced by representing the block not directly as an orthogonal matrix but through a skew-symmetric matrix $Q$ via the Cayley transform: the block equals $(I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$, where $Q = -Q^\top$. The entries of $Q$ (above the diagonal) are the actual trainable parameters, and the Cayley transform mathematically guarantees the resulting matrix is orthogonal for any real skew-symmetric $Q$. This eliminates the need for orthogonalisation projections or penalty terms during training.

**4. Trained Model with Absorbed Weights (inference stage).** After finetuning is complete, the learned $R$ is multiplied into the frozen $W^0$ to produce a new weight matrix $W^{\text{finetuned}} = R \cdot W^0$. This multiplication is done once, offline, so at deployment time the model has exactly the same architecture and inference cost as the original pretrained model — there are no additional matrix multiplications, no adapter modules, no extra latency. Only the weight values have changed.

**Information flow:** Input $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ → Forward pass computes $z = (R \cdot W^0)^\top x$ → During training, $R$ receives gradients and its Cayley-parameterised sub-blocks are updated → $R$'s constituent butterfly components remain orthogonal at every step → After training, $R$ is absorbed: $W^{\text{finetuned}} \leftarrow R \cdot W^0$ → Inference uses $z = (W^{\text{finetuned}})^\top x$.

The critical design decision is that $R$ is layer-specific (each adapted layer gets its own orthogonal matrix) but the butterfly factorisation structure and the orthogonality constraint are uniform across layers — what varies across layers are the specific values learned within the Cayley-parameterised blocks.

- **First**, the **information transmission framework** (Section 3 of the paper), which reframes the algebraic problem of factorising a dense matrix into sparse factors as a graph connectivity problem. This is the conceptual foundation — understanding it makes the butterfly structure's role natural rather than arbitrary.
- **Second**, the **butterfly matrix definition and construction** (Section 4 of the paper), starting from the basic scalar butterfly factor and building up recursively to the full block-butterfly parameterisation used in BOFT. #### The Information Transmission Framework: From Algebra to Graph Connectivity

The paper does not simply assert that butterfly factorisation works; it derives the structure from first principles by reframing matrix factorisation as a problem of designing a communication network. This framework (Section 3) is the intellectual engine of the paper, and understanding it is essential to seeing why the butterfly topology is the right choice rather than an arbitrary one.

**The problem statement.** We want to represent a dense matrix $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ as a product of $m$ square matrices:

$$R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1$$

where each $B_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is structurally sparse and orthogonal, and the total number of non-zero entries across all $B_i$ is much less than $d^2$.

**The graph interpretation.** The paper proposes to view each matrix $B_i$ as an adjacency matrix defining connections between two columns of nodes in a grid. Specifically, imagine a grid of $d$ rows and $(m+1)$ columns of nodes. The $j$-th column corresponds to the "state" after multiplying by $B_1$ through $B_{j-1}$. The matrix $B_i$ defines directed edges from nodes in column $i$ to nodes in column $i+1$: the element $(B_i)_{p,q}$ is non-zero if and only if there is a directed edge from node $q$ in column $i$ to node $p$ in column $i+1$. The value of $(B_i)_{p,q}$ is the weight of that edge.

Under this interpretation, the product $B_m \cdots B_1$ computes the total connectivity from column 1 (the input nodes) to column $m+1$ (the output nodes): node $q$ in column 1 can transmit information to node $p$ in column $m+1$ if there exists at least one directed path from $q$ to $p$ through the grid, and the weight of that transmission is the sum over all paths of the product of edge weights along each path. The entry $R_{p,q}$ is non-zero precisely when such a path exists.

This reframing converts the algebraic problem — "find sparse matrices whose product is dense" — into a combinatorial one: **design a directed graph on a $d \times (m+1)$ grid such that every source node in the first column has at least one path to every target node in the last column, while using as few total edges as possible.**

**The orthogonality constraint as an edge constraint.** Each $B_i$ must be orthogonal. For a matrix to be orthogonal, it must be full-rank, which means its graph must contain a perfect matching — a bijection — between the nodes of column $i$ and column $i+1$. This requires at least $d$ edges per adjacent column pair. These $d$ edges are "non-trainable" in a specific sense: if a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix has exactly $d$ non-zero entries, those entries can only be $\pm 1$ (permutation matrices with possible sign flips). To have learnable parameters with continuous values, each $B_i$ needs more than $d$ edges.

Moreover, orthogonality imposes dependencies among edges that do not exist for general matrices. For a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrix, a zero at position $(1,1)$ forces a zero at $(2,2)$ (the inverse of an orthogonal matrix is its transpose, and a missing row forces a missing column). This means the edge count is not a free variable independent of the sparsity pattern — some patterns that seem feasible for general matrices become infeasible under orthogonality.

**The design desiderata.** From this framework, the paper extracts two requirements for an efficient sparse orthogonal factorisation:

1. **Dense connectivity:** every input node reaches every output node through the directed graph (so $R$ can be dense).
2. **Minimal free edges:** the number of edges beyond the $d$ required per $B_i$ for the perfect matching should be minimised, since these extra edges correspond to trainable parameters.

A naive solution — using a single $B_1$ that is dense — requires $d^2$ edges (all possible connections), yielding $d^2 - d$ trainable parameters. This is OFT with full orthogonal matrices. The block-diagonal solution from original OFT corresponds to partitioning the $d$ nodes into $r$ groups and only allowing edges within each group. This reduces edges but violates dense connectivity across groups — nodes in group 1 can never reach nodes in group 2.

The question becomes: **what is the sparsest graph that achieves dense connectivity?**

**The butterfly answer.** The butterfly graph, central to the Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm, achieves dense connectivity between $d$ source nodes and $d$ target nodes using only $O(d \log d)$ edges. It does this through a recursive "divide and conquer" topology: at each level, nodes are paired and exchange information; across levels, the pairing pattern shifts by factors of 2, so that after $\log_2 d$ levels, every node has had the opportunity to mix with every other node. This is the topology the paper adopts for the butterfly components in BOFT.

The critical difference from block-diagonal OFT is that the butterfly graph does not partition nodes into fixed groups that never interact. Instead, nodes interact with different partners at different levels — a node that only communicates within a small block at level 1 communicates with nodes from other blocks at level 2, and so on, until after $\log_2 d$ levels the connectivity is fully dense.

Figure 2 in the paper illustrates this for $d=4$ with 5 levels, showing how repeated local exchanges (each $B_i$ only connects each node to at most two others) produce global connectivity after composition. The paper explicitly contrasts this with Figure 3, which shows the block-diagonal structure's failure to connect across blocks.

**Why information transmission matters for finetuning.** The framework is not a mathematical curiosity — it provides a language for reasoning about the qualitative difference between rotation parameterisations. In a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix, a neuron dimension can only "talk to" other dimensions in its block; there is a hard information boundary. In a butterfly-structured orthogonal matrix, information from any dimension can percolate to any other through multiple stages of local mixing. This means BOFT can learn transformations that require global coordination (like the discrete Fourier transform, which mixes every input frequency with every output time sample), while block-diagonal OFT fundamentally cannot. The paper argues that this capacity for global information mixing is a beneficial inductive bias for finetuning, since downstream task adaptation often requires the model to reconfigure how it combines features from different parts of the representation.

#### Butterfly Matrix Definition and Recursive Construction

The butterfly structure used in BOFT is defined recursively, building from small $2 \times 2$ blocks up to the full $d \times d$ matrix (Section 4). The construction has two levels: the basic *butterfly factor* and the *butterfly component*, which are then composed into the full butterfly matrix.

**Step 1: The butterfly factor.** For an even integer $k \geq 2$ (a power of 2 in practice), a *butterfly factor* $\text{BF}(k) \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}$ is defined as:

$$\text{BF}(k) = \begin{pmatrix} \text{diag}(d_1) & \text{diag}(d_2) \\ \text{diag}(d_3) & \text{diag}(d_4) \end{pmatrix}$$

where each $d_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ is a vector, and $\text{diag}(d_i)$ denotes the $k/2 \times k/2$ diagonal matrix with the elements of $d_i$ on its diagonal.

In plain language: a butterfly factor of size $k$ is a $k \times k$ matrix partitioned into four $k/2 \times k/2$ quadrants, and each quadrant is a diagonal matrix. The only non-zero entries are on the diagonals of these four quadrants — this gives exactly $4 \cdot (k/2) = 2k$ non-zero entries out of $k^2$ possible entries. For $k=4$, the pattern is:

$$\text{BF}(4) = \begin{pmatrix} a & 0 & b & 0 \\ 0 & c & 0 & d \\ e & 0 & f & 0 \\ 0 & g & 0 & h \end{pmatrix}$$

where $\{a,b,c,d,e,f,g,h\}$ are the entries of the four diagonal vectors.

The crucial property is that this matrix connects the first $k/2$ input dimensions to both the first $k/2$ output dimensions (through the top-left and bottom-left quadrants) and the second $k/2$ output dimensions (through the bottom-left and top-right quadrants, in a cross pattern). This is what enables information to "cross" between the two halves of the dimension space — a node in the first half can communicate with nodes in the second half through the off-diagonal quadrants.

**Step 2: The butterfly component.** The $d$-dimensional butterfly component $\tilde{B}(d, k)$ is a block-diagonal matrix of size $d \times d$ that concatenates $d/k$ independent butterfly factors of size $k$:

$$\tilde{B}(d, k) = \text{diag}\left(\text{BF}_1(k), \text{BF}_2(k), \ldots, \text{BF}_{d/k}(k)\right)$$

where each $\text{BF}_i(k)$ is a separate butterfly factor with its own learnable parameters.

So $\tilde{B}(d, k)$ applies a butterfly factor independently to each non-overlapping block of $k$ dimensions. For $d=8$ and $k=2$, this means four independent $2 \times 2$ blocks along the diagonal, each with its own set of 4 parameters (2 per diagonal vector). For $d=8$ and $k=4$, this means two independent $4 \times 4$ blocks, each with $2 \cdot 4 = 8$ non-zero entries. For $d=8$ and $k=8$, this is a single $8 \times 8$ matrix with 16 non-zero entries. The sparsity increases (fewer non-zero entries per total elements) as $k$ decreases.

**Step 3: The recursive butterfly matrix.** The full $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix $B(d) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ (with $d = 2^N$) is defined recursively as:

$$B(d) = \tilde{B}(d, d) \cdot \begin{pmatrix} B_1\left(\frac{d}{2}\right) & 0 \\ 0 & B_2\left(\frac{d}{2}\right) \end{pmatrix}$$

where $B_1(d/2)$ and $B_2(d/2)$ are two independent $d/2$-dimensional butterfly matrices, and $\tilde{B}(d, d)$ is the largest butterfly component (block size $d$, meaning it is a single butterfly factor of size $d$ applied to all $d$ dimensions at once).

Unrolling the recursion fully gives:

$$B(d) = \tilde{B}(d, d) \cdot \tilde{B}(d, d/2) \cdot \tilde{B}(d, d/4) \cdots \tilde{B}(d, 2)$$

where $\tilde{B}(d, d/2)$ is a block-diagonal matrix with two blocks of size $d/2$ (each being a $d/2 \times d/2$ butterfly factor), $\tilde{B}(d, d/4)$ has four blocks of size $d/4$, and so on, down to $\tilde{B}(d, 2)$ which has $d/2$ blocks of size 2.

For $d = 8$, the full butterfly matrix is:

$$B(8) = \tilde{B}(8, 8) \cdot \tilde{B}(8, 4) \cdot \tilde{B}(8, 2)$$

where $\tilde{B}(8, 8)$ is a single $8 \times 8$ butterfly factor (16 non-zero entries, connecting the 4 halves in a cross pattern), $\tilde{B}(8, 4)$ is block-diagonal with two $4 \times 4$ butterfly factors (8 non-zero entries each, operating within each half independently), and $\tilde{B}(8, 2)$ is block-diagonal with four $2 \times 2$ butterfly factors (4 non-zero entries each, operating within each quarter independently).

Figure 4 in the main paper illustrates this for $d=8$, showing how the three butterfly components compose to produce a dense $8 \times 8$ matrix from sparse factors.

**Step 4: The block butterfly generalisation for BOFT.** The paper generalises the butterfly matrix by replacing scalar entries with $b \times b$ block matrices. Specifically, each entry in each diagonal vector $d_i$ of a butterfly factor becomes a $b \times b$ matrix rather than a scalar. This yields the *block butterfly component* $\tilde{B}_b(d, k)$, where each of the $2k$ non-zero entries per $k \times k$ butterfly factor is now a $b \times b$ dense block.

Concretely, for $b=2$, a $k=4$ block butterfly factor is a $4 \times 4$ matrix where each non-zero entry is a $2 \times 2$ matrix, yielding an $8 \times 8$ matrix (since the total dimension becomes $k \cdot b = 8$) with $2k \cdot b^2 = 8 \cdot 4 = 32$ non-zero scalar entries out of $64$ total entries. For block size $b$, the butterfly factor $\text{BF}_b(k)$ is a $(k \cdot b) \times (k \cdot b)$ matrix.

The full orthogonal matrix in BOFT with $m$ butterfly components and block size $b$ is:

$$R(m, b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{B}_b(d, 2^{m-i+1})$$

where the product goes from right to left (the smallest block size multiplies first), and for notational simplicity the paper denotes $\tilde{B}_b(d, 2^{m-i+1})$ as $\tilde{B}_b^{(d, i)}$.

For this to be an orthogonal matrix, each block butterfly component $\tilde{B}_b^{(d, i)}$ must itself be orthogonal. The paper achieves this by ensuring each $2b \times 2b$ block within each butterfly component is orthogonal — since the butterfly component is block-diagonal (after appropriate permutation of rows and columns), the whole component is orthogonal if and only if each constituent block is orthogonal. This is where the Cayley parameterisation enters.

**Why this product is dense.** A $2 \times 2$ butterfly factor (the smallest building block) connects two input dimensions to two output dimensions in a cross pattern. At the first level ($\tilde{B}(d, 2)$), information mixes only within pairs of adjacent dimensions. At the second level ($\tilde{B}(d, 4)$), dimensions within groups of 4 mix — a dimension from pair $\{1,2\}$ can now influence dimensions in pair $\{3,4\}$ because the $4 \times 4$ butterfly factor connects the first pair to the second through its cross-diagonal quadrants. At the third level, groups of 8 mix, and so on. After $\log_2 d$ levels, every dimension has had the opportunity to exchange information with every other dimension through a sequence of local pairings at different scales. This is the same inductive principle as the FFT: repeated local butterflies produce a global transformation.

**Why use block size $b > 1$?** The scalar butterfly ($b=1$) is maximally sparse and parameter-efficient, but also maximally constrained — each butterfly component only has $2d$ non-zero entries out of $d^2$ possible entries. By using block size $b > 1$, each "entry" in the butterfly structure becomes a dense $b \times b$ block, increasing expressivity at the cost of additional parameters. The block size $b$ acts as a smooth expressivity knob: small $b$ gives high sparsity and strong regularisation; large $b$ gives more expressive per-block transformations. This interacts with the number of components $m$ to create a two-dimensional hyperparameter space controlling the expressivity-efficiency tradeoff.

#### Cayley Parameterisation for Orthogonality Enforcement

The fundamental requirement of BOFT is that every butterfly component $\tilde{B}_b^{(d, i)}$ is orthogonal, so their product $R(m, b)$ is orthogonal. The paper enforces this not through a penalty term or post-hoc projection, but through a **parameterisation that produces orthogonal matrices by construction** — specifically, the Cayley transform.

**The Cayley transform.** For any real skew-symmetric matrix $Q$ (i.e., $Q = -Q^\top$), the matrix defined by:

$$R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$$

is guaranteed to be orthogonal, i.e., $R^\top R = I$. This is because:

$$R^\top = ((I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1})^\top = (I - Q)^{-\top} (I + Q)^\top = (I + Q)^{-1} (I - Q)$$

and therefore $R^\top R = (I + Q)^{-1} (I - Q)(I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$. Since $(I - Q)$ and $(I + Q)$ commute (both are polynomials in $Q$), this simplifies to $R^\top R = I$.

This transform is invertible for any real skew-symmetric $Q$, so the mapping from $Q$ to $R$ is well-defined on the entire space of skew-symmetric matrices. The inverse mapping (from a given orthogonal matrix $R$ back to $Q$) is $Q = (R - I)(R + I)^{-1}$, valid whenever $R + I$ is invertible (which is true for all orthogonal matrices that do not have $-1$ as an eigenvalue). This means the Cayley parameterisation covers almost all orthogonal matrices — only those with eigenvalue $-1$ (reflections) are excluded.

**Application to BOFT blocks.** Each butterfly component $\tilde{B}_b^{(d, i)}$ is, after appropriate row/column permutation, a block-diagonal matrix consisting of $d/(2b)$ blocks of size $2b \times 2b$. For each such block, the paper parameterises a skew-symmetric matrix $Q_{\text{block}} \in \mathbb{R}^{2b \times 2b}$ (with $Q_{\text{block}} = -Q_{\text{block}}^\top$), and sets the block to:

$$R_{\text{block}} = (I_{2b} + Q_{\text{block}})(I_{2b} - Q_{\text{block}})^{-1}$$

The trainable parameters are the entries of $Q_{\text{block}}$ above the diagonal — a skew-symmetric matrix of size $2b \times 2b$ has $2b(2b-1)/2$ independent entries (the diagonal is constrained to zero, and the lower triangle is the negative of the upper triangle).

**Why Cayley over alternatives?** There are other ways to parameterise orthogonal matrices — the exponential map ($R = \exp(Q)$ for skew-symmetric $Q$) is a common alternative, as is the matrix logarithm or Givens rotation composition. The paper's choice of Cayley transform follows from prior work (Liu et al., 2021; Qiu et al., 2023) and offers several practical advantages:

- **Exact orthogonality:** Unlike penalty-based methods that encourage approximate orthogonality through regularisation, Cayley guarantees exact orthogonality to numerical precision for any $Q$. There is no approximation error.
- **Smooth parameterisation:** The mapping $Q \mapsto R$ is smooth (in fact, rational), so gradients flow cleanly through the matrix inverse operation. Standard automatic differentiation handles $(I - Q)^{-1}$ without issues.
- **Identity initialisation:** When $Q = 0$ (all entries zero, a valid skew-symmetric matrix), $R = (I + 0)(I - 0)^{-1} = I$. So initialising all trainable $Q$ entries to zero yields $R = I$, which means the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained model.
- **Coverage of $\text{SO}(d)$:** The Cayley transform maps the entire space of skew-symmetric matrices onto $\text{SO}(d)$ — the set of orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$ (proper rotations). For finetuning, restricting to $\text{SO}(d)$ rather than the full $\text{O}(d)$ (which includes reflections) is reasonable since we want continuous deformations from identity, and reflections require passing through singular configurations.

**Computational cost.** Computing $(I - Q)^{-1}$ for each $2b \times 2b$ block requires a matrix inversion. Since $2b$ is typically small (e.g., $b=2$ gives $4 \times 4$ blocks; $b=8$ gives $16 \times 16$ blocks), this inversion is cheap and parallelisable across blocks. The total cost per forward pass is dominated by the matrix multiplications in the butterfly product, not by the Cayley inversion.

#### Identity Initialisation and Forward Pass Integration

**The principle.** BOFT must start from the exact pretrained model — before any finetuning, the adapted model should produce identical outputs to the pretrained model. This is standard practice in PEFT (LoRA initialises its low-rank matrices to zero), but it takes a specific form in BOFT due to the multiplicative structure.

**How identity initialisation works.** Each $2b \times 2b$ block in each butterfly component is parameterised by a skew-symmetric matrix $Q$ via Cayley transform. Initialising $Q = 0$ (all entries zero) yields $R_{\text{block}} = I_{2b}$ for each block, which means every butterfly component $\tilde{B}_b^{(d, i)}$ is the identity matrix $I_d$. The product of identity matrices is the identity matrix:

$$R(m, b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{B}_b^{(d, i)} = \prod_{i=1}^{m} I_d = I_d$$

Therefore, at initialisation, the forward pass computes:

$$z = (R(m, b) \cdot W^0)^\top x = (I_d \cdot W^0)^\top x = (W^0)^\top x$$

which is exactly the pretrained model's forward pass. There is no random perturbation, no small-norm initialisation — the model starts identically to the pretrained model, and any deviation occurs only through gradient updates to the $Q$ matrices.

**Why this is important.** If BOFT started from a random orthogonal matrix, the model would begin in a random rotated feature space, potentially destroying pretrained knowledge before any training even occurs. Identity initialisation ensures that the finetuning process is anchored at the pretrained optimum, and the learned orthogonal matrix represents a continuous deformation from identity — a rotation that preserves the original representation structure while adapting it to the new task.

**The forward pass during training.** For a linear layer with pretrained weights $W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ and input $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the BOFT-augmented forward pass is:

$$z = (R(m, b) \cdot W^0)^\top x$$

In practice, this is implemented as: compute $W^0 x$ first (the pretrained computation), then multiply by $R(m, b)$ on the left: $z = R(m, b) \cdot (W^0)^\top x$. The matrix $R(m, b)$ is the product of $m$ sparse butterfly components, so computing $R(m, b) \cdot v$ for a vector $v$ can be done by sequentially multiplying $v$ by $\tilde{B}_b^{(d, 1)}, \tilde{B}_b^{(d, 2)}, \ldots, \tilde{B}_b^{(d, m)}$, each of which is a sparse matrix-vector multiplication with $O(d \cdot b)$ cost. This is more efficient than forming the dense $R(m, b)$ explicitly and then multiplying.

During backpropagation, gradients flow through the Cayley transform (via the matrix inverse in $(I - Q)^{-1}$) and through the chain of matrix multiplications, updating the entries of each $Q$ matrix for each butterfly component.

**Absorbing $R$ after training.** At deployment, the learned $R(m, b)$ can be multiplied once into $W^0$:

$$W^{\text{finetuned}} = R(m, b) \cdot W^0$$

Since $W^0$ is frozen and $R(m, b)$ is now fixed, this produces a single $d \times n$ matrix. Inference then uses the standard linear layer $z = (W^{\text{finetuned}})^\top x$, with no additional computation, no extra parameters, and no change to the model architecture. This absorption step is what allows BOFT to claim "no inference latency" — unlike adapter methods that add trainable modules which must be evaluated at every forward pass, BOFT's parameters are fully folded into the weights.

**Applicability to different layer types.** The paper applies BOFT to linear layers in transformers (the query, key, value, and output projections in self-attention, and the feedforward layers in MLPs). The same mechanism works for any linear transformation — BOFT learns an orthogonal matrix $R$ of dimension matching the input dimension $d$ of the weight matrix $W^0$. For layers with a bias term, the bias can either be frozen, finetuned separately, or absorbed into the computation analogously.

#### Multiplicative Dropout

LoRA uses standard Dropout on its low-rank update — randomly zeroing entries of $A$ or $B$ during training to prevent overfitting. This works because the update is additive: $W^0 + \text{Dropout}(AB)$. In BOFT, the update is multiplicative ($R \cdot W^0$), so zeroing individual entries of $R$ would have a different and potentially more disruptive effect — it would selectively erase certain neuron interactions rather than simply attenuating the update. The paper therefore introduces a **multiplicative Dropout** mechanism tailored to the butterfly structure (Section 4, "Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT").

**The mechanism.** Each butterfly component $\tilde{B}_b^{(d, i)}$ can be permuted into $2b \times 2b$ block-diagonal form. Multiplicative Dropout operates at two granularities:

1. **Component-level dropout:** Randomly select $p_1$ percent of the $m$ butterfly components and replace them with identity matrices. This means for those selected components, the orthogonal transformation for that training step is skipped — the component contributes no rotation.

2. **Block-level dropout:** Within each butterfly component that is not dropped entirely, randomly select $p_2$ percent of the $2b \times 2b$ diagonal blocks and replace them with $2b \times 2b$ identity matrices. This means within a component, only a subset of the dimension blocks undergo rotation; the rest pass through unchanged.

The replacement with identity matrices (rather than zero matrices) is crucial: setting a block to zero would create a non-invertible transformation, destroying information and violating the orthogonality of the overall $R$. Setting it to identity means the corresponding dimensions are simply not rotated for that training step, which is a structured form of regularisation that preserves the angle-preservation property of the overall transformation.

**Dropout rates.** The paper specifies the two dropout probabilities $p_1$ and $p_2$ as hyperparameters, with typical values around $p_1 = 0.1$ and $p_2 = 0.1$ (as used in the GLUE experiments, Table 7 of Appendix A.1, with dropout rates ranging from 5% to 15% depending on the task). The dropout is applied independently at each training step, so different blocks and components are dropped in different iterations, effectively training an ensemble of sub-networks with varying numbers of active butterfly components.

**Why multiplicative Dropout is necessary.** Without dropout, the orthogonal matrix $R$ can overfit to the training data by learning task-specific rotations that memorise training examples rather than generalising. Multiplicative Dropout encourages the model to not rely on any single butterfly component or block — if information can flow through multiple paths in the butterfly graph, dropping some paths forces the remaining paths to learn robust transformations. This is analogous to standard Dropout's effect of preventing co-adaptation of neurons, but adapted to the structured, multiplicative nature of the orthogonal parameterisation.

**Why not standard Dropout?** Applying standard Bernoulli Dropout to individual entries of $R$ would break orthogonality — the resulting matrix would not preserve angles, defeating the core motivation of OFT/BOFT. Even if one were to apply Dropout to the $Q$ matrices (the skew-symmetric parameters), zeroing entries of $Q$ would still produce an orthogonal matrix via Cayley transform, but the effect on $R$ would be unpredictable — zeroing a single entry of $Q$ changes every entry of $R$ in a non-local way. The multiplicative Dropout approach of setting entire blocks to identity is both orthogonality-preserving and semantically interpretable: it temporarily reduces the "rotation budget" of the model rather than randomly perturbing the learned transformation.

#### Parameter Count Analysis and Expressivity Guarantees

**Parameter count formula.** For a linear layer of size $d \times n$, BOFT with $m$ butterfly components and block size $b$ uses a total number of trainable parameters given by:

$$\#\text{params} = \frac{1}{2} (b - 1) d m$$

where $d$ is the input dimension of the layer, $b$ is the block size ($b \geq 2$), and $m$ is the number of butterfly components.

**Derivation.** Each butterfly component $\tilde{B}_b^{(d, i)}$ consists of $d/(2b)$ blocks, each of size $2b \times 2b$. Each such block is parameterised by a skew-symmetric matrix $Q_{\text{block}}$ of size $2b \times 2b$, which has $2b(2b-1)/2$ independent entries (the upper triangle). However, the Cayley parameterisation with block size $b$ uses $b \times b$ diagonal blocks parameterised as $b \times b$ orthogonal matrices, and the total effective trainable parameters per component are $d(2b-1)/2$ (or equivalently $d \cdot b$ with Cayley constraints). The paper states the effective count as $\frac{1}{2} (b-1) d$ per component for the block size $b$, yielding $\frac{1}{2} (b-1) d m$ for $m$ components.

To verify: for $b=2$, each $4 \times 4$ block (size $2b \times 2b$) has a skew-symmetric $Q$ of size $4 \times 4$ with $4 \cdot 3 / 2 = 6$ independent parameters. There are $d/4$ such blocks, giving $6d/4 = 1.5d$ parameters per component. The formula $\frac{1}{2} (b-1) d = \frac{1}{2} (2-1) d = 0.5d$ per component — this discrepancy suggests the paper's formula counts the effective degrees of freedom after accounting for the Cayley transform's relationship between $Q$ entries and the resulting orthogonal matrix. For the purposes of this analysis, the key point is the scaling: parameters grow linearly with $d$, linearly with $m$, and are independent of $n$ (the output dimension), making BOFT particularly efficient for wide layers.

**Comparison with alternatives.** For a $d \times n$ linear layer:
- Full OFT (dense orthogonal matrix): $O(d^2)$ parameters — independent of $n$, but quadratic in $d$.
- Block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ (and $r = d/b$ blocks): $O(bd)$ parameters — linear in $d$, but with fixed sparsity pattern.
- LoRA with rank $r$: $O(r(d + n))$ parameters — linear in both $d$ and $n$, but additive rather than multiplicative.
- BOFT(m,b): $O(m b d)$ parameters — linear in $d$, $m$, and $b$, independent of $n$.

For the butterfly matrix specifically ($m = \log_2 d$, $b=2$), the parameter count is $O(d \log d)$, which is a significant reduction from $O(d^2)$ for full OFT. For $d = 1024$, this gives roughly $d \log_2 d = 1024 \cdot 10 = 10,240$ parameters per layer versus $d^2 = 1,048,576$ for full OFT — a 100× reduction.

**Expressivity: OFT as a special case.** When $m = 1$ and $b$ is arbitrary, BOFT reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size $2b$. This is because a single butterfly component $\tilde{B}_b(d, 2)$ is a block-diagonal matrix with $d/(2b)$ blocks of size $2b$, each of which is orthogonal. This is exactly the OFT parameterisation. When $m=1$ and $b = d/2$, BOFT reduces to full OFT (a single dense orthogonal matrix parameterised via Cayley). So BOFT(m,b) strictly generalises OFT — any OFT configuration can be expressed as a BOFT configuration with $m=1$, and BOFT with $m > 1$ can represent transformations that block-diagonal OFT cannot.

**Expressivity: Theorem 1 and full orthogonal group coverage.** The paper proves Theorem 1 in two parts (Appendix F):

**Part 1: BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size.** Since the additional butterfly components can be set to identity, any block-diagonal OFT configuration is achievable by BOFT with the same block size and $m > 1$. Therefore, the hypothesis class of BOFT strictly contains that of OFT with the same block size.

**Part 2: A generalised construction can represent any orthogonal matrix.** By allowing an extended form with pairs of butterfly matrices multiplied as $B_{d-1,1}(d) B_{d-1,2}(d)^\top \cdots B_{1,1}(d) B_{1,2}(d)^\top$, the paper shows that any orthogonal matrix can be represented. This follows from the result in Dao et al. (2020) that the "kaleidoscope hierarchy" (which this construction is a special case of) can represent any orthogonal matrix using $d-1$ Householder reflections, each of which can be expressed as a product of butterfly matrices. The proof sketch: any orthogonal matrix can be decomposed via QR factorisation into $d-1$ Householder reflections; each Householder reflection can be represented as a butterfly matrix product; therefore the product of $d-1$ such butterfly-matrix pairs can represent any orthogonal matrix.

Concretely, the generalised expression is:

$$R_G(m_1, b_1, m_2, b_2, \ell) = R_{\ell, 1}(m_1, b_1) R_{\ell, 2}(m_2, b_2)^\top \cdots R_{1,1}(m_1, b_1) R_{1,2}(m_2, b_2)^\top$$

When $m_1 = m_2 = \log_2 d$, $b_1 = b_2 = 2$, and $\ell = d-1$, this can represent the entire orthogonal group $\text{SO}(d)$.

**Why full expressivity isn't always desired.** The paper is careful to note that "better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance in finetuning, as full finetuning, despite its universal expressiveness, often yields unsatisfactory performance" (Section 5). The point of BOFT is not to maximise expressivity but to find a **structured subset** of the orthogonal group that provides a beneficial inductive bias — one that allows sufficient flexibility to adapt to downstream tasks while restricting the hypothesis space enough to prevent overfitting. The butterfly structure achieves this by its multi-scale mixing property: dimensions mix at progressively larger scales, which is a natural prior for many feature transformations, but the mixing is constrained by the butterfly topology rather than being completely unconstrained.

**Expressivity simulation (Figure 5).** The paper conducts a simulation to empirically measure how well BOFT matrices with different configurations $(m, b)$ can approximate random dense orthogonal matrices of size $512 \times 512$. The procedure: generate random orthogonal matrices, then for each BOFT configuration, find the BOFT matrix that minimises the Frobenius norm difference (approximation error). The results (Figure 5) show that:

- BOFT consistently achieves lower approximation error than OFT at the same parameter budget — e.g., BOFT(9,2) has better expressivity than BOFT(1,16) (i.e., OFT with block size 16) while using fewer parameters.
- Smaller block size $b$ with more components $m$ is more parameter-efficient than larger $b$ with fewer $m$ — e.g., BOFT(6,4) uses much fewer parameters than BOFT(2,16) but achieves similar approximation error.
- The Pareto frontier is achieved by configurations with small $b$ and correspondingly large $m$, validating the butterfly factorisation approach.

**Spectral property preservation (Section 5).** A key theoretical property inherited from OFT is that BOFT perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix. Consider the singular value decomposition $W^0 = U \Sigma V^\top$. After BOFT, the adapted weight matrix is $R(m, b) W^0 = (R(m, b) U) \Sigma V^\top$. Since $R(m, b)$ is orthogonal, $R(m, b) U$ is also an orthogonal matrix. Therefore, the singular values $\Sigma$ are unchanged — the largest singular value (spectral norm) is identical before and after finetuning. This has been shown to benefit training stability and generalisation (Miyato et al., 2018; Yoshida and Miyato, 2017), since the Lipschitz constant of the layer is preserved and cannot explode during training. In contrast, LoRA's additive update $W^0 + AB$ can change the singular values arbitrarily.

#### Configuration Space and Hyperparameter Selection

The paper presents BOFT as a family parameterised by $(m, b)$, where $m$ is the number of butterfly components and $b$ is the block size. These two hyperparameters control the expressivity-efficiency tradeoff:

- **$m$ (number of butterfly components):** Controls the depth of the butterfly hierarchy. $m = 1$ gives block-diagonal OFT. $m = \log_2 d$ gives a fully dense orthogonal butterfly matrix (for scalar blocks, $b=1$). Increasing $m$ increases expressivity (more levels of cross-block mixing) and increases parameter count linearly. In the experiments, the paper uses $m = 2$ to $m = 6$ depending on the task and parameter budget.

- **$b$ (block size):** Controls the granularity of the butterfly components. $b=2$ means each "entry" in the butterfly factor is a $2 \times 2$ matrix; $b=16$ means each entry is a $16 \times 16$ matrix. Smaller $b$ gives more parameter efficiency but more constrained transformations; larger $b$ gives more expressivity per component. The block size also interacts with the dimension $d$ — the butterfly structure requires that $d$ is divisible by $2b \cdot 2^k$ for appropriate $k$.

The product $m \cdot b$ approximates the effective "rank-like" capacity: BOFT(m,b) with small $b$ and large $m$ uses the butterfly topology to achieve dense mixing with few parameters; BOFT(m,b) with large $b$ and small $m$ relies more on within-block expressivity and less on the butterfly structure.

**Configuration naming.** The paper uses BOFT($m$, $b$) throughout. For the controllable generation experiments, it also reports using the number of blocks $r = d / b$ rather than the block size $b$, following the convention of the original OFT paper. In this notation, BOFT with $r$ blocks and $m$ components has block size $b = d/r$, so larger $r$ means smaller blocks and fewer parameters.

**How configurations are chosen for experiments.** The paper does not propose an automated method for selecting $(m, b)$. Instead, it manually selects configurations that match the parameter budget of competing methods for fair comparison. For example (Table 1):
- OFT with $b=16$ uses 0.79M parameters on DeBERTaV3-base
- BOFT with $m=2$, $b=8$ uses 0.75M parameters — slightly fewer, but similar budget
- BOFT(1,16) would be identical to OFT(b=16); BOFT(2,8) adds a second butterfly component while halving the block size to keep parameter count similar

This manual matching allows the paper to claim that BOFT's improvements come from the butterfly structure (better inductive bias) rather than simply having more parameters.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Matrix Factorization as Information Transmission Enables Principled Sparsity Design

The paper's most conceptually distinctive contribution is not the butterfly structure itself — butterflies have been used in ML before (Dao et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2022) — but rather the **information transmission framework** that transforms the problem of "find a sparse factorization of a dense orthogonal matrix" into a graph connectivity design problem. This reframing (Section 3) is what elevates BOFT from "we tried butterfly matrices and they worked" to a principled methodology with clear desiderata.

Prior to this work, the design of structured matrices for parameter efficiency was largely *ad hoc*: choose a pattern (block-diagonal, low-rank, circulant), try it, and see if it works. There was no unified language for reasoning about what makes one sparsity pattern better than another for a given objective. The paper's framework provides exactly this language: a sparse factorization $R = B_m \cdots B_1$ is good if its induced graph achieves **dense connectivity** (every input reaches every output) with **minimum free edges** (fewest parameters beyond those forced by orthogonality). This converts an algebraic question into a combinatorial one with clear evaluation criteria.

The significance of this move extends beyond BOFT. The framework is *generative*: it doesn't just analyse existing factorizations, it enables the design of new ones. The paper explicitly notes this in Section 7:

> "Our information transmission framework further enables us to draw inspiration from a distinct research discipline – computer networking, where the efficiency of a network topology for transmitting information is heavily studied."

This points to a research programme where network topologies from computer science (butterfly, Benes, Clos, fat-tree, etc.) can be imported wholesale as structured matrix factorizations with known connectivity properties. The butterfly graph is the first and most natural example (it's the classical solution for connecting $N$ inputs to $N$ outputs with $O(N \log N)$ switches), but it's unlikely to be the last or the optimal one. The framework thus shifts the conversation from "what pattern should we use?" to "what graph achieves our connectivity and sparsity goals?", which is a more tractable and principled question.

This is a **fundamental conceptual advance** rather than an incremental refinement. It doesn't just improve OFT — it provides a new way to think about structured matrix design for any application where composition of sparse factors matters.

---

### Innovation 2: The Butterfly Structure Provides a Continuous Interpolation Between Identity and Full Orthogonal Matrices

The paper's second major insight is that the butterfly factorization solves a specific structural problem that block-diagonal OFT could not: it provides a **smooth, tuneable path** between the identity matrix (maximum regularity, minimum expressivity) and dense orthogonal matrices (maximum expressivity, minimum regularity). This is not a minor tweak — it fundamentally changes the nature of the expressivity-efficiency tradeoff from a discrete choice to a continuous spectrum.

The original OFT forced a hard choice: pick a block size $b$. If $b$ is small, you get parameter efficiency but severe structural constraints (no cross-block mixing). If $b$ is large, you get expressivity but quadratic parameter growth. There is no intermediate — a block-diagonal matrix with block size 16 is fundamentally incapable of mixing dimensions across block boundaries, regardless of what values it learns. The hypothesis class is a *disjoint collection* of independent subspaces.

BOFT changes this by introducing a second axis: the number of butterfly components $m$. With $m=1$, BOFT reduces exactly to block-diagonal OFT. As $m$ increases, cross-block mixing is progressively introduced at increasing scales — first adjacent pairs, then groups of 4, then groups of 8, and so on. At $m = \log_2 d$, the mixing is fully global (dense connectivity). The block size $b$ remains an independent knob controlling per-block expressivity. The result is a 2D hyperparameter space $(m, b)$ that smoothly interpolates between:
- **BOFT(1, 2)**: extreme sparsity, block-diagonal with tiny blocks, minimal parameters
- **BOFT(1, d)**: full dense OFT, maximal parameters
- **BOFT(log₂ d, 2)**: dense butterfly with scalar blocks, $O(d \log d)$ parameters
- **BOFT(log₂ d, b)**: dense butterfly with $b \times b$ blocks, richer per-block transformations

This matters because it means practitioners can **dial in** the right amount of structured expressivity for their task, rather than being forced to choose between "too constrained" and "too many parameters." The paper demonstrates this empirically: for controllable generation (Figure 8), increasing $m$ from 1 to 6 at fixed block count $r=32$ monotonically improves landmark error, showing that the additional mixing levels consistently help, without requiring the practitioner to guess the right block-chopping upfront.

The conceptual advance here is the recognition that **structure can be added in layers rather than chosen as a single pattern**. This is a design principle that generalizes beyond butterflies: one could imagine other hierarchical factorizations (wavelet-like, tree-structured, scale-space) that add mixing at progressively larger scales. The butterfly is one instance of a broader idea — that parameter-efficient structured matrices should be **constructed compositionally** from sparse factors that each contribute a specific, interpretable kind of connectivity.

This is a **significant refinement** of OFT rather than a completely new paradigm, but the refinement is substantial enough to enable OFT's application to domains (language, vision, segmentation) where the original block-diagonal version was too constrained or too parameter-hungry to compete with LoRA.

---

### Innovation 3: Empirical Demonstration That Orthogonal Finetuning Is a General-Purpose PEFT Method, Not a Niche Diffusion Technique

Before this paper, orthogonal finetuning was known only for controlling text-to-image diffusion models (Qiu et al., 2023). The original OFT paper demonstrated strong results on controllable generation and subject-driven generation, but it was unclear whether the method's advantages — angle preservation, spectral norm preservation, training stability — would transfer to other domains, or whether they were specific to the peculiarities of diffusion model finetuning (where preserving pretrained knowledge is especially critical to avoid catastrophic forgetting of visual concepts).

This paper answers that question decisively. By applying BOFT across:
- **Language understanding** (GLUE with DeBERTaV3, Table 1): BOFT(2,8) achieves 89.89 average vs. 89.77 for OFT(b=16) and 88.50 for LoRA(r=8), all at ~0.75M parameters
- **Massive multitask knowledge** (MMLU with Llama-2-7B, Table 2): BOFT(2,8) at 0.12% parameters achieves 47.9% 5-shot accuracy vs. 47.3% for LoRA(r=32) at 0.25% parameters
- **Mathematical reasoning** (GSM8K and MATH, Table 3): BOFT(2,8) reaches 50.6% on GSM8K and 8.6% on MATH vs. 50.2% and 7.8% for LoRA(r=32), using half the parameters
- **Visual transfer learning** (VTAB-1K with DINOv2, Table 4): BOFT(m=4,b=4) achieves 77.4% average vs. 76.6% for LoRA(r=4) and 77.3% for OFT(b=16)
- **High-quality segmentation** (SAM, Table 5): BOFT-SAM matches HQ-SAM's performance with only 3% of the trainable parameters

The consistent pattern — BOFT matching or exceeding LoRA and OFT across diverse modalities and task types, often with fewer parameters — establishes orthogonal finetuning as a **legitimate general-purpose alternative to LoRA**, not a domain-specific technique. This is significant because the PEFT field has largely converged on LoRA as the default method; having a well-validated alternative with complementary strengths (geometric regularization, spectral norm preservation) enriches the practitioner's toolkit and opens the door to hybrid approaches.

The paper also provides the first evidence that orthogonal finetuning scales to large language models (Llama-2-7B), which was an open question given the original OFT's focus on diffusion models with relatively small UNet architectures. The fact that BOFT works on 7B-parameter transformers with only 0.12% trainable parameters, and improves over LoRA on knowledge-intensive benchmarks like MMLU, suggests that angle preservation may be particularly valuable for maintaining factual knowledge during finetuning — a hypothesis the paper doesn't fully explore but which the results support.

This contribution is **incremental in method but fundamental in empirical scope**: it doesn't propose a new algorithm, but it transforms our understanding of where and when orthogonal finetuning is useful, upgrading it from a specialised tool for one domain to a broadly applicable technique.

---

### Innovation 4: Free Weight Interpolation on the Orthogonal Manifold via Factor-Wise Identity Replacement

One of the paper's most intriguing findings is buried in Section 6.3 and Figure 10: after training BOFT with multiple butterfly components, one can **sequentially replace individual components with identity matrices** — with no retraining — and the resulting interpolated models produce semantically meaningful, smoothly varying outputs. Setting all components to identity recovers the original Stable Diffusion. Keeping all components gives the fully finetuned model. Intermediate configurations (3 of 5 components active, 2 of 5, etc.) produce outputs that blend the controlled and uncontrolled behaviours in a coherent way.

This property is **not shared by LoRA or block-diagonal OFT**. In LoRA, setting the low-rank matrices to zero gives the pretrained model, but there is no natural sequence of partial zeroings that produces a smooth interpolation — the rank is a global property, not a compositional one. In block-diagonal OFT, setting some blocks to identity would affect only those specific dimension groups, producing unpredictable and likely incoherent outputs rather than a smooth blend. BOFT's compositional structure — the fact that the orthogonal matrix is a product of independent factors, each contributing a distinct "layer" of mixing — is what enables this interpolation.

The paper interprets this as evidence that "the hypothesis weight space (i.e., model space) in BOFT can well preserve the semantics and effectively eliminate many bad local minima." More concretely, it suggests that each butterfly component learns a semantically meaningful level of transformation — early components (small block sizes) handle fine-grained within-group rotations, later components (large block sizes) handle cross-group mixing — and that these levels are sufficiently independent that removing them in sequence produces a semantically coherent trajectory through weight space.

This has practical implications for **model deployment and personalisation**: one could train BOFT once with many components and then, at deployment time, select the "strength" of finetuning by choosing how many components to activate, trading off between adherence to the finetuning task and preservation of pretrained behaviour — without any additional training. The paper doesn't develop this into a method, but the observation opens an intriguing direction for controllable model adaptation that goes beyond what single-knob methods like LoRA rank can offer.

This is a **serendipitous empirical discovery** rather than a designed feature of the method, but it has the potential to become a significant practical capability if developed further. It is the kind of finding that reframes how one thinks about what structured matrix factorizations can provide: not just parameter efficiency, but *interpretable, composable model subspaces*.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on a wide range of benchmarks: GLUE (8 sentence classification/regression tasks, development set, following standard splits from Zhang et al. 2023); MMLU (57 tasks, zero-shot and 5-shot, following Hendrycks et al. 2021); GSM8K and MATH (mathematical reasoning, test splits, following Cobbe et al. 2021 and Hendrycks et al. 2021); VTAB-1K (19 image classification tasks with 800 training samples each, following Zhai et al. 2019); HQSeg-44K (4 high-quality segmentation datasets: DIS, COIFT, HRSOD, ThinObject, following Ke et al. 2023); and text-to-image generation tasks (ADE20K for segmentation-to-image, CelebV-HQ for landmark-to-face, DreamBooth dataset for subject-driven generation, following Qiu et al. 2023). Each dataset was chosen to test a different aspect of adaptation: semantic understanding, knowledge retention, reasoning, visual transfer, dense prediction, and generative control.

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses four distinct model families at different scales: DeBERTaV3-base (~184M parameters) for GLUE, chosen because it represents a strong but not overwhelming pretrained NLU model; Llama-2-7B for MMLU, GSM8K, and MATH, chosen because it is a widely-used open LLM at a scale where parameter-efficient finetuning is economically relevant; DINOv2-large (~304M parameters) for VTAB-1K, chosen because it is a state-of-the-art self-supervised vision transformer; SAM (ViT-H image encoder + mask decoder) for high-quality segmentation, chosen because it is a canonical vision foundation model with a well-defined adaptation task; and Stable Diffusion 2.1 for text-to-image generation, chosen to maintain continuity with the original OFT evaluation. The paper states DeBERTaV3 and DINOv2 were used as provided by HuggingFace; Llama-2-7B was loaded from Meta's release.

- **Metrics.** For GLUE: accuracy (MNLI, SST-2, QQP, QNLI, RTE, MRPC), Matthew's correlation (CoLA), and average Pearson-Spearman correlation (STS-B), reported individually and averaged following the standard GLUE protocol. For MMLU: accuracy (%) averaged over 57 tasks, reported separately for 0-shot and 5-shot settings, with breakdowns by subject category (Humanities, STEM, Social Sciences, Other). For GSM8K and MATH: exact match accuracy (%). For VTAB-1K: Top-1 classification accuracy (%) on the original test set of each task, averaged across all 19 tasks and broken down by task category (Natural, Specialized, Structured). For SAM: mIoU and mBIoU (mean boundary IoU) following HQ-SAM, averaged across the four datasets. For controllable generation: landmark error (Euclidean distance between predicted and ground-truth facial landmarks, lower is better) and mIoU (for segmentation-to-image). For subject-driven generation: qualitative evaluation only, following the standard practice in the DreamBooth literature.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against a comprehensive set of existing methods: **Full finetuning** (all parameters updated); **BitFit** (Zaken et al., 2022) — bias terms only; **H-Adapter** (Houlsby et al., 2019) and **P-Adapter** (Pfeiffer et al., 2020) — adapter-based methods; **LoRA** with ranks r=4, 8, 16, 32 (Hu et al., 2022) — the dominant PEFT baseline; **AdaLoRA** (Zhang et al., 2023) — dynamic rank allocation; **FacT** (Jie and Deng, 2023) — tensor factorisation; **GLoRA** (Chavan et al., 2023) — generalised LoRA; **OFT** with various block sizes (Qiu et al., 2023) — the direct predecessor; **HQ-SAM** (Ke et al., 2023) — for segmentation, representing the state-of-the-art with additional trainable modules; and **Linear probing** — only the classification head trained, as a lower bound. For the text-to-image experiments, the primary baselines are LoRA and OFT at matched parameter budgets.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not measure compute in FLOPs or wall-clock time for most experiments. Instead, fairness is established through **matched trainable parameter counts**: for each experiment, BOFT configurations are chosen (by selecting $m$ and $b$) such that their total trainable parameters approximately equal or are less than those of the strongest competing methods. The paper explicitly reports parameter counts in every results table (e.g., Table 1: OFT b=16 at 0.79M vs. BOFT m=2 b=8 at 0.75M; Table 4: LoRA r=4 at 1.77M vs. BOFT m=6 b=2 at 1.11M). For the diffusion model experiments, parameter budgets are segmented into small (~2.5M), medium (~10M), and large (~20M) regimes, with methods compared within each regime (Table 6). The paper notes that BOFT has slightly higher training runtime overhead than OFT due to the sequential matrix multiplications in the butterfly product, but this cost is not quantified in the tables — only the zero inference latency after weight absorption is emphasised.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper states that "results are averaged over 5 random seeds, and the gains have passed significant tests with p < 0.05" (Section 6 introduction). For each task, a grid search over learning rate and dropout rate is conducted for each method, and the best configuration's test-set performance after the final epoch is reported. For VTAB-1K, the paper follows the standard protocol: each of the 19 tasks provides 800 training samples; models are trained on this set and evaluated on the original test set. There is no k-fold cross-validation — each dataset has a fixed train/test split. Hyperparameter details for each experiment are provided in Appendix A (Tables 7 and 8 for GLUE and VTAB-1K learning rates; Section A.3 for Llama experiments; Section A.4 for SAM; Section A.5 for diffusion models).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Natural Language Understanding on GLUE (Table 1)

The headline result is that BOFT(m=2, b=8) achieves an average GLUE score of 89.89, outperforming LoRA(r=8) at 88.50 (+1.39 points), AdaLoRA at 89.46 (+0.43), and OFT(b=16) at 89.77 (+0.12), while using the smallest parameter budget among all compared methods at 0.75M trainable parameters. This is the first demonstration that orthogonal finetuning can match or exceed LoRA on standard NLU benchmarks — the original OFT paper did not evaluate on GLUE.

Breaking down by task: BOFT outperforms or matches LoRA on 7 of 8 tasks. The largest margins are on CoLA (BOFT: 73.91 vs. LoRA: 69.82, +4.09 points), RTE (BOFT: 88.81 vs. LoRA: 85.20, +3.61), and MRPC (BOFT: 92.40 vs. LoRA: 89.95, +2.45). On MNLI, BOFT is marginally behind LoRA (90.25 vs. 90.65) — the only task where LoRA holds an advantage. On SST-2, BOFT achieves 96.44 vs. LoRA's 94.95 (+1.49). On QQP, QNLI, and STS-B, the methods are within 0.5 points of each other.

A non-obvious pattern: OFT(b=16) already outperforms LoRA on 6 of 8 tasks with 0.79M parameters (89.77 average), and BOFT(2,8) further improves on this despite having slightly fewer parameters (0.75M). This suggests the butterfly structure's cross-block mixing provides genuine benefit beyond what block-diagonal OFT offers, even at moderate block sizes. The improvement from BOFT(1,16) (which is equivalent to OFT(b=16)) to BOFT(2,8) is not merely from different block size — BOFT(2,8) has block size 16 (since $b=8$ with $m=2$ multiplies to effective block coverage of $2b=16$), but achieves dense connectivity within those 16 dimensions through the butterfly product, whereas OFT(b=16) has independent $16 \times 16$ blocks with no cross-block interaction within each block's 16 dimensions.

#### Massive Multitask Language Understanding on MMLU (Table 2)

Llama-2-7B finetuned on Alpaca with BOFT(m=2, b=8) achieves 47.9% 5-shot accuracy and 46.7% 0-shot accuracy, compared to LoRA(r=32) at 47.3% / 45.9% and OFT(b=16) at 47.5% / 46.2%. The parameter budgets: BOFT uses 0.12% of Llama-2-7B's parameters; LoRA(r=32) uses 0.25% (more than double); OFT(b=16) uses 0.13%.

The category breakdown is informative: BOFT's advantage is largest in the "Other" category (55.1%, vs. LoRA's 54.7% for 5-shot) and Humanities (44.5% vs. LoRA's 42.9%), while STEM is nearly tied (39.0% vs. 38.7%). The 0-shot results show a slightly larger BOFT advantage: 46.7% vs. 45.9% for LoRA(r=32), with the gap concentrated in Humanities (44.3% vs. 42.5%) and Social Sciences (53.1% vs. 52.8%). The paper does not report standard errors for these category-level breakdowns, so the reliability of the per-category comparisons is unclear — the overall score difference (47.9% vs. 47.3%) is within 0.6 percentage points, which may or may not be statistically significant depending on the variance across the 57 tasks.

A subtle but important result: BOFT(m=2, b=8) produces a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with effective block size 16 (since $b=8$ with 2 components means each block butterfly component has $2b=16$), and yet it outperforms OFT(b=16) — which also has block size 16 — by 0.4 points in 5-shot. This is the cleanest evidence that the butterfly structure's cross-mixing within each 16-dimensional block provides benefits beyond what independent block-diagonal rotations offer. If the two methods had identical expressivity (both block-diagonal with block size 16), they should perform identically; the fact that they don't implies the butterfly factorization introduces a qualitatively different inductive bias even when the effective block size matches.

#### Mathematical Reasoning on GSM8K and MATH (Table 3)

On GSM8K, BOFT(m=2, b=8) reaches 50.6% accuracy, compared to 50.2% for OFT(b=16), 50.2% for LoRA(r=32), and 14.6% for the base Llama-2-7B. The margins are very small — 0.4 points over both baselines — but BOFT uses the fewest parameters (0.12% vs. 0.25% for LoRA and 0.13% for OFT). On MATH, the differences are more pronounced: BOFT achieves 8.6% vs. 7.8% for LoRA(r=32) and 8.4% for OFT(b=16), a +0.8 and +0.2 point improvement respectively, representing more than 10% relative improvement over LoRA. The base model scores 2.5%.

The paper provides a case study in Appendix E comparing Llama-2 and BOFT outputs on 6 example questions. These examples suggest that BOFT-finetuned Llama-2 produces more systematic step-by-step reasoning ("James bought 5 packs of beef... so he bought a total of 5 * 4 = 20 pounds of beef") compared to the base model's sometimes garbled arithmetic ("So he paid 5 * 4 * 5.50 = 137.50 dollars"), but these are cherry-picked examples and cannot substitute for quantitative error analysis. The paper does not provide a breakdown of error types (calculation errors, reasoning errors, misinterpretation) that would help explain why BOFT helps on MATH more than GSM8K.

#### Visual Transfer Learning on VTAB-1K (Table 4)

On the full VTAB-1K benchmark with DINOv2-large, BOFT(m=4, b=4) achieves 77.9% average accuracy — the highest across all methods — versus 76.6% for LoRA(r=4), 77.3% for OFT(b=16), and 74.6% for full finetuning. The parameter counts: BOFT uses 1.77M, LoRA uses 1.77M, OFT uses 2.10M. Three BOFT configurations are evaluated: (m=2, b=8) with 1.99M parameters achieves 77.4%, (m=4, b=4) with 1.77M achieves 77.9%, and (m=6, b=2) with 1.11M achieves 77.1%.

The key observations from the per-task breakdown:

- **Natural tasks** (Cifar100, Caltech101, DTD, Flowers102, Pets, SVHN, Sun397): BOFT(m=4,b=4) averages approximately 84.8% (computed from the 7 natural task accuracies). The highest individual scores are Flowers102 (99.7%, matched by most methods — likely near ceiling), Pets (95.0%), and SVHN (93.0%). BOFT's advantages on natural tasks are small but consistent — it leads on 4 of 7 tasks within this category.

- **Specialized tasks** (Camelyon, EuroSAT, Resisc45, Retinopathy): BOFT is competitive but not dominant. On EuroSAT, LoRA(r=4) achieves 96.4% vs. BOFT's 96.4-96.6% range. On Resisc45, BOFT(m=4,b=4) achieves 91.6%, slightly behind the 91.5% of OFT(b=16). On Retinopathy, BOFT performs at 77.3%, similar to OFT's 77.2% and LoRA's 77.4%. The specialized tasks show no method has a clear systematic advantage.

- **Structured tasks** (Clevr-Count, Clevr-Dist, DMLab, KITTI-Dist, dSpr-Loc, dSpr-Ori, sNORB-Azim, sNORB-Ele): This is where orthogonal methods show their largest advantages. On dSpr-Loc (location prediction), BOFT(m=4,b=4) achieves 93.9% vs. LoRA's 85.8% (+8.1 points). On sNORB-Azim (azimuth prediction), BOFT reaches 36.2% vs. LoRA's 31.8% (+4.4 points). On dSpr-Ori (orientation prediction), BOFT gets 62.0% but full finetuning achieves 62.0% as well, suggesting this task may not benefit from PEFT constraints. On Clevr-Count, BOFT(m=2,b=8) reaches 84.5% vs. 74.7% for LoRA (+9.8 points). These large gaps on structured tasks — which require spatial reasoning, counting, and geometric understanding — suggest that the angle-preservation property of orthogonal finetuning may be particularly valuable for tasks where the pretrained features encode spatial relationships that LoRA's additive updates can disrupt.

BOFT(m=6,b=2) with only 1.11M parameters still achieves 77.1%, outperforming LoRA(r=4) at 1.77M (76.6%) and nearly matching OFT(b=16) at 2.10M (77.3%). This demonstrates that the butterfly structure can maintain competitive performance even at extreme parameter efficiency.

#### High-Quality Segmentation with SAM (Table 5, Figure 6, Figure 11)

On HQSeg-44K, BOFT-SAM(m=2, b=8) achieves 89.5% mIoU and 82.2% mBIoU on average across the four datasets, using only 0.06M trainable parameters — approximately 3% of HQ-SAM's 1.33M and less than 0.2% of full SAM finetuning's 4.06M. Despite this extreme parameter efficiency, BOFT-SAM matches HQ-SAM's mIoU (89.5% vs. 89.1%) and slightly exceeds its mBIoU (82.2% vs. 81.8%).

Breaking down by dataset: BOFT-SAM performs best on ThinObject (91.7% mIoU, 81.8% mBIoU), where it significantly exceeds HQ-SAM's 89.5% mIoU. On DIS, BOFT-SAM achieves 78.4% mIoU, comparable to HQ-SAM's 78.6% and full finetuning's 78.9%. On COIFT and HRSOD, performance is within 1-2 points of the best method. The paper notes that "BOFT-SAM has exactly the same inference speed as SAM, while, in contrast, HQ-SAM has additional modules that affect its inference speed" — meaning the 3% parameter budget and zero inference overhead make BOFT a strictly more efficient alternative to the purpose-built HQ-SAM architecture.

Figure 6 provides qualitative examples: BOFT-SAM produces visibly cleaner boundaries on challenging cases (thin structures, overlapping objects) compared to the base SAM, and Figure 11 (Appendix B) shows additional examples where BOFT-SAM resolves fine details that the base SAM misses or merges.

#### Controllable Text-to-Image Generation (Table 6, Figures 7, 8, 10, 16, 17)

On the landmark-to-face (L2F) task, BOFT(m=5, r=16) achieves the lowest landmark error of 5.667, compared to OFT(r=2) at 6.407, LoRA(r=128) at 8.038, and the small-budget BOFT(m=2, r=32) at 8.070 — all at comparable parameter budgets of ~20M, ~21M, ~20M, and ~2.7M respectively. The progression is clear: as butterfly components increase from m=2 (error 8.070) to m=5 (error 6.387 at r=16) to m=4 with r=8 (error 5.667), the additional mixing levels consistently improve control precision.

The small-budget comparison (~2.7M parameters) is particularly revealing: BOFT(m=2, r=32) achieves error 8.070, while OFT(r=16) achieves 8.876 and LoRA(r=16) achieves 8.878. This is a 9-10% relative improvement over both baselines at identical parameter counts — the clearest evidence that the butterfly structure's inductive bias provides benefits beyond what block-diagonal or low-rank parameterizations can offer at the same efficiency level.

On segmentation-to-image (S2I, Table 9 in Appendix A.5), the pattern differs: OFT(r=2) at 20.89M parameters achieves mIoU 29.44, while BOFT(m=4, r=8) at 20.76M achieves 28.83, and LoRA(r=128) at 20.17M achieves 8.038. The paper notes that "the S2I task is generally easier than the L2F task and does not require strong finetuning flexibility" — the best performance for all methods falls in a narrow range (24-29 mIoU), and OFT's slight edge over BOFT on this specific task suggests that when the task is simple, the additional mixing capacity of the butterfly structure may not be necessary and could slightly over-regularise. However, at smaller parameter budgets (2-3M), BOFT still outperforms OFT (specific numbers not provided in the main paper, but stated as "BOFT can still achieve better control performance than OFT given less amount of parameter budget, demonstrating its parameter efficiency").

The convergence analysis (Figure 8) with fixed r=32 across varying m shows that larger m not only improves final performance but accelerates convergence: by epoch 5, BOFT(m=6) has already reached approximately error 10, while OFT(m=0) is above error 20. The curves are monotonically ordered by m — each additional butterfly component shifts the entire learning curve downward — which the paper interprets as evidence that "a dense orthogonal matrix can transform neurons more effectively due to its more efficient information transmission."

The weight interpolation experiment (Figure 10) is not a quantitative result but a qualitative demonstration: using BOFT(m=5, r=16), the paper sequentially replaces butterfly components with identity matrices (right-to-left in the multiplication order) and shows that the generated images smoothly transition from landmark-controlled faces to uncontrolled Stable Diffusion outputs. With all 6 matrices active (0 replaced), the output closely follows the facial landmarks. With 3 remaining, the image still shows structure from the control but begins to exhibit more variability. With 0 remaining (all identity, equivalent to the base SD with the trained control head), the output is an uncontrolled face. The paper does not quantify the "smoothness" of this interpolation (e.g., via perceptual metrics), but Figure 10 visually supports the claim of a semantically meaningful trajectory through weight space.

#### Subject-Driven Generation (Figures 9, 12-15)

There are no quantitative metrics for subject-driven generation — the evaluation is purely qualitative, following the convention in the DreamBooth literature where CLIP-based metrics are known to correlate poorly with human judgment of subject fidelity. The paper provides extensive visual comparisons (Figures 9, 12-15 in appendices) across 5 subject categories: backpack, teapot, vase, sneaker, glasses, toy, stuffed animal, and dog.

The qualitative claim (Section 6.3) is that "BOFT can achieve the best of both worlds by simultaneously demonstrating good subject identity preservation as well as accurate text prompt following ability," while OFT tends toward better identity preservation but weaker prompt following, and LoRA shows better prompt following but weaker identity preservation. The toy duck example in Figure 9 illustrates this: for the prompt "a cube shaped [V] toy," BOFT generates a recognisably cubed shape with the duck's colors and texture, while OFT preserves the duck's shape too faithfully (failing the "cube shaped" prompt) and LoRA produces something cube-shaped but with less recognisable identity.

These qualitative results are suggestive but not conclusive — the paper does not report a user study, does not quantify identity preservation (e.g., via face recognition metrics for the dog and human subjects), and does not report the number of images generated or the selection criterion for the shown examples. The consistency of the pattern across 5 subject categories strengthens the claim, but the lack of quantification limits the strength of conclusions that can be drawn about the relative magnitude of BOFT's advantage.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Block size $b$ vs. number of components $m$ (VTAB-1K, Table 4)**: Three BOFT configurations with similar parameter counts are compared: BOFT(m=2,b=8) at 1.99M achieves 77.4%, BOFT(m=4,b=4) at 1.77M achieves 77.9%, and BOFT(m=6,b=2) at 1.11M achieves 77.1%. The best performance comes from an intermediate configuration (m=4, b=4), not the extremes, suggesting that both very small block sizes (b=2, requiring many components to achieve dense mixing) and larger block sizes with few components (m=2, b=8) are suboptimal compared to a balanced middle ground. However, the differences (77.4% → 77.9% → 77.1%) are within 0.8 percentage points across a parameter range of 1.11M to 1.99M, so the optimal configuration is task-dependent and the performance surface may be relatively flat.

- **Number of butterfly components $m$ on controllable generation (Figure 8)**: With fixed r=32, increasing m from 0 (OFT) to 6 monotonically decreases landmark error at epoch 20. The largest jump is from m=0 to m=2 (error drops from ~14 to ~10), with diminishing returns beyond m=4. BOFT(m=6) achieves the lowest error but the gap from m=4 to m=6 is smaller than from m=2 to m=4, suggesting that the benefits of additional mixing levels saturate — likely because after $\log_2(d/b)$ levels, the connectivity is already fully dense and further components provide redundant rather than novel mixing paths.

- **Parameter budget scaling on controllable generation (Table 6)**: Across three budget regimes (~2.7M, ~10M, ~20M), BOFT with appropriate $(m, b)$ consistently achieves lower error than LoRA and OFT at the same budget. The improvement is largest in absolute terms at the highest budget (BOFT 5.667 vs. OFT 6.407, a 0.74 point reduction) and largest in relative terms at the smallest budget (BOFT 8.070 vs. OFT/LoRA ~8.87, a 9% reduction). This suggests the butterfly structure's benefits are not merely from having enough parameters to "brute force" expressivity — the structured inductive bias helps even (or especially) when the parameter budget is severely constrained.

- **Multiplicative Dropout rates (GLUE, Table 7 in Appendix)**: The paper sweeps dropout rates per task, with values ranging from 5% to 15%. The CoLA task uses the lowest dropout (5%), while the larger datasets (MNLI, QQP) use 10-15%. The paper does not provide an ablation comparing performance with and without multiplicative Dropout, so the contribution of this mechanism to overall performance cannot be isolated from the reported results.

- **LoRA rank scaling comparison (Tables 2, 3)**: On MMLU, LoRA(r=16) achieves 47.0% 5-shot vs. LoRA(r=32) at 47.3% — a 0.3 point gain from doubling rank. BOFT(m=2,b=8) at 47.9% outperforms both while using fewer parameters than LoRA(r=32). On GSM8K, LoRA(r=32) achieves 50.2%, and BOFT achieves 50.6%. This shows that BOFT's advantage is not simply from having "effectively more parameters" than the LoRA baseline — LoRA's performance saturates at r=32 while BOFT extracts more performance from fewer parameters, suggesting a qualitatively different (and more parameter-efficient) form of expressivity.

- **Expressivity simulation (Figure 5, Figure 19 in Appendix I)**: The paper approximates random dense orthogonal matrices with BOFT configurations and measures Frobenius error. BOFT consistently lies on a better Pareto frontier than OFT: for a given parameter budget, BOFT achieves lower approximation error; for a given approximation error, BOFT uses fewer parameters. The simulation with finetuned DINOv2 matrices (Figure 19) replicates this finding on "real" orthogonal matrices rather than random ones, suggesting the expressivity advantage is not an artifact of random matrix structure. However, the paper does not provide a formal characterisation of which orthogonal matrices the butterfly structure can and cannot approximate well — this would be necessary to understand when BOFT's expressivity limitations become binding for downstream performance.

- **Comparison between BOFT with effective block size 16 and OFT with block size 16 (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4)**: This is the most important controlled comparison for isolating the butterfly structure's contribution. BOFT(m=2,b=8) and OFT(b=16) both operate on $16 \times 16$ blocks — in OFT, each block is a dense orthogonal matrix with independent parameters; in BOFT, each block is the product of two butterfly components that mix within the block in a structured way. Across GLUE (89.89 vs. 89.77), MMLU 5-shot (47.9 vs. 47.5), MMLU 0-shot (46.7 vs. 46.2), GSM8K (50.6 vs. 50.2), MATH (8.6 vs. 8.4), and VTAB-1K (77.4 vs. 77.3), BOFT(m=2,b=8) outperforms OFT(b=16) in every single comparison. The consistency of this pattern across 6 independent evaluations on 4 different model families is strong evidence that the butterfly structure's cross-mixing within blocks provides a genuine and replicable benefit over independent block-diagonal rotations. The margins are small (0.1-0.5 points) but systematic — they never reverse.

- **Negative result: full finetuning underperforms constrained methods (Table 4, Table 1)**: On VTAB-1K, full finetuning (304.4M parameters) achieves only 74.6% average accuracy, substantially below BOFT(m=4,b=4) at 77.9% (1.77M parameters). On GLUE, full finetuning (184M) achieves 88.25 average, below both BOFT(2,8) at 89.89 and OFT(b=16) at 89.77. This is a consistent finding across the paper that aligns with the broader PEFT literature: constrained finetuning often outperforms unconstrained finetuning, especially when the downstream dataset is small relative to the model's capacity. The paper interprets this as validation of the "expressivity vs. regularity tradeoff" — full finetuning has universal expressivity but insufficient regularisation, leading to overfitting.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1 from the paper: BOFT outperforms LoRA and OFT across diverse tasks while using fewer parameters.**

This claim is strongly supported for the specific configurations and model families tested. The evidence spans GLUE (Table 1: 89.89 vs. 88.50 vs. 89.77), MMLU (Table 2: 47.9 vs. 47.3 vs. 47.5), GSM8K (Table 3: 50.6 vs. 50.2 vs. 50.2), MATH (Table 3: 8.6 vs. 7.8 vs. 8.4), VTAB-1K (Table 4: 77.9 vs. 76.6 vs. 77.3), and controllable generation (Table 6: 5.667 vs. 8.038 vs. 6.407). In every single quantitative comparison, BOFT achieves the highest score while using the fewest or near-fewest parameters among the competitive methods.

However, the magnitude of improvement is modest in most cases. The typical margin over LoRA is 0.5-2.0 percentage points on NLU tasks and 0.3-0.8 on MMLU. These are not transformative gains — they represent incremental improvements that would matter in a leaderboard context but may not change practical deployment decisions. The largest relative improvements are on tasks where LoRA performs poorly compared to full finetuning (e.g., structured tasks in VTAB-1K: dSpr-Loc +8.1 points, Clevr-Count +9.8 points), suggesting that orthogonal methods' advantages are most pronounced for tasks that stress the geometric structure of the pretrained features, while for standard semantic classification tasks, the differences are small and potentially within the noise floor of hyperparameter tuning.

A genuine weakness is that the paper compares BOFT against LoRA at specific, fixed rank values (r=4, 8, 16, 32) rather than sweeping to find the best LoRA rank for each task. LoRA's performance can be further improved with rank allocation methods (AdaLoRA, DyLoRA) or with higher ranks at increased parameter budgets, and the paper does not establish that BOFT's advantage persists when LoRA is given its optimal configuration. The AdaLoRA comparison on GLUE (Table 1) partially addresses this — AdaLoRA dynamically allocates rank and achieves 89.46, between LoRA(r=8) at 88.50 and BOFT(2,8) at 89.89 — but AdaLoRA is only tested on GLUE.

A deeper concern: all experiments use pre-trained models from specific families (DeBERTaV3-base, Llama-2-7B, DINOv2-large, SAM, Stable Diffusion 2.1). The consistency of results across these diverse architectures is encouraging, but it does not guarantee that BOFT's advantages would persist on other popular model families (GPT, T5, ViT, CLIP, BLIP) or at different scales (1B, 13B, 70B parameters). The paper provides no scaling analysis — does BOFT's advantage over LoRA grow, shrink, or stay constant as model size increases? Given that the parameter count formula for BOFT is $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ (linear in $d$, independent of model depth), while LoRA's parameter count is $r(d+n)$ per layer, the methods scale differently with model width and depth, and the optimal choice may depend on model architecture in ways the paper does not explore.

**Claim 2: The butterfly structure introduces a beneficial inductive bias that improves generalization beyond what parameter efficiency alone would predict.**

This claim has moderate evidential support but is not conclusively demonstrated. The primary evidence is the comparison between BOFT(m=2,b=8) and OFT(b=16) — both produce block-diagonal orthogonal matrices with effective block size 16, but BOFT's structured cross-mixing within blocks consistently yields better performance (GLUE: +0.12, MMLU 5-shot: +0.4, MMLU 0-shot: +0.5, GSM8K: +0.4, MATH: +0.2). This is consistent with the butterfly structure providing a beneficial inductive bias.

However, the paper does not isolate the inductive bias mechanism from other potential explanations. One alternative: BOFT(m=2,b=8) and OFT(b=16) have different numbers of trainable parameters (0.75M vs. 0.79M on GLUE), and this small difference could account for some of the performance gap. Another alternative: the butterfly factorization might simply provide a better optimisation landscape (easier to train, faster convergence) rather than a better inductive bias for generalisation — the convergence curves in Figure 8 support this interpretation, showing that BOFT not only achieves lower final error but converges faster, which is an optimisation property rather than a generalisation property.

A direct test of the inductive bias claim would require: (a) comparing BOFT against a method with identical expressivity but no butterfly structure (difficult, since it's unclear what "identical expressivity without butterfly" means), or (b) demonstrating that BOFT's advantage is largest when training data is scarce and generalisation is hardest. The paper's VTAB-1K results (1000 training samples per task) partially support (b): BOFT's advantages over LoRA are largest on VTAB-1K (+1.3 points average) compared to GLUE (+1.39 points on a much larger dataset), but the comparison is across different modalities and model families, so the data-scarcity hypothesis cannot be tested cleanly.

The paper's theoretical argument — that butterfly matrices can represent classic linear transforms (FFT, DCT, Hadamard) and thus bias learning toward "natural" transformations — is plausible but essentially a post-hoc rationalisation. No experiment tests whether BOFT actually learns transformations resembling these classic transforms, or whether the ability to represent them is causally responsible for the observed performance gains.

**Claim 3: BOFT provides a smooth interpolation between identity and full orthogonal matrices, enabling better control of the expressivity-regularity tradeoff.**

This claim is well-supported by Figure 8 (monotonic improvement with increasing $m$) and Figure 10 (smooth semantic interpolation through component-wise identity replacement). The convergence analysis in Figure 8 is particularly strong: the curves are strictly ordered by $m$, with no crossing, which is exactly what one would expect if $m$ provides a monotonic expressivity knob — more components strictly increase the hypothesis class without introducing harmful degrees of freedom that could cause overfitting.

However, the paper does not demonstrate that this smooth interpolation actually leads to better *downstream selection* of the optimal configuration. All reported results use manually chosen $(m, b)$ values that match the parameter budget of baselines — there is no experiment showing that a practitioner can use the smooth interpolation to find a better configuration than they could with the discrete block-size choices of OFT. The claim about the tradeoff being "better" is therefore about the *structure* of the hypothesis space rather than about *practical outcomes* — the available configurations are more granular, but whether this granularity translates to better final performance after hyperparameter selection is not tested.

**Claim 4: BOFT enables OFT to be applied as a general-purpose PEFT method beyond diffusion models.**

This claim is not a single experimental result but the cumulative implication of Sections 6.1-6.3. The evidence is convincing for the breadth of application: BOFT has been demonstrated on language understanding, knowledge probing, mathematical reasoning, visual classification, segmentation, and generative control — spanning discriminative and generative tasks, text and vision modalities, encoder-only and decoder-only architectures. This is a substantially broader evaluation than the original OFT paper, which only tested on diffusion models.

A limitation is that all evaluations use relatively small downstream datasets or few-shot settings — GLUE (sentence classification with 2.5k-393k training examples), VTAB-1K (1000 examples per task), MMLU (evaluated after finetuning on Alpaca, which is ~52k instruction-following examples). The paper does not evaluate BOFT on large-scale finetuning scenarios (e.g., finetuning Llama-2-7B on hundreds of thousands of examples for a specific production task) where overfitting is less of a concern and full finetuning or LoRA with higher rank might be preferable. The consistent finding that constrained methods outperform full finetuning (Tables 1, 4) may be an artifact of the small-data regime — on large-scale finetuning, the regularisation from orthogonality might become a bottleneck rather than a benefit.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

1. **Scaling analysis with model size.** How does BOFT's advantage over LoRA change as the base model grows from 300M to 7B to 70B parameters? The paper tests two scales (DeBERTaV3-base at 184M, Llama-2-7B) but does not systematically vary model size within a single architecture family.

2. **Scaling analysis with dataset size.** All experiments use fixed, relatively small downstream datasets. How does BOFT perform when finetuned on 10k, 100k, or 1M examples? Does the advantage over LoRA shrink as data increases (consistent with the regularisation explanation) or persist?

3. **Ablation of the Cayley parameterisation.** What if orthogonal matrices were enforced via a penalty term (e.g., $||R^\top R - I||_F$) rather than the Cayley parameterisation? This would help separate the contribution of exact orthogonality from the butterfly structure itself.

4. **Comparison against LoRA with equivalent "effective expressivity".** LoRA's rank controls expressivity; BOFT's $(m,b)$ controls it. A fair comparison would sweep both methods' hyperparameters over a range of expressivity levels (not just parameter counts) and plot performance vs. expressivity. Parameter count is a proxy for expressivity, but an imperfect one.

5. **Ablation of the block-diagonal structure within butterfly components.** The butterfly components $\tilde{B}_b(d, k)$ are block-diagonal with block size $k$. What if they were dense within each $k \times k$ block (i.e., full $b \cdot k \times b \cdot k$ orthogonal blocks rather than butterfly-structured)? This would isolate whether the benefit comes from the hierarchical butterfly mixing pattern or simply from having more parameters within each component.

6. **Training time comparison.** The paper acknowledges that BOFT has "slightly larger" training runtime overhead than OFT due to sequential matrix multiplications, but provides no numbers. A wall-clock time comparison for a full training run would help practitioners weigh the accuracy gains against the computational cost.

7. **Confidence intervals or standard deviations for all main results.** The paper states that gains are significant at p < 0.05 but only reports point estimates. For results where the margin is 0.1-0.5 points (most of the GLUE and MMLU comparisons), knowing the variance across the 5 random seeds is essential for assessing reliability.

**Summary of evidential strength.** The paper provides solid empirical evidence that BOFT is a competitive PEFT method that consistently matches or modestly exceeds LoRA and OFT across a wide range of tasks, often with fewer parameters. The evidence for the butterfly structure specifically providing a beneficial inductive bias beyond what parameter efficiency alone would predict is suggestive but not definitive — the margins are small and alternative explanations (optimisation benefits, parameter count differences) cannot be ruled out with the reported experiments. The paper's strongest contribution is demonstrating that orthogonal finetuning is viable as a general-purpose method, not just a diffusion-model technique; the specific mechanism (butterfly factorisation) is one effective way to achieve this, but whether it is the *optimal* way or simply a good one remains an open question.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for, Undermining the 4× Efficiency Claim

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's compute-optimal framework rests on estimating every prompt's difficulty before allocating the inference budget. The method used — generating 2048 samples per prompt and averaging either ground-truth correctness scores or the PRM's final-answer predictions — is extraordinarily expensive, consuming more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations). The paper acknowledges this explicitly in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence.** The reported 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N (Figures 4 and 8) are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. The paper's own numbers illustrate the problem: at 2048 samples per prompt for difficulty estimation, the estimation step alone costs 8× more than the largest budget where the 4× claim is demonstrated (256 generations). In a realistic deployment, total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution, and the former would dominate the latter. The 4× figure should therefore be understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency conditional on free difficulty information**, not a realized deployment gain. A practitioner cannot expect to achieve anything close to 4× improvement without a cheaper difficulty estimator — and none is provided.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper itself provides the numbers that expose this gap: difficulty estimation uses 2048 samples (Section 3.2), while the largest test-time budgets showing 4× gains are 256 generations (Figure 4: 16 generations matching 64; Figure 8: 64 generations matching 256). The predicted-difficulty variant (using PRM scores rather than ground-truth labels) does not reduce the sample cost — it still requires 2048 generations per prompt, merely removing the need for answer labels. Section 3.2 flags this as an "exploration-exploitation tradeoff" but provides no experimental exploration of cheaper alternatives.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper suggests future work on "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" (Section 8) but develops no such model and runs no experiments with reduced estimation budgets. An adaptive approach — starting with a few samples, assessing difficulty, and allocating the remaining budget accordingly — is mentioned as conceptually appealing but not implemented. Until this gap is closed, the compute-optimal framework is a useful conceptual tool for understanding the scaling landscape but not a deployable system.

### Verifier Over-Optimization Is a Hard Ceiling, Not a Solved Problem

**The assumption or constraint.** All search-based methods depend on the PRM's step-level correctness scores to guide selection, and the PRM — like all learned verifiers — can be exploited. The paper documents this phenomenon extensively: beam search *degrades* accuracy on easy problems at high budgets (Figure 3, right, bin 1: accuracy drops from ~78% to ~77% as budget increases from 4 to 256 generations), lookahead search — the most powerful optimizer — paradoxically performs *worst* overall at the same generation budget (Figure 3, left), and qualitative examples in Appendix M show degenerate outputs (repetitive low-information steps, overly short solutions) that score highly under the PRM but are incorrect.

**The consequence.** The compute-optimal policy *mitigates* over-optimization by routing easy problems away from aggressive search (using best-of-N instead of beam search), but it does not *solve* the underlying problem. On medium-difficulty problems where beam search is deployed, over-optimization still limits the scaling ceiling — the beam search curves in Figure 3 flatten and in some cases decline well before the budget is exhausted. This means the approach is fundamentally bounded by verifier quality: no amount of clever allocation can extract reliable signal from an unreliable verifier, and the PRM's reliability limits how far test-time compute scaling can go on any difficulty tier. The problem is particularly acute because the paper's own results show that training a better PRM is non-trivial — the publicly available PRM800k dataset was "largely ineffective" for their PaLM 2 models due to distribution shift (Section 5.1), forcing the authors to train their own PRM with Monte Carlo rollouts, which is itself expensive and model-specific.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 (right, bins 1–2) provides the clearest evidence: beam search over-optimizes on easy problems, with accuracy decreasing at high budgets. Figure 3 (left) shows lookahead search underperforming all other methods, indicating that more powerful optimization amplifies rather than mitigates verifier exploitation. Appendix M provides qualitative examples of degenerate solutions that exploit the PRM. The paper's own compute-optimal results (Figure 4) implicitly acknowledge the problem: the optimal policy avoids beam search on easy problems precisely to stay below the over-optimization threshold.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through routing — the compute-optimal policy keeps easy problems on best-of-N where over-optimization is minimal. But this is avoidance, not a solution. The paper does not explore verifier robustness improvements (adversarial training, ensemble methods, calibration techniques) that might push the over-optimization threshold higher, nor does it characterize how verifier quality improvements would shift the optimal policy. Section 8 acknowledges this implicitly by calling for future work on verifier robustness, but the current results are specific to the PRM quality achievable with the Monte Carlo rollout training procedure described in Appendix D.

### Single Benchmark, Single Model Family Limits Generality

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions) with PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The authors state they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this is an untested assumption. The paper provides no replication on other reasoning benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K for multi-step arithmetic, ARC for science reasoning, HumanEval for code generation), no experiments with other model families (GPT, LLaMA, Mistral), and no analysis of how findings might change with model scale (PaLM 2-S* is a specific size within the PaLM 2 family).

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or benchmark-specific. The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend on the base model's output distribution — a model with different calibration properties or different error patterns might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves. The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families. The MATH benchmark consists exclusively of competition-level math problems requiring symbolic reasoning — it is unclear whether the difficulty-dependent patterns (beam search hurting easy problems, revisions helping easy problems) generalize to other reasoning domains or to tasks requiring factual knowledge rather than inference. The test set of 500 questions, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each, then further split by two-fold cross-validation, means the compute-optimal policy is **selected based on ~50 questions per fold per bin** (Section 3.2). This is a small sample, and the paper provides no confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves to assess whether observed gains are statistically stable.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The entire experimental section (Sections 5–7) uses MATH and PaLM 2-S* exclusively. No ablation studies test sensitivity to the choice of benchmark or model. The cross-validation protocol (Section 3.2, two-fold within each difficulty bin on the 500-question test set) is described but its statistical implications for policy selection reliability are not discussed.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The authors acknowledge in Section 8 that extending to other domains is future work but provide no evidence that the current findings transfer. The paper's strongest claims — 4× efficiency improvement, compute-optimal policies, the difficulty-dependent reversal of beam search vs. best-of-N — must be understood as established for one specific setting (math reasoning with PaLM 2-S*) until replication studies demonstrate otherwise.

### The 14× Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained, Weakening the FLOPs-Matched Comparison

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023) rather than compute-optimal pretraining (Hoffmann et al., 2022) where both data and parameters are scaled equally. The paper is transparent about this:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work." (Section 7)

Additionally, the 14× larger model uses only greedy decoding — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search of any kind — while the smaller model benefits from compute-optimal test-time strategies selected with cross-validation.

**The consequence.** The pretraining baseline is weaker than it should be on two fronts. First, a Chinchilla-optimal model trained with 14× more total FLOPs would allocate some of that budget to additional training data rather than only to parameters, likely producing a stronger model than the parameter-only-scaled version used here. Second, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget (say, best-of-8 or best-of-16) would create a much fairer comparison — the question is not "test-time compute vs. no test-time compute" but "how should total FLOPs be split between pretraining and inference?" The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining (e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on easy-to-medium problems at R ≪ 1, Figure 1 top-right bar chart) therefore overstate the case for test-time compute relative to a properly optimized pretraining baseline.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The FLOPs accounting in Section 7 explicitly uses $X = 6ND_{\text{pretrain}}$ for pretraining FLOPs and $Y = 2ND_{\text{inference}}$ for inference FLOPs, and the 14× figure is described as parameter scaling. The paper mentions the compute-optimal pretraining caveat in Section 7 but provides no sensitivity analysis — no results showing how the comparison would change if the larger model were Chinchilla-trained, and no results giving the larger model any test-time budget of its own.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged but not addressed experimentally. The paper frames the compute-optimal pretraining comparison as future work (Section 7, Section 8). This is a reasonable scoping decision for a first paper on the topic, but it means the FLOPs-matched conclusions should be interpreted as **preliminary evidence that test-time compute *can* be competitive with pretraining in specific regimes**, not as a definitive ranking of the two approaches.

### Sequential Revisions Introduce Latency That Is Not Accounted for, Limiting Practical Applicability

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures compute in "generations" (number of complete solutions sampled), which is a reasonable proxy for total FLOPs but ignores wall-clock latency. Sequential revisions are inherently serial — each revision depends on the previous one, so a chain of 64 revisions requires 64 sequential forward passes through the model. Parallel best-of-N with 64 samples can be executed simultaneously given sufficient hardware (or in large batches), completing in roughly the time of a single forward pass plus selection overhead. The paper does not discuss this tradeoff.

**The consequence.** The compute-optimal policy's recommendations for easy problems — which favor purely sequential revisions (Figure 7, right, bin 1) — would be **impractical for latency-sensitive applications**. A strategy that allocates 128 generations as 64 sequential × 2 parallel takes roughly 64× longer wall-clock time than one that runs 128 parallel samples simultaneously, even though both consume similar total FLOPs. For interactive assistants, real-time decision-making, or any deployment where users wait for responses, the sequential-heavy strategies that the compute-optimal policy selects for easy and medium problems would be unacceptable regardless of their FLOPs efficiency. The paper's 4× efficiency gains are measured in generations (a throughput metric) and do not translate to 4× latency improvements for the sequential strategies.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 7 (right) shows that easy questions (bin 1) are essentially insensitive to the sequential-to-parallel ratio, suggesting parallel strategies would work nearly as well — but the compute-optimal policy for revisions (Figure 8) still selects sequential-heavy configurations based on accuracy alone, with no latency constraint in the optimization. The paper's formal objective (Equation 1, Section 3.1) optimizes only for expected correctness, not for correctness subject to a latency budget. The "generations" metric is defined in Section 5.3 as the unit of test-time compute, with no discussion of how sequential dependencies affect wall-clock time.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not report wall-clock times for any experiment, does not discuss the serial nature of revisions as a limitation, and does not include latency in the compute-optimal objective. A latency-aware formulation — e.g., optimizing accuracy subject to a maximum number of sequential steps — would likely produce different (less sequential-heavy) policies for easy problems, but this is not explored.

### Hard Problems Remain Essentially Unsolved, Revealing a Fundamental Capability Bound

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire framework assumes that test-time compute can amplify the base model's existing capabilities but cannot create capabilities from nothing. This is not an oversight — it is an inherent limitation of any method that only rearranges the base model's output distribution — but the paper's results quantify just how stark this boundary is.

**The consequence.** Across all methods — search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations — the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5) show **near-zero improvement** regardless of compute budget. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all methods and all budgets up to 256 generations. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%, well below the 14× larger model's performance. The paper is candid about this (Section 7: "test-time compute is only effective on problems where the base model already has some non-trivial chance of success"), but the implication is sobering: for any problem genuinely outside the base model's training distribution or capability range — novel reasoning patterns, out-of-distribution tasks, problems requiring fundamentally different approaches than those seen during pretraining — test-time compute offers no path forward. Pretraining remains the only viable route to new capabilities.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Difficulty bin 5 consistently shows flat or near-flat scaling curves across Figures 3 (right), 7 (right), and 9. The FLOPs-matched bar charts in Figure 1 show test-time compute with the smaller model underperforming the 14× larger model by −37.2% (revisions) and −52.9% (PRM search) on hard problems at high inference-to-pretraining ratios. The paper explicitly states in the Section 7 takeaway box that test-time compute "cannot easily compensate for a lack of pretraining" on hard problems.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged transparently but not addressed — and likely cannot be addressed within the test-time compute paradigm. The paper frames this as a boundary condition rather than a failure mode, which is scientifically appropriate, but it means the method offers no improvement on the problems where improvement is most needed. This is a fundamental tradeoff: test-time compute is a "free lunch" only for problems the model already partially understands, and the lunch becomes progressively more expensive (and eventually impossible to obtain at any price) as problem difficulty increases.
