# JetMoE: Reaching Llama2 Performance with 0.1M Dollars

**ArXiv:** [2404.07413](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07413)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **JetMoE-8B**, a new large language model that achieves performance competitive with Llama2-7B while being trained for under $0.1 million using only 1.25T tokens of public data and 30,000 H100 GPU hours.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **JetMoE-8B**, a new large language model that achieves performance competitive with Llama2-7B while being trained for under $0.1 million using only 1.25T tokens of public data and 30,000 H100 GPU hours. The model employs a **Sparsely-gated Mixture-of-Experts** (SMoE) architecture with sparse activation in both attention and feed-forward layers — attention experts share key and value projections for stability while the router selects only two of eight experts per token — yielding 8B total parameters but only 2.2B activated per input token (reducing inference computation by approximately 70% compared to Llama2-7B). On the OpenLLM Leaderboard, JetMoE-8B achieves a 53.0 average score, outperforming Llama2-7B (51.0), Gemma-2B (46.4), and DeepseekMoE-16B (51.1), while JetMoE-8B-Chat reaches a 6.681 MT-Bench score — surpassing Llama2-13B-Chat (6.650) — establishing that cost-effective LLM training with sparse attention and feed-forward experts can match much larger dense models only when training data mixtures are carefully phased with higher-quality data concentrated in the learning rate decay stage.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: State-of-the-Art LLMs Are Financially Inaccessible

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **training a competitive large language model costs far too much money for most research institutions and startups to participate**. The paper opens with a direct statement of this tension — LLMs have "achieved remarkable results" but their "increasing resource demand has become a major obstacle to the development of powerful and accessible super-human intelligence." This is not just a financial complaint; it represents a structural barrier that determines *who gets to build frontier AI systems* and, by extension, *whose priorities and values shape those systems*.

The scale of the cost problem has escalated dramatically over the past few years. While the paper doesn't enumerate exact costs of competing models, the broader landscape is well-known: GPT-3's training was estimated at several million dollars, GPT-4 and Gemini-level models likely cost hundreds of millions, and even "open" dense models like Llama2-7B — which the paper uses as its primary benchmark — require compute budgets that remain out of reach for most academic labs. The paper implicitly draws a line: at $0.1M (approximately 1% of what GPT-4-level training budgets are estimated to cost), JetMoE-8B attempts to demonstrate what's possible at a price point that a well-funded academic lab, a small startup, or a national research institute could realistically afford.

This matters for several reasons that go beyond mere cost savings:

- **Democratization of research**: When only large corporations can train competitive models, the research agenda is shaped by corporate priorities — product integration, user engagement, monetization. Academic researchers studying model behavior, safety, fairness, or linguistic phenomena are forced to either use stale open-source models (often months or years behind the frontier) or study black-box APIs where they cannot inspect internals. A model that can be reproduced and studied fully in an academic setting reopens questions that proprietary development forecloses.

- **Reproducibility crisis in LLM research**: The paper notes explicitly that even nominally "open" MoE models like DeepseekMoE, Mixtral 8x7B, Grok-1, and DBRX "are not truly open-sourced as the training recipes are not published and may contain proprietary datasets inaccessible outside of large corporations." This creates a reproducibility gap: researchers can download model weights but cannot verify claims about training methodology, cannot isolate which design choices caused which behaviors, and cannot build directly on the training pipeline. JetMoE-8B positions itself as a response to this by providing "all training parameters and data mixtures" — the recipe, not just the cake.

- **Environmental and infrastructure equity**: Training costs are a proxy for energy consumption and hardware requirements. Reducing the cost to train a competitive model by an order of magnitude or more (the paper doesn't claim a specific multiple, but $0.1M is substantially below even conservative estimates for Llama2-7B's training cost) correspondingly reduces the carbon footprint and hardware barrier to entry. This is particularly relevant for researchers in regions with limited access to GPU clusters.

### The Efficiency Gap: Dense Models Waste Computation

Beyond the absolute cost problem, the paper identifies a specific architectural inefficiency in the dominant paradigm. Most LLMs — including Llama, Pythia, GPT-3, and Mistral — are **dense models** that "use all of their parameters during inference and training." This means that for every token processed, the entire parameter set is activated: every weight matrix is multiplied, every neuron computes its activation, regardless of whether that particular token needs that particular computation.

This is wasteful in a deep sense. Natural language is highly heterogeneous: a token in a mathematical derivation requires different processing than a token in a narrative dialogue, which differs from a token in Python code. A dense model must allocate the same computational capacity to all of these, meaning it either over-provisions for simple cases (wasting compute) or under-provisions for complex ones (sacrificing quality). The paper frames the MoE architecture as a response to this: by sparsely activating only a subset of parameters per token, the model can specialize different experts for different types of linguistic computation while keeping total FLOPs per token low.

The specific efficiency claim the paper makes is quantitative: JetMoE-8B has 8B total parameters but activates only 2.2B per token, reducing inference computation by "about 70% compared to Llama2-7B." This is significant because it means JetMoE-8B is not just cheaper to train — it's cheaper to *deploy*, which matters for applications where inference costs dominate (chatbots, API services, on-device deployment). The paper's architecture thus attacks both sides of the cost equation: training cost (through efficient sparse training) and inference cost (through sparse activation).

### Prior MoE Work: Strong Performance, Weak Openness

The paper situates itself against two categories of prior work on MoE models, and finds both wanting in different ways.

**Proprietary or semi-open MoE models perform well but are irreproducible.** The paper cites DeepseekMoE (Dai et al., 2024), Mixtral 8x7B (Jiang et al., 2024), Grok-1 (xai-org, 2024), and DBRX (Databricks, 2024) as examples of MoE architectures that "achieve excellent performance" but whose "training recipes are not published and may contain proprietary datasets." This is a crucial distinction: the weights may be downloadable, but without knowing the training data composition, the hyperparameter schedule, the data filtering pipeline, or the alignment procedure, the scientific contribution is only partially accessible. You can *use* Mixtral, but you cannot *understand* why it works, *reproduce* it from scratch, or *isolate* the contribution of architecture versus data versus training recipe.

**Open-source MoE attempts have underperformed.** On the other side, the paper points to OpenMoE (Xue et al., 2024) as the primary open-source MoE effort, noting that its "performance is only on par with weak dense models with similar activation parameters, such as OpenLLaMA and TinyLLaMA." This is a damning assessment: if an MoE model with 8B total parameters performs like a 2B dense model, then the extra parameters are effectively dead weight — they add memory and communication overhead without improving outputs. The open-source community had not yet demonstrated that MoE architectures could match the dense model performance curve, which means the claimed efficiency benefits of MoE remained theoretical rather than demonstrated in openly-verifiable settings.

This creates a specific gap that JetMoE-8B aims to fill: **an MoE model that is simultaneously (1) fully open-source in training recipe and data, and (2) competitive with strong dense models.** The paper's positioning is essentially: we are doing for MoE what the open-source community has done for dense models (reproducing and sometimes exceeding proprietary performance), but at a price point that makes the result accessible.

### The Attention Bottleneck in Standard MoE

A more technical gap the paper identifies concerns *where* sparsity is applied in existing MoE architectures. Most MoE models — including the ones cited above — apply sparse activation **only to the feed-forward layers (FFD)**, leaving the attention layers dense. This is the standard design inherited from Shazeer et al. (2017) and the original MoE transformer implementations. The feed-forward layers are the most parameter-heavy component of a transformer (typically accounting for roughly two-thirds of parameters), so replacing them with MoE layers captures most of the parameter-efficiency benefit.

However, the paper argues — through its architectural design choices, building on ModuleFormer (Shen et al., 2023) — that this leaves a bottleneck: as you scale up the number of feed-forward experts and the model's total parameter count, the attention layers, which are still dense, become the performance bottleneck. They consume a fixed computational budget per token regardless of how many total parameters the model has, which means that at very high expert counts, attention computation dominates and the benefits of sparse feed-forward layers diminish.

The solution, which JetMoE-8B inherits from ModuleFormer and extends, is to apply sparse activation to **both** attention and feed-forward layers. This is the Mixture of Attention Heads (MoA) approach, where different attention experts learn different attention patterns and the router selects which ones to activate per token. The paper's innovation within this framework is the sharing of key-value projections across attention experts — a practical stabilization technique that reduces parameters and memory while preventing the training instability that can arise when multiple attention experts independently learn similar key-value mappings.

This dual sparsity is what allows JetMoE-8B to claim such a high ratio of total-to-active parameters (8B:2.2B, approximately 3.6:1) while maintaining training stability. A standard MoE with sparse feed-forward only would need more active parameters to achieve comparable performance, reducing the inference efficiency gain.

### The Data Quality Problem and Phased Training

A final gap the paper addresses — implicitly through its methodology rather than through explicit critique — concerns how training data is scheduled during pretraining. The dominant paradigm in most LLM training runs is to train on a fixed data mixture throughout, with only the learning rate varying. The paper instead adopts a two-phase approach, following miniCPM (Hu et al., 2024) and Gemma (Team et al., 2024):

- **Phase 1 (warmup and stable learning rate, ~1T tokens)**: A broad mixture of web data, code, and math focused on quantity and diversity.
- **Phase 2 (learning rate decay, ~250B tokens)**: A curated mixture of higher-quality data including synthetic datasets, textbooks, and instruction-formatted conversations.

The motivation is straightforward: early in training, the model needs diverse exposure to learn general linguistic patterns and factual knowledge, and web-scale data is appropriate. Late in training, when the model is solidifying its representations and the learning rate is dropping, higher-quality curated data helps the model refine its outputs toward more useful, instruction-following, and accurate behavior. The paper doesn't claim this is novel — it explicitly credits prior work — but it does position this as a critical ingredient in achieving the cost-performance tradeoff. Without the phased data strategy, the same compute budget would likely produce a weaker model, because the final annealing stage disproportionately influences downstream task performance.

### How JetMoE-8B Positions Itself

The paper's positioning can be understood as occupying a specific, previously empty point in a three-dimensional space defined by:

1. **Performance**: Must match or exceed Llama2-7B, the standard open-source dense baseline.
2. **Openness**: Full disclosure of architecture, training data, hyperparameters, and training code — going beyond weight releases to enable full reproduction.
3. **Cost**: Under $0.1M total training budget, making reproduction feasible for academic labs and small companies.

Prior work occupies various edges of this space: Llama2 is strong and moderately open (weights released, some training details) but expensive to train; Mixtral is strong and has open weights but closed training recipe; OpenMoE is open but weak; GPT-3 is strong but fully proprietary. JetMoE-8B aims to be the first model that is simultaneously strong, fully open, and cheap — a combination that, if validated, would represent a genuinely new category of research artifact.

The paper also positions itself as a **platform for future research** rather than a final product. The explicit framing is that by providing "all training parameters and data mixtures," the work aims to "encourage collaboration and further advancements in the field of accessible and efficient LLMs." The limitations section (Section 7) reinforces this by acknowledging that no ablation studies were performed due to budget constraints — the model is a starting point for the community to analyze, reproduce, and improve, not a finished optimization.

This is a meaningful distinction. Many corporate model releases are endpoints — optimized products with undisclosed development processes. JetMoE-8B is closer to a research baseline: a fully-documented configuration that others can use as a reference point for studying MoE training dynamics, data mixture effects, or the interaction between sparse attention and downstream task performance. Its value is not just in the model itself but in the reproducible training procedure it establishes.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The system being built is a **large language model called JetMoE-8B** that processes text through a transformer architecture where only a small fraction of the total parameters are activated for any given input token. The problem it solves is the prohibitive cost of training and running competitive LLMs — the "shape" of the solution is a sparsely-activated architecture that expands total parameter count (for capacity) while keeping per-token computation low (for efficiency), trained on a carefully phased mixture of entirely public datasets with full transparency about every design choice.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The JetMoE-8B system has four major components:

1. **Sparsely-gated Mixture-of-Experts (SMoE) Layers** — every transformer block contains two types of expert layers (attention and feed-forward), each with 8 experts but only 2 activated per token via a learned router. This is the core efficiency mechanism.

2. **Attention Experts with Shared KV Projections** — each attention expert has its own query and output projections but shares key and value projections across all experts in a layer, reducing parameters while enabling diverse attention patterns.

3. **Feed-Forward Experts** — standard SwiGLU MLP experts that each learn different processing specializations, selected by the same router mechanism.

4. **Two-Phase Training Data Pipeline** — a broad web-scale mixture (Phase 1, ~1T tokens) followed by a curated high-quality mixture (Phase 2, ~250B tokens during learning rate decay), with explicit dataset percentages and sources.

Information flows as follows: a token enters the model → the router computes affinity scores for all 8 attention experts and all 8 feed-forward experts → only the top-2 scoring experts in each layer are activated → the token is processed through the selected attention expert (using shared KV projections) and the selected feed-forward expert → the outputs are weighted by the router probabilities and summed → the process repeats for all 24 transformer layers.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the SMoE mechanism — the router, top-k selection, and how sparse activation achieves the total-vs-active parameter gap — because every subsequent component depends on understanding this routing logic.
- **Second**, the feed-forward experts, which are architecturally simpler and serve as the baseline for how experts process tokens once selected.
- **Third**, the attention experts with shared KV projections, which is the architectural novelty and requires understanding both standard multi-head attention and how sparsity changes it.
- **Fourth**, the load balancing losses (auxiliary loss and z-loss), which are essential for making sparse training work in practice and prevent mode collapse.
- **Fifth**, the training infrastructure choices (pipeline parallelism over expert parallelism, Megatron + Megablocks) because they explain how the architecture is actually executed at scale.
- **Sixth**, the hyperparameters and two-phase data schedule, which tie the architecture to the training recipe and are critical for reproducibility.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **architecture and training methodology paper** whose core idea is that applying sparse activation to both attention and feed-forward layers — combined with a carefully phased, fully open-source data mixture — enables competitive LLM performance at roughly an order of magnitude lower cost than comparable dense models.

#### The Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (SMoE) Mechanism

The SMoE layer is the fundamental building block that enables JetMoE-8B to have 8B total parameters while only activating 2.2B per token. Understanding it requires working through three sub-mechanisms: the router, the top-k selection, and the weighted combination.

**Router computation.** Every SMoE layer contains a router — a single linear transformation that maps the input token representation to a vector of scores, one per expert. Formally, given an input vector `$x \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{\text{emb}}}$` where `$D_{\text{emb}} = 2048$`, the router computes:

$$s = W_{\text{rtr}} x$$

where `$W_{\text{rtr}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D_{\text{emb}}}$` is the expert embedding matrix (also called the router weight matrix), `$N = 8$` is the number of experts per layer, and `$s \in \mathbb{R}^8$` is the vector of raw affinity scores (logits) indicating how relevant each expert is to the current token.

**What it computes:** For each of the 8 experts in the layer, the router takes the dot product between the token's `$D_{\text{emb}}$`-dimensional representation and that expert's learned embedding vector. The result is 8 scalar scores — higher scores mean the expert is more relevant to the token. This is a simple linear operation with no activation function; all non-linearity comes from the subsequent top-k and softmax steps.

**Why this form:** A linear router is computationally cheap (costing `$N \times D_{\text{emb}}$` multiply-adds per token) and allows the experts themselves to develop distinct "specializations" that the router learns to recognize. Using a deeper or non-linear router would add latency that defeats the purpose of sparse activation, since the router is on the critical path for every token. The linear form also makes the load balancing loss tractable (discussed later), since the router logits directly control expert assignment.

**Top-k sparse gating.** The raw scores `$s$` are converted to a sparse probability distribution through a two-step process:

$$g(e \mid x) = \begin{cases} \text{softmax}(\text{Top}_k(s))_i, & s_i \in \text{Top}_k(s) \\ 0, & s_i \notin \text{Top}_k(s) \end{cases}$$

where `$\text{Top}_k$` is the operator that selects the indices of the `$k = 2$` largest values from `$s$`, and `$g(e \mid x)$` is the gating weight (a scalar between 0 and 1) assigned to expert `$e$` for input `$x$`.

**What it computes:** First, the router identifies which 2 experts out of 8 have the highest affinity scores for this token — these are the only experts that will process the token. Second, it applies softmax *only over those 2 scores* (not over all 8), converting them to a probability distribution where the two activated experts each receive a non-zero weight that sums to 1. All 6 unselected experts receive a weight of exactly 0, meaning their computation is entirely skipped for this token.

**Why this form:** The key property is that `$g(e \mid x) = 0$` for unselected experts enables *conditional computation* — the forward pass through those experts' parameters is never executed, saving FLOPs. The softmax over only the top-k ensures that the weights are normalized and differentiable with respect to the selected experts' scores (enabling gradient-based training), while the hard zeroing of unselected experts is non-differentiable but acceptable because the router only needs gradients through the selected paths. Using `$k = 2$` means the model activates `$2/8 = 25\%$` of experts per layer, which the paper sets as a hyperparameter chosen to approximately double the computation of a comparable 1B-parameter dense model (since each token goes through 2 experts instead of 1 dense equivalent).

An important subtlety: the gating weights `$g(e \mid x)$` are used to *weight the outputs* of the selected experts, not to weight the inputs. Each selected expert receives the same input `$x$` and produces its full output; the router only controls how much each expert's output contributes to the final result.

**Expert output combination.** Once the two selected experts have processed the token, their outputs are combined:

$$y = \sum_{e=1}^{N} g(e \mid x) \cdot f_e(x)$$

where `$f_e(x)$` is the output of expert `$e$` when given input `$x$`, and `$y$` is the final output of the SMoE layer.

**What it computes:** For each of the 8 experts, if `$g(e \mid x) = 0$` (the expert was not selected), the term contributes nothing and `$f_e(x)$` was never computed — this is where the computational savings come from. For the 2 selected experts, their outputs are multiplied by their respective gating weights (which sum to 1) and added together. The result `$y$` is a weighted mixture of the two experts' outputs, with the mixing proportions determined by the router's confidence in each expert.

**Why this form:** The weighted sum is the standard MoE combination mechanism (from Shazeer et al., 2017) and has two important properties. First, it is fully differentiable with respect to both the expert parameters (through `$f_e(x)$`) and the router parameters (through `$g(e \mid x)$`), enabling end-to-end gradient-based training. Second, it allows the model to *interpolate* between expert behaviors — if a token is ambiguous between two specializations, both experts contribute proportionally to their relevance, producing a blended output. The alternative (hard selection, picking only one expert) would be less expressive and would make load balancing harder.

**The per-layer duplication.** JetMoE-8B applies this SMoE mechanism *twice per transformer block* — once for the attention experts and once for the feed-forward experts — each with its own independent router. This means every token goes through two separate routing decisions per layer: one selecting 2 of 8 attention experts, one selecting 2 of 8 feed-forward experts. The routers do not share parameters across layers or across attention/feed-forward within a layer, allowing different layers to develop different expert specializations. Across all 24 layers, there are `$24 \times 8 \times 2 = 384$` total experts (192 attention + 192 feed-forward), but any given token only activates `$24 \times 2 \times 2 = 96$` of them, achieving the 8B-to-2.2B active parameter ratio.

#### Feed-Forward Experts

Each feed-forward expert is architecturally simple — a standard 2-layer MLP with a SwiGLU activation — but the interaction between multiple experts and the router creates the specialization dynamic.

**Expert architecture.** For a given expert `$e$`, the feed-forward computation is:

$$f_{\text{mlp}}(x) = W_{\text{out}} \, \sigma(W_{\text{in}} x)$$

where `$W_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{2D_{\text{ffd}} \times D_{\text{emb}}}$` is the input projection matrix, `$W_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{\text{emb}} \times D_{\text{ffd}}}$` is the output projection matrix, `$\sigma$` is the SwiGLU activation function, `$D_{\text{ffd}} = 5632$` is the feed-forward hidden dimension, and `$D_{\text{emb}} = 2048$` is the model dimension.

The SwiGLU activation deserves explanation. A standard SwiGLU splits the input projection into two halves: `$W_{\text{in}} x$` produces a vector of size `$2D_{\text{ffd}}$`, which is split into two `$D_{\text{ffd}}$`-dimensional vectors `$a$` and `$b$`. The activation computes `$a \otimes \text{SiLU}(b)$` where `$\otimes$` is element-wise multiplication and SiLU (Sigmoid Linear Unit) is `$z \cdot \sigma(z)$`. This is then projected back to `$D_{\text{emb}}$` by `$W_{\text{out}}$`.

**What it computes:** For a selected expert, the input token representation `$x$` (size 2048) is first projected to a higher-dimensional space (size `$2 \times 5632 = 11264$`), then gated through the SwiGLU non-linearity (which provides both a multiplicative gate and a non-linear activation), then projected back down to 2048 dimensions. The output is a transformed representation of the token that the expert has learned to specialize in — for example, one expert might learn to process mathematical notation while another learns narrative prose.

**Why this form:** The SwiGLU activation (introduced by Shazeer, 2020) has become the standard for modern LLMs because it empirically outperforms ReLU and GELU on language modeling tasks while adding only a modest computational overhead. The `$2D_{\text{ffd}}$` input dimension (rather than `$D_{\text{ffd}}$`) accounts for the gating mechanism splitting the projection in half. The hidden dimension `$D_{\text{ffd}} = 5632$` is set so that each expert has approximately the capacity of a 1B-parameter model's feed-forward layer, and with top-2 activation the total feed-forward computation per token is roughly equivalent to a 2B dense model's feed-forward layer.

**Expert specialization.** The 8 feed-forward experts per layer are not manually assigned to tasks — specialization emerges from training. The router learns to send tokens with certain linguistic properties (mathematical notation, code syntax, narrative structure) to specific experts, and those experts' parameters adapt to process those token types effectively. The load balancing loss (discussed later) ensures that all experts get used roughly equally across the training data, preventing a collapse where the router sends all tokens to one or two experts. The paper does not analyze what specializations actually emerge (due to budget constraints preventing ablation studies), but this is the intended mechanism.

#### Attention Experts with Shared Key-Value Projections

This is JetMoE-8B's key architectural innovation over standard MoE models. Extending the Mixture of Attention Heads (MoA) concept from Zhang et al. (2022) and ModuleFormer (Shen et al., 2023), JetMoE-8B makes attention itself sparse while introducing shared KV projections for training stability.

**Standard multi-head attention recap.** In a dense transformer, the attention layer computes:

1. Project input `$x$` to queries `$q$`, keys `$k$`, and values `$v$` using learned projection matrices `$W_q$`, `$W_k$`, `$W_v$`.
2. Compute attention scores as `$\text{softmax}(q k^T / \sqrt{d_{\text{head}}})$`.
3. Weight values `$v$` by attention scores to produce context vectors.
4. Project concatenated context vectors back to model dimension using `$W_o$`.

In a dense model, all heads in all layers use the same `$W_k$` and `$W_v$` for all tokens — there is no token-dependent selection of which key-value projections to use.

**JetMoE-8B's attention expert design.** Each attention expert `$e$` contains four trainable projection matrices:

- `$W^e_q \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{\text{emb}} \times D_{\text{att}}}$`: the query projection (owned by each expert independently)
- `$W^e_o \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{\text{att}} \times D_{\text{emb}}}$`: the output projection (owned by each expert independently)  
- `$W_k \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{\text{emb}} \times D_{\text{att}}}$`: the key projection (**shared** across all 8 attention experts in a layer)
- `$W_v \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{\text{emb}} \times D_{\text{att}}}$`: the value projection (**shared** across all 8 attention experts in a layer)

where `$D_{\text{att}} = H \times D_{\text{head}}$`, with `$H = 4$` attention heads per expert and `$D_{\text{head}} = 128$` (derived from the total: with 8 experts × 4 heads = 32 total heads, and `$D_{\text{model}} = 2048$`, we have `$D_{\text{head}} = 2048 / (8 \times 2) = 128$` since only 2 experts are active... though this exact arithmetic isn't fully specified in the paper; the paper explicitly states `$D_{\text{head}} = 128$` and `$n_{\text{kv heads}} = 16$` in Table 1, which refers to the total number of key-value heads across all experts when considering shared projections).

**What happens step by step for a token:**

1. **Shared KV projection (computed once):** The input token `$x$` is projected to keys and values using the shared matrices, producing `$k = W_k x$` and `$v = W_v x$`. These are computed exactly once per token, regardless of how many attention experts are activated — this is the efficiency gain from sharing.

2. **Router selects experts:** The attention router (separate from the feed-forward router) computes affinity scores for all 8 attention experts and selects the top 2. Only these 2 experts will perform the full attention computation.

3. **Per-expert query projection:** For each selected expert `$e$`, the token is projected to queries using that expert's own query matrix: `$q_e = W^e_q x$`. This means different experts can ask different "questions" about the same keys and values — one expert might attend to local syntax while another attends to long-range semantic dependencies.

4. **Multi-head attention with RoPE:** Inside expert `$e$`, the standard multi-head attention is computed:
   - The query `$q_e$` is split into 4 heads of dimension 128.
   - The shared key `$k$` and value `$v$` are also split into 4 heads.
   - Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE, Su et al., 2024) are applied to queries and keys to encode positional information — this is standard practice since absolute position encodings don't generalize well to variable sequence lengths.
   - Scaled dot-product attention computes context vectors per head.
   
   $$a_e = \text{MHA}(q_e, k, v)$$

   where MHA includes RoPE application, attention score computation, and value weighting.

5. **Output projection:** The concatenated context vectors are projected back to model dimension using the expert's output matrix: `$o_e = W^e_o a_e$`. This output then gets multiplied by the router's gating weight `$g(e \mid x)$` for that expert and summed with the other selected expert's weighted output.

**Why shared KV projections?** The paper explicitly states this design choice is "to improve the training and inference efficiency" and "to improve training stability." The efficiency argument is straightforward: keys and values are computed once instead of 2 or 8 times, reducing the total attention computation. The stability argument is more subtle: when each expert learns its own key-value projections, experts can develop divergent key-value representations that make the router's job harder (the router sees the input `$x$`, but the attention patterns depend on `$k$` and `$v$` which are different per expert). Sharing KV projections means all experts operate over the same key-value space, making expert selection about *which attention patterns to apply* rather than *which key-value space to work in*. The paper also notes in Table 1 that `$n_{\text{kv heads}} = 16$`, which likely means the shared KV projection produces 16 key-value heads total (2 active experts × 4 heads × 2 for key-value would suggest 8 heads needed, but 16 may account for all experts' heads being pre-computed once — the exact head allocation between shared and expert-specific projections is not fully detailed).

**What makes this different from standard MoE attention:** In standard MoE models (like Mixtral or DeepseekMoE), attention layers are dense — all attention parameters are used for every token. Only the feed-forward layers are sparsely activated. JetMoE-8B's innovation is making attention itself sparse: the router selects which 2 of 8 attention experts to use, meaning the query and output projections of the unselected 6 experts are never computed. This is what allows the model to claim sparse activation "in both the attention and feed-forward layers" — the paper's central architectural claim.

**The RoPE integration:** Rotary Position Embeddings are applied within the MHA computation on `$q_e$` and `$k$` before the attention scores are computed. This encodes relative position information into the attention mechanism itself (rather than adding position embeddings to the input), which is important because it allows the shared keys to carry positional information that all experts can use regardless of their query projections. The RoPE parameters are not expert-specific; they are a fixed transformation applied uniformly.

**Head allocation:** With 8 attention experts, 4 heads per expert, and top-2 activation, the model effectively uses `$2 \times 4 = 8$` attention heads per token — comparable to the number of heads in a standard dense transformer of similar activation size. The total of `$8 \times 4 = 32$` heads across all experts provides capacity for diverse attention patterns (different experts can learn different head specializations), but per-token computation is only for 8 heads. This is another dimension of the total-vs-active parameter efficiency.

#### Load Balancing During Pretraining

Sparse MoE training is notoriously unstable without explicit mechanisms to prevent the router from collapsing — sending all or most tokens to a single expert, which wastes the capacity of other experts and defeats the purpose of the architecture. JetMoE-8B uses two complementary loss terms to address this.

**Frequency-based auxiliary loss (load balancing loss).** This loss, introduced in Fedus et al. (2021) for Switch Transformers, encourages the router to distribute tokens evenly across experts:

$$\text{loss}_b = N \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i P_i$$

where `$N = 8$` is the number of experts, `$f_i$` is the fraction of tokens in the batch dispatched to expert `$i$` (the empirical usage frequency), and `$P_i$` is the fraction of router probability mass allocated to expert `$i$` (computed as the average of `$g(i \mid x)$` over all tokens in the batch, *before* the top-k operation — i.e., using the softmax over all 8 experts, not just the top 2).

**What it computes:** For each expert `$i$`, multiply the fraction of tokens it actually processed (`$f_i$`) by the average probability the router assigned to it (`$P_i$`), sum over all experts, and multiply by `$N$`. The product `$f_i P_i$` is minimized when the distribution is uniform (each expert gets `$1/N$` of tokens and `$1/N$` of probability), because the sum of products of two probability distributions is minimized when both are uniform. The multiplication by `$N$` normalizes so the loss equals 1 under a uniform distribution, providing a scale-invariant target.

**Why this form:** The product `$f_i P_i$` penalizes *both* over-utilization (high `$f_i$`) and under-utilization (low `$f_i$`) of experts relative to the router's affinities. If an expert receives many tokens but the router assigns it low probability, `$P_i$` is small and the penalty is moderate — the router is being forced to use experts it doesn't prefer, which is the load balancing objective. Critically, `$P_i$` is computed from the softmax over *all* experts, not the top-k gating weights, because the auxiliary loss needs to influence the router's pre-selection scores to change which experts get selected. The top-k operation is non-differentiable with respect to the selection itself, so gradients flow through `$P_i$` to adjust the router weights.

**Router z-loss.** This loss, introduced in Zoph et al. (2022) for ST-MoE, penalizes large router logits to improve training stability:

$$\text{loss}_z = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} \left( \log \sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(x_{i,j}) \right)^2$$

where `$B$` is the number of tokens in the batch, `$N = 8$` is the number of experts, and `$x_{i,j}$` is the router's raw logit for token `$i$` and expert `$j$` (i.e., the `$j$`-th component of `$s = W_{\text{rtr}} x$` before any softmax or top-k operation).

**What it computes:** For each token, compute the log-sum-exp of all 8 router logits — this is the log of the partition function, essentially measuring the "total magnitude" of the router's output for that token. Square this value, and average over all tokens in the batch. The result is a single scalar that grows when router logits are large in magnitude.

**Why this form:** Large router logits cause two problems. First, they make the softmax distribution extremely peaked, meaning the router becomes overconfident and stops exploring different expert assignments — this leads to routing collapse where tokens always go to the same experts. Second, large logits cause numerical instability in the softmax computation (exponentiating large numbers produces infinities). The z-loss penalizes the squared log-sum-exp, which encourages the router to keep logits in a moderate range. The log-sum-exp form (rather than, say, L2 norm of logits) is used because it's the quantity that directly enters the softmax denominator and thus controls the peakiness of the distribution. Squaring it penalizes large deviations symmetrically (both positive and negative large logits contribute).

**Combined training objective.** The final loss is:

$$\text{loss} = \text{loss}_{\text{lm}} + \alpha \cdot \text{loss}_b + \beta \cdot \text{loss}_z$$

where `$\text{loss}_{\text{lm}}$` is the standard language modeling cross-entropy loss (next-token prediction), `$\alpha = 0.01$` is the weight for the load balancing loss, and `$\beta = 0.001$` is the weight for the z-loss.

**What it computes:** The total training objective is the sum of three terms: the primary language modeling objective (which drives the model to predict text accurately), a small load balancing penalty (1% weight) that prevents expert collapse, and a very small z-loss penalty (0.1% weight) that stabilizes training. The weights are chosen following ST-MoE (Zoph et al., 2022) — they are small enough that the primary LM objective dominates, but large enough to prevent the failure modes they address.

**Why these weights:** The load balancing loss at 0.01 is a standard choice from the MoE literature. Too large a weight would force uniform routing at the expense of expert specialization (the model would route randomly rather than learning meaningful specializations). Too small and routing collapse occurs. The z-loss at 0.001 is even smaller because it's a regularizer rather than a core objective — it only needs to prevent logits from growing unboundedly, which a small penalty suffices to do. The paper does not ablate these values due to budget constraints.

**Application to both attention and feed-forward routers.** Both the attention MoE layers and the feed-forward MoE layers have their own routers, and both incur these auxiliary losses. The paper does not specify whether the losses are computed separately per router and summed, or averaged — the standard approach (following Fedus et al., 2021) is to compute and sum the losses independently for each MoE layer, then average across layers, but this detail is not explicitly confirmed in the paper.

#### Training Infrastructure and Parallelism Strategy

The paper makes a non-standard choice in its parallelism strategy that is worth understanding, as it reveals practical constraints of training sparse MoE models at scale.

**Framework.** Training uses Megatron-LM (Shoeybi et al., 2019) as the base training framework, with Megablocks (Gale et al., 2023) integrated for MoE support. Megatron provides the distributed training primitives (model parallelism, data parallelism, pipeline parallelism), while Megablocks provides efficient kernels for sparse expert computation — specifically, the "dropless" MoE implementation where tokens are not dropped even if they would exceed an expert's capacity.

**Pipeline parallelism over expert parallelism.** The standard approach for training MoE models with Megatron is to use **expert parallelism**: different experts within a layer are placed on different GPUs, and tokens are routed across the network to their assigned expert's device. This works well when the expert load is balanced — each GPU processes roughly the same number of tokens.

JetMoE-8B instead uses **pipeline parallelism** (Narayanan et al., 2021): different *layers* of the model are placed on different GPUs, but all experts within a layer are on the same GPU. The paper gives two explicit reasons for this choice, both worth examining in detail:

1. **Smaller hidden state reduces pipeline communication cost.** SMoE models typically have narrower hidden states than dense models of comparable total parameters — JetMoE-8B uses `$D_{\text{emb}} = 2048$`, which is smaller than the ~4096 that a 7-8B dense model would typically use. Pipeline parallelism sends activations between pipeline stages, and the communication volume scales with the hidden dimension. A narrower model means less data to transfer between GPUs, making pipeline parallelism more efficient than it would be for a wider dense model.

2. **Dropless MoE causes load imbalance across experts, making expert parallelism inefficient.** The paper uses the dropless MoE implementation from Megablocks (Gale et al., 2023) and ModuleFormer (Shen et al., 2023). In a standard "token-drop" MoE, if too many tokens are routed to a single expert, excess tokens are dropped (skipped) to maintain fixed batch sizes. The dropless approach instead processes all tokens through their assigned experts, but this means that in any given batch, different experts may receive different numbers of tokens — sometimes dramatically different, despite the load balancing loss. With expert parallelism, this load imbalance translates directly to computation imbalance across GPUs: the GPU hosting a popular expert does more work than others, leaving the other GPUs idle and reducing overall throughput. Pipeline parallelism avoids this because all experts in a layer are on the same GPU, so within-layer load imbalance doesn't cross device boundaries — only across-layer imbalance matters, which is easier to manage through micro-batch scheduling.

**Cluster configuration.** Training was conducted on a cluster with 12 nodes and 96 H100 GPUs total. Within each node, GPUs are connected via NVLink (high-bandwidth, low-latency intra-node communication). Between nodes, InfiniBand is used for fast inter-node communication. This is a standard high-performance training cluster configuration; the key point is that the paper uses readily available hardware (H100 GPUs are commercial off-the-shelf) rather than custom interconnects or specialized hardware.

**The modification to support MoA and z-loss.** The paper notes they "further modified the training framework to support MoA (Section 2.3) and z-loss (Section 2.4)." This means Megatron and Megablocks did not natively support sparsely-gated attention with shared KV projections or the z-loss computation — these were custom additions. The specific modifications are not detailed, but likely involve:
- Custom forward and backward kernels for the MoA layer that handle the shared KV projections and per-expert query-output projections within the sparse expert framework.
- Additional loss computation and gradient propagation for the z-loss term, which requires computing the log-sum-exp of router logits and adding the gradient contribution to the router weights.

#### Hyperparameters and Training Configuration

The hyperparameters are chosen to target a model with approximately 2× the per-token computation of a 1B-parameter dense model, achieved by setting 8 experts with top-2 activation. Table 1 in the paper specifies the key values:

| Hyperparameter | Value | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| `$P_{\text{total}}$` | 8B | Total parameters across all experts |
| `$P_{\text{active}}$` | 2B | Parameters activated per token |
| `$n_{\text{layers}}$` | 24 | Transformer layers (each with attention + feed-forward MoE) |
| `$D_{\text{model}}$` | 2048 | Hidden dimension of the transformer |
| `$N_{\text{experts}}$` | 8 | Experts per MoE layer |
| Top-k | 2 | Experts selected per token per layer |
| `$n_{\text{kv heads}}$` | 16 | Total key-value heads (shared across attention experts) |
| `$D_{\text{head}}$` | 128 | Dimension per attention head |
| `$D_{\text{mlp}}$` | 5632 | Feed-forward hidden dimension (the `$D_{\text{ffd}}$` in equations) |

**Why these values:** The paper states they are "selected based on the common practice for the 1B transformer language model." The reasoning chain is: (1) start with a standard 1B dense transformer architecture, (2) replace all dense attention and MLP layers with MoE versions, (3) set 8 experts and top-2 activation so the per-token computation is roughly 2× that of the 1B base (since 2 experts activate instead of 1 dense layer), (4) the total parameter count becomes approximately 8B because there are 8 experts but only 2 are active. The hidden dimension 2048 and 24 layers are consistent with a ~1B parameter dense model architecture. The feed-forward hidden dimension 5632 is approximately 2.75× the model dimension, which is in the typical range for transformer FFN expansion ratios (2.5× to 4×).

**Optimizer and learning rate schedule.** The model is trained with AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) — the standard Adam optimizer with decoupled weight decay, which is the dominant choice for LLM training. Specific optimizer configurations:
- Maximum learning rate: `$5 \times 10^{-4}$`
- Weight decay: 0.1
- Gradient clipping: 1.0
- Batch size: 4M tokens (with sequence length 4096, this means approximately 1024 sequences per batch)

**The Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) learning rate schedule.** Rather than a standard cosine decay, JetMoE-8B uses the WSD schedule introduced in Hu et al. (2024) for miniCPM:

$$\text{lr}(s) = \begin{cases} \frac{s}{W} \cdot \eta, & s < W \\ \eta, & W \leq s < S \\ f(s - S) \cdot \eta, & S \leq s < S + D \end{cases}$$

where `$s$` is the current training step, `$\eta = 5 \times 10^{-4}$` is the maximum learning rate, `$W$` is the number of steps at the end of the warmup stage, `$S$` is the number of steps at the end of the stable stage, `$D$` is the number of steps in the decay stage, and `$f(s - S)$` is a decreasing function satisfying `$0 < f(s - S) \leq 1$`.

**What it computes:** The schedule has three phases:
1. **Warmup** (`$s < W$`): Learning rate linearly increases from 0 to `$\eta$` over `$W$` steps. In JetMoE-8B, this stage lasts for 10 billion tokens.
2. **Stable** (`$W \leq s < S$`): Learning rate stays constant at `$\eta$`. This is where most training happens — the model learns at full speed.
3. **Decay** (`$S \leq s < S + D$`): Learning rate decreases from `$\eta$` toward a final value according to the decreasing function `$f$`. In JetMoE-8B, this stage spans 250 billion tokens, and the initial and final learning rates in this stage are set to 10% of `$\eta$` (meaning `$f(0) = 0.1$` and `$f(D) \approx 0$`).

**Why this form:** The WSD schedule is explicitly designed to facilitate phased training. The stable phase — with a constant high learning rate — allows the model to learn effectively from the broad, diverse Phase 1 data. The decay phase — with a gradually decreasing learning rate — is when higher-quality Phase 2 data is introduced. The idea (following miniCPM and Gemma) is that the final annealing phase disproportionately influences the model's eventual behavior, so concentrating high-quality data in this phase gives the best return on investment. A standard cosine schedule would start decaying the learning rate much earlier, meaning high-quality data introduced late in training would be seen with a very low learning rate and have less impact.

The paper specifies the specific token counts: warmup for 10B tokens, decay for 250B tokens. The stable phase duration can be inferred: total training tokens are 1.25T, so the stable phase accounts for approximately `$1.25\text{T} - 10\text{B} - 250\text{B} \approx 990\text{B}$` tokens — roughly 80% of training.

#### Two-Phase Training Data Mixture

The data strategy is a critical component of JetMoE-8B's cost-effectiveness. The model is trained on entirely public, open-source datasets with no proprietary data — a core part of the paper's reproducibility claim.

**Phase 1 data (~1T tokens, warmup and stable learning rate).** This phase uses a broad mixture focused on quantity and diversity:
- **RefinedWeb** (Penedo et al., 2023): A 600B token extract from CommonCrawl processed through the MacroData Refinement pipeline for quality filtering. This is the primary source of general web text.
- **StarCoder** (Li et al., 2023b): Code from The Stack v1.2 spanning 86 programming languages, preprocessed through visual inspection, filtering, deduplication, and low-data language reweighting. This provides code pretraining.
- **The Pile** (Gao et al., 2020): An 825 GB diverse English text corpus including Wikipedia, NIH ExPORTER, ArXiv, Books3, BookCorpus2, OpenSubtitles, YTSubtitles, and Enron Emails.
- **peS2o from Dolma** (Soldaini et al., 2024): Academic papers from the Semantic Scholar corpus, part of the larger Dolma dataset.
- **OpenWebMath** (Paster et al., 2023): 14.7B tokens of mathematical web text.

The exact percentages for Phase 1 are not tabulated in the paper (only Phase 2 percentages are given in Table 2), but Figure 2 shows the overall data mixture. The Phase 1 data is characterized by being large-scale, diverse, and web-derived — suitable for learning general linguistic patterns, factual knowledge, and broad capabilities.

**Phase 2 data (~250B tokens, learning rate decay).** This phase introduces additional high-quality, curated datasets at higher proportions. Table 2 provides the detailed breakdown, which I will reproduce and explain by category:

**Natural language pretraining data (continuing from Phase 1):**
- RefinedWeb: 39.8%
- Pile Wikipedia: 6.7%
- Pile StackExchange: 4.8%
- Pile arXiv: 1.0%
- Pile remaining (other Pile subsets): 5.1%
- Dolma peS2o: 1.0%

These datasets continue providing broad world knowledge and general text, but note that the overall proportion of web data (RefinedWeb) drops relative to Phase 1 as higher-quality data is introduced.

**Natural language SFT data (new in Phase 2):**
- xP3x, OpenAssistant, OpenHermes: 7.3%
- UltraChat, Oasst-octopack

The xP3x (Crosslingual Public Pool of Prompts eXtended, Muennighoff et al., 2023b) is a collection of prompts and datasets spanning 277 languages and 16 NLP tasks. OpenAssistant (LAION-AI, 2023) is a human-generated, human-annotated assistant-style conversation corpus from over 13,500 volunteers. OpenHermes 2.5 (Teknium, 2023) is a 1M-sample compilation of synthetic instruction and chat data. UltraChat (Ding et al., 2023; Tunstall et al., 2023b) is a filtered subset of 1.4M ChatGPT-generated dialogues. These datasets teach the model instruction-following and conversational behavior.

**Textbook data (new in Phase 2):**
- UltraTextbooks: 4.8%

UltraTextbooks (Locutusque, 2024) is a comprehensive collection of high-quality synthetic and human-written textbooks compiled from multiple sources including mini-peS2o, programming books, textbooks, and curated web collections. This provides structured, high-quality educational content that helps the model learn coherent, well-organized knowledge representation.

**Code pretraining data (continuing from Phase 1):**
- Starcoder Github: 19.6%

**Code SFT data (new in Phase 2):**
- Magicoder-OSS, Magicoder-Evol: 3.8%
- Code-290k-ShareGPT, CommitPackFT, Evol-Code Alpaca

Magicoder datasets (Wei et al., 2023) use the OSS-INSTRUCT approach where an LLM generates new coding problems inspired by open-source code snippets. Code-290k-ShareGPT (ajibawa 2023, 2024) contains ~290K code-related conversations. CommitPackFT (Muennighoff et al., 2023a) contains filtered high-quality git commit messages resembling natural language instructions. Evol-Code Alpaca (Luo et al., 2023) adapts the Evol-Instruct method for code instructions. These datasets teach the model to follow coding instructions and generate code in response to natural language queries.

**Math data (new in Phase 2):**
- Open-web-math, algebraic-stack: 5.8%
- TemplateGSM, StackMathQA

OpenWebMath is 14.7B tokens of mathematical web text. Algebraic-stack (from Proof-Pile-2, Azerbayev et al., 2023) contains 11B tokens of numerical computing, computer algebra, and formal mathematics. TemplateGSM (Zhang et al., 2024b) contains over 7 million grade school math problems with code and natural language solutions. StackMathQA (Zhang, 2024) is a curated collection of 2 million mathematical Q&A pairs from Stack Exchange. This heavy math emphasis in Phase 2 likely contributes to JetMoE-8B's strong GSM8k performance (27.8%, the highest among compared models in Table 3).

**Why the two-phase approach?** The paper explicitly credits miniCPM (Hu et al., 2024) and Gemma (Team et al., 2024) for this strategy. The intuition is that early training benefits from scale and diversity (learning the statistical structure of language and basic world knowledge), while late training benefits from quality and task relevance (refining the model's behavior toward useful, accurate outputs). The learning rate decay phase is when the model "locks in" its final representations and behaviors, so feeding higher-quality data during this phase shapes those final representations toward desirable outcomes. The paper acknowledges that the specific mixture "might not be ideal" given budget constraints and "serves as a good starting point" for future optimization.

#### Model Alignment

After pretraining, JetMoE-8B undergoes a two-stage alignment process to produce the chat-capable JetMoE-8B-Chat model. The alignment recipe is relatively standard but is documented in detail for reproducibility.

**Distilled Supervised Fine-Tuning (dSFT).** The first stage trains the base model to follow instructions by learning from a teacher model's outputs.

1. **Data Distillation:** For a set of seed prompts `$\{x_j^0\}_{j=1}^J$`, a teacher model `$\pi_T$` (GPT-4 in practice, though the paper doesn't explicitly name the teacher) generates responses `$y_j^0$`. Instructions may be refined to create the final dataset `$C = \{(x_j, y_j)\}_{j=1}^J$`.

2. **Instruction Tuning:** The student model `$\pi_{\text{dSFT}}$` is trained to maximize the likelihood of the teacher's responses:

$$\pi_{\text{dSFT}} = \arg\max_\pi \sum_{(x,y) \in C} \log \pi(y \mid x)$$

where the sum is over all instruction-response pairs in the dataset, and `$\pi(y \mid x)$` is the probability the student model assigns to response `$y$` given instruction `$x$` (computed as the product of per-token conditional probabilities).

**What it computes:** For each training example, the model sees an instruction and computes the probability of each token in the teacher's response, conditioned on all previous tokens. The objective maximizes the sum of log-probabilities of the correct tokens — this is standard next-token prediction, but now the "correct" tokens are defined by the teacher model rather than by ground-truth data. The expectation is approximated by the arithmetic mean over a batch of training samples.

**Why this form:** This is standard supervised fine-tuning — the same objective as pretraining but on a curated instruction-following dataset. The key difference from pretraining is the data distribution: instead of diverse web text, the model sees structured instruction-response pairs, which teaches it to produce helpful, coherent responses to user queries. The "distilled" label comes from using a stronger teacher model (GPT-4) to generate the training responses.

**dSFT datasets:** The model is fine-tuned on a combination of: UltraChat 200k, Airoboros-3.2 (Durbin, 2023), Code-Feedback (Zheng et al., 2024b), Orca-math-word-problems-200k (Mitra et al., 2024), SystemChat (abacusai, 2024), and Capybara (Daniele & Suphavadeeprasit, 2023). The chat template follows Zephyr-7b-beta.

**dSFT hyperparameters:** Learning rate `$2 \times 10^{-5}$` with Adam optimizer (not AdamW — notably, no weight decay is mentioned for this stage), batch size 128, 3 epochs. This is a relatively standard SFT configuration: low learning rate to avoid catastrophic forgetting of pretraining knowledge, moderate batch size, and a small number of epochs (the data is high-quality so fewer passes are needed).

**Distilled Direct Preference Optimization (dDPO).** The second stage refines the dSFT model using preference data — pairs of responses where one is preferred over the other — to align the model's outputs with human preferences.

The theoretical foundation is **KL-constrained optimization.** The optimal policy `$\pi_r^*$` maximizes expected reward while staying close to a baseline policy `$\pi_0$`:

$$\pi_r^*(y \mid x) = \arg\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x \sim d_0} \left[ \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)} [r(x, y)] - \eta \, \text{KL}(\pi(\cdot \mid x) \parallel \pi_0(\cdot \mid x)) \right]$$

where `$r(x, y)$` is a reward function indicating how good response `$y$` is for prompt `$x$`, `$\eta$` is a regularization parameter balancing reward maximization and staying close to the baseline, `$\text{KL}(\pi \parallel \pi_0)$` is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the new policy and the baseline, and `$d_0$` is the prompt distribution.

**What it computes:** This objective says: find a policy that gets high rewards on average, but don't deviate too far from the baseline policy `$\pi_0$`. The KL penalty prevents the model from "reward hacking" — finding responses that score highly under the reward model but are nonsensical or unhelpful because they exploit quirks of the reward function. The baseline `$\pi_0$` is typically the dSFT model or the base pretrained model.

**Preference-driven reward function.** DPO's key insight (from Rafailov et al., 2023) is that the optimal reward function can be expressed in terms of the optimal policy and the baseline policy:

$$r^*(x, y) = \eta \log \left( \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{dSFT}}(y \mid x)} \right) + \eta \log Z(x)$$

where `$Z(x)$` is a normalization term (partition function) that depends only on `$x$`, not on `$y$`.

**What it computes:** This expresses the implicit reward as the log-ratio of probabilities between the optimal policy and the baseline dSFT policy, scaled by `$\eta$`. If the optimal policy assigns higher probability to response `$y$` than the baseline does, the reward for that response is positive — the optimization has learned to prefer it. The `$Z(x)$` term ensures normalization across all possible responses for prompt `$x$`.

**Why this form:** This is the critical insight that enables DPO to work without explicitly training a separate reward model. By rearranging the KL-constrained objective, Rafailov et al. (2023) showed that the reward function can be eliminated from the optimization entirely, leaving an objective that only requires the policy model and the baseline model's probabilities. This avoids the complexity of RLHF (training a reward model, then running reinforcement learning), replacing it with a simpler supervised loss.

**DPO optimization objective.** The actual training loss for aligning `$\pi_\theta$` with teacher preferences is:

$$\pi_\theta = \arg\max_\pi \sum_{(x, y_w, y_l) \in D} \log \sigma \left( \eta \log \frac{\pi(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{dSFT}}(y_w \mid x)} - \eta \log \frac{\pi(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{dSFT}}(y_l \mid x)} \right)$$

where `$D$` is a dataset of preference triples: prompt `$x$`, preferred response `$y_w$` (the "winner"), and dispreferred response `$y_l$` (the "loser"), `$\sigma$` is the sigmoid function `$\sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$`, and `$\pi_{\text{dSFT}}$` is the fixed dSFT model serving as the baseline.

**What it computes:** For each preference pair, the model computes the log-ratio of probabilities (how much more likely the current policy makes the winner vs. how much more likely the baseline makes the winner) minus the same for the loser. This difference represents how much the current policy prefers the winner over the loser relative to the baseline. The sigmoid converts this to a probability (between 0 and 1) that the winner is actually better, and the log is maximized — meaning the model is trained to assign higher relative probability to preferred responses and lower relative probability to dispreferred responses. The `$\eta$` parameter scales the difference; larger `$\eta$` means stronger preference signal.

**Why this form:** This is the standard DPO loss from Rafailov et al. (2023). It is a binary cross-entropy loss where the "label" is 1 (the winner should be preferred) and the "logit" is the scaled difference in log-ratios. The loss is minimized when the current policy's log-ratio for the winner substantially exceeds its log-ratio for the loser. Critically, this loss only requires forward passes through both the current model and the frozen dSFT model — no reward model training, no RL sampling, no value function estimation. The paper uses offline DPO (static preference data), noting it "provides stable learning and simpler tuning compared to RLHF" but acknowledging it "faces challenges with distribution shifts between the dataset and the evolving policy."

**dDPO dataset and hyperparameters.** The model is fine-tuned on the UltraFeedback dataset (Cui et al., 2023), which contains binary preference labels — for each prompt, two responses are compared and one is marked as preferred. Key hyperparameters: learning rate `$5 \times 10^{-7}$` with AdamW (note the return to AdamW with weight decay), batch size 128, 1 epoch. The learning rate is an order of magnitude lower than dSFT, which is typical for preference optimization — the model is already a good instruction-follower from dSFT, and DPO makes subtle adjustments. Only 1 epoch is used because preference data is high-quality and the model can overfit to preference patterns with multiple passes.

**Alignment infrastructure and cost.** The alignment process uses the Alignment Handbook (Tunstall et al., 2023a) with PyTorch 2 (He & Yu, 2023; Ansel et al., 2024) and DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 (Rajbhandari et al., 2020) for memory-efficient distributed training. The entire alignment process — both dSFT and dDPO stages combined — takes only 60 H100 GPU hours, which the paper highlights as demonstrating that JetMoE-8B "can also be finetuned with limited compute budgets (e.g., consumer-grade GPUs)." This is a meaningful claim: 60 H100 hours is roughly equivalent to a few days on a single 8-GPU node, or could be done on consumer hardware (though likely with more time and engineering).

**MT-Bench evaluation configuration.** For the final chat evaluation on MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023), the paper specifies task-dependent temperature settings following the official FastChat implementation: Writing and Roleplay tasks use temperature 0.7 (higher creativity), Extraction, Math, Coding, and Reasoning use temperature 0.0 (deterministic/precise), and STEM and Humanities use temperature 0.1 (slight variability). This detail matters because temperature strongly affects MT-Bench scores, and matching the official configuration enables fair comparison to other models evaluated on MT-Bench.

#### Summary of Key Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Sparse activation on both attention and feed-forward layers** rather than feed-forward only: extends the parameter-efficiency benefits of MoE to the attention mechanism, preventing attention from becoming a computational bottleneck as expert count scales. This is the architectural novelty over standard MoE models.

- **Shared key-value projections across attention experts** rather than per-expert KV projections: improves training stability by ensuring all experts operate over the same key-value space, and reduces computational overhead by computing KV projections once per token. The paper explicitly states this is for stability and efficiency.

- **Two-phase training with higher-quality data concentrated in the learning rate decay phase** rather than uniform data throughout training: following miniCPM and Gemma, the annealing phase disproportionately shapes final model behavior, so allocating the highest-quality (and more expensive to curate) data to this phase maximizes return on data investment.

- **Pipeline parallelism over expert parallelism** despite the latter being standard for MoE: justified by the dropless MoE implementation causing within-layer load imbalance (which expert parallelism would propagate across devices) and the narrow hidden state of SMoE models (which reduces pipeline communication costs). A practical engineering choice driven by the specific properties of the architecture and MoE implementation.

- **WSD learning rate schedule** rather than cosine decay: enables the two-phase data strategy by maintaining a high learning rate through the broad-data phase and only decaying during the high-quality-data phase. The constant high-rate phase allows the model to learn effectively from diverse but noisier data.

- **DPO over RLHF** for preference alignment: simpler to implement and tune, requires only static preference data, avoids training a separate reward model or running online RL. The paper acknowledges the tradeoff (distribution shift with offline data) but accepts it given the budget constraints and simplicity goals.

- **Full public data and open training recipe** rather than proprietary data or closed methodology: the paper's core value proposition — JetMoE-8B is not just a model release but a reproducible training procedure. Every dataset is publicly available, all hyperparameters are listed, and the code is open-source.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Dual Sparse Activation as a Unified Efficiency Principle, Not Just a Feed-Forward Optimization

The dominant assumption in the MoE literature — from Shazeer et al. (2017) through Mixtral (Jiang et al., 2024), DeepseekMoE (Dai et al., 2024), and DBRX (Databricks, 2024) — has been that sparse activation belongs exclusively in feed-forward layers. The reasoning was straightforward: feed-forward layers contain roughly two-thirds of transformer parameters, so replacing them with MoE captures most of the parameter-efficiency benefit without touching the more delicate attention mechanism. Attention was treated as architecturally fixed — a dense computation that just needed to work correctly for the rest of the model to function.

JetMoE-8B challenges this assumption at the conceptual level by treating sparsity not as a feed-forward optimization trick but as a **unified architectural principle** that should apply to every parameter-heavy component of the transformer. The paper's key move is recognizing that as expert counts scale up in feed-forward MoE, the *dense* attention layers become the performance bottleneck — they consume a fixed compute budget per token regardless of how many total parameters the model has, which means the efficiency gains from sparse feed-forward layers eventually plateau. Making attention itself sparse (via the Mixture of Attention Heads, or MoA, approach inherited from ModuleFormer, Shen et al., 2023) removes this bottleneck and allows the total-to-active parameter ratio to scale further.

What makes this a genuine insight rather than an obvious extension is the **shared key-value projection** design choice. In a naive sparse attention implementation, each attention expert would learn its own query, key, value, and output projections — 4 matrices per expert. With 8 experts per layer and 24 layers, that would add substantial parameter overhead and, more critically, create training instability as experts develop incompatible key-value representations. The shared KV projection elegantly resolves this: by computing keys and values once per token (shared across all experts in a layer) and only letting experts differ in their query and output projections, the model preserves the expressive power of diverse attention patterns while maintaining a consistent key-value space that stabilizes training. The paper explicitly states this choice is "to improve training stability" — this is not just an optimization, it's a diagnosis that the stability problems in sparse attention stem from divergent key-value spaces, and a surgical fix that addresses the root cause.

This is a **conceptual reframing** of MoE design rather than an incremental improvement. Prior work asked: "How can we make feed-forward layers more efficient using sparsity?" JetMoE-8B asks: "What would a transformer look like if we designed every component around the principle that only a fraction of parameters should activate per token?" The answer involves different design constraints for attention than for feed-forward — shared projections, careful head allocation — but the unified principle is what's novel. The evidence that this matters comes from the architecture's efficiency numbers: 8B total parameters activating only 2.2B per token (a ~3.6:1 ratio), which achieves approximately 70% inference compute reduction compared to Llama2-7B. A standard feed-forward-only MoE would need to activate more parameters per token to achieve comparable performance because the dense attention layers would limit how much the feed-forward sparsity can reduce the total active parameter count.

The significance extends beyond this single model. By demonstrating that sparse attention can work at scale with the right stabilization techniques (shared KV projections, z-loss, pipeline parallelism), the paper opens a design space that prior MoE work had implicitly foreclosed. Future MoE architectures no longer need to treat attention as an immutable dense block — they can consider different sparsity patterns, different sharing strategies, and different head allocations, all within the unified framework of "every component should be sparsely activated."

---

### Innovation 2: The $0.1M Price Point as a Deliberate Reproducibility Boundary, Not Just a Cost Claim

Most papers that report training costs do so as a flex — "we trained this massive model for $X million" — or as an afterthought. JetMoE-8B inverts this: the **$0.1M (under $100,000) training budget is not a side effect but a design constraint**, and the paper treats it as a first-class research contribution. This is a fundamentally different framing of what constitutes a meaningful result in LLM research.

The field has internalized a narrative — reinforced by scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) and years of corporate model releases — that competitive LLM performance requires multi-million-dollar training budgets minimum, and that pushing the frontier necessarily means pushing costs higher. This narrative has structural consequences: it determines who can participate in LLM research, what kinds of questions get asked, and which models become the standard baselines that subsequent work compares against.

JetMoE-8B challenges this narrative not through argument but through existence proof. By reporting a fully specified training recipe that achieves Llama2-7B-competitive performance at a $0.1M budget using only public data and 30,000 H100 GPU hours, the paper establishes a concrete reference point: **this is what the frontier looks like at a price academic labs can afford**. The claim is not that JetMoE-8B is the best possible model at any price — it's that competitive performance is achievable at a much lower cost floor than generally thought, and the recipe is fully reproducible.

What makes this an intellectual contribution (rather than just a brag about frugality) is the paper's treatment of the cost constraint as a **methodological forcing function**. The two-phase data strategy, the choice of pipeline parallelism over expert parallelism, the decision to skip ablation studies entirely — all of these are downstream consequences of the $0.1M budget constraint, and the paper documents them transparently. The budget is not just a number in the abstract; it shapes every design decision, and the paper makes those tradeoffs visible. This is a different kind of research contribution than a new architecture or a new training algorithm — it's a demonstration that careful engineering under tight resource constraints can produce results that the field assumed required industrial-scale investment.

The paper also implicitly draws a contrast with "open-weight" models that release trained parameters but not training recipes. Models like Mixtral 8x7B and DeepseekMoE are downloadable but their training pipelines, data mixtures, and hyperparameter schedules remain proprietary. JetMoE-8B positions full recipe transparency as part of the cost claim: the $0.1M figure is only meaningful because the paper provides enough detail that someone else could (in principle) reproduce the result for a similar budget. Without the recipe, the cost claim would be unverifiable. With the recipe, it becomes a challenge to the field: if we can do this for $0.1M with public data, what's the actual cost floor for state-of-the-art LLM performance, and why aren't we finding it?

This is a **framing innovation**, not a technical one. It changes what the community considers a meaningful benchmark for LLM development — from "maximum performance at any cost" toward "maximum performance at a specified, reproducible cost." The significance is that it creates a new category of research artifact: the fully-reproducible, cost-constrained model, which can serve as a shared reference point for studying architecture tradeoffs, data mixture effects, and training dynamics in a setting where anyone can replicate the baseline.

### Innovation 3: Phased Data Strategy as a Budget-Aware Optimization, Not Just a Curriculum

The practice of changing data distribution during training has precedent — notably in miniCPM (Hu et al., 2024) and Gemma (Team et al., 2024), which JetMoE-8B explicitly credits. However, prior work largely motivated phased training as a *curriculum learning* strategy: start with easy/diverse data, end with hard/focused data, and the model learns more effectively. JetMoE-8B reframes this as a **budget allocation problem**: high-quality data is expensive to curate (or in the paper's case, to include at scale while staying within the $0.1M budget for compute and data processing), so it should be concentrated in the training phase where it has the highest marginal impact per token.

The key insight is the alignment between the learning rate schedule and the data schedule. The Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) schedule maintains a high constant learning rate through the stable phase (~80% of training), where the model trains on broad, diverse, lower-cost web data (RefinedWeb, Starcoder, The Pile). The learning rate only decays in the final ~20% of training, which is exactly when the higher-quality, more expensive curated data (UltraTextbooks, OpenHermes, synthetic math and code datasets) is introduced. This coupling means that high-quality data is *only* seen when the learning rate is dropping and the model is solidifying its representations — the phase where each training token has the largest influence on final model behavior.

The departure from prior work is subtle but significant. A standard curriculum learning approach might introduce high-quality data based on training progress (e.g., "after N steps" or "when validation loss plateaus"), independent of the learning rate schedule. JetMoE-8B's approach ties the two together explicitly, motivated by the WSD scheduler's property that the annealing phase disproportionately shapes downstream performance. This is not just "better data later" but a specific hypothesis about *how* data quality and learning rate interact: that high-quality data has the highest return when the model is being annealed into its final state, and that lower-quality but more diverse data is sufficient for the exploration phase where the learning rate is high.

The evidence for this being more than just a curriculum is the specific data composition in Phase 2 (detailed in the paper's Table 2 and discussed in Section 3). The Phase 2 mixture includes substantial amounts of instruction-formatted data (xP3x, OpenAssistant, OpenHermes at 7.3%), textbook data (UltraTextbooks at 4.8%), and synthetic math/code datasets that would be expensive or impossible to include at the same scale in the full 1.25T token training run. By concentrating these in the 250B-token decay phase, the paper achieves a "rich get richer" effect: the model learns general linguistic competence from 1T tokens of broad data, then refines toward helpful, accurate, instruction-following behavior from 250B tokens of curated data at a fraction of the cost of training on curated data throughout.

This is an **operational insight** with practical implications for anyone training LLMs on a budget. It suggests a specific resource allocation strategy: spend your curation budget on the final 10-20% of training, not evenly across the whole run, and use a learning rate schedule that preserves high learning rates through the broad-data phase. The insight is not that curricula work (known) but that the interaction between data quality scheduling and learning rate scheduling can be optimized jointly to maximize performance per dollar of data curation cost. The paper doesn't ablate this choice — it can't, given the $0.1M budget — but it provides a concrete, fully-specified recipe that future work can analyze and refine.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary evaluation suite is the **OpenLLM Leaderboard** (Table 3), which aggregates performance across multiple benchmarks and provides comparable scores for other open-source models. The individual tasks included are: ARC-challenge (Clark et al., 2019), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021), TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), and GSM8k (Cobbe et al., 2021). Additional evaluations include **HumanEval** (Chen et al., 2021) and **MBPP** (Austin et al., 2021) for code generation, evaluated using the BigCode Evaluation Harness (Ben Allal et al., 2022). For chat capability, the model is evaluated on **MT-Bench** (Zheng et al., 2023) using a strong LLM judge (GPT-4-0613 checkpoint). The exact number of test examples per benchmark is not specified in the paper, but these are standardized evaluation suites with fixed test sets.

- **Base model(s).** JetMoE-8B is the primary model, an 8B-parameter Sparsely-gated Mixture-of-Experts architecture with 2.2B active parameters per token, trained on 1.25T tokens from public datasets. For comparison, three external open-source models are evaluated: **Llama2-7B** (Touvron et al., 2023) — a dense 7B-parameter model trained on 2T tokens and the paper's primary baseline; **DeepseekMoE-16B** (Dai et al., 2024) — a 16B MoE model activating 2.8B parameters, trained on 2T tokens; and **Gemma-2B** (Team et al., 2024) — a 2B dense model trained on 2T tokens. These baselines were chosen to span the range of model sizes and architectures against which JetMoE-8B's cost-performance tradeoff can be assessed: Llama2-7B as the standard open-source dense model at comparable scale, DeepseekMoE as a strong MoE competitor, and Gemma-2B as a dense model with similar active parameter count.

- **Metrics.** For the OpenLLM Leaderboard benchmarks, the paper reports **accuracy** (percentage of correctly answered questions) following the same evaluation methodology used by the OpenLLM Leaderboard to ensure comparability. For code generation (HumanEval and MBPP), the metric is **pass@1** with greedy decoding — the fraction of problems where the model's first generated solution passes all test cases — reported as the mean pass@1 across both benchmarks. For MT-Bench, the metric is the **MT-Bench score**, a single numerical rating (on a scale approximately 1-10) assigned by a GPT-4 judge assessing the quality of model responses across multi-turn conversations spanning 8 task categories (Writing, Roleplay, Extraction, STEM, Humanities, Reasoning, Math, Coding).

- **Baselines.** The primary comparison in Table 3 includes three models: **Llama2-7B**, **DeepseekMoE-16B**, and **Gemma-2B**. For MT-Bench (Table 4 and Figure 3), additional baselines are included for context: **GPT-4** (score 9.014), **GPT-3.5-turbo** (7.995), **Claude-v1** (7.923), **Llama-2-13b-chat** (6.650), **Vicuna-13b-v1.3** (6.413), **Wizardlm-13b** (6.353), and **Llama-2-7b-chat** (6.269). These baselines are taken from prior published results on MT-Bench, not re-evaluated by the authors. Notably, the paper includes no ablation comparisons against JetMoE-8B variants with different architectural choices (e.g., dense attention, different expert counts, different top-k values), which the authors explicitly attribute to the $0.1M budget constraint in Section 7.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a generation budget as the unit of compute measurement — this is a descriptive paper reporting final trained model performance, not a test-time scaling study. The relevant resource accounting is the **training budget**: $0.1M total cost, using 30,000 H100 GPU hours and 1.25T training tokens. For inference, the efficiency comparison is made on a per-token activation basis: JetMoE-8B activates 2.2B parameters per token versus Llama2-7B's 7B, yielding approximately 70% inference computation reduction. At evaluation time, MT-Bench uses temperature settings of 0.7 (Writing, Roleplay), 0.0 (Extraction, Math, Coding, Reasoning), and 0.1 (STEM, Humanities) following the official FastChat configuration. Code benchmarks use greedy decoding (temperature 0, or equivalent deterministic sampling).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report any cross-validation or statistical significance testing. All results in Tables 3 and 4 are single-point evaluations on standard test sets. No confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical tests are reported for any of the benchmark comparisons. For MT-Bench, the evaluation uses a single LLM judge (GPT-4-0613), and no inter-annotator agreement or judging consistency metrics are reported — though this is standard practice for MT-Bench evaluations, it means the reliability of small score differences (e.g., JetMoE-8B-Chat at 6.681 versus Llama-2-13b-chat at 6.650) cannot be statistically assessed. The paper also does not specify whether the OpenLLM Leaderboard results are from a single evaluation run or averaged over multiple seeds, which matters because LLM benchmark performance can exhibit non-trivial variance across evaluation runs depending on prompting and sampling configuration.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### OpenLLM Leaderboard and Code Benchmarks

The headline result from Table 3 is that **JetMoE-8B achieves an OpenLLM Leaderboard average of 53.0%, outperforming Llama2-7B (51.0%), DeepseekMoE-16B (51.1%), and Gemma-2B (46.4%)**. When including code benchmarks, the All Average is 47.6%, compared to 45.5% for Llama2-7B, 47.3% for DeepseekMoE, and 43.2% for Gemma. This is the central quantitative claim: a model trained for under $0.1M on 1.25T tokens matches or exceeds models trained on 2T tokens at comparable or larger scales.

Breaking down by individual benchmarks reveals a more nuanced picture:

- **HellaSwag (commonsense reasoning):** JetMoE-8B achieves 80.5%, substantially outperforming Llama2-7B (78.6%), DeepseekMoE (79.8%), and Gemma (71.8%). This is JetMoE's strongest relative performance.

- **MMLU (multi-task knowledge):** JetMoE-8B scores 49.2%, outperforming Llama2-7B (46.9%), DeepseekMoE (46.3%), and Gemma (41.8%). The 2.3 percentage point gap over Llama2-7B on this knowledge-intensive benchmark is notable given the 0.75T token deficit in training data.

- **TruthfulQA (truthfulness):** JetMoE-8B scores 41.7%, substantially outperforming Llama2-7B (38.8%), DeepseekMoE (36.1%), and Gemma (33.1%). This is the largest relative gap among the non-code benchmarks.

- **GSM8k (grade-school math):** JetMoE-8B scores 27.8%, dramatically outperforming Llama2-7B (14.5%), DeepseekMoE (17.3%), and Gemma (16.9%). The near-doubling of Llama2-7B's math performance is the most striking individual result in Table 3 and likely reflects the heavy math emphasis in Phase 2 training data (OpenWebMath, algebraic-stack, TemplateGSM, StackMathQA collectively representing 5.8% of Phase 2 data plus whatever proportion was carried from Phase 1).

- **ARC-challenge (scientific reasoning):** JetMoE-8B scores 48.7%, underperforming Llama2-7B (53.1%) and DeepseekMoE (53.2%). This is one of two benchmarks where JetMoE-8B is below the Llama2 baseline, and the gap of approximately 4.4 percentage points is substantial.

- **WinoGrande (commonsense pronoun resolution):** JetMoE-8B scores 70.2%, underperforming Llama2-7B (74.0%) and DeepseekMoE (73.7%). This is the second benchmark where JetMoE-8B trails.

- **MBPP (Python programming):** JetMoE-8B scores 34.2%, matching DeepseekMoE (34.0%) and substantially outperforming Llama2-7B (20.8%) and Gemma (28.0%). This is a strong code generation result.

- **HumanEval (Python function completion):** JetMoE-8B scores 14.6%, underperforming DeepseekMoE (25.0%) and Gemma (24.4%), though slightly above Llama2-7B (12.8%). This is notably weak — worse than a 2B dense model (Gemma) — and the paper does not discuss this specific failure.

The pattern that emerges is that JetMoE-8B is particularly strong on knowledge-intensive (MMLU, TruthfulQA), mathematical (GSM8k), and commonsense reasoning (HellaSwag) tasks, but weaker on scientific reasoning (ARC-challenge) and certain code tasks (HumanEval). The paper does not provide per-task analysis or hypothesize about these patterns, but they are consistent with the data mixture: heavy math and textbook data in Phase 2 would boost MMLU, GSM8k, and TruthfulQA, while the relatively lower proportion of scientific reasoning data (only peS2o at 1.0% and arXiv at 1.0% in Phase 2) might explain the ARC-challenge weakness. The HumanEval weakness is harder to explain given the substantial code data (Starcoder at 19.6% in Phase 2 plus additional code SFT data at 3.8%), but may reflect that the code SFT data emphasizes instruction-following over algorithmic problem-solving.

#### MT-Bench Chat Performance

The headline from Table 4 is that **JetMoE-8B-Chat achieves an MT-Bench score of 6.681, surpassing Llama-2-13b-Chat (6.650)** — a model with roughly 6× the active parameters and substantially more training compute. This is the paper's primary alignment evaluation and the basis for the claim in the abstract that "JetMoE-8B-Chat surpasses the Llama2-13B-Chat model."

The score places JetMoE-8B-Chat between Llama-2-13b-chat (6.650) and GPT-4 (9.014), but much closer to the Llama-2-13b-chat end. The gap to proprietary frontier models is substantial: GPT-3.5-turbo achieves 7.995, Claude-v1 achieves 7.923, and GPT-4 achieves 9.014. JetMoE-8B-Chat is competitive with the 13B open-source models (Vicuna-13b-v1.3 at 6.413, Wizardlm-13b at 6.353) and comfortably ahead of Llama-2-7b-chat (6.269).

**Figure 3 (MT-Bench radar plot)** provides a per-category breakdown comparing JetMoE-8B-Chat to GPT-3.5-turbo. The paper states that JetMoE-8B-chat is "relatively weak in coding and extraction compared to GPT-3.5-turbo" and hypothesizes this "might be due to the smaller model size leading to suboptimal reasoning capability in these tasks." The radar plot shows that JetMoE-8B-Chat's strongest categories relative to GPT-3.5-turbo appear to be in areas like Writing, Roleplay, and Humanities (exact values are not tabulated in the paper — only the visual radar plot is provided). The coding weakness is consistent with the HumanEval result (14.6% pass@1).

A critical nuance for interpreting the MT-Bench result: the score difference between JetMoE-8B-Chat (6.681) and Llama-2-13b-chat (6.650) is only 0.031 points — less than half a percent. Without confidence intervals or test-retest reliability metrics, it is impossible to determine whether this difference is statistically meaningful or within the noise of the LLM-judge evaluation process. MT-Bench scores from GPT-4 judges are known to exhibit non-trivial variance depending on judge checkpoint, prompting format, and random seed. The paper's claim of "surpassing" Llama-2-13b-Chat should be understood as a point estimate with unknown reliability.

#### Training Cost Efficiency

While not a single quantitative result, the paper's cost claims require scrutiny against the experimental evidence. The $0.1M budget is broken down as 30,000 H100 GPU hours for pretraining plus 60 H100 GPU hours for alignment. At current cloud H100 pricing (roughly $2-4/GPU-hour at the time the paper was written, though prices vary substantially by provider and reservation type), 30,000 GPU-hours maps to approximately $60,000-$120,000 in compute cost, consistent with the claimed under-$0.1M budget inclusive of data processing, engineering time, and other overhead.

The efficiency comparison to Llama2-7B requires noting that **Llama2-7B's training cost is not publicly disclosed by Meta**, so the paper's implicit claim that JetMoE-8B achieves comparable performance at a fraction of the cost cannot be directly verified. Llama2-7B was trained on 2T tokens with a dense architecture, requiring approximately 7× more FLOPs per token than JetMoE-8B (since Llama2 activates all 7B parameters versus JetMoE's 2.2B). If we assume comparable hardware efficiency, Llama2-7B's training would cost roughly 2T/1.25T × 7B/2.2B ≈ 5.1× more FLOPs than JetMoE-8B, or approximately $0.5M at the same per-FLOP cost — still an order of magnitude below what most practitioners assume for Llama2-7B training costs. The discrepancy likely arises from the fact that JetMoE-8B's sparse training is less hardware-efficient per-FLOP than dense training (sparse operations have lower hardware utilization), so the cost-to-FLOP ratio is not directly comparable. The paper does not engage with this complexity, simply stating the $0.1M figure without detailed cost accounting methodology.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper contains **no formal ablation studies**. Section 7 explicitly states: "Due to the limited $100k budget, we can not afford any ablation study for the model architecture." This is a significant limitation that the paper is transparent about, but it means that essentially all design choices — dual sparse activation, shared KV projections, 8 experts with top-2, the specific Phase 2 data mixture percentages, the WSD schedule parameters, the load balancing loss weights — are asserted without empirical justification within this specific architecture and training setup. The paper instead relies on citations to prior work that justified these choices in other contexts: ModuleFormer for dual sparse activation, ST-MoE for the loss weights, miniCPM for the WSD schedule and phased data strategy. Whether these choices interact synergistically or whether some are irrelevant (or harmful) in combination is untested.

**What is implicitly ablated:** The comparison to Llama2-7B, DeepseekMoE, and Gemma in Table 3 serves as an implicit ablation of the overall JetMoE recipe against alternative architectures and training approaches. However, these comparisons confound multiple variables simultaneously — architecture (dense vs. MoE, sparse attention vs. dense attention), training data (JetMoE's custom mixture vs. each model's proprietary data), training tokens (1.25T vs. 2T), and training recipe (WSD schedule, phased data, load balancing losses). The observed performance differences cannot be attributed to any specific component of JetMoE-8B's design.

**What would have been informative but was not run:** Several targeted comparisons would substantially strengthen the paper's claims. A JetMoE-8B variant with dense attention (feed-forward-only MoE, like Mixtral) would isolate the contribution of the sparse attention mechanism. A variant with uniform data distribution throughout training (no Phase 2 enrichment) would test the phased data strategy's importance. A variant with a standard cosine learning rate schedule would isolate the WSD scheduler's contribution. A comparison to a 2B dense model trained on the exact same data mixture (to control for data quality effects) would help separate architectural efficiency from data quality effects. The paper acknowledges this gap in Section 7, noting that "in the future, it would be interesting to further study the actual contribution of different components to the final results."

**The MT-Bench radar plot as a qualitative analysis:** Figure 3 provides a per-category breakdown comparing JetMoE-8B-Chat to GPT-3.5-turbo, which serves as a qualitative diagnostic of the model's relative strengths and weaknesses. This is the closest the paper comes to an analysis of *where* the model's performance comes from, though it does not compare to the more relevant Llama2 baselines per-category, making it difficult to determine whether JetMoE-8B-Chat's category profile differs from other open-source chat models in meaningful ways.

### Critical Assessment

#### Do the experiments support the claim that JetMoE-8B "outperforms the Llama2-7B model"?

The OpenLLM Leaderboard average in Table 3 supports this claim directionally — 53.0 vs. 51.0 — but with important qualifications. First, the 2.0 percentage point gap in averages is driven by a few benchmarks with large gaps (GSM8k: +13.3 points, TruthfulQA: +2.9, HellaSwag: +1.9, MMLU: +2.3) partially offset by benchmarks where JetMoE-8B underperforms (ARC-challenge: -4.4, WinoGrande: -3.8). The "outperforms" claim is true in arithmetic mean but masks a *qualitatively uneven* performance profile — JetMoE-8B is not uniformly better than Llama2-7B; it is substantially better on some tasks and meaningfully worse on others. This matters for practitioners considering JetMoE-8B as a Llama2-7B replacement: the model you should choose depends on your task distribution, not just on the headline average.

Second, the comparison is between a 1.25T-token model (JetMoE-8B) and a 2T-token model (Llama2-7B). JetMoE-8B achieves its performance with 37.5% fewer training tokens, which is impressive, but this also means the comparison is not isolated to architecture — it confounds architecture, data composition, data quantity, and training recipe. If Llama2-7B were trained on JetMoE's data mixture for the same 1.25T tokens, would it perform better or worse? If JetMoE-8B were trained for 2T tokens, would the gap widen or narrow? These questions are unanswerable from the reported experiments.

Third, the paper does not evaluate Llama2-7B directly using its own code; the Llama2-7B numbers in Table 3 are taken from the OpenLLM Leaderboard, which aggregates results from multiple evaluators potentially using slightly different prompting formats, tokenization, or evaluation harnesses. The paper does not specify whether JetMoE-8B's evaluation exactly matches the OpenLLM Leaderboard's methodology for each compared model, which could introduce systematic variance. The statement "for most benchmarks, we use the same evaluation methodology as in the OpenLLM leaderboard to be comparable to other models" suggests but does not guarantee identical evaluation conditions.

#### Do the experiments support the claim that "JetMoE-8B-Chat surpasses the Llama2-13B-Chat model"?

The MT-Bench scores in Table 4 show JetMoE-8B-Chat at 6.681 versus Llama-2-13b-chat at 6.650 — a difference of 0.031 points, or approximately 0.5%. This is an extremely narrow margin. Without any reported measure of statistical reliability (confidence intervals, multiple evaluation runs, inter-judge agreement), it is impossible to determine whether this difference reflects a genuine performance gap or evaluation noise. MT-Bench evaluations using GPT-4 judges are known to be sensitive to prompt formatting, judge model version, and even the ordering of responses. A difference of 0.031 out of ~10 is within the range that could plausibly arise from these sources of variance.

The more defensible interpretation of the MT-Bench results is that **JetMoE-8B-Chat achieves roughly comparable chat quality to Llama-2-13b-Chat despite having ~6× fewer active parameters and much lower training cost**. This is still a meaningful result — achieving 13B-class chat performance from a model that activates only 2.2B parameters per token is a significant efficiency demonstration. But the paper's claim of *surpassing* specifically is not rigorously supported given the razor-thin margin and absence of statistical characterization. The paper would be stronger if it acknowledged this uncertainty rather than treating the point estimate as definitive.

#### Do the experiments support the claim that "LLM training can be much more cost-effective than generally thought"?

This is the paper's most important and most defensible claim, but it needs careful scope definition. The experiments demonstrate that **for under $0.1M in compute, using entirely public data, it is possible to train an 8B-parameter MoE model that is competitive with Llama2-7B (a model whose training cost, while undisclosed, is widely assumed to be substantially higher)**. This is a genuine existence proof that challenges the assumption that competitive LLM training requires multi-million-dollar budgets.

However, "much more cost-effective than generally thought" is a vague claim that depends on what "generally thought" means. The paper does not establish a clear baseline expectation — it does not cite specific cost estimates for comparable models that the field believed were necessary, does not survey prior beliefs about minimum viable training budgets, and does not engage with existing work on cost-efficient LLM training (such as the literature on data-efficient pretraining, distillation, or staged training). The claim lands rhetorically but is not empirically grounded in a well-defined prior that is being refuted.

Furthermore, the $0.1M figure covers only GPU compute for the final training run. It explicitly excludes the cost of data curation (the paper used existing public datasets, but accessing, filtering, deduplicating, and mixing them requires engineering time and compute), the cost of hyperparameter tuning (the paper handpicked values based on prior work, which represents implicit experimentation costs absorbed by the community), the cost of failed or exploratory runs, and the cost of the human expertise required to engineer the training pipeline, integrate Megatron with Megablocks, and implement the custom MoA and z-loss modifications. In industrial settings, the fully-loaded cost of producing a model like JetMoE-8B — including all R&D, failed experiments, data engineering, and personnel — would be substantially higher than $0.1M. The paper's cost claim is better understood as a marginal compute cost for the final reproducible training run given pre-existing infrastructure, expertise, and data curation pipelines, not as the total cost for an independent team starting from scratch.

#### Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design

**No ablation studies whatsoever (Section 7 acknowledges this).** This is the most significant experimental limitation. Every architectural and training choice — dual sparse activation, 8 experts, top-2, shared KV projections, WSD schedule, Phase 2 data composition, load balancing loss weights — is made based on prior work or intuition, with no empirical evidence that any specific choice contributes positively in this particular configuration. It is entirely possible that some of these choices are unnecessary or even harmful, and that a simpler configuration would achieve comparable or better performance at even lower cost. The paper's value as a scientific contribution is therefore limited to existence proof ("this specific recipe works") rather than understanding ("these are the components that matter and why").

**Single training run with no error characterization.** All results in Tables 3 and 4 are from a single pretraining run and a single alignment run. Training LLMs involves substantial randomness (data ordering, dropout, distributed training non-determinism), and final benchmark performance can vary non-trivially across training seeds (as documented in, e.g., the Pythia suite, Biderman et al., 2023). Without multiple training runs or even multiple evaluation runs, the reported numbers represent a single sample from an unknown distribution. This makes it impossible to determine whether JetMoE-8B's advantage over Llama2-7B on the OpenLLM Leaderboard average (53.0 vs. 51.0) would replicate with a different random seed — a 2-point difference could plausibly be within the training variance.

**Confounded comparison to Llama2-7B.** The two models differ on architecture (MoE vs. dense), training data (JetMoE custom public mixture vs. Meta's proprietary mixture), training tokens (1.25T vs. 2T), learning rate schedule (WSD vs. cosine), and model size (8B total/2.2B active vs. 7B dense). Any of these differences could account for the performance gap. The paper cannot attribute the result to architectural efficiency specifically — the data mixture alone, which includes substantial Phase 2 enrichment with high-quality synthetic and curated data (UltraTextbooks, OpenHermes, math SFT data), could drive most of the observed improvements, especially on knowledge-intensive tasks like MMLU and TruthfulQA. A 2B dense model trained on the exact same JetMoE data mixture would be a much more informative control, isolating the architecture contribution from the data contribution.

**Small evaluation breadth for the cost claim.** The paper evaluates on the OpenLLM Leaderboard tasks, two code benchmarks, and MT-Bench — a standard but relatively narrow evaluation suite compared to modern comprehensive LLM evaluations (which often include dozens of benchmarks spanning reasoning, knowledge, safety, bias, multilinguality, and long-context capabilities). The paper does not evaluate on reading comprehension (e.g., SQuAD, Natural Questions beyond what's in MMLU), summarization, translation, safety benchmarks (e.g., TruthfulQA is included but no adversarial safety evaluations), or bias/fairness metrics. The claim of "competitive performance" is therefore scoped to the specific benchmarks evaluated, and may not generalize to the broader capabilities expected of a general-purpose LLM.

**No inference efficiency benchmarking.** The paper claims approximately 70% inference computation reduction compared to Llama2-7B based on active parameter counts (2.2B vs. 7B), but does not provide any actual inference latency or throughput measurements. Sparse MoE models have unique inference characteristics — expert routing decisions add latency, the shared KV projections change memory access patterns, and the dropless MoE implementation may have different batch-size scaling properties than dense models. The claimed efficiency gains are theoretical and based on FLOP counting, not on measured wall-clock performance. For practitioners deciding whether to deploy JetMoE-8B instead of Llama2-7B, actual throughput and latency numbers on standard hardware would be essential information that the paper does not provide.

**MT-Bench evaluation limitations.** Using a single LLM judge (GPT-4-0613) with no reported reliability metrics makes the small score margins difficult to interpret. Additionally, MT-Bench evaluates only 80 multi-turn conversations across 8 categories (10 per category), which is a very small sample for reliable comparison — a difference of 0.031 in average score could be driven by performance differences on just a handful of questions. The paper does not report per-category MT-Bench scores against Llama2-13b-chat (only against GPT-3.5-turbo in Figure 3), so the claim of surpassing Llama2-13b-chat cannot be diagnosed — we don't know whether the advantage is uniform across categories or concentrated in a few where JetMoE happens to perform well.

**Missing comparisons to similarly-priced models.** The paper compares JetMoE-8B to models that are either significantly more expensive to train (Llama2-7B, DeepseekMoE-16B) or significantly smaller (Gemma-2B). A more informative comparison for the cost-effectiveness claim would be to other models in the ~$0.1M training budget range — for example, how does JetMoE-8B compare to the best model that can be trained for $100K using a dense architecture on the same data? Without this comparison, the paper demonstrates that MoE can achieve good performance at low cost, but not that MoE is *more* cost-effective than dense architectures at the same budget — it might be that an equivalently-priced dense model trained identically except for architecture would perform similarly or better.

#### Experiments that would have strengthened the paper

**A dense baseline trained on the same data mixture with the same compute budget.** This is the single most informative missing experiment. Train a ~2B-parameter dense model (matching JetMoE's active parameter count) or a ~8B dense model (matching total parameters) on the exact JetMoE data mixture for the same 30,000 H100 GPU hours. This would isolate the architecture effect from the data effect and directly answer the question: does the MoE architecture actually improve performance per dollar compared to a dense model, or is the entire benefit coming from the data mixture and training recipe?

**A JetMoE variant with dense attention (feed-forward MoE only).** This would isolate the contribution of the sparse attention mechanism — the paper's primary architectural novelty — from the feed-forward MoE (which is standard). If a JetMoE with dense attention performs similarly, then the sparse attention is not contributing to performance and the additional complexity is unjustified.

**Multiple training runs with different random seeds.** Even 2-3 runs would allow reporting mean and standard deviation for the key benchmarks, providing the statistical characterization necessary to evaluate whether the reported performance gaps are reliable.

**Inference latency and throughput measurements on standard hardware.** Comparing JetMoE-8B's actual tokens-per-second and time-to-first-token against Llama2-7B on the same GPU would validate the claimed 70% inference efficiency improvement and provide the practical deployment information that the architectural claims depend on.

**Ablation of Phase 2 data enrichment.** A training run where the same total tokens are trained with a uniform data mixture throughout (no Phase 2 enrichment) would quantify the contribution of the phased data strategy, which the paper presents as critical but never empirically validates within this specific setup.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: Zero Ablation Studies Mean Every Design Choice Is an Untested Hypothesis

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper makes numerous architectural and training decisions — dual sparse activation on both attention and feed-forward layers, 8 experts with top-2 routing, shared key-value projections across attention experts, the WSD learning rate schedule, the specific Phase 2 data mixture percentages, the load balancing loss weights (α = 0.01, β = 0.001) — all based on prior work and intuition rather than empirical validation within this specific configuration. Section 7 acknowledges this directly:

> "Due to the limited $100k budget, we can not afford any ablation study for the model architecture. The hyperparameters and data mixtures are also handpicked based on the empirical results from previous works (Shen et al., 2023; Zoph et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2024)."

**The consequence:** The paper's central claim — that dual sparse activation combined with a carefully phased data mixture enables competitive performance at low cost — cannot be decomposed into the contributions of individual components. It is entirely possible that some design choices are unnecessary or even harmful. For instance, the sparse attention mechanism (the paper's primary architectural novelty) might contribute negligibly to performance while adding implementation complexity, and a dense-attention variant with the same data mixture might achieve comparable results. Alternatively, the Phase 2 data enrichment might drive most of the observed gains, making the MoE architecture incidental rather than causal. A practitioner attempting to reproduce or extend this work cannot prioritize which design elements to preserve and which to modify — the recipe works as a whole, but the active ingredients are unknown.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** No ablation experiments whatsoever. The only comparative evidence comes from Table 3, which compares the full JetMoE-8B recipe against entirely different models (Llama2-7B, DeepseekMoE-16B, Gemma-2B) that differ simultaneously on architecture, training data, training tokens, and training recipe. These comparisons confound all variables and cannot isolate any single design choice's contribution. The MT-Bench radar plot (Figure 3) provides per-category performance relative to GPT-3.5-turbo but does not compare against models that isolate architectural choices.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation. Section 7 notes that "in the future, it would be interesting to further study the actual contribution of different components to the final results," explicitly deferring the ablation analysis to future work by the community. Given the paper's framing as a platform for future research rather than a finished optimization, this transparency about the limitation is appropriate, but it means the paper provides an existence proof ("this specific recipe works") rather than understanding ("these components matter and here is why").

### Limitation 2: The Difficulty Estimation Cost Problem — The $0.1M Figure Excludes Costs Necessary for Reproducibility

This limitation is structurally similar to the one I identified in the reference example's Section 6, where the compute-optimal paper's difficulty estimation cost was unaccounted for. Here, the analog is the **implicit and excluded costs** of data curation, hyperparameter selection, and infrastructure engineering.

**The assumption or constraint:** The $0.1M figure represents the marginal GPU compute cost for the final pretraining run (30,000 H100 hours) plus alignment (60 H100 hours). This figure explicitly excludes:

- **Data curation costs:** The paper uses established public datasets (RefinedWeb, StarCoder, The Pile, Dolma, etc.), but these datasets were produced by other teams at substantial cost. Accessing, downloading, filtering, deduplicating, mixing, and preprocessing 1.25T tokens of data requires significant engineering time, storage, and compute that is not included in the reported budget. The Phase 2 data mixture (Table 2) includes synthetically generated datasets (OpenHermes 2.5, TemplateGSM, Magicoder, UltraChat, UltraTextbooks) that were created using expensive teacher models (GPT-4, ChatGPT) — this distillation cost is absorbed by the dataset creators, not by JetMoE-8B's budget.

- **Hyperparameter selection:** The paper handpicks hyperparameters "based on the common practice for the 1B transformer language model" (Section 4.2) and "from previous works" (Section 7). This represents absorbed knowledge from prior experimentation — both by the community (ST-MoE, miniCPM, ModuleFormer) and by the authors themselves in developing the training pipeline. The cost of running the experiments that established these defaults (the learning rate of 5e-4, the 8-expert configuration, the WSD schedule parameters, the load balancing loss weights) is not included.

- **Infrastructure engineering:** The paper modified Megatron-LM and Megablocks to support Mixture of Attention Heads and z-loss computation (Section 4.1). This software engineering effort — which requires deep expertise in distributed training frameworks, CUDA kernels, and model parallelism strategies — is not costed. A team without this pre-existing expertise would face substantial personnel and time costs to reproduce the training infrastructure.

- **Failed runs and exploratory experiments:** The paper reports only the final successful training run. Any failed attempts, debugging cycles, or exploratory configurations are excluded from the budget.

**The consequence:** The $0.1M figure is best understood as the marginal compute cost of the final reproducible training run given access to (a) pre-existing curated public datasets, (b) pre-existing knowledge of effective hyperparameters from prior work, (c) a pre-built and modified training framework, and (d) the expertise to operate it. A research lab starting from scratch — without the datasets pre-downloaded, without the modified Megatron-Megablocks integration, without the implicit hyperparameter knowledge — would face substantially higher total costs (easily 2-5× the reported $0.1M, depending on personnel costs and infrastructure maturity). The claim that "LLM training can be much more cost-effective than generally thought" is therefore scoped to the marginal compute of the final training run under favorable preconditions, not to the fully-loaded cost of independent reproduction.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The paper does not itemize excluded costs or discuss the gap between marginal compute cost and total reproduction cost. The $0.1M figure is stated in the abstract and introduction without detailed accounting methodology. The paper acknowledges that "all training parameters and data mixtures have been detailed in this report" — which reduces the hidden-knowledge barrier — but does not account for the cost of producing those data mixtures or the infrastructure they run on. The fact that the alignment phase takes "60 H100 GPU hours" is helpfully specific, but no analogous specificity is provided for data preprocessing, framework modification, or hyperparameter tuning.

**Mitigation status:** The paper partially mitigates this by providing full training recipe transparency — the data mixture (Table 2), hyperparameters (Table 1), learning rate schedule, and parallelism strategy are all specified, reducing the need for downstream teams to rediscover them. But the fundamental cost gap between marginal compute and total reproduction cost is not addressed or acknowledged. The paper implicitly relies on the open-source community's existing investments (in datasets, frameworks, and prior experimentation) while claiming the resulting cost as a property of the architecture and training methodology. A more complete cost accounting would distinguish between costs absorbed by the ecosystem and costs borne by the reproducing team.

### Limitation 3: Performance Is Uneven Across Tasks, with Meaningful Weaknesses Unexplored

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper reports aggregate OpenLLM Leaderboard averages (53.0 vs. 51.0 for Llama2-7B) and presents the headline claim that JetMoE-8B "outperforms the Llama2-7B model." This aggregate claim masks substantial per-task variance that has practical consequences for model selection.

**The consequence:** A practitioner choosing between JetMoE-8B and Llama2-7B based on the headline average would be misled if their application skews toward tasks where JetMoE-8B is weaker. Specifically, from Table 3:

- **ARC-challenge (scientific reasoning):** JetMoE-8B scores 48.7% versus Llama2-7B's 53.1% — a deficit of 4.4 percentage points, or approximately 8.3% relative degradation. This is a non-trivial gap on a benchmark that tests multi-step scientific reasoning, suggesting JetMoE-8B may underperform on tasks requiring structured scientific inference.

- **WinoGrande (pronoun resolution):** JetMoE-8B scores 70.2% versus Llama2-7B's 74.0% — a deficit of 3.8 percentage points. This task requires resolving ambiguous pronoun references, a capability important for discourse understanding and conversational coherence.

- **HumanEval (Python function completion):** JetMoE-8B scores 14.6%, which is worse than Gemma-2B (24.4%) — a model with approximately the same number of active parameters but a dense architecture. This is a striking underperformance on a standard code generation benchmark, especially given the substantial code data in JetMoE-8B's training mixture (Starcoder at 19.6% of Phase 2 data plus additional code SFT data). The paper does not discuss this result, despite it being the largest relative weakness in the evaluation suite.

The practical implication is that JetMoE-8B is not a drop-in replacement for Llama2-7B across all tasks. On knowledge-intensive (MMLU, TruthfulQA), mathematical (GSM8k), and commonsense reasoning (HellaSwag) tasks, JetMoE-8B shows clear advantages. On scientific reasoning, pronoun resolution, and certain code generation tasks, it meaningfully underperforms. The paper's aggregate claim obscures this heterogeneity.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Table 3 provides the per-task breakdown, so the data exists for readers to discover this pattern themselves. However, the paper's text does not discuss the ARC-challenge, WinoGrande, or HumanEval weaknesses — the narrative focuses on the tasks where JetMoE-8B leads. The MT-Bench radar plot (Figure 3) shows that JetMoE-8B-Chat is "relatively weak in coding and extraction compared to GPT-3.5-turbo," which the paper attributes to "the smaller model size leading to suboptimal reasoning capability in these tasks." But this hypothesis is not tested — there is no comparison to actual smaller dense models to determine whether the weakness is a general property of 2B-active-parameter models or specific to the JetMoE architecture and training recipe.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not investigate or mitigate the task-specific weaknesses. The data mixture provides some clues — the relatively low proportion of scientific text (arXiv at 1.0%, peS2o at 1.0% in Phase 2) might explain the ARC-challenge weakness, while the HumanEval weakness is harder to explain given the abundant code data. Future work could address this through targeted data augmentation for underperforming domains or through architectural modifications to improve the specific reasoning patterns these tasks require. Without ablation studies isolating the causes of these weaknesses, practitioners cannot confidently predict whether similar weaknesses would appear on related but unevaluated tasks.

### Limitation 4: No Inference Efficiency Validation — the 70% Compute Reduction Claim Is Theoretical

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper claims that JetMoE-8B's architecture "reduc[es] inference computation by about 70% compared to Llama2-7B" (Section 1), based on the ratio of active parameters: JetMoE-8B activates 2.2B parameters per token while Llama2-7B activates all 7B, yielding a ~3.2:1 ratio (approximately 69% reduction). This is a FLOP-counting argument that assumes sparse expert computation has the same hardware efficiency as dense computation.

**The consequence:** In practice, sparse MoE inference has unique overheads that FLOP counting does not capture:

- **Expert routing overhead:** For every token, the router must compute affinity scores for all 8 experts in both the attention and feed-forward layers (24 layers × 2 router types = 48 routing decisions per token, each involving an 8-way dot product). While the router is linear and relatively cheap, it adds latency on the critical path and requires gathering and scattering token embeddings to the correct expert computations. With top-2 activation, tokens must be dispatched to 2 of 8 experts and their results combined — this token routing introduces communication and scheduling overhead not present in dense models.

- **Memory access patterns:** Sparse expert computation involves irregular memory access — different tokens go to different experts, meaning the GPU must load different weight matrices for different tokens in the same batch. This breaks the regular, predictable memory access patterns that dense matrix multiplications rely on for high hardware utilization. The dropless MoE implementation (Gale et al., 2023) mitigates this but does not eliminate it — sparse kernels typically achieve lower FLOP utilization (percentage of theoretical peak) than dense kernels.

- **Batch size sensitivity:** Sparse MoE models require sufficiently large batch sizes for load balancing to work effectively — with small batches, the router may assign uneven numbers of tokens to different experts, causing some expert computations to be underutilized while others become bottlenecks. This matters for latency-sensitive applications (e.g., interactive chat with batch size 1) where load imbalance cannot be amortized over many tokens.

- **KV cache and memory footprint:** JetMoE-8B has 8B total parameters that must be stored in memory even though only 2.2B are active per token. The KV cache for attention — even with shared key-value projections — must store keys and values for all tokens in the context, and the memory footprint scales with total model size rather than active parameter count. For long-context inference, memory rather than compute is often the bottleneck, and JetMoE-8B's 8B parameter memory footprint is larger than Llama2-7B's 7B.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** None. The paper provides no inference latency measurements, no throughput benchmarks, no memory footprint analysis, and no comparison of tokens-per-second between JetMoE-8B and Llama2-7B on any hardware configuration. The 70% reduction claim is purely architectural — a comparison of theoretical multiply-add operations based on active parameter counts — with no empirical validation of actual wall-clock performance.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not acknowledge or attempt to measure this limitation. The inference efficiency claim is presented without qualification, and the absence of empirical validation is not discussed. For a paper whose core value proposition is efficiency — both in training cost and inference compute — the lack of inference benchmarking is a significant gap. A practitioner considering deployment would need to run their own latency and throughput benchmarks on their target hardware to determine whether the theoretical FLOP reduction translates to actual cost savings, and whether the memory overhead of 8B total parameters erodes the benefits in memory-constrained settings.

### Limitation 5: Training-Inference Token Ratio Confounds the Cost-Effectiveness Claim — the Model Trains on 1.25T Tokens but Generates for an Unknown Number

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper's cost-effectiveness argument centers on comparing training cost to achieved performance, without accounting for the *deployment scale* at which the model will be used. This is the inference side of the training-inference tradeoff — analogous to the FLOPs-matched comparison framework in the reference example paper (Section 7), but entirely absent here.

**The consequence:** The economic case for JetMoE-8B depends heavily on how many tokens the model generates during its operational lifetime relative to how many tokens it was trained on. If we define `$R = D_{\text{inference}} / D_{\text{pretrain}}$` (the ratio of total inference tokens to training tokens), then:

- **For low `$R$` scenarios** (e.g., research prototyping, one-time evaluation, generating a fixed corpus of synthetic data): The training cost dominates total expenditure, and JetMoE-8B's low training budget ($0.1M) is the primary economic advantage. The ~70% inference compute reduction is a secondary benefit that matters little in absolute terms because total inference tokens are small.

- **For high `$R$` scenarios** (e.g., production chatbot serving millions of queries per day, API services, large-scale batch inference): The inference cost dominates, and JetMoE-8B's per-token efficiency advantage (activating 2.2B vs. 7B parameters) becomes the primary economic driver. However, without empirical inference throughput measurements (see Limitation 4), the magnitude of this advantage cannot be quantified. Moreover, in high-volume deployments, the cost of serving infrastructure, networking, and reliability engineering often dominates per-token compute costs — the inference compute savings from sparse activation may be a small fraction of total operational expenditure.

- **For very high `$R$` scenarios**: If the model generates orders of magnitude more tokens than it was trained on, a small per-token efficiency disadvantage of a dense model (e.g., a hypothetical 2B dense model trained for $0.2M on the same data) could be more than offset by the dense model's simpler serving infrastructure and higher hardware utilization. The paper provides no framework for assessing this tradeoff.

The paper also does not discuss how the 2.2B active parameters per token compares to alternative architectures at the same inference budget. A dense 2B-parameter model would activate all 2B parameters per token (same as JetMoE-8B) but with higher hardware utilization and no routing overhead. If a dense 2B model trained on the same data mixture achieved comparable performance to JetMoE-8B, it would be strictly preferable for inference — same per-token compute, simpler serving, lower memory footprint. The paper does not evaluate such a baseline (see Section 5's discussion of missing comparisons).

**What evidence exists in the paper:** None. The paper reports only training cost (30,000 H100 hours for 1.25T tokens) and architectural activation ratios (2.2B/8B), with no analysis of how these trade off against each other at different deployment scales. The `$D_{\text{inference}} / D_{\text{pretrain}}$` ratio is never discussed or parameterized.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not address this limitation. The cost-effectiveness claim is implicitly scoped to training cost only, with inference efficiency mentioned as an additional benefit but not integrated into the economic analysis. A more complete treatment would provide: (1) empirical inference throughput measurements at various batch sizes and sequence lengths, (2) a comparison to dense models with the same active parameter count (2B) trained on the same data, and (3) an analysis of the break-even `$R$` ratio at which JetMoE-8B's training cost advantage is offset by any inference efficiency disadvantages relative to alternative architectures.

### Limitation 6: Single Training Run and Single Evaluation Mean No Statistical Reliability for the Headline Comparisons

**The assumption or constraint:** All results in Tables 3 and 4 come from a single training run and (for each benchmark) a single evaluation pass. The paper reports no standard deviations, no confidence intervals, no multiple-seed training runs, and no test-retest reliability measurements. The OpenLLM Leaderboard comparison scores for Llama2-7B, DeepseekMoE, and Gemma are taken from the public leaderboard, which aggregates results from different evaluators potentially using slightly different evaluation configurations.

**The consequence:** The narrow margins on key comparisons — JetMoE-8B's 53.0 vs. Llama2-7B's 51.0 OpenLLM Leaderboard average (a 2.0-point gap), and JetMoE-8B-Chat's 6.681 vs. Llama-2-13b-chat's 6.650 MT-Bench score (a 0.031-point gap) — cannot be interpreted as reliable evidence of superiority without statistical characterization. LLM training is subject to substantial randomness: data ordering, dropout, distributed training non-determinism (arising from floating-point non-associativity in parallel reductions), and random weight initialization all contribute to variation in final model performance across training seeds. The Pythia project (Biderman et al., 2023) has documented that identically-architected models trained with different random seeds can exhibit non-trivial performance variation on downstream benchmarks. Whether a 2-point OpenLLM Leaderboard difference exceeds this seed-to-seed variance is unknown for JetMoE-8B's architecture and training setup.

The MT-Bench comparison is particularly fragile. The 0.031-point gap (6.681 vs. 6.650) represents approximately 0.5% of the score range. MT-Bench uses 80 multi-turn conversations judged by a single LLM (GPT-4-0613), and LLM-based evaluation is known to exhibit sensitivity to prompt formatting, response ordering, and judge model version. Without multiple evaluation runs or inter-judge reliability metrics, a difference of this magnitude could plausibly arise from evaluation noise rather than genuine chat quality differences. The paper's claim that JetMoE-8B-Chat "surpasses" Llama-2-13b-Chat should be interpreted as a point estimate from a single evaluation with unknown reliability.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The paper provides point estimates only — single numbers per benchmark per model. Section 5 specifies that code benchmarks use greedy decoding with mean pass@1 reported, but the number of evaluation runs contributing to that mean and whether pass@1 is computed over multiple generations or a single greedy output is not detailed. The MT-Bench evaluation (Section 6) specifies temperature settings per task category and the use of GPT-4-0613 as judge, but does not report the number of evaluation passes, confidence intervals, or judge agreement rates. The OpenLLM Leaderboard results for JetMoE-8B are not accompanied by standard deviations or information about the evaluation framework version used.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not acknowledge this limitation or attempt to mitigate it through multiple runs, bootstrap confidence intervals, or sensitivity analysis. Given the $0.1M budget constraint, running multiple pretraining seeds would be prohibitively expensive — but running multiple evaluation passes (with different random seeds for sampling-based benchmarks) would be essentially free and would provide at least within-model variance estimates for JetMoE-8B. For the comparison models (Llama2-7B, DeepseekMoE, Gemma), the paper relies on publicly reported scores from the OpenLLM Leaderboard, which has its own consistency properties and version history that the paper does not discuss. Future work could address this by releasing evaluation harness configurations and multiple-seed evaluation results to enable more rigorous comparison, even if multiple training runs remain infeasible.
