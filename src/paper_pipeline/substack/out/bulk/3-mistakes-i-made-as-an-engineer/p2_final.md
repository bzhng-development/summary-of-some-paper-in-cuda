Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (the host turns the mic on himself, reflecting on three mistakes he made as an engineer that only became visible once he stepped into a management role):

**People problems are as important as "real" problems**

As an IC, Peterman watched several engineers on other teams become unhappy, grow less productive, and eventually leave the company—and each time, he simply didn't pay attention, keeping his focus locked on his own projects. The pattern only became legible after he became a manager and gained a higher-level view of how everyone was doing. From that vantage point, it was "much more obvious how people problems affect the team even before anyone decides to leave." The insight that landed hardest: having motivated, happy teammates is not a soft cultural nicety—it is *critical* to getting things done. For that reason, **people problems are just as important as problems with the code itself**. The framing reclassifies morale and interpersonal friction from a distraction to a first-class engineering concern.

**Hiring well is one of the best uses of your time**

When he was an engineer, Peterman always ignored hiring. It felt like a side hobby compared to shipping projects that had direct business impact. After hiring three engineers in a single year and watching the impact they had, he calls his previous stance "shocked at how wrong I was." The arithmetic he now uses: even if you work extra hours, you might—if you're lucky—contribute an additional 50% to your team. But if you hire just one strong engineer, your team gains an additional 100% of your productivity. And hiring, he notes, takes much less time than working longer hours. The unspoken flip side is that *not* hiring is an invisible tax; every month a strong candidate isn't in the seat is a month of lost multiplicative output that no amount of personal crunch can recover.

**Get to know your coworkers**

As a junior engineer, Peterman only focused on his code. He never went to social events because he was so intent on having an impact—a degree of focus he now calls "a little extreme," though he acknowledges many engineers share a similar mindset. The problem, he came to see, is that "all projects of consequence come from working together with others." If you don't build connections with relevant people ahead of time, you'll only realize it too late—when you finally reach out and they aren't responsive. He was lucky to have an excellent manager early on who did that cross-team relationship work on his behalf, making other teams help him and his teammates. Now that a large part of his own job is doing the same for others, he sees how essential it is. Junior engineers, he warns, focus a little too much on just the code; there are plenty of other areas where a small time investment compounds enormously. His closing self-assessment—"Are you blind like I was?"—frames the whole piece not as a lecture but as a former blind spot confessed in public.

---

The thread connecting all three mistakes is a version of **people problems are just as important as problems with the code itself**—a frame that reclassifies relationship-building, morale, and hiring from "soft" side concerns into first-order engineering leverage. His **hiring arithmetic** (one strong hire = 100% productivity gain vs. a theoretical 50% from crunch) gives that frame a concrete, almost ruthless efficiency logic. And the confession that he only saw these things after becoming a manager—when the higher-level view made the patterns "much more obvious"—doubles as both a mea culpa and a shortcut for engineers still in the blind spot.
