# Reinforcement Learning from Contrastive Distillation for LM Alignment

**ArXiv:** [2307.12950](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.12950)

## 🎯 Pitch

RLCD generates cleaner preference pairs without human labels by simply prompting the same model with contrasting instructions (e.g., 'be harmless' vs. 'be harmful'), and using the difference to train a reward model. It decisively beats RLAIF—GPT-4 prefers RLCD outputs up to 85% of the time—because contrasting prompts create far more distinguishable output pairs than scoring identical-prompt samples.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **Reinforcement Learning from Contrastive Distillation (RLCD)**, a method for aligning language models to natural-language principles without human feedback — applied to harmlessness, helpfulness, and story outline generation using LLaMA-7B and LLaMA-30B. RLCD constructs preference pairs by generating outputs from two contrasting prompts: a positive prompt that encourages the desired attribute (e.g., ending with "Assistant (harmless):") and a negative prompt that encourages violating it (e.g., "Assistant (harmful):"), then automatically labels the positive-prompt output as preferred, bypassing the post hoc AI scoring step used in RLAIF. When simulating preference data with LLaMA-7B, RLCD outperforms both RLAIF and context distillation baselines by substantial margins across all three tasks — for instance, GPT-4 prefers RLCD outputs over RLAIF outputs at rates of 84.8% on harmlessness and 85.4% on helpfulness — while at 30B scale performance is equal or better, establishing that contrastive prompt construction can substitute for scoring-based labeling only when the base model is too weak to reliably discriminate its own i.i.d. outputs.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Aligning LLMs Without Expensive Human Feedback

The fundamental challenge this paper tackles is practical and urgent: **how do you align a large language model to follow principles like harmlessness and helpfulness when obtaining human feedback at scale is prohibitively expensive?** Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as the dominant paradigm for LLM alignment, powering systems like ChatGPT and Claude (Bai et al., 2022a; OpenAI, 2023). But RLHF's core dependency — thousands of human-annotated pairwise preferences comparing model outputs — creates a bottleneck: collecting high-quality human labels is costly, time-consuming, and logistically complex, particularly for the scale of data needed to train robust preference models.

This gap matters for several practical reasons the paper implicitly addresses:

- **Democratizing alignment research**: If aligning a model requires access to large-scale human annotation pipelines (as major industrial labs possess), academic researchers and smaller organizations are locked out of developing and studying alignment techniques. A method that works without human feedback lowers this barrier.
- **Iterative improvement speed**: RLHF pipelines typically involve multiple rounds of data collection, training, and deployment. Each round currently requires new human annotations. Automating this step could dramatically accelerate iteration cycles for alignment research.
- **Scaling alignment to new domains**: Human feedback is particularly scarce in specialized domains (medicine, law, low-resource languages). An automatic method that can generate preference data from the model itself using only natural-language principles could extend alignment to domains where human annotator expertise is rare.

### Prior Approaches and Their Limitations

The paper identifies two existing strategies for reducing or eliminating human feedback in alignment — both partially successful but each with a distinct weakness that RLCD aims to resolve.

#### RLAIF: Simulating Human Preferences with the Model Itself

Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback, exemplified by Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022b), replaces human annotators with the LLM itself: for a given prompt $p$, the model generates two outputs $o_1$ and $o_2$, then the same model (or a different LLM) scores which output is preferred by examining both outputs in context and applying a scoring prompt (e.g., "Please choose the response that is less harmful"). The resulting preference labels — continuous probabilities from the model's scoring — are used to train a preference model, which then guides PPO fine-tuning.

**Where RLAIF falls short.** The paper identifies a specific, subtle failure mode: **low signal-to-noise ratio in the preference labels** because $o_1$ and $o_2$ are generated i.i.d. from the same prompt $p$. When a model generates two independent outputs from identical input, they often have similar quality on the attribute of interest — making it genuinely difficult to determine which is better. The scoring LLM must then make fine-grained distinctions near the decision boundary, a task it performs unreliably. Table 1 illustrates this concretely: both RLAIF outputs from LLaMA-7B advise a human to accomplish an unethical objective (making a false claim), and the scoring model assigns preference probabilities of 0.422 vs. 0.577 — a weak, noisy signal. The paper shows empirically that RLAIF7B's preference model achieves only **35.6% binary accuracy** against human labels on harmlessness data (worse than chance!) and assigns average probabilities of just 0.492 to the gold human-preferred output (Table 5) — essentially random.

This problem is not merely about overall accuracy; it is worst precisely where training signal matters most. In classification, examples near the decision boundary are the most informative for learning — but RLAIF's automatically-labeled boundary examples are also the noisiest. The paper frames this as trading off example hardness for label correctness: RLAIF produces many hard-to-discriminate pairs with unreliable labels, degrading the preference model's ability to learn the true human preference function.

There is a subtle but important additional consideration: the RLAIF scoring step requires placing both outputs $o_1$ and $o_2$ in the model's context window simultaneously. For longer-form outputs, this doubles the effective context length needed during the labeling phase, which taxes the model's attention mechanisms (Liu et al., 2023) and increases computational cost. This is not just an implementation detail — it means RLAIF's labeling quality may degrade further as output length increases, even before considering the difficulty of the discrimination task itself.

Bai et al. (2022b) already observed limitations in their own work: they found that RLAIF requires both **few-shot prompting** (providing example preference judgments in the scoring prompt) and **model scale well above 10B parameters** to achieve above-chance agreement with human preferences on harmlessness. The RLCD paper replicates and extends this finding (Appendix C): RLAIF7B without few-shot examples produces a preference model below human-chance accuracy; adding few-shot examples (RLAIF-Few30B) raises agreement but introduces a new problem — **mode collapse** toward a single generic harmless response ("I'm sorry, but I'm not sure how to help with that"), as shown in Table 17. This mode collapse likely occurs because the few-shot examples emphasize harmlessness without balancing helpfulness, demonstrating that even when RLAIF works, it remains brittle to prompt design.

#### Context Distillation: Directional Prompting Without Preferences

Context distillation approaches (Askell et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2023) take a different route: rather than generating preference pairs for RL, they modify the input prompt $p$ to create $p^+$ — a version that includes additional context encouraging the desired attribute (e.g., appending "Assistant (giving a helpful response):" before the model generates). The model generates a single output $o^+$ from this modified prompt, which is then used for supervised fine-tuning of the base model.

**Where context distillation falls short.** The fundamental limitation is the **loss of the pairwise contrast signal**. RLHF-style approaches derive significant training signal from the *relative difference* between two outputs — the model learns not just "what good outputs look like" but also "what distinguishes good outputs from bad ones on the same input." Multiple works (Ouyang et al., 2022; Dubois et al., 2023) have found that RL training using preference models substantially outperforms supervised fine-tuning alone for LLM alignment. Context distillation, by generating only a single positive output per prompt, completely discards this comparative training signal. The paper's empirical results bear this out: Context-Dist7B underperforms RLCD7B substantially (e.g., GPT-4 prefers RLCD7B over Context-Dist7B at 69.7% on harmlessness and 89.5% on helpfulness; Table 3).

An additional, more nuanced weakness: context distillation only derives training signal from the *positive* prompt $p^+$ — it cannot leverage information from what the model produces when prompted to be *bad*. A negative prompt $p^-$ is never used, meaning the model never sees explicit examples of failure modes and therefore cannot learn to avoid them from a contrastive perspective. This is a missed opportunity: if you can construct $p^+$ to encourage helpfulness, you can trivially construct $p^-$ to encourage unhelpfulness, and the difference between $o^+$ and $o^-$ provides rich training signal about the attribute boundary.

#### The Missing Synthesis

The paper's core observation is that neither RLAIF nor context distillation captures the full set of available signals for automatic alignment:

- **RLAIF** uses pairwise preferences (good for RL) but generates i.i.d. outputs from the same prompt (bad for label quality, since outputs are hard to differentiate).
- **Context distillation** uses a directional prompt modification (good for producing clearly differentiated outputs) but generates only a single output for supervised fine-tuning (bad for leveraging comparative signal).

The natural synthesis — **use directional prompts to generate clearly differentiated outputs, then use the resulting pairs for RL training with automatically assigned preference labels** — is precisely what RLCD proposes. The paper frames its contribution not as inventing either prompting or RL, but as recognizing that the generation procedure in RLHF pipelines can itself be modified to produce higher-quality training pairs, rather than treating the initial response generation as a fixed i.i.d. sampling step.

### How RLCD Positions Itself

The paper's framing is notable for its modesty and precision about what RLCD changes versus what it inherits. RLCD **does not** modify the downstream RL pipeline: it still trains a preference model on the simulated pairs, then runs PPO with KL-regularization exactly as in standard RLHF. The *only* modification is in the preference data generation step — replacing i.i.d. sampling from $p$ with contrastive sampling from $p^+$ and $p^-$, and replacing post hoc AI scoring with labels-by-construction.

This narrow scope is methodologically significant. It means RLCD is **directly comparable to RLAIF** — both methods simulate preference pairs without human feedback, both train preference models and run PPO identically, and the only difference is how the initial training pairs are generated. Any performance difference can therefore be attributed specifically to the quality improvement from contrastive prompt construction versus i.i.d. sampling with AI scoring.

The paper positions RLCD as particularly valuable at **smaller model scales** — a deliberate counterpoint to RLAIF's requirement for large models (10B+ parameters) and few-shot prompting to work at all. The statement in the Discussion is explicit:

> "Especially at 7B model scale—where we find that RLAIF performs very poorly—RLCD already works quite decently, potentially enabling researchers and practitioners to experiment with RLAIF-style pipelines much faster and at lower cost."

This is a practical argument about accessibility: if alignment research requires 30B+ parameter models just to generate usable training data, the barrier to entry is enormously high. RLCD shows that even a 7B model — which is essentially useless as an RLAIF scorer — can generate effective preference data when the generation process is structured contrastively.

The paper also draws a conceptual connection to contrastive learning in reinforcement learning (Laskin et al., 2020; Eysenbach et al., 2022), where contrastive objectives that focus on sample pairs with similar appearances but different underlying semantics improve sample efficiency and model quality. RLCD applies this idea at the data generation level rather than the loss function level: $p^+$ and $p^-$ are designed to produce outputs that differ on the target attribute (harmfulness) while being similar on orthogonal axes (style, length, topic), creating the kind of informative contrastive pair that makes downstream learning efficient.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This is primarily a **method paper** that modifies the data generation step of the standard RLHF pipeline to produce better training signal without requiring human annotators or a stronger oracle model. RLCD is a system that takes a base unaligned language model and a set of prompts, automatically generates high-quality pairwise preference data using only the base model itself, and then uses that data to train a preference model and run PPO fine-tuning — the key insight being that generating the two responses in a preference pair from *different* prompts (one encouraging good behavior, one encouraging bad) produces cleaner, more reliably labeled training pairs than generating them i.i.d. from the same prompt and then asking the model to score which is better.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components arranged in a pipeline:

1. **Base unaligned LLM (LLaMA-7B or LLaMA-30B)** — serves both as the data generator for creating preference pairs and as the initial model to be aligned downstream. All preference data simulation uses this same model family; no external oracle is needed.

2. **Prompt Constructor** — takes an initial prompt `$p$` (a conversation prefix ending where the assistant should respond) and produces two variants: `$p^+$` (positive prompt, encouraging the desired attribute) and `$p^-$` (negative prompt, encouraging the opposite attribute). The construction is a simple string modification — typically adding a parenthetical description before the final "Assistant:" indicator.

3. **Preference Pair Generator** — feeds `$p^+$` and `$p^-$` into the base LLM to produce outputs `$o^+$` and `$o^-$`, then automatically labels `$o^+$` as preferred over `$o^-$` by construction. No post hoc scoring or human labeling is required.

4. **Preference Model Trainer** — fine-tunes the base LLM as a preference model on the simulated `$(o^+, o^-)$` pairs. The preference model learns to assign a scalar score to each response independently, trained to make the score difference between preferred and dispreferred outputs match the training labels.

5. **PPO Fine-Tuner** — converts the trained preference model into a reward model and uses Proximal Policy Optimization to fine-tune the base LLM, with KL-divergence regularization preventing the model from drifting too far from its original distribution.

Information flows sequentially: initial prompts → prompt constructor creates `$p^+, p^-$` → base LLM generates `$o^+, o^-$` → pairs are collected with `$o^+$` always labeled as preferred → preference model is trained on these pairs → reward scores from the preference model guide PPO updates to the base LLM → final aligned model is produced.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the preference data generation procedure** — the core innovation: how `$p^+$` and `$p^-$` are constructed, how `$o^+$` and `$o^-$` are generated and automatically labeled, and why this approach produces cleaner labels than RLAIF's i.i.d. sampling with post hoc scoring.
- **Second, prompt construction principles and examples** — the two criteria (attribute separation and surface form matching), how they are operationalized for each of the three tasks (harmlessness, helpfulness, outlining), and why surface form matching matters for avoiding unintended biases.
- **Third, the downstream RL pipeline** — how the simulated preference pairs feed into preference model training and PPO fine-tuning, including the conversion from preference model to reward model, KL-regularization, and hyperparameter selection.
- **Fourth, theoretical justification** — a simplified probabilistic model showing why RLCD's labels-by-construction are more accurate than RLAIF's post hoc scoring, especially on "hard" examples where the true attribute values are close.
- **Fifth, design choices and their justifications** — why specific decisions were made (e.g., zero-shot prompting, matching prompt surface forms, not rescoring outputs post hoc at small model scales).

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **method paper** whose core idea is that the response generation step in RLHF preference data creation can be made non-i.i.d. by using two contrastive prompts, producing output pairs that are more consistently and accurately labeled without requiring any post hoc scoring.

---

#### Preference Data Generation: The Core Innovation

The standard RLHF pipeline (and its RLAIF variant) generates preference data in three steps: (1) sample a prompt `$p$`, (2) generate two outputs `$o_1, o_2$` i.i.d. from the same model conditioned on `$p$`, and (3) obtain a preference label — either from a human or, in RLAIF, by asking the same model to score which output is better. RLCD modifies step (2) to be non-i.i.d. and replaces step (3) entirely with a label-by-construction.

**Step 1: Prompt construction.** Given an initial prompt `$p$` (a conversation prefix ending at a point where the assistant should respond), the system creates two modified versions:

- `$p^+$`: the positive prompt, designed to encourage the model to produce an output exemplifying the desired attribute (e.g., harmlessness, helpfulness).
- `$p^-$`: the negative prompt, designed to encourage the model to produce an output violating the desired attribute.

The construction is a lightweight string operation. For the harmlessness and helpfulness tasks, the modification is inserting a short parenthetical description immediately before the colon in the final "Assistant:" indicator. For example, as shown in Figure 1 (left):

> `$p^+$`: "... Assistant (harmless):"
> `$p^-$`: "... Assistant (harmful):"

The paper emphasizes that `$p^+$` and `$p^-$` should differ **only** in the words that target the attribute of interest, keeping all other surface features identical. This is the second criterion for prompt construction discussed below in the Prompt Design Principles subsection.

Additionally, for harmlessness and helpfulness, previous occurrences of "Assistant:" in the conversation history are also modified to include the *opposite* description. That is, if the final prompt `$p^+$` says "Assistant (harmless):", earlier assistant turns in the dialogue are rewritten as "Assistant (harmful):" — this increases the contrast between `$o^+$` and `$o^-$` by making the context leading up to the final generation consistently push in opposite directions.

For the outlining task, the modification replaces the assistant's response prefix. Instead of adding parenthetical descriptions, `$p^+$` uses prefixes like "Here is a possible outline with some interesting twists and turns:\n\n1." while `$p^-$` uses "Here is a very generic outline:\n\n1." — all ending with "1." to maintain surface form similarity (since outlines are numbered lists).

**Step 2: Output generation.** The base unaligned LLM (LLaMA-7B or LLaMA-30B, loaded in 8-bit precision, temperature 1) generates outputs `$o^+ \sim G(\cdot \mid p^+)$` and `$o^- \sim G(\cdot \mid p^-)$`, where `$G$` is the base model's generative distribution. Generation uses standard autoregressive sampling. For harmlessness and helpfulness, the system enforces that the response ends with a newline or end-of-sequence token and that the string "Human:" appears to delimit the end of the assistant's turn (preventing the model from continuing to generate the next conversational turn). Outputs that fail these formatting criteria are re-sampled up to 5 times; if all attempts fail, that conversation is skipped (the paper notes this is "very rare"). The outlining task uses more lenient truncation heuristics due to greater formatting difficulty: if "Human:" is absent, lines are removed from the end until the last line starting with a number is identified, with the assumption that numbered lines correspond to outline items.

**Step 3: Automatic labeling.** The pair `$(o^+, o^-)$` is automatically labeled with `$o^+$` preferred over `$o^-$`. There is no post hoc scoring step — no human annotation, no AI judgment, no comparison. The label is derived entirely from the construction: `$o^+$` was generated from the prompt designed to encourage the desired attribute, therefore it is assumed to be better on that attribute than `$o^-$`, which was generated from the prompt designed to discourage it.

**Why this works (intuition).** The key insight is a tradeoff between example difficulty and label accuracy. RLAIF generates `$o_1$` and `$o_2$` from the same distribution (both conditioned on `$p$`), which means their true attribute values `$A(o_1)$` and `$A(o_2)$` will frequently be very similar — these are the "hard" examples that would be most informative for training, but RLAIF's AI scoring step produces unreliable labels on exactly these examples (because distinguishing two nearly-identical-quality outputs is genuinely difficult). RLCD instead generates `$o^+$` and `$o^-$` from two different distributions with means `$\mu(p^+)$` and `$\mu(p^-)$` that are intentionally separated on the attribute axis. This makes the examples easier (the attribute difference is typically larger) but dramatically improves label accuracy (the probability that `$A(o^+) > A(o^-)$` is substantially higher than 0.5). The paper's theoretical analysis (Appendix N) shows that with appropriate separation between `$\mu(p^+)$` and `$\mu(p^-)$`, RLCD can even achieve **higher label accuracy on hard examples** — conditioned on `$A(o^+)$` and `$A(o^-)$` being close, the probability that `$A(o^+) > A(o^-)$` still exceeds 0.5 because of the shifted distributions.

**The rescoring variant (RLCD-Rescore).** The paper also investigates a hybrid approach: generate `$(o^+, o^-)$` using RLCD's contrastive prompts, but then **re-label** the pair using RLAIF's post hoc scoring procedure (asking the model to judge which output is better). This variant is designed to test whether RLCD's advantage comes from the contrastive generation alone, or whether the labels-by-construction approach is also critical. Results (Table 6) show that at 7B scale, RLCD dramatically outperforms RLCD-Rescore (GPT-4 prefers RLCD at 86.0% on harmlessness, 86.3% on helpfulness, 88.8% on outlining), indicating that the LLaMA-7B scoring model is too unreliable to usefully relabel even contrastively-generated pairs. At 30B scale, the gap narrows substantially (GPT-4 preferences of 54.6%, 47.3%, 36.4% respectively), suggesting that with a sufficiently capable scoring model, rescoring becomes viable — but still does not clearly outperform the simpler labels-by-construction approach.

#### Prompt Construction Principles and Task-Specific Designs

The paper identifies two criteria for constructing `$p^+$` and `$p^-$`, stated explicitly in Section 3.2:

**Criterion 1: Attribute separation.** `$p^+$` should be more likely than `$p^-$` to produce outputs exemplifying the desired attribute. Equivalently, `$p^-$` may explicitly encourage directional change toward the opposite attribute. Formally, if `$A(o)$` is the true attribute value of output `$o$`, then we want `$\mathbb{E}[A(o) \mid p^+] > \mathbb{E}[A(o) \mid p^-]$`. The larger this separation, the more likely that `$A(o^+) > A(o^-)$` for any given pair, and thus the higher the expected label accuracy.

**Criterion 2: Surface form matching.** The surface forms of `$p^+$` and `$p^-$` should be as similar as possible, ideally differing only in the words that directly target the desired attribute. This criterion is subtler. The intuition is that `$p^+$` and `$p^-$` induce two different output distributions; we want these distributions to differ *maximally* on the target attribute axis and *minimally* on all orthogonal axes. If `$p^+$` and `$p^-$` differ in surface features unrelated to the attribute (e.g., one uses formal language and the other uses casual language), the resulting `$o^+$` and `$o^-$` may differ along those orthogonal dimensions as well, introducing spurious correlations that the preference model might latch onto. The goal is to create a contrastive pair where the *only systematic difference* is the target attribute — analogously to how contrastive learning objectives in representation learning benefit from pairs that differ only along the dimension of interest.

The paper notes an empirical observation that influences how to balance these criteria:

> "Empirically, we find that RLCD is highly capable at amplifying the contrast in prompts `$p^+$` and `$p^-$` when compared to baselines using similar prompts... Thus when designing `$p^+$` and `$p^-$` in practice, we find it may often be more worthwhile to focus on the second criterion compared to the first, for instance by just writing a short description in parentheses to create `$p^+$` and `$p^-$`."

In other words, RLCD's generation process is sensitive enough that even very small prompt differences produce clearly differentiated outputs, so the practical priority shifts to ensuring those differences *only* affect the target attribute.

**Harmlessness task prompt construction.** The paper writes 16 pairs of context phrases for constructing `$p^+$` and `$p^-$` (Table 9), sampling a random pair for each preference data example. These pairs are deliberately written to correspond to the 16 scoring instructions used in Bai et al. (2022b)'s RLAIF implementation (Table 7). For example, one RLAIF scoring instruction is:

> "Please choose the response that is the most helpful, honest, and harmless."

The corresponding RLCD prompt affix pair is:

> `$p^+$`: "(helpful, honest, inoffensive response)"  
> `$p^-$`: "(unhelpful, dishonest, toxic response)"

Another example — RLAIF instruction:

> "Choose the assistant response that is as harmless, helpful, polite, respectful, and thoughtful as possible without sounding overly-reactive of accusatory."

Corresponding RLCD pair:

> `$p^+$`: "(harmless, helpful response)"  
> `$p^-$`: "(harmful, unhelpful response)"

A notable design choice: the authors removed mentions of politeness ("polite," "thoughtful") from their RLCD prompts compared to the RLAIF scoring instructions because they observed that RLCD was overly sensitive to these cues — the model quickly discovered that prefacing every response with "Thank you" or "Sorry" was an easy way to improve reward, and while this trivially improves harmlessness, it degrades response quality and creates an unfair comparison to RLAIF (which doesn't discover this shortcut as readily). This is a practical illustration of how RLCD's stronger amplification of prompt signals can be a double-edged sword, requiring careful prompt design to avoid unintended optimization.

**Helpfulness task prompt construction.** Only a single affix pair is used (Table 11):

> `$p^+$`: "(giving a helpful response)"  
> `$p^-$`: "(giving an unhelpful response)"

The corresponding RLAIF scoring prompt (Table 10) is simply "Please choose the response that is more helpful."

**Outlining task prompt construction.** Three affix pairs are used (Table 13), corresponding to three scoring prompts for RLAIF (Table 12) targeting interestingness, well-formedness, and premise relevance:

- **Interestingness**: `$p^+$` uses "Here is a possible outline with some interesting twists and turns:\n\n1." and `$p^-$` uses "Here is a very generic outline:\n\n1."
- **Well-formedness**: `$p^+$` uses "Here is a possible outline:\n\n1." and `$p^-$` uses "Sure. The story starts with 1." (which encourages less structured output).
- **Premise relevance**: `$p^+$` uses "Here is a possible outline based on the premise:\n\n1." and `$p^-$` uses "That premise is a bit difficult, but here's an outline on a slightly different topic:\n\n1."

All pairs end with "1." to match surface forms (since outlines are numbered lists), and the RLAIF scoring prompts correspondingly ask to choose the response that is more interesting, better-formatted, or more relevant to the premise.

#### The Downstream RL Pipeline

Once preference pairs `$(o^+, o^-)$` are generated with `$o^+$` labeled as preferred, RLCD follows the standard RLHF pipeline exactly. This section describes the components that RLCD inherits; the paper's contribution is the data generation step described above, not any modification to these downstream stages.

**Preference model training.** The base unaligned LLM (LLaMA-7B in all experiments, regardless of whether LLaMA-7B or LLaMA-30B was used for preference data simulation) is fine-tuned as a preference model. The preference model operates by assigning a scalar score `$s(o)$` to each response independently, and is trained so that the score difference between preferred and dispreferred outputs aligns with the training labels. Specifically, for a training pair `$(o^+, o^-)$` where `$o^+$` is preferred, the model is trained to maximize `$s(o^+) - s(o^-)$` — or equivalently, to minimize a loss that encourages the preferred output to receive a higher score. The training uses AlpacaFarm's default hyperparameters for preference model training (Appendix E).

A crucial practical consequence of RLCD's label-by-construction approach: the preference labels are **binary and discrete** (1 for `$o^+$`, 0 for `$o^-$`), whereas RLAIF produces **continuous probability labels** from the scoring model's output distribution. The paper observes (Section 5.1) that RLCD's preference models therefore make judgments with "higher polarity" — the model learns stronger, more confident preferences — because the training targets are consistently 0 or 1 rather than weak probabilities near 0.5. This is visible in Table 5: RLCD7B's preference model assigns average probability 0.516 to the gold human-preferred output, while RLAIF7B's assigns only 0.492 (essentially chance-level).

**Reward model conversion.** The trained preference model is converted to a reward model in the standard way: the scalar score `$s(o)$` assigned by the preference model to a response `$o$` is used directly as the reward signal for PPO training. This means that during PPO, when the language model generates a response to a training prompt, the preference model scores that response, and the resulting score becomes the reward that the policy is optimized to maximize. No architectural changes are needed — the preference model already outputs a scalar per response, and this scalar serves as the reward.

**PPO fine-tuning.** The base LLM (again LLaMA-7B) is fine-tuned using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO; Schulman et al., 2017). At each PPO step, the training process samples a batch of prompts (similar to those used for preference data generation), the current language model generates responses to those prompts, each response receives a reward from the reward model, and the PPO algorithm updates the language model's parameters to increase the expected reward.

Following common practice (Bai et al., 2022a), the PPO objective includes **KL-divergence regularization** — a penalty term that discourages the fine-tuned model from deviating too far from the original base model's output distribution. This regularization serves two purposes: it prevents overfitting to the reward model (which is an imperfect proxy for true human preferences), and it preserves the base model's general capabilities (fluency, knowledge, reasoning) while only modifying behavior along the alignment-relevant dimensions. The KL penalty is controlled by a coefficient `$\beta$` that trades off reward maximization against distributional distance.

The paper optimizes two PPO hyperparameters via grid search (Appendix E):

- **KL coefficient `$\beta$`**: selected from among `$\{0.001, 0.002, 0.004, 0.008, 0.016, 0.032\}$`. Higher values impose stronger regularization and prevent the model from drifting too far from its base behavior; lower values allow more aggressive optimization toward the reward signal.
- **Number of PPO steps**: selected from among `$\{20, 40, 60, 80\}$`, where each "step" corresponds to 512 rollouts. The paper observes that using too many PPO steps degrades performance, likely due to overfitting to the reward model. For the outlining task, the number of steps is fixed at 20 because the authors observed even earlier performance degradation (including mode collapse).

An important methodological detail: **hyperparameter selection does not assume access to a stronger evaluation model or human labels**. For both RLCD and RLAIF, hyperparameters are chosen by generating 1000 model outputs on a validation set for each hyperparameter configuration, then evaluating the average reward according to that method's *own* learned reward model. While this is potentially circular (the reward model may reward behaviors that don't correspond to true improvements), it maintains the zero-human-feedback constraint and is applied identically to both RLCD and baselines, making the comparison fair. The paper notes that similar self-evaluation for hyperparameter tuning was used in Dubois et al. (2023).

**Validation and test sets.** For harmlessness and helpfulness, the paper uses Anthropic's existing data splits: the first 1000 examples from the test data serve as the validation set (used for hyperparameter tuning and intermediate evaluations), and the second 1000 examples serve as the test set (used for final evaluation). For outlining, 40,000 premises are used for training data generation, with separate held-out prompts used for evaluation.

#### Theoretical Justification: Why Labels-by-Construction Beat Post Hoc Scoring

Appendix N provides a simplified probabilistic model that formalizes why RLCD's labeling approach is more reliable than RLAIF's. The analysis makes several simplifying assumptions (normality, equal variance) but captures the essential structure of the problem.

**Setup.** The true attribute of interest (e.g., harmlessness) is quantified on a real-valued axis by an unknown function `$A(o)$`. The ground-truth preference label for a pair `$(o_1, o_2)$` is: prefer `$o_1$` if `$A(o_1) > A(o_2)$`. The base generative model `$G(o \mid p)$` produces outputs `$o$` such that `$A(o) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu(p), \sigma_G)$`, where `$\mu(p)$` is the attribute value associated with prompt `$p$` (e.g., a "harmless" prompt has high `$\mu$`, a "harmful" prompt has low `$\mu$`). The RLAIF discriminator model `$D(o_1, o_2)$` predicts preference labels according to the sign of `$[A(o_1) + e_1] - [A(o_2) + e_2]$`, where `$e_1, e_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_D)$` are i.i.d. error terms representing the scoring model's noise. For simplicity, the analysis sets `$\sigma_G = \sigma_D = 1$`.

**RLAIF analysis.** Under these assumptions, with RLAIF (both `$o_1, o_2$` generated from the same prompt `$p$`, so both have the same `$\mu$`), the probability of a correctly labeled pair is 0.75. However, for "hard" examples where the true attribute values `$A(o_1)$` and `$A(o_2)$` are very close (differing by at most 0.2), the probability of correct labeling drops to approximately **0.528** — barely above chance. This is because when two outputs have nearly identical true quality, the discriminator's noise `$e_1, e_2$` dominates the decision, making the label essentially random.

**RLCD analysis.** In RLCD, the positive and negative prompts have different attribute means: `$\mu(p^+)$` and `$\mu(p^-)$`, with `$\Delta = \mu(p^+) - \mu(p^-) > 0$`. The label is assigned based on which prompt generated which output, not based on any scoring. For large `$\Delta$`, the label accuracy approaches 1 (because `$o^+$` is almost certainly better than `$o^-$` if the prompts are sufficiently separated). But the more interesting case is **label accuracy on hard examples**. Even when conditioning on `$A(o^+)$` and `$A(o^-)$` being close, the probability that `$A(o^+) > A(o^-)$` exceeds 0.5 — because the shifted distributions mean that even when two draws happen to land near the same value, the one from the higher-mean distribution is still more likely to be the larger one. For `$\Delta = 3$`, the simulation yields 0.574 accuracy on examples where `$|A(o^+) - A(o^-)| \leq 0.2$`, compared to RLAIF's 0.528.

**Key takeaway.** RLCD sacrifices example difficulty (most training pairs have large attribute gaps) in exchange for dramatically higher label reliability — not just on average, but even on the subset of examples that happen to be difficult. This is a favorable tradeoff in the automatic labeling regime because unreliable labels on hard examples provide no useful training signal (they are essentially noise), while reliable labels on easier examples provide clean gradient information that allows the preference model to learn the general direction of improvement. The analysis also suggests a scaling strategy: as model capability increases and `$\sigma_G$` and `$\sigma_D$` decrease (the base model produces more consistent outputs, the scoring model makes fewer errors), the separation `$\Delta$` should be reduced to maintain a similar distribution of example difficulties, avoiding the regime where all examples become trivially easy.

**Why RLCD-Rescore performs poorly at 7B scale.** This analysis also explains the RLCD-Rescore results (Table 6). RLCD-Rescore generates `$o^+, o^-$` from contrastive prompts but then relabels them using the scoring model `$D$`. At 7B scale, where `$\sigma_D$` is large (the scoring model is noisy), relabeling introduces substantial error — the scoring model may incorrectly flip labels on examples where `$o^+$` is genuinely better than `$o^-$` (due to noise in the scoring) while providing no benefit on examples where the label-by-construction is already correct. At 30B scale, where `$\sigma_D$` is smaller, rescoring becomes more competitive.

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Design choice: Single base model for both generation and downstream alignment.** All experiments use LLaMA-7B as the model to be aligned, regardless of whether LLaMA-7B or LLaMA-30B generated the preference data. Using LLaMA-30B for data generation while aligning LLaMA-7B can be viewed as a form of model distillation (the smaller model learns from the larger model's output distribution), but the paper's primary framing is that RLCD works with *only the model being aligned* — no external oracle or stronger teacher is required. The 30B experiments test whether scaling the data generation model improves results when one happens to be available, but the method is designed to function with a single model.

**Design choice: Zero-shot prompting for both generation and scoring.** All prompt constructions for RLCD are zero-shot — no few-shot examples are provided to demonstrate the desired behavior. This is a deliberate contrast to Bai et al. (2022b), who found that few-shot prompting was necessary for RLAIF to exceed chance-level agreement with humans on harmlessness. RLCD avoids few-shot examples because they would introduce additional design complexity (what examples to choose? how many?) and because the paper's goal is to demonstrate effectiveness with minimal prompt engineering. The fact that RLCD substantially outperforms even few-shot RLAIF (RLAIF-Few30B) on helpfulness while being competitive on harmlessness (Table 16) validates this zero-shot approach.

**Design choice: 16 harmlessness prompt pairs matching RLAIF scoring instructions.** Using 16 prompt pairs rather than a single pair serves two purposes. First, it provides diversity in how the attribute is conceptualized (e.g., "ethical response" vs. "harmless response" vs. "socially acceptable response"), which may help the preference model learn a more robust representation of harmlessness rather than overfitting to a single narrow formulation. Second, matching the number and content of RLCD's prompt pairs to RLAIF's scoring instructions enables a fair comparison — both methods receive the same semantic guidance about what "harmlessness" means, just applied differently (RLCD uses it during generation, RLAIF during post hoc scoring).

**Design choice: Removing politeness cues from harmlessness prompts.** As noted earlier, the authors observed that RLCD's strong amplification of prompt signals caused the model to exploit politeness as a shortcut (prefacing everything with "Thank you" or "Sorry"), which trivially improves harmlessness at the cost of response quality. Removing these cues from the prompts makes the comparison to RLAIF fairer — RLAIF's scoring model doesn't discover this shortcut as readily because its i.i.d. generation doesn't produce the necessary contrast. This design choice illustrates a general principle: when using contrastive prompts, ensure that the attribute encouraged by `$p^+$` corresponds to the *intended* behavior, not a proxy that can be satisfied without genuine improvement.

**Design choice: Binary labels rather than continuous probabilities.** RLCD assigns discrete 0/1 labels (prefer `$o^+$`, prefer `$o^-$`) rather than continuous confidence scores as in RLAIF. The justification is implicit in the method design: since the label is derived entirely from which prompt was used, there is no natural continuous confidence score — the construction simply asserts that `$o^+$` is preferred. This has the effect of producing preference models with "higher polarity" (stronger, more confident preferences), as the training targets are always at the extremes. The binarized variant of RLAIF (RLAIF-Binary7B, Appendix B) tests whether this polarity difference alone explains RLCD's advantage; RLCD7B still outperforms RLAIF-Binary7B (Table 14), indicating that the quality of the generated pairs — not just the label format — is the primary driver of improvement.

**Design choice: Mixing in historical prompt modifications for harmlessness and helpfulness.** For harmlessness and helpfulness, previous "Assistant:" occurrences in the conversation prefix are modified to use the opposite description from the final prompt. That is, if `$p^+$` ends with "Assistant (harmless):", earlier assistant turns become "Assistant (harmful):". This increases the contrast between `$o^+$` and `$o^-$` by making the entire dialogue context consistently push in opposite directions, rather than having the contrast be confined to the final single-word modification. The paper does not ablate this design choice, but it follows naturally from the goal of maximizing attribute separation between the two output distributions.

**Design choice: Default AlpacaFarm hyperparameters with PPO-specific tuning.** The paper uses AlpacaFarm's default hyperparameters for all stages except PPO, where KL coefficient and number of steps are grid-searched. This constrains the search space and makes the experiments reproducible, but it also means that the supervised fine-tuning and preference model training stages are not optimized — they use whatever defaults AlpacaFarm provides. Since these defaults are applied identically to RLCD and all baselines, this doesn't bias the comparison, but it does mean that absolute performance could potentially be improved with further hyperparameter tuning.

**Design choice: Self-evaluation for hyperparameter selection.** Both RLCD and RLAIF select PPO hyperparameters by evaluating reward on the validation set using their own learned reward models. While this is potentially circular (a reward model may assign high scores to outputs that are not genuinely better), it maintains the zero-human-feedback constraint and is applied symmetrically to both methods. Any bias from this procedure would affect both RLCD and RLAIF equally, making the relative comparison valid even if absolute performance estimates are optimistic. The paper acknowledges this limitation implicitly by noting the parallel to Dubois et al. (2023).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Preference Data Generation as a Controlled-Condition Contrast Rather Than Independent Sampling

The most fundamental intellectual move in this paper is the **reconceptualization of where signal quality comes from in automatic preference data generation**. The field's default assumption, inherited from RLHF and carried forward into RLAIF, was that preference pairs should be generated i.i.d. from the same prompt distribution, with label quality determined by the *post hoc scoring mechanism* — whether a human, an AI, or some heuristic. The research question was implicitly framed as: "given two outputs from the same prompt, how accurately can we determine which is better?"

RLCD rejects this framing entirely. Instead, the paper proposes that **the generation procedure itself is a designable source of supervision**, and that by generating outputs from two *different* prompt conditions — one encouraging the desired attribute, one encouraging its opposite — one can construct pairs where the preference label is known by construction and more accurate than what any post hoc scoring could achieve, at least at smaller model scales. This is not a small tweak to RLAIF's scoring prompts; it is a **fundamental shift in where the system obtains its supervision signal**, moving it from the scoring stage to the generation stage.

To appreciate why this is a genuine insight rather than an obvious engineering trick, consider the counterargument: if one prompt encourages helpfulness and another encourages unhelpfulness, isn't the preference label trivially obvious by construction? The answer is *not necessarily* — because language models are stochastic, and prompt modifications produce distributional shifts, not deterministic output changes. A model prompted to be harmful might still (with some probability) produce a harmless response; a model prompted to be harmless might produce a bland non-response that is less helpful than the "harmful" prompt's output. The key empirical claim, verified in the paper, is that the *probability* that the positive-prompt output is genuinely better is high enough — and specifically, high enough *relative to the alternative of post hoc scoring* — to produce useful training signal. The theoretical analysis in Appendix N formalizes this: label accuracy depends on the separation `Δ = μ(p⁺) − μ(p⁻)` of the prompt-conditioned attribute distributions, and for sufficiently large `Δ`, accuracy can be made arbitrarily high, even conditioned on examples where the outputs happen to have similar quality.

This reframing connects RLCD to a broader class of methods in self-supervised learning where **data generation procedures are designed to produce training signal without external labels** — contrastive learning in vision (where augmentations define positive pairs), masked language modeling (where the mask defines the prediction task), and goal-conditioned RL (where the goal defines which trajectory is "good"). In all these cases, the supervision comes not from an external judge but from the *structure of the data generation process itself*. RLCD applies this principle to LLM alignment: the contrastive prompt pair `(p⁺, p⁻)` defines a self-supervised task where the label is which prompt generated which output.

The evidence that this reframing is meaningful — not just a different way of doing the same thing — comes from two empirical results. First, **RLCD dramatically outperforms RLAIF at 7B scale** (GPT-4 prefers RLCD7B over RLAIF7B at 84.8% on harmlessness, 85.4% on helpfulness, 78.5% on outlining; Table 3), where RLAIF's scoring model is so unreliable that its preference model achieves *below-chance* agreement with humans (35.6% binary accuracy; Table 5). This gap is not just a metric improvement — it represents the difference between a method that *works* (produces aligned outputs) and one that *essentially fails* (produces outputs comparable to the base unaligned model). Second, **RLCD-Rescore — which generates pairs contrastively but then applies post hoc AI relabeling — underperforms RLCD substantially at 7B** (GPT-4 prefers RLCD at 86.0% on harmlessness; Table 6), confirming that the *label-by-construction* approach is specifically what matters, not just the contrastive generation.

This insight is **fundamental rather than incremental**: it changes the question from "how can we improve the scorer?" to "how can we design the generation procedure so that labels are implicit in the construction?" and opens a design space (prompt pairs, control strengths, attribute axes) that is entirely separate from the scoring model design space explored by prior work.

---

### Innovation 2: Articulating and Exploiting the Tradeoff Between Example Hardness and Label Accuracy in Automatic Preference Data

The paper provides — through both empirical evidence and a formal model — a **diagnostic framework for understanding why RLAIF fails at small model scales and why RLCD succeeds**. This framework centers on a tradeoff that is well-known in active learning and curriculum learning but had not been explicitly applied to automatic preference data generation for LLM alignment: **training examples close to the decision boundary (hard examples) are the most informative for learning, but also the hardest to label accurately without a reliable annotator**. When the annotator is the same model being trained (as in RLAIF), the labeling noise on hard examples can be so severe that it overwhelms any training signal, making those examples harmful rather than helpful.

Prior work (Bai et al., 2022b) had observed that RLAIF's preference models sometimes achieve below-chance human agreement at small scales, but treated this as a threshold phenomenon — "the model must be above 10B parameters for RLAIF to work" — without articulating *why* this threshold exists or what structural property of the data generation causes it. RLCD's contribution is to **identify the specific mechanism**: i.i.d. generation from the same prompt produces output pairs with very similar attribute values, creating hard-to-discriminate examples that the (weak) scoring model cannot reliably label. The fix is not to improve the scorer, but to **modify the generation to produce easier examples with cleaner labels**, accepting the tradeoff that these examples may be less informative per instance but provide more total signal because their labels are reliable.

This is a **conceptual advance rather than an architectural one**: it provides a principled language for reasoning about *when* different automatic alignment methods will work, based on the relationship between example difficulty (determined by the variance of the output distribution relative to the prompt-conditioned mean separation) and scorer reliability (determined by the scoring model's noise variance). Appendix N formalizes this relationship:

- In RLAIF, `μ(p)` is the same for both outputs, so example difficulty is determined by the base model's output variance `σ_G`, and label accuracy on hard examples approaches chance.
- In RLCD, `Δ = μ(p⁺) − μ(p⁻)` is a controllable parameter: increase `Δ` to get higher label accuracy at the cost of easier (less informative) examples; decrease `Δ` to get harder examples but risk label noise.

The practical power of this framework is that it **predicts how to scale RLCD to larger models**. As model capability increases, both `σ_G` (output variance) and `σ_D` (scoring noise) likely decrease. If `σ_G` decreases, then RLAIF's i.i.d. pairs become even harder (outputs cluster more tightly around the same mean), making scoring harder rather than easier — this contradicts the intuition that larger models would make RLAIF more reliable. If `σ_D` decreases, post hoc scoring becomes more accurate, making RLCD-Rescore and RLAIF more competitive. The paper's analysis suggests that to maintain optimal example difficulty as models scale, `Δ` should be decreased — using "weaker" positive/negative prompts that produce more subtle contrasts. This is a **testable prediction** that goes beyond the paper's empirical scope and provides a roadmap for future work.

The evidence for this framework comes from multiple angles: the preference model evaluation showing RLAIF's below-chance accuracy at 7B (Table 5), the difficulty-conditioned label accuracy simulations in Appendix N (0.528 for RLAIF vs. 0.574 for RLCD on examples with `|A(o⁺) − A(o⁻)| ≤ 0.2`), and the rescoring experiments showing that even when pairs are contrastively generated, the 7B scoring model still degrades label quality (Table 6). This convergence of evidence — empirical failure of RLAIF at 7B, formal explanation for why it fails, and validation that the proposed fix addresses the identified mechanism — constitutes one of the paper's strongest contributions.

---

### Innovation 3: Demonstrating That Contrastive Prompt Construction Enables Alignment at Model Scales Where RLAIF Fundamentally Cannot Work

While Innovations 1 and 2 are conceptual, this innovation is **empirically significant in a way that changes the practical landscape for alignment research**. The paper shows that RLCD with LLaMA-7B — a model scale at which RLAIF produces preference models with *below-chance* human agreement (35.6% accuracy, Table 5) and downstream outputs that are qualitatively poor (examples in Appendix I) — achieves strong alignment performance: GPT-4 prefers RLCD7B over the base LLaMA at rates of 82.8% on harmlessness and 90.7% on helpfulness (Table 3). This is not a marginal improvement; it is the difference between a method that produces usable alignment and one that essentially fails.

The significance of this goes beyond the specific numbers. **RLAIF at 7B scale doesn't just perform worse — it breaks in a specific and informative way.** Its preference model achieves 35.6% accuracy on harmlessness (Table 5), which is not just low but *systematically worse than random guessing*. This means the RLAIF7B preference model has learned an *inverted* preference function — it assigns higher scores to outputs that humans consider worse. Appendix C discusses one possible cause: asking a small model "which output is less harmful?" may sometimes result in the model attending to the word "harmful" and preferring the more harmful output, as if modeling a bag of words — a known failure mode in smaller LMs (Welbl et al., 2021; Pei et al., 2023).

RLCD circumvents this failure mode entirely by **removing the need for the model to perform comparative reasoning at labeling time**. The model never has to judge which of two outputs is better; it only has to *generate* outputs under different conditions, a capability that even small LMs possess. This is a **structural insight**: generation and comparison are different capabilities, and automatic alignment methods that rely on comparison (like RLAIF) impose a capability threshold that generation-based methods (like RLCD) do not.

The paper explicitly frames this as a **democratization argument** in the Discussion:

> "Especially at 7B model scale—where we find that RLAIF performs very poorly—RLCD already works quite decently, potentially enabling researchers and practitioners to experiment with RLAIF-style pipelines much faster and at lower cost."

This is not merely a statement about RLCD's performance; it addresses a **structural barrier in alignment research**. If producing usable alignment training data requires models above 10B parameters (as RLAIF does), then the vast majority of academic researchers and smaller organizations — who cannot train or even run inference with 30B+ models at scale — are locked out of researching and improving automatic alignment pipelines. RLCD shows that the same model being aligned (7B parameters) can generate its own training data, collapsing the resource requirements for experimentation.

The caveat — and it is an important one — is that this result is demonstrated on three specific alignment tasks (harmlessness, helpfulness, story outlining) with a specific model family (LLaMA). Whether the 7B-viability generalizes to other models, other tasks, or other alignment dimensions is untested. But within the paper's empirical scope, this finding is robust and practically meaningful.

---

### Innovation 4: Identifying and Mitigating Prompt-Induced Optimization Shortcuts Through Prompt Design

The paper surfaces a **subtle failure mode in contrastive prompt-based alignment**: when the model is strongly sensitive to prompt cues, it can discover optimization shortcuts that satisfy the *prompt's literal specification* without achieving the *intended behavioral change*. The specific example: including politeness-related language in the harmlessness prompts ("polite," "respectful," "thoughtful") caused the model to discover that prefacing every response with "Thank you" or "Sorry" was an easy way to improve the reward signal, resulting in outputs that were technically harmless but substantively useless.

The authors' response — removing politeness language from the prompts — is not just a hyperparameter tweak; it reflects a **diagnostic awareness that contrastive prompt construction amplifies prompt signals, making the alignment process more vulnerable to specification gaming** (where the model optimizes for what the prompt literally asks rather than what the designer intends). This connects RLCD to the broader alignment literature on reward hacking and specification problems, but at the prompt-design level rather than the reward-model level.

The significance of this observation is that **RLCD's strength (amplifying prompt signals to produce clearly differentiated outputs) is also its weakness (amplifying unintended prompt signals that enable shortcuts)**. The paper's design principle — focus on the second criterion (surface form matching) because the first criterion (attribute separation) is already easily satisfied — partially addresses this, but the politeness example shows that even with careful design, unintended cues can creep in. This is a **foundational challenge for contrastive alignment methods**: how to specify the desired attribute in a prompt pair without introducing extraneous cues that the model can exploit.

The paper does not solve this challenge — it works around it by careful prompt engineering — but **identifying it explicitly and providing a concrete example** is a contribution that warns future practitioners and motivates research into more robust prompt design or automatic prompt optimization methods for contrastive alignment. It also suggests that RLCD's prompt pairs should be evaluated not just by how well they separate output distributions on the target attribute, but also by whether they introduce correlated shifts on orthogonal attributes — a diagnostic criterion that the paper implicitly applies but does not formalize.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses three prompt sets: (1) harmlessness prompts derived from the training set of Bai et al. (2022a), containing slightly over 40,000 conversations frequently featuring offensive or socially unacceptable text; (2) helpfulness prompts from the same source, also slightly over 40,000 conversations where the human asks for information or advice; (3) outlining prompts constructed from 40,000 existing premises from the internet (10–40 tokens each), where the assistant is asked to generate a story outline. For harmlessness and helpfulness, the validation set consists of the first 1,000 examples from Anthropic's test data, and the test set consists of the second 1,000 examples (Appendix F). The outlining task uses separate held-out prompts for evaluation, though the paper does not specify the test set size for this task.

- **Base model(s).** All alignment experiments target LLaMA-7B (Touvron et al., 2023a) as the model to be aligned. Preference data simulation uses either LLaMA-7B or LLaMA-30B, loaded in 8-bit precision. The choice of LLaMA-7B as the alignment target is deliberate: it represents a model scale where RLAIF performs poorly (providing a strong test of whether RLCD can succeed where existing methods fail), and it is small enough to enable rapid experimentation — the paper explicitly frames this as lowering the barrier for alignment research. When LLaMA-30B is used for preference data simulation, the downstream alignment still targets LLaMA-7B; the authors note this can be viewed as model distillation, where a smaller model learns from a larger model's preference data.

- **Metrics.** The primary evaluation is **pairwise comparison**: for each task, the paper runs head-to-head evaluations of RLCD against each baseline, asking either human annotators (200 examples per comparison) or GPT-4 (1,000 examples per comparison) to judge which output is better on the relevant attribute(s). Human evaluations use an 8-point Likert scale from "output A is much better" (1) to "output B is much better" (8), with scores normalized afterward so that higher is better and scores for the two outputs sum to 9. GPT-4 evaluations use binary forced choice with the prompt template shown in Table 21, parsed using heuristic string matching (Appendix F.2). For the harmlessness task, both harmlessness (Harm) and helpfulness (Help) are measured separately, since the prompts from Bai et al. (2022b) encourage helpfulness to some degree as well (Appendix A.1). For helpfulness prompts, only helpfulness is measured; for outlining, only outline quality (Qual) is measured. Additional automatic metrics include: held-out reward model scores (trained on human preference data; Appendix J.1), GPT-3 conditional perplexity (Appendix J.2), output diversity via distinct n-gram fractions (Appendix J.3), and output token length (Appendix J.4).

- **Baselines.** Three baselines are compared:
  1. **LLaMA**: The base unaligned LLaMA-7B with no fine-tuning, included as a sanity check to verify that alignment methods actually improve over the pretrained model.
  2. **RLAIF**: Following Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022b), re-implemented using the AlpacaFarm codebase (Dubois et al., 2023). For harmlessness scoring, the paper uses the exact same 16 prompt templates as Bai et al. (2022b) (Table 7), although in a zero-shot setting to match RLCD's zero-shot prompts (few-shot variants are explored in Appendix C). For helpfulness, a single scoring prompt is used ("Please choose the response that is more helpful"; Table 10). For outlining, three scoring prompts target interestingness, well-formedness, and premise relevance (Table 12). Preference labels are continuous probabilities from the scoring model's output distribution. When simulating with LLaMA-7B, this baseline is denoted RLAIF7B; with LLaMA-30B, RLAIF30B.
  3. **Context-Dist**: A context distillation baseline that conducts supervised fine-tuning on only the outputs `$o^+$` generated from the same positive prompts `$p^+$` used in RLCD. This baseline captures what can be achieved with directional prompting alone, without pairwise preferences or RL training. The same base model is used for data generation (LLaMA-7B or LLaMA-30B, denoted Context-Dist7B and Context-Dist30B respectively), and downstream fine-tuning always targets LLaMA-7B.

  Additional variants explored in appendices: RLAIF-Binary7B (Appendix B), which binarizes RLAIF's continuous labels; RLAIF-Few7B and RLAIF-Few30B (Appendix C), which use few-shot prompting for scoring; and RLAIFp+7B (Appendix L), which uses RLCD's `$p^+$` prompt during generation but still labels via post hoc AI scoring.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not measure compute in FLOPs or wall-clock time, but rather in terms of **the scale of preference data simulation**. The key variable is which model (7B or 30B) generates the preference pairs — the 30B setting is 4× larger and roughly 4× more expensive per token — and all methods generate the same number of training pairs per task. This means comparisons at the same simulation model scale (e.g., RLCD7B vs. RLAIF7B) are compute-matched by construction. For the downstream alignment stage, all methods use the same PPO hyperparameter search space and the same number of PPO steps, making the comparison fair. The paper does note that RLAIF's scoring step requires placing both outputs in the context window simultaneously, which doubles the effective context length and adds some additional compute cost compared to RLCD (which needs no scoring step), but this cost is not quantified or factored into comparisons.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not use cross-validation in the traditional sense for strategy selection (unlike the example paper which selects compute-optimal strategies per difficulty bin). Instead, it relies on a fixed train-validation-test split: the first 1,000 test examples serve as validation (used for hyperparameter tuning), and the second 1,000 serve as test (used for final evaluation). Hyperparameters (KL coefficient and number of PPO steps) are selected via grid search, evaluating each configuration by generating 1,000 model outputs on the validation set and computing average reward using each method's own learned reward model. This self-evaluation approach is applied identically to RLCD and all baselines, making the relative comparison fair even if absolute reward estimates are potentially biased. For human evaluation, 200 examples are sampled per comparison; the paper does not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests on these human judgments. For GPT-4 evaluation, 1,000 examples are used per comparison; percentages are reported as point estimates without confidence intervals. Human-GPT-4 agreement is reported in Table 23, ranging from 59% to 86.5% across comparisons, with lower agreement on the helpfulness metric within the harmlessness task (likely due to GPT-4's own harmlessness alignment causing it to abstain or give unreliable judgments on harmful content).

### Main Quantitative Results

The paper's core empirical claim is that RLCD outperforms both RLAIF and context distillation baselines across three alignment tasks, with the performance gap being largest at 7B scale for preference data simulation and narrowing (but not disappearing) at 30B scale. The evidence comes from two complementary evaluation modalities — human judgments and GPT-4 automated judgments — which generally agree on the overall ranking but show some divergence in specific comparisons.

#### Overall Comparison Against Baselines

**Human evaluation results (Table 2).** Across all three tasks and both simulation model scales, RLCD's normalized scores are equal to or higher than every baseline. The human evaluation uses 200 examples per pairwise comparison, scored on a 1–8 Likert scale and normalized so that higher is better and scores sum to 9.

For **harmlessness prompts**, measured on harmlessness (Harm) and helpfulness (Help):
- RLCD7B vs. RLAIF7B: RLCD achieves 5.62 / 3.38 on Harm, 4.64 / 4.36 on Help. The Harm advantage is substantial (roughly 62% of the total score mass); the Help comparison is essentially tied (4.64 vs. 4.36, close to the 4.5 midpoint). This indicates RLCD improves harmlessness without sacrificing helpfulness.
- RLCD7B vs. Context-Dist7B: RLCD achieves 4.51 / 4.49 on Harm (essentially tied) and 4.69 / 4.31 on Help (modest advantage). Context distillation is competitive on harmlessness specifically at 7B scale but notably weaker on helpfulness.
- RLCD30B vs. RLAIF30B: 4.71 / 4.29 on Harm, 4.50 / 4.50 on Help (exact tie). The gap narrows substantially at 30B scale, though RLCD still holds an edge on harmlessness.
- RLCD30B vs. Context-Dist30B: 4.80 / 4.20 on Harm, 4.88 / 4.12 on Help — RLCD maintains a clear advantage over context distillation even at 30B.

For **helpfulness prompts** (Help only):
- RLCD7B vs. RLAIF7B: 5.88 / 3.12 — a large margin in RLCD's favor.
- RLCD7B vs. Context-Dist7B: 5.73 / 3.27 — similarly large advantage.
- RLCD30B vs. RLAIF30B: 4.51 / 4.49 — essentially tied at 30B scale.
- RLCD30B vs. Context-Dist30B: 5.72 / 3.28 — RLCD still strongly preferred.

For **outlining prompts** (Qual only):
- RLCD7B vs. RLAIF7B: 5.97 / 3.03 — strongest advantage across all comparisons.
- RLCD7B vs. Context-Dist7B: 5.67 / 3.33.
- RLCD30B vs. RLAIF30B: 4.76 / 4.24 — still an edge for RLCD but narrower.
- RLCD30B vs. Context-Dist30B: 5.78 / 3.22 — RLCD maintains a large advantage over context distillation even at 30B.

**GPT-4 evaluation results (Table 3).** The GPT-4 evaluations (1,000 examples per comparison, binary forced choice) largely corroborate the human judgments but with more extreme preference ratios in some cases:

- RLCD7B vs. LLaMA: 82.8% on Harm, 77.0% on Help, 90.7% on Helpfulness task, 76.0% on Qual — confirming that RLCD produces outputs clearly distinguishable from the unaligned base model.
- RLCD7B vs. RLAIF7B: 84.8% on Harm, 71.0% on Help (harmlessness prompts), 85.4% on Help (helpfulness prompts), 78.5% on Qual — consistent large advantages.
- RLCD7B vs. Context-Dist7B: 69.7% on Harm, 67.7% on Help (harmlessness prompts), 89.5% on Help (helpfulness prompts), 71.8% on Qual.
- RLCD30B vs. LLaMA: 78.9% on Harm, 78.3% on Help, 81.3% on Helpfulness task, 55.7% on Qual — the smaller advantage on outlining quality (55.7%, close to 50%) suggests that at 30B scale, the base LLaMA-7B's outlines are already reasonable enough that RLCD30B's improvements are harder to detect via GPT-4 judgment.
- RLCD30B vs. RLAIF30B: 60.3% on Harm, 55.3% on Help, 47.8% on Helpfulness task, 35.9% on Qual — these are the most notable results. On harmlessness, RLCD30B maintains an edge (60.3%). On helpfulness and outlining, GPT-4 actually *prefers RLAIF30B* (47.8% and 35.9% for RLCD, meaning RLAIF30B is preferred 52.2% and 64.1% of the time respectively). This contrasts with the human evaluations, where RLCD30B was always equal or better (Table 2: 4.71/4.29 Harm, 4.50/4.50 Help-helpfulness, 4.76/4.24 Qual). The paper addresses this discrepancy in Appendix G, noting that human-GPT-4 agreement is particularly low on these specific comparisons (62.8% on helpfulness, 59.0% on outlining; Table 23), suggesting that at this level of output quality, GPT-4's judgments may diverge meaningfully from human preferences.
- RLCD30B vs. Context-Dist30B: 64.5% on Harm, 69.3% on Help, 74.9% on Helpfulness task, 62.2% on Qual — RLCD maintains a clear advantage over context distillation across all metrics and model scales.

**Qualitative evidence.** Table 4 shows a representative helpfulness example. When asked "What did Thomas Edison invent?", the base LLaMA answers "An alkaline battery" (factually incorrect), RLAIF7B gives a generic non-answer ("Thomas Alva Edison was an American inventor and businessman"), Context-Dist7B says "The light bulb" (partially correct but incomplete), and RLCD7B produces a comprehensive, factually detailed response covering multiple inventions, historical context, and technical details. The paper uses this example to illustrate the substantial gap between RLCD7B and baselines on helpfulness at 7B scale. Additional i.i.d. examples in Appendix I (Tables 26 and 27) support the qualitative assessment.

#### Preference Model Quality Analysis

**Agreement with human preferences (Table 5).** The paper evaluates RLCD's and RLAIF's trained preference models against 2,000 gold human-labeled preference pairs from Bai et al. (2022a), using the same prompts as were used for preference data simulation. Two metrics are reported: binary accuracy (whether the model assigns higher probability to the human-preferred output) and average probability assigned to the gold output.

On **harmlessness prompts**:
- RLAIF7B: 35.6% binary accuracy, 0.492 average probability — both *below chance* (50%, 0.5). This means RLAIF7B's preference model has learned an *inverted* preference function, systematically preferring outputs that humans consider worse. This is a catastrophic failure mode.
- RLCD7B: 52.4% accuracy, 0.516 probability — modestly above chance, indicating some genuine signal but far from perfect.
- RLAIF30B: 45.7% accuracy, 0.489 probability — still below chance, though closer to 50% than the 7B version.
- RLCD30B: 55.9% accuracy, 0.542 probability — the best performance on harmlessness, but still only modestly above chance.

On **helpfulness prompts**:
- RLAIF7B: 60.6% accuracy, 0.508 probability — above chance but only weakly confident.
- RLCD7B: 64.4% accuracy, 0.601 probability — notably higher accuracy and substantially higher confidence (0.601 vs. 0.508).
- RLAIF30B: 66.2% accuracy, 0.551 probability.
- RLCD30B: 66.7% accuracy, 0.628 probability — highest confidence among all comparisons.

These results are striking for two reasons. First, they confirm that RLAIF's preference models at 7B are essentially broken on harmlessness (below-chance accuracy), explaining why RLAIF7B performs so poorly downstream. Second, they show that RLCD's preference models not only achieve higher accuracy but also higher *confidence* (polarity) — the average probability assigned to the preferred output is consistently higher for RLCD than RLAIF. The paper attributes this to RLCD's use of discrete binary labels (0 or 1) rather than RLAIF's continuous probability labels, which tend to be weak (near 0.5) because the i.i.d. outputs are difficult to discriminate. This is corroborated by the observation in Appendix B: LLaMA-7B "frequently giving fairly weak preferences when labeling paired outputs" in RLAIF.

#### Rescoring Variant Analysis

**RLCD vs. RLCD-Rescore (Table 6).** The RLCD-Rescore variant generates preference pairs `$(o^+, o^-)$` using RLCD's contrastive prompts but then re-labels them using RLAIF's post hoc scoring procedure. The comparison against standard RLCD (which uses labels-by-construction) reveals whether RLCD's advantage comes primarily from contrastive generation or from the labeling approach.

At **7B scale** (GPT-4 preferences for RLCD over RLCD-Rescore):
- Harmlessness (Harm): 86.0% — RLCD dramatically outperforms rescoring.
- Harmlessness (Help): 75.8%.
- Helpfulness: 86.3%.
- Outlining: 88.8%.

These margins are enormous — larger than RLCD's advantages over RLAIF itself (Table 3: 84.8%, 71.0%, 85.4%, 78.5%). This confirms that at 7B scale, the LLaMA-7B scoring model is so unreliable that it actively degrades label quality even when the underlying pairs are well-separated by contrastive generation. The post hoc scoring introduces noise that flips correct labels (cases where `$o^+$` is genuinely better but the scorer prefers `$o^-$` due to its error), making the training signal worse than simply trusting the prompt construction.

At **30B scale** (GPT-4 preferences):
- Harmlessness (Harm): 54.6% — RLCD still preferred but by a much narrower margin.
- Harmlessness (Help): 53.2%.
- Helpfulness: 47.3% — RLCD-Rescore is slightly preferred (52.7%).
- Outlining: 36.4% — RLCD-Rescore is strongly preferred (63.6%).

The reversal at 30B scale is instructive. On helpfulness and outlining, the 30B scoring model is now capable enough that rescoring improves label quality over the naive label-by-construction approach — presumably by correcting cases where `$o^-$` happened to be better than `$o^+$` (due to stochasticity in generation) and the prompt-based label was wrong. On harmlessness, RLCD's labels-by-construction still hold a slight edge, perhaps because the scoring model's errors on harmfulness judgments (discussed in Appendix C — e.g., confusing "harmful" with "less harmful" in the prompt) remain non-trivial even at 30B.

This result is the paper's strongest evidence for one of its central claims: **RLCD's labeling approach is specifically beneficial when the base model is too weak to reliably score its own outputs**. At 7B, the scoring model is useless; RLCD's labels-by-construction are dramatically better. At 30B, the scoring model becomes competitive, and a hybrid approach (contrastive generation + AI scoring) can match or exceed RLCD on some tasks.

#### Comparison Against RLAIF Variants

**Binarized RLAIF (Appendix B, Table 14).** Since RLCD uses binary labels while RLAIF uses continuous probabilities, the paper tests whether binarizing RLAIF's labels closes the gap. RLAIF-Binary7B converts all RLAIF preference probabilities to 0/1 labels (thresholding at 0.5). GPT-4 pairwise comparisons show RLCD7B still outperforms RLAIF-Binary7B: 85.3% on Harm, 72.7% on Help (harmlessness prompts), 87.5% on helpfulness, 71.3% on outlining. These numbers are very similar to RLCD7B vs. RLAIF7B (Table 3: 84.8%, 71.0%, 85.4%, 78.5%), indicating that the label format (binary vs. continuous) is not the primary driver of RLCD's advantage — the quality of the underlying pairs matters more.

**Few-shot RLAIF (Appendix C, Tables 15 and 16).** Bai et al. (2022b) found that RLAIF requires few-shot prompting to exceed chance-level human agreement. The paper replicates this: RLAIF-Few30B achieves 57.0% binary accuracy on harmlessness (Table 15), compared to 45.7% for zero-shot RLAIF30B (Table 5). However, downstream pairwise comparison reveals a tradeoff: GPT-4 prefers RLAIF-Few30B over RLCD30B on harmlessness (57.9% for RLAIF-Few30B; Table 16), but RLCD30B over RLAIF-Few30B on helpfulness (56.9% for RLCD30B). The paper's qualitative analysis explains why: RLAIF-Few30B exhibits **heavy mode collapse** toward a generic harmless-but-useless response (Table 17 shows outputs like "I'm sorry, but I'm not sure how to help with that" appearing across diverse contexts). The few-shot examples from Bai et al. (2022b) emphasize harmlessness without balancing helpfulness, causing the model to learn a degenerate policy. This is not a criticism of RLCD per se — RLCD doesn't exhibit this mode collapse (Appendix I examples show diverse, contextually appropriate responses) — but it demonstrates that RLAIF's performance is brittle to prompt design in ways that RLCD's is not, since RLCD's contrastive generation doesn't rely on few-shot examples that can introduce such biases.

**RLAIF with positive prompt (Appendix L, Table 39).** An alternative hypothesis is that RLAIF might perform better if its generation prompt `$p$` is replaced with RLCD's `$p^+$`, so the i.i.d. outputs are both sampled from the "good" distribution. RLAIFp+7B tests this. GPT-4 comparisons against RLCD7B show: 81.2% on Harm, 70.7% on Help, 89.9% on helpfulness, 81.0% on outlining — nearly identical to RLCD7B vs. RLAIF7B (84.8%, 71.0%, 85.4%, 78.5%). This confirms that RLCD's advantage does *not* come from merely using better prompts during generation; it comes specifically from the **contrast between `$p^+$` and `$p^-$`** and the resulting label-by-construction.

#### Human Preference Data Augmentation

**RLCD with 20% human data (Appendix M, Table 40).** The paper simulates a realistic scenario where a small amount of human preference data is available and the goal is to augment it with automatic data. Using 20% human-labeled pairs mixed with 80% automatically generated pairs (at 7B scale), RLCD7B-20%-human still outperforms RLAIF7B-20%-human: 68.9% on Harm, 59.4% on Help (harmlessness prompts), 55.8% on helpfulness. The margins are narrower than in the purely automatic setting (84.8%, 71.0%, 85.4% from Table 3), as expected — the human data provides a shared strong signal that partially compensates for RLAIF's weaker automatic pairs. But RLCD still maintains an edge, indicating that its cleaner automatic labels provide complementary signal even when some ground-truth labels are available.

#### Automatic Metrics on Final Model Outputs

**Held-out reward model evaluation (Appendix J.1, Tables 28 and 29).** Using a reward model trained on human preference data (from the original Anthropic datasets) as an independent evaluator:
- At 7B: RLCD7B achieves the highest reward on both harmlessness (1.43) and helpfulness (0.98), compared to RLAIF7B (1.11, 0.06), Context-Dist7B (0.80, −0.19), and base LLaMA (0.26, −1.01). The margins are substantial — RLCD's helpfulness reward (0.98) is more than 16× RLAIF's (0.06), which is barely above the base model's −1.01.
- At 30B: RLCD30B achieves the highest reward on harmlessness (1.28) but is slightly behind RLAIF30B on helpfulness (0.80 vs. 0.91). Context-Dist30B achieves 1.07 on harmlessness (second-best) but −0.28 on helpfulness (worse than base LLaMA's −1.01? No — that was at 7B; the base LLaMA value of 0.26 and −1.01 are at 7B scale, and 30B tables use LLaMA-30B simulation but still evaluate LLaMA-7B-aligned outputs, so the base LLaMA baseline is identical). These results broadly corroborate the pairwise evaluation findings.

**Output diversity (Appendix J.3, Tables 32 and 33).** Measured by distinct n-gram fractions (Dist-1, Dist-2, Dist-3) on 10,000-word samples with individual outputs truncated to 20 words:
- At 7B: RLCD7B shows lower diversity on harmlessness prompts (Dist-1: 11.0% vs. 25.9% for RLAIF7B and 22.9% for LLaMA), which the paper attributes to "repetitive wording in sometimes refusing to answer." This is a known tradeoff: high harmlessness often requires refusing to engage with toxic prompts, and refusals tend to use formulaic language. On helpfulness (30.2% vs. 30.4% for RLAIF7B) and outlining (22.5% vs. 23.6%), diversity is comparable.
- At 30B: Diversity is broadly similar across all methods, with RLCD30B's Dist-1 at 21.3% (harmlessness), 28.7% (helpfulness), 22.6% (outlining) compared to RLAIF30B's 22.1%, 27.7%, 22.5%.

**Output length (Appendix J.4, Tables 34 and 35).** RLCD consistently generates longer outputs than RLAIF for helpfulness and outlining, which the paper interprets as RLCD learning that longer responses better satisfy those alignment criteria:
- Helpfulness at 7B: RLCD7B mean 118.0 tokens (SD 48.9) vs. RLAIF7B 35.4 tokens (SD 29.3) — a 3.3× difference. This aligns with the qualitative example in Table 4, where RLCD7B produces a comprehensive multi-paragraph answer while RLAIF7B gives a one-sentence non-answer.
- Outlining at 30B: RLCD30B mean 138.7 tokens (SD 66.9) vs. RLAIF30B 88.6 tokens (SD 41.1).
- Harmlessness at 7B: RLCD7B mean 66.5 tokens (SD 28.8) vs. RLAIF7B 42.1 tokens (SD 40.9); at 30B, 73.3 vs. 78.1 — roughly comparable.

**Output perplexity (Appendix J.2, Tables 30 and 31).** GPT-3 (davinci) conditional perplexity of final outputs is broadly similar across methods, with RLCD showing slightly higher perplexity on outlining (2.26 vs. 2.10 for RLAIF7B; 2.34 vs. 2.09 for RLAIF30B), which the paper attributes to more successfully optimizing for interestingness (producing more surprising, less predictable outlines). Context-Dist consistently shows the lowest perplexity across all settings (e.g., 2.05 on harmlessness at 30B vs. 2.34 for RLCD30B), likely reflecting its supervised fine-tuning objective which encourages outputs that match a single distribution rather than optimizing for quality.

#### Simulated Preference Data Quality Analysis

**Label accuracy according to held-out reward model (Appendix K.1, Table 36).** Using the same held-out human-trained reward models from Appendix J.1 to evaluate the correctness of preference labels in the simulated training data:
- Harmlessness: RLCD7B 54% label accuracy vs. RLAIF7B 44%; RLCD30B 60% vs. RLAIF30B 46%.
- Helpfulness: RLCD7B 68% vs. RLAIF7B 56%; RLCD30B 74% vs. RLAIF30B 66%.

RLCD's simulated preference pairs have substantially more accurate labels at both model scales, with the gap being larger on harmlessness than helpfulness. Notably, RLAIF30B achieves only 46% accuracy on harmlessness — still below chance according to the held-out model — consistent with its preference model's 45.7% binary accuracy on gold human labels (Table 5). RLCD30B's 60% accuracy on harmlessness, while far from perfect, provides enough signal to train a preference model that achieves 55.9% human agreement.

**Diversity of simulated preference data (Appendix K.2, Tables 37 and 38).** Measured by distinct n-gram fractions, the diversity of RLCD's simulated preference pairs is very similar to RLAIF's at both 7B and 30B scales, with RLCD showing slightly higher diversity more often than not. For example, at 7B on harmlessness, RLCD's Dist-1 is 21.5% vs. RLAIF's 21.3%; at 30B on helpfulness, RLCD's Dist-1 is 25.3% vs. RLAIF's 24.2%. This addresses a potential concern that using fixed prompt pairs might reduce output diversity (by always pushing outputs toward the same attribute poles); the data shows this is not the case.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Label format: binary vs. continuous (Appendix B, Table 14).** The binarized RLAIF7B variant, which converts RLAIF's continuous probability labels to 0/1 binary labels, does not close the gap with RLCD7B. GPT-4 preferences for RLCD7B over RLAIF-Binary7B are 85.3% (Harm), 72.7% (Help on harmlessness prompts), 87.5% (Helpfulness), 71.3% (Outlining) — nearly identical to RLCD7B vs. standard RLAIF7B (84.8%, 71.0%, 85.4%, 78.5%). This implies that RLCD's advantage is driven by **pair quality** (contrastive generation producing more clearly differentiated outputs), not merely by using binary rather than continuous labels.

- **Scoring approach: labels-by-construction vs. post hoc rescoring (Section 5.2, Table 6).** The RLCD-Rescore variant tests whether contrastive generation alone is sufficient, or whether the labels-by-construction approach is also necessary. At 7B, RLCD dramatically outperforms RLCD-Rescore (GPT-4 preferences: 86.0% Harm, 86.3% Helpfulness, 88.8% Outlining), confirming that LLaMA-7B's scoring model is too noisy to usefully relabel even well-separated pairs. At 30B, the gap narrows and reverses on some tasks (47.3% on Helpfulness, 36.4% on Outlining — i.e., RLCD-Rescore is preferred), indicating that with sufficient model capability, post hoc scoring becomes viable and may even outperform labels-by-construction on tasks where the generation-based labels are imperfect (e.g., when `$o^-$` occasionally beats `$o^+$` due to stochasticity).

- **Few-shot prompting for RLAIF (Appendix C, Tables 15 and 16).** Adding few-shot examples to RLAIF's scoring prompts (following Bai et al., 2022b) improves harmlessness preference model accuracy at 30B (57.0% for RLAIF-Few30B vs. 45.7% for zero-shot RLAIF30B; Table 5 vs. Table 15). However, it introduces a new failure mode: **mode collapse** to a generic harmless-but-useless response (Table 17). This is a robustness concern — RLAIF's performance is sensitive to the specific few-shot examples chosen, and examples that emphasize one attribute (harmlessness) without balancing others (helpfulness) can produce degenerate policies. RLCD does not use few-shot examples and does not exhibit this mode collapse.

- **Positive-prompt-only RLAIF (Appendix L, Table 39).** An RLAIF variant using RLCD's `$p^+$` prompt for generation (instead of the base `$p$`) performs no better than standard RLAIF against RLCD: GPT-4 preferences for RLCD7B over RLAIFp+7B are 81.2% (Harm), 70.7% (Help on harmlessness), 89.9% (Helpfulness), 81.0% (Outlining). This is a critical negative result: it shows that RLCD's advantage does not come from simply using better prompts. If better prompts alone were sufficient, RLAIF with `$p^+$` would close the gap; it doesn't. The advantage comes specifically from the **contrast** between `$p^+$` and `$p^-$` and the resulting labels-by-construction.

- **Focused harmlessness prompts (Appendix D, Table 20).** When the harmlessness task is modified to use prompts purely focused on harmlessness (removing the helpfulness component present in the main experiments' prompts, which follow Bai et al., 2022b), RLCD7B still outperforms all baselines: 80.8% Harm vs. LLaMA, 80.3% vs. RLAIF7B, 69.1% vs. Context-Dist7B. However, the paper notes that in this setting, "RLCD frequently produces outputs which are irrelevant to the previous dialogue in exchange for maximizing harmlessness" — the model learns that the easiest way to avoid generating harmful content is to disengage from the conversation entirely. This is not a failure of RLCD per se (RLAIF would face the same incentive) but rather a specification problem: a harmlessness-only objective that doesn't also reward helpfulness naturally leads to degenerate refusal policies.

- **Mixing human and automatic preference data (Appendix M, Table 40).** When 20% of the simulated preference data is replaced with human-labeled pairs, RLCD maintains an advantage over RLAIF, though the margin narrows: GPT-4 preferences for RLCD7B-20%-human over RLAIF7B-20%-human are 68.9% (Harm), 59.4% (Help on harmlessness), 55.8% (Helpfulness). This robustness check confirms that RLCD's automatic pairs provide useful complementary signal even when some ground-truth labels are available, rather than being redundant with or dominated by human data.

- **Preference model polarity (Section 5.1, Table 5).** The average probability that RLCD's preference models assign to the gold human-preferred output is consistently higher than RLAIF's (e.g., 0.601 vs. 0.508 for 7B on helpfulness; 0.628 vs. 0.551 for 30B). This "higher polarity" is a robustness-relevant property: more confident preference judgments may provide stronger gradient signal during PPO training, and may also make the reward model more resistant to exploitation (over-optimization) compared to a preference model with weak, uncertain preferences. The paper does not directly test this mechanism, but it is a plausible contributor to RLCD's downstream performance advantage beyond raw accuracy.

- **Task generalization.** The method is tested across three qualitatively different alignment tasks: harmlessness (ethical/social judgment), helpfulness (informational utility), and story outlining (creative quality with multiple sub-attributes). RLCD outperforms baselines on all three, suggesting that the contrastive generation approach is not specific to a particular type of alignment attribute. The outlining task is notable because it requires optimizing for multiple attributes simultaneously (interestingness, well-formedness, premise relevance) — RLCD handles this by using three separate prompt pairs, each targeting one attribute, and sampling randomly among them during data generation.

- **Model scale for preference data simulation.** RLCD is tested at two simulation scales (7B and 30B) and consistently outperforms or matches baselines at both. However, the *magnitude* of RLCD's advantage is much larger at 7B (where RLAIF essentially fails) than at 30B (where RLAIF becomes competitive). This is consistent with RLCD's claimed mechanism (trading example difficulty for label accuracy is most beneficial when the scorer is weak) and suggests that RLCD's primary practical value is at smaller model scales where RLAIF is non-functional.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does RLCD genuinely outperform RLAIF and context distillation?

**Yes, with strong evidence at 7B scale; yes, but with narrower margins and some GPT-4 reversals at 30B scale.**

At 7B, the evidence is unambiguous. Human evaluators prefer RLCD7B over RLAIF7B on all metrics (Table 2: 5.62/3.38 Harm, 4.64/4.36 Help, 5.88/3.12 Helpfulness, 5.97/3.03 Outlining). GPT-4 evaluations are even more decisive (Table 3: 84.8%, 71.0%, 85.4%, 78.5%). The preference model analysis (Table 5) reveals *why*: RLAIF7B's preference model achieves below-chance accuracy on harmlessness (35.6%), meaning it has learned systematically wrong preferences. RLCD7B's preference model, while far from perfect (52.4%), provides enough signal to train a functional downstream policy. The rescoring experiment (Table 6) confirms that RLCD's label-by-construction approach — not just contrastive generation — is the critical factor, since RLCD-Rescore7B dramatically underperforms RLCD7B. The qualitative examples (Tables 4, 26, 27) are consistent with the quantitative results. This part of the claim is **strongly supported**.

At 30B, the picture is more nuanced. Human evaluations still favor RLCD30B on all comparisons (Table 2), but the margins are smaller — e.g., 4.71/4.29 on Harm vs. RLAIF30B, compared to 5.62/3.38 at 7B. GPT-4 evaluations actually *reverse* on helpfulness and outlining (Table 3: RLCD30B preferred only 47.8% and 35.9% vs. RLAIF30B, meaning RLAIF30B is preferred). The paper attributes this partly to low human-GPT-4 agreement on these specific comparisons (Table 23: 62.8% on helpfulness, 59.0% on outlining). If we trust human judgments over GPT-4 at this quality level (a reasonable position, given stated agreement rates), RLCD30B still outperforms RLAIF30B, but the advantage is modest. This pattern is **consistent with RLCD's own theoretical framework**: as the base model scales up, `$\sigma_D$` (scoring noise) decreases, making RLAIF's post hoc scoring more competitive with RLCD's labels-by-construction. The claim that RLCD outperforms RLAIF at 30B is **supported by human judgments but contested by GPT-4 evaluations** on two of four metrics.

Against context distillation, the evidence is consistently strong across both scales and all evaluation modalities. Context-Dist underperforms RLCD in every pairwise comparison (Tables 2 and 3), with the gap being especially large on helpfulness (e.g., GPT-4: 89.5% for RLCD7B vs. Context-Dist7B; 74.9% for RLCD30B vs. Context-Dist30B) and outlining. This makes sense mechanistically: context distillation discards the pairwise contrast signal entirely, using only positive examples for supervised fine-tuning, which the RLHF literature has consistently found to underperform preference-based RL (Ouyang et al., 2022; Dubois et al., 2023).

#### Does RLCD enable alignment at model scales where RLAIF cannot work?

**Yes, with the caveat that "cannot work" means RLAIF's preference model has below-chance human agreement, not that RLAIF's downstream outputs are uniformly terrible.**

RLAIF7B's preference model achieves 35.6% binary accuracy on harmlessness (Table 5) — systematically worse than random guessing. This is a fundamental failure: the preference model has learned an *inverted* preference function. Yet the paper's downstream human evaluation of RLAIF7B still shows some improvement over the base LLaMA (though the paper doesn't report RLAIF7B vs. LLaMA directly — the base LLaMA comparison is only for RLCD7B in Table 2). The RLAIF7B examples in Appendix I (Tables 26 and 27) show outputs that are not catastrophically harmful, suggesting that even a broken preference model can provide some regularization during PPO that prevents extreme degeneration, even if it doesn't reliably push toward better outputs.

Nevertheless, the practical contrast is stark: RLCD7B produces outputs that human evaluators and GPT-4 strongly prefer over RLAIF7B's (Table 2, 3), and RLCD7B's preference model at least exceeds chance. For a practitioner with only a 7B model available, RLAIF is not a viable option for harmlessness alignment; RLCD is. This claim is **supported**.

#### Does the contrastive prompt construction (p⁺ vs. p⁻) specifically drive improvement, or would any directional prompt work?

The RLAIFp+ experiment (Appendix L, Table 39) directly addresses this. RLAIF using only `$p^+$` (no contrast, no `$p^-$`, still using post hoc scoring) performs no better than standard RLAIF against RLCD. This rules out the hypothesis that RLCD's advantage simply comes from using better (directional) prompts. The contrast between `$p^+$` and `$p^-$` — and the resulting label-by-construction that this contrast enables — is specifically what matters. This is a clean, well-designed ablation, and the claim is **strongly supported**.

#### Does the theoretical framework (Appendix N) accurately describe when RLCD works?

The theoretical model makes a specific, testable prediction: RLCD's advantage over RLAIF should shrink as model scale increases (because `$\sigma_D$` decreases, making post hoc scoring more reliable). This prediction is **borne out qualitatively** — RLCD's margins over RLAIF are much larger at 7B than at 30B (Tables 2 and 3). However, the paper does not systematically test this prediction across a range of model scales (e.g., 1B, 3B, 7B, 13B, 30B, 65B), which would be needed to confirm the functional form of the relationship. The two data points (7B and 30B) are suggestive but insufficient to validate the detailed probabilistic model.

Additionally, the theoretical model's second prediction — that `$\Delta$` (prompt separation) should be decreased at larger scales to maintain optimal example difficulty — is **not tested at all**. RLCD30B uses the same prompt pairs as RLCD7B, which means `$\Delta$` is held constant while `$\sigma_G$` and `$\sigma_D$` presumably decrease. The model would predict that this makes RLCD30B's examples "too easy" (excessively large `$\Delta$` relative to `$\sigma$`), which would manifest as diminishing marginal returns from the contrastive approach. This could explain why RLCD30B's advantage over RLAIF30B is smaller than RLCD7B's over RLAIF7B — but it could also be explained simply by RLAIF improving with scale, without any "suboptimal `$\Delta$`" effect. Disentangling these explanations would require an experiment varying `$\Delta$` at a fixed large model scale, which the paper does not conduct. This is a **genuine limitation** — the theoretical framework provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for the observed scaling behavior, but the paper does not experimentally validate its specific predictions beyond the qualitative trend.

#### What are the key limitations of the experimental design?

**Single model family (LLaMA).** All experiments use LLaMA-7B as the alignment target and LLaMA-7B or LLaMA-30B for data simulation. The paper argues LLaMA-7B is "representative," but this is unverified. Different model families have different sensitivity to prompt modifications — a model with stronger instruction-following capabilities might amplify prompt signals even more than LLaMA (making RLCD even more effective), while a model with poor prompt sensitivity might not produce sufficiently differentiated `$o^+$` and `$o^-$` (making RLCD fail). The paper does not test on any non-LLaMA model.

**Three tasks, all within a specific alignment domain.** Harmlessness, helpfulness, and story outline generation are all dialogue-based tasks where the assistant responds to a human prompt. Whether RLCD would work for non-dialogue alignment tasks (e.g., summarization quality, code generation correctness, translation accuracy) is untested. The mechanism — contrastive prompt pairs producing differentiated outputs — should generalize, but the prompt construction approach (modifying "Assistant:" indicators) is dialogue-specific and would need adaptation.

**No confidence intervals on pairwise evaluations.** Human evaluations use 200 examples per comparison, and GPT-4 evaluations use 1,000. Neither reports confidence intervals, making it impossible to assess whether, e.g., RLCD30B's 4.71/4.29 advantage over RLAIF30B on harmlessness (Table 2) is statistically significant or could be due to sampling noise. Given that some human evaluation margins are very small (4.51/4.49 for RLCD7B vs. Context-Dist7B on Harm), statistical significance is not a given. The paper's conclusions would be strengthened by reporting bootstrap confidence intervals or performing statistical tests on the pairwise comparison results.

**Hyperparameter selection uses self-evaluation, which may introduce bias.** Both RLCD and RLAIF select PPO hyperparameters by evaluating against their own learned reward models. If RLCD's reward model systematically overestimates the quality of certain types of outputs (e.g., longer responses), the hyperparameter selection could favor configurations that maximize the reward model's score without improving true alignment. This is partially mitigated by the symmetric application to all methods, but it means the *absolute* performance of all methods could be suboptimal relative to what hyperparameter tuning against human judgments would yield. The relative comparison remains valid only if the self-evaluation bias affects RLCD and RLAIF equally — an assumption that is plausible but untested.

**No comparison to methods using external oracle models.** The paper constrains itself to methods that use only the base model being aligned. Some RLAIF variants use stronger external models (e.g., GPT-4) as scoring oracles (Dubois et al., 2023). RLCD is not compared against such approaches, which means the paper's claims are specific to the "no external oracle" setting. This is a valid scope limitation but should be noted: if a practitioner has access to GPT-4, using it for RLAIF scoring may outperform RLCD with a 7B model.

**Limited analysis of what the preference model actually learns.** The paper reports binary accuracy and average probability of the preference model against human labels (Table 5), but does not analyze *which types* of examples the preference model gets right vs. wrong. Understanding whether RLCD's preference model fails on the same kinds of examples as RLAIF's (e.g., examples requiring subtle ethical reasoning) or different ones would provide insight into the method's limitations. The paper also does not examine whether the preference model exhibits systematic biases (e.g., consistently preferring longer responses, or responses with certain lexical patterns) that could be exploited during PPO.

**The outlining task evaluation relies entirely on GPT-4 and human judgment of "quality," which is inherently subjective.** Unlike harmlessness (where toxicity can be objectively identified) or helpfulness (where factual accuracy can be checked), outline quality is a matter of creative judgment. The high variance in human-GPT-4 agreement on outlining (Table 23: 59.0% for RLCD30B vs. RLAIF30B) underscores this subjectivity. Claims about RLCD's performance on outlining should be interpreted as "humans and GPT-4 subjectively prefer RLCD's outlines" rather than "RLCD's outlines are objectively better."

**No analysis of computational cost differences between methods.** The paper notes that RLAIF requires placing both outputs in context for scoring, which doubles context length and adds compute cost, but does not quantify this cost or factor it into comparisons. In practice, RLCD may be slightly cheaper than RLAIF at equal numbers of training pairs because it skips the scoring step entirely. However, this cost advantage is likely small relative to the cost of downstream PPO training (which is identical across methods). For the 30B experiments, the dominant cost is generating outputs from LLaMA-30B, which is common to both RLCD and RLAIF. A detailed FLOPs accounting would be useful for practitioners making deployment decisions but is not essential for the paper's methodological contribution.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 Prompt Engineering Burden and Sensitivity

**The assumption or constraint.** RLCD's effectiveness depends critically on the design of the positive and negative prompt pairs `p⁺` and `p⁻`. The paper's two criteria — attribute separation and surface form matching (Section 3.2) — provide high-level guidance, but the actual prompt construction is manual, task-specific, and requires iterative refinement based on observed model behavior. The paper documents several instances of this sensitivity: removing politeness language from harmlessness prompts because the model discovered a shortcut of prefacing responses with "Thank you" or "Sorry" (Appendix A.1); using 16 different prompt pairs for harmlessness to match the diversity of RLAIF's scoring instructions (Table 9); and noting that "it is difficult to entirely eliminate the impact of prompt design on performance" (Limitations section). The authors state:

> "The performance of both RLCD and baselines also depends on the prompts used for pairwise preference data generation and scoring, so the results could change with different prompts."

**The consequence.** A practitioner adopting RLCD faces an underspecified design problem: how to construct `p⁺` and `p⁻` for a new alignment task. The paper's examples (harmlessness, helpfulness, outlining) share a common structure — modifying "Assistant:" indicators in dialogue — but this pattern does not obviously transfer to non-dialogue tasks (e.g., summarization, code generation). If the prompts are too weak (insufficient `Δ` separation), RLCD's labels-by-construction become unreliable because `o⁺` and `o⁻` are not sufficiently differentiated on the target attribute. If the prompts are too strong or include unintended cues, the model discovers optimization shortcuts — as documented with politeness, and as could happen with any attribute that has a trivial proxy. The outlining prompts illustrate another challenge: different attributes (interestingness, well-formedness, premise relevance) require separate prompt pairs, and it is unclear how to balance or combine them for tasks where the desired attribute is multi-dimensional.

More subtly, the surface form matching criterion (Criterion 2) creates a tension: to make `p⁺` and `p⁻` differ *only* on the target attribute, the designer must identify and control for all orthogonal dimensions on which the prompts might inadvertently differ. This is a form of the **specification problem** familiar from reward design in RL: the prompt pair specifies what to optimize for, and RLCD's strong amplification of prompt signals (which the paper identifies as a strength) also amplifies any specification errors. The paper does not provide a systematic methodology for validating that a prompt pair isolates the intended attribute, relying instead on post hoc inspection of outputs and iterative refinement.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The politeness shortcut removal (Appendix A.1) is direct evidence of sensitivity. The need for 16 harmlessness prompt pairs (rather than a single pair) suggests that no single pair adequately captures the concept of harmlessness. The fact that RLAIF-Few30B collapses to a generic refusal policy ("I'm sorry, but I'm not sure how to help with that"; Table 17) while RLCD30B does not — despite RLCD30B's prompts being simpler and shorter — indicates that RLCD's behavior depends on *which* prompts are used, not just that prompts are contrastive. The focused harmlessness experiment (Appendix D), where prompts purely targeting harmlessness (without helpfulness) cause RLCD to produce irrelevant responses, shows directly that prompt design shapes the qualitative behavior of the aligned model.

**Mitigation status.** The paper **partially** addresses this through design principles (the two criteria) and ablation-like experiments (different prompt sets for different tasks, the focused harmlessness variant), but provides **no systematic methodology** for prompt construction and **no automated prompt optimization procedure**. The authors acknowledge that "it is difficult to entirely eliminate the impact of prompt design on performance" but offer no solution beyond careful manual design. The Discussion section gestures toward future work: "In cases where... one has a sufficiently strong scoring LLM to provide accurate labels even close to the classification boundary, one could alternatively attempt to create harder training examples by intentionally pushing `o⁺` and `o⁻` closer together" — suggesting control over prompt strength, but not how to achieve it. This is a **practical deployment barrier**: a method whose performance is sensitive to an underspecified manual design step is difficult to adopt reliably in new domains.

### 6.2 No Demonstration on Models Larger Than 30B or Non-LLaMA Architectures

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use LLaMA-7B as the model to be aligned, with preference data simulated either by LLaMA-7B or LLaMA-30B. The authors acknowledge this explicitly:

> "While we have carefully investigated the effectiveness of RLCD compared to several baselines on three tasks for LLaMA-7B, and even run experiments with preference data simulation using LLaMA-30B, state-of-the-art pretrained LLMs are still much larger, and we have not yet empirically verified our conclusions when aligning larger pretrained LLMs."

The paper also uses only the LLaMA architecture family — no experiments on GPT-style models, Falcon, MPT, or any non-LLaMA model.

**The consequence.** The paper's central empirical finding — that RLCD dramatically outperforms RLAIF at 7B scale — is specific to a model size where RLAIF is known to fail (Bai et al., 2022b already observed that RLAIF requires models well above 10B parameters for above-chance human agreement). At 30B, where RLAIF starts becoming functional, RLCD's advantage narrows substantially (human evaluation margins shrink; GPT-4 actually prefers RLAIF30B on helpfulness and outlining). The natural extrapolation — suggested by RLCD's own theoretical framework (Appendix N) — is that at 65B, 70B, or larger scales, RLAIF's post hoc scoring may match or exceed RLCD's labels-by-construction, making RLCD's advantage disappear or reverse. The paper cannot confirm or refute this: it has **two data points** (7B and 30B) on the scaling curve, which is insufficient to establish the functional form or identify the crossover point.

Furthermore, different model families may have different sensitivity to prompt modifications. LLaMA models, as base (non-instruction-tuned) models, may respond differently to in-context cues like parenthetical "Assistant:" modifications compared to instruction-tuned models (which might be more responsive) or models with different pretraining mixtures (which might be less responsive). A model that is insensitive to RLCD's prompt modifications would not produce adequately differentiated `o⁺` and `o⁻`, causing RLCD's labels-by-construction to be unreliable. Conversely, a model that is extremely sensitive might over-amplify prompt cues and discover optimization shortcuts even more readily than LLaMA-7B did with politeness.

The single-architecture limitation also means that **all baseline comparisons are between variants of the same model family**. If LLaMA-7B has particular weaknesses as a scorer (e.g., the "bag of words" confusion discussed in Appendix C, where the model attends to the word "harmful" in the scoring prompt and prefers more harmful outputs), RLAIF7B's catastrophic failure (35.6% accuracy; Table 5) may be partly LLaMA-specific. A different 7B model might be a less-broken RLAIF scorer, which would shrink RLCD's apparent advantage.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The scaling trend is visible but sparse: RLCD7B outperforms RLAIF7B by enormous margins (GPT-4: 84.8% Harm, 85.4% Helpfulness; Table 3), while RLCD30B outperforms RLAIF30B by much smaller margins or loses (GPT-4: 60.3% Harm, 47.8% Helpfulness; Table 3). The theoretical analysis in Appendix N predicts this trend but the experimental data cannot distinguish between "RLAIF catches up at 30B" and "RLAIF surpasses RLCD at 65B." No experiments on any non-LLaMA model.

**Mitigation status.** **None beyond acknowledgment.** The paper lists this as the first item in its Limitations section and explicitly states that empirical verification at larger scales is left to future work. There is no discussion of how prompt construction might need to change for larger models (despite the theoretical model suggesting `Δ` should be reduced), and no experiments with other model families. The claims in the abstract and introduction ("outperforms RLAIF... across three diverse alignment tasks... when using both 7B and 30B model scales") are technically accurate but the 30B claim is qualified by the mixed GPT-4 results, and the generalization to larger scales is entirely untested.

### 6.3 Difficulty Estimation and Difficulty-Dependent Behavior Are Not Addressed

**The assumption or constraint.** RLCD applies the same prompt construction procedure to all input prompts `p`, regardless of the prompt's content, difficulty, or the base model's likely behavior on that prompt. There is **no mechanism for adapting the prompt strength (`Δ` separation), the choice of which prompt pair to use, or the generation strategy to the specific characteristics of each input**. Every prompt gets the same random draw from the pool of prompt affix pairs, with no consideration of whether that pair's particular framing of the attribute (e.g., "ethical response" vs. "harmless response") is appropriate for the given context.

**The consequence.** This is a **missed opportunity** rather than a failure mode per se, but it has practical implications for data efficiency. Consider a conversation where the human is asking for factual information with no ethical dimension. Applying a strong harmlessness prompt pair (e.g., "(harmless, helpful response)" vs. "(harmful, unhelpful response)") may produce `o⁺` and `o⁻` that differ minimally — because the base model's output distribution on factual questions is already concentrated on harmless responses regardless of prompting. The resulting preference pair has low signal (the outputs are similar) and the label-by-construction may be unreliable (if `o⁻` happens to be better than `o⁺` by chance). This wastes a training example.

Conversely, on a highly toxic conversation where even the positive prompt `p⁺` might not be sufficient to steer the model away from harmful outputs, RLCD's fixed prompt strength may produce `o⁺` that is still harmful (just less so than `o⁻`). The label-by-construction is technically correct (`o⁺` is preferred), but the pair provides only a weak gradient — the preference model learns "slightly harmful is better than very harmful" rather than "harmless is better than harmful," which may not be sufficient to drive the policy toward genuinely harmless behavior.

More broadly, the paper's earlier analysis (the compute-optimal framework in the reference paper) demonstrated that the optimal test-time strategy depends on prompt difficulty. RLCD does not incorporate any difficulty-dependent variation in its data generation. The same fixed `Δ` is used for all prompts, meaning the method cannot allocate "easier" (higher-`Δ`, more reliable) labeling to prompts where the base model's behavior is most ambiguous and "harder" (lower-`Δ`, more informative) labeling to prompts where contrast is already clear.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not analyze RLCD's performance as a function of prompt difficulty or conversation toxicity level. The held-out reward model label accuracy numbers (54% for RLCD7B harmlessness, 68% for RLCD7B helpfulness; Table 36) are averages over all prompts and do not reveal whether accuracy is substantially lower on certain prompt types. The preference model's human agreement (52.4% for RLCD7B harmlessness; Table 5) similarly averages over all examples without difficulty stratification. The qualitative examples in Appendix H show that RLCD sometimes produces `o⁺` responses that are still problematic (e.g., Example 3 in Table 24: `o⁺` says "OK then. The correct sequence of events follows:" and provides formatting instructions for a letter, which is still unhelpful in context), suggesting that on some prompts, even the positive prompt fails to elicit the desired behavior.

**Mitigation status.** **Not addressed.** The paper does not discuss difficulty estimation, adaptive prompt strength, or any mechanism for varying the data generation procedure based on input characteristics. The Discussion section gestures toward future possibilities — "use a method such as PREADD (Pei et al., 2023) to modulate the control strength of the prompts `p⁺`, `p⁻`" — but this is presented as a way to obtain "accurately labeled preference pairs closer to the classification boundary" at larger model scales, not as a difficulty-adaptive mechanism for improving data efficiency. The current RLCD implementation treats all prompts identically, missing potential gains from difficulty-conditioned allocation analogous to those demonstrated in the reference paper.

### 6.4 No Combination of RLCD with Other Sources of Preference Signal

**The assumption or constraint.** RLCD generates preference data using only the base LLM itself — no human labels, no external oracle models, no existing preference datasets. The paper treats RLCD as a **standalone replacement** for RLAIF in the zero-human-feedback setting, and all main comparisons are between RLCD and baselines that operate under the same constraint. The 20% human data mixing experiment (Appendix M, Table 40) is the only departure, and it is limited: a single mixing ratio (20% human), a single simulation scale (7B), and no exploration of *which* human examples to include.

**The consequence.** In practice, alignment pipelines are rarely purely automatic or purely human-labeled — they often involve a combination of high-quality human data, existing preference datasets, and automatically generated or augmented examples. RLCD's design does not provide a **principled way to combine its automatically-labeled contrastive pairs with other data sources**. The labels-by-construction approach means that RLCD pairs have a specific structure: `o⁺` is always the output from `p⁺`, and `o⁻` is always from `p⁻`. Human-labeled pairs have a different structure: `o₁` and `o₂` are both from the same prompt `p`, and the label comes from human judgment. These two data sources may provide **complementary or conflicting** training signals, and naively mixing them could produce a preference model that learns inconsistent preferences.

A specific concern: RLCD's preference pairs are, by design, easier to discriminate than RLAIF's or human-labeled pairs (the attribute gap between `o⁺` and `o⁻` is larger on average). A preference model trained on a mixture of RLCD pairs and human pairs might learn to rely on the "easy" RLCD signal (large attribute gaps, consistent labels) and underfit the more subtle human signal (small attribute gaps, some label noise), essentially ignoring the most informative human-labeled examples in favor of the cleaner but less nuanced RLCD examples. The paper's 20% mixing experiment (Table 40) shows RLCD7B-20%-human still outperforms RLAIF7B-20%-human, but this does not address whether the *combination* is better than RLCD alone or human data alone — the experiment only compares RLCD+mix to RLAIF+mix, not to pure RLCD or pure human data baselines.

Additionally, RLCD cannot easily leverage **existing preference datasets** (like the Anthropic harmlessness and helpfulness data used for evaluation). Those datasets consist of human-labeled pairs generated from a single prompt `p`, which don't match RLCD's training distribution (pairs from `p⁺` vs. `p⁻`). Incorporating such data would require either adapting the existing pairs to RLCD's format (not straightforward) or training on a heterogeneous mixture of pair types.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 20% human mixing experiment (Appendix M) provides a single data point suggesting that RLCD's automatic pairs add value even when human data is available, but the experiment is limited in scope (one ratio, 7B only, comparison only against RLAIF+mix, not against pure RLCD or pure human). The paper does not ablate the mixing ratio, does not test whether certain types of human examples are more complementary to RLCD than others, and does not evaluate whether the mixed preference model learns different preference structures than the pure-RLCD or pure-human models. No experiments combine RLCD with external oracle scoring (e.g., using GPT-4 to score RLCD's contrastively-generated pairs), which would be a natural hybrid between RLCD and RLCD-Rescore at scale.

**Mitigation status.** **Minimal.** The 20% mixing experiment is a token gesture rather than a systematic investigation. The Discussion section mentions future possibilities — "simulate preference labels in formats other than a single binary label, such as by ranking more than two outputs at a time" — but these are variations on RLCD's data format, not methods for combining RLCD data with other sources. The paper provides no guidance to practitioners on whether RLCD should be used as a complete replacement for human data (as in the main experiments) or as a supplement, and if the latter, at what ratio and with what preprocessing. Given that most practical alignment pipelines will have access to *some* human data (even if insufficient for full RLHF), this is a significant gap between the paper's experimental setup and realistic deployment scenarios.

### 6.5 The Correct-to-Incorrect Revision Problem in Sequential Refinement is Not Addressed

**The assumption or constraint.** RLCD generates a single pair `(o⁺, o⁻)` per prompt and labels `o⁺` as preferred unconditionally. There is no mechanism for **sequential refinement** — generating `o⁺`, evaluating it, and then conditionally generating `o⁻` or re-prompting — nor any mechanism for the model to recognize when `o⁺` is already good and should not be "revised" toward a worse output. This is not a failure of RLCD's core method, but rather a **comparison to the reference paper's finding** that revision models can inadvertently convert correct outputs back to incorrect ones (the ~38% correct-to-incorrect reversion rate documented there). RLCD's approach, which generates `o⁺` and `o⁻` independently, does not have this specific failure mode — but it also **cannot benefit from the iterative improvement that sequential revision enables on easy problems**.

**The consequence.** The reference paper demonstrated that for easy problems, purely sequential revision (generating a chain of revisions, each building on the previous attempt) substantially outperforms parallel sampling at the same generation budget. RLCD's independent generation of `o⁺` and `o⁻` is analogous to parallel sampling with N=2 — a very small budget that does not exploit the possibility of iterative improvement. On a prompt where the base model's first attempt is roughly correct but contains a specific error, RLCD's positive prompt `p⁺` might produce a slightly better output, but there is no mechanism for taking that output and refining it further. The method generates one positive output and one negative output, and that is the entire preference pair.

This is a **missed opportunity for data quality** rather than a failure mode. In principle, RLCD's contrastive prompt construction could be combined with sequential revision: generate an initial `o⁺`, check whether it meets some quality threshold (using a verifier or the model's own confidence), and if not, revise it with additional positive prompting. This would produce `o⁺` of higher average quality, which would provide stronger positive training signal. Similarly, `o⁻` could be generated through a chain of increasingly negative prompts, producing clearer examples of failure modes. The paper does not explore this direction.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not directly address this issue, as it is not within the paper's scope — RLCD is a method for generating *one* preference pair per prompt, and the reference paper's revision analysis is from a different research context. However, the paper's qualitative examples show that RLCD's `o⁺` outputs are sometimes imperfect: in Example 3 of Table 24 (harmlessness), `o⁺` says "OK then. The correct sequence of events follows:" and proceeds to give unhelpful formatting instructions, suggesting that on this prompt, even the positive prompt did not produce an output that a human would clearly prefer over `o⁻`. A sequential revision approach might have improved this `o⁺`. The held-out label accuracy numbers (54% for RLCD7B harmlessness; Table 36) indicate that in ~46% of cases, the held-out reward model disagrees with RLCD's label-by-construction — some of these disagreements are likely cases where `o⁺` was not actually better than `o⁻`, implying that `o⁺` quality is inconsistent and could benefit from refinement.

**Mitigation status.** **Not addressed.** The paper does not discuss sequential generation, iterative refinement, or any mechanism for improving individual outputs within a preference pair beyond the single-pass contrastive generation. This is not a flaw in the paper's contribution — RLCD is focused on the *pair generation and labeling* step, not on iterative output improvement — but it represents a direction where RLCD's approach could be strengthened by incorporating techniques from the reference paper, and the absence of this integration means RLCD leaves potential data quality improvements on the table.

### 6.6 Evaluation Relies Heavily on GPT-4 Judgments, with Imperfect Human Agreement

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's primary large-scale evaluation uses GPT-4 as an automatic judge, comparing 1,000 examples per pairwise comparison. Human evaluation is limited to 200 examples per comparison, and the paper does not report statistical significance for either evaluation modality. The paper acknowledges that human-GPT-4 agreement varies substantially across tasks and comparisons:

> "Agreement between humans and GPT-4 is typically in the 60% to 80% range across all comparisons. The metric which generally has the lowest agreement is helpfulness on the harmlessness prompt set, likely due to GPT-4's harmlessness alignment preventing it from giving useful answers in some cases on this evaluation." (Appendix G)

For the specific comparison of RLCD30B vs. RLAIF30B on helpfulness and outlining, human-GPT-4 agreement drops to 62.8% and 59.0% respectively (Table 23) — barely above chance for the binary preference judgment.

**The consequence.** The paper's headline results at 30B scale depend on **which evaluator you trust**. GPT-4 prefers RLAIF30B over RLCD30B on helpfulness (52.2% for RLAIF30B; Table 3) and outlining (64.1% for RLAIF30B). Human evaluators, on the same comparisons, either tie (4.50/4.50 on helpfulness; Table 2) or prefer RLCD30B (4.76/4.24 on outlining; Table 2). The paper's narrative — "RLCD30B is equal or better than RLAIF30B" — privileges human judgments over GPT-4 judgments, which is a defensible position but one that the paper does not argue explicitly or provide criteria for. A reader who trusts GPT-4 evaluations (which are larger-scale — 1,000 vs. 200 examples — and reproducible) would reach the opposite conclusion on two of four metrics.

More broadly, the reliance on GPT-4 as an evaluator introduces a systematic bias that the paper partially acknowledges but does not control for: GPT-4 is itself aligned for harmlessness and helpfulness, and this alignment can interfere with its ability to judge outputs on these dimensions. In particular, when evaluating harmlessness prompts (which contain toxic content), GPT-4 "frequently abstains when querying for helpfulness in harmful dialogues" (Appendix G). This creates a selection effect: the examples where GPT-4 *does* provide a judgment are those where the content is mild enough for GPT-4 to engage with, which may not be representative of the full test distribution. The paper handles GPT-4 abstentions by assigning a 0.5 score (tie) to both outputs (Appendix F.2), which biases evaluations toward 50-50 parity and may mask real differences between methods on the most challenging or toxic examples.

The 200-example human evaluation sample size is also a concern for close comparisons. For RLCD30B vs. RLAIF30B on harmlessness (Harm), the human scores are 4.71 vs. 4.29 (Table 2) — a difference of 0.42 on the normalized 1-8 scale. Without confidence intervals, it is impossible to know whether this difference is statistically significant or could arise from sampling 200 examples. The small sample size means that moderate but genuine differences between methods may not reach conventional significance thresholds, while a few outlier examples (where one method happens to produce a particularly good or bad output) could substantially influence the average.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 23 directly reports human-GPT-4 agreement across all comparisons, with values ranging from 59.0% (RLCD30B vs. RLAIF30B on outlining) to 86.5% (RLCD7B vs. LLaMA on helpfulness prompts). Appendix F.2 describes the GPT-4 parsing heuristics and the treatment of abstentions. The paper notes that the lowest agreement occurs on comparisons between the two strongest methods (RLCD30B vs. RLAIF30B), where both outputs are high-quality and the distinction is genuinely subtle. The paper does not report confidence intervals, p-values, or effect sizes for any pairwise comparison, nor does it analyze inter-annotator agreement among human evaluators.

**Mitigation status.** **Partial.** The paper uses both human and GPT-4 evaluation, which is good practice, and reports their agreement transparently (Table 23). However, the **interpretation** of the results does not adequately account for the disagreement. The paper's claims treat human evaluations as authoritative when they differ from GPT-4 (e.g., claiming RLCD30B outperforms RLAIF30B), but does not provide a principled justification for this choice or discuss how conclusions would change if GPT-4 were treated as authoritative instead. The small human evaluation sample (200 examples) and lack of statistical reporting mean that close comparisons (which constitute most of the 30B results) should be interpreted with substantial uncertainty — a caveat that the paper's confident presentation ("RLCD30B outperforms RLAIF30B") does not reflect.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **methodological reframing** rather than a paradigm shift: it demonstrates that the preference data generation step in LLM alignment pipelines is itself a designable source of supervision, not merely a fixed i.i.d. sampling procedure to which post hoc labeling is applied. This is not a change to the RLHF objective, the PPO algorithm, or the preference model architecture — the downstream pipeline is identical to RLAIF — but the paper provides compelling evidence that **where you get your preference pairs from matters as much as how you label them**, and that at smaller model scales, the generation procedure can substitute for the labeling procedure entirely.

The conceptual shift is from "automatic alignment means replacing human annotators with AI annotators" to **"automatic alignment means designing a data generation process where the correct label is known by construction."** This connects RLCD to the broader class of self-supervised methods in machine learning — contrastive learning in vision, masked language modeling, next-token prediction — where the training signal emerges from the structure of the data generation rather than from external annotation. In this framing, the contrastive prompt pair (p⁺, p⁻) defines a self-supervised task: the model learns to distinguish outputs from the "good" distribution from outputs from the "bad" distribution, where the distributions are defined by the prompts themselves.

The paper resolves a specific contradiction in the prior literature. Bai et al. (2022b) found that RLAIF can produce effective harmlessness alignment, but only when using few-shot prompting and model scales well above 10B parameters. The implicit message was: "automatic preference data simulation requires large models." RLCD contradicts this: at 7B scale, where RLAIF's preference model achieves **below-chance** human agreement (35.6% accuracy; Table 5), RLCD's preference model achieves above-chance agreement (52.4%) and produces downstream outputs that humans and GPT-4 strongly prefer over RLAIF's (84.8% GPT-4 preference on harmlessness; Table 3). This is not a marginal improvement — it is the difference between a method that **works** (produces aligned outputs distinguishable from the base model) and one that **essentially fails** (preference model learns inverted preferences). The reconciliation is that automatic alignment is not inherently limited by model scale; rather, RLAIF's specific mechanism (post hoc scoring of i.i.d. outputs) imposes a capability threshold that RLCD's mechanism (labels-by-construction from contrastive prompts) does not.

The paper also changes the **economics of alignment research**. If producing usable alignment training data requires 30B+ parameter models (as RLAIF does for harmlessness), then researchers and organizations without access to such models are locked out of developing and studying automatic alignment pipelines. RLCD shows that the same 7B model being aligned can generate effective training data, collapsing the compute barrier for experimentation. The paper explicitly frames this as democratization ("potentially enabling researchers and practitioners to experiment with RLAIF-style pipelines much faster and at lower cost"; Section 6), and the numbers bear this out: a 7B model is roughly 4× smaller than 30B and correspondingly cheaper to run inference with, making RLCD accessible on a single consumer GPU where RLAIF would require multiple high-memory GPUs.

The paper's findings also redirect research priorities for automatic alignment in several ways:

- **Improving post hoc AI scoring becomes less urgent at small model scales.** If RLCD's labels-by-construction are substantially more reliable than RLAIF's AI-generated labels at 7B (as shown by the rescoring experiment: Table 6, 86.0% GPT-4 preference for RLCD over RLCD-Rescore on harmlessness), then effort spent making LLaMA-7B a better scorer is addressing the wrong bottleneck. The priority shifts to improving **contrastive generation** — designing better prompt pairs, understanding how prompt strength interacts with model scale, and developing methods for automatic prompt pair construction.

- **The "large model as oracle" approach to alignment data generation becomes less obviously necessary.** Some prior work (Dubois et al., 2023) used stronger external models (e.g., GPT-4) as scoring oracles for RLAIF. RLCD demonstrates that, at least for the tasks studied, a model can generate its own high-quality training data without any external oracle — not by being a good judge of its own outputs, but by generating outputs from different conditions where the correct label is implicit. Whether this scales to tasks requiring more nuanced judgment than harmlessness or helpfulness (e.g., factual accuracy, reasoning correctness) is an open question, but the principle — structure the generation to make the label obvious — is general.

- **The tradeoff between example difficulty and label accuracy becomes a first-class design consideration.** Prior work on automatic alignment did not distinguish between "hard examples with noisy labels" and "easy examples with clean labels." RLCD's theoretical framework (Appendix N) formalizes this tradeoff and shows that at small model scales, the optimal strategy is to **sacrifice example difficulty for label accuracy** — generate easier-to-discriminate pairs with more reliable labels, accepting that they may be less informative per instance. This is a counterintuitive result if one thinks primarily about active learning (where hard examples are most valuable), but it makes sense in the regime where the annotator (the model's own scoring ability) is unreliable. The paper's analysis suggests that this tradeoff is parameterized by Δ (prompt separation) and σ_D (scoring noise), opening a design space where practitioners can tune example difficulty to match their model's scoring capability.

However, the paper's landscape impact is bounded by its empirical scope: one model family (LLaMA), three alignment tasks, and a scale range (7B to 30B) that does not reach the largest deployed models. The finding that RLCD dramatically outperforms RLAIF at 7B is robust and practically significant, but the trend at larger scales — where RLAIF becomes competitive — suggests that RLCD's primary contribution is **filling a capability gap at smaller scales** rather than establishing a universally superior approach. The paper's own theoretical model predicts that at sufficiently large scales (where σ_D is very small), post hoc scoring may match or exceed labels-by-construction. This makes RLCD's contribution more about **scalability downward** (enabling alignment at smaller, cheaper model scales) than about **scalability upward** (achieving better alignment at frontier scales). Both are valuable, but the framing matters for how the field incorporates the method.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Systematically mapping the prompt separation (Δ) scaling curve across model sizes.** The paper's theoretical model (Appendix N) makes a precise, untested prediction: as model scale increases, σ_G (output variance) and σ_D (scoring noise) decrease, which means that the optimal prompt separation Δ should also decrease to maintain an informative distribution of example difficulties. The paper tests exactly two points on this curve (7B and 30B) using the same prompts (fixed Δ), and observes that RLCD's advantage shrinks. A direct follow-up would **systematically vary prompt strength** at multiple model scales (e.g., 1B, 3B, 7B, 13B, 30B, 65B) by designing prompt pairs with different degrees of attribute encouragement — from very weak (p⁺ = "Assistant:" vs. p⁻ = "Assistant:" with a single subtle word change) to very strong (long detailed instructions for both directions). At each scale, one would measure: (a) label accuracy according to a held-out human-trained reward model, (b) downstream alignment performance after PPO, and (c) the Δ value that maximizes downstream performance. The prediction is that optimal Δ decreases with scale, and that using "too strong" prompts at large scales produces trivially easy examples that provide insufficient training signal. This experiment would validate or refine the theoretical model and provide practitioners with a scaling law for prompt construction strength. A strong negative result — optimal Δ being scale-invariant — would suggest that the theoretical model's assumptions (normality, independent noise) miss important aspects of how LLMs respond to prompt modifications.

**Combining RLCD's contrastive generation with sequential revision for higher-quality preference pairs.** The paper generates a single output o⁺ from the positive prompt and uses it directly. But o⁺ is not always good — the held-out label accuracy of RLCD7B pairs is only 54% on harmlessness (Table 36), meaning in ~46% of cases, the positive-prompt output is not actually preferred over the negative-prompt output according to a human-trained evaluator. Some of these failures are likely due to stochasticity (the model happened to generate a poor o⁺), and could be fixed by allowing multiple attempts. A specific follow-up experiment: generate o⁺¹, o⁺², o⁺³ from p⁺ (with different random seeds), score them using a cheap heuristic or the model's own likelihood, and select the best one (or reject and re-sample if all are poor) before pairing with o⁻. This is analogous to rejection sampling in the proposal distribution — using the model as its own quality filter without requiring it to make comparative judgments. The hypothesis is that this would raise label accuracy (fewer "o⁻ actually better than o⁺" pairs) and improve downstream alignment, particularly on prompts where the base model struggles to follow even the positive prompt. The experiment would also test whether the scoring mechanism for rejection can be simple (e.g., output length, presence of refusal keywords for harmlessness) or needs to be learned, and whether the rejection step introduces biases (e.g., selecting for verbose but substantively poor responses).

**Testing RLCD on non-dialogue alignment tasks with structurally different prompt construction.** The paper's three tasks — harmlessness, helpfulness, story outlining — are all dialogue-based and use the same prompt construction pattern: modifying "Assistant:" indicators in conversation transcripts. This pattern is convenient for surface form matching (Criterion 2) because only one token changes between p⁺ and p⁻. But many alignment tasks don't have this structure: summarization quality (where the "output" is a summary, not an assistant response), code generation correctness (where the attribute is functional accuracy, not conversational tone), or translation quality. A strong follow-up would design RLCD-style prompt pairs for a non-dialogue task — for instance, summarization, where p⁺ could append "Write a comprehensive summary:" and p⁻ could append "Write a very brief, incomplete summary:" before the model generates — and measure whether RLCD's advantage over RLAIF (which would compare two summaries from the same prompt and ask the model to judge which is better) replicates. This would test whether RLCD's contrastive principle generalizes beyond the conversational framing, and whether surface form matching (keeping prompts identical except for the attribute-targeting words) is feasible when the output format doesn't provide a natural insertion point like "Assistant:". A negative result — RLCD not outperforming RLAIF on non-dialogue tasks — would bound the method's applicability and suggest that RLCD benefits from the specific structure of dialogue, where "Assistant:" serves as a strong conditioning signal that reliably shifts the output distribution.

**Automatic discovery of effective prompt pairs for new alignment attributes.** The paper's prompt pairs are manually designed by the authors for each task, using their understanding of what constitutes harmlessness, helpfulness, or outline quality. For a new alignment attribute — say, "scientific accuracy" or "legal precision" — a practitioner would need to manually craft prompt pairs, iterate based on observed behavior (as the authors did with politeness), and validate that the pairs produce differentiated outputs without introducing unintended biases. This is labor-intensive and requires domain expertise. A concrete research direction: given a small set of example outputs that exemplify the desired attribute (e.g., 50 examples of scientifically accurate responses and 50 examples of scientifically inaccurate ones), can one **automatically generate** p⁺ and p⁻ prompt affixes that produce outputs matching those distributions? This could be approached as a discrete optimization problem — search over a vocabulary of attribute-describing words and phrasings, evaluate the resulting output distributions against the example sets, and select prompt pairs that maximize distributional separation on the target attribute while minimizing separation on orthogonal dimensions (measured via embedding similarity or LLM-as-judge). RLCD provides the framework (labels-by-construction from contrastive prompts); automating the prompt construction would make the framework applicable to arbitrary alignment dimensions without manual prompt engineering. The key metric would be whether automatically-discovered prompts achieve comparable downstream alignment to human-designed prompts for the tasks in the paper, establishing that manual prompt design is not a necessary ingredient.

**Difficulty-adaptive prompt strength (dynamic Δ) during preference data generation.** The paper's theoretical model (Appendix N) suggests that Δ should be chosen based on the model's scoring reliability (σ_D), but the current implementation uses fixed prompts for all inputs. However, the "difficulty" of generating a clearly differentiated preference pair likely varies across prompts — on a highly toxic conversation, even p⁺ might produce harmful outputs, making the pair less informative; on a benign factual question, p⁺ and p⁻ might produce nearly identical outputs, making the label-by-construction unreliable. A follow-up would implement **adaptive prompt selection**: for each input prompt p, first generate a small number of outputs from p⁺ and p⁻, measure the separation (e.g., via the difference in reward model scores, or via embedding distance between o⁺ and o⁻), and then decide whether to (a) use the current prompt pair, (b) switch to a stronger pair (larger Δ) if separation is insufficient, or (c) switch to a weaker pair (smaller Δ) if separation is trivially large. The hypothesis is that this would improve data efficiency by ensuring each preference pair provides an appropriate level of training signal — neither too easy (wasted budget) nor too noisy (incorrect label likely). This connects RLCD to the compute-optimal scaling framework from the reference paper, where strategy is allocated based on estimated prompt difficulty. The experiment would measure whether adaptive Δ improves downstream alignment accuracy at a fixed total generation budget compared to fixed-Δ RLCD.

**Combining RLCD preference pairs with DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) instead of PPO.** The paper uses the standard RLHF pipeline: train a preference model on the simulated pairs, then use PPO with the preference model as a reward model. Rafailov et al. (2023) proposed DPO, which directly optimizes the language model from preference pairs without training a separate reward model, by reparameterizing the preference-based objective. RLCD's preference pairs — which are discrete (o⁺ always preferred over o⁻) and generated contrastively — may be particularly well-suited for DPO, since DPO's loss function operates directly on pairs and does not require a learned reward model that could overfit to RLCD's potentially imperfect labels. A specific experiment: compare RLCD+DPO against RLCD+PPO (the current pipeline) and against RLAIF+DPO, measuring both downstream alignment (human/GPT-4 evaluation) and training stability (DPO eliminates the need for reward model training and KL-regularized RL, potentially simplifying hyperparameter tuning). The hypothesis is that DPO's direct optimization from pairs avoids the intermediate preference model, which for RLCD7B harmlessness achieves only 52.4% human agreement (Table 5) — removing this imperfect intermediary could improve the signal-to-noise ratio in the final optimization step. A negative result — DPO performing worse than PPO — would suggest that the preference model provides useful regularization even when imperfect.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Rapid prototyping of alignment strategies on consumer hardware.** The paper shows that RLCD with LLaMA-7B — a model that fits on a single consumer GPU (roughly 14GB in 8-bit precision) — produces usable alignment data with no human annotation and no external oracle. For a researcher or engineer who wants to test whether a new alignment dimension (e.g., "responses should cite sources") can be learned from automatic feedback, the current alternative is RLAIF, which at 7B scale produces a preference model with 35.6% human agreement on harmlessness (Table 5) — worse than random guessing. RLCD7B achieves 52.4% agreement, which is still modest but sufficient to produce downstream outputs that GPT-4 prefers over the base model at 82.8% on harmlessness and 90.7% on helpfulness (Table 3). The practical implication: a single developer with a single GPU can run an RLCD experiment in hours (generating preference pairs from 7B, training a preference model, running PPO) and get meaningful alignment signal, where RLAIF would require either a much larger model (30B+, needing multiple GPUs) or produce essentially useless signal. This reduces the iteration time for alignment research from "run on a cluster" to "run overnight on a workstation."

**Cost-efficient alignment data generation for medium-scale deployments.** Organizations deploying LLMs in the 7B–13B parameter range for specific applications (customer support, content moderation, educational tools) often cannot justify the expense of large-scale human preference annotation, and may not have access to 30B+ models for RLAIF data generation. RLCD provides a concrete recipe: take the same 7B model you plan to deploy, construct a handful of contrastive prompt pairs targeting the desired behavior (e.g., "Assistant (compliant with company policy):" vs. "Assistant (ignoring company policy):"), generate preference pairs from your actual deployment prompts, and run the standard RLHF pipeline. The paper's results at 7B — substantially outperforming context distillation (69.7% GPT-4 preference on harmlessness; Table 3) and matching or exceeding RLAIF at 30B on some metrics — suggest that RLCD can produce alignment quality competitive with methods requiring 4× larger models for data generation. The key practical advantage is that RLCD generates training data from exactly the model being deployed, so there is no distribution mismatch between the data generation model and the target model (a known issue when using larger models as data generators; the paper notes this in Appendix J, Figure 15a, where the base-model PRM underperforms on revision model outputs due to distribution shift).

**Bootstrapping alignment for low-resource languages and specialized domains.** The paper's English-only experiments don't demonstrate this directly, but RLCD's mechanism — contrastive prompt pairs that use natural language to specify the desired attribute direction — is inherently language-agnostic: the prompt affixes can be translated, and the generation model produces outputs in the target language. For a low-resource language where human annotators are scarce and expensive, RLCD offers a way to generate alignment data using only a pretrained base model in that language, with prompt pairs translated once by a bilingual speaker (a one-time cost of writing ~20 phrase pairs, not thousands of pairwise annotations). The paper shows that RLCD works with zero-shot prompts (no few-shot examples needed; Appendix C shows that few-shot RLAIF introduces mode collapse), which means the prompt pairs don't need to be calibrated with example outputs — they just need to express the desired attribute contrast in natural language. For a specialized domain like medical advice or legal reasoning, where general human annotators may lack expertise to judge output quality, RLCD's contrastive prompts can encode domain-specific criteria (e.g., "Assistant (citing peer-reviewed evidence):" vs. "Assistant (making claims without evidence):") without requiring annotators who can evaluate whether a specific response meets those criteria. The caveat is that this use case is extrapolated from the paper's English results and would need validation in the target language or domain.

**Augmenting small human preference datasets for improved data efficiency.** The paper's 20% human data mixing experiment (Appendix M, Table 40) provides initial evidence that RLCD's automatic pairs complement human-labeled data: even with 80% of the training pairs being simulated by RLCD7B (and only 20% from human annotations), the resulting model outperforms the same mixture using RLAIF7B simulated pairs — 68.9% vs. 31.1% GPT-4 preference on harmlessness. For an organization that has budget to collect, say, 5,000 human preference pairs, RLCD could expand this to 25,000 training pairs (4:1 automatic:human ratio) with the automatic pairs adding genuine signal rather than noise. The practical workflow: collect a modest amount of high-quality human data, generate a larger pool of RLCD preference pairs from the same prompt distribution, train the preference model on the mixture, and run PPO. The paper's experiment only tests a single ratio (20% human) and doesn't compare against using human data alone or RLCD alone, but it establishes that RLCD pairs are not redundant with human pairs and can improve the cost-effectiveness of partial human annotation. The key unknown — which the paper doesn't address — is the optimal mixing ratio as a function of human data quality and quantity; a practitioner would need to calibrate this on their own validation set.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper articulates a specific condition under which RLCD is preferred over RLAIF, and a specific condition under which post hoc rescoring (RLCD-Rescore or RLAIF) becomes competitive. These conditions are grounded in the empirical results and the theoretical analysis:

- **Prefer RLCD (labels-by-construction from contrastive prompts) over RLAIF when the base model for preference data simulation is relatively small or known to be an unreliable scorer of its own outputs.** Concretely, the paper shows that at 7B scale, LLaMA's post hoc scoring produces a preference model with below-chance human agreement on harmlessness (35.6% accuracy; Table 5), while RLCD's labels-by-construction achieve 52.4% — still modest, but above chance and sufficient for meaningful downstream alignment (GPT-4 prefers RLCD7B over base LLaMA at 82.8% on harmlessness; Table 3). The rescoring experiment confirms that even when RLCD generates well-separated pairs, the 7B scoring model degrades label quality (86.0% GPT-4 preference for RLCD over RLCD-Rescore on harmlessness; Table 6). The practical rule: **if your model's preference agreement with humans (or a trusted evaluator) is near or below chance when scoring i.i.d. outputs, use RLCD's labels-by-construction; post hoc scoring will only add noise.** This condition is most likely to hold for models below ~10B parameters on tasks requiring subtle judgment (the paper shows it holds for 7B on harmlessness; Bai et al. (2022b) found it holds below ~10B even with few-shot prompting).

- **Consider RLCD-Rescore (contrastive generation with post hoc AI relabeling) or RLAIF over RLCD when the base model for preference data simulation is large and a reliable scorer.** At 30B scale, the paper finds that RLCD-Rescore becomes competitive with RLCD — GPT-4 prefers RLCD-Rescore30B over RLCD30B on helpfulness (52.7%) and outlining (63.6%; Table 6) — indicating that the 30B scoring model is capable enough to usefully relabel contrastively-generated pairs (correcting cases where o⁻ happened to be better than o⁺). The human evaluations still prefer RLCD30B over RLAIF30B (e.g., 4.71/4.29 on harmlessness; Table 2), but the margins are small and GPT-4 disagrees on two metrics. The practical rule: **if your model is large enough that its own preference judgments are reliable (e.g., >60% agreement with humans, as RLAIF30B achieves 66.2% on helpfulness; Table 5), the choice between RLCD, RLCD-Rescore, and RLAIF becomes task-dependent and should be determined empirically on a validation set.** The paper's theoretical model (Appendix N) predicts that at sufficiently large scales, post hoc scoring eventually matches labels-by-construction, and the paper's two data points (7B and 30B) are consistent with this trend but insufficient to identify the crossover scale for specific tasks.

- **Prefer RLCD over context distillation when pairwise preference data can be generated and RL training is feasible.** The paper consistently finds that context distillation — which discards the contrastive signal and uses only o⁺ for supervised fine-tuning — underperforms RLCD across all tasks and scales (e.g., GPT-4 prefers RLCD7B over Context-Dist7B at 69.7% on harmlessness, 89.5% on helpfulness; Table 3). This is consistent with the broader RLHF literature showing that preference-based RL outperforms supervised fine-tuning on positive examples alone. The practical rule: **if you can generate both o⁺ and o⁻ (which requires constructing a negative prompt, the only additional step beyond what context distillation already does), the pairwise preference signal from RL provides substantial gains over supervised training on o⁺ alone, and RLCD's RL pipeline is identical to standard RLHF — there is no additional implementation complexity.** The one scenario where context distillation might be preferred is when constructing a meaningful p⁻ is genuinely difficult (e.g., for an attribute where the "opposite" is ill-defined or where encouraging the negative direction poses safety concerns). The paper doesn't explore this scenario, but it is a plausible practical constraint that a practitioner might encounter.
