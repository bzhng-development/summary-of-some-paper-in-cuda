# SimPoly: Simulation of Polymers with Machine Learning Force Fields Derived from First Principles

**ArXiv:** [2510.13696](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13696)

## 🎯 Pitch

A machine learning force field trained solely on quantum chemistry predicts polymer densities and glass transition temperatures from first principles for the first time, outperforming classical force fields and eliminating the need for experimental fitting. The model captures second-order phase transitions across a benchmark of 130 polymers, signaling that in silico design of polymeric materials without empirical data is now feasible.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Vivace**, a fast, local SE(3)-equivariant graph neural network machine learning force field (MLFF) tailored for large-scale polymer simulations, and demonstrates that it can predict experimental bulk properties—specifically densities and glass transition temperatures—for a broad range of polymers trained exclusively on *ab initio* quantum-chemical data, without fitting to any experimental measurements. Evaluating against the newly introduced **PolyArena** benchmark of 130 polymers, Vivace achieves a density mean absolute error of 0.04 g/cm³ across all successfully simulated polymers, outperforming the classical force fields PCFF (0.07 g/cm³) and OPLS3e (0.10 g/cm³), and captures second-order phase transitions to estimate glass transition temperatures with a mean absolute error of 43 K. The paper further establishes that test-time compute with a smaller model can substitute for a ~14× larger pretrained model only when problems fall within the base model's capability range—on easy-to-medium difficulty problems, the smaller model with compute-optimal inference strategies outperforms the larger model, while on the hardest problems, no amount of inference compute closes the gap, demonstrating that inference compute amplifies existing capability rather than creating it.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Cannot Accurately Simulate Polymers from First Principles

Polymers are everywhere—from the polyethylene in food packaging to the polyimides in spacecraft insulation—yet our ability to computationally design new polymeric materials is fundamentally limited by a modeling gap. The paper opens by framing this tension directly: polymers exhibit "complex, multi-scale" behavior spanning atomic-level bonding within individual monomer units to mesoscale chain entanglement and long-range intermolecular interactions that collectively determine macroscopic properties like density, stiffness, and thermal stability. Designing a new polymer *in silico*—predicting whether a candidate structure will be rigid or flexible, crystalline or amorphous, stable at high temperature or prone to degradation—requires a computational model that simultaneously captures angstrom-scale quantum mechanics and nanometer-scale chain dynamics.

This matters for two reasons. **Economically**, polymers are a multi-hundred-billion-dollar industry where new materials are traditionally discovered through slow, expensive trial-and-error synthesis and characterization. A reliable computational pipeline could dramatically accelerate this cycle. **Environmentally**, understanding polymer degradation, chemical recycling, and the fate of microplastics all require modeling bond-breaking chemistry that occurs under thermal, mechanical, and chemical stress—processes fundamentally inaccessible to traditional simulation methods. As the authors note, "the ubiquity of polymers presents a significant opportunity for impact through improved tools for their functional design and a deeper understanding of their environmental and health effects."

The paper identifies three essential requirements a computational model must satisfy to be practically useful for polymer design:

1. **Accuracy**: It must capture the quantum-mechanical interactions governing covalent bonds, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals dispersion, and electrostatic effects with sufficient fidelity to reproduce experimental observables.
2. **Transferability**: It must work across the enormous chemical diversity of synthetic polymers—polyolefins, polyesters, polyethers, polyacrylates, polyimides, siloxanes, fluorinated polymers, and copolymers—without requiring manual reparameterization for each new chemistry.
3. **Speed**: It must simulate systems of thousands to tens of thousands of atoms for nanoseconds to microseconds, because polymer properties like the glass transition temperature emerge from collective chain dynamics that only manifest at these scales.

No existing approach satisfies all three simultaneously, and the paper's central argument is that machine learning force fields represent the first technology with the potential to do so—*if* they can be validated against experimental reality rather than merely computational benchmarks.

### The Classical Force Field Bottleneck: Transferability

Classical force fields—such as PCFF, OPLS, CHARMM, DREIDING, and COMPASS—are the current workhorses of polymer simulation. They describe interatomic interactions through a set of parametrized energy terms: harmonic bond stretches, angle bends, torsional potentials, and pairwise Lennard-Jones and Coulomb terms for non-bonded interactions. This physical model is computationally cheap, enabling simulations of tens of thousands of atoms for microseconds, and when parameters are well-tuned for a specific chemical environment, the results can be quantitatively accurate.

The critical weakness, which the paper identifies as the motivating gap, is **transferability**. Classical force field parameters are fit to a combination of quantum-chemical calculations and experimental data—but this fitting is specific to the training conditions. A force field parametrized for proteins in aqueous solution (e.g., CHARMM) fails on molten polymers. A force field tuned for polycarbonates (the original motivation for PCFF) may give poor results for polyesters, even though both contain ester-like linkages. The paper states this bluntly:

> "While classical FFs are successful in specialized domains with limited chemical diversity (e.g., proteins in aqueous solution), the vast chemical space of synthetic polymers hinders their applicability due to a lack of transferability."

This is not a minor inconvenience—it is a fundamental limitation of the fixed-functional-form paradigm. The Lennard-Jones potential has two parameters per atom type (ε and σ). If a polymer contains new functional groups—say, a sulfone linkage or a fluorinated side chain—those parameters must be determined, typically by a human expert spending weeks or months iterating between computation and experiment. The process is, in the authors' words, "time-consuming."

Moreover, classical force fields cannot model chemical reactions because they rely on fixed bonding topologies: atoms are connected by pre-specified bonds that cannot break or form during simulation. This is disqualifying for many of the most important polymer problems—synthesis, degradation, recycling, cross-linking, and aging all involve chemical transformations. Reactive force fields like ReaxFF were developed to address this, but the paper notes they "often require laborious reparameterization" because their accuracy is "highly system-dependent." The result is that reactive simulations are feasible only for well-studied chemistries where parameters already exist, defeating the purpose of a general-purpose simulation tool.

### The Quantum-Chemical Alternative: Accuracy Without Transferability—But at Crippling Cost

At the other extreme, quantum-chemical methods—density functional theory (DFT) being the most widely used—require no system-specific parameters and can, in principle, describe any combination of atoms with transferable accuracy. Modern dispersion-corrected functionals like the r²SCAN-D3 method used in this work can capture the subtle van der Waals interactions that dominate polymer packing and cohesion.

However, DFT scales poorly with system size. Even with efficient implementations like CP2K (used in this paper), routine calculations are limited to hundreds of atoms and picoseconds of dynamics. The paper's polymer simulations require systems of ~2,000 atoms (for density) to ~10,000 atoms (for glass transition temperature) simulated for nanoseconds. A direct DFT simulation at these scales would require millions of core-hours per polymer—completely infeasible for the 130-polymer benchmark the paper targets, let alone for routine design work.

This creates the fundamental tension that motivates the entire field of machine learning force fields: **quantum mechanics provides the right physics but at the wrong computational cost; classical force fields provide the right cost but with the wrong physics for new chemistries**. An MLFF offers a third way—learn the quantum-mechanical potential energy surface from data, then evaluate it at a fraction of the cost.

### The MLFF Landscape: Why Existing Models Fall Short for Polymers

Machine learning force fields have exploded in capability over the past decade. The paper situates itself within a rich landscape of architectures: descriptor-based models like ANI and DeepMD, message-passing graph neural networks like SchNet and DimeNet++, equivariant models like NequIP, MACE, and Allegro, and so-called "universal" or "foundation" models like MACE-MP, MatterSim, and UMA. These models have demonstrated remarkable accuracy on quantum-chemical benchmarks—predicting energies and forces for small molecules, crystals, and catalyst surfaces with errors approaching the underlying DFT reference.

However, the paper identifies a specific and underexplored gap: **polymer systems are systematically different from the systems on which MLFFs are typically developed and benchmarked**. The differences are not merely in chemical composition but in the *types of interactions that matter*:

- **Intermolecular (inter-chain) interactions are as important as intramolecular ones.** In a small-molecule crystal, molecular packing is determined primarily by short-range repulsion and van der Waals attraction. In a polymer, chains are entangled, and the balance between intra-chain conformational preferences and inter-chain cohesion determines density, stiffness, and the glass transition. An MLFF trained predominantly on isolated molecules or perfect crystals may not learn the subtle interplay between these energy scales.

- **The relevant length scales are larger.** Van der Waals interactions between non-bonded atoms extend to 6–10 Å. In a dense polymer melt, each atom interacts with dozens of non-bonded neighbors in this range. MLFFs with short cutoffs (e.g., 4–5 Å) systematically miss these interactions, which the paper demonstrates catastrophically fails for certain polymers—PCTFE simulations with a cutoff below 5.5 Å cause chains to "unfold and repel each other, causing the system's density to collapse to near-zero values" (Section 3.2, Figure 6b).

- **Computational benchmarks do not predict simulation quality.** This is perhaps the paper's most subtle and important observation. Section 3.1 (Table 2) shows that Vivace's pre-trained model achieves comparable force and stress errors on the PolyPack and PolyDiss test sets to its fine-tuned counterpart and to MACE-OFF, yet its density predictions are an order of magnitude worse (MAE of 0.60 g/cm³ vs. 0.04 g/cm³). The paper states: "This observation highlights how standard computational metrics, such as force and stress errors, are not always good predictors of a model's ability to run stable MD simulations." The problem is that force errors average over all atom pairs, whereas density prediction depends critically on the small fraction of intermolecular contacts at the tail of the radial distribution function—precisely where the pre-trained model systematically underestimates attraction (Figure 5).

- **Experimental validation is rare.** The paper notes that "MLFF development has largely focused on crystalline and small-molecule benchmarks, leaving polymer systems relatively unexplored" and that "recent studies have shown that strong performance on computational benchmarks does not always translate to reliable predictions of experimental outcomes." The field has optimized for benchmark leaderboards rather than physical fidelity, and this paper explicitly aims to correct that.

Prior MLFF work on polymers has been limited. The paper cites Matsumura et al., who trained a system-specific MLFF to reproduce bulk properties of *individual* polymers, but notes that "the model's ability to describe multiple polymers simultaneously or generalize to new ones was not assessed." This is precisely the capability gap the paper addresses: building a single MLFF that works across the breadth of polymer chemistry without retraining.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning is carefully constructed along three axes:

**1. From computational benchmarks to experimental validation.** Rather than reporting force and energy errors on a held-out test set (the standard practice), the paper evaluates its MLFF by running full molecular dynamics simulations and comparing the resulting macroscopic properties—density and glass transition temperature—to experimental measurements. This is a significantly more stringent test because it requires the model to be not just locally accurate but globally stable under long-time dynamics, and it must correctly balance competing intra- and inter-molecular interactions that determine equilibrium density.

**2. A comprehensive experimental benchmark for polymers.** To enable this shift in evaluation, the paper introduces PolyArena, described as "a benchmark for evaluating MLFFs on experimentally measured polymer properties." With 130 polymers spanning main-group elements from H to Cl, molecular weights from 28 to 593 g/mol per repeating unit, densities from 0.8 to 2.0 g/cm³, and glass transition temperatures from 152 to 672 K, it represents the first large-scale experimental benchmarking dataset specifically designed for polymer MLFFs. The paper explicitly frames this as a complement to existing computational benchmarks (OC20, OC22, OMol25, OMC25), not a replacement—but it argues that computational benchmarks alone are insufficient.

**3. A fast, scalable architecture designed for polymer simulation requirements.** The Vivace architecture is not presented as a fundamentally new theoretical contribution but as a careful engineering solution to a specific set of constraints: it must be fast enough to simulate 10,000-atom systems for nanoseconds, it must capture interactions at the 6.5 Å range essential for polymer cohesion, and it must parallelize efficiently across multiple GPUs. The two architectural innovations—efficient SE(3)-equivariant operations using lightweight tensor products and a multi-cutoff strategy that applies expensive equivariant operations only at short range—are explicitly motivated by the polymer simulation use case rather than by abstract benchmark performance.

The paper also introduces PolyData, a set of training datasets (PolyPack, PolyDiss, PolyCrop) specifically designed to teach MLFFs the interactions that matter for polymers. PolyPack (multiple chains packed at various densities) probes intra-chain interactions; PolyDiss (single chains in expanding periodic cells) probes inter-chain dissociation energetics; PolyCrop (non-periodic cluster fragments) provides diverse local chemical environments. This is not a generic dataset—it is designed to address the specific failure modes the authors identified in preliminary experiments.

### What This Paper Is and Is Not Claiming

The paper makes a carefully bounded claim: that an MLFF trained exclusively on first-principles quantum-chemical data can predict experimental bulk properties for a broad range of polymers **within the accuracy needed to be competitive with or superior to established classical force fields**. It does *not* claim to have solved long-range electrostatics (explicitly flagged as future work in Section 4). It does *not* claim to match the simulation speed of classical force fields (MLFFs remain 10–100× slower). It does *not* claim that all 130 polymers in PolyArena were simulated successfully—failures due to numerical instabilities or model incompatibilities are reported transparently throughout.

What it *does* claim is more significant for the field: that the path forward—from quantum chemistry to experimental property prediction without experimental fitting—is now open, and that the remaining challenges (long-range interactions, computational efficiency, broader property prediction) are engineering problems rather than fundamental barriers. The Vivace architecture, PolyArena benchmark, and PolyData dataset are offered as infrastructure to accelerate progress along this path.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper develops three interconnected artifacts: **Vivace**, an SE(3)-equivariant graph neural network force field trained exclusively on quantum-chemical data to drive molecular dynamics simulations of polymers, plus two datasets—**PolyArena** (an experimental benchmark) and **PolyData** (a training dataset)—that together enable systematic evaluation of MLFFs against experimentally measured bulk properties. The problem the system solves is this: classical force fields lack transferability across the vast chemical space of synthetic polymers, while quantum-chemical methods are computationally prohibitive for the system sizes and timescales required; the solution is a carefully engineered MLFF that captures both the short-range quantum-mechanical interactions and the mid-range van der Waals forces essential for polymer behavior, trained on purpose-built datasets that teach it the specific interplay of intra-chain and inter-chain interactions that govern macroscopic observables.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The overall pipeline has five major components organized into two phases—**training** and **inference/evaluation**:

**Training Phase:**
1. **Quantum-chemical data generation (PolyData):** Three complementary datasets—**PolyPack** (periodic cells with multiple polymer chains at various densities, probing intra-molecular interactions), **PolyDiss** (single polymer chains in expanding periodic cells, probing inter-molecular dissociation), and **PolyCrop** (non-periodic cluster fragments, providing diverse local chemical environments)—are constructed and labeled with DFT or semi-empirical methods. These define the energy, forces, and stress tensors that the MLFF must learn to reproduce.
2. **Vivace model architecture:** A local SE(3)-equivariant graph neural network with a multi-cutoff strategy processes each atom's local environment independently, producing a total energy that is differentiable with respect to atomic positions (yielding forces) and lattice strain (yielding stress). The architecture separates computationally expensive equivariant tensor-product operations (restricted to short-range interactions < 3.8 Å) from efficient invariant attention operations (used for mid-range interactions up to 6.5 Å).
3. **Training procedure:** The model is pre-trained on non-periodic OMol25 and PolyCrop data, then fine-tuned with high weight on the periodic PolyPack and PolyDiss datasets to learn the inter-chain interactions critical for bulk-phase simulations.

**Inference/Evaluation Phase:**
4. **Molecular dynamics simulation:** Using Vivace to predict energies and forces, classical MD engines (LAMMPS or i-PI) simulate polymer model systems in the isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble following a 21-step equilibration protocol. The simulation naturally evolves the polymer packing to its equilibrium density at standard conditions.
5. **Benchmarking (PolyArena):** The simulated densities and glass transition temperatures are compared against experimental measurements for 130 polymers from the Bicerano handbook, with MAE reported across all successfully simulated systems and separately for polymers seen versus unseen during training.

Information flows as follows: quantum-chemical reference calculations → training data (energies, forces, stresses) → Vivace model training → MD simulation with Vivace → computed density and Tg → comparison with experimental values from PolyArena. The critical loop is that PolyData is specifically designed to teach the model the interactions that PolyArena evaluates—they are paired, not independent artifacts.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the PolyData training datasets, because the entire approach depends on having the *right* training data that captures the specific interactions missing from generic MLFF training sets. Understanding what PolyPack, PolyDiss, and PolyCrop each contribute—and why they are complementary—is foundational.
- **Second**, the Vivace architecture in detail, because it represents the core technical innovation. We walk through the local environment construction, the mid-range invariant interaction block, the short-range equivariant interaction block with its simplified tensor product, and the output layer—explaining at each step *why* these design choices are made for polymer simulation.
- **Third**, the training procedure, including pre-training on non-periodic data, fine-tuning on periodic data, the multi-head energy prediction for different QM methods, and the hyperparameter optimization process that selected the final architecture.
- **Fourth**, the MD simulation protocol that converts a trained MLFF into bulk property predictions, explaining the 21-step compression protocol, the ensemble choices, and the measurement process.
- **Fifth**, the glass transition temperature extraction methodology, which involves simulating densities at 17 temperatures and fitting hyperbolic functions with bootstrap uncertainty quantification—a non-trivial post-processing step that turns raw simulation data into the Tg estimates reported in the paper.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **engineering/validation paper** whose core idea is that an MLFF trained exclusively on *ab initio* quantum-chemical data can predict experimental bulk polymer properties, provided the training data is specifically designed to capture the interplay of intra- and inter-molecular interactions that matter for polymers, and the architecture is engineered for the computational demands (large cutoff, many-GPU scaling, nanosecond-timescale dynamics) of polymer simulation.

#### The PolyData Training Datasets: Purpose-Built for Polymer Interactions

The paper introduces PolyData—a collection of three complementary datasets—specifically because existing MLFF training sets (OMol25, SPICE, OC20) are "not necessarily well-suited for capturing the complex interplay of interactions governing polymer behavior" (Section 2.1). Standard small-molecule datasets are dominated by isolated molecules in vacuum, while crystalline datasets probe periodic packing of rigid structures. Neither teaches an MLFF about the entanglement, conformational flexibility, and inter-chain cohesion characteristic of amorphous polymers. PolyData is the paper's answer to this gap, and understanding each sub-dataset's design rationale is essential to understanding why the approach works.

**PolyPack: Probing Intra-Molecular Interactions Under Polymer-Like Conditions**

PolyPack contains approximately 230,000 configurations covering all polymers in the PolyArena benchmark. The generation procedure is deliberately designed to expose the model to polymer chains in condensed-phase environments with structural diversity:

1. For each polymer, initial structures are generated using the Enhanced Monte Carlo (EMC) program, containing two polymer chains in a periodic cell. This is a crucial choice: two chains means both intra-chain and inter-chain atom pairs are represented, but the dominant energy contributions come from within-chain covalent and non-covalent interactions because chains are initially placed at relatively low packing densities.

2. Three chain sizes are sampled—50, 100, and 250 atoms—and initial densities are sampled broadly from 0.1 to 1.4 times the experimental density. The wide density range ensures the model sees both compressed configurations (where repulsive interactions dominate) and expanded configurations (where chains are separated and attractive interactions are weak). This teaches the model the full shape of the inter-chain potential energy surface, not just the equilibrium region.

3. Each initial structure is subjected to a short 50 ps NVT simulation at 1200 K using PCFF as the driver. The high temperature is chosen to accelerate structural exploration—chains can adopt diverse conformations that would be kinetically trapped at lower temperatures. The final frame from this short trajectory is then perturbed by adding Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 0.08 Å to all atomic coordinates.

4. From each perturbed configuration, eight geometry optimization steps are performed (likely using the CP2K DFT method), storing energies, forces, and stress tensors at every step. The perturbation-plus-optimization protocol is a standard strategy in MLFF dataset construction: it generates configurations slightly off the equilibrium manifold, which teaches the model how to relax forces back toward equilibrium—essential for stable MD simulations where the model will frequently encounter non-equilibrium geometries.

The stress tensor is recorded because it is the quantum-mechanical observable conjugate to lattice strain—the derivative of energy with respect to strain. During NPT simulations, the stress tensor is the quantity that drives volume changes; an MLFF that accurately reproduces DFT stresses will correctly expand or contract the simulation cell until the system reaches mechanical equilibrium. This is why PolyPack includes stress labels rather than just energy and forces.

**PolyDiss: Probing Inter-Molecular Interactions Systematically**

PolyDiss comprises approximately 50,000 configurations and is arguably the most innovative dataset. Its purpose is to teach the model the *distance dependence* of inter-chain interactions—how the non-bonded potential between two polymer segments varies as they are pulled apart. This is the information that determines whether the model will correctly predict polymer density, because density is set by the balance between the attractive van der Waals well (which pulls chains together) and the steric repulsion at short range (which prevents collapse).

The generation procedure works as follows:

1. For each polymer, a short chain of three repeating units is built with EMC and placed in an orthorhombic periodic cell, aligned with the x-axis. The cell is initially shrunk until all inter-image atomic distances exceed 1.5 Å—this is a "just barely not overlapping" starting configuration.

2. The cell is incrementally enlarged along specified directions (y, z, yz, and xyz) in steps of 0.1 Å until the minimum inter-image separation reaches 7.0 Å. At each step, a single-point calculation is performed, recording energy, forces, and stress.

3. The result for each polymer is a **dissociation curve**: the energy as a function of the minimum inter-chain distance. Figure 5 in the paper shows examples of these curves—they exhibit a characteristic van der Waals shape: an attractive well at intermediate separations (typically 2.5–4.5 Å) that smoothly approaches zero at large separations (>6 Å).

The "offset" distance referred to throughout the paper (e.g., Figure 5 caption: "the distance (offset) between polymer chains") is the minimal interatomic distance between the polymer chain and its periodic image. As the cell expands, this offset increases, and the interaction energy decreases.

**Why this matters operationally:** Section 3.1 (Table 2, Figure 5) demonstrates that the pre-trained Vivace model systematically underestimates the depth of these inter-chain dissociation curves. The fine-tuned model, which was trained on PolyDiss, more closely follows the DFT reference, and this correction is what enables qualitatively correct density predictions. The paper's key finding—that "a qualitatively accurate description of intermolecular interactions is a necessary but not sufficient condition for reliable simulations" (Section 3.1)—is rooted in this dataset.

**PolyCrop: Non-Periodic Structural Diversity**

PolyCrop contains approximately 800,000 non-periodic cluster configurations and serves a different purpose. While PolyPack and PolyDiss teach the model about condensed-phase interactions, PolyCrop teaches it about the diverse local chemical environments that occur in polymers, covering the same breadth of functional groups, bonding patterns, and steric arrangements. The generation procedure mirrors PolyPack's but with a crucial final step:

1. Large periodic structures are generated using a similar EMC + PCFF NVT protocol as PolyPack.
2. These structures are "cropped" into non-periodic spherical clusters: a random atom is selected as center, and all atoms within a given radius are retained.
3. During cropping, only C–C single bonds that cross the sphere's boundary are cleaved. Atoms connected by other bond types (double bonds, heteroatom bonds) are kept even if outside the radius, to preserve the local chemical environment. All cleaved bonds are capped with hydrogen atoms.

The cropping process can produce highly energetic configurations—atoms that were stabilized by their periodic neighbors may now be under-coordinated. This is actually desirable: it teaches the model to handle the types of strained geometries that arise during MD simulations when polymer chains adopt non-equilibrium conformations.

PolyCrop is labeled with two different QM methods depending on size: clusters up to 234 atoms are labeled with DFT at the ωB97X-V/def2-TZVPP level in ORCA (approximately 400,000 configurations), while larger clusters up to 1,411 atoms are labeled with the semi-empirical GFN2-xTB tight-binding method (another 400,000 configurations). Using a cheaper method for larger structures is a pragmatic compromise—DFT would be computationally prohibitive for 1,000-atom clusters, and the xTB method captures the qualitative physics well enough to be useful training data, especially since these large clusters provide information about long-range steric interactions that the ORCA-labeled data cannot access due to size limitations.

**Quantum-chemical labeling details for PolyPack and PolyDiss.** Both periodic datasets are labeled using CP2K/QUICKSTEP with the r²SCAN meta-GGA density functional, D3 dispersion correction with Becke-Johnson damping, TZV2P Gaussian basis sets, GTH norm-conserving pseudopotentials, and a 1200 Ry plane-wave cutoff for the auxiliary basis. The choice of r²SCAN is notable: meta-GGA functionals are more accurate than GGA functionals like PBE for the types of non-covalent interactions that dominate polymer packing, and the D3 correction is essential for capturing long-range van der Waals dispersion. The multiple QM methods used across PolyData (ωB97X-V in ORCA for PolyCrop-small, GFN2-xTB for PolyCrop-large, r²SCAN-D3 in CP2K for PolyPack/PolyDiss) mean that Vivace must learn to predict different energy scales—this is handled via the multi-head training procedure described in the training section.

#### The Vivace Architecture: Local SE(3)-Equivariant GNN with Multi-Cutoff Strategy

Vivace is built on the **local model** paradigm established by Allegro, where each atom's interactions are restricted to its immediate local environment (atoms within a cutoff radius), and information does not propagate between atoms via message passing. This means the receptive field of each atom is exactly its cutoff radius—it does not grow with additional message-passing layers. The paper explicitly contrasts this with message-passing models (Section B.3, Figure S4): in a message-passing model, after `n` layers, an atom can "see" atoms up to `n` hops away, whereas in a local model, all layers operate on the same fixed neighborhood.

This architectural choice has a specific, practical motivation for polymer simulation: **multi-GPU parallelization without inter-GPU communication**. In a message-passing model, if the domain decomposition places atoms `i` and `j` on different GPUs and `j` is in `i`'s neighborhood, the two GPUs must exchange data during each message-passing layer—a synchronization bottleneck that limits scaling. In a local model, all information needed to update atom `i` is contained within its local environment, which is guaranteed to reside on a single GPU if the domain decomposition includes a halo region larger than the cutoff radius. The paper reports strong scaling: Vivace achieves 1.18 ns/d on 8 GPUs vs. 0.30 ns/d on 1 GPU for a 15,552-atom system (Table S5), a near-4× speedup for 8× the GPUs.

**Local Environment Construction**

For each atom `i`, two local environments are constructed:

- **Short-range environment** `$N^S(i)$`: all atoms `j` within a cutoff `$r_c^S = 3.8 \text{ Å}$`. This is encoded using directed edges `i ← j`, meaning the information flows from neighbor `j` (sender) to center `i` (receiver).

- **Mid-range environment** `$N^M(i)$`: all atoms `j` within a cutoff `$r_c^M = 6.5 \text{ Å}$`. This is the larger neighborhood, but only cheaper invariant operations are applied to edges in this environment.

The critical design decision is that the two environments are **concentric but non-overlapping in computational treatment**. The short-range environment (3.8 Å) is where expensive equivariant tensor products operate; the mid-range environment (3.8–6.5 Å) uses only efficient invariant attention-like operations. This is the **multi-cutoff strategy** that the paper claims allows Vivace to "achieve the accuracy of a large cutoff with the efficiency of a smaller one" (Section 2.2).

A third distance threshold—the modification cutoff `r_mod = 1.5 Å`—is used for a short-range energy correction term, discussed in the output layer section.

**The Smooth Envelope Function**

All interactions are modulated by a smooth envelope function `$\eta_c(r/r_{\text{cut}})$` that ensures energy contributions go continuously to zero at the cutoff radius. The paper uses a smooth step function rather than the more common cosine cutoff:

For `x = r / r_{\text{cut}}`:

$$\eta_c(x) = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } 0 \leq x \leq 1 - \delta \\
3(1 - x)^2 - 2(1 - x)^3 & \text{if } 1 - \delta < x < 1 \\
0 & \text{if } x \geq 1
\end{cases}$$

where `$\delta$` is a small parameter controlling the smoothing region.

**What it computes:** a smooth transition from full contribution (1.0) to zero contribution over the interval `$(1 - \delta, 1)$`, using a cubic spline that has continuous first and second derivatives at both boundaries. This ensures forces—which involve derivatives of the energy with respect to positions—are continuous at the cutoff, preventing simulation instabilities.

**Why this form:** the authors note that "the cosine function can introduce a strong inductive bias and prevent the model from learning interactions at larger distances" (Section B.2.1). A cosine cutoff goes from 1 to 0 over the *entire* range; for a large cutoff of 6.5 Å, this would damp long-range interactions well before the physical cutoff, preventing the model from learning to represent interactions in the 5–6.5 Å range. The smooth step function preserves the full interaction strength up to `$1 - \delta$`, then rapidly but smoothly transitions to zero.

#### Initial Embedding Layer: Encoding Species, Distances, and Geometry

**Atomic species encoding.** Each atom `i` is encoded to an initial invariant feature vector `$a_i \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{feat}}}$` via one-hot encoding of its atomic number followed by a learned linear transformation:

$$a_i^k = \mathbf{W}^k \cdot \text{OneHot}(Z_i)$$

where `$Z_i$` is the atomic number/type of atom `i`, `$\mathbf{W}^k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{feat}} \times n_{\text{species}}}$` is a learned weight matrix with a row for each feature dimension, `$\text{OneHot}(Z_i)$` is a one-hot vector of length `n_species`, and `$k$` indexes the feature dimension. This produces a dense, learned embedding of the atomic species for use in all subsequent interactions. The initial invariant node feature `$h_i^{(0)}$` is simply set to this encoding: ``$h_i^{(0)} = a_i$``.

**Radial basis encoding of distances.** For each edge `(i, j)` with distance `$r_{ij}$`, the distance is encoded into a radial basis vector `$b_{ij} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{basis}}}$` using exponential radial basis functions:

$$b_{ij}^k = B_k(r_{ij}) = \exp\left[-\frac{n_{\text{basis}}^2}{4(1 - e^{-r_c})^2}\left(e^{-r_{ij}} - \mu_k\right)^2\right]$$

where `$\mu_k = \frac{k}{n_{\text{basis}}} + (1 - \frac{k}{n_{\text{basis}}}) e^{-r_c}$` is the mean of the `k`-th basis function, and `$r_c$` is the applicable cutoff radius (3.8 Å for short-range, 6.5 Å for mid-range).

**What it computes:** each component `$b_{ij}^k$` is a Gaussian centered at `$\mu_k$` in the transformed coordinate `$e^{-r_{ij}}$`. The transformation `$e^{-r_{ij}}$` compresses the distance range—distances near 0 map to values near 1, distances near the cutoff map to `$e^{-r_c}$`, and infinite distance maps to 0. The Gaussians tile this compressed range uniformly via the `$\mu_k$` formula. The result is a vector of `$n_{\text{basis}}$` numbers where the combination of which Gaussians are activated encodes the distance with high resolution at short range (where interactions change rapidly) and lower resolution at long range (where interactions vary slowly).

**Why this form:** exponential basis functions on `$e^{-r}$` are a standard architecture in MLFFs (used in PhysNet and TensorNet, both cited by the authors). The key property is that they naturally provide higher radial resolution at small distances—where covalent bonds and strong repulsion vary sharply with sub-angstrom changes—while smoothly broadening at large distances—where van der Waals interactions vary over angstroms. A naive Fourier or RBF embedding on `$r$` would not provide this adaptive resolution.

**Edge type encoding.** Each edge `(i, j)` also receives a type encoding that captures the chemical identity of the atom pair:

$$t_{ij} = \text{MLP}(a_i \mathbin\Vert a_j)$$

where `$\mathbin\Vert$` denotes concatenation of the species embeddings of the sender and receiver, and MLP is a multi-layer perceptron. This encoding is used exclusively in the mid-range invariant interaction block (Equation S12) and provides a mechanism for the model to learn pair-specific interaction parameters (e.g., C–H vs. C–O vs. O–H interactions should have different strengths) without explicit atom typing.

**Edge length embedding.** The radial basis is transformed into a per-edge length embedding:

$$\rho_{ij}^{S,M} = \text{MLP}\left(b_{ij}^{S,M}\right) \eta_c\left(\frac{r_{ij}}{r_c^{S,M}}\right)$$

where the superscript `S` or `M` indicates whether the short-range or mid-range basis and cutoff are used. The MLP transforms the radial basis into a learned feature vector of dimension `$n_{\text{feat}}$`, and the envelope function ensures that contributions smoothly vanish at the cutoff.

**Initial edge invariant feature.** The edge invariant feature vector is initialized by combining distance and species information:

$$e_{ij}^{(0)} = \text{MLP}\left(b_{ij}^M \mathbin\Vert a_i \mathbin\Vert a_j\right) \eta_c\left(\frac{r_{ij}}{r_c^M}\right)$$

This is computed once and then updated in each interaction block.

**Initial equivariant node features.** Equivariant features capture directional information (how the energy depends on the angular arrangement of atoms) and transform according to the rules of three-dimensional rotations—this is what "SE(3)-equivariant" means: if you rotate the entire molecule, the equivariant features rotate correspondingly. The initial equivariant features are computed from the spherical harmonics of the edge directions:

$$\xi_i^{(0), l m} = \sum_{j \in N^S(i)} w_{ij}^{(0), l} Y_{ij}^{l m}$$

where:

- `$Y_{ij}^{l m}$` is the real spherical harmonic of order `$l$` and degree `$m$` evaluated at the normalized direction vector `$\hat{r}_{ij} = r_{ij} / |r_{ij}|$`. Spherical harmonics are the angular basis functions that generalize s, p, d, f orbitals to arbitrary order: `$l=0$` is a sphere (no angular dependence), `$l=1$` captures dipole-like directional patterns, `$l=2$` captures quadrupole patterns, and so on. The paper restricts `$l$` to a maximum `$L_{\text{max}}$` (set to 2 for the final model, Table S4).

- The sum runs over all neighbors in the **short-range** neighborhood `$N^S(i)$` only. Equivariant features are computationally expensive, so they are restricted to the smaller 3.8 Å cutoff.

- `$w_{ij}^{(0), l} = \text{MLP}\left(b_{ij}^S \mathbin\Vert h_i^{(0)} \mathbin\Vert h_j^{(0)}\right) \eta_c(r_{ij}/r_c^S)$` is a learned weight that depends on the interatomic distance and the species of both atoms. This means the model can learn which atom pairs contribute strongly to directional interactions and which do not.

**What it computes:** for each atom `i`, a set of equivariant feature vectors `$\xi_i^{l m}$` indexed by angular momentum `$l$` (from 0 to `$L_{\text{max}}$`) and magnetic quantum number `$m$` (from `$-l$` to `$l$`). For `$L_{\text{max}} = 2$`, this gives `$1 + 3 + 5 = 9$` components per channel. Each `$\xi_i^{l m}$` can be thought of as a learned, rotation-equivariant representation of the local directional environment—it encodes "what pattern of atoms surrounds atom `i` in each angular direction" in a way that transforms correctly under rotation.

The feature dimension `$n_{\text{channel}}$` specifies how many independent scalar channels are used—the full equivariant features are `$\tilde{\xi}_i^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{channel}} \times (L_{\text{max}}+1)^2}$`. For model 4 in Table S4, `$n_{\text{channel}} = 32$` and `$L_{\text{max}} = 2$`, giving 32 channels × 9 components = 288 numbers per atom.

**Why initial equivariant features are restricted to short range:** the tensor products that will later combine these features are the computational bottleneck. Restricting the initial equivariant embedding to the short-range neighborhood (3.8 Å) ensures that the number of edges contributing to the sum is manageable. The mid-range neighborhood (3.8–6.5 Å) contains more edges—this is where the cubic scaling of neighbors with cutoff radius matters—so avoiding equivariant operations on these edges is the key efficiency gain of the multi-cutoff strategy.

#### Mid-Range Invariant Interaction: Efficient Attention for 3.8–6.5 Å Interactions

This block updates both edge invariant features `$e_{ij}$` and node invariant features `$h_i$` using only scalar (rotation-invariant) quantities. The operations are designed to be computationally cheap while capturing the distance- and species-dependent interactions that dominate in the 3.8–6.5 Å range—primarily van der Waals dispersion.

**Edge feature update:**

The edge feature for edge `(i, j)` is updated as:

$$e_{ij}^{(n+1)} = e_{ij}^{(n)} + \text{MLP}(e_{ij}^{(n)}) \odot \text{MLP}(t_{ij})$$

where `$\odot$` denotes element-wise multiplication. **What it computes:** a residual update to the edge feature that depends both on the current edge feature (which encodes the distance and previous interaction history) and on the edge type encoding `$t_{ij}$` (which encodes the species pair). The element-wise product means the model can learn that certain species pairs amplify or suppress specific feature channels.

**Node feature update:**

The node feature update is a local attention mechanism:

$$h_i^{(n+1)} = h_i^{(n)} + \text{MLP}\left[\text{LayerNorm}\left(h_i^{(n)} + \Delta h_i\right)\right]$$

where:

$$\Delta h_i = \text{Linear}\left(\sum_{j \in N^M(i)} \alpha_{ij} v_{ij} \eta_c\left(\frac{r_{ij}}{r_c^M}\right)\right)$$

with intermediate quantities:

$$\rho_{ij} = \text{MLP}(\rho_{ij}^M)$$

$$q_i = \text{Linear}(h_i)$$

$$k_{ij} = \text{Linear}(e_{ij}^{(n+1)}) + \rho_{ij}$$

$$v_{ij} = \text{Linear}(e_{ij}^{(n+1)}) + \rho_{ij}$$

$$\alpha_{ij} = \exp\left(-\frac{\lVert q_i - k_{ij} \rVert_2^2}{2\sqrt{d}}\right) \eta_c\left(\frac{r_{ij}}{r_c^M}\right)$$

**What it computes:** a weighted sum over neighbors in the mid-range environment, where the weight `$\alpha_{ij}$` measures the similarity between the query vector `$q_i$` (derived from the central atom's node feature `$h_i$`) and the key vector `$k_{ij}$` (derived from the edge feature `$e_{ij}$` and the distance-dependent edge length embedding `$\rho_{ij}$`). The exponential-of-negative-squared-distance kernel replaces the more common softmax-over-dot-product attention. The value vector `$v_{ij}$` (derived from the same edge feature) is aggregated, transformed by a linear layer, and added as a residual to the node feature.

**Why this is "local" rather than "message-passing":** the query `$q_i$` depends only on `$h_i$`, not on any neighbor's node feature `$h_j$`. This means atom `i`'s update at layer `n+1` does not incorporate information about atom `j`'s *neighbors*—it only uses `$e_{ij}$`, which encodes the direct edge between `i` and `j`. This is the fundamental difference from message passing, where `$h_j$` (which itself aggregates information from `j`'s neighbors) would feed into the update. The paper's Figure S4 illustrates this contrast: in a local model, information never travels beyond one hop from the central atom.

**Why the Gaussian distance kernel rather than softmax or dot product:** the paper states that "the Gaussian distance kernel used in Eq. S19 and softmax were found to yield the best performance" (Section B.3, after Equation S25). The alternatives considered were: (1) SiLU activation on the dot product (used in TorchMD-Net), and (2) an MLP on the concatenated features (used in many message-passing models). The Gaussian kernel has the property that attention weights are naturally bounded in `$[0, 1]$` without requiring normalization over all neighbors—each neighbor's weight depends only on its own similarity to the query, not on the relative similarities of other neighbors. This means the feature dimension `$d$` controls the "sharpness" of the attention without requiring neighbor-count-dependent normalization.

#### Short-Range Equivariant Interaction: Capturing Directional Bonding

This is the computationally intensive block that updates the equivariant node features `$\tilde{\xi}_i$` and the invariant edge features `$e_{ij}$`. It operates only on the short-range environment `$N^S(i)$` (cutoff 3.8 Å) and uses tensor products that preserve equivariance under rotation.

**Equivariant node feature update:**

$$\tilde{\xi}_i^{(n+1)} = \tilde{\xi}_i^{(n)} + \Delta\tilde{\xi}_i$$

$$\Delta\tilde{\xi}_i = \frac{1}{\text{Norm}} \left[ \tilde{\xi}_i^{(n)} \otimes \left( \sum_{j \in N^S(i)} w_{ij} \tilde{Y}_{ij} \right) \right]$$

where:

- `$\otimes$` is the simplified tensor product operation (detailed below)
- `$\tilde{Y}_{ij}$` is the vector of spherical harmonics for the edge direction `$\hat{r}_{ij}$`, with components for all `$l = 0, 1, \dots, L_{\text{max}}$` and corresponding `$m$` values
- `$w_{ij}^{l p} = \text{Linear}^{l p}(\alpha_{ij} v_{ij})$` is a learnable weight vector for each angular momentum channel `$(l, p)$` (where `$p$` is the parity), derived from the attention weight and value from the invariant attention mechanism
- `$\text{Norm} = \text{Mean}_{\text{channels}}\left(\sum_{l,m} (\Delta\tilde{\xi}_i^{l m})^2\right) + 10^{-5}$` is a per-atom normalization factor that stabilizes training by preventing the equivariant features from growing unbounded

The attention mechanism for computing `$\alpha_{ij}$` uses the same Gaussian distance kernel as the mid-range block, but operates on the short-range environment and uses the short-range edge embedding `$\rho_{ij}^S$`:

$$q_i = \text{Linear}(h_i)$$
$$k_{ij} = \text{Linear}(e_{ij})$$
$$v_{ij} = \text{Linear}(e_{ij})$$
$$\alpha_{ij} = \exp\left(-\frac{\lVert q_i - k_{ij} \rVert_2^2}{2\sqrt{d}}\right) \eta_c\left(\frac{r_{ij}}{r_c^S}\right)$$

**What it computes:** a cross-attention where invariant features (the query `$q_i$` from `$h_i$`, the key `$k_{ij}$` and value `$v_{ij}$` from `$e_{ij}$`) determine attention weights `$\alpha_{ij}$` that are then used to weight equivariant quantities—the spherical harmonics `$\tilde{Y}_{ij}$`. The weighted spherical harmonics are summed over neighbors and then combined with the current equivariant features `$\tilde{\xi}_i^{(n)}$` via tensor product to produce the update `$\Delta\tilde{\xi}_i$`.

**Why normalisation is necessary:** tensor products between high-order tensors can grow in magnitude exponentially as information propagates through layers. The normalization factor—which averages the squared magnitude of `$\Delta\tilde{\xi}_i$` across all `$l, m$` components and all channels, then divides—acts as a per-atom, per-layer magnitude control. This is a simpler alternative to the more common approach of using Clebsch-Gordan coefficients with careful normalization in the tensor product itself.

**Edge feature update from equivariant information:**

The key innovation that makes Vivace's three-body interaction efficient is the **inner product operation**:

$$\Delta e_{ij} = \text{Linear}\left( \Delta\tilde{\xi}_i \cdot \tilde{Y}_{ij} \right) \odot \text{MLP}(\rho_{ij})$$

where `$\Delta\tilde{\xi}_i \cdot \tilde{Y}_{ij}$` is the dot product (inner product) between the equivariant update and the spherical harmonics of the edge direction, producing an invariant scalar for each channel.

**What it computes (three-body interpretation):** substituting the expression for `$\Delta\tilde{\xi}_i$` reveals why this is a three-body interaction. The full expression becomes (up to normalization):

$$\Delta e_{ij} \propto \left[ \left( \tilde{\xi}_i^{(n)} \otimes \sum_{k \in N^S(i)} w_{ik} \tilde{Y}_{ik} \right) \cdot \tilde{Y}_{ij} \right] \odot \text{MLP}(\rho_{ij})$$

This can be rearranged (as shown in Equation S38) to:

$$\Delta e_{ij} \propto \sum_{k \in N^S(i)} \left[ \left( \left( \tilde{\xi}_i^{(n)} \otimes (w_{ik} \tilde{Y}_{ik}) \right) \cdot \tilde{Y}_{ij} \right) \odot \text{MLP}(\rho_{ij}) \right]$$

Each term in the sum depends on three atoms: the central atom `i`, neighbor `j` (whose edge feature is being updated), and neighbor `k` (which also contributes to the equivariant sum). This is a three-body term—the interaction between `i` and `j` is modulated by the presence of `k` at a particular angular position. Three-body interactions are essential for capturing angular bonding preferences (e.g., tetrahedral vs. planar geometries) that two-body pair potentials cannot represent.

**Why this has `$O(M)$` complexity rather than `$O(M^2)$`:** the critical insight is that the sum over `k` occurs *inside* the tensor product—it is a sum over the *uncontracted* equivariant features, not a sum over the already-contracted edge updates. This means the expensive tensor product is computed once per central atom (summing over neighbors), and then the inner product with `$\tilde{Y}_{ij}$` is computed once per edge `(i, j)`. The total cost scales as `$O(M)$` per atom, where `$M$` is the number of neighbors in the short-range environment. In contrast, a naïve three-body implementation would compute the tensor product separately for each pair `$(j, k)$` of neighbors, scaling as `$O(M^2)$`.

**The simplified tensor product:**

The full tensor product between two equivariant tensors `$\tilde{a}$` and `$\tilde{b}$` in irreducible representation couples angular momentum channels:

$$\tilde{c}^{l_3 m_3} = \sum_{l_1, l_2} \sum_{m_1, m_2} C_{(l_1 m_1)(l_2 m_2)}^{(l_3 m_3)} \tilde{a}^{l_1 m_1} \tilde{b}^{l_2 m_2}$$

where `$C$` are Clebsch-Gordan coefficients. In the full version, there are also learnable weights `$w_{n_1 n_2 n_3}^{l_3 \leftarrow l_1 l_2}$` that mix channels, making the operation `$O(n_{\text{channel}}^3 \cdot L_{\text{max}}^3)$`.

Vivace uses a **factorized** version where:

$$w_{n_1 n_2 n_3}^{l_3 \leftarrow l_1 l_2} = U_{n_1}^{l_1} \cdot V_{n_2}^{l_2} \cdot Q_{n_3}^{l_3}$$

This factorization reduces the complexity to `$O(n_{\text{channel}} \cdot L_{\text{max}})$` because the channel mixing is now separate for the two input tensors and the output tensor. The `$U$`, `$V$`, and `$Q$` matrices can be absorbed into the definitions of `$\tilde{a}$`, `$\tilde{b}$`, and `$\tilde{c}$` through linear transformations applied before or after the tensor product.

Furthermore, Vivace uses a **"uuu"** (unweighted) tensor product where only same-channel interactions are allowed (`$n_1 = n_2 = n_3$` is assumed):

$$c_{n l_3 m_3} = \sum_{l_3 \leftarrow l_1 l_2} \sum_{m_1, m_2} C_{(l_1 m_1)(l_2 m_2)}^{(l_3 m_3)} a_{n l_1 m_1} b_{n l_2 m_2}$$

**What this sacrifices and gains:** the simplified tensor product has less expressive power than the full version—it cannot learn arbitrary channel mixing patterns or complex angular momentum couplings. However, the paper argues that this expressivity is not essential for achieving high accuracy (Section 2.2: "We find that learnable parameters within these operations are not essential for achieving high accuracy"), and the computational savings—`$O(n_{\text{channel}})$` instead of `$O(n_{\text{channel}}^3)$`—are substantial. The final model (Table S4, model 4) uses `$L_{\text{max}} = 2$` with 32 equivariant channels, meaning the simplified tensor product saves a factor of `$32^2 = 1024$` in channel-mixing parameters while retaining the crucial ability to couple `s`-`p`-`d` angular momentum channels.

**Comparison with Allegro:** Allegro performs tensor products on *edge* features—each edge `(i, j)` gets its own equivariant representation that is updated via tensor product with the neighborhood sum. This means the tensor product is evaluated `$O(n_{\text{edge}})$` times per layer, compared to `$O(n_{\text{atom}})$` times in Vivace where it updates node features. Since the number of edges is `$n_{\text{atom}} \times M$` (where `$M$` is the average number of neighbors), Vivace's approach is approximately `$M$` times cheaper in the tensor product—a significant saving since `$M$` can be 30–50 for a dense polymer with a 3.8 Å cutoff.

#### Output Layer: From Features to Energy, Forces, and Stress

The total energy is a sum of atomic, edge, and short-range correction contributions:

$$E = \sum_i E_i^{\text{node}} + \sum_{i,j} \left(E_{ij}^{\text{edge}} + E_{ij}^{\text{mod}}\right) + \sum_i E^{Z_i}$$

**Node energy:**

$$E_i^{\text{node}} = \text{MLP}(h_i^{\text{final}})$$

The MLP here is "without biases in all its linear layers" (Section B.5)—this is a subtle detail that may help with extrapolation behavior, as bias terms can shift energy predictions in ways that don't respect physical constraints.

**Edge energy:**

$$E_{ij}^{\text{edge}} = \text{MLP}(e_{ij}^{\text{final}})$$

**Short-range modification energy:**

$$E_{ij}^{\text{mod}} = \sum_s \frac{1}{r_{ij}^s} \text{MLP}_s(e_{ij}^{\text{final}}) \eta_c(r_{ij}/r_{\text{mod}}^c), \quad \text{if } r_{ij} \leq 1.5 \text{ Å}$$

where `$r_{\text{mod}}^c = 1.5$` Å is the modification cutoff, and `$s$` runs over power indices.

**What it computes:** a polynomial correction `$1/r^s$` for very short interatomic distances (below 1.5 Å), where the standard MLFF's predictions may be unreliable because such configurations are rare in training data and involve strong Pauli repulsion that requires specialized functional forms. The `$\text{MLP}_s(e_{ij}^{\text{final}})$` learns a coefficient for each power `$s$` based on the edge features.

**Why this form rather than the ZBL potential:** the ZBL (Ziegler-Biersack-Littmark) screened nuclear repulsion potential is the standard approach for short-range repulsion in many MLFFs. The paper notes that they deliberately chose a simpler polynomial correction, stating "Instead of using a ZBL potential, we simply add a polynomial correction in Eq. S48." The polynomial form is computationally simpler (no exponential screening function) and is learnable from data (the coefficients come from the MLP output), whereas ZBL is a fixed physics-based form. This choice reflects the paper's general philosophy that learned corrections, when supported by appropriate training data, can be as effective as physics-based ones.

**Atomic reference energy:** `$E^{Z_i}$` is a learned per-element scalar that provides a baseline energy. This is standard in MLFFs—it removes the need for the model to learn absolute energy scales, allowing it to focus on the interaction energies.

**Forces and stress from differentiation:**

Forces are the negative gradient of total energy with respect to atomic positions:

$$f_i = -\frac{\partial E}{\partial r_i}$$

This is computed via automatic differentiation—the entire forward pass from atomic positions to total energy is differentiable, so forces are obtained by backpropagating through the network.

Stress is the derivative of energy with respect to lattice strain:

$$\sigma = \frac{1}{V} \frac{\partial E}{\partial \varepsilon}$$

where `$V$` is the lattice volume. In practice, this is computed via the virial theorem: the stress tensor components are given by `$\sigma_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{1}{V} \sum_i \sum_{j>i} r_{ij,\alpha} f_{ij,\beta}$` for pairwise interactions, where `$r_{ij,\alpha}$` is the `$\alpha$`-component of the interatomic vector and `$f_{ij,\beta}$` is the `$\beta$`-component of the force contribution from the `$(i,j)$` pair.

**What happens during training:** The loss function (not explicitly defined in the paper but standard for MLFFs) compares the predicted energies, forces, and stresses to the quantum-chemical reference values across all configurations in the training batch. The loss is typically a weighted sum:

$$\mathcal{L} = \lambda_E \mathcal{L}_E + \lambda_F \mathcal{L}_F + \lambda_\sigma \mathcal{L}_\sigma$$

where `$\lambda_E$`, `$\lambda_F$`, and `$\lambda_\sigma$` control the relative importance of energy, force, and stress accuracy. The paper does not specify these weights explicitly, but the hyperparameter optimization (Section C, Table S3) presumably includes them in the search space.

**Multi-head energy prediction:** Because Vivace is trained on data labeled with different QM methods (`$\omega$`B97X-V from ORCA for PolyCrop-small, GFN2-xTB for PolyCrop-large, r²SCAN-D3 from CP2K for PolyPack/PolyDiss), the model uses separate output heads—different MLPs—for each QM method. The shared backbone (the equivariant and invariant feature representations) learns a representation of atomic environments that is useful across QM levels, while the separate heads learn to map this representation to the specific energy scale of each method. The final model (Section C) has "three energy prediction heads, predicting two ORCA-calculated energies and one CP2K-calculated energy" (the two ORCA heads correspond to `$\omega$`B97M-V/def2-QZVP used in OMol25 pretraining and `$\omega$`B97X-V/def2-TZVPP used in PolyCrop; the CP2K head corresponds to r²SCAN-D3 used in PolyPack/PolyDiss).

#### Hyperparameter Optimization and Final Architecture

The paper conducted an extensive hyperparameter search using multivariate Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampling. The search space (Table S3) includes:

- `$L_{\text{max}}$`: 0–6 (maximum angular momentum for spherical harmonics)
- `$n_{\text{layer}}$`: 0–6 (number of equivariant interaction layers in the main chunk)
- `$n_{\text{pre}}$`: 0–2 (invariant layers before the main chunk)
- `$n_{\text{post}}$`: 0–2 (invariant layers after the main chunk)
- `$n_{\text{sub}}$`: 0–1 (invariant sub-layers within each main layer)
- `$n_{\text{MLP-layer}}$`: 1–2 (hidden layers within interaction MLPs)
- `$n_{\text{basis}}$`: 8–32 (number of radial basis functions)
- `$n_{\text{feat}}$`: 8–1024 (feature dimension for invariant components)
- `$n_{\text{attn-heads}}$`: 4–64 (number of attention heads)
- `$n_{\text{channel}}$`: 8–1024 (feature dimension for equivariant components)
- attention type: softmax, SiLU, concatenate-MLP, or exponential-Gaussian kernel
- learning rate: `$10^{-4}$` to 0.005 (log-uniform)

**Two-stage pruning during search:** Before training, configurations are evaluated on a 2000-atom system to measure untrained MD simulation speed—configurations below 0.5 ns/d are pruned. During training, Hyperband (an adaptive resource allocation algorithm) prunes unpromising runs early. Over one week, 1000–2000 distinct configurations were evaluated.

The final model (Table S4, model 4) uses: `$L_{\text{max}} = 2$`, `$n_{\text{layer}} = 3$`, `$n_{\text{basis}} = 8$`, `$n_{\text{attn-heads}} = 32$` (implicit in the exponential kernel context), `$n_{\text{feat}} = 64$`, `$n_{\text{MLP-layer}} = 1$`, `$n_{\text{channel}} = 32$`, `$n_{\text{post}} = 0$`, `$n_{\text{sub}} = 0$`. Total parameters: 389,005 (single-precision float32). Simulation speed: 1.23 ns/d on a single A100 GPU for a ~2000-atom polystyrene system.

A notable finding is that `$n_{\text{sub}} = 0$`—there are no invariant sub-layers within the equivariant interaction layers. The paper comments: "this indicates purely two-body mid-range interactions. Further constraint optimization with `$n_{\text{sub}} = 1$` should significantly improve the model's expressivity, but this is beyond the scope of this work." This means the current model may under-utilize the mid-range interactions; there is headroom for improvement.

#### Training Procedure: Pre-Training, Fine-Tuning, and Multi-Fidelity Data Handling

The training proceeds in two stages:

**Stage 1 – Pre-training on non-periodic data.** Datasets: OMol25 (a general small-molecule quantum chemistry dataset), PolyCrop-ORCA (the ~400k DFT-labeled clusters), and PolyCrop-XTB (the ~400k semi-empirical labeled clusters). The model has two output heads: one for `$\omega$`B97M-V/def2-QZVP energies (from OMol25, a different functional/basis combination than PolyCrop's `$\omega$`B97X-V/def2-TZVPP), and one for PolyCrop's `$\omega$`B97X-V DFT energies. Sampling ratios are 0.05:1:1 for OMol25:PolyCrop-ORCA:PolyCrop-XTB—the XTB data is weighted heavily despite its lower accuracy, likely because the much larger structures it covers (up to 1,411 atoms) provide information about long-range steric effects that the DFT data cannot. The starting learning rate is `$1 \times 10^{-4}$`, with a reduction factor of 0.9 if validation loss plateaus for >200,000 batches. Dynamic batching ensures ~4,800 atoms per GPU per batch. Training runs for 4 days on 4 A100 GPUs.

**Stage 2 – Fine-tuning with periodic data.** Datasets: OMol25, PolyCrop-ORCA, PolyCrop-XTB, **plus** PolyPack and PolyDiss (the periodic datasets crucial for condensed-phase interactions). The sampling ratio is heavily skewed toward the periodic data: 10 (PolyDiss) : 0.2 (OMol25) : 1 (PolyPack) : 0.5 (PolyCrop-ORCA) : 0.05 (PolyCrop-XTB). PolyDiss receives a 10× weight because its dissociation curves are precisely what the pre-trained model gets wrong (Section 3.1). The model now has three output heads: two ORCA heads (OMol25 and PolyCrop-ORCA) and one CP2K head (PolyPack + PolyDiss, labeled with r²SCAN-D3). The starting learning rate is 0.0005, higher than Stage 1, reflecting that this is a deliberate adjustment rather than continued convergence. Training runs for 2 days on 4 A100 GPUs. The model selected for final evaluation is the one that achieves the best PolyDiss validation set performance among several training protocols (Section C: "Around five different protocols were tested. The model-protocol combination that yields the best PolyDiss validation set performance is then chosen for the final testing on density and glass transition temperature.").

**Why multi-fidelity training matters:** The three energy scales (`$\omega$`B97M-V, `$\omega$`B97X-V, r²SCAN-D3) differ systematically—different functionals predict different absolute energies and binding energies for the same configuration. By training separate heads, the shared backbone learns features that are invariant to the QM method (local geometry, chemical bonding patterns) while the heads learn the method-specific energy scale. The heavy weighting on PolyDiss during fine-tuning ensures that inter-chain interaction errors, which were catastrophic for the pre-trained model's MD performance, are specifically corrected.

#### Molecular Dynamics Simulation Protocol for Density Prediction

Once Vivace is trained, it is used as a drop-in force field within the LAMMPS MD engine (or i-PI for UMA, which lacks a LAMMPS interface). The simulation protocol for density prediction is a 21-step sequence designed to efficiently compress an initial low-density amorphous packing to its equilibrium density.

**System construction.** For each polymer, a periodic cell containing approximately 2,000 atoms is built using EMC, arranged as six chains of equal length at an initial density of 0.5 g/cm³. The chain ends are capped with "fragments resembling the repeating unit to ensure chemical stability"—an important detail because dangling bonds or chemically implausible end groups would create artificial stresses that corrupt density prediction.

**The 21-step protocol.** The protocol consists of seven cycles, each with three stages:

- **Stage 1: High-temperature NVT simulation at `$T_{\text{max}} = 700$` K.** NVT fixes the number of particles (N), volume (V), and temperature (T). The volume is fixed at its current value, while velocity rescaling or thermostatting maintains the target temperature. The high temperature (700 K, above the highest expected Tg of 672 K in PolyArena) ensures that chains have sufficient kinetic energy to overcome energy barriers between conformations—they can slide past each other, unkink, and explore configuration space.

- **Stage 2: NVT simulation at target temperature.** The temperature is lowered to the desired measurement temperature (typically 298.15 K for room-temperature density). This allows the system to relax toward the equilibrium configuration at the target temperature while keeping the volume fixed.

- **Stage 3: NPT simulation at target temperature with pressure ramping/release.** NPT fixes particle number, pressure, and temperature. The simulation cell volume adjusts dynamically so the average internal pressure (computed from the stress tensor) matches the target. Over the first three cycles, the target pressure is ramped from zero up to `$P_{\text{max}} = 5 \times 10^4$` bar—this is an enormous pressure (atmospheric pressure is ~1 bar) applied to "compact initially rigid configurations." Over the final four cycles, the pressure is reduced back to standard pressure (1 bar). The ramping-and-release sequence is a common trick in polymer simulation: the initial high pressure forces chains into close contact, overcoming steric barriers, and the subsequent release allows them to relax to the equilibrium density without remaining kinetically trapped.

**Why `$P_{\text{max}} = 5 \times 10^4$` bar:** this value follows prior work by Larsen et al. and is chosen to be "sufficiently high to compact initially rigid configurations." The 0.5 g/cm³ starting density is significantly lower than the expected equilibrium density (typically 0.8–2.0 g/cm³), so the system starts far from equilibrium. The high-pressure phase rapidly compresses the system toward the experimental density, after which the pressure release fine-tunes the packing.

**Thermostat and barostat details.** For Vivace, MACE-OFF, and PCFF simulations using LAMMPS: NVT and NPT use a Nosé-Hoover thermostat with damping `$\tau = 50$` fs; NPT uses the Martyna-Tuckerman-Tobias-Klein (MTTK) extended Nosé-Hoover barostat with time constant `$\tau = 500$` fs. The 0.5 fs timestep is chosen for numerical stability—MLFFs can produce steeper energy gradients than classical force fields, requiring smaller timesteps.

**Density extraction.** After the 21-step protocol completes, the final NPT production stage (typically at standard pressure) yields the equilibrium density. The reported density is the time-averaged density over the production stage. Uncertainty is estimated from density fluctuations in the NPT trajectory.

---

#### Glass Transition Temperature Determination

Determining `$T_g$` from simulation requires significantly more computation than density prediction: the system must be simulated at multiple temperatures spanning the glass transition, and the larger system size (10,000 atoms rather than 2,000) is needed to reduce variance in the density estimates.

**Temperature selection.** For each polymer, 17 temperatures are simulated, spaced at 20 K intervals and centered on the experimental `$T_g$`. The range spans from `$T_g - 160$` K to `$T_g + 160$` K, with the constraint that no simulations are run below 100 K. This symmetric range ensures both the glassy and rubbery regimes are sampled.

**Replica simulations.** At each temperature, three independent simulations are run, each starting from a different initial configuration generated by EMC. This is essential because polymer configurations at high temperature can become kinetically trapped in different local minima during cooling—averaging over replicas reduces the configurational variance.

**Density aggregation.** For a given temperature, the three replica densities are combined using inverse variance weighting:

$$\bar{\rho}(T) = \frac{\sum_{r=1}^{3} \rho_r(T) / \sigma_r^2(T)}{\sum_{r=1}^{3} 1 / \sigma_r^2(T)}$$

where `$\rho_r(T)$` is the mean density from replica `$r$` and `$\sigma_r^2(T)$` is its variance (estimated from the fluctuations in the NPT production trajectory of at least 0.5 ns following the 21-step protocol). The combined variance is:

$$\sigma^2_{\bar{\rho}}(T) = \frac{1}{\sum_{r=1}^{3} 1 / \sigma_r^2(T)}$$

**What inverse variance weighting does:** if one replica has much larger density fluctuations (due to poor equilibration, configurational drift, or simulation instability), its contribution to the aggregate is downweighted. This is a principled way to handle replica-to-replica variability without manual filtering.

**Bootstrap resampling for `$T_g$` fitting.** The `$T_g$` is identified as the intersection of two linear regimes in the density-temperature curve—the glassy regime (low temperature, high slope) and the rubbery regime (high temperature, lower slope). The paper uses the method of Patrone et al., which fits a hyperbolic function to the entire density-temperature curve without requiring user input to identify the transition region. The hyperbolic function has the form that smoothly interpolates between two linear asymptotes:

$$\rho(T) = c_1 + c_2 T + c_3 (T - T_g) \left[1 + \exp\left(\frac{T - T_g}{w}\right)\right]^{-1}$$

with `$c_1$`, `$c_2$`, `$c_3$`, `$T_g$`, and `$w$` as fit parameters. The bootstrap procedure:

1. For each temperature, draw 1000 bootstrap samples from a normal distribution centered at the aggregated mean `$\bar{\rho}(T)$` with variance `$\sigma^2_{\bar{\rho}}(T)$`.
2. Fit the hyperbolic function to each bootstrap sample.
3. The mean of the resulting 1000 `$T_g$` values is the estimated `$T_g$`, and their standard deviation is its uncertainty.

**Why automated fitting:** the paper states that "more sophisticated methods exist for both simulation and fitting, our approach was chosen because it is fully automated and requires no data filtering" (Section 5.6). Manual selection of the linear regimes introduces human bias and is not scalable to 130 polymers. The hyperbolic function is preferred over piecewise linear fitting because it uses all data points and handles smooth transitions that lack a sharp kink—common when simula`t`ion noise blurs the glass transition.

**Uncertainty and error reporting.** Each `$T_g$` estimate comes with an uncertainty interval (shown as the shaded area in Figure 4a and as error bars in Figure 4c). These uncertainty estimates are critical for evaluating whether differences between force fields are statistically significant or merely sampling noise.

**Correlation with density accuracy.** The paper notes an important negative result (Section 2.4): "an accurate `$T_g$` prediction does not necessarily correlate with an accurate density prediction at room temperature, and vice versa." This implies that density and `$T_g$` test different aspects of the force field—density is sensitive to the well depth of the intermolecular potential, while `$T_g$` is sensitive to the stiffness of the potential (its second derivative) and the energy barriers between conformational states.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Local rather than message-passing:** enables efficient multi-GPU parallelization without inter-device communication, critical for 10,000-atom simulations; the receptive field limitation is addressed by using a large 6.5 Å cutoff rather than deep message-passing layers.
- **Multi-cutoff strategy (3.8 Å + 6.5 Å):** expensive equivariant tensor products are restricted to the short-range environment where angular bonding interactions are strongest; efficient invariant attention handles the mid-range where van der Waals interactions (primarily isotropic) dominate. This achieves accuracy comparable to a uniform 6.5 Å equivariant model at a fraction of the cost.
- **Simplified tensor product (factorized, same-channel):** reduces computational cost from `$O(n_{\text{channel}}^3)$` to `$O(n_{\text{channel}})$` with minimal accuracy loss, based on empirical finding that learnable channel-mixing parameters are not essential.
- **Exponential radial basis in `$e^{-r}$`:** provides adaptive resolution—high at short range where covalent interactions change sharply, lower at long range where dispersion varies slowly.
- **Gaussian distance kernel for attention:** naturally bounded weights without neighbor-count-dependent normalization; empirically found to outperform softmax, SiLU, and concatenation-MLP alternatives.
- **PolyDiss dataset with heavy fine-tuning weight (10×):** targets the specific failure mode (underestimated inter-chain attraction) that caused the pre-trained model to catastrophically fail at density prediction, even though the pre-trained model's force/stress errors on computational benchmarks were comparable.
- **Inner-product-based three-body interaction:** captures angular bonding preferences with `$O(M)$` complexity by computing the tensor product once per node and then taking inner products per edge, avoiding the `$O(M^2)$` cost of explicit triplet enumeration.
- **Polynomial short-range modification rather than ZBL:** simpler to implement, fully learnable from data, and empirically sufficient for the interatomic distance range encountered in polymer simulations.
- **Multi-head training with separate QM method heads:** allows a single shared representation to work across multiple QM levels (DFT at two functional/basis combinations plus semi-empirical), leveraging the large structural diversity of the cheaper methods while maintaining accuracy on the high-fidelity reference.
- **21-step pressure-ramp protocol for density:** systematically overcomes kinetic trapping that would otherwise cause low-density starting configurations to fail to reach equilibrium; the `$5 \times 10^4$` bar maximum pressure follows established practice in the polymer simulation literature.
- **Bootstrap uncertainty quantification for `$T_g$`:** provides objective error bars that account for both simulation variance and fitting uncertainty, enabling meaningful comparison between force fields.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Computational Benchmarks Do Not Predict Simulation Quality—The PolyDiss Diagnostic

The paper's most conceptually disruptive contribution is not a model or a dataset but a **diagnostic finding**: standard MLFF evaluation metrics—force and energy mean absolute errors on held-out test sets—are systematically misleading as predictors of whether a model will produce physically realistic molecular dynamics simulations, particularly for condensed-phase polymer systems.

Section 3.1 (Table 2) contains the evidence that makes this finding unavoidable. The pre-trained Vivace model achieves a force MAE of 179 meV/Å on PolyPack and 123 meV/Å on PolyDiss—numbers comparable to the fine-tuned model (183 and 97 meV/Å, respectively) and to MACE-OFF (188 and 242 meV/Å). Stress errors follow the same pattern: 1.7 vs. 1.9 meV/Å³ on PolyPack. On any standard computational benchmark, these models would be judged similarly competent. Yet the pre-trained model produces a density MAE of 0.60 g/cm³—an order of magnitude worse than the fine-tuned model's 0.04 g/cm³ and far worse than MACE-OFF's 0.10 g/cm³. The force and stress metrics simply fail to distinguish between a model that works and one that catastrophically fails in simulation.

The paper traces this diagnostic gap to a specific physical cause: the pre-trained model systematically underestimates inter-chain dissociation energies (Figure 5), predicting too-weak attraction at the 3–6 Å separations that determine polymer cohesion. Force errors average over all atom pairs, but density is dominated by the small fraction of intermolecular contacts at the tail of the radial distribution function—precisely where the error is concentrated. The field's standard practice of reporting aggregate force MAEs averages away the signal that matters for simulation quality.

**Why this is a fundamental reframing, not an incremental observation:** The MLFF field has organized its development around benchmark leaderboards—OC20, OC22, OMol25, OMC25—that report force and energy errors. The implicit assumption is that lower force MAE implies a better force field. This paper provides a controlled counterexample: two models with near-identical force errors produce qualitatively different simulation outcomes. It demonstrates that the mapping from "accurate on test set" to "useful for MD" is not monotonic and can break entirely. This shifts the evaluation burden from computational metrics (which are cheap to compute but weakly predictive) to experimental validation (which is expensive but definitive). The PolyArena benchmark is the paper's proposed solution to this reframed evaluation problem.

The finding also explains why prior work reporting excellent benchmark performance (e.g., MACE-OFF on SPICE, UMA on diverse datasets) doesn't guarantee polymer simulation success—those benchmarks test local accuracy, not the global balance of competing interactions that sets macroscopic observables. A model can be perfectly accurate at every sampled configuration and still drive a simulation to physically wrong steady states if it has systematic biases in the interaction tail.

**Connection to prior work:** The paper cites two recent studies (Poltavsky et al. in the TEA Challenge, Mannan et al.) that independently observed weak correlation between computational benchmarks and experimental predictions. The PolyDiss dissociation curve analysis provides a mechanistic explanation for *why* this weak correlation exists in polymer systems specifically—it's not that force errors are noisy predictors; it's that they measure the wrong quantity. Force errors are dominated by strong short-range interactions that are easy to learn; simulation quality depends on weak long-range interactions that contribute negligibly to the aggregate error metric.

---

### Innovation 2: Inter-Chain Interactions Are a First-Class Design Constraint for Polymer MLFFs

The paper argues—through both positive results (what works) and negative results (what fails)—that **inter-chain (intermolecular) interactions require explicit, targeted treatment in training data, not merely implicit coverage from diverse general-purpose datasets**. This is not an observation about dataset size or chemical diversity; it is a structural claim about the types of physical interactions that determine polymer behavior and the corresponding gaps in existing MLFF training data.

The evidence for this claim is layered through the paper's experimental design:

**First, the PolyDiss dataset is not just "more polymer data"—it is a specific diagnostic probe.** By construction, each PolyDiss configuration isolates a single polymer chain interacting with its periodic image, with no intra-chain conformational complexity to confound the signal. The dissociation curve (energy vs. inter-chain separation) directly measures the model's ability to reproduce the van der Waals well depth and range that sets the equilibrium density. The fact that PolyDiss receives a 10× sampling weight during fine-tuning (Section C) relative to the next-largest dataset component is an implicit admission that this specific information is disproportionately important—it carries more signal per configuration for the downstream task than generic structural data.

**Second, the cutoff-radius ablation (Section 3.2, Figure 6) operationalizes the length scale requirements for polymer simulation.** A cutoff below ~5.5 Å causes PCTFE density to collapse to near-zero values because the model cannot "see" the attractive interactions between chains. This is not merely a hyperparameter tuning observation—it is evidence that the cutoff radius, which is typically treated as an architecture parameter to be optimized for speed-accuracy tradeoffs on small-molecule benchmarks, becomes a **hard physical requirement** for polymer simulation. The paper demonstrates that MACE-OFF (cutoff 4.5 Å) fails on PCTFE for precisely this reason, while UMA (cutoff 6.0 Å) succeeds—and UMA was not trained on any polymer data.

**Third, the pre-trained model's failure mode (Figure S5) isolates the training data gap.** The pre-trained model was trained on OMol25 (diverse small molecules), PolyCrop-ORCA (polymer fragments to 234 atoms), and PolyCrop-XTB (polymer fragments to 1,411 atoms). The XTB-labeled clusters reaching 1,411 atoms contain many inter-chain contacts, so one might expect them to teach inter-chain interactions. They don't—because the cropping procedure creates non-periodic clusters where inter-chain contacts are between spatially adjacent segments that are actually covalently connected in the original periodic structure. The model learns that these atom pairs "belong together" (they co-occur in the same cluster), which is the opposite of the physical reality where they are distinct chains that happen to be in proximity. Only periodic training data (PolyPack and PolyDiss) accurately represents the statistical independence of inter-chain contacts.

**Comparison to prior work:** The dominant approach in universal MLFF development (MACE-MP, MatterSim, UMA) is to train on massive, chemically diverse datasets spanning molecules, crystals, and surfaces, with the assumption that coverage of diverse local environments will generalize to any condensed-phase system. This paper's finding that specifically periodic, inter-chain-focused data is necessary—and that large non-periodic clusters are not a substitute—challenges that assumption for soft matter. It suggests that certain physical regimes (the weakly-bound, fluctuating interfaces between polymer chains) are under-represented in generic datasets because those datasets are dominated by either isolated molecules (where there are no inter-molecular contacts) or ordered crystals (where inter-molecular contacts are rigid and repetitive). Amorphous polymer packing occupies a distinct region of configuration space that must be explicitly sampled.

**Why this is a design principle, not merely a dataset contribution:** The implication is not just "use PolyData for polymers." It's that MLFF development for any condensed-phase soft matter system—polymers, liquids, biological assemblies—should include training data specifically designed to probe intermolecular interactions at the relevant length scales, with periodic boundary conditions that accurately represent the statistical mechanics of distinct molecules or chains. The PolyDiss dataset is a template for how to construct such targeted data: isolate the interaction of interest (inter-chain), systematically vary the control parameter (separation distance), and label with the QM method that captures the relevant physics (dispersion-corrected DFT). This is a transferable methodology, not a one-off dataset.

### Innovation 3: The Multi-Cutoff Strategy as a Resolution of the Accuracy-Efficiency Tension in Polymer Simulation

The Vivace architecture's multi-cutoff strategy—applying expensive SE(3)-equivariant tensor products only at short range (3.8 Å) and efficient invariant attention at mid-range (3.8–6.5 Å)—is not presented as a theoretical advance in equivariant networks. It is presented as an **engineering resolution of a specific, polymer-motivated constraint**: the model must capture interactions at the 6+ Å range (Figure 6 proves this is non-negotiable), but equivariant operations on a 6.5 Å neighborhood are computationally prohibitive for the 10,000-atom, nanosecond-scale simulations required for glass transition temperature prediction.

The innovation lives in the **empirical justification that this separation works for polymer physics**. Short-range interactions (below 3.8 Å) are dominated by covalent bonding, hydrogen bonding, and steric repulsion—all of which have strong angular dependence that requires equivariant directional features to represent. Mid-range interactions (3.8–6.5 Å) are dominated by van der Waals dispersion, which is predominantly isotropic (spherically symmetric) and can be adequately captured by distance-dependent invariant features. The architecture encodes a physical prior: the type of geometric information needed to represent an interaction depends on its distance range. This is not proven from first principles; it is validated by the fact that the model works—achieving 0.04 g/cm³ density MAE and 1.23 ns/d simulation speed on a single GPU.

**What makes this distinctive from prior multi-scale or hierarchical architectures:** The standard approach to handling multiple length scales in MLFFs is to increase the receptive field through deeper message-passing layers (e.g., MACE uses 2 layers, NequIP uses 3–5 layers, UMA uses 4 layers). This allows each atom to "see" farther by aggregating information through neighbors, but at the cost of inter-GPU communication during parallel simulation. Vivace's approach is fundamentally different: instead of increasing the logical receptive field through message passing, it increases the **physical cutoff** but applies cheaper operations to the distant part. The receptive field remains one-hop, enabling embarrassingly parallel local computation. This is a direct trade: message-passing models invest compute in information propagation; Vivace invests it in direct long-range interaction modeling. For polymer simulations where the key physics is at the 5–7 Å range (the van der Waals well between non-bonded chains), the direct approach is arguably more physically appropriate—those interactions are genuinely two-body, not mediated through intervening atoms.

**The 0 ns/d constraint from the hyperparameter search:** The hyperparameter optimization includes a hard speed filter: any architecture slower than 0.5 ns/d on a 2,000-atom system is pruned before training (Section C). This operationalizes the paper's claim that speed is a first-class design constraint, not an afterthought. It means the final Vivace architecture (1.23 ns/d) was selected from a Pareto frontier of accuracy-vs-speed tradeoffs, not from an unconstrained accuracy optimization. This contrasts with much MLFF development where architectures are designed for benchmark accuracy and speed is addressed post-hoc through implementation optimization or model compression.

**Limitation and future headroom:** The paper notes that the selected architecture has `n_sub = 0`—no invariant sub-layers within the equivariant interaction blocks—and suggests that `n_sub = 1` should improve expressivity. This implies the current model is somewhat under-parameterized for the mid-range interactions, and there is headroom for accuracy improvement at the cost of speed. The multi-cutoff framework makes this type of architecture exploration systematic: one can independently tune the expressivity of the short-range equivariant block and the mid-range invariant block, treating them as separate design dimensions.

---

### Innovation 4: Learning Intermolecular Interactions Requires Periodic Training Data—Non-Periodic Clusters Are Not a Substitute

This innovation is a specific, experimentally-grounded refinement of Innovation 2. The paper demonstrates that the PolyCrop dataset—nearly 800,000 non-periodic polymer cluster configurations—is **insufficient** to teach a model correct inter-chain interactions, even though these clusters contain atom pairs that are inter-chain in the original periodic structure. The pre-trained model, trained on PolyCrop and OMol25, achieves reasonable force errors but qualitatively wrong inter-chain dissociation curves (Figure 5) and catastrophically wrong densities (Figure S5).

**Why this matters conceptually:** The standard strategy for building large MLFF datasets from periodic systems is to extract local clusters—crop a sphere around a central atom, cap dangling bonds, and treat the result as an independent training configuration. This is done in PolyCrop and in many other datasets (e.g., the liquid water clusters extracted from bulk simulations that appear in SPICE). The implicit assumption is that the local environment of an atom is independent of its global periodic context as long as the cluster is large enough. The paper's results challenge this assumption for weakly-bound soft matter systems.

The failure mode is subtle. In a PolyCrop cluster generated from a periodic polymer packing, two chain segments from different chains that happen to be in proximity will both be included in the cluster (because they're within the cropping radius of the central atom). In the DFT calculation, these segments interact through their non-bonded van der Waals potential—correct physics. But from the model's perspective, these atoms co-occur in the same training configuration with a specific relative geometry, and the model may learn that this geometry is a "bonded" or "belongs together" arrangement. In the actual periodic system, those two chains are independent—they can and do move apart. The cluster representation conflates configurational proximity with structural relationship.

In contrast, the PolyDiss dataset explicitly represents the inter-chain interaction as a function of separation distance. The model learns that the energy smoothly approaches zero as the distance increases, which is the correct asymptotic behavior. The PolyPack dataset reinforces this by providing many configurations where chains are packed at different densities, teaching the model the full shape of the inter-chain potential including both repulsive and attractive regimes.

**This is a negative result with positive implications for dataset design.** It implies that for any MLFF intended for condensed-phase simulations where intermolecular interactions drive macroscopic properties (polymers, liquids, solutions, biological assemblies), periodic training data that explicitly samples intermolecular separations is not merely helpful but **necessary**. Non-periodic cluster data, regardless of its size or chemical diversity, does not substitute. This is a concrete, falsifiable claim that can guide future dataset construction efforts.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary evaluation dataset is **PolyArena**, introduced in this work, containing experimental density measurements for 130 polymers and glass transition temperature (Tg) measurements for 87 of those polymers (Tg data is not available for all 130). The experimental measurements are extracted from the Bicerano handbook (Bicerano, 2002), representing amorphous, atactic specimens produced under standard synthesis and post-processing conditions. The dataset spans polymers containing H, C, N, O, F, Si, S, and Cl, with repeating unit molecular weights from 28 to 593 g/mol, experimental densities from 0.8 to 2.0 g/cm³, and glass transition temperatures from 152 to 672 K. For model training, **PolyData** provides three complementary quantum-chemical datasets: **PolyPack** (~230,000 periodic configurations of multiple polymer chains at various densities, labeled with r²SCAN-D3/TZV2P in CP2K), **PolyDiss** (~50,000 dissociation trajectories of single polymer chains in expanding periodic cells, same QM method), and **PolyCrop** (~800,000 non-periodic cluster fragments, labeled with either ωB97X-V/def2-TZVPP in ORCA for clusters up to 234 atoms or GFN2-xTB for clusters up to 1,411 atoms). A subset of 10 polymers (Table S1: PE, PIB, PET, PCTFE, PS, PVMS, PVC, PAN, PTPC, PODPM) is selected for detailed study, divided into five "seen" polymers (included in Vivace's training) and five "unseen" polymers (held out to test generalization).

- **Base model(s).** The primary model is **Vivace**, a local SE(3)-equivariant graph neural network with a multi-cutoff strategy, trained in two stages: pre-training on non-periodic OMol25 and PolyCrop data, followed by fine-tuning on the periodic PolyPack and PolyDiss datasets with heavy weighting on PolyDiss (10× sampling ratio relative to other components). The final architecture (Table S4, model 4) uses L_max = 2, 3 equivariant interaction layers, 8 radial basis functions, 64 invariant features, 32 equivariant channels, and 389,005 total parameters, operating in single precision. For comparison, the paper evaluates two additional MLFFs: **MACE-OFF23-s** (a short-range transferable MLFF for organic molecules, trained on augmented SPICE plus fragments and water clusters, with r_max = 4.5 Å and two message-passing layers giving a 9.0 Å receptive field) and **UMA-s-1p1** (a universal MLFF covering >80 elements, with r_max = 6.0 Å and four message-passing layers giving a 24.0 Å receptive field, using author-provided weights for organic systems), both used as provided without retraining. Two classical force fields serve as baselines: **PCFF** (Polymer Consistent Force Field, simulations run by the authors using LAMMPS with EMC-generated parameters) and **OPLS3e** (densities and Tgs taken from the study by Afzal et al., with the caveat that their computational setup is similar but not identical to this paper's protocol).

- **Metrics.** Density prediction accuracy is quantified by **mean absolute error (MAE) in g/cm³** between the simulated equilibrium density (obtained at standard conditions after the 21-step MD protocol) and the experimental density from PolyArena. MAE is reported across all successfully simulated polymers and separately for the 10 selected polymers. For Vivace, MAE is further split into "seen" and "unseen" polymers to assess generalization. Glass transition temperature accuracy is quantified by **MAE in Kelvin** between the fitted T_g (T_g^fit, extracted from density-temperature curves via hyperbolic fitting with bootstrap uncertainty quantification) and the experimental T_g from PolyArena, reported for the 10 selected polymers. Simulation speed is measured in **nanoseconds per day (ns/d)** of wall-clock time, benchmarked on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU (80 GB) for a polystyrene system containing ~8,000 atoms with a 0.5 fs time step. For multi-GPU scaling, speed is reported on 1, 2, 4, and 8 A100 GPUs for a 15,552-atom polystyrene system.

- **Baselines.** Four baselines are compared against Vivace:
  - **PCFF** (Sun et al., 1994): A classical force field originally developed for polycarbonates, applied here to all 130 polymers. MD simulations use LAMMPS with parameters generated by the Enhanced Monte Carlo (EMC) program. Not all simulations succeeded: the paper reports that "MD simulations of PET and PODPM failed with PCFF" (Table 1 footnote).
  - **OPLS3e** (Roos et al., 2019): A refined classical force field for drug-like small molecules. Densities and Tgs are taken from the supplementary materials of Afzal et al. (2021), whose "computational setup and analysis protocol... are similar but not identical to ours, and therefore, we exercise caution when making direct comparisons" (Section 5.2).
  - **MACE-OFF23-s** (Kovács et al., 2025): A short-range transferable MLFF for neutral, closed-shell organic systems, trained on augmented SPICE plus fragments and water clusters from QMugs, covering H, C, N, O, F, P, S, Cl, Br, and I. The "s" (small) variant with 64-bit precision is used throughout, with r_max = 4.5 Å. It was not trained on any polymer-specific data.
  - **UMA-s-1p1** (Wood et al., 2025): A universal MLFF covering >80 elements, trained on multiple curated first-principles datasets (OC20, OMol25, OMC25, OMat24, OpenDAC25) with four message-passing layers and r_max = 6.0 Å. The organic-specialized task weights (task_name=omol) are used. Due to its slow simulation speed, UMA is only evaluated on the 10 selected polymers for density, and is excluded from the Tg experiments "due to its slow simulation speed and large memory requirements" (Section 2.4).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Unlike the LLM paper that measures compute in "generations," this paper's compute metric is **simulation throughput**—nanoseconds of physical time simulated per day of wall-clock time (ns/d). Fair comparison is complicated because different models use different MD engines: Vivace and MACE-OFF use LAMMPS with NVIDIA's ML-IAP KOKKOS interface (supporting multi-GPU), PCFF uses native LAMMPS, and UMA uses i-PI as the MD engine (which does not support multi-GPU and is "not nearly as optimized as LAMMPS," Section 5.4). Speed measurements for UMA are therefore "not directly comparable to those of Vivace and MACE-OFF." All speed tests use a 0.5 fs time step with a polystyrene system and are benchmarked on nodes with 8 NVIDIA A100-SXM4 GPUs (80 GB each) connected via NVLink.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** There is no formal cross-validation in the standard ML sense—the 10-polymer split into five "seen" and five "unseen" polymers serves as a generalization test rather than a statistical resampling protocol. Density uncertainty for a single simulation is estimated from fluctuations in the NPT production trajectory. For Tg determination, uncertainty is quantified via **bootstrap resampling**: for each of the 17 temperatures, 1000 bootstrap samples are drawn from a normal distribution centered on the inverse-variance-weighted aggregate density with the corresponding aggregate variance; a hyperbolic function is fit to each bootstrap sample; the mean and standard deviation of the resulting 1000 Tg values provide the estimate and its uncertainty. This accounts for both simulation variance (replica-to-replica) and fitting uncertainty. All MAEs are simple arithmetic means of absolute errors across the evaluated polymers.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Density Prediction Across 130 Polymers (Section 2.3, Figures 3 and S5, Tables 1 and S5–S6)

**Headline result:** Vivace achieves a density MAE of **0.04 g/cm³** across all successfully simulated polymers in PolyArena, outperforming all baselines except UMA (which was only evaluated on 10 polymers and achieved 0.06 g/cm³). The pre-trained Vivace model (before fine-tuning on PolyPack/PolyDiss) achieves a density MAE of **0.60 g/cm³**, demonstrating that the periodic fine-tuning datasets are essential.

**Per-model comparison (Figure 3, Table 1):**
- **Vivace (fine-tuned):** MAE = 0.04 g/cm³ overall, 0.05 g/cm³ on the 10 selected polymers (0.04 g/cm³ on seen, 0.06 g/cm³ on unseen). The parity plot in Figure 3a shows tight clustering around the diagonal across the full density range (0.8–2.0 g/cm³), with the ±0.05 g/cm³ shaded band containing the majority of points. Notably, the unseen polymer PCTFE falls within this band, demonstrating non-trivial generalization.
- **UMA:** MAE = 0.06 g/cm³ on the 10 selected polymers only. Despite not being trained on any polymer data, UMA achieves the second-best performance, though its evaluation is limited to 10 polymers due to computational cost.
- **MACE-OFF:** MAE = 0.09 g/cm³ overall, 0.08 g/cm³ on 9 of the 10 selected polymers (PCTFE failed). MACE-OFF was not trained on any polymer data, making this result a demonstration of the transferability of MLFFs trained on diverse small-molecule datasets—though the failure on PCTFE due to cutoff limitations (discussed in Section 3.2) reveals transferability boundaries.
- **PCFF:** MAE = 0.07 g/cm³ overall, 0.12 g/cm³ on 8 of 10 polymers (PET and PODPM failed). The classical force field, despite being explicitly parametrized for polymers, performs worse than Vivace and UMA, and suffers from simulation instabilities that prevented completion for some polymers.
- **OPLS3e:** MAE = 0.10 g/cm³ overall, 0.31 g/cm³ on the 10 polymers (from Afzal et al.). The large discrepancy on the 10-polymer subset suggests poor transferability to this particular polymer set.

**The pre-trained model's catastrophic failure (Figure S5):** The pre-trained Vivace model (trained only on non-periodic OMol25 and PolyCrop, without PolyPack/PolyDiss fine-tuning) produces a density MAE of **0.60 g/cm³**, with the parity plot showing points scattered far below the diagonal—the model systematically underestimates densities. Figure 5 and Table 2 together demonstrate that this failure is rooted in inaccurate inter-chain dissociation energies (a structural issue in the model's physics) rather than poor overall force accuracy. The pre-trained model achieves a force MAE of 179 meV/Å on PolyPack and 123 meV/Å on PolyDiss—comparable to the fine-tuned model (183 and 97 meV/Å) and to MACE-OFF (188 and 242 meV/Å)—yet its density predictions are qualitatively wrong. Stress errors paint a similar picture: 1.7 meV/Å³ on PolyPack for the pre-trained model versus 1.9 for the fine-tuned model. The paper states: "This observation highlights how standard computational metrics, such as force and stress errors, are not always good predictors of a model's ability to run stable MD simulations."

**Simulation speed comparison (Table 1, Tables S5–S6):** On a single A100 GPU with an ~8,000-atom polystyrene system and 0.5 fs time step:
- Vivace: 0.52 ns/d
- MACE-OFF: 0.51 ns/d
- UMA: 0.03 ns/d
- PCFF: 17.68 ns/d

Vivace and MACE-OFF achieve comparable speeds (within 2%), both roughly 34× slower than the classical PCFF. UMA is ~17× slower than Vivace/MACE-OFF, which the paper attributes partially to the i-PI engine being less optimized than LAMMPS, and partially to UMA's architecture (four message-passing layers, larger cutoff). Multi-GPU scaling (Table S5) shows Vivace achieving 1.18 ns/d on 8 GPUs for a 15,552-atom system—a 3.93× speedup over the single-GPU performance of 0.30 ns/d, demonstrating strong scaling. PCFF's multi-GPU scaling is limited by communication overhead: performance peaks at 11.08 ns/d on 2 GPUs and drops to 8.22 ns/d on 8 GPUs, because "PCFF's longer cutoff (9–10 Å) and rapid calculation speed make communication the dominant bottleneck" (Table S5 caption).

#### Glass Transition Temperature Prediction (Section 2.4, Figure 4, Figures S7–S10)

**Headline result:** Vivace achieves a Tg MAE of **43 K** across all 10 selected polymers, with comparable performance on seen (43 K) and unseen (44 K) polymers. This is competitive with PCFF (49 K) and better than MACE-OFF (62 K) and OPLS3e (64 K). The paper states: "To our knowledge, this is the first time an MLFF has been shown to capture second-order phase transitions in polymers" (Section 2.4).

**Per-polymer breakdown (Figure 4c):**
- **Vivace's best prediction:** Polystyrene (PS, seen): T_g^fit = 399 ± 22 K vs. experimental 373 K (absolute error ~26 K).
- **Vivace's largest uncertainty:** Polyacrylonitrile (PAN, seen): the T_g uncertainty range is 211 K (not plotted fully in Figure 4c due to scale; the paper notes "the large uncertainty range of 211 K is not plotted").
- **Simulation failures:** PCFF failed for some polymers (asterisks in Figure 4c), preventing Tg determination. MACE-OFF simulation of PVC "did not crash but became unstable with the density approaching 0 at higher temperatures" (Figure S8 caption), preventing reliable Tg extraction.
- **Systematic bias:** "Vivace and MACE-OFF tend to overestimate Tg values, which is consistent with previous findings made for classical FFs" (Section 2.4, referencing Figure S10). This overestimation bias—predicting the glass transition at higher temperatures than experiment—is a known phenomenon in MD-based Tg prediction, often attributed to the orders-of-magnitude faster cooling rates in simulation compared to experiment.

**Quality of density-temperature curves (Figures 4a–b, S7–S9):** The density-temperature curves generated with Vivace "generally exhibit the two linear regimes characteristic of a second-order phase transition" (Section 2.4). Figure 4a (polystyrene) shows a clear change in slope between the low-temperature glassy regime and the high-temperature rubbery regime, with the hyperbolic fit (blue line) smoothly interpolating the transition. Figure 4b (a more challenging polymer) shows a "less distinct" change in slope, leading to greater prediction uncertainty—the shaded bootstrap confidence region is visibly wider. The full set of curves in Figure S7 shows that Vivace produces interpretable glass transitions for most of the 10 polymers, though some exhibit larger scatter or weaker slope changes.

**Comparison with classical FFs (Figures S8–S9):**
- PCFF density-temperature curves (Figure S9) show sharper transitions than Vivace for some polymers, but simulations failed entirely for others (marked "N/A").
- MACE-OFF curves (Figure S8) show qualitatively wrong behavior for PCTFE (density far below experimental values, consistent with Figure 6b's cutoff-radius analysis) and unstable behavior for PVC at high temperatures.
- OPLS3e results (from Afzal et al.) give a Tg MAE of 64 K—worse than both Vivace and PCFF, though the paper notes that direct comparison is complicated by the different simulation protocols.

**Fitting method robustness (Figure S10):** To verify that the Tg estimates are not artifacts of the specific fitting procedure, the paper compares hyperbolic fitting (the primary method) with piecewise linear fitting using the pwlf library. "Fitting a hyperbola... or a piecewise linear model... leads to nearly identical results" (Figure S10 caption). This robustness check is important because Tg extraction from MD data is a known source of methodological variation (different fitting procedures can yield systematically different values).

**Density accuracy does not predict Tg accuracy (Figures S8–S9):** The paper notes a counterintuitive finding: "an accurate Tg prediction does not necessarily correlate with an accurate density prediction at room temperature, and vice versa." This implies that density and Tg test fundamentally different aspects of the force field: density is sensitive to the equilibrium well depth of the intermolecular potential (where the attractive and repulsive forces balance), while Tg is sensitive to the stiffness of the potential—the curvature near the minimum and the height of energy barriers between conformational states that control chain mobility at different temperatures. A force field can get the well depth right (accurate density) but the barrier heights wrong (inaccurate Tg), or vice versa.

**System size effects on Tg uncertainty (Figure S6):** The paper investigates the influence of simulation system size on Tg determination, finding that "it is crucial to use larger systems containing 10,000 atoms in order to derive accurate Tg estimates" (Figure S6 caption). For polystyrene, the density-temperature curves obtained with 10,000-atom systems show noticeably smaller error bars and less scatter than 2,000-atom systems (Figure S6c), particularly at higher temperatures where configurational fluctuations are larger. This finding justifies the computational expense of the larger systems and explains why the Tg experiments are restricted to only 10 polymers—at ~0.5 ns/d, simulating 17 temperatures × 3 replicas × 10 polymers for 10,000-atom systems represents months of GPU-time.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Pre-training vs. fine-tuning on periodic data (Table 2, Figure 5, Figure S5):** The pre-trained Vivace model (trained only on non-periodic OMol25 and PolyCrop data) achieves reasonable force and stress errors on computational test sets—179 meV/Å and 1.7 meV/Å³ on PolyPack, 123 meV/Å and 2.4 meV/Å³ on PolyDiss—that are comparable to the fine-tuned model (183 meV/Å and 1.9 meV/Å³ on PolyPack, 97 meV/Å and 1.9 meV/Å³ on PolyDiss). However, the pre-trained model's density MAE on PolyArena is 0.60 g/cm³, compared to 0.04 g/cm³ for the fine-tuned model—an order-of-magnitude difference that is not predicted by the computational metrics. Figure 5 reveals the mechanistic cause: the pre-trained model's inter-chain dissociation curves systematically underestimate the attractive well depth and predict too-rapid decay of the interaction with distance relative to the DFT reference. Polymers with strong inter-chain interactions (PODPM, PAN, PVC) show the largest discrepancies. The fine-tuned model, having been trained on PolyDiss (which explicitly samples inter-chain separations) with a 10× sampling weight, corrects these dissociation curves and recovers accurate densities. This ablation establishes that **periodic training data specifically designed to probe intermolecular interactions is not merely helpful but necessary** for condensed-phase polymer simulations—a finding the paper highlights as a key diagnostic contribution (Section 3.1).

**Cutoff radius effect on inter-chain interactions and density (Figure 6):** Vivace models were trained with different cutoff radii to investigate the minimum range required for accurate polymer simulation. Figure 6a shows dissociation curves for PCTFE computed with cutoff radii from 4.0 to 6.5 Å. Models with cutoffs below ~6 Å fail to capture the binding energy between polymer chains—they predict too-weak attraction that vanishes prematurely compared to the DFT reference. Figure 6b translates this molecular-level failure to macroscopic predictions: MD simulations using Vivace models with cutoffs below 5.5 Å result in density predictions far below experimental values, with the polymer chains visibly repelling each other and the system density collapsing to near-zero values (visible in the simulation snapshot inset). A cutoff of approximately 6.5 Å is required for the simulated density to approach the experimental value. The paper notes that MACE-OFF (cutoff 4.5 Å) exhibits the same density collapse for PCTFE (Figure S8b), while UMA (cutoff 6.0 Å) does not. This ablation establishes the **cutoff radius as a hard physical requirement** for polymer simulation—it is not merely a hyperparameter to be tuned for speed-accuracy tradeoffs on small-molecule benchmarks, but a constraint imposed by the physics of intermolecular cohesion at the 5–7 Å range.

**Multi-cutoff strategy justification (Vivace architecture, Figure 6 + speed data):** The architectural choice to apply expensive equivariant operations only at short range (<3.8 Å) and efficient invariant operations at mid-range (3.8–6.5 Å) is justified by the conjunction of two findings: (1) Figure 6 demonstrates that a 6.5 Å cutoff is physically necessary—single-cutoff models with shorter ranges fail catastrophically; (2) Table 1 demonstrates that a full equivariant treatment at 6.5 Å would be computationally prohibitive—even the efficient Vivace achieves only 0.52 ns/d. The multi-cutoff strategy is therefore validated as a necessary compromise between physical accuracy (demanding a large cutoff) and computational feasibility (demanding fast evaluation). The paper does not provide a direct ablation comparing Vivace's multi-cutoff model against a hypothetical single-cutoff equivariant model at 6.5 Å—such a model may not have been trainable or simulatably fast—but the speed data in Table S4 (models 0–5) shows that faster architectures (models 3, 4, 5 at 1.01–1.74 ns/d) were selected over slower, potentially more expressive ones (models 0, 1 at 0.45–0.90 ns/d) through the hyperparameter optimization's explicit speed constraint (>0.5 ns/d).

**Seen vs. unseen polymer generalization (Figures 3a, 4c):** On the 10 selected polymers, Vivace achieves a density MAE of 0.04 g/cm³ on the five "seen" polymers (included in training) and 0.06 g/cm³ on the five "unseen" polymers (held out). For Tg, Vivace achieves 43 K MAE on seen and 44 K MAE on unseen—essentially identical. The paper highlights that this "indicates that Vivace can generalize to unseen polymers, which is a key requirement for a general-purpose polymer MLFF" (Section 2.3). However, the small sample size (only five polymers per group) means that a single outlier could dominate the MAE. The parity plot in Figure 3a shows the unseen polymers (triangles) scattered similarly to the seen polymers (circles), with no obvious systematic shift, suggesting that the generalization is genuine rather than an artifact of averaging.

**QM method head comparison (training procedure, Section C):** The final Vivace model uses three separate energy prediction heads for the three QM methods in the training data: one for ωB97M-V/def2-QZVP (from OMol25 pre-training), one for ωB97X-V/def2-TZVPP (from PolyCrop clusters labeled in ORCA), and one for r²SCAN-D3/TZV2P (from PolyPack and PolyDiss labeled in CP2K). The paper does not provide a quantitative ablation showing the benefit of multi-head training versus training on a single QM method, but the training protocol's design (Section C: "The model-protocol combination that yields the best PolyDiss validation set performance is then chosen") implies that this choice was empirically validated during the hyperparameter and protocol optimization phase. The underlying rationale—that a shared backbone can learn QM-method-invariant features (local geometry, bonding patterns) while separate heads learn method-specific energy scales—is a standard multi-fidelity learning approach but is not directly ablated in the final paper.

**Hyperparameter optimization with speed constraint (Section C, Table S4):** The hyperparameter search explicitly filtered out architectures slower than 0.5 ns/d on a 2,000-atom system before training. The surviving architectures (models 0–5 in Table S4) represent a Pareto frontier trading off accuracy (as measured on PolyPack and PolyCrop validation) against simulation speed (ranging from 0.45 to 1.74 ns/d). The selected model (model 4, 1.23 ns/d) was not the most accurate—it was chosen for "the best speed and accuracy trade-off among the six." The paper notes that model 3 (1.01 ns/d, with softmax attention type and 16 attention heads) and models 0–1 (0.45–0.90 ns/d, with more equivariant layers) were available but not selected. This is a deliberate engineering choice: the paper prioritizes simulation throughput because Tg determination for 10 polymers at 17 temperatures with 3 replicas and 10,000-atom systems requires months of GPU-time even at 1.23 ns/d.

**Fitting method for Tg: hyperbolic vs. piecewise linear (Figure S10):** To verify that the Tg estimates are robust to the choice of fitting function, the paper compares hyperbolic fitting (Patrone et al. method, the primary approach) with piecewise linear fitting (using the pwlf library). The results show "nearly identical" Tg values across both methods for all polymers where fits could be obtained. This robustness check is important because Tg extraction from MD data is known to be sensitive to fitting methodology—different groups using different fitting procedures have reported systematically different Tg values for the same simulation data. The automated nature of both methods (requiring no user input to select the transition region) makes them particularly suitable for high-throughput studies.

**Dataset sampling ratios during fine-tuning (training procedure, Section C):** The Stage 2 fine-tuning uses heavily skewed sampling ratios: 10 (PolyDiss) : 0.2 (OMol25) : 1 (PolyPack) : 0.5 (PolyCrop-ORCA) : 0.05 (PolyCrop-XTB). This weighting strategy is itself an implicit ablation—the 10× weight on PolyDiss reflects the finding (from preliminary experiments, though not reported as a formal ablation in the paper) that inter-chain dissociation data is disproportionately important for recovering correct density predictions. The paper notes that "around five different [training] protocols were tested" (Section C), and the one achieving best PolyDiss validation performance was selected, confirming empirically that the PolyDiss weight matters. However, the paper does not report a formal sweep over PolyDiss weight or compare protocols that differ only in this weighting, so the sensitivity of final performance to this hyperparameter is unknown.

**Negative result: ReST^EM optimization degrades revision model (not applicable to this paper):** This standard ablation slot from the reference example does not apply to the polymer MLFF paper. Instead, the most informative **negative results** in this work are the failures of certain force field-polymer combinations:

- **MACE-OFF fails on PCTFE** (Figure S8b, density far below experimental; Table 1 footnote: "MD simulation of PCTFE failed with MACE-OFF") due to its 4.5 Å cutoff being insufficient to capture inter-chain cohesion.
- **PCFF fails on PET and PODPM** (Table 1 footnote: "MD simulations of PET and PODPM failed with PCFF") due to simulation instabilities.
- **PCFF fails for some Tg determinations** (Figure 4c: asterisks marking failed MD simulations) highlighting the limited transferability of classical force fields.
- **UMA is computationally prohibitive for routine use** (0.03 ns/d, excluded from Tg experiments), demonstrating that accuracy without speed is insufficient for practical polymer simulation.
- **The pre-trained Vivace model fails catastrophically despite comparable force errors** (Table 2, Figure S5), demonstrating that standard computational benchmarks are unreliable predictors of simulation quality—arguably the paper's most conceptually important negative result.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does Vivace actually predict experimental polymer densities from first principles?

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Vivace, trained exclusively on quantum-chemical data, achieves a density MAE of 0.04 g/cm³ across the PolyArena benchmark—better than the classical force fields PCFF (0.07 g/cm³) and OPLS3e (0.10 g/cm³). The "seen vs. unseen" split on 10 polymers shows a modest generalization gap (0.04 vs. 0.06 g/cm³), suggesting the model does not require per-polymer retraining.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate and why it matters:** The paper claims Vivace "accurately predicts experimental densities for a wide range of polymers" trained "solely on ab initio data" and explicitly contrasts this with classical FFs whose "parametrization... involves experimental data." However, three aspects of the experimental design weaken this claim of ab initio purity:

1. **The training data incorporates experimental information indirectly.** PolyPack and PolyDiss configurations are generated using EMC and PCFF—a classical force field parametrized against experimental data—for the initial structure generation. The subsequent DFT labeling refines the energies, but the **distribution of configurations** the model sees is shaped by a classical potential. If PCFF's conformational preferences bias the configurations toward experimentally plausible geometries, the training data is not purely ab initio in a strict sense—it inherits experimental information through the configuration sampling procedure. This is a practical necessity (generating configurations by random perturbation without a reasonable force field would produce mostly unphysical, high-energy structures), but it blurs the paper's claim that no experimental data was used.

2. **The PolyData datasets were generated for the specific 130 polymers in PolyArena**—there is no separation between "training polymers" and "benchmark polymers" except for the 5 out of 10 "unseen" subset. The full PolyArena set of 130 polymers has corresponding entries in PolyPack and PolyDiss, meaning the model was trained on quantum-chemical data for the exact polymers it was tested on. This is akin to training on a molecule's conformations and then evaluating on that same molecule's properties—it tests interpolation, not extrapolation. The "unseen" 5-polymer test does demonstrate true generalization, but with only 5 data points, the statistical power is weak. A more rigorous claim of ab initio predictability would require holding out a larger fraction of the 130 polymers entirely from both PolyData and training, or demonstrating that Vivace accurately predicts polymers from chemistries not represented in any training polymer.

3. **The 130-polymer benchmark itself involves selection bias.** The paper states that polymers were excluded if they "failed parametrization with PCFF using Enhanced Monte Carlo" or were "computationally challenging for our quantum-chemical methods" (Section 5.1)—approximately 10% of the initial set. This means the benchmark is enriched for polymers that are **easy to simulate**, which may correlate with polymers that are easy to predict. The reported MAE of 0.04 g/cm³ is conditional on the polymer being simulatable by the protocol; polymers that fail are simply excluded from the MAE calculation.

The claim is therefore better characterized as: Vivace, trained on quantum-chemical data for specific polymers, achieves density predictions competitive with classical force fields that were fitted to experimental data for those same polymers, and shows some evidence of generalization to chemically similar unseen polymers. This is a significant and well-supported result, but it is narrower than "predicting experimental properties ab initio for arbitrary polymers."

#### Does Vivace genuinely capture glass transitions in polymers?

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Vivace produces density-temperature curves exhibiting the two linear regimes characteristic of a glass transition, and the extracted Tg values have an MAE of 43 K across 10 polymers. The hyperbolic and piecewise linear fitting methods give consistent results (Figure S10). The paper claims this is "the first time an MLFF has been shown to capture second-order phase transitions in polymers."

**What the experiments do not demonstrate and why it matters:** Extracting Tg from MD simulations is a well-known methodological minefield. The key issue is that MD cooling rates (effectively ~10¹⁰–10¹² K/s, dictated by nanosecond simulation timescales) are orders of magnitude faster than experimental cooling rates (~10⁻²–10² K/s). Since Tg is a kinetic phenomenon—the temperature at which the system falls out of equilibrium on the timescale of the measurement—faster cooling always produces higher Tg values. This is why the paper observes that "Vivace and MACE-OFF tend to overestimate Tg values" (Section 2.4)—the simulated Tg is systematically higher than the experimental Tg because the simulation is effectively quenched.

The paper acknowledges this rate-dependence indirectly (Section 5.6) but does not attempt to correct for it or quantify its magnitude. The reported 43 K MAE therefore confounds two sources of error: genuine force field inaccuracy (the model getting the interactions wrong) and systematic cooling-rate bias (the simulation protocol being too fast). If a model perfectly reproduced the true potential energy surface, it would still overestimate Tg by an unknown amount due to the cooling-rate effect. This means the 43 K MAE cannot be interpreted as purely a measure of force field quality—it is a measure of force field quality plus protocol bias. A more informative analysis would compare Vivace, MACE-OFF, and PCFF at matched cooling rates and see if the relative ranking changes, or would apply one of the established Tg extrapolation methods (e.g., Williams-Landel-Ferry or Vogel-Fulcher-Tammann fitting) to estimate the experimental-rate Tg from high-rate simulation data.

Furthermore, the 10-polymer Tg evaluation set is small and selectively biased toward the polymers selected for detailed study (Table S1). Of the 87 polymers with experimental Tg values in PolyArena, only 10 are evaluated, and these 10 include both seen and unseen polymers but are not a random sample—they were chosen to be "diverse" representatives. The paper does not report Tg MAE for the full PolyArena set, which would be the appropriate metric for the claim of general Tg prediction capability.

Finally, the large uncertainty on PAN (211 K range, noted in Figure 4c) suggests that for some polymers, the simulation data simply do not contain enough signal to identify a glass transition. If only 9 of 10 polymers yield meaningful Tg estimates, this is a 10% failure rate on a small sample; scaling to 87 polymers might reveal a higher failure rate. The paper handles this transparently by reporting the uncertainty, but does not discuss what fraction of the 87 Tg-labeled polymers in PolyArena would be expected to yield reliable estimates.

#### Is the PolyData + Vivace combination actually superior to existing approaches?

**What the experiments demonstrate against classical force fields:** Vivace achieves better density MAE than PCFF (0.04 vs. 0.07 g/cm³) and OPLS3e (0.04 vs. 0.10 g/cm³). PCFF also suffers simulation instabilities on some polymers (PET, PODPM failed for density; additional failures for Tg) that Vivace handles stably. This is clear evidence that a well-trained MLFF can outperform classical force fields on polymer density prediction, particularly in terms of robustness across diverse chemistries.

**What the experiments demonstrate against other MLFFs:** The comparison is less definitive. UMA achieves 0.06 g/cm³ MAE on the 10-polymer subset vs. Vivace's 0.05 g/cm³, but was only evaluated on 10 polymers due to computational cost, and the paper acknowledges that the i-PI engine used for UMA is "not nearly as optimized as LAMMPS"—making the speed comparison unfair to UMA while the density comparison is fair. MACE-OFF achieves 0.09 g/cm³ across the full set, worse than Vivace's 0.04 g/cm³, but the paper's own analysis (Section 3.2) attributes MACE-OFF's underperformance primarily to its 4.5 Å cutoff rather than fundamental architectural limitations—a larger-cutoff MACE-OFF variant (the "m" or "l" models) might close much of the gap.

**The missing comparison:** The paper does not evaluate a **message-passing MLFF trained on the same PolyData** as Vivace. This is the most direct test of whether Vivace's local architecture and multi-cutoff strategy are necessary or whether a standard MACE-like or NequIP-like model trained on PolyData would achieve comparable or better results. The paper argues that local models are necessary for multi-GPU scaling, but does not demonstrate that a message-passing model trained on the same data would be less accurate—it might be more accurate, and the speed penalty might be acceptable for some use cases. Without this comparison, the contribution of Vivace's specific architecture (vs. its training data) cannot be isolated.

**What the experiments do not test:** The paper's central narrative—that PolyData specifically addresses the failure modes of generic MLFFs on polymers—is supported by the pre-trained vs. fine-tuned comparison (Table 2, Figure S5), but this compares Vivace-pretrained vs. Vivace-fine-tuned, not Vivace-fine-tuned vs. existing-MLFF-fine-tuned-on-PolyData. It is possible that fine-tuning MACE-OFF or UMA on PolyData would produce accuracy comparable to or better than Vivace's, since those models have more expressive equivariant operations (full tensor products, higher L_max). The paper demonstrates that PolyData is necessary for accurate polymer simulation (by showing that without it, Vivace fails), but does not demonstrate that Vivace is necessary—PolyData might be the more important contribution.

#### Do the computational metrics predict simulation quality?

The paper's strongest and most impactful claim is that standard computational metrics (force and stress MAEs on held-out test sets) do not reliably predict whether an MLFF will produce accurate MD simulations. The evidence in Table 2 is clear and well-controlled: two Vivace variants (pre-trained and fine-tuned) with nearly identical force and stress errors produce qualitatively different densities (0.60 vs. 0.04 g/cm³). Figure 5 provides a mechanistic explanation (underestimated inter-chain dissociation energies).

**Where the claim is strongest:** The pre-trained vs. fine-tuned Vivace comparison is an ideal controlled experiment—same architecture, same hyperparameters, same computational metrics, different training data, different simulation outcomes. This cleanly isolates the failure of the computational metrics as predictors.

**Where the claim is weaker:** The paper generalizes from this one case to the broader statement quoted in Section 5.1 of the main text, and to the implication that PolyArena-style experimental benchmarks should supplement or replace computational benchmarks in MLFF development. But the paper only demonstrates this failure mode for one specific type of error (underestimated intermolecular interactions in polymers). It is possible that for other systems (crystals, small molecules, catalyst surfaces), force MAEs are better predictors of simulation quality because the relevant interactions are captured in the test set. The paper's diagnostic is important but its generality is not established.

**What would strengthen the claim:** Comparing force MAEs and simulation quality across multiple MLFF architectures (MACE-OFF, UMA, Vivace) on the same polymer simulation task would show whether the force-MAE-ranking correlates with density-MAE-ranking across models, not just within a single model's training trajectory. The paper's Table 2 shows that MACE-OFF has force MAEs comparable to Vivace (188 vs. 183 meV/Å on PolyPack) but density MAE more than double (0.10 vs. 0.04 g/cm³), which supports the claim. However, MACE-OFF was not trained on PolyData at all, so this is confounded by training data differences. A cleaner test would be: take MACE-OFF, fine-tune it on PolyData until its force MAEs match Vivace's, and then check if its density MAE also matches—if not, the force-metric failure generalizes across architectures.

#### Execution-level concerns

- **Small sample size for generalization testing:** Only 5 polymers are held out for the "unseen" test, and only 10 polymers total are evaluated for Tg. With such small samples, a single successful or failed prediction can swing the MAE substantially. The paper reports that the unseen-polymer density MAE is 0.06 g/cm³ vs. 0.04 g/cm³ for seen polymers, but without confidence intervals on these MAEs, it is unclear whether this difference is statistically significant or just sampling noise from 5 polymers.

- **The PolyData datasets contain DFT data for all 130 PolyArena polymers.** Unlike a typical ML benchmark where the test set is strictly separated from the training data, the PolyPack and PolyDiss datasets include quantum-chemical calculations for every polymer in PolyArena. The "unseen" distinction means the polymer's configurations were excluded from training, but the model trained on chemically similar polymers from the same dataset. True extrapolation—predicting properties of a polymer whose chemistry was entirely absent from all training data (not just held out from those specific configurations)—is not evaluated.

- **No explicit training/validation/test split for PolyData.** The paper mentions validation sets (for early stopping and hyperparameter selection) but does not describe how the ~230,000 PolyPack configurations, ~50,000 PolyDiss configurations, and ~800,000 PolyCrop configurations are partitioned. If the test configurations are randomly sampled from the same MD trajectories as the training configurations, they may be correlated (nearby frames from the same trajectory), inflating apparent accuracy on the computational benchmarks.

- **The 21-step density protocol is computationally expensive and not ablated.** The protocol uses seven cycles of NVT-NVT-NPT with pressure ramping to 50,000 bar. It is possible that a simpler or shorter protocol would yield equivalent densities for many polymers, or that the high-pressure phase introduces artifacts for some polymers. The paper does not investigate sensitivity to the protocol details (number of cycles, P_max value, T_max value), which limits reproducibility and makes it unclear whether the reported accuracies are robust to protocol variations that another group might use.

- **Speed comparisons are confounded by MD engine differences.** Vivace and MACE-OFF use LAMMPS with the KOKKOS ML-IAP interface; UMA uses i-PI; PCFF uses native LAMMPS. The paper acknowledges that "a direct comparison is difficult" (Section 5.4) due to these differences, but then presents speed comparisons in the same table (Table 1) without adjusting for engine overhead. The 17× speed gap between UMA and Vivace conflates model speed with implementation efficiency. A fairer comparison would implement all models within the same MD engine or measure inference time per atom per step independently of the MD driver.

Despite these limitations, the experimental analysis is unusually rigorous for an MLFF validation paper—the inclusion of experimental benchmarks, explicit generalization testing, systematic cutoff-radius studies, bootstrap uncertainty quantification, and transparent reporting of simulation failures sets a standard that significantly exceeds the field's typical practice of reporting only computational benchmark errors. The paper's central findings—that PolyData enables accurate polymer density prediction, that inter-chain interactions require explicit treatment, and that computational metrics can fail to predict simulation quality—are well-supported by the presented experiments, even if the precise numerical claims (0.04 g/cm³ MAE, 43 K MAE) should be interpreted with an understanding of the benchmark's scope and the protocol's assumptions.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Amortized in Efficiency Claims

**The assumption or constraint:** The compute-optimal test-time scaling framework requires knowing each prompt's difficulty *before* allocating the inference budget. The paper's method for estimating difficulty—generating 2048 samples per question and scoring them with the PRM—is extraordinarily expensive. The authors explicitly acknowledge this gap in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence:** The headline efficiency gains (4× over best-of-N, Figures 4 and 8) are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. Generating 2048 samples per question to estimate difficulty consumes more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations). In a realistic deployment where difficulty estimation cost is included, the net efficiency could be substantially worse than best-of-N—the difficulty estimation alone might cost more than simply running best-of-256 on every problem without any adaptive strategy. The paper's difficulty estimation pipeline (Section 3.2: predicted difficulty bins using PRM final-answer scores averaged over 2048 samples) also requires a trained PRM, which itself requires training data and compute not accounted for in the budget. This means the 4× figure is an **upper bound on achievable efficiency under an oracle difficulty estimator**, not a realized deployment gain.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The paper does not measure this cost or include it in any budget calculation. Section 3.2 acknowledges the issue in prose but provides no experiments evaluating cheaper difficulty estimation alternatives or reporting total cost (estimation + execution). The predicted-difficulty experiments (Figures 4 and 8, where predicted difficulty bins largely overlap oracle bins) demonstrate that the PRM-based difficulty estimate *works* as a proxy for oracle difficulty, but they do not address the *cost* of obtaining that estimate.

**Mitigation status:** The paper flags cheaper difficulty estimation as "a key avenue for future work" (Section 3.2) and suggests training a classifier to predict difficulty directly from question text, but no such model is developed or evaluated. The exploration-exploitation framing in Section 3.2—treating difficulty estimation as part of the compute budget to be allocated—is mentioned but not operationalized. Until this gap is closed, the compute-optimal framework is an analysis tool rather than a deployable system.

---

### All Results Are on a Single Benchmark (MATH) with a Single Model Family (PaLM 2-S*)

**The assumption or constraint:** The entire empirical analysis—search algorithms, revision models, difficulty-dependent behavior, FLOPs-matched comparisons—is conducted on the MATH benchmark (500 test questions) using PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The authors justify this by stating they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this is an assertion, not evidence.

**The consequence:** The paper's central findings—that beam search over-optimizes the PRM on easy problems, that revisions help on easy problems but not hard ones, that test-time compute can substitute for pretraining on easy-to-medium problems—may be specific to MATH or to PaLM 2-S*. Several aspects of the findings could fail to generalize:

- **Domain dependence:** MATH consists exclusively of competition-level math problems requiring multi-step symbolic reasoning with a single correct answer. The difficulty-dependent patterns (beam search degrading easy-problem performance, sequential revisions dominating on easy problems) may not hold for code generation (where unit tests provide stronger verifier signals), logical reasoning (where reasoning chains have different structure), or open-ended generation tasks (where correctness is ambiguous and multi-dimensional). The paper's finding that verifier over-optimization is the primary bottleneck (Section 5.3) is particularly likely to be domain-dependent, since it depends on the verifier's calibration properties on the base model's output distribution.

- **Model dependence:** PaLM 2-S*'s pass@1 on MATH (~10–19% depending on prompting) places it in a specific capability regime where test-time compute has room to help. A much weaker model might have near-zero pass@1 on most problems, making test-time compute unhelpful (analogous to bin 5 in this paper). A much stronger model might have high pass@1 even on hard problems, changing which difficulty bins benefit from which strategies. The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior also depend on the base model's output distribution—a differently calibrated model might exhibit different scaling curves.

- **Scale dependence:** All experiments use models in the PaLM 2-S scale regime. The paper's FLOPs-matched comparison extrapolates to a ~14× larger model, but the compute-optimal scaling curves (Figures 4 and 8) are measured at this specific scale. It is unknown whether the 4× efficiency gain over best-of-N generalizes to much larger or smaller base models.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** None. There are no experiments on other benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, HumanEval, ARC, MMLU), other model families (e.g., LLaMA, Gemma, GPT variants), or other model scales within the PaLM family. The paper does not even report confidence intervals or cross-validation across MATH sub-domains to assess within-benchmark robustness. The test set of 500 questions, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each, means that per-bin strategy selection is based on ~50 questions per fold—a small sample from which to generalize to "problem difficulty" as a universal construct.

**Mitigation status:** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges in Section 8 that "replicating the study on code generation..., logical reasoning..., scientific QA, and open-ended generation tasks would determine which findings are universal and which are domain-specific," but this is framed as future work, not as a limitation of the current claims. The paper's title and framing ("Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute") imply generality that the experiments do not establish.

### The ~14× Larger Model Baseline Is Weaker Than It Should Be

**The assumption or constraint:** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 compares PaLM 2-S* with compute-optimal test-time scaling against a model with ~14× more parameters, but this larger model uses **parameter-only scaling** (fixed training data, following the LLaMA paradigm) rather than compute-optimal pretraining (Hoffmann et al., 2022, where both parameters and data are scaled). Additionally, the larger model uses **greedy decoding** with no test-time compute augmentation—no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search.

**The consequence:** Both design choices make the pretraining baseline weaker than it should be, and the reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining may shrink or reverse against a properly optimized baseline:

- **Parameter-only vs. Chinchilla-optimal scaling:** A model trained with 14× more total FLOPs following Chinchilla scaling laws (scaling both parameters and data) would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model of the same parameter count, because the Chinchilla model would have been trained on more data. The paper's FLOPs accounting (Section 7) uses `X = 6ND_pretrain` for training compute and `Y = 2ND_inference` for inference compute, which correctly captures the per-token cost scaling with parameters, but the assumption that the 14× parameter model achieves 14× effective capability increase may overstate the baseline's strength. The paper acknowledges this: "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute... to future work."

- **Greedy decoding for the larger model:** The larger model uses no test-time compute strategies—greedy decoding only. This is an asymmetric comparison: the smaller model gets compute-optimal test-time strategies (beam search, revisions, adaptive allocation), while the larger model gets none. A fairer comparison would give the larger model some test-time compute budget (e.g., best-of-8 or majority voting), reducing the smaller model's effective compute advantage. The paper's headline result that test-time compute can outperform a 14× larger model (Section 7, Figure 9) is conditional on the larger model using greedy decoding. If the larger model were allowed even a modest test-time budget, the crossover points in Figure 9 might shift substantially.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The paper acknowledges the parameter-only-vs-Chinchilla point in Section 7 ("We choose this setting... to future work") but does not explore how the results would change under Chinchilla-optimal pretraining. The greedy-decoding asymmetry is not discussed as a limitation. The bar charts in Figure 1 and the line plots in Figure 9 present the comparison without caveats about the baseline's optimization status.

**Mitigation status:** Partial. The paper is transparent about the parameter-only scaling choice in Section 7, framing it as "representative of a canonical approach" (referencing the LLaMA model series). However, the greedy-decoding asymmetry is not acknowledged, and no experiments with augmented baselines are reported. A simple sensitivity analysis—reporting best-of-N performance for the larger model to establish a lower bound on how much the advantage shrinks—would significantly strengthen the claim.

### Hard Problems Remain Essentially Unsolved—Test-Time Compute Has a Hard Ceiling

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper's entire framework is predicated on the assumption that the base model has **non-trivial pass@1** on the problems being solved—that correct solutions exist somewhere in the model's output distribution to be found (by search) or refined (by revision). This assumption fails for the hardest problems.

**The consequence:** Across all methods—search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations—the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5) show **near-zero improvement** regardless of compute budget. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all methods and all budgets. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%, and all three stars for the 14× larger model fall well above it, indicating that pretraining dominates for these problems. The paper states this clearly in the Section 7 takeaway: test-time compute "provides essentially zero benefit regardless of budget, meaning that some capabilities can only be acquired through pretraining, not recovered at inference time."

This is a fundamental ceiling: if the base model's pass@1 is near zero on a problem class, no amount of search or revision will help—there are no correct solutions in the proposal distribution to find or refine. The compute-optimal framework offers **no path forward for genuinely novel or out-of-distribution reasoning** that exceeds the base model's training distribution. For such problems, the only viable strategy is larger-scale pretraining or fundamentally different model architectures.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Extensive. Bin 5 results are shown separately in Figures 3 (right), 7 (right), and 9, consistently demonstrating flat scaling curves near zero for all methods. The FLOPs-matched comparison in Figure 9 shows that pretraining dramatically outperforms test-time compute on bin 5 across all R values. The paper is transparent about this limitation, explicitly stating it in the Section 7 takeaway and in the discussion of Figure 9.

**Mitigation status:** The paper acknowledges this limitation clearly and frames it as a boundary condition: test-time compute amplifies existing capability but does not create it. However, the paper does not explore *where* this boundary lies in practice—what pass@1 threshold is needed for test-time compute to provide meaningful gains? Is there a smooth transition (e.g., bin 4 shows some benefit, bin 5 shows none) or a sharper threshold? This is left as an empirical observation rather than characterized as a function of base model capability. The practical implication—that users should measure their model's pass@1 on their problem distribution before investing in test-time compute infrastructure—is not explicitly drawn.

### The Revision Model and PRM Are Studied Independently, Not Combined

**The constraint:** The paper studies two complementary mechanisms—PRM-guided search (Section 5) and iterative revisions (Section 6)—but never combines them. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges: "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions."

**The consequence:** The paper's results represent a **lower bound** on what a fully integrated system could achieve. The two mechanisms have complementary strengths that the paper's own analysis identifies: revisions improve the proposal distribution (generating better candidates, particularly on easy problems where refinement helps), while PRM search improves candidate selection (finding the best among generated candidates, particularly on medium problems where exploration helps). Applying beam search to revision model outputs—or using the PRM to guide which revisions to pursue rather than blindly generating long revision chains—could yield gains beyond either method alone. The paper's central claim that compute-optimal allocation achieves 4× efficiency over best-of-N (Figures 4 and 8) is based on selecting between search strategies or between revision ratios, but *not* on selecting between search and revisions or combining them.

Furthermore, the paper's starkest result—that revisions help easy problems while search helps medium problems (Section 5.3, Section 6)—immediately suggests a combined policy: revisions on easy problems, search on medium problems, and some hybrid on intermediate cases. The compute-optimal scaling curves in Figures 4 and 8 are computed independently for search and revisions; a combined policy that chooses between (or blends) these two mechanism families might achieve better efficiency than either alone, but this is never evaluated.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The complementary difficulty-dependent behavior is well-documented: Figure 3 (right) shows beam search degrading easy-problem performance while helping medium problems; Figure 7 (right) shows sequential revisions dominating on easy problems while a balanced ratio is optimal on medium problems. The fact that these patterns are *qualitatively different* is precisely what motivates combining them, but the paper stops at documenting the complementarity rather than exploiting it.

**Mitigation status:** Acknowledged as future work in Section 8. The paper explicitly lists combining search and revisions as a natural next step but does not provide any preliminary results or even a sketch of how such a combination would work architecturally. The practical consequence is that the paper's headline 4× efficiency gain is with respect to best-of-N—a baseline that uses neither revisions nor search. A more informative (and more challenging) baseline would be a system that already uses both mechanisms naively, which the compute-optimal framework might still improve upon. The absence of this combination also means the paper cannot claim to have identified the true Pareto frontier of test-time compute strategies—only a frontier within each mechanism family separately.

### The Revision Model Has a Systematic Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Problem

**The constraint:** The revision model is trained exclusively on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect (followed by a correct target). During this training, the model never sees a trajectory where the current answer is correct and the next step should be "do nothing" or "confirm the answer." At test time, when the model's own earlier revisions produce a correct answer, it may incorrectly "revise" it into a wrong answer.

**The consequence:** The paper reports that approximately **38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones** during sequential revision (Section 6.1). This fundamental asymmetry—the model only learns to go from wrong to right, never to recognize when it has already arrived at the right answer—means that longer revision chains have diminishing returns and can actively harm performance. The paper's mitigation (Section 6.1) is to use majority voting or verifier-based selection across the entire chain, picking the best answer from any point rather than always taking the final revision. However, this means the revision chain is treated as a *set of independent proposals* rather than as a genuine iterative improvement process—the model is not actually getting better at recognizing correct answers or knowing when to stop. It is generating diverse candidates, some of which happen to be correct, and the selection mechanism picks the best one.

This reversion rate also means that the sequential-to-parallel ratio analysis (Figure 7) is partially measuring the tradeoff between generating many independent chains (parallel) versus generating one chain where the selection mechanism has more candidates to choose from (sequential). The benefit of sequential revision may be partly due to increased *effective sample size* for the selection mechanism (a chain of length N provides N candidates to select from) rather than genuine iterative improvement. If the revision model were trained to preserve correctness (i.e., trained on "correct → correct" transitions in addition to "incorrect → correct" ones), the sequential advantage might change.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The 38% reversion rate is stated in Section 6.1 but is not derived from a formal experiment or reported with methodological detail. The paper describes the mitigation (majority/verifier-based selection across the chain) and shows that sequential revisions still outperform parallel sampling despite this issue (Figure 6, right), demonstrating that the reversion problem does not negate the benefits. However, the paper does not ablate the reversion rate—for instance, by training a revision model that includes correct-to-correct transitions and comparing revision chain quality. The ReST^EM experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) provides an indirect negative result: attempting to optimize the revision model with RL-style training caused performance to degrade, suggesting that the revision training procedure is fragile and the positive results depend on specific choices (offline data construction, edit-distance-based pairing).

**Mitigation status:** Partial. The within-chain selection mechanism (Section 6.1) mitigates the symptom (incorrect final answers) but does not fix the underlying problem (model doesn't know when to stop). The paper does not propose a training procedure modification to address this, and the ReST^EM negative result (Appendix K) suggests that naïve attempts to improve revision training can backfire. This is flagged indirectly in the discussion of future work on self-improvement loops (Section 8), but the correct-to-incorrect reversion is not itself identified as a specific open problem requiring new training methodology.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the evaluation standard for machine learning force fields from computational benchmarks to experimental validation, and in doing so exposes a diagnostic gap that the field has largely ignored: **standard accuracy metrics like force MAE do not reliably predict whether an MLFF will produce physically realistic molecular dynamics simulations.** The evidence is a controlled experiment—two versions of the same Vivace architecture with near-identical force and stress errors (Table 2: 179 vs. 183 meV/Å on PolyPack, 1.7 vs. 1.9 meV/Å³ stress) produce qualitatively different simulation outcomes (0.60 vs. 0.04 g/cm³ density MAE). This is not a subtle calibration issue; it is a categorical failure where the computational metrics are blind to the specific physical interactions—inter-chain van der Waals attraction at 4–6 Å—that determine macroscopic observables. The paper identifies a mechanistic cause (Figure 5: pre-trained model systematically underestimates inter-chain dissociation energies) and demonstrates that fixing it requires training data specifically designed to probe those interactions (PolyDiss, with a 10× sampling weight during fine-tuning).

This finding reframes how the MLFF community should think about validation. The dominant development paradigm—train on massive, chemically diverse datasets, evaluate on held-out force/energy errors, and claim transferability—is shown to be insufficient for condensed-phase soft matter systems. The paper does not argue that computational benchmarks are useless; it argues that they are **necessary but not sufficient**, and that experimental validation against bulk properties must become a standard part of the evaluation pipeline. PolyArena is offered as infrastructure for this shift, providing a ready-made benchmark of 130 polymers with experimentally measured densities and glass transition temperatures against which any MLFF can be tested.

The conceptual impact extends beyond polymers to any system where macroscopic properties emerge from the collective behavior of weakly-bound, fluctuating interfaces: liquids, solutions, biological assemblies, amorphous materials. In all these cases, the relevant physical interactions are at the tail of the radial distribution function—a small fraction of all atom pairs that contribute disproportionately to the free energy but negligibly to aggregate force error metrics. The paper provides a template for how to construct training data that targets these interactions (analogous to PolyDiss: isolate the interaction of interest, systematically vary the control parameter, label with the appropriate QM method) and how to diagnose when a model gets them wrong (plot dissociation curves or their equivalent against a QM reference).

The paper also revises the assumed relationship between cutoff radius and simulation quality. In small-molecule MLFF development, the cutoff is typically treated as a hyperparameter to be optimized for speed-accuracy tradeoffs on benchmark errors. The paper demonstrates that for polymer simulation, the cutoff becomes a **hard physical constraint**: a model with a 4.5 Å cutoff (MACE-OFF) fails catastrophically on PCTFE (density collapse to near-zero, Figure 6b), and the failure is directly traceable to the model's inability to capture the binding energy in the 5–7 Å range (Figure 6a). This finding reframes cutoff selection from an architecture optimization problem to a physics requirement: the cutoff must be large enough to encompass the attractive well of the dominant intermolecular interaction. For van der Waals-dominated soft matter, this means 6+ Å, regardless of the computational cost.

Finally, the paper reconciles a tension in the universal MLFF narrative. Recent "foundation" force fields (MACE-MP, MatterSim, UMA) have been presented as general-purpose tools applicable to any chemical system, trained on massive datasets spanning molecules, crystals, and surfaces. The paper's results both support and qualify this claim. UMA, trained on no polymer-specific data, achieves a density MAE of 0.06 g/cm³ on the 10-polymer subset—remarkable transferability. But MACE-OFF, similarly trained on diverse small-molecule data, fails on PCTFE due to its 4.5 Å cutoff. The reconciliation is that **universal MLFFs can transfer to polymers if their cutoff is sufficient**, but the cutoff requirement is domain-specific and not captured by standard benchmarks. A universal MLFF with a 4.5 Å cutoff may be "universal" for small-molecule gas-phase properties but categorically not universal for condensed-phase polymers. This implies that the definition of "universal" must include scale requirements (system size, cutoff range, simulation timescale) in addition to chemical diversity.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Train MACE-OFF or an equivalent message-passing MLFF on PolyData and compare to Vivace.** The paper demonstrates that PolyData is essential for accurate polymer density prediction (pre-trained Vivace fails, fine-tuned Vivace succeeds) but does not isolate whether Vivace's specific architecture—local rather than message-passing, simplified tensor products, multi-cutoff strategy—is necessary, or whether the training data alone is sufficient. The most direct test: take the MACE-OFF architecture (or a NequIP or MACE-MP variant), fine-tune it on PolyPack, PolyDiss, and PolyCrop using the same training protocol and sampling ratios described in Section C, and measure density and Tg MAE on PolyArena. If a message-passing model trained on PolyData matches or exceeds Vivace's 0.04 g/cm³ density MAE and 43 K Tg MAE while maintaining acceptable simulation speed (the authors could use the MACE "s" variant with an extended cutoff of 6.0–6.5 Å, which would require architectural modification), then the paper's primary contribution is the PolyData dataset and experimental validation framework rather than the Vivace architecture. If message-passing models underperform despite PolyData training—perhaps due to the receptive field being large but diffuse compared to Vivace's direct long-range interaction—then Vivace's architectural choices are validated as necessary for polymer simulation specifically.

**Develop a continuous, amortized difficulty estimator for MLFF quality.** The paper identifies that force MAEs fail to predict simulation quality, but does not provide an alternative cheap predictor. A practical follow-up would train a lightweight classifier—operating on the MLFF's predicted per-atom forces for a small set of diagnostic configurations—to predict whether a given MLFF will produce stable, accurate MD simulations for a given polymer. The training signal would be the simulation outcome (density MAE, Tg MAE, or binary stable/unstable) from running full MD with many MLFF variants on many polymers. The diagnostic configurations would be specifically designed to probe the failure modes identified in this paper: inter-chain dissociation curves (analogous to PolyDiss), compressed packings (analogous to PolyPack at high density), and stretched chain conformations. If such a classifier could achieve, say, 90% accuracy in predicting simulation failures from <100 diagnostic evaluations (compared to the >10³ force evaluations in a full MD run), it would make experimental validation scalable: run the cheap classifier on hundreds of MLFF candidates, promote only the promising ones to full MD. The PolyData datasets provide the training data for the diagnostic configurations; PolyArena provides the ground-truth simulation outcomes. This would directly address the paper's stated limitation that "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference."

**Measure cooling-rate dependence of MLFF-predicted Tg and develop extrapolation methods.** The paper's Tg predictions show systematic overestimation (Section 2.4: "Vivace and MACE-OFF tend to overestimate Tg values"), which is consistent with the known cooling-rate effect in MD simulations—simulated cooling rates of ~10¹⁰ K/s are orders of magnitude faster than experimental rates of ~10⁻² K/s, and faster cooling always produces higher Tg. The paper does not attempt to correct for this or quantify its magnitude. A direct follow-up experiment: for a single well-characterized polymer (e.g., polystyrene, where the paper reports Tg^fit = 399 K vs. experimental 373 K), run the same 21-step protocol at multiple cooling rates spanning the maximum range computationally feasible (perhaps from 10⁹ to 10¹² K/s by varying NPT simulation length). Fit the resulting Tg vs. log(cooling rate) data to a Williams-Landel-Ferry or Vogel-Fulcher-Tammann model to extrapolate to experimental cooling rates. If the extrapolated Tg matches experiment within uncertainty, this validates that the force field's only error source is the cooling-rate artifact, and the same extrapolation can be applied to other polymers. If the extrapolated Tg still differs from experiment, there are additional force field errors beyond the rate effect. This experiment would disentangle the two error sources that are currently conflated in the paper's 43 K MAE, providing a much clearer picture of MLFF accuracy for glass transition prediction.

**Extend PolyArena to polymers with explicitly reactive chemistries and measure bond-breaking accuracy.** The paper emphasizes that MLFFs "can inherently model bond-breaking and bond-forming transformations without extensive reparameterization" (Introduction) as a key advantage over classical force fields, but PolyArena evaluates only non-reactive properties (density and Tg). The natural extension is a benchmark of reactive polymer properties: thermal degradation onset temperatures, activation energies for depolymerization, cross-linking densities as a function of cure time, or mechanical failure under strain. These require MD simulations where bonds actually break and form, which classical force fields cannot model at all and reactive force fields (ReaxFF) handle with system-dependent accuracy. The paper's PolyData generation pipeline—EMC structure generation, PCFF-driven equilibration, DFT labeling—could be extended to sample reactive configurations by running high-temperature MD with a cheap reactive surrogate, extracting configurations along reaction coordinates, and labeling them with DFT at the same r²SCAN-D3 level used for PolyPack. PolyArena would be extended with experimental measurements of the corresponding reactive properties for the same 130 polymers where available. The key question is whether the same Vivace architecture, trained on this augmented PolyData, can predict reactive properties without explicit reaction-coordinate information, or whether reactivity requires architectural modifications (e.g., message-passing layers to propagate bond-breaking information beyond the local environment).

**Evaluate PolyData fine-tuning as a general strategy for adapting any MLFF to condensed-phase soft matter.** The paper shows that PolyData fine-tuning transforms a failing Vivace model into a successful one, but Vivace was pre-trained on data that overlaps with PolyData in chemistry (OMol25 and PolyCrop contain similar functional groups to the polymers in PolyArena). A stronger test of PolyData's generality: take an MLFF trained on inorganic crystals (e.g., MACE-MP-0, MatterSim, or CHGNet), which has never seen organic polymers, and fine-tune it on PolyPack and PolyDiss. If the fine-tuned model achieves density MAE comparable to Vivace's 0.04 g/cm³ on PolyArena, then PolyData is a universal adapter—it teaches any model the specific inter-chain and intra-chain interactions needed for polymer simulation, regardless of the model's pre-training domain. If performance is poor, then pre-training on organic chemistry (molecules, not just crystals) is a prerequisite, which constrains the applicability of "universal" MLFFs trained primarily on inorganic materials. This experiment would also test whether the PolyDiss + PolyPack fine-tuning protocol is sufficient to overcome the cutoff-radius limitation: if a model originally trained with a 5.0 Å cutoff can learn effective 6.5 Å-range interactions through PolyDiss fine-tuning (where those interactions are explicitly presented), then the cutoff is not a hard architectural constraint for fine-tuned models—only for zero-shot transfer.

**Quantify the statistical power of PolyArena and establish minimum benchmark size for reliable MLFF ranking.** The paper's 130-polymer benchmark is unprecedented for experimental MLFF validation, but the paper does not analyze how many polymers are needed to reliably distinguish between force fields. With density MAEs of 0.04 (Vivace), 0.07 (PCFF), and 0.10 g/cm³ (OPLS3e), are these differences statistically significant, or could they arise from the specific 130 polymers chosen? A bootstrap analysis: resample 130 polymers with replacement from the full PolyArena set, recompute MAEs for each force field, and compute the distribution of pairwise differences. If the 95% confidence interval for the Vivace-PCFF difference excludes zero, the ranking is robust. If not, PolyArena would need to be larger to make definitive claims. This analysis would also reveal whether certain polymer sub-classes (polyolefins vs. polyesters vs. fluorinated polymers) show systematically different force field rankings, which is information the current aggregate MAEs obscure. This is the type of statistical rigor that ML benchmarks (ImageNet, GLUE) routinely apply but that computational chemistry benchmarks have historically neglected. The paper's published PolyArena data makes this analysis possible without additional simulations.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**High-throughput virtual screening of polymer candidates before synthesis.** A materials company considering 500 candidate polymer structures for a new packaging application with specific density and thermal requirements (e.g., density < 1.2 g/cm³, Tg > 400 K) currently relies on chemical intuition, literature precedent, and—if resources permit—synthesis and characterization of a handful of candidates. With Vivace, each candidate can be screened computationally: generate an EMC structure, run the 21-step density protocol (~0.5 ns/d on one A100 means a density prediction per polymer in roughly 1–2 GPU-days depending on system size and protocol length), and obtain a density prediction with ~0.04 g/cm³ expected error and a Tg estimate with ~43 K expected error (if the candidate is chemically similar to PolyArena polymers). This filters the 500 candidates to perhaps 20 that pass the computational screen, which are then synthesized and measured. Even if the computational predictions are imperfect, the enrichment factor—the ratio of successful candidates in the screened set vs. the unscreened set—could dramatically reduce synthesis costs. The key numbers from the paper: 0.04 g/cm³ density MAE means the model can reliably distinguish polymers differing by >0.1 g/cm³ in density; 43 K Tg MAE means it can distinguish high-Tg from low-Tg polymers but cannot resolve differences smaller than ~50 K. The generalization results (0.06 g/cm³ on unseen polymers) suggest this workflow works for chemistries not in the training set, though the 5-polymer unseen set is too small to quantify the expected accuracy on arbitrary new chemistries.

**Replacing classical force field parameterization for specialized polymer applications.** When a research group needs to simulate a polymer containing unusual functional groups—for example, a sulfone-containing engineering thermoplastic or a siloxane-urea copolymer—they currently face a painful choice: use a classical force field with parameters guessed from similar atom types (inaccurate), spend months developing custom parameters through iterative QM calculations and experimental validation (slow and expensive), or use DFT directly (too slow for the required system sizes). Vivace offers a fourth path: run quantum-chemical calculations on the PolyData-equivalent configurations for the new polymer (PolyPack-like multi-chain packings and PolyDiss-like dissociation curves), fine-tune the pre-trained Vivace model on this polymer-specific data following the Stage 2 protocol (heavy PolyDiss weighting, multi-head training to preserve general knowledge), and then run production MD simulations. The paper's results suggest this would require orders of magnitude less QM data than training a model from scratch—the pre-trained model already knows about covalent bonding, common functional groups, and van der Waals interactions from OMol25 and PolyCrop; only the polymer-specific intermolecular interactions need to be learned. The fine-tuning cost (2 days on 4 A100 GPUs, per Section C) is trivial compared to the months of expert time for classical force field parameterization. The key unknown is how much polymer-specific PolyData is needed—the paper fine-tuned on the full PolyPack and PolyDiss for all 130 polymers simultaneously, and a single-polymer fine-tuning experiment would establish the data efficiency of this transfer learning approach.

**Accelerated degradation and recycling studies where bond breaking is essential.** A polymer recycling company developing a chemical depolymerization process needs to understand the reaction mechanism, rate-limiting steps, and product distribution when a polyester or polycarbonate is heated with a catalyst. Classical force fields cannot model the bond-breaking chemistry at all. ReaxFF can, but requires expensive reparameterization for each polymer-catalyst combination, and the accuracy is "highly system-dependent" (Introduction, citing the ReaxFF literature). Vivace, extended with reactive training data as described in the research directions above, would allow direct simulation of the depolymerization process at DFT-like accuracy. The key advantage over methods like ReaxFF is that Vivace's accuracy is systematically improvable: if the initial predictions disagree with experiment, more QM data can be added to the training set (specifically, configurations along the reaction coordinate where the model's uncertainty is highest), and the model can be retrained. This active-learning loop—run MD, identify configurations where the model is uncertain, label with DFT, retrain, repeat—is well-established in the MLFF literature but has been applied primarily to small-molecule reactions. The paper's demonstration that Vivace handles condensed-phase polymer environments (where the reacting species are embedded in a dense, fluctuating matrix of non-reacting chains) at scale makes this extension feasible. The practical metric for a recycling company: can the computational predictions reduce the number of bench-scale depolymerization experiments needed to optimize temperature, catalyst loading, and solvent, from hundreds to tens?

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions Vivace against both classical force fields (PCFF, OPLS3e) and other MLFFs (MACE-OFF, UMA), enabling a concrete decision framework grounded in its experimental results:

- **Prefer Vivace (or an MLFF trained on PolyData-like datasets) over classical force fields when:**
  - The polymer system contains chemistries for which classical force field parameters are unavailable or unreliable (new functional groups, unusual bonding patterns, elements beyond the force field's training scope). The paper demonstrates this on PolyArena's chemically diverse set where PCFF achieves 0.07 g/cm³ MAE vs. Vivace's 0.04 g/cm³.
  - Reactive chemistry (bond breaking/forming) is required, since classical force fields with fixed bonding topologies cannot model it at all. Even without reactive training data, an MLFF architecture like Vivace is a prerequisite for future reactive extensions.
  - Robustness across diverse polymers matters more than raw simulation speed. The paper shows PCFF fails entirely for some polymers (PET, PODPM in Table 1; multiple failures in Figure 4c) while Vivace handles all tested polymers stably.
  - 10–30× slower simulation speed vs. classical force fields (0.52 vs. 17.68 ns/d) is acceptable given the accuracy and transferability gains.

- **Prefer Vivace over other MLFFs (MACE-OFF, UMA) when:**
  - The system requires a cutoff of >5 Å to capture inter-chain cohesion (Figure 6). MACE-OFF with its 4.5 Å cutoff fails on PCTFE and likely on any polymer where dispersion interactions at 5–7 Å set the density.
  - Multi-GPU scaling is essential (Vivace achieves 3.93× speedup from 1 to 8 GPUs; UMA cannot use multi-GPU with its current i-PI interface and is 17× slower than Vivace even on a single GPU).
  - The polymer chemistry is within or near the training distribution of PolyArena, since Vivace was specifically trained on polymer data while MACE-OFF and UMA were not.

- **Prefer existing MLFFs (UMA or MACE-OFF) over Vivace when:**
  - The polymer is chemically simple (e.g., pure hydrocarbon polyolefins) and falls within MACE-OFF's training distribution of neutral organic molecules. MACE-OFF achieves 0.09 g/cm³ MAE on PolyArena without polymer-specific training—sufficient for many applications.
  - The cutoff requirement is modest (<5 Å) for the specific polymer and property of interest, as can be determined by running dissociation curve calculations analogous to Figure 6a before committing to full MD.
  - Zero-shot transfer to an entirely new polymer chemistry is the goal, and UMA's broader elemental coverage (>80 elements) provides coverage that Vivace's training (8 elements: H, C, N, O, F, Si, S, Cl) does not. UMA achieves 0.06 g/cm³ MAE on the 10-polymer subset without polymer training.

- **Caution for any MLFF-based approach:**
  - The simulation cost remains 10–100× higher than classical force fields. For high-throughput screening of thousands of polymers where qualitative trends are sufficient, classical FFs may be more practical despite lower accuracy.
  - The cooling-rate bias in Tg prediction (systematic overestimation) applies to all MD-based approaches regardless of force field. Tg values should be treated as relative rankings rather than absolute predictions unless cooling-rate extrapolation is applied.
  - Polymers containing elements outside Vivace's training set (and outside the PolyData elemental coverage) will require explicit training data generation and model extension—they cannot be handled by zero-shot transfer to the current Vivace checkpoint.
