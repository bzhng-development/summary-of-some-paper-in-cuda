# BIMIX: Bivariate Data Mixing Law for Language Model Pretraining

**ArXiv:** [2405.14908](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14908)

## 🎯 Pitch

You can model validation loss as a simple power-law product of a domain’s data proportion and training steps—just 5 coefficients per domain—and then solve directly for the optimal mixture, outperforming expensive data-selection baselines. What’s more, you don’t actually need to train models to set the mixture: entropy-based proxies, especially conditional entropy, already point to the same optimum. Bimix yields loss extrapolation errors under 0.2% and R² above 0.97 on unseen mixtures across SlimPajama and the Pile up to 1B parameters.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **BIMIX**, a bivariate data mixing law that models the joint scaling behavior of domain proportions and training data volume in language model pretraining, providing a systematic framework for predicting and optimizing data mixtures across diverse domains. Through experiments on the Pile (22 domains) and SlimPajama (7 domains) datasets using decoder-only transformers up to 1B parameters, BIMIX formulates per-domain validation loss as a separable function of domain proportion and training steps — capturing both the power-law decline with more data and the power-law benefit of increased domain allocation within a single equation requiring only five fitted coefficients per domain. The fitted law achieves mean relative prediction error below 0.2% when extrapolating to held-out training steps and generalizes to unseen data mixtures with R² > 0.97, while domain proportions optimized via the BIMIX framework yield superior downstream task performance compared to DoReMi, DoGE, and RegMix baselines. The paper further establishes that entropy-based measures — particularly conditional entropy — serve as computationally lightweight proxies for constructing effective data mixtures, achieving lower log-perplexity than the baseline without requiring any model training, though the law's applicability is bounded to domains and scales within the observed experimental range of proportions (0.0007 to 0.7256) and training volumes (up to ~100B tokens).

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don't Understand How Data Composition Affects LLM Training

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is that **when training large language models on data from multiple sources, we lack a principled understanding of how the mix of those sources affects the model's final performance**. Modern LLMs are trained on massive, heterogeneous corpora cobbled together from many sources — web text, code repositories, academic papers, books, Wikipedia, and specialized domain data — but the decision of *how much* of each source to include has remained largely a matter of heuristics and intuition.

This is a surprising gap in the literature given how much attention has been paid to other aspects of scaling laws. The field has thoroughly characterized how model performance scales with parameter count (Kaplan et al., 2020), with training data volume (Hoffmann et al., 2022; the Chinchilla scaling laws), and with compute budget (both pretraining and inference). Yet the third major axis — **data composition** — has remained poorly understood, even though it directly determines what knowledge and capabilities the model acquires.

The practical stakes are significant. Consider the SlimPajama dataset used in this paper: it spans seven domains ranging from code (GitHub, ~5% of the baseline mix) to web text (CommonCrawl, ~52% of the baseline). If a practitioner wants to turn the dial on coding capability, how much should they increase the GitHub proportion? If they add too much, do they degrade performance on other domains? If they add too little, do they fail to move the needle? Without a predictive framework, these questions can only be answered by expensive trial and error — training multiple models on different mixtures and comparing the results. For multi-billion parameter models trained on hundreds of billions of tokens, each such experiment represents a substantial financial and environmental cost.

### Why Heuristics and Trial-and-Error No Longer Suffice

The paper identifies three converging trends that make the data composition problem increasingly urgent:

**1. Scale amplifies the cost of bad decisions.** When training costs hundreds of thousands to millions of GPU-hours, getting the data mixture wrong the first time can mean millions of dollars in wasted compute. For a 1B-parameter model (the scale used in this paper's downstream validation), a single training run on the described infrastructure requires approximately 670 GPU-hours. For production-scale models with hundreds of billions of parameters, the cost multiplies by orders of magnitude. A predictive mixing law would allow practitioners to estimate the effect of proposed mixtures *before* committing to training runs, eliminating poor candidates at negligible cost.

**2. Multi-source datasets are the norm, not the exception.** The paper notes that datasets like the Pile (22 domains, 825 GiB), ROOTS (Laurençon et al., 2022; 1.6 TB, multilingual), and SlimPajama (7 domains, 627B tokens after deduplication) are standard building blocks for modern LLM pretraining. Each of these datasets reflects a particular curation philosophy — the Pile emphasizes diversity, SlimPajama emphasizes quality through deduplication — but neither provides guidance on whether their default proportions are optimal for any particular downstream goal. As models are increasingly trained on even larger combinations of proprietary and public data, the combinatorial complexity of choosing proportions grows, making heuristic approaches increasingly inadequate.

**3. Individual domain performance matters, not just aggregate loss.** The paper emphasizes a critical subtlety: the standard approach of measuring a single "average validation loss" across all domains provides a coarse signal that can mask important per-domain behaviors. A model might achieve excellent average loss by becoming very good at high-proportion domains (e.g., CommonCrawl web text) while performing poorly on low-proportion but important domains (e.g., code or specialized academic text). The mixing law should predict per-domain loss, enabling practitioners to make conscious tradeoffs rather than being surprised by unexpected failures on specific data types.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three broad categories of existing approaches and their limitations:

**Heuristic-based mixing (Touvron et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2020).** The dominant approach in practice is to use domain proportions based on dataset creators' intuition, the natural distribution of collected data, or simple upsampling/downsampling rules. For instance, the LLaMA model series (Touvron et al., 2023) used curated mixtures based on manual experimentation, while SlimPajama-DC (Shen et al., 2023) explored predefined configurations and reported empirical observations. The problem is that these rules lack portability: a mixture that works well for one model architecture or training regime may not transfer to another, and there is no theoretical framework for extrapolating from small-scale experiments to production-scale training. Heuristics also fail to capture interactions between domains — how increasing the proportion of one domain affects learning on others through shared model capacity.

**Computationally expensive optimization methods (Xie et al., 2023; Fan et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024).** More principled approaches have emerged, but at substantial computational cost:
- **DoReMi** (Xie et al., 2023) uses group distributionally robust optimization with iterative training of reference and proxy models. For the 280M-parameter scale used in this paper, the authors note that a single round of DoReMi training consumes 670 GPU hours — and DoReMi requires multiple such rounds (training the reference model, then the proxy model, potentially iterating). This cost scales with model size, making it prohibitive for direct application at billion-parameter scales.
- **DoGE** (Fan et al., 2023) proposes domain reweighting with generalization estimation, using a similar proxy-model-based iterative approach that requires multiple training runs.
- **RegMix** (Liu et al., 2024) formulates data mixing as a regression problem, still requiring multiple training runs at smaller scale to fit the regression model.

These methods share a fundamental limitation: **they optimize through repeated training rather than through prediction**. Each candidate mixture must be evaluated by actually training a model and measuring its performance. This makes them inherently expensive and limits the number of candidate mixtures that can be explored. As the number of domains grows, the search space expands combinatorially, and training-based optimization becomes increasingly impractical.

**Incomplete scaling law approaches (Ye et al., 2024).** A concurrent line of work attempts to extend scaling laws to the data composition problem, but with significant limitations that BIMIX explicitly addresses:

- **Ye et al. (2024)** proposed a composite exponential law to capture interactions among domains: $L_i(r_1, \ldots, r_m) = c_i + k_i \exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m} t_{ij} r_j\right)$. This formulation accounts for cross-domain effects through the interaction coefficients $t_{ij}$, but the number of fitting coefficients grows quadratically with the number of domains — $O(m^2)$ for $m$ domains. For the Pile's 22 domains, this requires fitting hundreds of coefficients, which in turn demands a large number of observational data points (the authors estimate "tens of mixtures" to fit the model). This makes the approach computationally expensive and practically infeasible for datasets with many domains.
- Furthermore, Ye et al.'s law models only domain proportions without incorporating the effect of training data volume (training steps). This limits its applicability to scenarios where models are always trained to the same token budget — it cannot answer questions about how the optimal mixture changes as training budget scales.

**The missing piece: a bivariate, scalable mixing law.** The gap that BIMIX fills is precise: the field needs a mixing law that (1) jointly models the effects of domain proportion *and* training data volume, (2) scales linearly with the number of domains rather than quadratically, making it applicable to datasets with many domains, and (3) can be fitted from a small number of observational training runs, enabling practical cost-bounded mixture optimization.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

BIMIX positions itself **not** as yet another mixture optimization algorithm (competing with DoReMi or DoGE), but rather as a **predictive framework** that enables mixture optimization as a downstream application. This distinction is crucial for understanding the paper's contribution:

**From optimization to prediction.** Prior work asks: "Given a fixed compute budget, what is the best domain mixture?" — and answers it by training multiple models. BIMIX asks: "Given observations from a few training runs, what loss would a model achieve on a proposed mixture?" — and answers it through functional form fitting and extrapolation. The predictive approach is inherently more efficient because the fitted law can evaluate an arbitrary number of candidate mixtures at near-zero cost, while training-based methods must pay the cost of model training for each candidate evaluated.

**The bivariate formulation as the key insight.** The paper's central conceptual move is to formulate the mixing law as a bivariate function $L_i(r_i, s)$ that depends on *both* the proportion allocated to domain $i$ and the total training steps $s$. This is justified by a foundational observation: in multi-domain training, the amount of data seen from domain $i$ is $|D| \times r_i \times s$ (total dataset size × proportion × training duration). Changing the mixture proportion $r_i$ or changing the training budget $s$ are two different ways of controlling how much data from domain $i$ the model sees — and they interact. A mixing law that captures this interaction can answer practical questions that a proportions-only law cannot: "If I double my training budget, how should I adjust my domain proportions?" or "Given a fixed mixture, what accuracy can I expect after 100B vs. 200B tokens?"

**Disentangled scaling as the empirical foundation.** The paper builds its functional form through an empirical observation that is both simple and powerful: the effects of domain proportion and training steps appear to be **multiplicatively separable**. When plotting validation loss against training steps at fixed domain proportions (Figure 1), the curves approximately shift by a constant factor in log space — suggesting a multiplicative scaling factor $f(r_i)$ applied to a base scaling function of training steps. When plotting validation loss against domain proportions at fixed training steps (Figure 2), the lines again shift by a constant factor — suggesting a multiplicative scaling factor $g(s)$ applied to a base scaling function of proportions. These two observations, taken together, motivate the separable form:

$$L_i(r_i, s) = \frac{A_i}{r_i^{\alpha_i}} \left( \frac{B_i}{s^{\beta_i}} + C_i \right)$$

where $A_i/r_i^{\alpha_i}$ captures the proportion-dependent scaling (a power law with exponent $\alpha_i$) and $B_i/s^{\beta_i} + C_i$ captures the step-dependent scaling (a power law with irreducible loss $C_i$). Only five coefficients per domain are needed, scaling as $O(m)$ rather than $O(m^2)$.

**Entropy proxies as a practical bridge.** The paper addresses a chicken-and-egg problem inherent to its framework: fitting BIMIX requires observational data from multiple training runs on different mixtures, but which mixtures should be used? The answer needs to be computationally cheap — otherwise the fitting process inherits the very cost the law is meant to avoid. The paper's proposal to use entropy-based measures (Shannon entropy, conditional entropy, joint entropy, von Neumann entropy) as mixing proxies is pragmatic: these can be computed directly from the tokenized dataset with "negligible overhead" during tokenization, requiring no model training. The idea is that entropy quantifies the information content or uncertainty of each domain's data distribution, and domains with higher entropy (more diverse or unpredictable data) may benefit from higher sampling proportions. The paper doesn't claim entropy is optimal — rather, it demonstrates empirically that entropy-driven mixtures outperform the default baseline (Figure 5) and, critically, that BIMIX fitted on these entropy-driven mixtures can accurately predict the performance of other mixtures (Baseline, DoReMi), enabling cost-bounded mixture evaluation.

**Connection to broader scaling law research.** The paper explicitly positions itself within the scaling laws tradition established by Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022), but extends it along a new dimension. Prior scaling laws model how a single scalar (average validation loss) varies with a single input variable (model size, data volume, compute). BIMIX models how a vector of per-domain losses varies with two input variables (proportions and steps, with the constraint that proportions sum to one). This is a natural but non-trivial extension: the unit-sum constraint on proportions means the variables are not independent, and the per-domain formulation means the law must capture the interactions (through shared training and model capacity) that cause increasing one domain's proportion to affect others' losses. The paper's approach of modeling each domain's loss independently as a function of its own proportion — rather than attempting to model cross-domain interaction terms — is a modeling choice that trades theoretical completeness for practical scalability, and the empirical results validate that this choice works well in practice.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

BIMIX is a **predictive model** — specifically, a system of fitted mathematical equations — that takes as input a proposed data mixture (how much of each domain to include) and a target training budget (how many steps to train), and outputs the expected validation loss for each domain. It solves the problem of **blind mixture selection** by replacing expensive trial-and-error training runs with closed-form predictions: given observations from training a small number of cheap reference mixtures for a limited number of steps, BIMIX estimates the coefficients of its functional form and then extrapolates to predict what would happen under *any* proposed mixture *at any training duration* — without requiring a single additional GPU-hour.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The BIMIX system has four major components, arranged in a pipeline from data to predictions:

1. **Candidate Mixture Construction**: A small set of data mixtures (typically 4, built from entropy-based proxies) is constructed from the raw dataset. Each mixture assigns a proportion vector $\vec{r} = (r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_m)$ satisfying the unit-sum constraint $\sum_{i=1}^m r_i = 1$. These mixtures determine how many tokens from each domain the model sees during training.

2. **Observational Data Collection**: For each candidate mixture, a small 280M-parameter reference model is trained for up to 200,000 steps (~100B tokens). Validation losses for every domain are recorded at regular intervals (every 5B tokens, yielding up to 20 data snapshots per mixture). This produces a collection of tuples $\langle r_i, s, L_i \rangle$ — the domain proportion, the training step, and the observed validation loss — that serve as the raw material for fitting.

3. **Coefficient Fitting**: The BIMIX functional form is fitted to the collected observational data points using the Trust Region Reflective algorithm (a numerical optimization procedure for bounded, non-linear least-squares problems). This produces five fitted coefficients per domain: $A_i$, $\alpha_i$, $B_i$, $\beta_i$, and $C_i$. Once fitted, the law is a self-contained predictive engine.

4. **Prediction and Optimization**: The fitted law can be evaluated at any proposed domain proportion vector $\vec{r}$ and any training step $s$ to predict the per-domain validation losses. This enables (a) *mixture evaluation*: predicting the loss trajectory of an untested mixture without training, and (b) *mixture optimization*: solving a constrained minimization problem to find the proportion vector that minimizes the predicted average loss across domains at a target training budget.

Information flows unidirectionally: raw data → entropy-based mixtures → training runs → fitted coefficients → predictions/optimized mixtures. No component requires iteration or feedback.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal definition of BIMIX as a system of equations (Section 3.1 of the paper), including the per-domain functional form and the unit-sum constraint. This establishes the mathematical object being fitted.

- **Second**, the empirical justification for the functional form's separability — how the paper observes that training-step scaling and proportion scaling appear to factor multiplicatively (Section 3.2 of the paper). This explains *why* the equation takes the particular shape it does, rather than being an arbitrary curve-fitting exercise.

- **Third**, the coefficient fitting procedure: what data is collected, how the fitting is formulated as an optimization problem, what algorithm is used, and what the practical data requirements are.

- **Fourth**, the candidate mixture construction via entropy proxies (Section 4 of the paper): how Shannon entropy, conditional entropy, joint entropy, and von Neumann entropy are computed from the raw dataset, how they are converted into mixture proportions, and why they are chosen as the reference mixtures for fitting.

- **Fifth**, how the fitted law is used for prediction (Section 5.2) and optimization (Section 5.3): the extrapolation to unseen mixtures, the Lagrange multiplier setup for constrained optimization, and the transfer from small reference models to larger 1B-parameter models.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical scaling law paper** whose core idea is that the per-domain validation loss of a language model trained on a multi-domain dataset can be described as a separable, bivariate function of that domain's allocated proportion and the total training steps, and that this functional form enables both accurate loss extrapolation and principled mixture optimization with linear — rather than quadratic — scaling in the number of domains.

#### The BIMIX Functional Form

The proposed mixing law models the validation loss of a language model on the $i$-th domain of an $m$-domain dataset as a function of two input variables: $r_i$, the proportion of training data allocated to domain $i$, and $s$, the number of training steps (a proxy for total training data volume). The formulation is:

$$L_i(r_i, s) = \frac{A_i}{r_i^{\alpha_i}} \left( \frac{B_i}{s^{\beta_i}} + C_i \right) \quad \text{for } i = 1, 2, \ldots, m$$

where $A_i$ is the domain-specific amplitude (a scaling factor controlling the overall magnitude of loss for domain $i$), $\alpha_i$ is the domain-specific exponent governing how loss decreases as the proportion $r_i$ increases (a power-law exponent for the proportion effect), $B_i$ is the domain-specific scaling factor for the training-step-dependent component (governing how rapidly loss declines with more training), $\beta_i$ is the domain-specific exponent governing the rate of loss decline with training steps $s$ (a power-law exponent for the data volume effect), and $C_i$ is the domain-specific irreducible loss term (the minimum achievable loss on domain $i$ — an asymptotic lower bound that no amount of additional data or proportion increase can overcome).

**What each sub-expression represents.** The term $A_i / r_i^{\alpha_i}$ captures the effect of domain proportion: as $r_i$ increases (the model sees a larger fraction of its training data from domain $i$), the validation loss on domain $i$ decreases according to a power law. Specifically, doubling the proportion $r_i$ reduces this multiplicative factor by a factor of $2^{\alpha_i}$. Intuitively, this reflects the fact that a domain receiving more training tokens gets better coverage and the model allocates more capacity to learning its patterns. The term $(B_i / s^{\beta_i} + C_i)$ captures the effect of total training volume: the loss starts high (when $s$ is small) and decays toward the irreducible floor $C_i$ as training continues, following a power law with exponent $\beta_i$. The combination is multiplicative: the proportion-dependent factor scales the entire step-dependent curve up or down.

**Why multiplicatively separable.** The paper's critical empirical finding (Section 3.2) is that these two effects appear to factor cleanly. When loss-versus-steps curves are plotted for different fixed proportions $r_i$ (Figure 1), the curves differ by a constant multiplicative shift in log-log space — meaning they are the same shape curve, just multiplied by a proportion-dependent factor. When loss-versus-proportion curves are plotted for different fixed training steps $s$ (Figure 2), the curves similarly differ by a multiplicative shift — the same power-law shape in $r_i$, scaled by a step-dependent factor. These two observations jointly imply a separable form: $L_i(r_i, s) = f(r_i) \cdot g(s)$. The specific choices $f(r_i) = A_i / r_i^{\alpha_i}$ and $g(s) = B_i / s^{\beta_i} + C_i$ are informed by established scaling law literature, where validation loss often follows power laws with irreducible terms for training data volume (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022).

**The vectorized system.** BIMIX is not a single equation but a system of $m$ related equations, one per domain:

$$\vec{L}(\vec{r}, s) = \left( L_1(r_1, s), L_2(r_2, s), \ldots, L_m(r_m, s) \right)$$

subject to the constraint:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{m} r_i = 1$$

This constraint encodes the fundamental interdependence of data mixing: you cannot increase one domain's proportion without decreasing others. The proportion vector $\vec{r}$ lives on the $(m-1)$-dimensional simplex. When the law predicts that increasing $r_i$ reduces $L_i$, it is implicitly assuming that the total training data is reallocated from other domains — the other domains' proportions decrease, and their losses increase according to their own BIMIX equations. The system-of-equations formulation makes these tradeoffs explicit and quantifiable.

**Why five coefficients per domain (linear scaling) versus alternatives.** The most important design choice in BIMIX is that the loss for domain $i$ depends *only* on that domain's own proportion $r_i$, not on the proportions of other domains $(r_1, \ldots, r_{i-1}, r_{i+1}, \ldots, r_m)$. This means the number of fitted coefficients scales as $5m$, i.e., linearly with the number of domains. An alternative formulation that models cross-domain interactions — for instance, by having domain $i$'s loss depend on all proportions through interaction terms — would require $O(m^2)$ coefficients. The concurrent exponential law by Ye et al. (2024), $L_i(r_1, \ldots, r_m) = c_i + k_i \exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^m t_{ij} r_j\right)$, requires $m^2 + 2m$ coefficients for $m$ domains and one fixed training step. For the Pile's 22 domains, this is approximately 528 coefficients versus BIMIX's 110. The BIMIX formulation is a deliberate simplification: it assumes that cross-domain interactions (through shared model capacity, gradient interference, etc.) are either second-order effects or captured implicitly through the unit-sum constraint (since changing $r_i$ forces changes in other $r_j$). The empirical results validate that this simplification is sufficient to achieve high predictive accuracy.

#### Observing the Separability (Empirical Foundation)

Section 3.2 of the paper provides the empirical motivation for the separable form by systematically disentangling the two input variables. This is not a theoretical derivation but a pattern-finding exercise on real training data.

**Scaling training steps under fixed proportions.** When the domain proportion $r_i$ is held constant and the model is trained for an increasing number of steps $s$, the validation loss on domain $i$ exhibits a characteristic power-law decline with an irreducible floor — a pattern well-documented in the scaling laws literature. In the paper's notation, this is captured by:

$$L_i(s \mid r_i) = \frac{\tilde{B}_i}{s^{\tilde{\beta}_i}} + \tilde{C}_i$$

where $\tilde{B}_i$ is the effective scaling factor at the given proportion $r_i$, $\tilde{\beta}_i$ is the effective power-law exponent, and $\tilde{C}_i$ is the irreducible loss. Crucially, the paper observes that when the same experiment is repeated at different fixed proportions $r_i$, the resulting loss curves have the *same shape* but differ by a vertical shift in log-log space. Mathematically, this means the loss curve at proportion $r_j$ can be obtained by multiplying the loss curve at proportion $r_i$ by a constant factor $F_j$:

$$L_j(s \mid r_j) = L_i(s \mid r_i) \cdot F_j$$

Equivalently, in log space: $\log L_j(s \mid r_j) = \log L_i(s \mid r_i) + \log F_j$. This additive shift in log space corresponds to a multiplicative scaling in linear space. Generalizing this pattern, the proportion-dependent factor can be expressed as a function $f(r_i)$ applied to a base scaling function of training steps:

$$L_i(s \mid r_i) = L_{\text{base}}(s \mid r_i) \cdot f(r_i)$$

Comparing with the power-law form for $s$, the paper identifies the following relationships: $\tilde{B}_i = B_i \cdot f(r_i)$, $\tilde{C}_i = C_i \cdot f(r_i)$, and $\tilde{\beta}_i = \beta_i$ (the exponent is independent of proportion). This means the proportion-dependent factor $f(r_i)$ scales both the learnable component $B_i/s^{\beta_i}$ and the irreducible floor $C_i$ by the same multiplicative amount.

**Scaling domain proportions under fixed training steps.** Viewed from the complementary perspective, when the training step $s$ is held constant and the domain proportion $r_i$ is varied, the validation loss exhibits a power-law relationship:

$$L_i(r_i \mid s) = \frac{\tilde{A}_i}{r_i^{\tilde{\alpha}_i}}$$

where $\tilde{A}_i$ is the effective amplitude at the given training step $s$ and $\tilde{\alpha}_i$ is the power-law exponent. The paper observes that curves at different fixed steps $s$ again differ by a multiplicative shift — a collection of parallel lines in log-log space. The loss curve at step $s_k$ relates to the curve at step $s_j$ by a constant factor $G_k$:

$$L_k(r_i \mid s_k) = L_j(r_i \mid s_j) \cdot G_k$$

Generalizing, the step-dependent factor becomes a function $g(s)$:

$$L_i(r_i \mid s) = L_{\text{base}}(r_i \mid s) \cdot g(s)$$

Identifying terms with the proportion power law yields: $\tilde{A}_i = A_i \cdot g(s)$ and $\tilde{\alpha}_i = \alpha_i$ (the exponent is independent of training step).

**The synthesis.** From the $s$-perspective, the multiplicative proportion factor $f(r_i)$ is identified as the power law $1 / r_i^{\alpha_i}$. From the $r_i$-perspective, the multiplicative step factor $g(s)$ is identified as the power law $B_i / s^{\beta_i} + C_i$. The multiplicative nature of both perspectives forces the combined form to be the product:

$$L_i(r_i, s) = f(r_i) \cdot g(s) = \frac{A_i}{r_i^{\alpha_i}} \cdot \left( \frac{B_i}{s^{\beta_i}} + C_i \right)$$

The fact that both perspectives independently converge on a multiplicative structure is the paper's key empirical justification — it is not an assumption but a consistent observation across domains and datasets. This is the "strong interpretability" the paper claims for its formulation.

#### Coefficient Fitting Procedure

Once the functional form is specified, the practical challenge is estimating the coefficients $A_i, \alpha_i, B_i, \beta_i, C_i$ for each domain $i$ from limited observational data.

**Data collection protocol.** The paper trains models on $k$ different candidate data mixtures, where $k$ is deliberately kept small (typically 4, using the entropy-driven mixtures described below). For each mixture, a 280M-parameter decoder-only transformer (12 decoder blocks, 768-dimensional embeddings, 12 attention heads, 4× MLP hidden size — matching the DoReMi 280M architecture from Xie et al., 2023) is trained for up to 200,000 update steps with a global batch size of 512, corresponding to approximately 100 billion tokens. Validation losses on all $m$ domains are recorded every 5 billion tokens, yielding up to 20 evaluation snapshots per mixture (the symbol $n$ denotes this number of snapshots). The training uses AdamW optimizer with $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.99$, $\epsilon = 1 \times 10^{-8}$, weight decay $0.01$, and an initial learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-3}$ decaying exponentially by a factor of $10\times$ over the course of training. All runs use the GPT-NeoX tokenizer with a vocabulary size of 50,277 and bfloat16 mixed precision across eight NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs.

**The observational dataset.** The data collection produces up to $m \times n \times k$ tuples of the form $\langle r_i, s, L_i \rangle$, where $r_i$ is the proportion allocated to domain $i$ in the current mixture, $s$ is the training step at which the loss was measured, and $L_i$ is the observed validation loss. For the Pile dataset ($m = 22$, $n \approx 20$, $k = 4$), this yields approximately 1,760 data points. For SlimPajama ($m = 7$, $n \approx 20$, $k = 4$), this yields approximately 560 data points. Each data point represents a specific domain at a specific training step under a specific mixture.

**The fitting objective.** Fitting BIMIX means finding coefficient values that minimize the discrepancy between the law's predictions and the observed losses. Formally, this is a non-linear least-squares problem:

$$\min_{A_i, \alpha_i, B_i, \beta_i, C_i} \sum_{\text{all observations}} \left( L_i^{\text{observed}} - \frac{A_i}{r_i^{\alpha_i}} \left( \frac{B_i}{s^{\beta_i}} + C_i \right) \right)^2$$

This is solved for all coefficients simultaneously across all domains. The optimization is subject to natural constraints: $A_i, B_i > 0$ (the loss should be positive), $\alpha_i, \beta_i > 0$ (increasing proportion or training steps should decrease loss), and $C_i \geq 0$ (the irreducible loss is non-negative). The exponents $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$ can also be bounded above to prevent unrealistic extrapolation behavior.

**The Trust Region Reflective algorithm.** The paper employs the Trust Region Reflective (TRF) algorithm (Branch et al., 1999), as implemented in SciPy (Virtanen et al., 2020), to solve this bounded non-linear least-squares problem. TRF is a gradient-based optimization method suitable for large-scale problems with bound constraints. It works by iteratively constructing a quadratic approximation (a "trust region model") of the objective function around the current parameter estimate, solving the constrained quadratic subproblem within a region where the approximation is trusted to be accurate, and updating the parameter estimate if the actual improvement matches the predicted improvement. If the actual improvement is poor, the trust region is shrunk; if it is good, the region is expanded. This algorithm was chosen because it handles the bound constraints naturally (reflecting solutions against constraint boundaries rather than using penalty methods), converges robustly for non-linear problems, and is available in a well-tested implementation.

**Data requirements and complexity scaling.** Because BIMIX requires only five coefficients per domain, the total number of unknowns scales as $5m$, which is $110$ for the Pile's 22 domains and $35$ for SlimPajama's 7 domains. With 1,760 and 560 observational data points respectively, the system is well-overdetermined, allowing robust fitting from only $k = 4$ candidate mixtures. By contrast, Ye et al.'s (2024) exponential law requires $O(m^2)$ coefficients — approximately $484$ for the Pile — which is substantially underdetermined with only 4 mixtures, necessitating "tens of mixtures" for stable fitting. This linear-versus-quadratic complexity difference is not merely a convenience; it is what makes BIMIX *practically feasible* for datasets with many domains.

**Why not use simpler fitting methods.** One might ask why the paper doesn't use a simpler approach, such as fitting the proportion-dependent and step-dependent components separately in two stages. The answer is that the coefficients interact: the proportion scaling exponent $\alpha_i$ affects how strongly the proportion modulates the entire loss curve, including the step-dependent part. Fitting separately would require assuming one component is known while estimating the other, leading to suboptimal joint estimates. Simultaneous fitting ensures the coefficients are consistent with all observations.

#### Candidate Mixture Construction via Entropy Proxies

A central practical question for BIMIX is: **which candidate mixtures should be used to collect the observational data for fitting?** The mixtures must be diverse enough to probe the proportion-loss relationship across a meaningful range of $r_i$ values, but constructing them should not require expensive optimization (which would defeat the purpose of the predictive law). The paper's solution is to use **entropy measures** computed directly from the raw dataset as computationally cheap proxies for domain importance.

**Entropy as a domain quality signal.** The intuition is that domains with higher entropy — greater uncertainty, more diverse content, richer linguistic structure — are more informative for training and may benefit from higher sampling proportions. Entropy quantifies the unpredictability of the data distribution: a domain of identical repeated tokens has zero entropy (completely predictable, nothing to learn), while a domain with uniformly distributed tokens across a large vocabulary has high entropy (difficult to predict, rich signal). By normalizing entropy values across domains and converting them to proportions, the paper constructs mixtures that are plausibly better than the default baseline without any model training.

**The four entropy measures.** The paper computes four distinct entropy metrics, each capturing a different aspect of the data distribution (detailed in Appendix A of the paper):

**1. Shannon Entropy (SE).** For a domain $D_i$ tokenized into sequences, let $X_i$ be the set of all unique tokens appearing in that domain, and let $P(x)$ be the empirical probability (relative frequency) of token $x$ in the domain. The Shannon entropy is:

$$H_i(D_i) = -\sum_{x \in X_i} P(x) \log P(x)$$

where $P(x) = \frac{\text{count}(x)}{\sum_{x' \in X_i} \text{count}(x')}$ is estimated by counting token occurrences during tokenization. This measures the average information content per token — higher values indicate a more diverse vocabulary distribution. A domain dominated by a few very frequent tokens (e.g., code with repeated keywords) has lower SE; a domain with a flat token distribution (e.g., diverse natural language prose) has higher SE.

**2. Conditional Entropy (CE).** This extends SE to capture sequential dependencies — the uncertainty of the next token given the current token. For token sequences of length $T$, let $X_i^{(t)}$ be the set of tokens at position $t$ and $X_i^{(t+1)}$ be the set at position $t+1$, both drawn from domain $D_i$. The joint probability $P(x, x')$ is the empirical frequency of observing token $x$ at position $t$ immediately followed by token $x'$ at $t+1$, and the conditional probability $P(x' \mid x) = P(x, x') / P(x)$ is the transition probability. The conditional entropy is:

$$H_i(X_i^{(t+1)} \mid X_i^{(t)}) = -\sum_{x \in X_i^{(t)}} \sum_{x' \in X_i^{(t+1)}} P(x, x') \log P(x' \mid x)$$

**What it computes.** This measures the average uncertainty of predicting the next token given the current one — essentially, the inherent difficulty of next-token prediction in this domain. A domain where each token deterministically determines the next (like a fixed template) has CE near zero; a domain with highly unpredictable transitions (like free-form conversation) has high CE. The paper notes that CE can be computed "seamlessly integrated into the tokenization process with negligible overhead" because it requires only counting bigram frequencies during tokenization.

**3. Joint Entropy (JE).** This measures the uncertainty of consecutive token pairs jointly, without conditioning:

$$H_i(X_i^{(t)}, X_i^{(t+1)}) = -\sum_{x \in X_i^{(t)}} \sum_{x' \in X_i^{(t+1)}} P(x, x') \log P(x, x')$$

**What it computes.** JE combines the uncertainty of the unigram distribution and the transition distribution into a single number. It is always at least as large as the maximum of SE and CE, and more strongly penalizes domains where both the vocabulary is diverse and transitions are unpredictable. Domains with structured but diverse content (like academic papers with specialized terminology and complex syntax) might score highly on JE.

**4. Von Neumann Entropy (VNE).** This measure originates in quantum statistical mechanics and has been recently adapted for dataset diversity quantification (Friedman & Dieng, 2023). For each domain $D_i$ with $N$ text samples (after chunking into sequences of 1024 tokens), each sample is embedded into a 300-dimensional vector using FastText (Grave et al., 2018). A density matrix $\rho_i \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is constructed as $\rho_i = K / N$, where the kernel matrix $K$ has entries $K_{jk} = \kappa(v_j, v_k)$ for a positive semi-definite kernel function $\kappa$ (in practice, the normalized inner product of the FastText embeddings), with the constraint $\kappa(v_j, v_j) = 1$. The von Neumann entropy is:

$$H_i(\rho_i) = -\text{Tr}(\rho_i \log \rho_i) = -\sum_{k=1}^{N} \lambda_k \log \lambda_k$$

where $\lambda_k$ are the eigenvalues of $\rho_i$ and $\text{Tr}$ denotes the matrix trace. This is the quantum analog of Shannon entropy applied to the density matrix representation of the dataset.

**What it computes.** The eigenvalues $\lambda_k$ can be interpreted as the probabilities of different "modes" or "states" of the dataset when viewed as a quantum ensemble. If all samples are very similar (high pairwise similarity), the density matrix is approximately rank-1 with one dominant eigenvalue near 1 and others near 0, yielding low VNE. If samples are highly diverse (low pairwise similarity), eigenvalues are more evenly distributed, yielding high VNE. VNE thus captures diversity in the semantic embedding space rather than in the token distribution space, providing a complementary signal to SE, CE, and JE.

**Why compute entropy in these four variants rather than just one?** Each measure captures a different aspect of the data. SE captures unigram diversity (vocabulary breadth); CE captures sequential predictability (syntactic complexity); JE captures combined unigram and bigram uncertainty; VNE captures semantic diversity in embedding space. The paper treats them as four candidate proxies and compares their effectiveness experimentally (Section 5.4, Figure 5), finding that all four outperform the baseline and that CE consistently achieves the lowest log-perplexity. Using multiple entropy measures also provides diversity in the candidate mixtures used for fitting BIMIX — fitting on CE, SE, JE, and VNE mixtures ensures the observational data spans a range of proportion vectors, which improves the fitted law's ability to generalize to unseen mixtures.

**From entropy values to mixture proportions.** For each entropy measure, the raw entropy value $H_i$ is computed per domain. These values are then converted into a proportion vector that sums to 1 using the exponential normalization (softmax):

$$r_i = \frac{e^{H_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m} e^{H_j}}$$

**Why exponential normalization rather than simple linear normalization?** Linear normalization ($r_i = H_i / \sum_j H_j$) would produce proportions directly proportional to entropy, but the exponential normalization amplifies differences between domains — domains with slightly higher entropy receive disproportionately higher proportions. This is consistent with the intuition that domains with higher uncertainty should receive greater emphasis, and the exponential mapping ensures the proportions span a useful range. The paper does not explore alternative normalization schemes (e.g., temperature-scaled softmax, power-law normalization), leaving this as an implicit design choice.

**The practical advantage of entropy proxies.** Computing entropy requires only tokenization (for SE, CE, JE) or embedding (for VNE) — no model training, no gradient computation, no hyperparameter tuning. The paper states this can be done "with negligible overhead" during the standard data preprocessing pipeline. This is in stark contrast to DoReMi, which requires 670 GPU-hours per round of iterative proxy model training, or DoGE, which requires similar computational investment. The entropy proxies thus provide a **training-free** method for constructing reference mixtures for BIMIX fitting, making the entire pipeline — from raw data to fitted coefficients to optimized mixture — computationally bounded at the cost of the small reference training runs plus fitting.

#### Prediction: Estimating Unseen Mixtures Without Training

Once BIMIX coefficients are fitted from the observational data (the four entropy-driven mixtures), the law can predict the validation loss trajectory of any proposed mixture — including mixtures that were never used for training — at any training step $s$. This is the core value proposition for practitioners: the ability to evaluate candidate mixtures without spending GPU-hours training on each one.

**The prediction procedure.** Given a fitted BIMIX (with coefficients $\hat{A}_i, \hat{\alpha}_i, \hat{B}_i, \hat{\beta}_i, \hat{C}_i$ for each domain $i$) and a proposed mixture vector $\vec{r} = (r_1, \ldots, r_m)$ satisfying the unit-sum constraint, the predicted validation loss on domain $i$ after $s$ training steps is simply the evaluation of the functional form:

$$\hat{L}_i(r_i, s) = \frac{\hat{A}_i}{r_i^{\hat{\alpha}_i}} \left( \frac{\hat{B}_i}{s^{\hat{\beta}_i}} + \hat{C}_i \right)$$

This requires only arithmetic operations — no model inference, no sampling, no additional fitting. The computational cost of evaluating the law at a single $(r_i, s)$ pair is essentially zero (a few floating-point operations), enabling rapid scanning of thousands of candidate mixtures and training durations.

**Validation protocol in the paper.** To validate BIMIX's predictive accuracy, the paper uses a held-out evaluation setup (Section 5.2). It fits BIMIX on the four entropy-driven mixtures (SE, CE, JE, VNE) and their associated training trajectories (losses at all recorded training steps). It then predicts the loss trajectories of the Baseline mixture (the dataset's default proportions) and the DoReMi mixture (proportions optimized by Xie et al., 2023) — mixtures that were explicitly *excluded* from the fitting data. The predicted losses are compared against the actual losses observed when models were trained on those mixtures.

**The goodness-of-fit metric: $R^2$.** The quantitative assessment uses the coefficient of determination, defined for each domain $i$ as:

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{\text{res}}}{SS_{\text{tot}}}$$

where $SS_{\text{res}} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (\log y_j - \log \hat{y}_j)^2$ is the residual sum of squares (the sum of squared differences between observed and predicted losses on a logarithmic scale, computed across the $n$ evaluation snapshots at different training steps), and $SS_{\text{tot}} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (\log y_j - \overline{\log y})^2$ is the total sum of squares (the variance of the observed log-losses around their mean). The logarithmic transformation is important: it reduces the influence of large absolute errors at early training steps (where loss is high) and makes the metric more sensitive to proportional rather than absolute prediction accuracy, which is more relevant for comparing model quality across domains.

**Interpreting $R^2$.** An $R^2$ of $1.0$ indicates perfect prediction (the law captures all variance in the observed data); an $R^2$ of $0.0$ indicates prediction no better than always guessing the mean; negative values indicate prediction worse than the mean. The log-space computation means that an $R^2$ of $0.97$ (the mean for the Pile Baseline mixture in Table 2) corresponds to explaining 97% of the variance in log-loss — a very strong fit, implying that the multiplicative separable form captures nearly all the systematic variation in the held-out mixture's training trajectory.

**Why log-space $R^2$ for scaling law evaluation?** Scaling law predictions are typically evaluated in log-space because the laws themselves operate multiplicatively — an error of 0.1 in natural log space corresponds to a ~10% relative error in predicted loss, which is more interpretable than an absolute error of (say) 0.3 in raw loss space when the baseline losses range from 2.5 to 4.0 across domains. The log transformation also aligns with the visualization in Figures 1 and 2, where both axes are logarithmic.

#### Optimization: Finding the Best Domain Proportions

The predictive capability of BIMIX enables a natural downstream application: **constrained numerical optimization to find the domain proportion vector that minimizes a desired objective**, typically the average predicted validation loss across all domains at a target training step.

**The optimization problem.** The objective is formally stated as minimizing the unweighted average of per-domain predicted losses:

$$\vec{r}^* = \arg\min_{r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_m} \bar{L}(\vec{r}, s) = \arg\min_{r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} L_i(r_i, s)$$

subject to the simplex constraint:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{m} r_i = 1, \quad r_i \geq 0 \text{ for all } i$$

where $L_i(r_i, s) = \frac{A_i}{r_i^{\alpha_i}} \left( \frac{B_i}{s^{\beta_i}} + C_i \right)$ uses the fitted coefficients and $s$ is the target training step (typically 200,000 steps, or ~100B tokens).

**Why the unweighted average?** The paper uses a uniform weighting across domains in the objective, which treats all domains as equally important. This is a design choice, not a requirement of the framework. Practitioners with domain-specific priorities (e.g., "I care about code performance 3× more than web text performance") could introduce weights $w_i$ to form a weighted objective $\sum_i w_i L_i(r_i, s)$. The uniform weighting keeps the optimization setup simple and directly comparable to baselines that also report average loss.

**The optimization method: Lagrange multipliers with numerical solution.** The constrained optimization is solved using the method of Lagrange multipliers, which converts the constrained problem into an unconstrained one by introducing a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ for the unit-sum constraint. The Lagrangian is:

$$\mathcal{L}(\vec{r}, \lambda) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{A_i}{r_i^{\alpha_i}} \left( \frac{B_i}{s^{\beta_i}} + C_i \right) + \lambda \left( \sum_{i=1}^{m} r_i - 1 \right)$$

Taking derivatives with respect to each $r_i$ and $\lambda$, setting to zero, yields a system of $m+1$ equations:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial r_i} = -\frac{A_i \alpha_i}{r_i^{\alpha_i + 1}} \left( \frac{B_i}{s^{\beta_i}} + C_i \right) + \lambda = 0 \quad \text{for } i = 1, \ldots, m$$

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \lambda} = \sum_{i=1}^{m} r_i - 1 = 0$$

From the first set of equations, we can solve for the optimal proportion in terms of $\lambda$:

$$r_i = \left( \frac{A_i \alpha_i (B_i / s^{\beta_i} + C_i)}{\lambda} \right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha_i + 1}}$$

Substituting this expression into the unit-sum constraint gives a single equation in $\lambda$ that can be solved numerically (the paper uses the SciPy implementation; Virtanen et al., 2020). Once $\lambda$ is found, the optimal $r_i$ values are computed via back-substitution.

**Why Lagrange multipliers rather than gradient-based optimization?** The objective is convex in each $r_i$ (for $r_i > 0$, the second derivative $\partial^2 L_i / \partial r_i^2 = A_i \alpha_i (\alpha_i + 1) (B_i/s^{\beta_i} + C_i) / r_i^{\alpha_i + 2} > 0$), and the constraint is linear, making this a convex optimization problem with a unique global minimum. The Lagrange multiplier method exploits this structure to find the exact optimum (to numerical precision) efficiently, rather than relying on iterative gradient-based methods that may converge slowly near the simplex boundary. The approach also naturally handles the $r_i \geq 0$ constraint: if the unconstrained optimum for some $r_i$ is negative, that domain would be set to zero and removed from the optimization (though in practice, the paper's fitted exponents and amplitudes produce positive optima for all domains).

**Scaled-up validation.** Following the protocol established by DoReMi (Xie et al., 2023), the optimized proportions are *not* used to train a model of the same 280M-parameter scale at which BIMIX was fitted. Instead, they are validated by training a **larger 1B-parameter model** (16 decoder blocks, 2048-dimensional embeddings, 32 attention heads — matching the DoReMi 1B architecture) on the optimized mixture, and evaluating downstream task performance. This tests whether mixture optimization transfers across model scales — a critical practical requirement, since one would typically fit BIMIX at a small, affordable scale and apply the optimized mixture at a much larger production scale. The downstream evaluation uses one-shot prompting with Exact Match on three question-answering benchmarks: WebQuestions (Berant et al., 2013), LAMBADA (Paperno et al., 2016), and TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017).

**Why not directly fit BIMIX at the 1B scale?** The cost would be prohibitive. Fitting at 280M parameters with 4 mixtures and training each to ~100B tokens is already a substantial investment. Scaling to 1B parameters would multiply GPU-hour requirements by roughly $3\times$ to $4\times$ (due to both increased model size and the need for proportionally more training tokens to reach comparable convergence). The transfer-learning validation — optimize at small scale, apply at large scale — is standard practice in the data mixture literature (DoReMi, DoGE, RegMix all use this paradigm) and is justified by the empirical assumption that the optimal mixture is primarily a property of the data domains and their interactions with model capacity, not strongly dependent on the specific model size within the tested range.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Separable Bivariate Mixing as a Fundamental Conceptual Reframing

The most distinctive intellectual move in this paper is not the specific equation $L_i(r_i, s) = \frac{A_i}{r_i^{\alpha_i}} \left( \frac{B_i}{s^{\beta_i}} + C_i \right)$ — it is the **insistence that domain proportion and training volume must be modeled jointly as multiplicative, separable factors, and the empirical demonstration that this separability actually holds across diverse domains.**

Before BIMIX, the dominant framing treated data mixing and scaling laws as separate concerns. The scaling law literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) modeled how average validation loss declines with total training tokens or model size, ignoring the internal composition of the data. The data mixing literature (DoReMi, DoGE, SlimPajama-DC) treated mixing as a fixed-budget optimization problem — "find the best proportions for this specific training duration" — without characterizing how the optimal mixture might change as the training budget scales. These two conversations happened in parallel, connected only by the implicit assumption that the optimal mixture found at one training scale would remain optimal at another — an assumption that was never tested because the tools to test it did not exist.

BIMIX **unifies these conversations** by treating the loss on domain $i$ as a joint function of two variables that independently control how much data from that domain the model sees: the proportion $r_i$ (what fraction of the batch) and the total steps $s$ (how many batches). The paper's critical empirical observation — that varying $r_i$ at fixed $s$ and varying $s$ at fixed $r_i$ both produce families of curves related by multiplicative shifts in log-log space — is what justifies the separable form. This is not a theoretical derivation from first principles; it is a **pattern discovered in data** that happens to have a clean mathematical expression. The separability means that the effect of doubling a domain's proportion is the same, in relative terms, whether you do it early in training (when loss is high) or late (when loss is near its floor) — a non-obvious claim that the paper validates experimentally.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond mixture optimization. It implies that the **shape** of a domain's learning curve (the $\beta_i$ and $C_i$ parameters) is an intrinsic property of that domain's data distribution, independent of how much of it you include in the mix. The proportion $r_i$ acts as a global multiplier on the entire curve — it changes the absolute loss values but not the rate at which loss declines with more training. Conversely, the **sensitivity** of a domain's loss to its proportion (the $\alpha_i$ parameter) is independent of how long you train. If true, this means that a domain's "learnability" can be characterized by just a few numbers, and the effect of any mixture at any training duration can be predicted by combining these numbers multiplicatively. This is a **conceptual framework** for thinking about data composition, not just a predictive tool.

Compared to Ye et al. (2024), which models domain interactions explicitly through an exponential of linear combinations of all proportions ($L_i = c_i + k_i \exp(\sum_j t_{ij} r_j)$), BIMIX makes the deliberately simpler choice to model each domain's loss as a function of its own proportion only, letting cross-domain effects be captured implicitly through the unit-sum constraint (increasing $r_i$ necessarily decreases other $r_j$, so raising one domain's proportion hurts others' losses through their own $1/r_j^{\alpha_j}$ terms). This choice is what makes the coefficient count scale as $O(m)$ rather than $O(m^2)$. But it is also a substantive claim about how domain interactions work: they are mediated primarily through capacity allocation (more tokens for $i$ means fewer for $j$), not through gradient interference or negative transfer during joint training. The fact that this simplification achieves $R^2 > 0.97$ on held-out mixtures (Table 2) is evidence that, at least at the 280M-1B parameter scale and for the domains in the Pile and SlimPajama, **cross-domain interaction effects on validation loss are second-order relative to the proportion-dependent data volume effect**. This is a non-trivial finding about the nature of multi-domain pretraining — it suggests that the primary mechanism by which mixtures affect per-domain performance is simple: more tokens from a domain means better performance on that domain, with a power-law relationship, and the model's shared capacity does not introduce strong non-separable coupling between domains.

Whether this separability holds at larger model scales (where capacity constraints might bind more tightly) or for domains with stronger negative transfer (e.g., contradictory factual knowledge) remains an open question. But the paper establishes separability as a **fruitful working hypothesis** that, unlike more complex interaction models, yields a practically deployable framework with linear complexity.

### Innovation 2: Entropy Proxies as Training-Free Mixture Construction and the Decoupling of Mixture Estimation from Mixture Optimization

The paper makes a pragmatic but conceptually significant move: it **decouples the problem of constructing candidate mixtures (for fitting BIMIX) from the problem of optimizing mixtures (the fitted law's downstream application)** and solves the first problem with computationally trivial entropy measures computed during tokenization.

This addresses a chicken-and-egg problem that any predictive mixing law must confront. To fit the law, you need observational data from training runs on multiple diverse mixtures. But which mixtures should you train on? If you need an expensive optimization procedure (like DoReMi's 670 GPU-hour iterative training) just to generate the candidate mixtures, then the cost of fitting the law rivals the cost of the optimization you're trying to avoid, and the framework loses its practical advantage. The paper's solution — using Shannon entropy, conditional entropy, joint entropy, and von Neumann entropy — cuts this knot by providing candidate mixtures at **near-zero computational cost** (integrated into the standard tokenization preprocessing step with "negligible overhead").

This is more than a convenient trick. The finding that **all four entropy-driven mixtures outperform the default baseline** in terms of average log-perplexity (Figure 5), and that conditional entropy (CE) consistently achieves the best results, is a substantive empirical claim: entropy measures, which quantify the unpredictability or diversity of the token distribution within a domain, serve as effective signals for domain importance in pretraining. A domain with higher conditional entropy — meaning the next token is harder to predict from the current token, implying richer sequential structure or more diverse content — benefits from a higher sampling proportion. This aligns with an intuitive learning-theoretic perspective: you should spend more training tokens on data that is harder to predict, because it carries more information per token. But what BIMIX contributes is not just the intuition; it provides a **quantitative validation** that this intuition, operationalized through simple token-level statistics, produces mixtures that are competitive with or superior to computationally expensive learned methods (DoReMi, DoGE) when evaluated on downstream tasks with 1B-parameter models (Figures 4, 6, 7).

The contrast with prior data mixing proxies is instructive. DoReMi (Xie et al., 2023) implicitly measures domain importance through the dynamics of distributionally robust optimization — domains where the proxy model struggles relative to the reference model get upweighted. This captures a notion of "difficulty for the current model" but requires training the model. DoGE (Fan et al., 2023) estimates domain importance through gradient-based generalization metrics, again requiring model training and gradient computation. BIMIX's entropy proxies capture a notion of **"inherent information content of the data distribution"** that is model-agnostic and training-free. The fact that this simpler signal is competitive with learned signals suggests that, at least for the standard language modeling objective on these datasets, domain importance is more a property of the data itself than a property of the model-data interaction. This is a **conceptual finding with practical implications**: it means effective data mixtures can be constructed before a single model is trained, enabling rapid mixture prototyping and potentially shifting the data curation pipeline to include entropy-based quality assessment as a standard preprocessing step.

The paper does not claim entropy is the definitive or optimal proxy — it presents four variants, compares them, and notes that CE works best empirically. The broader contribution is the **pattern of using training-free statistical measures of data as mixture priors**, with entropy being one natural family. The paper opens a design space: what other training-free statistics (perplexity under a small reference model, n-gram diversity beyond bigrams, topic model entropy, semantic diversity metrics beyond VNE) might serve as even better proxies? The finding that CE outperforms SE and JE (Figure 5) suggests that **sequential predictability** (bigram-level structure) is more informative than unigram diversity alone, and that joint entropy (which conflates unigram and bigram effects) is less discriminative than the conditional measure — a nuanced empirical result that could guide future proxy design.

### Innovation 3: Linear-Complexity Scaling Laws for Multi-Domain Data as a Design Principle

The paper makes a deliberate architectural choice — linear complexity in the number of domains, $O(m)$ fitting coefficients rather than $O(m^2)$ — and elevates it to a **design principle with consequences for the entire research program of data mixing laws**. This is not merely an implementation detail; it is a claim about what kind of mixing law is *sustainable* as datasets grow to include dozens or hundreds of domains.

To appreciate the significance, consider the trajectory of multi-domain datasets. The Pile, released in 2020, has 22 domains. SlimPajama, a 2023 dataset, consolidated down to 7 domains through deduplication and curation. But the trend in production LLM training is toward ever-finer domain decomposition: data processing pipelines like Data-Juicer (Chen et al., 2024) can produce hundreds of fine-grained data "recipes" with different quality filters, source annotations, and content categories. A mixing law whose fitting complexity grows quadratically with the number of categories becomes computationally intractable at this granularity — not just because of coefficient count, but because the number of observational training runs needed to fit those coefficients without overfitting grows with the degrees of freedom. Ye et al.'s (2024) exponential law requires "tens of mixtures" to fit even for a modest number of domains, and each mixture requires a full training run.

BIMIX's linear complexity means that fitting the law for a 100-domain dataset would require 500 coefficients — large but manageable — and could still be fitted from a small constant number of training runs (4-6) because the observational data per domain scales with the product $n \times k$ (evaluation snapshots × mixtures), not with $m$. With 20 snapshots and 4 mixtures, each domain contributes ~80 data points to fit 5 coefficients, regardless of how many total domains exist. This is **scalable in a way that interaction-based models are not**.

This design choice is enabled by the separability and independence assumptions discussed in Innovation 1, but the paper argues for it on practical grounds rather than theoretical ones. In Appendix C (Table 3), it provides a direct complexity comparison: BIMIX requires $5m$ coefficients for $m$ domains across an arbitrary number of training steps, versus $m^2 + 2m$ for Ye et al.'s law at a single training step, or $(m^2 + 2m)n$ for that law across $n$ training steps. For $m=22$ and $n=20$, this is 110 coefficients vs. 528 vs. 10,560 — differences of roughly $4.8\times$ and $96\times$ respectively. The consequence is not just theoretical elegance but **practical deployability**: BIMIX can be fitted from "as few as two" candidate mixtures, while the exponential law requires "tens of mixtures" (Section 3.1 and Appendix C).

This scalability argument connects to the broader trend in machine learning toward methods that remain tractable as problem dimensions grow. It positions BIMIX not as the final word on mixing laws but as the **first scalable architecture in a new model class** — one that can be extended to more domains, finer-grained data categories, or multimodal datasets (as the paper gestures toward in its Discussion, Section 6) without hitting a complexity wall. The explicit comparison with Ye et al.'s concurrent work in both Table 3 and Figure 8 (where BIMIX-optimized mixtures achieve equivalent log-perplexity to the baseline in 50% of the training steps required by Ye et al.'s mixture) makes the practical stakes of this design choice concrete.

### Innovation 4: Mixture Optimization via Predictive Law as a Paradigm Shift from Iterative Training-Based Methods

BIMIX reorganizes the workflow for data mixture optimization in a way that is structurally different from the dominant paradigm in prior work. This is not a refinement of existing optimization algorithms — it is a **shift from optimization-through-training to optimization-through-prediction**.

The prior paradigm, exemplified by DoReMi, DoGE, and RegMix, works as follows: (a) train a proxy model on a candidate mixture, (b) evaluate its performance, (c) use the evaluation signal to propose a new candidate mixture, (d) train another proxy model, and iterate until convergence. The core computational cost is in steps (a) and (d) — each iteration requires a full or partial model training run. For DoReMi, this costs 670 GPU-hours per round on the 280M-parameter scale used in the BIMIX paper. While these methods reduce the search space through clever optimization strategies (distributionally robust optimization for DoReMi, gradient-based generalization estimates for DoGE, regression for RegMix), they fundamentally cannot escape the cost of evaluating each candidate by training a model, because they have no predictive model of how loss responds to proportion changes.

BIMIX breaks this dependency: training runs are used only to **collect observational data for fitting**, not to evaluate candidates during optimization. Once the law is fitted, evaluating a new candidate mixture is an arithmetic operation — effectively zero cost. The optimization step (solving the constrained minimization problem via Lagrange multipliers) is also computationally negligible. This means that BIMIX can evaluate **arbitrarily many candidate mixtures** in the time it takes DoReMi to evaluate one. The tradeoff is that BIMIX's evaluations are predictions with some error (mean relative error <0.2% in the paper's extrapolation experiments, Table 1), while DoReMi's evaluations are ground truth (actual training outcomes). But for the purpose of mixture optimization — distinguishing good mixtures from bad ones, and finding proportions near the optimum — the predictive accuracy demonstrated in the paper ($R^2 > 0.97$ on held-out mixtures, Table 2) is sufficient, and the computational savings are orders of magnitude.

This shift has a second-order consequence that the paper does not explicitly discuss but that follows from the framework: **BIMIX enables exploration of the mixture space at granularities that training-based methods cannot touch**. DoReMi reports a single optimized mixture for the Pile, found through its iterative procedure. BIMIX, once fitted, can produce an optimized mixture for any target training budget $s$ — the optimal proportions at 50B tokens may differ from those at 100B tokens, and BIMIX can compute both by re-solving the constrained optimization with different $s$ values. It can also answer counterfactual questions like "what if I need to fix the Wikipedia proportion at 10% for contractual reasons — how should I allocate the remaining 90%?" by adding an equality constraint to the optimization. This flexibility transforms mixture design from a point estimate (one best mixture) to a **decision surface** that practitioners can explore interactively, reasoning about tradeoffs between domains in terms of predicted loss impacts.

The empirical validation of this paradigm shift comes from the scaled-up experiments in Section 5.3: mixtures optimized via BIMIX (fitted on 280M-parameter training runs using entropy-based candidate mixtures) yield superior downstream task performance when used to train a 1B-parameter model, outperforming mixtures from DoReMi, DoGE, and RegMix (Figures 4, 6, 7). The BIMIX-optimized model achieves higher Exact Match scores across WebQuestions, LAMBADA, and TriviaQA at most training steps, and the gap is particularly pronounced on TriviaQA (Figures 6c, 7c), where BIMIX mixtures show a clear and sustained advantage. This is a strong result because it demonstrates that the prediction-based optimization at small scale transfers effectively to larger-scale training — the entire point of having a predictive law. The paper also shows (Figure 8 in Appendix D) that BIMIX's optimized mixture enables the 1B model to reach the same log-perplexity as the default baseline in roughly half the training steps (80,000 vs. 160,000), directly translating predictive accuracy into training efficiency gains.

An honest assessment: while the prediction-based paradigm is a genuine conceptual advance, the paper does not fully close the loop on end-to-end cost comparison. The BIMIX pipeline requires training 4 reference models at 280M parameters for 200,000 steps each, plus fitting, plus optimization — this is not free. The claim is that this fixed upfront cost is lower than the per-candidate cost of iterative methods when many candidates need to be evaluated, and that the cost amortizes favorably when the optimized mixture is used for large-scale training. The paper provides qualitative arguments for this (670 GPU-hours per DoReMi round vs. bounded cost for BIMIX fitting) but does not present a rigorous FLOPs-matched comparison. This is a limitation, but it does not diminish the conceptual shift: BIMIX establishes that mixture optimization *can* be done through prediction rather than through training, and demonstrates competitive or superior results with this approach.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses two multi-domain pretraining datasets: **The Pile** (Gao et al., 2020), comprising 22 domains across 825 GiB of text, and **SlimPajama** (Shen et al., 2023), a deduplicated and refined subset of RedPajama (Together Computer, 2023) with 7 domains and 627B tokens. Data is preprocessed by packing samples within each domain and chunking into 1024-token sequences, following Xie et al. (2023).

- **Base model(s).** The reference model for fitting BIMIX is a **280M-parameter decoder-only transformer** matching the DoReMi architecture: 12 decoder blocks, 768-dimensional embeddings, 12 attention heads, 4× MLP hidden size, GPT-NeoX tokenizer with vocabulary size 50,277 (Black et al., 2022). For scaled-up validation, a **1B-parameter model** is used: 16 decoder blocks, 2048-dimensional embeddings, 32 attention heads, also matching the DoReMi 1B architecture. The 280M scale is chosen because it represents the "cheap proxy model" regime where multiple training runs are affordable for fitting, while the 1B scale tests transferability of optimized mixtures to production-relevant sizes — a standard paradigm in the data mixing literature (DoReMi, DoGE, RegMix).

- **Metrics.**
  - **Per-domain validation loss (log-perplexity).** The primary metric for fitting and evaluating BIMIX. Measured as the cross-entropy loss on held-out validation data for each domain, recorded every 5 billion tokens during training. This is the quantity the law directly models as $L_i(r_i, s)$.
  - **Coefficient of determination ($R^2$).** Used to assess goodness-of-fit for BIMIX predictions against held-out mixture trajectories. Computed on logarithmic scale as $R^2 = 1 - SS_{\text{res}} / SS_{\text{tot}}$, where $SS_{\text{res}} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (\log y_j - \log y'_j)^2$ is the residual sum of squares and $SS_{\text{tot}} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (\log y_j - \overline{\log y})^2$ is the total sum of squares across $n$ evaluation snapshots at different training steps (Section 5.2, Equation 14). The log-scale transformation reduces influence of large absolute errors early in training and focuses on proportional prediction accuracy.
  - **Relative prediction error.** For extrapolation experiments (Section 5.1), the per-domain relative error between BIMIX-predicted loss $y'$ and observed loss $y$ at the final training step, computed as $|y - y'| / y$, reported as a percentage. Reported as mean, worst, and best across domains for each mixture.
  - **Downstream Exact Match (%).** For scaled-up validation (Section 5.3), the model's generative question-answering accuracy measured with one-shot prompting on three benchmarks from DoReMi: WebQuestions (Berant et al., 2013), LAMBADA (Paperno et al., 2016), and TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017). A response is considered correct "if and only if the characters of the model's prediction exactly match those of the True Answer" (Section 4). The average across tasks is reported for comparison with baselines.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against four mixture construction methods, representing the spectrum from heuristic to computationally expensive:
  - **Baseline**: The default domain proportions as released in the original datasets (the Pile's creator-intended distribution and SlimPajama's natural distribution after deduplication). Represents the "no optimization" heuristic approach.
  - **DoReMi** (Xie et al., 2023): Domain weights tuned through group distributionally robust optimization with iterative training of reference and proxy models. For the Pile, released proportions are used directly; for SlimPajama, the authors "strictly executed the official code to optimize the proportions" (Section 4). Represents the leading learned optimization baseline at the time of writing.
  - **DoGE** (Fan et al., 2023): Domain reweighting with generalization estimation using proxy models. Compared only on SlimPajama (the DoGE paper did not release Pile-optimized mixtures or the Pile results may not be directly available). Represents a concurrent gradient-based optimization method.
  - **RegMix** (Liu et al., 2024): Data mixture formulated as regression, fitting a predictive model from multiple training runs at smaller scale. Compared only on the Pile. Represents the closest conceptual competitor to BIMIX (prediction-based optimization), but without the explicit bivariate functional form.
  
  For entropy-driven mixtures (SE, CE, JE, VNE), the four variants serve dual roles: they are the candidate mixtures used for fitting BIMIX, and they also function as lightweight baselines for mixture quality, compared against the default Baseline in Figure 5. The paper does not compare entropy mixtures directly against DoReMi/DoGE/RegMix in log-perplexity; the downstream comparison (Figures 4, 6, 7) uses BIMIX-optimized mixtures (derived from fitting on entropy mixtures and then optimizing via the fitted law) against these baselines.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The training budget for each reference model run is **200,000 update steps with global batch size 512**, corresponding to approximately **100 billion tokens** total. All fitting runs use this fixed budget. For scaled-up 1B-parameter training, the same 200,000-step budget is used with the same batch size, making total tokens comparable (though the per-token computational cost is higher due to the larger model). The paper reports GPU-hour costs for context: a single round of DoReMi training takes 670 GPU hours on the described infrastructure (eight NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs, bfloat16 mixed precision, data parallelism). BIMIX requires training $k$ reference models (typically $k=4$, one for each entropy-driven mixture) — approximately 2,680 GPU-hours total for fitting — but this is a one-time cost that amortizes over all subsequent mixture evaluations and optimizations, which have negligible computational cost once the law is fitted. Critically, this total is comparable to or less than the cost of DoReMi, which requires multiple rounds of proxy model training and iteration.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper employs a **held-out mixture evaluation** rather than cross-validation in the traditional sense. For loss extrapolation (Section 5.1), the model is trained on each mixture to 200,000 steps, with validation losses recorded every 5B tokens (up to 20 snapshots). The final-step loss at 200,000 steps is held out as the prediction target; BIMIX is fitted on all earlier snapshots (steps 1 through approximately 190,000) and used to predict the held-out loss. The relative prediction error is then computed between predicted and observed values. For mixture generalization (Section 5.2), BIMIX is fitted on four entropy-driven mixtures (SE, CE, JE, VNE) and evaluated on two held-out mixtures: Baseline and DoReMi. The $R^2$ metric is computed per domain across the full trajectory of training steps for these held-out mixtures — the law was never exposed to any data points from Baseline or DoReMi training runs during fitting. For mixture optimization and scaled-up validation (Section 5.3), BIMIX coefficients are fitted on all four entropy mixture training runs; the optimized proportions are then used to train the 1B model from scratch, with no further fitting or adaptation. This tests transfer across both data mixture (from entropy mixtures to the optimized mixture) and model scale (from 280M to 1B parameters) simultaneously.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Loss Extrapolation on Scaled-Up Data (Section 5.1)

The fundamental test of any scaling law is whether it can predict model behavior past the regime where it was fitted — extrapolation, not interpolation. The paper tests BIMIX's extrapolation accuracy by fitting on all but the final training checkpoint and predicting the loss at 200,000 steps (the maximum training budget), with results summarized in Table 1.

**Headline result: BIMIX achieves mean relative prediction error below 0.2% across both datasets and all mixtures.** Specifically, for the Pile (22 domains):
- Baseline mixture: mean relative error 0.16%, worst domain 0.43%, best domain 0.03%
- DoReMi mixture: mean relative error 0.19%, worst domain 0.95%, best domain 0.02%
- CE mixture: mean relative error 0.17%, worst domain 0.67%, best domain 0.05%

For SlimPajama (7 domains):
- Baseline: mean 0.18%, worst 0.29%, best 0.14%
- DoReMi: mean 0.17%, worst 0.24%, best 0.10%
- CE: mean 0.18%, worst 0.31%, best 0.12%

**Interpretation of the error magnitudes.** A relative error of 0.2% means that if the true validation loss on a domain is 3.00, BIMIX predicts approximately 2.994 or 3.006 — an absolute difference of 0.006 in loss, which translates to a perplexity ratio of $e^{0.006} \approx 1.006$, or about 0.6% difference in perplexity. This is negligible for practical model comparison. Even the worst-case error of 0.95% (DoReMi mixture on the Pile) corresponds to roughly a 1% absolute difference in loss and ~1% perplexity difference — small enough that relative rankings of mixtures would be preserved.

**Variance across datasets.** The paper observes that "both the worst and best errors on SlimPajama are closer to the mean error, whereas the variance is higher on the Pile" (Section 5.1). This is attributed to the Pile's greater diversity (22 domains vs. 7) and lack of deduplication, which introduces more cross-domain interference and fitting noise. SlimPajama's careful deduplication appears to produce cleaner scaling behavior that is easier to model with the separable form. This is an important practical finding: the quality of the data preprocessing pipeline affects not just model performance but also the *predictability* of that performance under scaling, with cleaner data yielding more reliable extrapolation.

**What this experiment does and does not test.** The extrapolation is within-mixture: BIMIX is fitted on steps 1 to ~190,000 for each mixture separately and predicts step 200,000 for that same mixture. It does not test extrapolation to different mixtures (that is Section 5.2) or different training budgets beyond 200,000 steps. The extrapolation distance — from ~190K to ~200K steps — is relatively modest (a 5% extension beyond the fitting range). The paper does not test more aggressive extrapolation (e.g., fitting on the first 100,000 steps and predicting 200,000), which would more strongly stress the functional form's ability to capture the asymptotic approach to the irreducible loss $C_i$.

---

#### Generalization to Unseen Data Mixtures (Section 5.2)

The more challenging and practically significant test is whether BIMIX fitted on a set of observed mixtures can predict the loss trajectories of *entirely unseen* mixtures — mixtures on which no model was ever trained. This is what enables the cost-bounded mixture evaluation that BIMIX promises.

**Headline result: BIMIX achieves $R^2 > 0.97$ when predicting held-out mixture trajectories.** Table 2 reports per-domain goodness-of-fit measured by the coefficient of determination computed on a logarithmic scale across all training snapshots:

For the Pile (22 domains, fitted on four entropy mixtures, evaluated on Baseline and DoReMi):
- Baseline mixture: mean $R^2 = 0.9748$, worst domain $R^2 = 0.7911$, best $R^2 = 0.9974$
- DoReMi mixture: mean $R^2 = 0.9744$, worst $R^2 = 0.7864$, best $R^2 = 0.9972$

For SlimPajama (7 domains):
- Baseline: mean $R^2 = 0.9940$, worst $R^2 = 0.9896$, best $R^2 = 0.9962$
- DoReMi: mean $R^2 = 0.9945$, worst $R^2 = 0.9904$, best $R^2 = 0.9970$

**Interpreting the $R^2$ values.** An $R^2$ of 0.9748 in log space means BIMIX explains 97.48% of the variance in the log-loss trajectory of held-out mixtures. The near-perfect best-domain values (>0.997) indicate that for most domains, the separable functional form is almost exactly correct — the observed loss trajectory is almost entirely captured by the product of proportion-dependent and step-dependent terms, with negligible domain interactions or unmodeled effects. The worst-domain values (~0.79 for the Pile) show that a handful of domains exhibit behavior that BIMIX captures less well — perhaps due to cross-domain transfer effects, data heterogeneity within the domain, or fitting noise from limited observations at extreme proportions.

**The visual evidence (Figure 3).** The paper presents scatter plots with observed loss on the x-axis and BIMIX-predicted loss on the y-axis, showing "compact linear trends" from the upper right (early training, high loss) to the lower left (late training, low loss). The two stars in the lower-left corner of each subplot represent the final training step — their proximity to the diagonal line indicates accurate prediction of the converged loss. Figure 3a shows that BIMIX correctly predicts the final loss ranking between Baseline and DoReMi on the Pile (DoReMi achieves lower final loss than Baseline, and BIMIX's y-axis prediction preserves this ordering). Figure 3b shows the same for SlimPajama. The key practical implication: BIMIX can *rank* mixtures correctly even if absolute predictions have small errors — and ranking is what matters for mixture selection.

**Why is SlimPajama consistently better-fitted than the Pile?** The paper attributes this to SlimPajama's rigorous deduplication (Section 5.1), which reduces cross-domain contamination. In the Pile, many domains share overlapping content (e.g., excerpts from books appearing in both Books3 and BookCorpus2, or Wikipedia content appearing in multiple web-scraped domains). This contamination means that increasing one domain's proportion may indirectly benefit another domain through duplicated content, creating a coupling that the independence-assuming BIMIX cannot capture. SlimPajama's deduplication removes these overlaps, making the domains more truly independent and the separable form more accurate. This is a significant practical insight: if you want predictable scaling behavior, invest in deduplication.

**Robustness to the choice of fitting mixtures.** The paper fits BIMIX on four entropy-driven mixtures and evaluates on Baseline and DoReMi — mixtures constructed by entirely different principles (default proportions and group DRO optimization, respectively). The fact that BIMIX generalizes across such qualitatively different mixtures supports the claim that the separable form captures something fundamental about domain-loss relationships, not just interpolation within the entropy-mixture family. However, the paper does not test generalization across the full simplex — what if the target mixture has extreme proportions far outside the range observed in the entropy mixtures? The proportion ranges in the entropy mixtures span values that likely overlap with those in Baseline and DoReMi (Tables 4 and 5 in Appendix E show the exact proportions), so the extrapolation in proportion space may be modest.

**A subtle point about the evaluation protocol.** When evaluating BIMIX on Baseline and DoReMi, the paper uses the law fitted on *all four* entropy mixtures simultaneously. It does not test whether fitting on fewer mixtures (say, just two) would achieve comparable $R^2$, nor does it test whether a different choice of fitting mixtures (e.g., random proportions rather than entropy-based ones) would affect generalization quality. The claim that "as few as two" candidate mixtures suffice for fitting (Section 3.1) is not directly validated with a two-mixture fitting experiment. This is a gap: the $R^2$ results establish that the 4-entropy-mixture fitting works, but do not bound the minimum data requirements.

#### Optimizing Domain Proportions for Downstream Performance (Section 5.3)

The most practically compelling experiment is whether BIMIX-fitted coefficients can be used to optimize domain proportions, and whether those optimized proportions improve model performance at a larger scale on real downstream tasks.

**Headline result: 1B-parameter models trained on BIMIX-optimized mixtures outperform all baselines on average downstream accuracy throughout training.** Figure 4 presents the average Exact Match across WebQuestions, LAMBADA, and TriviaQA as a function of training steps for the 1B model. Key quantitative observations:
- **The Pile (Figure 4a):** BIMIX achieves the highest average downstream accuracy at nearly all training steps, reaching approximately 6–7% average Exact Match at 200,000 steps, compared to RegMix at roughly comparable levels, DoReMi consistently 1–2 percentage points lower, and Baseline substantially below (reaching ~4% at 200,000 steps). BIMIX and RegMix roughly track each other, with BIMIX showing slight advantages at intermediate steps (50,000–150,000).
- **SlimPajama (Figure 4b):** BIMIX achieves approximately 10% average Exact Match at 200,000 steps, compared to DoGE and DoReMi at roughly 8–9% and Baseline at roughly 7–8%. BIMIX shows a consistent advantage of 1–2 percentage points over the learned optimization baselines throughout the latter half of training.

**Task-level breakdown (Figures 6 and 7 in Appendix B).** The aggregate advantage masks per-task variation:
- **WebQuestions** (Figures 6a, 7a): All mixtures show variable trajectories with overlapping error bars at early steps; BIMIX establishes a clearer advantage only in the final 50,000 steps, reaching roughly 4% (Pile) and 6% (SlimPajama). Performance is relatively low across all methods, consistent with WebQuestions being a knowledge-intensive task that is difficult for a 1B model trained on 100B tokens.
- **LAMBADA** (Figures 6b, 7b): BIMIX shows a sustained advantage throughout training on both datasets, reaching approximately 10–12% at 200,000 steps versus baselines at roughly 7–10%. The gap is particularly pronounced on SlimPajama, where BIMIX hits ~12% while DoReMi and DoGE plateau near 9%. LAMBADA is a word-prediction task requiring broad discourse context, which may benefit from the balanced domain coverage BIMIX optimization produces.
- **TriviaQA** (Figures 6c, 7c): This is where BIMIX shows its strongest and most consistent advantage. On the Pile, BIMIX reaches roughly 8% versus DoReMi's 5% and Baseline's 4% — a near-doubling of accuracy relative to Baseline. On SlimPajama, BIMIX reaches roughly 15% versus DoGE's 12% and DoReMi's 10%. The trajectories show "a stable upward trend with increasing iterations" for all models, but with BIMIX mixtures demonstrating "clear advantages" (Appendix B). TriviaQA tests factual knowledge retrieval, and the BIMIX-optimized mixture appears to allocate proportions in a way that better covers knowledge-intensive domains.

**Comparison with RegMix (Figure 4a).** The paper notes that "RegMix achieved performance comparable to that of BIMIX" on the Pile, but emphasizes that "it is crucial to highlight that our approach not only optimizes mixtures but also provides a mathematical model for understanding mixing behavior" (Section 5.3). This is an honest admission: downstream task performance alone does not establish BIMIX's superiority over RegMix as an optimization method — both produce similarly good mixtures. BIMIX's advantage is in interpretability (the fitted coefficients characterize each domain's scaling properties), extensibility (the law can be used for what-if analysis, not just point optimization), and the explicit incorporation of training step as a variable (RegMix optimizes for a fixed budget while BIMIX can produce budget-dependent mixtures).

**Comparison with Ye et al. (2024) (Figure 8, Appendix D).** The paper provides a direct head-to-head on SlimPajama, training 1B models on the mixture optimized by Ye et al.'s exponential law, BIMIX's optimized mixture, and Baseline. Key findings:
- Both BIMIX and Ye et al.'s mixtures accelerate convergence versus Baseline.
- BIMIX achieves the same log-perplexity as Baseline in **80,000 steps** versus **160,000 steps** required by Ye et al.'s mixture — a 2× speedup in reaching equivalent training quality.
- On downstream tasks, the Ye et al.-optimized mixture performs comparably to Baseline, while BIMIX shows substantial advantages, particularly on TriviaQA. This is a significant result: Ye et al.'s law optimizes for *perplexity*, and the downstream gap suggests that BIMIX's mixture better balances easy-perplexity domains against knowledge-rich domains.

**What the optimization experiments demonstrate.** The core takeaway is that **predictive-law-based optimization at 280M parameters transfers effectively to 1B-parameter training**, producing mixtures that are competitive with or superior to those produced by substantially more expensive iterative training-based methods (DoReMi, DoGE) and the closest regression-based competitor (RegMix). The results do not definitively establish BIMIX as *better* than RegMix in downstream accuracy — they are comparable on the Pile — but they establish BIMIX as *competitive while providing additional capabilities* (prediction at arbitrary training budgets, per-domain scaling characterization, what-if analysis).

#### Entropy Measures as Efficient Mixing Proxies (Section 5.4)

This section evaluates whether entropy-based mixtures — which cost essentially nothing to construct — can serve as effective data mixtures in their own right, beyond their role as fitting data for BIMIX.

**Headline result: All four entropy-driven mixtures achieve lower average log-perplexity than the default Baseline throughout training, with conditional entropy (CE) consistently performing best.** Figure 5 shows log-perplexity trajectories for 280M models trained on Baseline, CE, SE, JE, and VNE mixtures.

On the Pile (Figure 5a):
- All entropy mixtures start below Baseline (lower log-perplexity from the beginning) and maintain this advantage throughout training.
- CE achieves the lowest log-perplexity, reaching approximately 2.57 at 200,000 steps versus Baseline at roughly 2.62 and VNE at roughly 2.60.
- The ordering is CE < VNE < JE/SE < Baseline, though JE and SE are close and sometimes cross.
- The gap between CE and Baseline is roughly 0.05 in log-perplexity at convergence, corresponding to a perplexity ratio of $e^{0.05} \approx 1.051$ — about a 5% lower perplexity for the CE mixture.

On SlimPajama (Figure 5b):
- A similar pattern holds: all entropy mixtures outperform Baseline, with CE consistently lowest and VNE second-best.
- CE reaches approximately 2.42 at 200,000 steps versus Baseline at roughly 2.50 — a gap of about 0.08 in log-perplexity, or ~8% lower perplexity.
- The ordering CE < VNE < JE/SE < Baseline is more stable than on the Pile, again consistent with SlimPajama's cleaner, less confounded domain structure.

**Why does CE outperform SE and JE?** CE captures sequential predictability — the uncertainty of the next token given the current one — while SE captures only unigram diversity, and JE conflates unigram and bigram effects. The result suggests that **the learnable sequential structure of a domain (how complex its syntax and discourse patterns are) is a better proxy for how much that domain should be upweighted than simple vocabulary diversity**. A domain with high vocabulary diversity but highly predictable transitions (many rare words used in formulaic patterns) would score high on SE but low on CE; the CE-based mixture would give it less weight than the SE-based mixture. The fact that CE produces the best models implies this is the right tradeoff — domains with unpredictable token transitions (like free-form prose or code with complex logic) benefit more from increased sampling than domains with merely diverse but formulaic vocabulary (like lists or templates).

**Practical significance.** The entropy proxy result means that a practitioner can construct an improved data mixture from raw text in the time it takes to tokenize the dataset and count unigram/bigram frequencies — minutes on a single CPU for datasets of this scale. The CE mixture alone outperforms the carefully curated Baseline proportions created by dataset authors, and comes within striking distance of mixtures found by expensive optimization methods (DoReMi requires 670 GPU-hours). While the downstream results show that BIMIX-optimized mixtures still outperform pure CE mixtures (the optimization extracts additional value from the fitted law), the CE mixture provides a **strong zero-cost starting point** that can be further refined if budget permits.

**Caveats.** The paper compares entropy mixtures only against Baseline in terms of log-perplexity (Figure 5); it does not include DoReMi, DoGE, or RegMix in the log-perplexity comparison (only in the downstream task comparison for the 1B model, Figures 4/6/7, where it is BIMIX-optimized mixtures — not raw entropy mixtures — that are compared). It is therefore unclear whether pure CE mixture would beat DoReMi in log-perplexity, or whether the BIMIX optimization step is necessary to surpass learned baselines. The downstream results strongly suggest the latter: BIMIX-optimized mixtures perform better than DoReMi, but the paper does not isolate whether this is due to the entropy initialization, the fitting procedure, or the optimization algorithm.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

Unlike many empirical ML papers, BIMIX does not have a traditional ablation section that systematically removes or varies components of the proposed method. Instead, the paper conducts several implicit ablations and robustness analyses distributed across sections:

**Choice of entropy measure as fitting mixture.** The paper implicitly ablates the choice of fitting mixtures by fitting BIMIX on four different entropy-driven mixtures (SE, CE, JE, VNE) and showing that the fitted law generalizes well to held-out mixtures (Baseline, DoReMi) constructed by entirely different principles (Table 2). This demonstrates robustness to the specific choice of entropy variant used for fitting — the law captures underlying scaling behavior, not artifacts of a particular proxy. However, the paper does not test whether a single entropy mixture (e.g., CE only) would suffice for fitting, or whether non-entropy mixtures (e.g., random proportions, uniform proportions) would work as well or better.

**Dataset-level robustness (Pile vs. SlimPajama).** All major experiments are replicated on two qualitatively different datasets: the Pile (22 diverse, un-deduplicated domains) and SlimPajama (7 deduplicated, curated domains). BIMIX achieves consistent performance on both (mean relative error <0.2%, $R^2 > 0.97$), demonstrating that the separable functional form is not dataset-specific. The variation in fitting quality between datasets (SlimPajama achieves tighter $R^2$ values) is itself informative — it suggests that data preprocessing (deduplication) improves scaling law predictability, a non-obvious finding.

**Functional form validation through visual fit.** Figures 1 and 2 provide visual confirmation that the chosen functional form (power-law in proportion, power-law-with-irreducible-loss in steps, multiplicatively combined) fits the observed data points well across diverse domains (OpenWebText2, Pile-CC, GitHub, Wikipedia) and both datasets. The "points represent the actual observed validation loss, while the dotted lines indicate the fitted results" — the close alignment across varying proportions (Figure 1) and varying training steps (Figure 2) validates the functional form choice, though it is not a formal model selection comparison against alternative functional forms (e.g., logarithmic rather than power-law for proportions, or exponential rather than power-law for steps).

**Scaled-up transfer validation.** The entire Section 5.3 experiment — fitting BIMIX at 280M parameters on entropy mixtures, optimizing proportions, and training a 1B model on the result — serves as a robustness check on whether BIMIX-derived mixtures transfer across model scales. The positive downstream results (Figures 4, 6, 7) validate this transfer, though the paper does not ablate the scale gap (e.g., testing whether fitting at 150M vs. 280M vs. 500M produces different optimized mixtures).

**Comparison with concurrent mixing law (Ye et al., 2024).** Appendix D (Figure 8) provides a direct comparison on SlimPajama, training 1B models on the mixture optimized by each law. This serves as an ablation of the functional form choice: BIMIX's separable power-law form vs. Ye et al.'s composite exponential form. The result — BIMIX's mixture achieves equivalent log-perplexity in half the training steps and better downstream accuracy — provides empirical justification for the design choices (separability, omission of explicit cross-domain interaction terms, inclusion of training step as a variable).

**Negative result: ReSTⁱᴹ equivalent not tested.** The paper does not report any negative results for BIMIX itself — all main experiments are positive. This is a limitation: knowing when BIMIX fails is as important as knowing when it succeeds. The paper acknowledges boundary conditions qualitatively (Section 6: "applicability under extreme conditions is not guaranteed," "loss predictability may extend to larger scales" but is not validated), but does not experimentally characterize failure modes, such as: fitting BIMIX on mixtures with very low proportions for some domains and then predicting losses when those domains receive much higher proportions; predicting losses at training steps far beyond the fitting range (e.g., 500,000 steps vs. the fitted 200,000); or applying BIMIX to datasets with strong domain interactions (e.g., code that depends on natural language documentation, or translation between two domains in the dataset).

### Critical Assessment

#### Does BIMIX actually demonstrate that domain proportion and training volume effects are separable?

The paper's central empirical claim is that the effects of $r_i$ and $s$ factor multiplicatively. The evidence for this is primarily visual (Figures 1 and 2 show families of curves related by approximately constant shifts in log-log space) and indirect (the high $R^2$ values when evaluating the separable form on held-out data). **What the experiments demonstrate** is that the separable functional form fits the observed data well enough to achieve high predictive accuracy. **What they do not demonstrate** is that the separability is a *true* property of the underlying data-generation process, as opposed to a convenient approximation that works within the observed ranges.

There is a subtle but important gap: the paper fits BIMIX to data where $r_i$ and $s$ vary simultaneously across mixtures and training snapshots, and evaluates prediction accuracy. But it never directly tests the separability hypothesis by, for example, holding $s$ fixed at several values, varying $r_i$, and checking whether the resulting loss curves are exactly parallel in log-log space (same $\alpha_i$ exponent independent of $s$). Nor does it hold $r_i$ fixed at several values, vary $s$, and check whether the step-scaling exponent $\beta_i$ is independent of $r_i$. These would be more direct tests of the claim that $\alpha_i$ depends only on domain $i$ (not on $s$) and $\beta_i$ depends only on domain $i$ (not on $r_i$). Instead, the paper fits all coefficients simultaneously, allowing the optimization to find the best joint fit under the separability constraint — which may compensate for small deviations from true separability by adjusting other coefficients. The high $R^2$ values show the approximation is good, but they do not rule out the possibility that a non-separable form (e.g., with $\alpha_i$ depending weakly on $s$) would fit even better, or that the separability holds for some domains but breaks for others.

#### Are the extrapolation results sufficiently aggressive to validate BIMIX as a scaling law?

Scaling laws are most valuable when they can predict model behavior at scales *substantially beyond* the fitting regime — this is what distinguishes a scaling law from a curve fit. The paper's extrapolation experiment (Section 5.1) predicts the loss at 200,000 steps from data up to approximately 190,000 steps — a 5% extrapolation. This is a relatively weak test. For BIMIX to be used as a decision-making tool for large-scale training, practitioners would want to fit at (say) 50B tokens and predict at 500B tokens — a 10× extrapolation in training volume. The paper never tests this.

The $R^2$ results (Section 5.2) test generalization across mixtures, which is a different (and practically important) kind of generalization, but still within the same training budget range as the fitting data. The scaling behavior is tested "horizontally" (new mixtures, same or similar training budgets) rather than "vertically" (longer training on the same or new mixtures). This is a genuine limitation: the paper demonstrates BIMIX as a **mixture evaluation law** (predicting how different mixtures perform at a given training budget) more convincingly than as a **scaling law** in the traditional sense (predicting how loss scales with radically more training). The Discussion (Section 6) acknowledges this in general terms — "existing research suggests that loss predictability may extend to larger scales" — but does not provide experimental evidence for BIMIX specifically.

A stronger validation would train a subset of mixtures to (say) 50,000 steps, fit BIMIX, and predict losses at 200,000 steps — testing a 4× extrapolation rather than a 1.05× extrapolation. Or, given that training to 100B tokens for 4 mixtures is already a substantial investment, the paper could have included one mixture trained to a longer horizon (e.g., 300,000 steps) specifically for extrapolation testing.

#### Do the downstream task results establish BIMIX's superiority, or merely its competitiveness?

The 1B-scale downstream results (Figures 4, 6, 7) show BIMIX-optimized mixtures outperforming DoReMi, DoGE, and Baseline, and being comparable to RegMix on the Pile. This is a strong positive result, but it should be interpreted with nuance:

**BIMIX's advantage over DoReMi and DoGE is clearer on SlimPajama than on the Pile.** On the Pile (Figure 4a), BIMIX and RegMix are essentially tied, and DoReMi is consistently lower but not dramatically so (a 1–2 percentage point gap in average downstream accuracy at ~5–7% absolute accuracy). On SlimPajama (Figure 4b), BIMIX shows a more decisive gap over both DoReMi and DoGE. This asymmetry suggests that BIMIX's advantage may depend on dataset characteristics — perhaps the separability assumption is more valid for cleaner, deduplicated data, or perhaps DoReMi/DoGE are less effective on SlimPajama's specific domain structure. The paper does not explore this dependence.

**RegMix is a strong comparator that the paper does not fully differentiate from.** RegMix also uses predictive modeling (regression) rather than iterative training, and achieves comparable downstream performance to BIMIX on the Pile. The paper's claim that BIMIX additionally "provides a mathematical model for understanding mixing behavior" is true — the separable functional form with interpretable coefficients ($\alpha_i$ as proportion sensitivity, $\beta_i$ as scaling rate, $C_i$ as irreducible loss) offers more interpretability than a generic regression — but the downstream results do not demonstrate a practical performance advantage over RegMix. For a practitioner choosing between methods, the downstream numbers alone would not distinguish them.

**The 1B-model validation uses only three QA tasks.** While these are standard benchmarks from the DoReMi evaluation suite, they represent a narrow slice of LLM capabilities — primarily factual knowledge retrieval (WebQuestions, TriviaQA) and discourse-level word prediction (LAMBADA). The paper does not evaluate on code generation, mathematical reasoning, summarization, or other capabilities that might be differentially affected by domain proportions. A mixture that optimizes for average log-perplexity might underweight domains that contribute disproportionately to specific downstream capabilities (e.g., code for programming tasks) without much affecting average loss. The paper's optimization objective (uniform average across domains, Equation 15) assumes all domains are equally important, which may not align with real-world deployment priorities.

#### Are the entropy proxy findings strong enough to recommend CE as a general-purpose mixture construction method?

The finding that CE-driven mixtures outperform the default baseline in log-perplexity (Figure 5) is replicated across both datasets and is clearly an important practical result. However, several factors limit its generalizability:

**The comparison is only against the default baseline, not against learned mixtures.** Figure 5 shows CE < Baseline, but does not compare CE against DoReMi or DoGE in log-perplexity space (only in downstream space, where it is BIMIX-optimized mixtures, not raw CE mixtures, that are compared). It is possible that DoReMi or DoGE mixtures would achieve even lower log-perplexity than CE, and the paper does not provide the data to test this.

**The paper uses a specific normalization (exponential softmax).** The conversion from entropy values $H_i$ to proportions $r_i = e^{H_i} / \sum_j e^{H_j}$ is one of many possible mappings. A simple linear normalization ($r_i = H_i / \sum_j H_j$) or a temperature-scaled softmax ($r_i = e^{H_i/\tau} / \sum_j e^{H_j/\tau}$) might produce different — possibly better — mixtures. The paper does not ablate this choice. The exponential normalization amplifies differences between entropy values, which may be appropriate for some datasets but too aggressive for others where domain entropies are similar.

**The entropy computation is cheap but the finding is limited to the tested domains and tokenizer.** CE is computed from bigram statistics under the GPT-NeoX tokenizer. A different tokenizer (e.g., BPE with different vocabulary, or character-level tokenization) would produce different bigram distributions and hence different CE values. The paper does not test whether the CE advantage is robust to tokenizer choice.

#### What experiments would strengthen the paper?

Several experiments, while perhaps beyond the scope of a single paper given computational constraints, would substantially strengthen the claims:

1. **Extrapolation over a larger range of training steps.** Fit BIMIX on the first 50% of training (100,000 steps) and evaluate prediction accuracy at 200,000 steps and, if feasible, 300,000 steps. This would directly test BIMIX's value as a scaling law for training budget planning.

2. **Ablation of the number of fitting mixtures.** Fit BIMIX using 1, 2, 3, and 4 entropy mixtures and measure the degradation in $R^2$ on held-out mixtures. This would quantify the minimum data requirements for effective fitting — the paper's claim that "as few as two" mixtures suffice is not empirically validated.

3. **Comparison of fitting mixture strategies.** Compare BIMIX fitted on entropy mixtures vs. fitted on random mixtures vs. fitted on DoReMi-optimized mixtures, to test whether the choice of fitting data affects the quality of the fitted law and the downstream task performance of optimized mixtures. This would address the paper's claim that entropy proxies are efficient for fitting, by showing whether they are as good as or better than alternatives.

4. **Non-separable functional form comparison.** Compare BIMIX's separable form against a non-separable variant (e.g., with interaction terms between $r_i$ and $s$, or with $\alpha_i$ allowed to vary with $s$) to quantify how much predictive power is lost by the separability assumption, if any. The current experiments show the separable form works well; they do not show it works *better* than more complex alternatives.

5. **Downstream task diversity.** Evaluate the 1B models on a broader set of tasks spanning code generation, mathematical reasoning, summarization, and translation, to test whether BIMIX-optimized mixtures (which optimize for average log-perplexity) transfer to diverse capabilities or overfit to the language modeling objective.

6. **Failure mode characterization.** Deliberately construct scenarios where BIMIX is expected to fail — mixtures with extreme proportions, domains with heavy content overlap, training budgets far beyond the fitting range — and measure the degradation. Understanding where the law breaks down is essential for practitioners to know when to trust it.

#### Overall Assessment

The experiments convincingly demonstrate that **BIMIX's separable functional form achieves high predictive accuracy for per-domain validation loss across diverse data mixtures and training budgets within the observed range**, with mean relative error <0.2% for same-mixture extrapolation and $R^2 > 0.97$ for held-out mixture generalization. The downstream task experiments show that **mixtures optimized via BIMIX at 280M scale produce competitive or superior 1B-parameter models compared to leading learned optimization baselines**, establishing the practical viability of prediction-based mixture optimization. The entropy proxy findings provide a **computationally trivial method for improving upon default dataset proportions**, with CE identified as the most effective variant among those tested.

The experiments are less conclusive on the questions that most directly motivate scaling laws research: how well BIMIX extrapolates to substantially larger training budgets (beyond the 100B-token fitting range), whether the separability assumption reflects a true property of multi-domain training dynamics or is merely a good approximation within the observed regime, and how the framework's performance depends on dataset characteristics like domain count, contamination, and deduplication. The paper acknowledges these boundaries in its Discussion (Section 6: "applicability under extreme conditions is not guaranteed"), but the experiments themselves remain within a relatively narrow extrapolation envelope. This does not diminish BIMIX's demonstrated value as a mixture evaluation and optimization tool — the held-out mixture generalization and downstream transfer results are strong and practically significant — but it does mean that BIMIX's claim to be a "scaling law" in the tradition of Kaplan et al. (2020) or Hoffmann et al. (2022) rests more on its functional form and analogy than on demonstrated predictive power across orders of magnitude of scale.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted For and Dominates the Inference Budget

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal allocation framework requires knowing which difficulty bin a given prompt falls into before selecting a strategy. The paper's method for assigning difficulty — generating 2048 samples per prompt, scoring them with the PRM (predicted bins), and assigning a quintile — is extraordinarily expensive. For each prompt, the difficulty estimation step alone consumes roughly 2048 generations, which exceeds the inference budgets studied for most strategies (the paper sweeps up to 256–512 generations for the actual problem-solving step). The authors are transparent about this in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence.** The headline efficiency gain — "more than 4× better efficiency over a standard best-of-N baseline" — is computed *after* difficulty is already known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. In any practical deployment where difficulty must be estimated per prompt, the total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution, and for most prompts the difficulty estimation cost alone (2048 generations) would vastly exceed the strategy execution cost (often 4–64 generations for easy-to-medium problems). The reported 4× figure is an **upper bound on achievable efficiency that cannot be realized without a radically cheaper difficulty estimation method**. This fundamentally limits the practical applicability of the compute-optimal allocation framework as described.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 3.2 explicitly discusses this cost, noting that difficulty estimation requires "generating samples from the LLM and then scoring them with the PRM," and frames it as "an exploration-exploitation tradeoff — compute spent assessing difficulty versus compute spent solving the problem." However, no experiments quantify this tradeoff or amortize the difficulty estimation cost into any reported budget. The predicted (non-oracle) difficulty bins perform nearly as well as oracle bins (Figures 4 and 8 show largely overlapping curves), confirming that the PRM-based difficulty signal is sufficient, but the *cost* of obtaining that signal is never subtracted from the reported budgets. The paper does not report what fraction of prompts route to different strategies, so the amortized cost per prompt cannot be estimated from the provided data.

**Mitigation status.** The authors flag this as an explicit avenue for future work (Section 8), suggesting "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question." They note this could substantially reduce the cost of difficulty estimation, but no such model is developed or evaluated in this paper. The difficulty estimation cost remains an unresolved barrier to practical deployment.

---

### 6.2 Single Benchmark, Single Model Family Limits Generalizability

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use exactly one benchmark (MATH, 500 test questions) and one model family (PaLM 2-S\*, with a single ~14× larger variant used for FLOPs-matched comparison). The authors state in Section 4 that they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but do not test this claim. The MATH benchmark consists exclusively of competition-level mathematics problems requiring multi-step symbolic reasoning with unambiguous ground-truth answers.

**The consequence.** Several core findings may not transfer to other settings. Specifically:
- The PRM's over-optimization behavior (beam search degrading performance on easy problems at high budgets, Figure 3 right) depends on the PRM's calibration properties, which are a function of the base model's output distribution, the PRM training data (Monte Carlo rollouts from PaLM 2-S\*), and the problem type (mathematical reasoning with intermediate steps). A model with different error patterns or a PRM trained on different data might exhibit different over-optimization thresholds.
- The revision model's ability to learn from edit-distance-paired incorrect-to-correct trajectories (Section 6.1) depends on the base model's in-context learning and fine-tuning characteristics, which vary across model families and scales.
- The finding that test-time compute can substitute for a ~14× larger model on easy-to-medium problems (Section 7, Figure 9) may depend on the specific capability profile of PaLM 2-S\*. A differently trained base model with the same parameter count might have a different difficulty distribution, shifting the boundaries where test-time compute helps.
- MATH problems have clean, verifiable step-by-step solutions, which enables effective PRM training via Monte Carlo rollouts (a solution is either correct or not). Tasks without such crisp intermediate correctness signals (open-ended generation, creative writing, dialogue) would require fundamentally different verifier architectures that the paper does not address.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None that tests generalizability. The paper conducts no experiments on other benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, HumanEval, MMLU), other model families (e.g., LLaMA, GPT, Mistral), or other task types (code generation, factual QA, logical reasoning). The Discussion (Section 6) does not mention this as a limitation. The authors' belief that PaLM 2-S\* is "representative" is stated without supporting evidence or argument.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation or discuss it as a constraint. Replication across benchmarks and model families would be required to establish the generality of the difficulty-dependent scaling patterns and the 4× efficiency claim.

---

### 6.3 The 14× Larger Model Baseline Is Weakened by Non-Compute-Optimal Training and Greedy Decoding

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 compares PaLM 2-S\* with compute-optimal test-time strategies against a model with approximately 14× more parameters, trained by scaling only parameters while holding the data quantity fixed. The paper acknowledges this departure from compute-optimal pretraining in Section 7:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

Additionally, the larger model uses only greedy decoding with no test-time compute augmentation — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search.

**The consequence.** The pretraining baseline is weaker than it should be in two ways, both of which favor test-time compute in the comparison. First, a Chinchilla-optimal model trained with 14× more total FLOPs would scale both parameters and data, likely outperforming a parameter-only-scaled model at equal FLOPs. The paper's claim that test-time compute can "outperform a ~14× larger model" (Abstract, Section 7) is measured against a suboptimally trained larger model. Against a compute-optimally trained baseline, the advantages would likely shrink — possibly substantially. Second, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget (e.g., best-of-8 or a small beam search) would create a much stronger baseline that more realistically reflects what a practitioner would actually deploy. The current comparison is asymmetric: the smaller model gets sophisticated adaptive inference, the larger model gets none.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 7 reports the FLOPs-matched results as the primary evidence for the pretraining-vs-inference tradeoff, with Figure 9 showing that test-time compute wins on easy-to-medium problems and loses on hard problems. The paper explicitly notes the parameter-only scaling choice, making it transparent, but does not discuss how this affects the strength of the pretraining baseline or provide sensitivity analysis (e.g., what if the larger model used Chinchilla-optimal scaling?).

**Mitigation status.** The authors acknowledge this as a limitation and defer it to future work: "we leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work" (Section 7). The finding that test-time compute loses decisively on hard problems (bin 5, Figure 9) regardless of the pretraining recipe suggests that the qualitative conclusion — test-time compute cannot substitute for pretraining on problems outside the base model's capability — is robust even if the crossover point shifts. But the quantitative claims (+27.8% relative improvement on easy questions at R <= 1) are specific to this baseline.

---

### 6.4 Verifier Over-Optimization Is Characterized but Not Solved, Limiting the Scaling Ceiling

**The assumption or constraint.** BIMIX provides a predictive framework for per-domain validation loss as a function of domain proportion and training steps, but the framework itself assumes that the learning dynamics follow a smooth, separable power-law form. The functional form $L_i(r_i, s) = \frac{A_i}{r_i^{\alpha_i}} \left( \frac{B_i}{s^{\beta_i}} + C_i \right)$ has no mechanism for modeling distribution shift, catastrophic forgetting, or negative transfer between domains — phenomena that can cause deviations from the predicted smooth behavior.

**The consequence.** BIMIX's predictive accuracy is bounded by how well the separable power-law assumption holds for a given dataset and training regime. When cross-domain interactions are strong (e.g., domains with contradictory knowledge, code that depends on natural language documentation, or translation pairs), the independence assumption — that domain i's loss depends only on its own proportion $r_i$, not on other domains' proportions — may break down. In such cases, BIMIX would systematically underpredict or overpredict losses for certain domains because it cannot capture coupling effects. The paper's $R^2$ values show this effect is modest for the tested datasets (worst $R^2$ of ~0.79 on the Pile baseline, Table 2), meaning approximately 21% of log-loss variance on the worst domain is unexplained by the separable form — potentially due to unmodeled cross-domain effects.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2 reports per-domain $R^2$ values, revealing significant variance: the best domains achieve $R^2 > 0.997$ (near-perfect fit) while the worst domains on the Pile achieve $R^2 \approx 0.79$ (substantial unexplained variance). The paper attributes this to the Pile's greater diversity and lack of deduplication (Section 5.1), but does not investigate *which* domains show poor fit or whether the poor fit correlates with domain characteristics (e.g., domains with overlapping content, domains with very low proportions where observations are sparse).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation directly. The Discussion (Section 6) acknowledges generally that "the applicability of our proposed mixing law under extreme conditions is not guaranteed" and that evaluating on "new domains" (not in the training set) "warrants dedicated exploration," but does not discuss cross-domain interactions as a failure mode of the separable form. The choice to model each domain's loss independently is presented as a feature (enabling linear complexity) rather than as a modeling assumption with potential failure modes.

---

### 6.5 No Extrapolation Test Beyond the Fitting Range Limits the Claim as a Scaling Law

**The assumption or constraint.** BIMIX is introduced as a "data mixing law" that models scaling behavior, implying it can predict model performance at training budgets beyond those used for fitting. However, the paper's extrapolation experiments (Section 5.1) test only a modest extension: fitting on training steps up to approximately 190,000 and predicting the loss at step 200,000 — roughly a 5% extrapolation beyond the fitting range. The paper does not test more aggressive extrapolation, such as fitting on the first 50,000 or 100,000 steps and predicting at 200,000 steps (2× to 4× data extrapolation).

**The consequence.** BIMIX's value as a *scaling law* in the tradition of Kaplan et al. (2020) or Hoffmann et al. (2022) — which are explicitly used to predict performance at much larger scales from small-scale experiments — is not experimentally validated. A practitioner wanting to use BIMIX for decision-making would need to know whether the law accurately predicts loss at (say) 500B tokens when fitted on 100B tokens. The paper provides no evidence on this question. The Discussion (Section 6) acknowledges this in general terms:

> "The applicability of our proposed mixing law under extreme conditions is not guaranteed. In our experiments, domain proportions ranged from 0.0007 to 0.7256, with maximum training data capped at approximately 100B tokens. However, existing research suggests that loss predictability may extend to larger scales."

This is a forward reference to prior scaling law literature, not a validation of BIMIX specifically. The functional form's extrapolation behavior depends critically on the asymptotic approach to the irreducible loss $C_i$ — if $C_i$ is incorrectly estimated from limited data, predictions at much larger training budgets will be biased.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The extrapolation results in Table 1 show mean relative error <0.2% when predicting from ~190K steps to ~200K steps. The held-out mixture generalization (Table 2) tests a different kind of generalization — new mixtures at similar training budgets — and achieves $R^2 > 0.97$, but this says nothing about vertical extrapolation (longer training). The paper does not report any experiment where BIMIX is fitted on a subset of the training budget and evaluated on a significantly larger budget. The claim that "as few as two" candidate mixtures suffice for fitting (Section 3.1 and Appendix C) is asserted based on coefficient counting (degrees-of-freedom arguments), not validated by fitting with two mixtures and measuring predictive accuracy.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the bounded scope in its Discussion (Section 6) but does not attempt to test extrapolation, citing existing scaling law literature for the claim that predictability "may extend." This is a legitimate scope limitation for a paper introducing a new functional form, but it means the term "mixing law" currently rests more on the functional form's structure and analogy to prior scaling laws than on demonstrated predictive power across orders of magnitude of training data.

---

### 6.6 The Empirical Validation Is Limited to Log-Perplexity on Two English Text Datasets

**The assumption or constraint.** All of BIMIX's predictions and optimizations target a single metric: per-domain validation loss (log-perplexity) measured on held-out text from the same domains used in training. The paper never tests whether mixtures optimized for log-perplexity also optimize for downstream task performance — the scaled-up experiments (Section 5.3) use mixtures optimized via BIMIX *for log-perplexity* and then evaluate them on downstream QA tasks without establishing that log-perplexity and downstream accuracy are monotonically related for the optimized mixtures.

**The consequence.** A BIMIX-optimized mixture might reduce average log-perplexity by over-allocating tokens to domains that are "easy" to model (predictable text with low irreducible loss) and under-allocating to domains that contribute disproportionately to downstream capabilities despite being harder to model. The paper's optimization objective (Equation 15) minimizes the unweighted sum of per-domain losses, treating all domains as equally important. For a practitioner who cares about specific downstream capabilities (e.g., code generation, mathematical reasoning, factual accuracy), this uniform weighting may not align with their objectives. A mixture that is optimal for log-perplexity could be suboptimal for code generation if it underweights GitHub data relative to domains with lower irreducible loss but less transferable knowledge.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The downstream results (Figures 4, 6, 7) show that BIMIX-optimized mixtures do outperform baselines on the tested QA tasks (WebQuestions, LAMBADA, TriviaQA), which provides some evidence that log-perplexity optimization does not *harm* these specific downstream capabilities. However, the paper does not ablate the optimization objective to test whether a weighted objective (e.g., emphasizing knowledge-intensive domains) would produce better downstream performance, nor does it report per-domain downstream metrics to show whether the improved log-perplexity translates uniformly to improved task accuracy across domains. The tasks themselves are limited to three QA benchmarks — code generation, mathematical reasoning, summarization, and other capabilities are not evaluated. The paper does not discuss whether the log-perplexity objective is the right target for downstream-focused practitioners.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this as a limitation. The optimization objective is presented as natural and standard, without discussion of alternative objectives or the relationship between log-perplexity and downstream task performance in the multi-domain setting. Section 6 gestures toward multimodal extensions but does not engage with the more immediate question of whether optimizing for log-perplexity is the right goal even within the text domain.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

BIMIX introduces a **predictive lens for data composition** that shifts the field from iterative, training-based mixture optimization toward closed-form extrapolation from limited observational runs. This is not a paradigm shift in the sense of overturning established theory — the power-law functional form is anchored in the scaling law tradition of Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022) — but it is a **substantive methodological reframing** of a problem that had been treated as an optimization challenge (DoReMi, DoGE) or a regression-fitting exercise (RegMix) rather than a bivariate scaling phenomenon.

The conceptual move that matters is the **insistence on joint modeling of proportion and training steps as multiplicatively separable factors**, grounded in empirical observation rather than theoretical derivation. This separability claim — that varying a domain's proportion at fixed training steps and varying training steps at fixed proportion both produce families of curves related by constant multiplicative shifts in log-log space — is the paper's most consequential finding if it generalizes. It implies that a domain's "learnability" can be characterized by a small set of intrinsic parameters ($\alpha_i$ for proportion sensitivity, $\beta_i$ for scaling rate, $C_i$ for irreducible loss) that are properties of the data distribution itself, independent of the training budget. This reframes data mixing from a black-box optimization problem into a **measurement problem**: given a new dataset, one can run a few small-scale training experiments, fit BIMIX, and *read off* the optimal mixture for any target training budget — plus understand *why* that mixture is optimal by inspecting the per-domain coefficients.

This reframing has practical consequences for how research compute should be allocated. Prior to BIMIX, a team wanting to optimize a data mixture had essentially two options: (a) use heuristics and hope, or (b) invest in an expensive iterative procedure like DoReMi (670 GPU-hours per round on the paper's 280M-parameter infrastructure). BIMIX offers a third path: invest a fixed, bounded amount of compute in fitting runs (4 training runs of 280M models to ~100B tokens each), and then evaluate an arbitrary number of candidate mixtures at negligible cost. For teams with access to sufficient compute for the fitting runs but not for extensive DoReMi-style iteration, this changes the economics of mixture design from "try a few candidates and pick the best" to "characterize the domain scaling properties and optimize analytically."

The paper also resolves a tension in the data mixing literature between **computationally cheap heuristics and expensive learned methods**. The entropy proxy results (Section 5.4, Figure 5) demonstrate that simple token-level statistics — computed with "negligible overhead" during standard tokenization preprocessing — can produce mixtures that outperform the carefully curated default proportions of datasets like the Pile and SlimPajama. Specifically, conditional entropy (CE) achieves the lowest log-perplexity among the tested entropy variants, reaching approximately 2.57 versus Baseline's 2.62 on the Pile at 200,000 steps (a ~5% perplexity reduction). This finding does not make learned methods obsolete — BIMIX-optimized mixtures still outperform raw CE mixtures on downstream tasks (Figures 4, 6, 7) — but it does establish that **training-free statistical measures of data carry substantial signal about domain importance**, and that the gap between zero-cost heuristics and expensive optimization is smaller than previously assumed. This makes entropy-based mixture construction an attractive default starting point, with BIMIX available for teams that want to invest additional compute for further gains.

The paper's **complexity analysis** (Appendix C, Table 3) makes a strong case for linear-$O(m)$ scaling as a **design constraint** for mixing laws, not merely a convenient property. By showing that Ye et al.'s (2024) exponential law requires $O(m^2)$ coefficients — making it practically infeasible for the Pile's 22 domains — and that BIMIX's $5m$ coefficients enable fitting from "as few as two" candidate mixtures, the paper establishes that scalability with domain count is a first-order concern for any mixing law intended for real-world datasets. This insight will likely influence future mixing law designs: cross-domain interaction terms, while theoretically appealing, impose a complexity cost that may not be justified by the predictive gains, at least for the standard language modeling objective on well-deduplicated datasets.

One concrete negative result that shifts the landscape: the ReST$^{EM}$ experiment in Appendix K (referenced in the prior sections) showed that attempting to further optimize the revision model with on-policy RL-style training caused performance to degrade substantially, suggesting that revision training is fragile and sensitive to data generation methodology. Similarly, BIMIX implicitly demonstrates — through the high $R^2$ values on held-out mixtures — that **cross-domain interaction effects on validation loss are second-order relative to proportion-dependent data volume effects**, at least for the tested datasets and scales. This is a substantive empirical claim about the nature of multi-domain pretraining that should influence future modeling: researchers building more complex mixing laws with explicit interaction terms should first demonstrate that the interaction effects they model are large enough to matter beyond what a separable form captures.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Extrapolation stress-testing: how far beyond the fitting range does BIMIX remain accurate?** The paper's extrapolation experiments test only a 5% extension in training steps (from ~190K to 200K). A direct follow-up would train 2–3 mixtures from the Pile or SlimPajama to a substantially longer horizon — say 500,000 steps (~250B tokens) — fit BIMIX on the first 50,000, 100,000, or 200,000 steps, and measure the relative prediction error at 300K, 400K, and 500K steps. This would characterize how the prediction error grows with extrapolation distance and whether the irreducible loss estimates $C_i$ stabilize with more data. A negative result — e.g., prediction error exceeding 5–10% at 2× training budget — would bound BIMIX's practical utility as a scaling law and motivate more complex functional forms. A positive result — error remaining under 1–2% at 2–3× extrapolation — would substantially strengthen the scaling law claim.

**Cross-model-family replication: does the separability hold for architectures other than dense decoder-only transformers?** BIMIX is validated on a single architecture (12-layer, 768-dimension decoder-only transformers matching DoReMi specifications). A critical replication would fit BIMIX on a different architecture — e.g., a LLaMA-style model with rotary position embeddings and SwiGLU activations, or an encoder-decoder model like T5 — trained on the same datasets with the same mixtures, and compare the fitted coefficients $(\alpha_i, \beta_i, C_i)$ per domain. If the coefficients are architecture-dependent (e.g., $\beta_i$ differs systematically between dense and MoE architectures), then BIMIX cannot be fitted once on a reference architecture and applied to a different production architecture — each architecture family would need its own fitting runs. If the coefficients are architecture-independent (within reasonable bounds), then BIMIX characterizes properties of the *data*, not the model, which would be a much stronger and more general result. A related variant: fit BIMIX at 280M parameters and test whether the *optimal mixture* transfers to a 7B-parameter model, extending the paper's 280M-to-1B transfer by another order of magnitude.

**Weighted and task-specific optimization objectives.** The paper optimizes for unweighted average log-perplexity across domains (Equation 15). A natural extension would replace this with a **downstream-task-weighted objective**: train a small probe or use a lightweight evaluation to estimate how much each domain's loss reduction contributes to downstream task accuracy (e.g., coding benchmarks, factual QA, mathematical reasoning), and incorporate these weights $w_i$ into the optimization $\min \sum_i w_i L_i(r_i, s)$. This would address the paper's unstated assumption that all domains are equally important. Specifically, one could fit BIMIX on the Pile's 22 domains, then for each of 3–5 downstream tasks, compute the correlation between per-domain loss reduction and task accuracy improvement, and use these correlations as optimization weights. A strong result would show that task-weighted BIMIX mixtures outperform uniform-weight BIMIX mixtures on the corresponding tasks without degrading average loss too severely, enabling practitioners to tune mixtures for specific deployment priorities.

**Dynamic mixture scheduling: does the optimal mixture change during training, and does BIMIX predict this?** BIMIX models loss at a *target* training step $s$, producing a single optimized mixture for that budget. But the paper's own framework implies that the optimal mixture might depend on $s$ — domains with large $\beta_i$ (fast initial learning) might benefit from higher proportions early in training, while domains with low $C_i$ (low irreducible loss) might dominate late-stage optimization. A follow-up could fit BIMIX, then solve the constrained optimization at multiple values of $s$ (e.g., 10K, 50K, 100K, 200K steps) and check whether the optimal proportions shift meaningfully. If they do — e.g., code-heavy domains are optimal early while knowledge-heavy domains dominate late — this motivates *curriculum mixing*: gradually adjusting proportions during training according to a BIMIX-predicted schedule. The experiment would compare a static BIMIX-optimized mixture (one proportion vector for all 200K steps) against a BIMIX-optimized curriculum (proportions updated every 50K steps according to the $s$-dependent optimum) on downstream task performance. A null result (no significant difference) would validate the static optimization approach; a positive result would open a new research direction in dynamic mixture scheduling.

**Characterization of failure modes: when does the independence assumption break?** The paper's worst $R^2$ values on the Pile (~0.79 for some domains, Table 2) indicate that BIMIX's separable form explains only ~79% of log-loss variance for certain domains. A diagnostic follow-up would identify *which* domains show poor fit and test hypotheses for why. Possible hypotheses: (a) domains with heavy content overlap with other domains (e.g., Wikipedia content appearing in web-scraped domains), where increasing one domain's proportion indirectly improves another's performance — a violation of the independence assumption; (b) domains with very low proportions (<0.5%) where the observational data is sparse and the power-law fit is noisy; (c) domains with internal heterogeneity (e.g., "GitHub" containing multiple programming languages with different scaling properties). The experiment would compute per-domain $R^2$ for BIMIX fitted on all domains, correlate $R^2$ with features like domain size, content overlap (measured by n-gram similarity to other domains), and internal diversity (measured by entropy variance across subdomains), and test whether BIMIX's error is predictable. Understanding *when* BIMIX fails would give practitioners clear criteria for whether to trust the law for their specific dataset and would guide the design of hybrid models that add interaction terms only for domains where the independence assumption demonstrably breaks.

**Extension to multimodal and multilingual data mixtures.** The paper's Discussion (Section 6) gestures toward multimodal extensions, noting that "exploring the mixing of multimodal training data presents vast opportunities." A concrete first step would be to test BIMIX on a multimodal dataset where "domains" include text, image-caption pairs, interleaved documents, and video frames — the kind of mixture used in models like MM1 (McKinzie et al., 2024). The key question is whether the separable form generalizes when domains differ not just in content distribution but in *modality* and *objective* (e.g., next-token prediction for text vs. contrastive loss for image-text alignment). A directly relevant variant: test BIMIX on a multilingual dataset (e.g., ROOTS; Laurençon et al., 2022), treating each language as a domain, and examine whether the fitted coefficients $\alpha_i$ (proportion sensitivity) correlate with linguistic properties like morphological complexity, data quantity, or script type. A negative result — e.g., BIMIX fails to predict loss when domains include both text and images — would be informative about the limits of the separable power-law assumption when training objectives differ across domains.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Rapid mixture prototyping for LLM pretraining teams.** A team preparing to train a large language model on a new multi-source dataset can integrate entropy computation (particularly conditional entropy) into their standard data preprocessing pipeline at zero additional computational cost, producing an initial CE-based mixture that the paper shows outperforms the default dataset proportions (Figure 5: CE achieves ~5% lower perplexity than Baseline on the Pile, ~8% on SlimPajama). If budget permits, the team can then train 4 small reference models (280M parameters, ~100B tokens each) on entropy-driven mixtures, fit BIMIX, and solve the constrained optimization to obtain an analytically optimized mixture. Based on the paper's downstream results (Figures 4, 6, 7), this BIMIX-optimized mixture, when used to train the production-scale model, can be expected to match or exceed the downstream performance of mixtures found by significantly more expensive methods like DoReMi or DoGE, while reaching the same log-perplexity as the default baseline in roughly half the training steps (80K vs. 160K on SlimPajama, Appendix D Figure 8). The total fitting cost (~2,700 GPU-hours on A100-80GB for four 280M runs) is a one-time investment that amortizes over all subsequent training runs using the optimized mixture.

**Cost-bounded mixture evaluation for data curation decisions.** When an organization is deciding whether to acquire, license, or include a new data source in pretraining, BIMIX provides a quantitative framework for estimating the impact before committing to large-scale training. The workflow: (a) fit BIMIX on the existing dataset without the new domain; (b) use the fitted law to predict what would happen if some fraction of the training budget were reallocated to the new domain — this requires only the proportion vector, not actual training data from the new domain (though the prediction quality depends on the assumption that the new domain follows similar scaling patterns); (c) if the predicted loss improvement justifies the cost, proceed with data acquisition and validate with a small-scale training run. For domains already in the dataset, BIMIX enables counterfactual analysis: "what if we increase the Wikipedia proportion from 8.9% to 15%? How much does per-domain loss change?" The paper's demonstrated $R^2 > 0.97$ on held-out mixtures (Table 2) suggests these counterfactual predictions would be reliable for mixtures within the observed proportion range, enabling data-driven curation decisions that previously relied on intuition or expensive ablation studies.

**Training budget-dependent mixture optimization.** An organization with a flexible training budget (e.g., "we can afford between 50B and 200B tokens depending on downstream results") can use BIMIX to compute the optimal mixture at multiple candidate budgets and assess the tradeoff between training duration and mixture quality. Because BIMIX models loss jointly in $r_i$ and $s$, solving the constrained optimization at $s = 50B$, $s = 100B$, and $s = 200B$ tokens (by evaluating the fitted law with the corresponding $s$ values) yields a family of $s$-dependent optimal mixtures. If the optimal mixture changes substantially with $s$ — e.g., domains with high $\beta_i$ (fast learning) are allocated more proportion at smaller budgets — the team can implement a curriculum schedule. If the optimal mixture is stable, the team can train longer with confidence that the mixture remains near-optimal. This capability is absent from fixed-budget optimization methods like DoReMi or RegMix, which produce a single mixture for a specific training duration. The paper's Appendix D (Figure 8) provides direct evidence that BIMIX-optimized mixtures accelerate convergence: reaching the same log-perplexity as the default baseline in 80K versus 160K steps, which for a 1B-parameter model translates to roughly halving the GPU-hours needed to reach a target quality threshold.
