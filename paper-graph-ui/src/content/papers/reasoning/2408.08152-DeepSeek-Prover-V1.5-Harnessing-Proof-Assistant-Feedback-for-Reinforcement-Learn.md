## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5**, an open-source 7B-parameter language model for formal theorem proving in Lean 4 that combines whole-proof generation with a **truncate-and-resume mechanism** (parsing generated proofs at the first verification error, discarding invalid code, and resuming generation from the successful prefix with tactic-state feedback) and **RMaxTS**, a Monte-Carlo tree search variant using **intrinsic-reward-driven exploration** (awarding the agent a maximal reward upon reaching an unseen tactic state, operationalized through discounted upper confidence bounds). The system achieves new state-of-the-art pass rates of 63.5% on miniF2F-test (a 13.5 percentage-point gain over the prior best of 50.0% from DeepSeek-Prover-V1) and 25.3% on ProofNet, establishing that reinforcement learning from proof assistant feedback provides genuine capability improvements — not merely better top-K selection — only when the base model already possesses non-zero probability of generating correct proofs on the target theorems.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Formal Theorem Proving Is Hard for Language Models

The central problem this paper addresses is deceptively simple to state: **how do we get a language model to produce a correct, machine-verifiable mathematical proof in a formal system like Lean 4?** Unlike natural language mathematics, where a human can judge whether a proof is "good enough," formal theorem proving requires every logical step to be mechanically checked against the foundational rules of the proof assistant. A single missing case, an unjustified rewrite, or a type mismatch anywhere in the proof causes the entire attempt to fail with a verification error.

This is a fundamentally harder problem than natural language mathematical reasoning for several reasons the paper makes explicit (Section 1):

**The syntax is rigid and unforgiving.** Lean 4 is both a programming language and a proof system. Models must generate code that compiles — correct imports, proper namespace management, precise tactic syntax — before any mathematical reasoning can even be evaluated. The paper's examples in Appendix B show proofs that involve `field_simp`, `nlinarith`, `omega`, `interval_cases`, `norm_num`, and dozens of other Lean-specific tactics, each with its own invocation syntax, argument conventions, and applicability conditions. A model that produces a brilliant mathematical insight but writes `rw [le_div_iff]` without proving the denominator is positive (as in `aime_1983_p9` in Appendix A) gets a zero reward — not partial credit.

**The model cannot "see" intermediate states during whole-proof generation.** In the whole-proof generation paradigm — which DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 inherits from its predecessor and extends — the model produces an entire proof block from the theorem statement, then submits it to Lean for verification. This means the model must mentally track the evolving proof state (what hypotheses are available, what the current goal is, what names have been introduced) across potentially dozens of tactic applications, without ever receiving the compiler's feedback. As the paper notes in Section 1:

> "the auto-regressive model may have incorrect beliefs on intermediate tactic states when generating long proofs"

This is a classic **compounding error** problem (Ross et al., 2011): if the model's internal representation of the proof state drifts from reality, every subsequent tactic is generated relative to a hallucinated state, making the entire remainder of the proof invalid. The error manifests not at the point where the model's belief diverges, but at the point where Lean detects an inconsistency — which may be many tactics later, making diagnosis difficult.

**Verification is binary and sparse.** Unlike natural language tasks where reward models can provide continuous feedback (e.g., "this answer is partially correct"), Lean 4 provides exactly one bit of feedback: the proof compiles (reward = 1) or it doesn't (reward = 0). If the proof fails, the model receives an error message (e.g., "failed to synthesize `LinearOrder ℂ`" in Figure 2), but no signal about which *aspect* of the proof was wrong or how close it was to success. This binary signal is what makes the reinforcement learning in this paper so challenging — the model must learn from extremely sparse feedback where the vast majority of attempts yield zero reward.

**The model must align abstract mathematics with concrete formal representation.** The gap between "I understand why this theorem is true" and "I can encode that understanding in Lean's type theory" is substantial. The paper observes that in DeepSeek-Prover-V1, models often relied on "high-level tactic calls to brute-force solutions" rather than structured mathematical reasoning (Section 2.2). These high-level tactics — like `nlinarith` (which solves nonlinear arithmetic automatically) or `aesop` (an automated proof search tactic) — can sometimes close a goal without the model understanding *why* the goal is satisfied. This works for simple problems but fails catastrophically on theorems requiring genuine mathematical insight: the high-level tactic attempts to brute-force, fails, and the model has no fallback reasoning strategy.

### Why This Problem Matters

The paper operates at the intersection of two domains with significant real-world implications:

**Automated mathematical reasoning is a grand challenge for AI.** Formal theorem proving sits at the apex of reasoning difficulty — it requires logical deduction, domain knowledge (algebra, analysis, number theory, topology), strategic planning (which lemma to prove next, which induction hypothesis to use), and syntactic precision (writing valid Lean code). Progress on this benchmark serves as a leading indicator for AI's capacity to perform reliable, verifiable reasoning. Unlike natural language QA or code generation, where correctness is often subjective or probabilistic, formal theorem proving offers an objective oracle: the proof compiles, or it doesn't. This makes it an unusually clean testbed for measuring genuine reasoning capability rather than pattern matching.

**Proof assistants are becoming practical tools for mathematicians.** Projects like Mathlib4 (Lean's mathematical library) aim to formalize vast swaths of modern mathematics in a machine-checkable format. But the current bottleneck is the sheer labor required: formalizing a single page of human mathematics can take days or weeks for an expert. Automated theorem provers that can reduce this burden — by generating proof sketches that humans then refine, or by handling routine lemmas automatically — could accelerate the formalization of mathematics, enabling machine verification of complex results in fields from cryptography to physics. The paper's focus on Lean 4 is strategic: Lean 4 is the most actively developed and widely adopted proof assistant in this space, and Mathlib4 is the largest unified mathematical library.

**Verifiable reasoning has downstream applications beyond mathematics.** The techniques developed here — training models to produce outputs that pass a formal verifier, using RL with binary compiler feedback, combining whole-output generation with fine-grained state feedback — have analogs in code generation (where unit tests can serve as verifiers), hardware design (where synthesis tools check correctness), and any domain where a formal specification exists. Advancing the state of the art in formal theorem proving provides a template for building AI systems that can reliably satisfy formal constraints.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper situates itself relative to two established paradigms in neural theorem proving (Section 3.5), each with distinct strengths and limitations:

**Paradigm 1: Multi-pass proof-step generation.** This approach, exemplified by GPT-f (Polu and Sutskever, 2020), Thor (Jiang et al., 2022), ReProver (Yang et al., 2023), Hypertree Proof Search (Lample et al., 2022), and InternLM2-StepProver (Wu et al., 2024), breaks the proving process into a sequence of individual tactic generations. At each step, the model receives the current proof state (all hypotheses and goals) from Lean, generates a single tactic, submits it for verification, and repeats. This tight feedback loop ensures the model always operates with accurate state information — it never hallucinates the proof state because Lean provides it after every step.

The critical advantage is precision: the model cannot compound errors across multiple tactics because each tactic is verified before the next is generated. This enables sophisticated tree search algorithms (like the Monte Carlo tree search in HTPS or the best-first search in GPT-f) to explore the space of possible proofs systematically, backing up and trying alternatives when a branch fails.

The critical disadvantage is **communication overhead**. Each tactic generation requires a round-trip between the language model (typically running on a GPU) and the Lean verifier (running on a CPU). For proofs requiring dozens of tactics, this creates a serial dependency chain: the model must wait for Lean to verify step $n$ before it can begin generating step $n+1$. This makes multi-pass methods computationally expensive in wall-clock time and limits the degree of parallelization. Furthermore, the language model must be invoked once per tactic rather than once per proof, multiplying the total number of model calls by the average proof length.

**Paradigm 2: Single-pass whole-proof generation.** This approach, used by DSP (Jiang et al., 2022), Subgoal-Prover (Zhao et al., 2023), LEGO-Prover (Wang et al., 2023), Lyra (Zheng et al., 2023), miniCTX (Hu et al., 2024), and DeepSeek-Prover-V1 (Xin et al., 2024), generates the entire proof in one shot and then verifies it. If the proof fails, the model generates a fresh attempt from scratch.

The critical advantage is **computational efficiency**. The model makes one forward pass to generate the full proof, Lean verifies it once, and the process is trivially parallelizable across multiple attempts (generate $N$ proofs simultaneously, verify them in parallel). There is no serial communication bottleneck.

The critical disadvantage is **lack of intermediate state information**. The model must implicitly track the proof state across the entire generation, and any error in this implicit tracking renders the remainder of the proof invalid. This is the compounding error problem discussed earlier. Moreover, when verification fails, the model receives an error message but cannot easily localize *where* in the proof the error occurred or *what aspect* of the reasoning was wrong. The feedback is per-proof, not per-step, making it difficult for the model to learn fine-grained proof-writing skills.

**The gap: no approach combined the strengths of both paradigms.** Prior to DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5, a model was either a proof-step generator (with accurate state feedback but serial overhead) or a whole-proof generator (with efficient batch inference but no intermediate feedback). There was no framework for a single model to operate in both modes simultaneously, or for a whole-proof generator to recover intermediate state information when needed without sacrificing the parallel efficiency of whole-proof generation.

### Specific Limitations of DeepSeek-Prover-V1

Since this paper is a direct successor to DeepSeek-Prover-V1 (Xin et al., 2024), it inherits and addresses several specific limitations of that system:

**Whole-proof generation without state feedback.** DeepSeek-Prover-V1 generated entire proofs and relied on retrying from scratch when verification failed. It had no mechanism to recover the successful prefix of a failed proof attempt or to incorporate tactic-state information into subsequent generations. Failed attempts were completely discarded, even if 90% of the tactics were correct.

**No reinforcement learning from proof assistant feedback.** DeepSeek-Prover-V1 relied entirely on supervised fine-tuning on synthetic proof data. It did not use the Lean compiler's feedback as a training signal during a reinforcement learning phase. This meant the model was optimized to imitate proof patterns in the training data, not to maximize the probability of producing verifiably correct proofs — a classic distribution mismatch between training objective and evaluation metric.

**No tree search during inference.** DeepSeek-Prover-V1 used pure parallel sampling: generate $N$ independent proofs, verify them, and take the first correct one. It had no mechanism for systematic exploration of the proof space — no search tree, no backtracking from failed branches, no state-guided expansion. If the model consistently made a particular type of error on a given theorem, generating more independent samples would not help because each sample suffered from the same blind spots.

**Limited mathematical reasoning in proof code.** The paper notes that DeepSeek-Prover-V1 proofs often consisted of sequences of high-level tactic calls without explanatory reasoning. The model learned to apply tactics like `nlinarith` or `simp` rote, without the kind of structured mathematical thinking that would allow it to recover when those tactics failed or to decompose complex goals into manageable subgoals.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's core innovation is **bridging the two paradigms** through the truncate-and-resume mechanism (Section 3.1). The key insight: **a whole-proof generation model can be retrofitted with proof-step feedback without changing the underlying generation paradigm.** When a generated proof fails verification, the system doesn't discard it — it identifies the first error, truncates the proof at that point (keeping all successfully verified tactics), extracts the current tactic state from Lean, and feeds this state information back to the model as a comment block appended to the successful prefix. The model then *resumes* generation from this informed starting point.

This is fundamentally different from both prior paradigms:
- Unlike pure whole-proof generation, the model now receives accurate intermediate state information and doesn't start from scratch on each attempt — it builds on successful work.
- Unlike pure proof-step generation, the model still generates multiple tactics at once (whole-proof generation), avoiding the per-tactic communication bottleneck. The truncation and resumption happen only when errors are detected, not on every single tactic step.
- Unlike tree search methods that require the model to generate one tactic at a time, RMaxTS expands the search tree by generating *paths* of multiple tactics in each expansion step, leveraging the efficiency of whole-proof generation within a tree search framework.

The paper frames its contributions as components of an **AlphaZero-like pipeline** (Section 5): expert iteration generates synthetic training data, reinforcement learning optimizes the model using compiler feedback as the reward signal, and Monte Carlo tree search explores the proof space systematically at inference time. The explicit analogy to AlphaZero (Silver et al., 2016, 2018) is revealing: just as AlphaZero combined a policy network (proposing moves) with MCTS (systematically exploring the game tree using those proposals), DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 combines a proof generation model (proposing proof completions) with RMaxTS (systematically exploring the proof search tree using those completions, guided by intrinsic rewards for discovering novel tactic states).

On the training side, the paper positions **reinforcement learning from proof assistant feedback (RLPAF)** as a genuine capability improver, not merely a top-K selection enhancer. This is a deliberate contrast to findings in natural language mathematics (DeepSeekMath, Shao et al., 2024), where RL primarily improved the model's ability to select the best from a set of candidates rather than improving its fundamental reasoning. The paper claims that in formal theorem proving — where verification is objective and binary — RL provides "a genuine enhancement of fundamental capabilities" (Section 2.4), evident even at small sample budgets.

On the inference side, the paper positions **RMaxTS as solving the exploration problem in sparse-reward proof search**. In standard MCTS for games like Go or Chess, rewards are dense — every terminal state provides a win/loss signal, and value functions can be learned to evaluate intermediate states. In theorem proving, rewards are extremely sparse: a search tree might contain thousands of nodes, but only the handful that represent complete, correct proofs yield a reward of 1. The rest yield 0. Standard MCTS with UCB exploration (driven solely by visitation counts) degenerates in this setting because it has no signal to distinguish promising but incomplete branches from dead ends. The paper's solution — intrinsic rewards for discovering novel tactic states, combined with discounted UCB to handle the non-stationarity of these intrinsic rewards as the search tree grows — is explicitly motivated by the hard-exploration literature in reinforcement learning (Krishnamurthy et al., 2016; Jin et al., 2020; Schmidhuber, 2010; Brafman and Tennenholtz, 2002).

### The Specific Gap in Reward Signal Design

A subtle but crucial motivation that the paper develops in Section 3.3 is the **non-stationarity of intrinsic rewards in tree search**. When intrinsic rewards are defined as "did we discover a new tactic state?", the expected value of this reward decreases over time: early in the search, almost every expansion discovers new states; later, most expansions revisit known states. Standard UCB1 (Auer et al., 2002) assumes stationary reward distributions and computes exploration bonuses based on *total* visit counts and *total* accumulated rewards. Under non-stationary intrinsic rewards, UCB1's value estimates are dominated by outdated high rewards from early exploration, causing the algorithm to over-exploit nodes that were novel early on but are no longer promising.

The paper's solution — discounted UCB (DUCB; Garivier and Moulines, 2011) with discount factor $\gamma = 0.99$ — exponentially downweights older reward observations, ensuring the value estimate tracks the *current* rate of novelty discovery rather than the historical average. This is a non-obvious design choice that directly addresses the interaction between intrinsic rewards and tree search dynamics. The paper validates this choice empirically (Figure 5): RMaxTS with standard UCB1 performs nearly as poorly as UCT without intrinsic rewards, confirming that the UCB variant is not a minor implementation detail but is essential to making intrinsic rewards work in this setting.

### Summary of Positioning

The paper sits at the convergence of three research threads:

1. **Neural theorem proving**, specifically whole-proof generation methods, which are computationally efficient but lack intermediate feedback and systematic search.
2. **Monte Carlo tree search with language models**, where the challenge is adapting MCTS (designed for discrete action spaces with small branching factors) to the open-ended generation space of an LLM.
3. **Reinforcement learning for language models**, where the challenge is extracting useful training signal from binary, sparse, delayed rewards (compiler verification) without a learned critic model.

The paper's unified contribution is showing that these threads can be woven together — a single model, trained with RL from compiler feedback, can serve as both an efficient whole-proof generator and a flexible proof-step generator within a tree search framework, and that intrinsic-reward-driven exploration can overcome the reward sparsity that would otherwise render tree search ineffective.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 is a **7-billion-parameter language model pipeline for formal theorem proving in Lean 4** that combines whole-proof generation with a truncate-and-resume mechanism and an intrinsic-reward-driven Monte Carlo tree search algorithm called RMaxTS. The system addresses the fundamental tension in neural theorem proving: **how to get accurate intermediate proof-state feedback (like proof-step generation methods) without sacrificing the computational efficiency of whole-proof generation**, by retrofitting a whole-proof generator to recover and resume from successful proof prefixes when verification fails, and to explore the proof space systematically using intrinsic rewards for discovering novel tactic states rather than relying solely on the sparse binary reward of a completed proof.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components, organized into a training pipeline and an inference pipeline that share a single language model backbone:

1. **DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-Base** — a 7B-parameter language model pre-trained on mathematics, code, and formal proof languages (Lean, Isabelle, Metamath) to establish strong priors for formal theorem proving. This is the foundation on which all subsequent components build.

2. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) Module** — takes the base model and fine-tunes it on an augmented dataset of Lean 4 proof code (~9.6M sequences) that includes (a) natural language chain-of-thought comments interleaved with tactic code, and (b) intermediate tactic-state information inserted as comment blocks, training the model to both reason mathematically in natural language and to utilize compiler feedback during proof generation. Output: DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-SFT.

3. **Reinforcement Learning from Proof Assistant Feedback (RLPAF) Module** — takes the SFT model and optimizes it using the GRPO algorithm with Lean's binary verification result (0 or 1) as the reward signal, sampling 32 candidate proofs per theorem and optimizing against the relative rewards within each group. Output: DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL.

4. **RMaxTS Monte Carlo Tree Search Engine** — takes the RL-trained model and constructs a proof search tree at the tactic level, where tree nodes represent proof states and edges represent tactic applications. The search uses a truncate-and-resume mechanism (parse generated proofs, keep the successful prefix up to the first error, extract the current tactic state from Lean, and resume generation from that prefix with the state as a comment) and an intrinsic-reward-driven selection policy that rewards discovering novel tactic states using discounted upper confidence bounds rather than standard UCB1. This operates post-training during inference.

Information flows as follows during **inference**: a theorem statement enters → the RL-trained model generates a whole-proof completion → Lean verifies the completed code → if correct, search terminates → if an error is detected, the proof is truncated at the first error → the successfully verified prefix is parsed into individual tactics, each becoming a node in the search tree → the current tactic state is extracted from Lean and appended as a comment to the prefix → the model generates a new proof segment from this augmented prompt → the process repeats until a correct proof is found or the sample budget is exhausted.

During **training**, the flow is: pre-trained base model → SFT on augmented proof data with tactic-state comments and chain-of-thought reasoning → RL using GRPO with Lean verification as reward → the RL-trained model is used both for single-pass generation and as the proposal engine within RMaxTS.

- **First, the pre-training stage (Section 2.1)**: what data the base model is trained on, what capabilities this establishes, and why formal languages require specialized pre-training beyond general mathematics.

This is primarily a **systems and methods paper** that builds a unified pipeline for formal theorem proving, where the core technical contributions are: (1) augmenting whole-proof generation with tactic-level state feedback through a truncate-and-resume mechanism, (2) applying online reinforcement learning from Lean compiler feedback to improve proof generation capabilities, and (3) designing an intrinsic-reward-driven Monte Carlo tree search algorithm that overcomes the extreme reward sparsity of formal proof search.

#### Pre-Training on Formal Mathematical Languages

The pre-training stage establishes the base model's fundamental capabilities in formal theorem proving and mathematical reasoning. The model is initialized from DeepSeekMath-Base (Shao et al., 2024), a 7B-parameter language model already trained on extensive mathematical corpora, and then undergoes further pre-training on high-quality datasets that include both code and natural language mathematical content, with specific focus on formal languages used in proof assistants (Lean, Isabelle, and Metamath). The purpose of this specialized pre-training is to teach the model the syntax and conventions of formal proof systems — vocabulary like `theorem`, `by`, `rw`, `simp`, `constructor`, `induction`, and dozens of other tactic keywords — before it ever attempts to generate complete proofs. This is essential because formal proof languages have rigid syntactic requirements that differ substantially from natural language mathematics; without exposure to formal language corpora, the model would struggle even to produce syntactically valid proof attempts.

The paper provides limited detail on the exact pre-training corpus composition (e.g., the ratio of formal language data to general mathematics or code data, the total number of tokens, or the pre-training hyperparameters), but states that the pre-trained model is designated as **DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-Base**. The effectiveness of this pre-training is validated immediately: the base model achieves a pass@128 of 29.7% on miniF2F-test using only 3-shot prompting (Figure 3), demonstrating that even without fine-tuning on Lean-specific proof data, the model has internalized sufficient knowledge of Lean syntax and mathematical reasoning to solve nearly one-third of high-school competition problems. For ProofNet, the base model achieves pass@128 of 9.7% with 3-shot prompting.

**Why formal language pre-training matters**: formal proof languages are fundamentally different from both natural language and general-purpose programming languages. They combine the type-theoretic rigor of dependently-typed programming with the strategic reasoning of mathematical proof construction. A model pre-trained only on natural language mathematics or general code would encounter entirely unfamiliar syntactic structures (tactic blocks, term mode vs. tactic mode, binder syntax like `(h₀ : ∀ x, ...)`) and would waste SFT capacity learning these basics rather than learning proof strategy. By establishing formal language competency during pre-training, the subsequent fine-tuning stages can focus on teaching the model *how to prove theorems* rather than *how to write Lean*.

#### Supervised Fine-Tuning: Data Curation and Augmentation

The supervised fine-tuning stage transforms the base model (which can generate syntactically valid Lean code but has limited proof-writing skill) into a specialized theorem prover by training on a carefully constructed dataset of ~9.6M proof sequences. The data curation process involves three key innovations: expert iteration for data generation, thought-augmented proof generation, and tactic-state insertion.

##### Expert Iteration for Proof Data Generation

The paper employs an **expert iteration** process (Polu and Sutskever, 2020) to generate the training data. Expert iteration is a self-improvement loop with four steps, repeated over multiple cycles:

1. **Generate proofs** using the current best language model. In early iterations, this starts from a relatively weak model; in later iterations, it uses progressively stronger models trained on data from previous iterations.

2. **Verify the generated proofs** using the Lean 4 prover. Only proofs that pass full verification are retained — all others are discarded. This ensures the training data contains only correct proofs, eliminating the risk of the model learning from incorrect examples.

3. **Retrain the model** on the accumulated set of verified proofs (augmented with the techniques described below).

4. **Use the retrained model** to generate additional proofs in the next iteration, exploring a wider range of theorems.

This loop converges toward a model that can reliably prove theorems from the training distribution while simultaneously expanding the coverage of the training data to include more challenging or diverse theorems. The paper does not report the number of expert iterations performed or the initial model used to seed the process, but notes that the data sources include Mathlib4 (the standard Lean 4 mathematical library), synthetic theorems from DeepSeek-Prover-V1 (Xin et al., 2024) and Lean Workbook (Ying et al., 2024), and the validation sets of miniF2F and ProofNet (used to provide diverse theorem statements, though the corresponding test problems are excluded to prevent contamination).

**Why expert iteration**: pure human-written formal proofs are scarce and expensive to produce. Mathlib4 contains thousands of theorems, but many are specialized lemmas that don't teach general proof-writing patterns. Expert iteration generates a massive amount of synthetic proof data that is guaranteed correct (verified by Lean), covers a broad range of mathematical domains, and is on-distribution for the model (generated by the model or its predecessors, so the proof style and tactic choices match the model's capabilities).

##### Thought-Augmented Proof Generation

The first major data augmentation is inserting natural language chain-of-thought reasoning into the proof code. The motivation comes from an observation about DeepSeek-Prover-V1's behavior (Section 2.2):

> "In natural language, models generate detailed deduction steps to construct proofs, whereas in Lean, they often rely on a sequence of high-level tactic calls to brute-force solutions. These high-level tactics, while effective, obscure their internal workings and outcomes, hindering the model's ability to resolve complex proof goals with structured mathematical reasoning."

To address this, the paper uses **DeepSeek-Coder V2 236B** (Zhu et al., 2024), a much larger and more capable model, to annotate the training data with natural language explanations in two forms:

1. **Complete solution-level reasoning**: a natural language explanation of the overall proof strategy inserted as a comment at the beginning of the proof block (before any tactics). This teaches the model to plan the proof holistically before executing tactics.

2. **Step-level reasoning**: specific natural language explanations inserted before individual tactics, explaining what each tactic does and why it advances the proof. This teaches the model to annotate its own tactical decisions with mathematical reasoning.

These annotations are formatted as Lean comments (using `--` for single-line comments and `/- ... -/` for block comments), which the Lean compiler ignores during verification but which the language model can learn to generate. The training data includes both **CoT mode** examples (with chain-of-thought comments) and **non-CoT mode** examples (without such comments), distinguished by different guiding prompts, so that the model learns to operate in both modes depending on the prompt it receives.

**Example of the format** (from Appendix A, the `aime_1983_p9` problem in CoT mode):

```
/-
To find the minimum value of (9x²sin²x + 4)/(x sin x) for 0 < x < π,
we need to show that it is at least 12. We start by noting that the
expression can be rewritten using the division property of inequalities.
We then use the fact that sin x and x are positive in the given range
to establish the necessary inequalities. Finally, we apply these results
to conclude that the minimum value is indeed 12.
-/
-- We start by ensuring that the product x * sin x is positive in the
given range.
have h1 : 0 < x * Real.sin x := by
  apply mul_pos
  -- x is positive in the range (0, π).
  exact h0.1
  -- sin x is positive in the range (0, π).
  exact Real.sin_pos_of_pos_of_lt_pi h0.1 h0.2
-- Using the division property of inequalities, we rewrite the expression.
rw [le_div_iff h1]
```

The block comment at the top provides the overall plan, and inline comments before each tactic explain the tactical reasoning. In non-CoT mode, this same proof would omit the natural language comments entirely, containing only the Lean code.

**Why this augmentation helps**: high-level tactics like `nlinarith` and `aesop` are powerful but opaque. When a model relies on them exclusively, it fails to develop the fine-grained reasoning capability needed for theorems where these tactics are insufficient. By training the model to generate natural language explanations alongside tactics, the CoT mode enforces that the model actually *understands* the mathematical reasoning — it must articulate the intermediate steps in natural language before encoding them as tactics. This creates a virtuous cycle: natural language reasoning guides tactic selection, and failed tactic applications can be diagnosed by comparing them to the intended reasoning.

##### Prompt Augmentation with Tactic State Information

The second major data augmentation is inserting intermediate tactic state information as comment blocks within proof code. This is essential for the truncate-and-resume mechanism that powers the tree search at inference time.

The process works as follows (Section 2.2):

1. **Extract tactic information**: The paper enhances the Lean REPL (Read-Eval-Print Loop; Leanprover Community, 2023) with data extraction tools from the LeanDojo project (Yang et al., 2023). For each tactic in a generated valid proof, the system extracts a **triple**: the position of the tactic in the code, the tactic state *before* the tactic is applied (what hypotheses are available, what goals remain), and the tactic state *after* the tactic is applied (how the hypotheses and goals changed).

2. **Insert tactic state comments**: For each tactic in the training data, the system inserts the pre-application tactic state as a comment block immediately before the tactic, formatted as `/- tactic state: ... -/`. The comment contains a structured representation of the current proof context, e.g.:
```
/- tactic state:
case a
f : ℂ → ℂ
h₀ : ∀ (x : ℂ), f x = (2 * x + 3) * (x - 4) + (2 * x + 3) * (x - 6)
h₁ : Fintype ↑(f ⁻¹' {0})
x : ℂ
⊢ f x = 0 ↔ x = -(3 / 2) ∨ x = 5
-/
```
This shows the current goal (`⊢ f x = 0 ↔ x = -(3/2) ∨ x = 5`) and all available hypotheses, providing a complete snapshot of the proof state at that point.

3. **Auxiliary prediction task**: During training, all tokens *after* the `/- tactic state: ` marker serve as **responses** (contributing to the training loss), while tokens *before* this marker serve as **prompts** (masked from the loss). This means the model is trained to *predict* the tactic state information that should appear after a successful tactic application, alongside predicting the subsequent proof steps. The model thus learns two things simultaneously: what the proof state should look like after each tactic (auxiliary task) and what code to generate next (main task).

**Training objective for SFT**: The paper does not provide the explicit loss function for the SFT stage (which is standard next-token prediction with a causal language modeling objective), but states the key hyperparameters: training continues for **9B tokens** (total tokens processed, not parameters), using a **batch size of 2,048**, a **constant learning rate of 1e-4**, with **100 warm-up steps** to stabilize training dynamics. Training examples are randomly concatenated to form sequences with a **maximum context length of 4,096 tokens**.

The resulting model is designated **DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-SFT**.

**Why insert tactic state information**: the truncate-and-resume mechanism (detailed in Section 3.1) requires the model to continue proof generation from an arbitrary intermediate point, armed with the current tactic state. If the model has never seen tactic state comments during training, it would not know how to interpret them — they would be out-of-distribution tokens that confuse rather than inform. By training the model to expect and utilize tactic state comments, the SFT stage teaches the model to use compiler feedback as a conditioning signal: "given this partial proof and this exact proof state, what tactic should come next?"

This is the key design choice that unifies whole-proof generation with proof-step generation. The model is trained to generate *either* from the theorem statement alone (standard whole-proof generation) *or* from an incomplete proof with tactic state feedback (truncate-and-resume), using the same model weights and the same generation paradigm. The only difference is the prompt format.

#### Reinforcement Learning from Proof Assistant Feedback (RLPAF)

The RL stage takes the SFT model and optimizes it to maximize the probability of generating verifiably correct proofs, using the Lean 4 compiler's binary verification result as the sole reward signal. The core insight is that SFT teaches the model to *imitate* correct proofs, but RL teaches the model to *produce* correct proofs — a crucial difference because imitating a distribution of proofs is not the same as maximizing pass@1 under verification.

##### Prompt Selection for RL Training

Not all theorems are suitable for RL training. If the SFT model already achieves 100% pass rate on a theorem, RL cannot improve it (no room for improvement). If the SFT model achieves 0% pass rate — it never generates a correct proof within the sampling budget — RL receives only zero-reward episodes and learns nothing (no positive signal to reinforce). The ideal prompt for RL is one where the SFT model has a **moderate success rate**: it sometimes generates correct proofs and sometimes doesn't, providing a mix of positive and negative examples that enables the model to learn which proof strategies work and which don't.

The paper's prompt selection strategy (Section 2.3) is:

> "We select theorems for which DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-SFT has a moderate success rate in generating correct proofs upon multiple attempts. This ensures that the model has room for improvement while still being able to receive positive feedback."

After filtering, approximately **4,500 unique theorem statements** are retained from the SFT dataset. Each theorem is prefixed with both CoT and non-CoT guiding prompts, ensuring the model improves in both generation modes. The paper does not specify the exact "moderate success rate" threshold (e.g., 10%–90%, or 20%–80%), but the principle is clear: RL requires non-zero reward probability to be effective.

##### Reward Definition

The reward signal for RL is the simplest possible:

> "Each generated proof receives a reward of 1 if verified as correct, and 0 otherwise."

This is **binary and sparse** — there are no partial rewards for proofs that are "almost correct," no step-level rewards for tactics that are individually valid, and no reward shaping based on distance to the goal. The only signal is whether the complete proof passes Lean's full verification. The sparsity of this reward is the central challenge: for hard theorems, the probability of generating a correct proof from the SFT model might be 1% or less, meaning 99% of RL rollouts yield zero reward and provide no learning signal. The prompt selection strategy (keeping only theorems with moderate success rates) mitigates this by ensuring there are enough positive examples, but the fundamental sparsity remains — the model must learn which of its many incorrect proof strategies to suppress based on a single binary outcome.

**Why binary rewards**: the paper states this explicitly — "formal theorem proving benefits from the rigorous verification of generated proofs by proof assistants, offering a significant advantage" over learned reward models, which can be noisy, miscalibrated, or gameable. The Lean compiler is an oracle: it never makes mistakes about whether a proof is correct. Using the compiler as the reward function eliminates reward model training, reward hacking, and reward distribution shift — problems that plague RLHF in natural language domains. The tradeoff is sparsity: the compiler provides no signal about *why* a proof failed or how to fix it, leaving the RL algorithm to discover this through trial and error.

##### GRPO: Group Relative Policy Optimization

The paper adopts GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as the RL algorithm, rather than the more widely used PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). The key difference between GRPO and PPO, and the reason for choosing GRPO, is that **GRPO does not require a separate critic model** — a neural network trained to predict the expected future reward from any given state. In standard PPO, the critic estimates the *value* of each state, and the advantage (how much better an action was than expected) is computed as the difference between the actual reward and the critic's prediction. This requires training the critic alongside the policy, doubling the computational cost and introducing potential instability from an inaccurate value function.

GRPO replaces the critic with a **group-relative baseline**. For each theorem prompt, the algorithm:

1. **Samples a group of $G$ candidate proofs** from the current policy $\pi_\theta$ (the model being trained). The paper uses $G = 32$ candidates per theorem.

2. **Computes the reward for each candidate** by submitting it to Lean for verification, producing a binary reward $r_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for each candidate $i \in \{1, \dots, G\}$.

3. **Normalizes rewards within the group** to compute advantages. Instead of comparing each reward to a learned value function, GRPO compares each reward to the *mean reward of the group*:

$$A_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}{\text{std}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}$$

where $A_i$ is the advantage for the $i$-th candidate, $r_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is its binary verification reward, $\text{mean}(r_1, \dots, r_G)$ is the average reward across the group of $G=32$ candidates, and $\text{std}(r_1, \dots, r_G)$ is the standard deviation of rewards within the group.

**What this equation computes**: the standardized advantage for each proof candidate relative to the group. If a candidate receives a reward of 1 and the group mean is 0.2, the advantage is positive (this candidate was better than average). If a candidate receives a reward of 0 and the group mean is 0.2, the advantage is negative (this candidate was worse than average). The normalization by standard deviation ensures that advantages are on a consistent scale regardless of the overall difficulty of the theorem.

**Operationally**: for each of the 32 candidates, the system checks whether the proof compiles in Lean, produces the 32 binary rewards, computes the mean and standard deviation, and assigns each candidate a standardized advantage score — a single real number that says how much better or worse this particular candidate was relative to the group's average performance.

**Why this form**: the group-relative normalization eliminates the need for a critic model. The group mean serves as a baseline that estimates the expected reward for this particular theorem, and the standardization ensures the advantages are unit-free (measured in standard deviations). This works particularly well for binary verification rewards because the distribution of rewards within a group of 32 candidates is informative: if a theorem is easy, most candidates will be correct (high mean, low variance); if it's hard, most will be incorrect (low mean, low variance); if it's at the model's frontier of capability, the group will contain a mix of successes and failures (intermediate mean, high variance), which is exactly the regime where RL provides the strongest learning signal. The paper's prompt selection strategy (keeping theorems with moderate success rates) ensures that groups often have this mixed composition.

4. **Optimizes the policy** using a clipped surrogate objective with a KL penalty:

$$\mathcal{L}^{\text{GRPO}} = \mathbb{E}_{(q, \mathbf{y}) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \min \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid q)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i \mid q)} A_i, \text{clip} \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid q)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i \mid q)}, 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon \right) A_i \right) - \beta \, D_{\text{KL}} \left( \pi_\theta \, \| \, \pi_{\text{ref}} \right) \right]$$

where $\pi_\theta$ is the current policy (the model being updated), $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the reference policy (the SFT model, frozen and used to prevent the RL model from diverging too far from its SFT initialization), $A_i$ is the group-relative advantage computed above, $\epsilon$ is the clipping parameter (typically 0.2, not explicitly stated in the paper), $\beta$ is the KL penalty coefficient (set to 0.02), and $D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the current policy and the reference policy.

**What this equation computes**: the expected clipped surrogate loss with a KL penalty. For each candidate proof (with its advantage $A_i$), the algorithm computes the probability ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid q)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i \mid q)}$, which measures how much more (or less) likely the current model is to generate this proof compared to the reference model. If the advantage is positive, the algorithm increases the current model's probability of generating this proof — but only up to a factor of $1 + \epsilon$, preventing excessively large updates that could destabilize training. If the advantage is negative, the algorithm decreases the probability — but only down to a factor of $1 - \epsilon$. The KL penalty term $\beta D_{\text{KL}}$ encourages the model to stay close to the reference distribution, preventing catastrophic forgetting of the SFT knowledge. The `min` operation ensures that for positive advantages, the algorithm uses the smaller of the clipped and unclipped ratios (preventing overly aggressive updates), and for negative advantages, it uses the larger (preventing overly aggressive suppression).

**Operationally**: the system takes each candidate proof, computes its log-probability under both the current model and the reference model, takes the ratio, clips it, multiplies by the standardized advantage, subtracts the KL penalty, and averages across all candidates and all prompts in the batch. This produces a single scalar loss that is minimized via gradient descent.

**Why this form**: the clipped surrogate objective is a standard PPO innovation that prevents the policy from changing too much in a single update. Without clipping, a candidate with a very high advantage could cause the model to massively increase its probability, potentially causing it to overfit to that specific proof and lose generalization. The KL penalty adds a second layer of regularization, ensuring the model doesn't diverge from its SFT initialization — crucial because the SFT model has learned proof-writing skills from 9.6M examples, and RL on only 4.5K prompts could easily overwrite this knowledge if unconstrained.

**Training hyperparameters**: the RL training uses a **constant learning rate of 5e-6**, the **KL penalty coefficient $\beta = 0.02$**, **32 candidate proofs per theorem** ($G=32$), **maximum proof length of 2,048 tokens**, and a **training batch size of 512**. The SFT model serves as both the initial model and the reference model for the KL divergence penalty. The resulting model is designated **DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL**.

**Why GRPO over PPO**: the paper states that GRPO "has demonstrated superior effectiveness and efficiency compared to PPO, primarily because it eliminates the necessity of training an additional critic model." This is a practical advantage in the theorem-proving setting where the state space (partial proofs with tactic states) is high-dimensional and a critic would be challenging to train. More subtly, the group-relative baseline is particularly well-suited to binary rewards with varying per-prompt difficulties: the baseline automatically adapts to each prompt's difficulty because it is computed from the group of candidates for that specific prompt, not from a global value function that must generalize across all prompts.

**Why RL provides genuine capability improvement**: Figure 3 shows that RL improves pass@K across all values of K, not just at high K. If RL merely improved the model's ability to select the best from a set of candidates (a "top-K enhancer"), the improvement would be largest at high K and negligible at low K. The paper observes the opposite: RL provides a consistent improvement from K=1 through K=128, indicating that the model is generating better proofs in each individual attempt, not just ranking them better. The paper explicitly contrasts this with findings from natural language mathematics (DeepSeekMath), where RL primarily boosted top-K performance.

#### Tactic-Level Tree Abstraction (Truncate-and-Resume)

The tree search mechanism operates at the **tactic level**, not the token level. Each node in the search tree represents a proof state (the collection of hypotheses and goals at that point in the proof), and each edge represents a single tactic application that transforms one proof state into another. The key innovation is the **truncate-and-resume mechanism** that converts whole-proof generations into these tactic-level tree nodes without requiring the model to generate one tactic at a time.

##### Truncate: Decomposing Whole-Proof Generations into Tree Nodes

When the model generates a proof attempt (whether from scratch or as a continuation from a partial prefix), the system:

1. **Submits the complete generated proof to Lean** for verification. This is the standard whole-proof generation step — the model produces the full proof code, and Lean checks it.

2. **If the proof is correct and complete**, the search procedure terminates immediately — a valid proof has been found. This is the success condition.

3. **If an error is detected**, the system identifies **the earliest verification error** — the first point in the generated proof where Lean encounters a problem (e.g., a tactic fails to apply, a type mismatch occurs, or a missing hypothesis prevents a rewrite). The system truncates the proof at this error, keeping all code before the error (which was successfully verified) and discarding everything after (which Lean never even considered).

4. **Parses the successful prefix into tactics**: the verified portion of the proof is segmented into individual tactic applications, each associated with its chain-of-thought comments (if in CoT mode). Each tactic represents a single proof step that was successfully verified by Lean.

5. **Creates tree nodes for each tactic**: each tactic becomes a node in the search tree, forming a path from the root (the theorem statement) to the frontier (the last successfully applied tactic). The edges between nodes represent tactic applications, and each node stores the proof state *after* its associated tactic is applied.

Figure 4 in the paper illustrates this process. A whole-proof generation that fails at a later tactic still yields a valid prefix of successfully applied tactics. These tactics are not discarded — they become permanent nodes in the search tree, and future search iterations can resume from any of them.

**Why this decomposition works**: the key property is that each tactic in the verified prefix has been independently confirmed by Lean to be correct. The failure at step $k$ does not invalidate the success of steps $1$ through $k-1$. By extracting these verified tactics, the system accumulates a library of valid proof fragments — even from failed proof attempts — that can serve as jumping-off points for future searches. This is fundamentally different from pure whole-proof generation, where a failed attempt yields zero information about which parts were correct.

##### Resume: Proof Generation from a Tree Node

When the tree search selects a node for expansion, the system needs to generate the next segment of the proof from that node's state. The resume process:

1. **Constructs the prompt**: the system takes the incomplete proof code stored along the path from the root to the selected node (all tactics that have been successfully applied so far), and appends the **current tactic state** as a comment block. This tactic state is extracted from Lean by replaying the verified tactics and capturing the state at the selected node. For example, if the last successfully applied tactic was `rw [le_div_iff h1]`, the appended comment shows:
```
/- tactic state:
x : ℝ
h0 : 0 < x ∧ x < π
h1 : 0 < x * x.sin
⊢ 12 * (x * x.sin) ≤ 9 * (x ^ 2 * x.sin ^ 2) + 4
-/
```

2. **Selects a tactic code from the node's set**: In Lean 4, different tactic codes can produce the same tactic state (e.g., two different sequences of `rw` commands might lead to identical proof obligations). To handle this, each tree node stores a **set of equivalent tactic codes** — all the different ways the search has reached this same proof state. When expanding a node, the system randomly selects one of these tactic codes to use as the continuation point. This is important because different proof syntaxes, even if equivalent in their effect on the proof state, provide different textual contexts for the model's next generation.

3. **Generates a whole-proof completion**: the model takes the concatenated prompt (incomplete code + selected tactic + tactic state comment) and generates the remainder of the proof in one shot — exactly as it would in whole-proof generation, but now conditioned on accurate intermediate-state information.

4. **Verifies and iterates**: the newly generated completion is submitted to Lean, and the process repeats — either the proof is complete (success), or the system truncates at the first error, extracts new verified tactics, adds them to the tree, and selects a new node for expansion.

**The crucial property of this mechanism**: it unifies proof-step generation and whole-proof generation. In each expansion step, the model generates *multiple* tactics at once (a proof segment, not a single tactic), preserving the computational efficiency of whole-proof generation. But by truncating at the first error and extracting the successful prefix, the system recovers all the benefits of step-level verification — accurate state information, the ability to build on partial successes, and the avoidance of compounding errors because the model is always conditioned on the true proof state (not its hallucinated state) when generating subsequent tactics. The search tree accumulates verified tactics from many different proof attempts, building a graph of known-good proof fragments that can be combined and extended.

**Why the virtual node trick**: in a standard MCTS for a game like Go, the number of possible actions from each state is fixed and known in advance (all legal moves). In theorem proving, the number of possible next tactics from a proof state is unbounded — the model can generate any valid Lean code, and there's no way to enumerate all possibilities. This is the "generative model" problem: the action space is defined by the language model's output distribution, not by a fixed set of rules. The paper's solution is the **virtual node** technique (Section 3.2): each tree node is assigned an imaginary child (denoted by the special token $\oslash$) that represents the action of "expand this node further." When the selection policy chooses the virtual child, the tree search agent invokes the model to generate new completions from that node, potentially adding new children. This allows the tree to grow dynamically — nodes can be expanded multiple times, each expansion potentially discovering new children that weren't present in previous expansions.

More specifically, the action space at a tree node $s$ is $\text{Children}(s) \cup \{\oslash\}$, where $\text{Children}(s)$ are the existing child nodes (tactics that have been generated and verified from this state in previous expansions) and $\oslash$ is the special action of invoking the model to generate new proof segments, potentially adding new children. The selection policy evaluates all actions (including the virtual node) using the Q-value and UCB bonus, and if the virtual node is selected, the expansion step is triggered — the model generates new tactics from $s$, they are verified, and new child nodes are added to the tree.

#### RMaxTS: Intrinsic-Reward-Driven Monte Carlo Tree Search

The RMaxTS algorithm adapts standard MCTS to the extreme reward sparsity of formal proof search by introducing intrinsic rewards that incentivize discovering novel proof states, combined with a discounted UCB mechanism that handles the non-stationarity of these rewards.

##### The Standard MCTS Loop (with Modifications)

RMaxTS follows the standard four-phase MCTS loop, with the **simulation phase integrated into expansion** because the whole-proof generation model inherently performs a rollout (generating multiple tactics at once) rather than predicting a single next action:

1. **Selection**: Starting from the root node (the theorem statement), the algorithm traverses the tree downward using a tree policy that balances exploitation (visiting nodes with high estimated value) and exploration (visiting nodes that haven't been tried much). The tree policy is applied recursively: at each node $s$, the algorithm selects the action $a$ (either an existing child or the virtual node $\oslash$) that maximizes the Q-value plus exploration bonus, and moves to the resulting state. If the virtual node is selected, the traversal stops at $s$ and proceeds to expansion.

2. **Expansion**: The model generates a whole-proof completion from the selected node $s$ (using the resume mechanism described above), submits it to Lean for verification, and the proof is truncated at the first error. The successfully verified prefix of tactics is parsed and added as a path of new nodes in the search tree, extending from $s$. Unlike standard MCTS for games (which typically adds one child per expansion), this expansion may add *multiple* nodes at once — one for each verified tactic in the segment.

3. **Simulation**: Integrated into expansion — the whole-proof generation itself serves as a rollout from the expanded state. There is no separate simulation phase because the model generates the entire remainder of the proof in one shot, and the verification result (success or earliest error) determines the outcome.

4. **Backpropagation**: The reward obtained from the expansion is propagated back along the selection trajectory from the root to the expanded node, updating the value statistics ($Q$ and $N$) for each state-action pair that was visited during selection.

##### Selection Policy: QUCB with Virtual Nodes

The tree policy at a node $s$ selects the action that maximizes an optimistic value estimate:

$$\text{TreePolicy}(s) = \arg\max_{a \in \text{Children}(s) \cup \{\oslash\}} Q_{\text{UCB}}(s, a)$$

where $a$ ranges over all existing children of $s$ plus the virtual node $\oslash$ representing the option to expand $s$ further, and $Q_{\text{UCB}}(s, a)$ is the sum of an exploitation term and an exploration bonus.

The value estimate $Q_{\text{UCB}}(s, a)$ is composed of two components:

$$Q_{\text{UCB}}(s, a) = \underbrace{Q(s, a)}_{\text{Exploitation}} + \underbrace{\text{UCB}(s, a)}_{\text{Exploration}}$$

where $Q(s, a)$ is a sample-based estimate of the expected reward for taking action $a$ from state $s$ (how often this action has led to proof completions in previous iterations), and $\text{UCB}(s, a)$ is an exploration bonus that decreases as the action is selected more frequently, encouraging the algorithm to try under-explored branches.

**What this equation computes**: the optimistic estimated value of taking action $a$ from state $s$. The exploitation term $Q(s, a)$ pulls the algorithm toward actions that have historically led to high rewards (completed proofs). The exploration term $\text{UCB}(s, a)$ pulls the algorithm toward actions that haven't been tried enough — their true value is uncertain, and the optimistic bonus assumes they might be better than they currently appear. The $\arg\max$ over all actions ensures that at each step, the algorithm selects the action with the highest combined score. When applied recursively from the root, this defines a path through the search tree that balances depth (exploiting known-good branches) and breadth (exploring new possibilities).

**Why the virtual node**: without the virtual node, the selection policy could only choose among existing children — once all children of a node have been tried, the algorithm would have no way to further explore that node unless it had infinitely many children (which it doesn't, because the model's output distribution is finite in practice). The virtual node $\oslash$ provides an infinite horizon for exploration: even after many children have been discovered at a node, the virtual node can still be selected, triggering another expansion that may discover new children. This is essential because the model's first few continuations from a node might all fail at the same point, but later completions (conditioned on different random seeds or prompt variations) might discover successful paths.

##### The Reward Sparsity Problem and Intrinsic Rewards

In formal theorem proving, the **extrinsic reward** from the Lean compiler is binary and extremely sparse:

$$R_{\text{extrinsic}}(\tau) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the proof is complete and verified} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where $\tau$ is the selection trajectory for the current iteration. Only complete, correct proofs receive a reward of 1; all incomplete or incorrect proofs receive 0. This means the vast majority of nodes in the search tree have $Q(s, a) = 0$ — they have never led to a completed proof, and the algorithm has no way to distinguish between a node that is "promising but incomplete" (e.g., a partial proof that is correct so far and just needs more tactics) and a node that is "dead" (e.g., a proof that made an unfixable mistake several tactics ago).

Standard MCTS with UCT (Kocsis and Szepesvári, 2006) relies on the UCB exploration bonus to break ties among zero-reward nodes. But with only visitation counts driving exploration, the algorithm degenerates into essentially random search — it expands nodes uniformly without any signal about which branches are more likely to succeed. The paper confirms this empirically (Figure 5): "UCT (without $R_{\text{intrinsic}}$)" performs no better than single-pass generation, because the search adds no value beyond the underlying model's generation quality.

The paper's solution is **intrinsic rewards** — rewards generated by the search algorithm itself, not by the environment (Lean), that incentivize the discovery of novel proof states. The core idea comes from RMax (Brafman and Tennenholtz, 2002) and curiosity-driven exploration (Schmidhuber, 2010): the agent effectively rewards itself for reaching states it hasn't seen before, encouraging it to explore widely and build a diverse search tree. In the context of proof search, a "state" is the tactic state (the collection of hypotheses and goals) after a tactic application, and "novel" means this exact combination of hypotheses and goals has not been recorded in the search tree before.

The specific intrinsic reward for an expansion step is:

$$R_{\text{intrinsic}}(\tau) = \mathbb{I}[\text{at least one new node is added to the search tree}]$$

where $\mathbb{I}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function (1 if the condition is true, 0 otherwise), and $\tau$ is the selection trajectory for the current iteration. The condition checks whether the current expansion added any node to the tree that wasn't already present — that is, whether the model generated a proof segment containing at least one tactic that led to a previously unseen proof state.

**What this equation computes**: a binary reward that is 1 if the current expansion discovered something new (added at least one node with a previously unseen tactic state to the tree), and 0 if the expansion only revisited already-known states (all tactics in the generated prefix led to states that already exist in the tree). This is a pure exploration bonus — it has nothing to do with whether the proof is progressing toward completion or whether the discovered state is "good" in any absolute sense. It simply rewards expanding the coverage of the proof state space.

**Operationally**: after each expansion step, the system checks each newly generated tactic against the existing tree nodes. If any tactic produces a proof state (the combination of all hypotheses and the current goal) that is not identical to any existing node in the tree, a new node is created and $R_{\text{intrinsic}} = 1$ for that expansion. If all generated tactics lead to states already in the tree (e.g., the model generated the exact same sequence as a previous attempt), no new nodes are added and $R_{\text{intrinsic}} = 0$.

**Why intrinsic rewards**: formal proof search exhibits a "hard-exploration" pattern (Krishnamurthy et al., 2016) where the reward signal is not just sparse (only a few terminal states yield reward) but also exponentially rare (the correct proof is a single path through an enormous space of possible tactic sequences). In such settings, exploration driven purely by extrinsic rewards is provably inefficient — the agent has no way to guide its search toward promising regions and instead wanders randomly. Intrinsic rewards address this by providing dense feedback: almost every early expansion discovers new states (high intrinsic reward), creating a gradient of reinforcement that guides the agent toward regions of the state space it hasn't yet explored. Over time, as the search covers more of the state space, intrinsic rewards become sparser (fewer novel states remain to be discovered), and the agent naturally transitions toward exploiting the accumulated knowledge.

The paper explicitly connects this to the ZeroRMax framework (Jin et al., 2020): since no extrinsic rewards are provided until a proof is completed (and even then, the reward is sparse), the algorithm is effectively driven by intrinsic rewards alone, setting $R(\tau) = R_{\text{intrinsic}}(\tau)$ for all backpropagation steps. The exploitation of extrinsic rewards only manifests indirectly — nodes that have been part of successful proof completions will have high $Q(s, a)$ values (from the few iterations where $R_{\text{extrinsic}} = 1$), and the selection policy will favor them when the exploration bonus decays sufficiently.

**An important subtlety**: the paper notes that intrinsic rewards are awarded per *node* in the expansion, not per *tactic*. The expansion may add multiple nodes (a path of several verified tactics), and the reward is 1 if *any* of them are novel, 0 only if *all* of them already exist. This is generous — it rewards expansions that find even one new state among a sequence of known ones — which further encourages exploration along partly-familiar paths.

##### Discounted UCB (DUCB) for Non-Stationary Intrinsic Rewards

The intrinsic reward defined above is **non-stationary**: its expected value decreases as the search tree grows and the space of novel proof states shrinks. Early in the search, almost every expansion discovers new states (high intrinsic reward). Later, most expansions revisit known states (low intrinsic reward). Standard UCB1 (Auer et al., 2002) assumes stationary reward distributions and computes value estimates using the arithmetic mean of all historical rewards:

$$W(s, a) = \sum_{\tau \in \Gamma(s, a)} R(\tau)$$

$$N(s, a) = |\Gamma(s, a)|$$

$$Q_{\text{UCB1}}(s, a) = \frac{W(s, a)}{N(s, a)} + \sqrt{\frac{2 \ln \sum_{a'} N(s, a')}{N(s, a)}}$$

where $W(s, a)$ is the cumulative reward for taking action $a$ from state $s$, $N(s, a)$ is the number of times this state-action pair has been selected, and $\Gamma(s, a) = \{\tau \mid (s, a) \in \tau\}$ is the set of all selection trajectories containing the pair $(s, a)$.

**The problem with UCB1 under non-stationary rewards**: the arithmetic mean $\frac{W(s, a)}{N(s, a)}$ gives equal weight to all historical rewards, regardless of recency. For a node that was heavily explored early in the search (when intrinsic rewards were high), the average will remain high even after the node stops producing novel states, because the early high rewards dominate the average. This causes the selection policy to over-exploit nodes that were valuable early on but are now exhausted — a form of "exploration inertia" where the algorithm keeps revisiting states that used to be productive but no longer are.

The paper's solution is **Discounted UCB (DUCB; Garivier and Moulines, 2011)**, which replaces the simple sum and count with exponentially weighted versions using a discount factor $\gamma \in (0, 1)$:

$$W_\gamma(s, a) = \sum_{t=1}^{N(s, a)} \gamma^{N(s, a) - t} R(\tau_t)$$

$$N_\gamma(s, a) = \sum_{t=0}^{N(s, a) - 1} \gamma^t$$

$$Q_{\text{DUCB}}(s, a) = \frac{W_\gamma(s, a)}{N_\gamma(s, a)} + \sqrt{\frac{2 \ln \sum_{a'} N_\gamma(s, a')}{N_\gamma(s, a)}}$$

where $\tau_1, \tau_2, \dots, \tau_{N(s,a)}$ are the trajectories containing $(s, a)$, ordered from oldest to newest, $\gamma$ is the discount factor (set to 0.99 in practice), $W_\gamma(s, a)$ is the discounted cumulative reward, $N_\gamma(s, a)$ is the discounted count of visits (which approaches $\frac{1}{1-\gamma}$ as the number of visits grows large), and $Q_{\text{DUCB}}(s, a)$ is the discounted estimate of the action value.

**What $W_\gamma(s, a)$ computes**: the sum of rewards for state-action pair $(s, a)$, where the reward from the $t$-th selection (counting from oldest to newest) is weighted by $\gamma^{N(s,a) - t}$. For the most recent selection ($t = N$), the weight is $\gamma^0 = 1$ (full weight). For the oldest selection ($t = 1$), the weight is $\gamma^{N-1}$, which is $0.99^{N-1}$ — approximately 0.37 if $N=100$, approximately 0.005 if $N=500$. This means old rewards are exponentially downweighted, and the value estimate primarily reflects recent outcomes.

**What $N_\gamma(s, a)$ computes**: the discounted count of visits, which normalizes $W_\gamma(s, a)$. This is the sum of the geometric series $1 + \gamma + \gamma^2 + \dots + \gamma^{N-1}$, which converges to $\frac{1}{1-\gamma} = 100$ as $N \to \infty$. This means the effective sample size is capped at 100, regardless of how many times the node is visited — visits beyond $\sim$100 contribute essentially zero to the estimate.

**Operationally**: every time the backpropagation step updates the statistics for a state-action pair, the system recomputes $W_\gamma$ and $N_\gamma$ using the discount factor, replacing the old unweighted estimates. The new estimates give higher weight to the most recent trajectory's reward. If the most recent expansion from this node produced no new states ($R_{\text{intrinsic}} = 0$), the value estimate immediately drops (because the newest, highest-weighted reward is 0). If a previously unproductive node suddenly discovers a novel state, its value estimate rapidly rises.

**Why $\gamma = 0.99$**: the discount factor determines the effective time horizon of the value estimate. At $\gamma = 0.99$, a reward observed 50 iterations ago has weight $0.99^{50} \approx 0.605$ (still significant), while a reward observed 200 iterations ago has weight $0.99^{200} \approx 0.134$ (mostly forgotten). This means the algorithm has a "memory" of roughly 100–200 iterations — it adapts to changes in the reward distribution within a few hundred selections, rather than being dominated by rewards from thousands of iterations ago (as UCB1 would be). The specific value of 0.99 is not ablated in the paper, but represents a standard choice from the DUCB literature that balances adaptability (wanting to forget old rewards quickly) against stability (wanting enough samples for reliable estimates).

**Why this form is critical**: Figure 5 empirically demonstrates that RMaxTS with standard UCB1 performs almost as poorly as UCT without intrinsic rewards, with accuracy roughly 2–3 percentage points lower than RMaxTS with DUCB at equivalent sample budgets. The failure mode is subtle: UCB1 *does* provide an exploration bonus (the UCB term), but without the discounting in the exploitation term, the value estimates are corrupted by outdated high rewards from early exploration, causing the policy to over-exploit exhausted nodes and under-explore genuinely promising new ones. DUCB fixes this by ensuring the value estimate tracks the *current* novelty-discovery rate of each node, not its historical average. This is the key algorithmic innovation that makes intrinsic rewards effective in this setting.

##### Backpropagation

After each expansion, the reward (purely intrinsic, since $R(\tau) = R_{\text{intrinsic}}(\tau)$ in the standard configuration) is propagated back along the selection trajectory $\tau = \{(\text{root}, s^{(1)}), (s^{(1)}, s^{(2)}), \dots, (s^{(|\tau|-1)}, s_t, \oslash)\}$, updating the discounted cumulative reward $W_\gamma$ and discounted visit count $N_\gamma$ for each state-action pair in the trajectory. This ensures that the selection policy for future iterations reflects the outcomes of recent expansions — if a particular path through the tree consistently discovers novel states, its entire prefix gets credit (via backpropagated intrinsic rewards), making it more likely to be selected again. Over time, this creates a bias toward exploring regions of the proof state space that the model can access (i.e., where it can generate verifiably correct tactics) but that haven't been exhaustively mapped.

**Handling the rare extrinsic reward**: when a complete, correct proof is discovered ($R_{\text{extrinsic}} = 1$), this reward is also backpropagated. The paper doesn't specify whether extrinsic and intrinsic rewards are backpropagated in the same step or whether the extrinsic reward overrides the intrinsic reward for that trajectory. In the ZeroRMax framework that the paper cites, the intrinsic reward is the primary driver, and extrinsic rewards are relatively rare events that provide occasional large bonuses along successful paths. The DUCB mechanism handles the non-stationarity of extrinsic rewards as well — as the search progresses and the policy improves, the probability of discovering correct proofs increases, and DUCB's discounting ensures that recent successes are weighted more heavily than ancient ones.

#### Parallelization of Monte Carlo Tree Search

The tree search is computationally intensive: each expansion requires one invocation of a 7B-parameter language model (on GPU) plus one invocation of the Lean 4 compiler (on CPU) to verify the generated proof. To make this feasible at scale, the paper implements three parallelization techniques from Chaslot et al. (2008), deployed on a cluster with thousands of CPU cores and multiple GPUs.

##### Root Parallelization

The system deploys **256 MCTS runners** per compute node, each running an independent search from the same root (theorem statement). Each runner maintains its own search tree and its own statistics, with no sharing between runners. Each runner has access to **one language model per GPU** and uses a **batch size of 512** for proof generation — meaning each GPU processes up to 512 proof generation requests simultaneously (batching across different tree search iterations within the runner). The Lean prover verification runs on a separate CPU cluster where **each proof verification task is handled by an individual process, created and terminated in a sandbox** — this sandboxing is critical because user-generated Lean code could potentially contain infinite loops, excessive memory consumption, or other pathological behaviors that must be isolated.

Both proof generation (by the language model) and verification (by Lean) are handled **asynchronously**. This means a runner doesn't block waiting for verification to complete — it submits a verification request, continues selecting and expanding other nodes while waiting, and processes the verification result when it arrives. This decouples the GPU-bound generation from the CPU-bound verification, allowing both to operate at full utilization without stalling.

##### Tree Parallelization

Within each runner, the tree operations are parallelized using **32 thread workers**. Each worker iteratively performs the full tree search loop:

1. **Selection**: traverse the tree from the root to select a node for expansion.
2. **Expansion**: invoke the language model (as part of a batch with other workers' requests) to generate a proof completion from the selected node.
3. **Verification**: submit the generated proof to Lean for verification (asynchronously).
4. **Backpropagation**: upon receiving the verification result, parse the proof (truncate at the first error, extract verified tactics, create new nodes), compute the reward, and propagate it back along the selection trajectory.

The 32 workers operate concurrently on the same search tree (with appropriate locking or message-passing to maintain consistency), effectively parallelizing the search iterations within a single runner. This is feasible because the MCTS tree is a shared data structure that can be read by many workers simultaneously (for selection) and updated atomically (for expansion and backpropagation).

##### Virtual Loss

When multiple thread workers share a search tree, there is a risk of **selection collisions**: multiple workers might independently select the same node for expansion, because their selection policies see the same statistics (before any of them have updated the statistics with their expansion results). This leads to redundant work — multiple workers expanding the same node in the same way — which reduces the effective diversity of the search.

The **virtual loss** technique (Chaslot et al., 2008) mitigates this. When a worker selects a node for expansion, it immediately backpropagates a **temporary reward of $R(\tau) = 0$** along the selection trajectory *before* the actual expansion result is known. This temporarily decreases the estimated value of that path (by adding a zero-reward observation), making it less likely that other workers will select the same path. When the actual expansion completes and the true reward (intrinsic and potentially extrinsic) is determined, the temporary zero reward is **overwritten** with the true value.

**Why this works**: virtual loss is a form of optimistic locking. By pessimistically assuming the pending expansion will yield zero reward, the algorithm makes that path *temporarily* less attractive to other workers, encouraging them to explore alternative paths. When the true reward arrives (which could be 1 if novel states were discovered), the path's value is corrected upward, but by that time the other workers have already committed to different expansions, achieving diversity of exploration. The paper notes that the temporary reward of 0 is chosen because it is conservative — it assumes the worst case (no new discovery) so that if the expansion actually discovers something valuable, the correction is positive (the path was better than the temporary estimate suggested).

#### Synthesis: Why the Components Form a Coherent System

The four components — pre-training, SFT with tactic-state augmentation, RL from proof assistant feedback, and RMaxTS — are not independent modules that happen to be used together. They form a mutually reinforcing pipeline where each stage addresses a limitation of the previous stage and provides capabilities that the next stage exploits:

- **Pre-training** gives the model syntactic competence in formal languages, without which SFT would waste capacity on basic syntax.
- **SFT with tactic-state augmentation** teaches the model to use compiler feedback (tactic state comments) as a conditioning signal, which is essential for the truncate-and-resume mechanism in RMaxTS. Without this, the model wouldn't know how to interpret the tactic state information provided during tree search.
- **RL from proof assistant feedback** improves the model's intrinsic proof-generation quality — it makes each expansion in the tree search more likely to generate correct tactics, which increases the density of verifiably correct nodes in the tree and makes the search more efficient. Conversely, the tree search's exploration discovers new proof states that could be fed back into SFT data generation in future expert iterations.
- **RMaxTS** compensates for RL's inability to perform systematic planning — the RL-trained model is better at generating individual proof segments, but still lacks the ability to backtrack from dead ends or systematically explore alternatives. The tree search provides the planning capability that the model (as a next-token predictor) lacks, while the model provides the proposal distribution that makes the search tractable.

The paper explicitly frames this as an **AlphaZero-like pipeline** (Section 5): expert iteration generates training data, RL improves the policy using environment feedback, and MCTS provides inference-time planning. The key difference from AlphaZero is that the environment (Lean) provides no intermediate rewards and no learned value function — the search must operate with binary, sparse, delayed rewards, which is why the intrinsic reward mechanism (RMaxTS) is necessary rather than a nice-to-have.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Unifying Whole-Proof Generation and Proof-Step Search Through a Truncate-and-Resume Abstraction

The paper's most conceptually distinctive contribution is not the tree search itself, but the **abstraction that makes tree search compatible with whole-proof generation**. Prior to this work, the field operated under an implicit dichotomy: you either used proof-step generation (one tactic at a time, tight feedback loop, amenable to tree search but computationally expensive per tactic) or whole-proof generation (generate the entire proof in one shot, computationally efficient but no intermediate state feedback, no systematic search). These were treated as fundamentally different paradigms requiring different model architectures, training objectives, and inference procedures.

The truncate-and-resume mechanism collapses this dichotomy. The key insight is that **a failed whole-proof generation is not zero information** — it contains a verifiably correct prefix of tactics that can be extracted, stored as tree nodes, and used as a launching point for subsequent generations. The model never needs to be retrained or architecturally modified to support step-level search; it generates entire proof segments in each expansion (preserving whole-proof efficiency), and the system retroactively decomposes these segments into tactic-level nodes by parsing the Lean compiler's error messages. This is fundamentally different from Hypertree Proof Search (Lample et al., 2022) or GPT-f (Polu and Sutskever, 2020), which require the model to generate exactly one tactic per step, incurring a serial communication cost proportional to proof length. It is also different from pure whole-proof methods like DeepSeek-Prover-V1 (Xin et al., 2024) or LEGO-Prover (Wang et al., 2023), which discard failed attempts entirely.

What makes this more than an engineering trick is that it **redefines the relationship between the model and the verifier**. In proof-step generation, the verifier is an active participant in the generation loop — the model queries it after every tactic, and the verifier's response determines what the model generates next. In whole-proof generation, the verifier is a passive judge — it evaluates the final output and returns a binary verdict. The truncate-and-resume mechanism creates a third relationship: the verifier becomes a **post-hoc annotator** that retroactively labels which parts of a generation were correct, and this annotation is fed back to the model as conditioning for the next generation. The model never "queries" the verifier mid-generation; it always generates complete proof segments. The verifier "grades" these segments after the fact, and the system uses the graded results to construct the prompt for the next whole-proof segment. This decouples generation from verification temporally (they happen in sequence, not interleaved) while still providing accurate intermediate-state information — a genuinely novel interaction pattern that preserves the throughput of whole-proof generation while recovering the precision of step-level feedback.

The evidence that this unification is non-trivial comes from the training requirement: the model must be explicitly taught to utilize tactic-state comments during SFT (Section 2.2, the prompt augmentation step). Simply appending tactic states to a model that was never trained to expect them would fail — the comments would be out-of-distribution tokens. The fact that the whole SFT pipeline is designed around supporting this unified inference mode (with auxiliary prediction of tactic states alongside main proof generation) indicates that this is not a post-hoc hack but a **deliberate design choice that propagates backward through the entire training pipeline**.

The significance beyond raw performance: this abstraction enables a single trained model to operate as both a whole-proof generator (for efficient batch inference) and a proof-step generator (for systematic tree search), using the same weights, the same generation paradigm, and the same prompts. This collapses the engineering complexity of maintaining separate models and pipelines for different inference strategies, and opens the door to hybrid approaches where the tree search dynamically decides how many tactics to generate per expansion based on confidence or budget constraints. The 63.5% pass rate on miniF2F-test (Table 1) is a consequence, but the architectural unification is the deeper contribution.

---

### Innovation 2: Intrinsic Rewards as a Solution to the Sparse-Reward Proof Search Problem, with Non-Stationarity Addressed by Discounted UCB

The paper's second major conceptual contribution is **diagnosing the specific failure mode of standard MCTS in formal proof search and designing a targeted solution**. The field knows that tree search can improve theorem proving — Hypertree Proof Search (Lample et al., 2022) demonstrated this — but the dominant assumption has been that search algorithms developed for games (where every terminal state provides a win/loss/draw signal) transfer directly to proof search (where rewards are binary and extremely sparse). The paper shows that this assumption is wrong.

The diagnostic move is precise: the paper identifies that standard MCTS with UCB exploration (UCT; Kocsis and Szepesvári, 2006), when applied to proof search without intrinsic rewards, **degenerates into performance comparable to non-search methods** (Figure 5, "UCT (without R_intrinsic)" vs. "w/o Tree Search"). This is not a modest underperformance — UCT provides essentially zero benefit over single-pass generation, meaning the entire tree search apparatus is wasted computation. The reason, which the paper makes explicit (Section 3.3), is that with only binary extrinsic rewards and no partial-credit signal, the UCB exploration bonus is driven entirely by visitation counts, which is equivalent to uniform random exploration among previously visited nodes. The search adds no intelligence beyond the model's generation quality.

The solution — intrinsic rewards for discovering novel tactic states — draws on the hard-exploration literature (Brafman and Tennenholtz, 2002; Jin et al., 2020) but applies it in a domain where the state space is defined by a generative language model's output distribution, not by a fixed set of state variables. This is not a straightforward application. In standard RMax, the agent maintains a table of known state-action pairs and awards itself a maximal reward upon reaching an unknown pair. In proof search, the "states" are proof states (collections of hypotheses and goals) that are not predefined — they emerge from the model's generations and are discovered dynamically. The innovation is recognizing that **proof states serve as a meaningful abstraction for exploration bonuses**, because different proof states represent genuinely different mathematical situations (different goals to prove, different hypotheses available), and discovering a new proof state means the search has expanded the frontier of accessible mathematics for this theorem. This is a domain-specific insight: in theorem proving, covering the state space is not just about efficient exploration, it is about mapping the space of possible proof strategies, which directly contributes to finding a complete proof.

The second-order insight — that **intrinsic rewards are non-stationary and standard UCB1 fails under this non-stationarity** — is what elevates this from a straightforward application of RMax to a genuine algorithmic contribution. The paper demonstrates that RMaxTS with standard UCB1 (Figure 5, "RMaxTS (DUCB → UCB1)") performs almost as poorly as UCT without intrinsic rewards, confirming that the choice of UCB variant is not a minor implementation detail but is essential to making intrinsic rewards work. The failure mode is subtle and would not be obvious without ablation: UCB1 uses arithmetic means of historical rewards, which become dominated by early high rewards (when novel states were abundant) and fail to reflect that a node has been exhausted. The discounted UCB (DUCB; Garivier and Moulines, 2011) with γ = 0.99 solves this by exponentially downweighting old observations, ensuring the value estimate tracks the *current* rate of novelty discovery. This insight — that non-stationarity is the bottleneck, not the choice of intrinsic reward function per se — is generalizable beyond theorem proving to any setting where intrinsic rewards are used to drive exploration in MCTS with LLMs.

The significance beyond raw performance: this work establishes that **exploration strategy design is a first-class problem in neural theorem proving**, not an afterthought. Prior tree search methods for theorem proving (HTPS, GPT-f's best-first search) relied on extrinsic reward signals or learned value functions. This paper shows that even without a value function or external reward, a properly designed exploration strategy can make tree search effective — but only if the non-stationarity of exploration bonuses is explicitly handled. This changes the research agenda: rather than pursuing ever-better value function approximations to densify the reward signal, a complementary path is to design exploration mechanisms that extract maximum information from the sparse signal that already exists. The paper explicitly notes (Section 5) that training a critic model for evaluating partial proofs remains future work, implying that intrinsic rewards and learned critics are complementary rather than competing approaches.

---

### Innovation 3: Reinforcement Learning from Proof Assistant Feedback Provides Genuine Capability Improvement, Not Merely Top-K Selection Enhancement

This is a **diagnostic finding with implications that extend beyond theorem proving**. The paper's third major contribution is empirical evidence that online reinforcement learning from binary compiler feedback — what they term RLPAF — improves the model's fundamental proof-generation capabilities, not just its ability to select the best from a set of candidates.

This matters because there is a live debate in the LLM reasoning community about what RL actually does. In natural language mathematics, DeepSeekMath (Shao et al., 2024) found that RL primarily improved top-K selection — the model got better at ranking its own outputs, but the underlying generation quality at K=1 didn't change much. This paper explicitly contrasts with that finding (Section 2.4): "we observe a genuine enhancement of fundamental capabilities in formal theorem proving. This improvement is evident not only with a small sample budget but also remains stable as the sample budget increases."

The evidence is in Figure 3: the RL-trained model (DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL) outperforms the SFT model across **all values of K**, including K=1 (single-attempt pass rate). If RL merely improved selection, the gap would be small at low K (where there's nothing to select among) and large at high K (where ranking matters). Instead, the gap is consistent — roughly 1–2 percentage points across all K on miniF2F, and 2–3 points on ProofNet — indicating that each individual proof attempt from the RL model is genuinely more likely to be correct than an attempt from the SFT model.

What makes this finding intellectually distinctive is **why** RL behaves differently in formal theorem proving versus natural language mathematics. The paper doesn't fully theorize this, but the mechanism is implicit in the training setup: in natural language, the reward model is learned and can be gamed (high reward ≠ correct reasoning). In formal theorem proving, the reward is the Lean compiler — an oracle that never makes mistakes. This means the RL signal is in perfect alignment with the true objective: every reward of 1 corresponds to a genuinely correct proof, and every reward of 0 corresponds to a genuinely incorrect one. There is no reward hacking, no distribution shift in the reward model, no ambiguity about what "correct" means. The RL algorithm can directly optimize for what we actually care about (proof correctness) rather than a proxy (reward model score), and this direct optimization improves the policy in ways that SFT's imitation learning cannot.

A subtler implication: this finding suggests that **the effectiveness of RL for reasoning is gated by the quality and alignment of the reward signal**, not by any inherent limitation of RL algorithms. When rewards are noisy or gameable (as in learned reward models for natural language), RL primarily improves selection/ranking. When rewards are perfect oracles (as in formal verification, unit tests, or other binary correctness signals), RL improves generation quality itself. This is a testable hypothesis that generalizes beyond theorem proving to code generation (where unit tests provide oracle rewards) and other domains with formal specifications.

The significance beyond raw performance: this finding changes how we should think about training pipelines for verifiable reasoning tasks. If RL with oracle rewards provides genuine capability gains (not just selection gains), then the optimal allocation of training compute shifts toward more RL and less SFT, especially for tasks where verification is cheap relative to data collection. It also suggests that the standard practice of doing extensive SFT before RL (to establish a non-zero reward probability, as this paper does) is a scaffolding step — the real capability gains come from RL, and the goal should be to transition to RL as early as possible.

---

### Innovation 4: Demonstrating That CoT and Non-CoT Generation Modes Are Complementary, Not Redundant, in Formal Proof Generation

This is a **smaller but practically significant** finding that challenges a common intuition. The standard assumption in the chain-of-thought literature (Wei et al., 2022) is that CoT is strictly better — it improves reasoning by making intermediate steps explicit, and any task that benefits from reasoning should use CoT. The paper's results complicate this picture for formal theorem proving.

Table 3 shows that on miniF2F-test, the CoT mode consistently outperforms the non-CoT mode (60.2% vs. 57.4% at 16 × 6400 samples for the RL model), which is expected. But the **mixture strategy** — allocating half the sample budget to CoT mode and half to non-CoT mode — outperforms either mode alone (61.1% for single-pass, 63.5% for RMaxTS). This means CoT and non-CoT are **solving different subsets of problems**, not overlapping.

The paper provides an explanation for this complementarity (Section 4.2) that is grounded in the nature of Lean theorem proving. In CoT mode, the model generates detailed natural language reasoning before each tactic, which encourages systematic, planned proof construction. This is effective for theorems that require genuine mathematical insight — where you need to understand *why* a proof works before you can encode it in tactics. In non-CoT mode, the model relies on high-level Lean tactics (like `nlinarith`, `aesop`, `omega`) to brute-force solutions, which is effective for computation-heavy problems where the mathematical reasoning is straightforward (e.g., verifying an algebraic identity) but the manual encoding would be tedious. The CoT model might over-engineer a simple computation (inserting unnecessary reasoning where a single `norm_num` call would suffice), while the non-CoT model might fail on a problem requiring strategic induction or case analysis because it lacks the reasoning scaffold to discover the proof structure.

This is not a finding about CoT being "sometimes harmful" in the way prior work has documented for certain natural language tasks. It's about **mode diversity as a resource**: two different prompting strategies induce different proof-generation behaviors from the same model, and these behaviors have different strengths. By combining them (half the budget to each), you get the union of their capabilities, not the intersection. This is a form of **ensemble diversity** achieved entirely through prompt engineering, without training separate models.

The significance beyond raw performance: this finding suggests that for formal reasoning tasks, the optimal strategy may be to **maintain a portfolio of generation modes** (with different levels of explicitness in reasoning, different levels of reliance on automation, different proof styles) and allocate inference budget across them, rather than committing to a single "best" mode. The paper's mixture strategy is simple (50/50 split), but one could imagine more sophisticated allocation policies that estimate difficulty and route problems to the appropriate mode — a direct parallel to the compute-optimal allocation frameworks that have emerged in other LLM scaling work. The fact that the mixture strategy pushes the state-of-the-art from 60.2% to 63.5% (a 3.3 percentage-point gain at 16 × 6400 samples, Table 3) indicates that mode diversity is a non-trivial source of capability, not a minor optimization.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on two benchmarks: **miniF2F** (Zheng et al., 2022), consisting of 244 validation and 244 test problems at the high-school competition level (AMC, AIME, IMO), originally in Lean 3 and manually converted to Lean 4.9.0; and **ProofNet** (Azerbayev et al., 2023), consisting of 185 validation and 186 test problems at the undergraduate level (real and complex analysis, linear algebra, abstract algebra, topology), also converted from Lean 3 to Lean 4.9.0.

- **Base model(s).** The base model is **DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-Base**, a 7B-parameter language model initialized from DeepSeekMath-Base (Shao et al., 2024) and further pre-trained on mathematics, code, and formal proof languages (Lean, Isabelle, Metamath). This is used as the foundation for all training stages. For the base model evaluation, 3-shot prompting is used with three proof demonstrations from the validation set: human-written proofs from Yang (2023) for miniF2F, and correct proofs generated by DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL for ProofNet. For the SFT and RL models, zero-shot prompting is used with CoT and non-CoT guiding prompts (examples in Appendix A).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **pass@K accuracy** — the fraction of problems for which at least one correct proof is generated within K attempts. For single-pass generation, K is the total number of proofs generated, with large K factorized for comparison to tree search methods. For tree search methods, K = N × T where N is the number of tree search attempts and T is the number of model generations invoked in tree expansions. For best-first-search methods, K = N × S × T where S is the number of tactics generated per expansion. Standard deviations are reported for results where multiple runs are feasible, using the notation μ ± σ.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against a broad set of prior methods: **General-purpose models** — COPRA with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (Thakur et al., 2023), Llemma-7B and Llemma-34B (Azerbayev et al., 2024). **Proof-step generation methods** — GPT-f (Polu and Sutskever, 2020; Polu et al., 2022), ReProver (Yang et al., 2023), LLMStep (Welleck and Saha, 2023), Hypertree Proof Search (Lample et al., 2022), Lean-STaR (Lin et al., 2024), InternLM2-Math-7B and InternLM2-Math-Plus-7B (Ying et al., 2024), InternLM2-StepProver (Wu et al., 2024). **Whole-proof generation methods** — TheoremLlama (Wang et al., 2024), DeepSeek-Prover-V1 (Xin et al., 2024). All baseline numbers are taken from the respective papers; no re-evaluation is performed.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For single-pass generation, the sample budget K is the total number of proof attempts, with each attempt being one independent whole-proof generation from the model. For tree search methods (RMaxTS and HTPS), K = N × T where N is the number of independent tree search runs and T is the number of model invocations per run (each expansion step counts as one invocation). For best-first-search methods (GPT-f, Llemma-based, InternLM2-StepProver), K = N × S × T where S is the number of tactic suggestions per expansion step. This factorization aligns computation budgets across different generation schemes by counting the total number of language model forward passes. The paper uses a maximum token limit of 2,048 per generation, temperature of 1, top-p of 0.95, and deploys each model on a single A100-40G GPU using vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) for sample generation. Verification uses the Lean 4 theorem prover with a 300-second time limit, importing Mathlib4 and Aesop.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For single-pass generation with smaller sample budgets (K ≤ 128), results are reported as mean ± standard deviation across multiple sampling runs (the number of runs is not explicitly stated, but the shaded regions in Figure 3 indicate standard deviation ranges). For larger sample budgets (K ≥ 3200), standard deviations are reported where feasible but are omitted for the very largest budgets (e.g., 16 × 6400) where the computational cost of multiple runs is prohibitive. For tree search results with RMaxTS, standard deviations are based on multiple independent tree search runs (the number of runs is implicitly the N in N × T). The paper does not use cross-validation for model selection — the training data for SFT and RL is constructed from sources that include the validation sets of miniF2F and ProofNet (Section 2.2 explicitly states that the validation sets are used in data curation and expert iteration), which means the test set is the only held-out evaluation. For the ProofNet benchmark, footnote ‡ in Table 2 acknowledges this explicitly: "Note that the validation set of ProofNet is used to perform expert iteration in supervised fine-tuning."

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Comparison Across Training Stages on Single-Pass Generation

Figure 3 and the accompanying tables present the progression of model capability through the three training stages — Base, SFT, and RL — evaluated on miniF2F-test and ProofNet-test.

**On miniF2F-test (Figure 3, left panel; Table 3):**

- **Base model (3-shot):** Achieves pass@128 of 29.7% ± 0.5%, demonstrating that specialized pre-training on formal languages alone provides non-trivial theorem-proving ability (nearly one-third of high-school competition problems solvable with 128 attempts).

- **SFT model (CoT mode):** Dramatically improves to pass@128 of 50.4% ± 0.4%, a gain of ~20.7 percentage points over the base model. The improvement is consistent across all values of K: at pass@32, the SFT model achieves 48.2% ± 0.6%; at pass@64, 49.6% ± 0.7%. The SFT model in non-CoT mode achieves similar performance (pass@128 of 49.8% ± 0.3%), indicating that the SFT training itself provides the bulk of the capability gain, with CoT providing only a small additional benefit at this stage.

- **RL model (CoT mode):** Further improves to pass@128 of 51.6% ± 0.5%, a gain of 1.2 percentage points over SFT. The improvement, while modest in absolute terms, is consistent across K: at pass@32, RL achieves 50.0% ± 0.5% vs. SFT's 48.2% ± 0.6% (a ~1.8 point gain). The RL model in non-CoT mode achieves pass@128 of 50.5% ± 0.6%, a smaller gain over SFT non-CoT (49.8% ± 0.3%). Critically, the improvement is present at all values of K, not just at high K — evidence that RL improves per-attempt proof quality, not just candidate ranking.

- **Large-budget scaling (Table 3):** At 3200 generations (single-pass), the RL model achieves 54.9% ± 0.7%, the SFT model achieves 53.3% ± 0.5%. At 4 × 6400 (25,600 generations), RL achieves 58.4% ± 0.6%, SFT achieves 55.8% ± 0.7%. At 16 × 6400 (102,400 generations), RL achieves 60.2% (no standard deviation reported), SFT achieves 57.4%. The gap between RL and SFT widens from ~1.6 points at 3200 generations to ~2.8 points at the maximum budget, suggesting RL's benefits compound with more sampling.

**On ProofNet-test (Figure 3, right panel; Table 2):**

- **Base model (3-shot):** Achieves pass@128 of 9.7% ± 0.7%, substantially lower than miniF2F, reflecting the greater difficulty of undergraduate-level theorems requiring abstract algebra, analysis, and topology.

- **SFT model (CoT mode):** Improves to pass@128 of 15.9% ± 0.6%, roughly doubling the base model's performance but still far below miniF2F levels. The SFT model in non-CoT mode matches this (15.9% ± 0.6%), indicating that on harder problems, the CoT/non-CoT distinction is less impactful at the SFT stage.

- **RL model (CoT mode):** Improves to pass@128 of 18.2% ± 0.5%, a gain of 2.3 percentage points over SFT — notably larger than the 1.2-point gain on miniF2F. This suggests RL is particularly beneficial on harder problems where the SFT model has more room for improvement. The RL model in non-CoT mode achieves 17.5% ± 0.5%.

- **Large-budget scaling (Table 2):** At 3200 generations, the RL model achieves 22.0% ± 0.5% on ProofNet-test (21.4% ± 0.3% on the validation set, 21.7% ± 0.4% on the combined set). At 4 × 6400, RL achieves 23.7% on the test set. Notably, the SFT model at 4 × 6400 also achieves 23.7% on the test set — the same as RL — while RL achieves 21.6% on the validation set vs. SFT's 22.2%. This suggests that on ProofNet, the advantage of RL is most pronounced at lower sample budgets and may diminish at very large budgets, unlike miniF2F where RL maintains a consistent advantage. However, the standard deviations are substantial (0.5–0.9 percentage points), and the sample sizes per bin are relatively small, making it difficult to draw firm conclusions about the RL vs. SFT comparison at these extreme budgets.

**The key claim about RL providing "genuine capability improvement":** The paper asserts in Section 2.4 that "we observe a genuine enhancement of fundamental capabilities in formal theorem proving. This improvement is evident not only with a small sample budget but also remains stable as the sample budget increases." The evidence in Figure 3 supports this claim: the RL advantage is present at pass@1 (visible in the leftmost points of the curves) and remains roughly constant or even widens as K increases. On miniF2F, the gap between RL and SFT is ~1–2 percentage points across all K from 1 to 128. On ProofNet, the gap is ~2–3 percentage points. If RL merely improved the model's ability to select the best candidate from a set (a "top-K enhancer"), the gap would be zero or negligible at K=1 and grow with K — this is not what the data show. The improvement is a uniform upward shift of the entire pass@K curve, consistent with each individual generation being of higher quality.

However, a qualification is needed: the absolute magnitude of the RL improvement is modest — 1–3 percentage points across both benchmarks at K=128. While this is statistically distinguishable given the reported standard deviations, it represents a relatively small fraction of the total capability gain from base to RL (most of which comes from SFT). The paper's framing emphasizes RL as a "genuine capability improver," but the magnitude suggests the improvement is incremental rather than transformative compared to the SFT stage.

#### Comparison of CoT vs. Non-CoT Generation Modes

The paper evaluates both CoT and non-CoT modes across SFT and RL models (Figure 3, Table 3), with the key finding that CoT consistently but modestly outperforms non-CoT, and that the two modes are complementary.

**At pass@128 (Figure 3, table inset):**
- SFT CoT: 50.4% ± 0.4% vs. SFT non-CoT: 49.8% ± 0.3% on miniF2F-test (0.6-point advantage).
- RL CoT: 51.6% ± 0.5% vs. RL non-CoT: 50.5% ± 0.6% on miniF2F-test (1.1-point advantage).
- On ProofNet-test, the RL CoT advantage is 18.2% ± 0.5% vs. 17.5% ± 0.5% (0.7-point advantage).

**At large budgets (Table 3, single-pass generation):**
- At 4 × 6400, RL CoT achieves 58.4% ± 0.5% vs. RL non-CoT 56.5% ± 0.5% (1.9-point advantage).
- At 16 × 6400, RL CoT achieves 60.2% vs. RL non-CoT 57.4% (2.8-point advantage).

The paper observes that "the advantage of CoT over the non-CoT mode is amplified as the sample budget increases" (Section 4.2). This is visible in the widening gap from 1.1 points at K=128 to 2.8 points at 16 × 6400. The interpretation offered is that "the incorporation of natural language chain-of-thought can diversify the planning pathways of theorem proving, potentially leading to a broader range of reasoning strategies and more innovative solutions" — in other words, CoT mode generates proofs from a different (and at high budgets, more productive) region of the solution space than non-CoT mode.

**The mixture strategy (Table 3, single-pass generation):**
- (2 + 2) × 6400 (half CoT, half non-CoT): 58.3% ± 0.6% for RL, vs. 58.4% ± 0.5% for pure CoT — essentially tied at this budget.
- (8 + 8) × 6400: 60.7% for RL, vs. 60.2% for pure CoT — a 0.5-point advantage for the mixture.
- (16 + 16) × 6400: 61.1% for RL, vs. 60.2% for pure CoT — a 0.9-point advantage for the mixture.

The mixture outperforms pure CoT by a small but consistent margin at larger budgets, indicating that CoT and non-CoT modes solve overlapping but non-identical sets of problems. The paper provides examples in Appendix B illustrating the different strengths: non-CoT mode succeeds on problems solvable through computational brute force with high-level tactics (e.g., `nlinarith`, `norm_num` chains), while CoT mode succeeds on problems requiring deliberate case analysis and structured reasoning that non-CoT mode fails to organize.

#### Tree Search Results: RMaxTS on miniF2F-test

Table 1 presents the tree search results on miniF2F-test, with the headline number being **63.5% for DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL + RMaxTS at 32 × 6400** (using the mixture strategy of CoT and non-CoT), which is the new state-of-the-art.

**Comparison of RL vs. SFT in tree search (Table 1, Table 3):**
- At 1 × 3200, RL + RMaxTS achieves 55.0% ± 0.7% vs. SFT + RMaxTS at 53.5% ± 0.4% (1.5-point advantage).
- At 4 × 6400, RL + RMaxTS achieves 59.6% ± 0.6% vs. SFT + RMaxTS at 56.3% ± 0.3% (3.3-point advantage).
- At 16 × 6400, RL + RMaxTS achieves 62.7% vs. SFT + RMaxTS at 59.0% (3.7-point advantage).

The RL advantage grows with the search budget, from 1.5 to 3.7 points, suggesting that RL improves exploration efficiency — the RL model generates proof segments that lead to novel tactic states more reliably, making the intrinsic-reward-driven search more effective.

**Comparison of RMaxTS vs. single-pass generation (Table 3, CoT mode, RL model):**
- At 4 × 6400, RMaxTS achieves 59.6% ± 0.6% vs. single-pass 58.4% ± 0.5% (1.2-point advantage).
- At 16 × 6400, RMaxTS achieves 62.7% vs. single-pass 60.2% (2.5-point advantage).

Tree search provides a modest but growing advantage as the budget increases, consistent with the interpretation that tree search is most valuable when the sampling budget is large enough to explore diverse proof paths — at small budgets, the overhead of building and maintaining the search tree may not be justified.

**The mixture strategy in tree search (Table 3):**
- (2 + 2) × 6400: 60.0% ± 0.8% vs. CoT-only 59.6% ± 0.6% (0.4-point advantage).
- (8 + 8) × 6400: 63.1% vs. CoT-only 62.7% (0.4-point advantage).
- (16 + 16) × 6400: 63.5% vs. CoT-only 62.7% (0.8-point advantage).

The mixture strategy provides a small additional boost in tree search as well, confirming the complementarity of the two generation modes. The headline 63.5% at (16 + 16) × 6400 represents a 1.1-point improvement over DeepSeek-Prover-V1's 50.0% in the single-pass setting.

**Comparison against prior state-of-the-art (Table 1):**
- InternLM2-StepProver (Wu et al., 2024): 54.5% at 64 × 32 × 100 = 204,800 search node expansions. This is the strongest prior baseline.
- DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL + RMaxTS: 55.0% ± 0.7% at just 1 × 3200 = 3,200 expansions — already surpassing InternLM2-StepProver's 54.5% with approximately **64× fewer** expansions. This is the most striking efficiency comparison in the paper.
- At 4 × 6400 = 25,600 expansions, RMaxTS achieves 59.6% ± 0.6%, a 5.1-point improvement over InternLM2-StepProver.
- At 16 × 6400 = 102,400 expansions, RMaxTS achieves 62.7%.
- At 32 × 6400 = 204,800 expansions (comparable to InternLM2-StepProver's budget), RMaxTS with the mixture strategy achieves 63.5%, an 8.0-point improvement.

The paper also notes that DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL in single-pass mode achieves 54.9% ± 0.7% at 3200 generations — already surpassing InternLM2-StepProver's 54.5% **without any tree search**, using pure whole-proof generation. This is a striking result: the RL-trained model's raw generation quality is competitive with the prior state-of-the-art's elaborate tree search.

**Single-pass scaling to extreme budgets (Table 1):**
- DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL at 3200: 54.9% ± 0.7%
- At 4 × 6400: 58.4% ± 0.6%
- At 16 × 6400: 60.2%

The improvement from 3200 to 102,400 generations (32× more compute) is only 5.3 percentage points, suggesting diminishing returns to parallel sampling. This is consistent with the whole-proof generation paradigm: each sample is independent, so the pass@K curve flattens as the "easy" theorems (within the model's high-probability region) are solved and only "hard" theorems (where the model rarely generates correct proofs) remain.

Table 2 presents results on ProofNet, where the headline number is **25.3% for DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL + RMaxTS at 4 × 6400** on the full ProofNet dataset.

**Comparison against prior state-of-the-art (Table 2):**
- ReProver (Yang et al., 2023): 13.8% on the full ProofNet. This was the prior state-of-the-art for ProofNet.
- InternLM2-StepProver (Wu et al., 2024): 18.1% on the full ProofNet.
- DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL + RMaxTS at 4 × 6400: 25.3% on the full dataset — a **7.2-point improvement** over InternLM2-StepProver.
- DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL single-pass at 4 × 6400: 22.6% on the full dataset — already 4.5 points above InternLM2-StepProver's best.
- DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL single-pass at 3200: 21.7% ± 0.4% on the full dataset — 3.6 points above InternLM2-StepProver.

**Comparison of RL vs. SFT on ProofNet (Table 2, tree search):**
- At 1 × 3200, SFT + RMaxTS achieves 21.9% ± 0.4% vs. RL + RMaxTS at 21.8% ± 0.4% — essentially tied.
- At 4 × 6400, SFT + RMaxTS achieves 24.8% vs. RL + RMaxTS at 25.3% — a 0.5-point advantage for RL.

The RL advantage on ProofNet is smaller than on miniF2F (0.5 vs. 3.7 points at comparable budgets). This may reflect the greater difficulty of ProofNet theorems: if the base model fundamentally struggles with undergraduate-level abstract algebra or topology, RL has less room to improve because the probability of generating any correct proof (and thus receiving positive RL feedback) is lower. The prompt selection strategy (keeping only theorems with moderate SFT success rates) mitigates this but cannot fully overcome it — if a theorem is simply beyond the model's capability, no amount of RL will create that capability.

**Single-pass scaling on ProofNet (Table 2):**
- DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL at 128: 19.1% ± 0.4% on the full dataset.
- At 3200: 21.7% ± 0.4%.
- At 4 × 6400: 22.6%.

The improvement from 128 to 25,600 generations (200× more samples) is only 3.5 percentage points, indicating even steeper diminishing returns than on miniF2F. This is consistent with ProofNet being inherently harder — more of its problems fall into the "hard" bin where the model has near-zero probability of generating a correct proof regardless of the number of attempts.

Table 3 provides the most comprehensive comparison of training and inference strategies on miniF2F-test, organized by prompt mode (non-CoT, CoT, mixture), generation method (single-pass vs. RMaxTS), and model (SFT vs. RL). The key comparisons have already been discussed above, but several patterns across this table deserve explicit attention:

- **RL provides a consistent benefit across all configurations**: In every row of Table 3 (every combination of prompt mode and generation method), the RL model outperforms the SFT model, usually by 1–4 percentage points.

- **RMaxTS provides a benefit that grows with sample budget**: At 4 × 6400, the RMaxTS advantage over single-pass is ~0.2–1.2 points (depending on prompt mode and model). At 16 × 6400, the advantage grows to ~0.6–2.5 points. The search benefit compounds with more compute because the tree accumulates more verified proof fragments and can explore more diverse paths.

- **The mixture strategy benefits both single-pass and RMaxTS**: For the RL model at 16 × 6400, mixture outperforms pure CoT by 0.9 points (single-pass) and 0.8 points (RMaxTS). The fact that this benefit persists in both paradigms confirms that mode diversity is a genuine source of capability, not an artifact of any particular inference strategy.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Intrinsic rewards and discounted UCB (Figure 5, right panel):** The paper ablates two core components of RMaxTS — the intrinsic reward mechanism and the discounted UCB — by comparing four conditions on miniF2F-test using DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL in CoT mode:
- **UCT (without R_intrinsic)**: Standard MCTS with UCB exploration but no intrinsic rewards. Achieves 58.2% ± 0.3% at 4 × 6400 and 61.1% at 16 × 6400 — performance essentially equivalent to single-pass generation (58.4% ± 0.5% at 4 × 6400, 60.2% at 16 × 6400). This demonstrates that **tree search without intrinsic rewards provides no benefit** over pure parallel sampling in the sparse-reward proof search setting.
- **RMaxTS (DUCB → UCB1)**: RMaxTS with intrinsic rewards but using standard UCB1 instead of discounted UCB. Achieves 58.6% ± 0.3% at 4 × 6400 and 60.7% at 16 × 6400 — only marginally better than UCT without intrinsic rewards, and substantially worse than RMaxTS with DUCB (59.6% ± 0.6% and 62.7%, respectively). This confirms that the **choice of UCB variant is critical**: standard UCB1's arithmetic mean of historical rewards is corrupted by outdated high intrinsic rewards from early exploration, causing the policy to over-exploit exhausted nodes.
- **RMaxTS (without tactic state)**: RMaxTS with intrinsic rewards and DUCB, but the model generates proof completions from raw incomplete code without the tactic state comment block. Achieves 58.4% ± 0.3% at 4 × 6400 and 61.1% at 16 × 6400 — again, essentially equivalent to single-pass generation. This demonstrates that the **tactic state feedback is essential** for the tree search to be effective: without the compiler-provided proof state information, the model cannot leverage the truncate-and-resume mechanism to make informed continuations.
- **RMaxTS (full)**: Achieves 59.6% ± 0.6% at 4 × 6400 and 62.7% at 16 × 6400 — significantly outperforming all ablation conditions.

The left panel of Figure 5 shows the pass@K curves within 6400 generation samples, providing finer granularity. The full RMaxTS curve is consistently above the ablation curves, with the gap widening at higher sample budgets. The three ablation conditions (UCT without R_intrinsic, RMaxTS with UCB1, RMaxTS without tactic state) all cluster near the single-pass generation baseline ("w/o Tree Search"), confirming that each component — intrinsic rewards, discounted UCB, and tactic state feedback — is individually necessary for tree search to add value.

**Complementarity of CoT and non-CoT modes (Table 3, Appendix B):** The mixture strategy ablation is discussed above in the main results. Appendix B provides qualitative evidence by presenting specific problems where non-CoT succeeds and CoT fails (`induction_pord1p1on2powklt5on2`, `imo_1960_p2`), and where CoT succeeds and non-CoT fails (`mathd_algebra_459`, `numbertheory_x5neqy2p4`, `amc12_2000_p12`). The non-CoT successes are computation-heavy problems solvable through cascades of high-level tactics; the CoT successes are problems requiring strategic case analysis or modular reasoning that non-CoT mode fails to organize.

**RL vs. SFT over increasing sample budgets (Figure 3):** While not presented as a formal ablation, the comparison of pass@K curves for SFT and RL across K from 1 to 128 serves as an implicit ablation of the RL stage. The consistent gap across all K values (discussed above) demonstrates that RL provides a uniform improvement rather than a top-K selection effect. The fact that this gap is present at K=1 (single attempt) is the key evidence: it means the RL model generates better proofs on the first try, which cannot be explained by improved ranking or selection.

**Comparison to DeepSeek-Prover-V1 (Table 1):** The paper's predecessor, DeepSeek-Prover-V1 (Xin et al., 2024), is evaluated only in the single-pass whole-proof generation setting and achieves 50.0% at 16 × 4096 = 65,536 generations on miniF2F-test. DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL achieves 54.9% ± 0.7% at 3200 generations — **higher accuracy with ~20× fewer generations**. At a comparable budget (16 × 6400 = 102,400 generations), V1.5 achieves 60.2%, a 10.2-point absolute improvement. This is not presented as a formal ablation but serves as the primary evidence that the training improvements (augmented SFT data, RL, CoT/non-CoT training) translate to substantially improved generation quality. However, the comparison is not perfectly controlled: V1 uses a different training pipeline, different data, and potentially different model architecture or size (the paper does not specify whether V1 and V1.5 are the same size, though both are described as having 7B parameters).

**Few-shot prompting for Base model vs. zero-shot for SFT/RL (Section 2.4):** The base model uses 3-shot prompting with proof demonstrations, while the SFT and RL models use zero-shot guiding prompts (CoT or non-CoT). This makes the base-vs-SFT comparison somewhat confounded — part of the large 20.7-point improvement from Base to SFT may be attributable to the SFT model's training data containing a much broader coverage of proof patterns, rather than to the SFT training procedure itself being superior to few-shot prompting of a capable model. The paper does not evaluate the SFT or RL models with few-shot prompting, or the base model with CoT/non-CoT zero-shot prompts, so the magnitude of the few-shot vs. zero-shot effect cannot be isolated. However, the base model's 3-shot performance (29.7%) is already well below the SFT model's zero-shot performance (48.2% at 32 generations), so even if few-shot prompting were partially responsible, the SFT training clearly provides substantial additional benefit.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim: DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 achieves new state-of-the-art on miniF2F (63.5%) and ProofNet (25.3%).

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Tables 1 and 2 convincingly show that DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL + RMaxTS achieves the highest reported numbers on both benchmarks, surpassing all prior methods by substantial margins (8.0 points over InternLM2-StepProver on miniF2F, 7.2 points on ProofNet). The comparisons are reported at equivalent computational budgets (total language model invocations for tree search methods), making the improvement a genuine efficiency gain rather than merely the result of using more compute.

**Weaknesses:** The comparisons rely on numbers reported in prior papers without independent re-evaluation. The conversion of miniF2F and ProofNet from Lean 3 to Lean 4.9.0 may introduce subtle differences — the paper states that the conversion used the version provided by Yang (2023), but does not verify that all problems are semantically equivalent to their Lean 3 originals or to the versions used by prior methods. If some problems became easier or harder due to differences in Mathlib4's lemma availability or tactic behavior, the cross-paper comparisons would be confounded. Additionally, the comparison to InternLM2-StepProver is on ProofNet "all" (validation + test), but InternLM2-StepProver's result may be on a different split or subset — the paper does not clarify.

#### Claim: Reinforcement learning provides genuine capability improvement, not just top-K selection enhancement.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figure 3 shows that RL improves pass@K across all K from 1 to 128 on both benchmarks, with the gap roughly constant. This is consistent with per-attempt quality improvement rather than ranking improvement.

**Weaknesses:** The absolute magnitude of improvement is modest — 1–3 percentage points at K=128 on both benchmarks. While statistically distinguishable given the standard deviations, the practical significance is limited: most of the capability gain (20+ points on miniF2F) comes from SFT. More importantly, the paper does not measure pass@1 directly — the leftmost points in Figure 3 are at K=1 in the pass@K computation, but the curves are constructed from the pass@K estimator (which involves sampling multiple times and computing the fraction of problems with at least one correct solution in K attempts, then adjusting with an unbiased estimator). The standard deviations at low K are large (visible in the shaded regions), making it difficult to distinguish a 1–2 point RL advantage from noise at K=1. A dedicated pass@1 experiment with many independent single-attempt evaluations per problem would provide stronger evidence.

**Additional concern:** The RL training uses prompts selected for "moderate success rate" — theorems where the SFT model already has non-zero probability of generating correct proofs. This means RL is trained on a **curriculum of easier problems** and may not improve (or may even impair) performance on the hardest problems where the SFT model rarely or never succeeds. The paper does not break down RL vs. SFT performance by problem difficulty, so it's impossible to tell whether the RL improvement is concentrated on already-solvable problems (consistent with the "moderate success rate" selection) or extends to harder ones.

#### Claim: RMaxTS with intrinsic rewards and discounted UCB enables effective Monte Carlo tree search in the sparse-reward proof search setting.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figure 5 provides clean ablation evidence: UCT without intrinsic rewards performs no better than single-pass generation, confirming that standard MCTS fails under sparse rewards. RMaxTS with intrinsic rewards but standard UCB1 also performs poorly, confirming that non-stationarity handling is essential. RMaxTS without tactic state feedback similarly fails, confirming that compiler information is necessary.

**Weaknesses:**
- The ablation is conducted on only one benchmark (miniF2F-test) with one model (DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL in CoT mode). The conclusions about intrinsic rewards and DUCB may not generalize to other models (e.g., weaker models where search might be more or less beneficial) or other benchmarks (e.g., ProofNet, where the search benefit is smaller).
- The discount factor γ = 0.99 is not ablated. The paper uses this value without justifying why 0.99 rather than 0.95 or 0.999. Given that the discounted UCB mechanism is identified as critical, understanding its sensitivity to γ would be valuable — does a slightly different γ cause the search to degrade to UCB1-like performance?
- The intrinsic reward formulation (binary: 1 if any new node added, 0 otherwise) is not compared against alternative intrinsic reward schemes. For example, one could reward the *number* of new nodes added (rather than a binary indicator), or use count-based exploration bonuses proportional to $1/\sqrt{N(s)}$ where $N(s)$ is the visit count of the state. The paper's choice is well-motivated by the RMax literature but not empirically justified against alternatives.
- The sample sizes for the ablation are small: the 4 × 6400 condition involves 4 independent tree searches of 6400 expansions each, which is computationally expensive but may provide only 4 data points for computing standard deviations. The reported standard deviations for the ablation conditions (0.3–0.6 percentage points) are based on these few runs, and the confidence intervals around the 1–2 point differences between conditions are probably overlapping.

#### Claim: CoT and non-CoT modes are complementary, and a mixture strategy outperforms either mode alone.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Table 3 shows that the mixture strategy (equal allocation to CoT and non-CoT) outperforms pure CoT by 0.5–0.9 points at large budgets, for both single-pass generation and RMaxTS. Appendix B provides qualitative examples of problems solved by one mode but not the other.

**Weaknesses:**
- The complementarity is demonstrated but not explained quantitatively. The paper does not report the Jaccard overlap (fraction of problems solved by both modes vs. by only one), the correlation of per-problem success rates, or any other quantitative measure of mode diversity. The 0.5–0.9 point improvement from the mixture strategy could be consistent with either substantial complementarity (the modes solve very different problem sets) or near-total overlap with a small random advantage from the mixture. Without this analysis, the claim of complementarity remains qualitative.
- The mixture strategy uses a simple 50/50 split, with no exploration of alternative allocation ratios. Could a 70/30 or 30/70 split perform better? The paper doesn't investigate.
- The complementarity is only demonstrated at very high sample budgets (4 × 6400 and above). At lower budgets (up to 128), the pure CoT mode is already at or near the mixture strategy's performance (Table 3 doesn't report mixture results at K=128), so the practical benefit of the mixture may be limited to the very-high-budget regime that is rarely used in practice.

#### General Weaknesses in Experimental Design

**Validation set contamination in ProofNet:** The paper explicitly states (Table 2, footnote ‡) that "the validation set of ProofNet is used to perform expert iteration in supervised fine-tuning." This means the ProofNet validation set is not a held-out evaluation — it is part of the training data for the SFT model. The paper continues to report validation set results in Table 2, but these numbers are inflated by training data contamination. The test set remains clean, and the paper appropriately reports test set results alongside validation results. However, the contamination reduces the usable evaluation data for ProofNet to only the 186 test problems, and the SFT model's performance on the validation set should not be used for model selection or comparison.

**Small test sets:** miniF2F-test has 244 problems, ProofNet-test has 186 problems. While this is standard for formal theorem proving benchmarks (which are labor-intensive to create), it means that pass rate differences of 1–3 percentage points correspond to only 2–7 problems on miniF2F and 2–5 problems on ProofNet. The statistical power to distinguish methods with small performance differences is limited, despite the reported standard deviations (which capture sampling variance but not finite-test-set variance).

**Computational cost of tree search is not quantified in wall-clock terms:** The paper measures computation in "generations" or "expansions," which aligns the counting across methods, but does not report actual runtime, GPU-hours, or cost. The tree search involves significant overhead beyond the model generations themselves — Lean verification, tree maintenance, parallelization coordination — that is not captured by the generation count metric. A method that requires 3200 tree search expansions may have substantially different wall-clock time than 3200 independent single-pass generations, even though they are counted as equivalent in the budget.

**RL prompt selection creates a distribution shift between training and evaluation:** The RL training uses a subset of ~4,500 theorems selected for moderate SFT success rate. The test benchmarks (miniF2F, ProofNet) may have a different difficulty distribution, with some problems being harder than anything in the RL training set. If RL improves performance primarily on theorems at the "moderate difficulty" frontier but has no effect (or negative effect) on very hard theorems, the aggregate benchmark improvement could mask heterogeneous effects. The paper does not report per-difficulty or per-topic breakdowns.

**No comparison to the base model with tree search:** The paper applies RMaxTS only to the SFT and RL models, not to the base model. A comparison of Base + RMaxTS vs. SFT + RMaxTS would isolate the contribution of SFT to search efficiency, and Base + RMaxTS vs. Base single-pass would test whether tree search can compensate for weaker generation quality. The absence of these comparisons leaves open the question of how much of RMaxTS's benefit comes from having a strong proposal model vs. from the search algorithm itself.

**Missing ablation: number of MCTS runners and thread workers:** The paper reports using 256 MCTS runners with 32 thread workers per tree (Section 3.4), but does not ablate these settings. Would 64 runners with 128 threads perform similarly? Is the tree search throughput-limited or quality-limited by these parallelism choices? The reported pass rates could depend on these infrastructure choices in non-obvious ways (e.g., more runners with less per-runner budget might have different exploration properties than fewer runners with more budget).

**No comparison between RMaxTS and alternative tree search methods using the same model:** The paper compares RMaxTS against prior tree search methods (HTPS, InternLM2-StepProver) that use different base models and training procedures. A within-model comparison — running RMaxTS vs. standard MCTS vs. best-first search using the same DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL model as the proposal engine — would isolate the contribution of the RMaxTS algorithm from the contribution of the stronger base model. The current results show that DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL single-pass (54.9% at 3200 generations) already surpasses InternLM2-StepProver with tree search (54.5%), which means at least some of RMaxTS's advantage over prior tree search methods comes from the better model, not the better search algorithm.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Truncate-and-Resume Mechanism Provides No Guarantee Against Tactic-Level Compounding Errors Within a Single Expansion

The paper frames the truncate-and-resume mechanism as a solution to the compounding error problem in whole-proof generation. When the model generates a proof segment and Lean detects an error at step $k$, the system truncates at that error, keeping steps $1$ through $k-1$ as verified tactics. However, this mechanism only corrects errors that Lean *detects*. It does not correct errors where the model's internal implicit proof state diverges from reality but the generated tactics happen to be syntactically valid and Lean-accepted through $k-1$ — even though they are semantically nonsensical for proving the theorem. In other words, the truncation point is governed by Lean's error messages, not by whether the tactics correspond to a coherent proof strategy.

**Consequence.** If the model generates a sequence of tactics that are individually valid (Lean accepts each one) but collectively lead to a dead-end proof state — for example, introducing a hypothesis that is true but irrelevant, or applying a rewrite that is legal but doesn't advance the proof — the truncate-and-resume mechanism will faithfully record these tactics as verified tree nodes and continue exploring from them. The search tree accumulates "successful" tactics that are mathematical dead ends, wasting search budget on branches that can never reach a proof. This is a form of **specification gaming at the tactic level**: the model is rewarded (by having its tactics accepted into the tree) for generating anything that passes Lean's syntax and type-checking, not for generating tactics that constitute progress toward the theorem. The intrinsic reward mechanism exacerbates this by rewarding discovery of *any* novel tactic state, regardless of whether that state is on a path to a proof.

**Evidence.** The paper does not measure or report the frequency with which verified tactics are mathematically irrelevant rather than genuinely useful. The ablation in Figure 5 shows that omitting intrinsic rewards reduces RMaxTS to single-pass performance, confirming that intrinsic rewards drive exploration — but it does not distinguish between exploration that discovers useful proof states and exploration that discovers irrelevant ones. The qualitative examples in Appendix B show successful proofs, but there is no analysis of failed searches: how many tree nodes, on average, represent dead-end states? What fraction of the search tree consists of branches that can never lead to a complete proof?

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges at a high level (Section 5) that training a critic model to evaluate partial proofs is a "promising future direction" and "a crucial and challenging problem that warrants further investigation," but provides no mechanism in the current system for distinguishing useful proof states from dead ends. The intrinsic reward only cares about novelty, not utility. This is a fundamental limitation of the RMaxTS approach in its current form: exploration is undirected, driven entirely by state-space coverage rather than by progress toward the proof goal.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted For, and There Is No Mechanism for Identifying Problems Where the Method Will Fail

The paper's strongest results (63.5% on miniF2F-test, 25.3% on ProofNet) are aggregate pass rates that include all problems in the test sets. However, the method provides no mechanism for estimating, before committing the full computational budget, whether a given theorem is likely to be solvable or is fundamentally beyond the model's capability. The deployment practitioner faces a binary decision: invest thousands of generations (and the associated GPU and CPU time for model inference and Lean verification) in attempting a proof, with no advance signal about whether the attempt will succeed.

**Consequence.** In a production theorem-proving deployment, compute budget is finite and must be allocated across many theorems. Without difficulty estimation, the system must either (a) spend the same budget on every problem (wasting compute on trivially easy problems that require few attempts and on impossibly hard problems that will never be solved regardless of budget), or (b) use a fixed budget per problem determined by trial and error, which is suboptimal for both easy and hard problems. The paper's single-pass results in Table 1 illustrate the consequence: scaling from 3,200 to 102,400 generations (32× more compute) improves pass rate from 54.9% to 60.2% on miniF2F — a gain of only 5.3 percentage points for 32× the cost. The vast majority of that additional compute is spent on problems that remain unsolved even at the maximum budget, with no way to identify them in advance and save the compute.

**Evidence.** The paper does not report any form of difficulty estimation, confidence calibration, or per-problem success probability. The pass@K curves in Figure 3 and the large-budget scaling in Tables 1–3 show aggregate performance but provide no per-problem breakdown that would reveal which problems are solved early (and could have used less budget) versus which are never solved (and consumed budget fruitlessly). The diminishing returns evident in the single-pass scaling curves (58.4% at 25,600 generations to 60.2% at 102,400 generations on miniF2F, Table 1) suggest that a significant fraction of the additional compute is wasted on unsolvable problems.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not mention difficulty estimation, budget allocation, or early stopping as design considerations. The tree search terminates when a correct proof is found or the sample budget is exhausted, with no intermediate assessment of whether the search is making progress. This is a direct contrast to work like the DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 concept's contemporary literature on compute-optimal test-time scaling, where difficulty estimation is treated as a first-class component of the system design. The absence of difficulty awareness means the paper's headline efficiency comparisons (e.g., matching InternLM2-StepProver's 54.5% with 64× fewer expansions) are computed after the fact — the system doesn't *know* it can achieve that efficiency on a per-problem basis, it simply runs the full budget and reports what fraction succeeded.

### The SFT and RL Training Pipelines Rely on Access to a 236B-Parameter Model for Data Annotation, Creating a Practical Dependency That Undermines the "7B Model" Narrative

The thought-augmented proof generation step (Section 2.2) uses DeepSeek-Coder V2 236B (Zhu et al., 2024) — a model roughly 34× larger than DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 — to annotate the training data with natural language chain-of-thought comments. This annotation is not a minor post-processing step; it fundamentally determines the quality of the CoT training data. The paper states:

> "We use DeepSeek-Coder V2 236B to enhance existing data in DeepSeek-Prover-V1 in two ways: first, by inserting a complete natural language solution at the beginning of the proof block, and second, by alternately inserting specific natural language steps for corresponding Lean tactics."

**Consequence.** The performance of DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 in CoT mode — which accounts for the best results on both benchmarks (60.2% single-pass, 63.5% with RMaxTS on miniF2F) — is contingent on the availability of a 236B-parameter model for training data generation. A research group or organization that has a 7B model but not a 236B model cannot replicate the full training pipeline. Moreover, it is impossible to determine from the paper's results how much of the CoT mode's advantage comes from the 7B model learning to reason in natural language (the claimed mechanism) versus simply distilling the reasoning patterns of a much more capable model into its own outputs. The non-CoT mode, which does not rely on the 236B model's annotations, achieves lower performance (57.4% vs. 60.2% at 16 × 6400 on miniF2F, Table 1), confirming that the annotation makes a measurable difference — but the paper provides no ablation where CoT annotations are generated by a comparably-sized model, which would isolate the contribution of the annotation format from the contribution of the annotator model's capability.

**Evidence.** The paper reports CoT vs. non-CoT performance throughout (Figure 3, Tables 1–3), consistently showing a CoT advantage of 1–3 percentage points. The data curation section (2.2) states that DeepSeek-Coder V2 236B is used for annotation but provides no comparison to annotations from smaller models or to human-written chain-of-thought. The CoT examples in Appendix A show sophisticated mathematical reasoning in the comments — reasoning that may reflect the 236B model's capabilities more than the 7B model's learned skills.

**Mitigation status.** Not acknowledged as a limitation. The paper treats the use of DeepSeek-Coder V2 236B as a standard data augmentation technique, not as a dependency. It does not discuss whether the 236B model is strictly necessary or whether similar results could be achieved with self-generated annotations (the 7B model annotating its own training data in a later expert iteration). The paper's framing as "an open-source 7B-parameter language model" is technically accurate for the released model weights but elides the practical dependency on a much larger model in the training pipeline.

### The Training Data Includes the Validation Sets of Both Benchmarks, and Expert Iteration May Overfit to the Test Set Distribution

The paper's data curation for SFT explicitly uses the validation sets of miniF2F and ProofNet as sources of theorem statements:

> "These theorems are sourced from various projects, such as the standard Lean 4 math library Mathlib4, synthetic theorems from DeepSeek-Prover-V1 and Lean Workbook, and validation sets from the miniF2F and ProofNet benchmarks."

This means theorems from the validation sets are included in the SFT training data. While the test sets are held out, the validation and test sets are drawn from the same benchmark distributions (high-school competition problems for miniF2F, undergraduate textbook problems for ProofNet). Expert iteration further compounds this concern: the SFT model generates proofs on the validation set, the verified proofs are added to the training data, and the retrained model is evaluated on the test set. If the validation and test sets share stylistic properties (theorem phrasing conventions, specific mathematical domains, common proof patterns), the model trained on validation-set proofs may perform better on the test set than on genuinely out-of-distribution theorems — not because it has learned general proof-writing skills, but because it has memorized proof patterns specific to those benchmarks.

**Consequence.** The reported test-set results may be overestimates of the model's generalization to novel formal theorems. This is particularly concerning for ProofNet, which the paper acknowledges explicitly (Table 2, footnote ‡):

> "Note that the validation set of ProofNet is used to perform expert iteration in supervised fine-tuning."

The ProofNet validation set contains 185 problems, and the test set contains 186 — roughly equal in size. If the two sets are drawn from the same undergraduate textbook domains (real analysis, linear algebra, abstract algebra, topology), the model may have effectively seen training examples from the same textbook chapters and theorem types that appear in the test set. The paper reports only aggregate results; it does not break down performance by mathematical domain to check whether the improvement is uniform or concentrated in domains well-represented in the validation set.

**Evidence.** Section 2.2 confirms the use of validation sets in SFT data. Table 2 reports ProofNet validation and test results separately, with RL achieving 21.6% on validation and 23.7% on test at 4 × 6400 single-pass — the test set performance is actually higher, which is unusual and could reflect the small sample sizes (~185 problems each) or differences in difficulty between the splits. The paper provides no analysis of domain-specific performance, no comparison of validation-to-test transfer, and no evaluation on a third benchmark that was entirely excluded from training.

**Mitigation status.** Partially acknowledged. The paper discloses the validation set usage in Table 2's footnote and in Section 2.2, which is transparent. However, it does not discuss the implications for test-set generalization, does not provide domain-level breakdowns, and does not evaluate on an external holdout benchmark not used in any stage of training. The standard defense — that the test set was never used in training and that expert iteration on the validation set is common practice in this literature (it mirrors how AlphaZero used self-play data from related positions) — is reasonable but incomplete given the small size and potential stylistic homogeneity of the benchmarks.

### The RMaxTS Algorithm Introduces Substantial Computational Overhead Beyond Model Generations, Making the Headline "Sample Budget" Comparisons Misleading

The paper aligns computational budgets across methods by counting "generations" (language model forward passes), arguing that this is the primary cost driver. For single-pass generation, one generation equals one proof attempt; for tree search methods, one generation equals one model invocation during a tree expansion. However, RMaxTS involves significant additional computation that is not captured by this metric: Lean verification of every generated proof segment (including partial segments that are subsequently truncated), tree maintenance operations (node insertion, state hashing for novelty detection, backpropagation with discounted statistics), parallelization coordination (32 thread workers per tree, virtual loss bookkeeping), and the root parallelization overhead of managing 256 independent MCTS runners.

**Consequence.** Two methods with the same "generation count" may have substantially different wall-clock times and total computational costs. The paper's headline comparison — that DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-RL + RMaxTS at 1 × 3200 generations (55.0%) matches InternLM2-StepProver at 64 × 32 × 100 = 204,800 expansions (54.5%) — is based purely on generation counts. But Lean verification of 3200 proof segments in a tree search framework (each requiring parsing, truncation, state extraction, and node insertion) may be comparable in wall-clock time to verifying many more independent single-tactic expansions in InternLM2-StepProver. The paper does not report actual runtime, CPU-core-hours, GPU-hours, or any cost metric that would enable a practitioner to make an informed hardware provisioning decision.

**Evidence.** Section 3.4 describes a substantial parallelization infrastructure: 256 MCTS runners, 32 thread workers per tree, a cluster with "thousands of CPU cores," asynchronous Lean verification in sandboxed processes, and batching of up to 512 proof generation requests per GPU. The computational scale of this deployment is not quantified beyond these architectural descriptions. The paper reports no runtime measurements, no cost estimates, and no comparison of wall-clock time between RMaxTS and single-pass generation at equivalent generation counts.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper uses generation count as the sole budget metric and does not discuss the overhead of tree search operations, verification, or parallelization. This is standard practice in the theorem-proving literature (Hypertree Proof Search, InternLM2-StepProver, and GPT-f all use similar accounting), but it makes the efficiency claims difficult to interpret operationally. A practitioner choosing between deploying single-pass generation with 100,000 independent samples (trivially parallelizable, no tree overhead) and RMaxTS with 25,600 tree expansions (complex infrastructure, Lean verification overhead per expansion, parallelization coordination) has no quantitative basis for the decision from the paper's reported results.
